Targeting MSR1 to Facilitate Efferocytosis: A Novel Strategy for Immune Homeostasis Regulation in Irreversible Pulpitis
Authors: Sitong Liu, Longrui Dang, Xiaolan Guo, Kailun Wu, Xiaofei Qu, Jing Xu, Buling Wu, Zhao Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo investigate the role of macrophage-mediated efferocytosis in resolution of inflammation during irreversible pulpitis, with a focus on the functional relevance of macrophage scavenger receptor 1 (MSR1).MethodsWhole-transcriptome sequencing was performed on pulp tissue from 3 healthy individuals and 3 with pulpitis, integrated with Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets (GSE77459, GSE92681; total n = 30). After batch correction, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (|Fold Change|>1.5, P < .05) and analyzed by Gene Ontology (GO) / Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment, CIBERSORT immune cell deconvolution, and machine learning. Efferocytosis activity was validated by immunofluorescence, Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), and Western blot (WB). MSR1 was selected for in vivo validation via small interfering RNA (siRNA) knockdown in a rat pulpitis model.ResultsA total of 467 differentially expressed genes were identified, which were enriched in immune response and phagosome-related pathways. Macrophage infiltration was significantly increased in pulpitis tissues, accompanied by upregulation of efferocytosis markers. Immunofluorescence showed that MER proto-oncogene tyrosine kinase (MERTK)-positive macrophages in human inflammatory dental pulp could phagocytize apoptotic cells positive for caspase 3 (CASP3) and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP). MSR1 is regarded as a key regulatory factor. Knockdown of Msr1 in rats can impair the clearance of apoptotic cells, reduce the expression of Mertk, and aggravate inflammation.ConclusionMSR1 maintains immune homeostasis in the dental pulp by promoting macrophage efferocytosis, providing a theoretical basis for targeted vital pulp therapy.Keywords: Pulpitis, Efferocytosis, MSR1, Macrophage, Animal model

Introduction

Pulpitis represents a common oral infectious condition, initiated by bacterial penetration into the dental pulp chamber and resulting in a persistent inflammatory reaction. Its characteristic pathological features encompass enhanced vascular permeability, elevated tissue pressure, and dysregulation of immune homeostasis. The distinctive anatomical configuration of dental pulp predisposes the inflammatory microenvironment to hypoxia and metabolic waste accumulation, thereby accelerating tissue necrosis and potentially propagating infection to periapical regions or jawbone.1,2 Although conventional root canal therapy effectively controls infection, it sacrifices pulp vitality, which compromises tooth strength and functionality.3,4 Vital pulp therapy (VPT) has recently gained attention as an effective approach to maintaining pulp vitality. However, therapeutic success critically depends on the meticulous regulation of the balance between inflammation resolution and tissue repair,5, 6, 7 yet key molecular checkpoints governing this process remain unclear.

Encased in mineralized dentin and lacking collateral circulation, the dental pulp forms a highly enclosed immune microenvironment. During pathogenic invasion, heightened tissue pressure, hypoxic conditions, and metabolic stress challenge the immune system to maintain a delicate balance between pathogen clearance and prevention of excessive inflammation.8,9 Under these dual stresses, macrophages play a central role in immune regulation through efferocytosis.10, 11, 12 By recognizing phosphatidylserine (“eat-me” signals) on apoptotic cells via MER proto-oncogene tyrosine kinase (MERTK) and T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing 4 (TIM4) receptors, macrophages trigger engulfment processes dependent on RAC1 and RHO GTPases.13 This process facilitates apoptotic cell uptake, promotes the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines including IL-10 and TGF-β, and concurrently inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome assembly together with the maturation of IL-1β and IL-18. Consequently, efferocytosis orchestrates both the clearance of cellular debris and the restoration of tissue homeostasis.14 This dual mechanism integrating silent debris removal with active immune modulation presents novel therapeutic opportunities for inflammatory disorders.15,16

To date, macrophage efferocytosis has been validated as a central mechanism of inflammation resolution in various pathological contexts.17, 18, 19 In atherosclerosis, macrophages recognize oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) on apoptotic cells via macrophage scavenger receptor 1 (MSR1), thereby activating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway to clear necrotic cores and stabilize plaques.20 In the tumor microenvironment, the “don’t-eat-me” signal mediated by the LILRB1/MHC-I axis inhibits macrophage phagocytosis, facilitating immune evasion, while targeted blockade of this pathway reprograms anti-tumor immune responses.21 In periodontitis, DEL-1 enhances macrophage clearance of apoptotic neutrophils through integrin αvβ3 and promotes inflammation resolution via the secretion of the pro-resolving mediator Resolvin D1.22 Despite these advances, the operational dynamics and regulatory potential of efferocytosis in pulpitis remain largely uncharted. Crucially, the impact of the pulp's confined microenvironment on macrophage phagocytic functionality warrants systematic investigation.

In this study, we integrated transcriptomic data from clinical samples and public databases to identify efferocytosis-related pathways in macrophages and verified the spatial co-localization of the efferocytosis marker with apoptotic cells in pulpitis tissues. Using machine learning algorithms, MSR1 was identified as a core regulatory gene, showing significantly upregulated expression in inflamed pulp, as illustrated in Figure 1. In vivo, a Sprague-Dawley rat pulpitis model was established to simulate physiological and pathological changes. In vivo experiments further revealed that Msr1 knockdown impaired efferocytosis capacity, evidenced by decreased Mertk expression, accumulation of apoptotic cells, and exacerbated inflammation. These findings suggest that MSR1 maintains immune homeostasis in the dental pulp by modulating macrophage phagocytic efficiency. This study not only provides the first molecular characterization of efferocytosis in pulpitis but also offers critical theoretical support for the VPT strategies that aim to preserve pulp vitality by targeting macrophage function.

Materials and methods

Collection of dental pulp tissue

Clinical pulp tissue samples were collected from patients in the outpatient department of Nanfang Hospital. All the subjects signed the written informed consent statement. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee (Application No. NFEC-2023-139). Details of the samples are provided in Table 1.

Participant details.

Pulp tissue samples were obtained from patients aged 18-31 years, all of whom were free from systemic diseases or periodontal disease. Healthy pulp tissue was collected from caries-free third molars or premolars extracted for orthodontic, impaction, or other therapeutic reasons. Inflamed pulp tissue was harvested from third molars with caries-related pulpitis, diagnosed as irreversible pulpitis. Diagnostic criteria included a history of spontaneous or nocturnal pain, or prolonged sensitivity to thermal stimuli, without periapical radiolucency. All clinical pulp samples were placed in normal saline and transported to the laboratory within 30 minutes of extraction.

Following tooth extraction, the surface blood was rinsed with saline. Teeth were wrapped tightly in sterile gauze and cracked open using a hammer. Pulp tissue was carefully removed using sterile dental probes and ophthalmic forceps, then placed into pre-chilled PBS and washed 3 times.

Whole transcriptome sequencing

The whole transcriptome sequencing data are from our previous studies. The experimental protocol for tissue collection, RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing can be found in the previous article.23 The original data was uploaded to the database of NCBI, with the serial number: PRJNA1140404.

Acquisition of public data from GEO

The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/info/datasets.html), maintained by NCBI, was used to obtain publicly available transcriptomic data. Series Matrix File data for pulpitis (GSE77459, GSE92681) were downloaded. These datasets include expression profiles from 24 patients: 11 with healthy pulp tissue and 13 with inflamed pulp tissue.

Differential expression and enrichment analyses

In total, expression data from 30 samples (14 healthy and 16 inflamed) were compiled. The Surrogate Variable Analysis (SVA) algorithm was used to correct batch effects, and principal component analysis (PCA) plots were generated to visualize data before and after correction. SVA correction effectively minimized batch effects across chips.

DEGs were identified using thresholds of |Fold Change| > 1.5 and P < .05. Volcano plots and heatmaps were generated using the R packages ggplot2 and pheatmap. Identified DEGs were uploaded to the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis.

Immune infiltration analysis

Immune cell infiltration was analyzed using the CIBERSORT algorithm, a widely used deconvolution tool based on support vector regression that estimates immune cell subtype composition in complex tissues. Using CIBERSORT, we estimated the relative proportions of 22 immune cell types in pulpitis samples and conducted Spearman correlation analyses between gene expression levels and immune cell fractions. Visualizations were produced using R packages including ggplot2 and heatmap.

Identification of efferocytosis-related genes

Efferocytosis-related genes (ERGs) were identified from multiple sources, including the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/), and published article.24 The keyword “Efferocytosis” was used to compile a comprehensive ERG set, supplemented with curated genes from key references. Differentially expressed ERGs (ERDEGs) were identified using the VennDiagram package in R.

Machine learning approaches

Three machine learning algorithms were applied to identify characteristic genes. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was performed using the glmnet package in R with a binomial family and an elastic net penalty (α=0.5). The optimal penalty parameter (λ.min) was determined by 10-fold cross-validation based on minimum binomial deviance, yielding a subset of candidate genes. A random forest classifier was constructed using the randomForest package in R with 500 initial trees. The optimal number of trees was determined by the minimum classification error rate, and gene importance was ranked by mean decrease in accuracy. Support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) was performed with the caret package using a radial basis function kernel. Feature subset sizes were set from 2 to 50, and cross-validation performance was evaluated by root mean square error. Genes at the point of minimum RMSE were selected as the final features.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR)

Tissue samples from human dental pulp or animals were collected using RNase-free instruments and immediately transferred into 1.5 mL RNase-free tubes. For mRNA extraction from animal tissues, magnetic beads of various sizes, together with 500 μL of lysis buffer were added, and the tissues were homogenized using a tissue grinder (Jingxin, Shanghai, China). Total RNA was then isolated using the Tissue RNA Purification Kit PLUS (EZB-RN001-plus, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and quantified by spectrophotometer. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA in a 20 μL reaction volume following the manufacturer’s protocol, diluted to 200 μL with DEPC-treated water, and stored at −20°C until use. Protein extraction and western blot (WB)

Total protein was isolated from pulp tissues using RIPA. Equal amounts of protein were separated on 12.5% SDS-PAGE gels and subsequently transferred onto 0.22 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk for 1 hour at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: GAPDH (Proteintech, 10494-1-AP), MERTK (HUABIO, ET1602-21), MSR1 (HUABIO, ER1913-21), TIM4 (HUABIO, ER61913). After washing, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP substrate, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ.

Paraffin embedding of pulp tissues

Extracted pulp tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 24 hours, rinsed under tap water overnight, dehydrated in graded ethanol, and cleared with xylene. Tissues were embedded in paraffin after 4 hours of infiltration. Tissue blocks were sectioned into 4-μm-thick slices using a microtome, and sections were floated in a water bath. Expanded sections were mounted onto adhesive slides, blotted to remove excess water, and dried overnight at 37 °C before storage at 4 °C.

Establishment of a rat model of pulpitis in bilateral maxillary first molars

All animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee (Application No. IACUC-LAC-20240301-001). The number of rats in each group was determined based on a previous study (n = 6).25 Male Sprague-Dawley rats (6-8 weeks old, weighing 150-250 g) were purchased from Zhuhai Baishitong Bio-Tech Co., Ltd (China) and housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility at the Animal Experimental Center.

Rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 2.5% avertin (0.1mL/10 g body weight). Small interfering RNA (siRNA) (TSINGKE, China) was mixed with in vivo-siRNA mate (GenePharma, China) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. A microinjector (Bolige, China) was used to inject 5 µL of the siRNA mixture submucosally into the buccal and palatal regions of the bilateral maxillary first molars. Injections were administered once daily for 3 consecutive days.

On the fourth day, rats were re-anesthetized and immobilized. Pulp exposure was performed using a BR-49 diamond bur (Bosch, Germany) at the center of the occlusal surface of the bilateral maxillary first molars. When dentin translucency turned red, a #35/40 K-file (Dentsply Sirona, USA) was used to penetrate the dentin and expose the pulp tissue. Subsequently, approximately 5 µL of the siRNA mixture was re-injected. The exposed pulp chambers were rinsed with 0.9% sterile saline and gently dried with sterile cotton pellets. A bonding agent (3M ESPE, USA) was applied with a microbrush and cured using a light-curing unit. The cavities were then sealed using flowable composite resin (3M ESPE, USA). At 72 hours post-operation, rats were euthanized by transcardial perfusion. The maxillary bones were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) for 24 hours, followed by overnight washing in running water. Decalcification was carried out in 10% EDTA solution (Leagene, China) at 37°C for 4 weeks. Subsequent tissue processing and sectioning were performed as previously described. Hematoxylin and eosin staining

Paraffin-embedded sections of both healthy and inflamed dental pulp tissues were placed in a 65 °C oven for 1 hour. After cooling at room temperature for 20 minutes, the sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a descending ethanol gradient. Sections were stained with hematoxylin solution, rinsed with running tap water, and differentiated in 0.5% hydrochloric acid-ethanol solution. Subsequently, eosin staining was performed. The sections were dehydrated with graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin. After the resin solidified, images were captured using an Image D2 upright microscope.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

For immunofluorescence staining, paraffin sections were dewaxed and rehydrated following the same procedure as described above. After immersion in distilled water for 5 minutes, the sections were incubated overnight in diluted Citrate-EDTA Antigen Retrieval Solution (Beyotime, China) in a 60 °C water bath. Tissue boundaries were delineated with a hydrophobic barrier pen, followed by 3 washes in PBST (3 minutes each). Sections were blocked in 5% BSA (Beyotime, China) for 1 hour at room temperature.

The primary antibodies used were as follows: Caspase-3 (Abmart, T40044, 1:100), PARP (Abmart, T40050, 1:100), MERTK (HUABIO, ET1602-21, 1:100), MSR1 (HUABIO, ER1913-21, 1:100), TNF-α (Abmart, PY19810, 1:300), TGF-β (Proteintech, 1:300). Secondary antibodies included FITC-labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) (Beyotime, China) and AF647-labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) (Beyotime, China).

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed using a Treble-Fluorescence Immunohistochemical Mouse/Rabbit Kit (ImmunoWay, China). Finally, sections were mounted with antifade medium containing DAPI (Solarbio, S2110, China) and imaged using a fully automated inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon, N31373).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). Each experiment was independently repeated at least 3 times. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between 2 groups were performed using paired t-tests. If the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated, non-parametric tests were applied instead. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. Levels of significance are indicated as follows: *, **, ***, and **** represent P < .05, P < .01, P < .001, and P < .0001, respectively.

Results

Identification of differentially expressed genes in pulpitis tissues

To identify gene expression alterations associated with pulpitis, we conducted whole-transcriptome sequencing on 3 samples of healthy human dental pulp tissue and 3 samples of human pulpitis tissue from our previous study.23

In addition, we integrated transcriptome data from the NCBI GEO public database, including datasets GSE77459 and GSE92681, along with our in-house dataset, resulting in a total of 30 samples (14 healthy pulp and 16 pulpitis tissues). Batch effects were corrected using the SVA algorithm, and PCA plots were generated to visualize batch correction (Figure 2A-B). The results showed that batch effects across datasets were substantially reduced after correction.

Differential expression and enrichment analyses reveal the involvement of efferocytosis in pulpitis. A-B. Integration of in-house transcriptomic data with datasets from the GEO database. Principal component analysis (PCA) plots demonstrate that batch effects were effectively reduced after correction using the Surrogate Variable Analysis (SVA) algorithm. C-D. Heatmap and volcano plot of DEGs identified between healthy and pulpitis tissues. Upregulated genes are shown in red, while downregulated genes are shown in blue. E-F. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of DEGs, highlighting pathways and biological processes potentially related to the inflammatory response and efferocytosis in pulpitis.

To characterize the transcriptional landscape of pulpitis, we conducted differential expression analysis with thresholds set at |Fold Change| > 1.5 and P < .05. A total of 346 genes were significantly upregulated and 121 genes were downregulated in pulpitis tissues. Figure 2C-D present the heatmap and volcano plot of DEGs, with red denoting upregulated and blue denoting downregulated genes.

To further explore the biological processes and functional pathways involved in pulpitis, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed on the identified DEGs (Figure 2E-F). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were significantly associated with immune-related biological processes such as leukocyte migration, leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, and leukocyte chemotaxis, highlighting the pivotal role of immune cell recruitment in the pathogenesis of pulpitis. In the cellular component category, genes were enriched in the external side of the plasma membrane, suggesting that immune responses may be initiated via pattern recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) by membrane-bound receptors. In the molecular function category, DEGs were enriched in terms such as immune receptor activity and cytokine receptor activity, indicating a systemic activation of inflammatory signaling pathways. KEGG pathway analysis further showed significant enrichment in the phagosome pathway, suggesting the involvement of phagocytic processes in pulpitis. This may reflect impaired phagocytosis in the inflamed microenvironment, thereby exacerbating tissue damage and driving inflammatory cascades. These findings provide multidimensional insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying pulpitis.

Macrophage infiltration is present in pulpitis tissues

Immune cells in inflamed tissues often exhibit marked heterogeneity in terms of their composition, abundance, activation status, and function within the local microenvironment. To investigate the immune landscape in pulpitis, we performed immune cell infiltration analysis based on integrated transcriptomic data. The results revealed widespread infiltration of various immune cell types in pulpitis tissues, including macrophages, dendritic cells, T cells, and B cells (Figure 3A). Notably, macrophages exhibited significantly elevated infiltration levels in the pulpitis group. Given their pivotal role as effector cells in efferocytosis, macrophages may play a key role in the immunoregulatory mechanisms of pulpitis.

Histopathological analysis further supported these findings. H&E staining revealed minimal immune cell presence in healthy pulp tissues, whereas inflammatory cell infiltration was evident in pulpitis tissues (Figure 3B). To specifically identify the infiltrating immune cell types, we performed immunofluorescence staining using CD68, a macrophage-specific surface marker. The results showed a markedly increased density of CD68+ cells in pulpitis tissues compared to healthy controls (Figure 3C), indicating the abnormal accumulation of macrophages at the lesion sites. This spatial distribution pattern aligns closely with the transcriptomic findings, further implicating macrophage infiltration as a potential driver of pulpitis progression.

Efferocytosis occurs in pulpitis tissues

To further elucidate the presence of efferocytosis in pulpitis tissues, we performed immunofluorescence staining to detect the expression of apoptosis- and efferocytosis-related markers. As shown in Figure 4A-B, fluorescence signals of CASP3 and MERTK were markedly stronger in inflamed pulp tissues compared to healthy controls. Notably, MERTK+ phagocytes were observed engulfing CASP3+/PARP+ apoptotic cells, confirming activation of the apoptotic cell clearance mechanism within the pulpitis microenvironment. qRT-PCR and WB analyses further validated a significant upregulation of MERTK and TIM4 at both mRNA and protein levels in human inflamed pulp tissues, molecularly supporting the activation of efferocytosis (Figure 4C-G).

Based on comprehensive mining from the GeneCards database, KEGG pathway database, and relevant literature, a total of 356 ERGs were compiled (Supplementary material). Intersection analysis with differentially expressed genes identified 31 ERDEGs, of which 29 were upregulated and 2 were downregulated (Figure 5A-B). Functional enrichment analyses of these ERDEGs revealed significant associations with pathways such as “cytokine DNA-sensing pathway,” “JAK-STAT signaling pathway,” and “arachidonic acid metabolism” (Figure 5C-D). Activation of the JAK-STAT pathway may enhance efferocytosis efficiency by regulating macrophage polarization,26, 27, 28 while aberrant arachidonic acid metabolites might participate in a bidirectional regulatory network between inflammation and efferocytosis.17,29 These findings provide important insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying efferocytosis regulation in pulpitis.

Identification and validation of key ERDEGs

To precisely identify core genes regulating efferocytosis, we employed 3 machine learning algorithms—LASSO regression, SVM, and random forest—to perform multi-model feature selection. LASSO regression analysis yielded 12 key genes (Figure 6A-B), SVM identified 15 key genes (Figure 6C), and random forest analysis detected 16 key genes (Figure 6D-E). Intersection analysis using a Venn diagram revealed 5 hub genes: ABCA1, MSR1, LILRB1, ELMO1, and IL18 (Figure 6F).

To validate the expression of these key genes in pulpitis tissues, qRT-PCR was performed, confirming that the expression trends of ABCA1, MSR1, LILRB1, and ELMO1 were consistent with transcriptomic data (Figure 7A-J), with MSR1 showing the greatest upregulation. Considering that MSR1 functions as a critical macrophage surface receptor recognizing “eat-me” signals initiating efferocytosis, we selected it for further investigation.

Immunofluorescence staining was conducted to assess MSR1 expression in pulpitis tissues. Results demonstrated markedly elevated MSR1 expression in both human and rat inflamed pulp tissues. Moreover, IF staining confirmed that MSR1 was specifically and highly expressed in macrophages within the inflamed pulp of humans and rats (Figure 7K-L).

In vivo validation of the role of Msr1 in the progression of pulpitis

To investigate the role of Msr1 in the progression of pulpitis in vivo, we successfully designed and synthesized siRNA targeting the rat Msr1 gene. Local injections of siRNA-Msr1 were administered to the buccal and palatal gingival tissues in the apical region of bilateral maxillary first molars for 3 consecutive days prior to pulp exposure (n=6). Immediately following pulp exposure, additional siRNA-Msr1 was injected into the exposed pulp chamber, which was then sealed with flowable resin. Tissues were harvested 72 hours post-procedure for further analysis (Figure 8A-B). Subsequently, the tissue mRNA was extracted for qRT-PCR experiments. The results showed that the expression of Msr1 mRNA was lower in healthy tissues, while it was highly expressed in the Control group and the NC group. The siRNA group achieved effective knockdown (Figure 8C). Moreover, the expressions of the efferocytosis-related markers Mertk and Tim4 decreased concomitantly with Msr1 downregulation, which also indicated that the efficacy of cell engulfment was affected (Figure 8D-E).

Immunofluorescence staining revealed negligible MSR1 expression in the pulp tissue of the Blank group. In contrast, both the Control and si-NC groups exhibited high MSR1 expression, whereas the si-Msr1 group showed significantly reduced MSR1 signals, confirming the effective silencing of the target gene by the siRNA (Figure 8F-G).

HE staining showed that pulp tissue in the Blank group maintained an intact structure, with odontoblasts arranged in a single palisade-like layer and no evident inflammatory cell infiltration. In both the Control and si-NC groups, localized necrosis was observed beneath the exposure site, with inflammatory cells limited to the upper one-third of the root canal. Notably, the si-Msr1 group exhibited aggravated inflammatory features, including inflammatory infiltration extending into the middle third of the root canal and prominent vascular dilation, indicating that MSR1 deficiency exacerbated pulpal inflammation (Figure 9A). Meanwhile, after reducing the expression of MSR1, the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α increased compared to the Control group and the si-NC group, while the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokine TGF-β decreased relatively (Figure 9B).

To further investigate the role of MSR1 in efferocytosis, we performed double immunofluorescence staining to detect 2 key markers: MERTK and PARP. In the Control and si-NC groups, scattered apoptotic cells were observed in the pulp chamber, accompanied by relatively high MERTK expression. However, in the MSR1 knockdown group, apoptotic cells abnormally accumulated within the pulp tissue, and MERTK expression was markedly reduced (Figure 9C-D). These findings suggest that MSR1 deficiency impairs the clearance of apoptotic cells, leading to the accumulation of cell debris and the release of DAMPs, which subsequently activate pro-inflammatory signaling pathways. This establishes a vicious cycle in which efferocytosis deficiency exacerbates inflammation.

Discussion

Through integrative multi-omics analysis combined with experimental validation, this study reveals a pivotal regulatory role of efferocytosis in the initiation and progression of pulpitis. Notably, macrophage surface receptor MSR1 was identified as a core molecule orchestrating efferocytosis and modulating inflammatory responses. These findings not only provide novel insights into the immune regulatory mechanisms underlying pulpitis but also highlight MSR1 as a potential therapeutic target for modulating inflammation in hard tissue diseases.

Integrated analysis of RNA-Seq data and publicly available GEO datasets demonstrated that DEGs in pulpitis tissues were significantly enriched in efferocytosis-related pathways such as “phagosome formation” and “leukocyte migration,” indicating an active apoptotic cell clearance mechanism in the inflamed pulp microenvironment. Consistently, experimental validation revealed elevated expression of the apoptosis marker CASP3 and the efferocytosis receptor MERTK in pulpitis tissues. Immunofluorescence staining further showed their co-localization within inflamed regions, suggesting that the accumulation of apoptotic cells triggers macrophage efferocytic responses. During this process, apoptotic cells release “find-me” signals to recruit macrophages to sites of inflammation and expose “eat-me” signals on their surface to engage macrophage-specific receptors, thereby facilitating the clearance of apoptotic debris.

This mechanism may play a dual role in pulpitis: on the one hand, efficient efferocytosis prevents secondary inflammation and maintains tissue homeostasis by removing dying cells in a timely manner; on the other hand, excessive efferocytic burden may lead to macrophage exhaustion, contributing to immune dysregulation and forming a vicious cycle of pathological progression.30,31 Given the unique features of the pulp microenvironment—including hypoxia, hyperosmolarity, and persistent pathogenic stimuli—the risk of an imbalance between apoptosis and immune clearance is significantly higher compared to other tissues.32

Although the regulatory mechanisms of efferocytosis have been extensively studied in diseases such as atherosclerosis and cancer,33,34 the macrophage response to apoptotic signals in pulpitis and its role in immune homeostasis remain poorly understood. To identify key regulators of efferocytosis, we employed a combined machine learning strategy using SVM-RFE, LASSO, and random forest algorithms35, 36, 37 to screen 356 ERDEGs, ultimately identifying 5 hub genes: ABCA1, MSR1, LILRB1, ELMO1, and IL18.

Among these, ABCA1 facilitates cholesterol efflux to apoA-I and HDL in macrophages. Its deficiency exacerbates atherosclerosis, whereas ABCG1 deficiency may attenuate it, possibly due to compensatory induction of ABCA1 and ApoE or oxysterol-mediated macrophage apoptosis—highlighting ABCA1’s potential role in efferocytosis.38,39 MSR1, a key scavenger receptor on macrophages, recognizes and internalizes apoptotic cells, playing an essential role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing inflammation. Its function is modulated by inflammatory cues and oxidative stress and involves crosstalk with other receptors and signaling pathways. For instance, LILRB1, an inhibitory receptor that binds MHC-I, acts as a “don’t-eat-me” signal. Overexpression of MHC-I suppresses macrophage phagocytosis of tumor cells via LILRB1 engagement.40,41 Barkal et al. demonstrated that blocking LILRB1 enhances macrophage-mediated phagocytosis,21 and co-administration of anti-LILRB1 with CD47/CD20 antibodies further promotes phagocytosis of lymphoma cells, underscoring its immunotherapeutic potential.42,43

ELMO1, a cytoplasmic protein involved in apoptotic cell engulfment, promotes macrophage efferocytic capacity. Its downregulation impairs phagocytosis, while Morioka et al. showed that ELMO1 enhances efferocytosis by forming a complex with DOCK180 to activate RAC1 signaling.44 IL18, a pro-inflammatory cytokine of the IL-1 family, is closely associated with macrophage activation syndrome, where macrophage overactivation is prominent.45,46 Although its role in efferocytosis remains unclear, these findings suggest IL18 may influence macrophage function during apoptotic cell clearance.

Further qRT-PCR analysis revealed that MSR1 showed the most significant differential expression among the hub ERDEGs in pulpitis tissues, indicating its potential as a central regulator of macrophage function in this context. Increasing evidence supports the involvement of MSR1 in regulating efferocytosis via multiple pathways. In a hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury model, MSR1 promoted β-catenin nuclear translocation by activating the PI3K-AKT-GSK3β signaling axis, driving macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and enhancing their phagocytic capacity, thereby alleviating inflammation.47,48 In cardiovascular disease, MSR1 forms functional complexes with TAM family receptors such as Tyro3, synergistically regulating apoptotic vascular smooth muscle cell clearance via the PI3K/Akt pathway and attenuating acute aortic dissection progression.49

In the present study, a rat pulpitis model further confirmed that Msr1 knockdown led to increased inflammatory cell infiltration, accumulation of apoptotic cells, and downregulation of MERTK, indicating impaired efferocytic capacity. These findings suggest that Msr1 maintains macrophage-mediated apoptotic cell clearance and prevents inflammation caused by debris retention, providing experimental evidence for targeting efferocytosis to control pulp tissue inflammation. However, the specific mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

This study is the first to integrate RNA-Seq and GEO multi-omics data to construct a macrophage-centered efferocytosis regulatory network in hard tissue inflammation. Key hub genes, including MSR1, ABCA1, and LILRB1, were identified, and in vivo validation demonstrated that Msr1 knockdown impairs apoptotic cell clearance and exacerbates inflammation in pulpitis. These findings underscore the crucial role of MSR1 in maintaining the balance between efferocytosis and inflammation, as its deficiency contributes to a feedback loop characterized by suppressed efferocytosis and amplified inflammation.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size of pulpitis tissues analyzed was relatively small and may be subject to selection bias due to regional, age-related, or pathogen-related variability. Second, the study focused specifically on irreversible pulpitis, which differs from reversible pulpitis in terms of its immune microenvironment; thus, future studies should include reversible cases to explore dynamic regulatory patterns of efferocytosis. Furthermore, the precise signaling pathways and interactive networks involving MSR1 remain unclear. Advanced techniques such as phosphoproteomics and co-immunoprecipitation assays will be necessary to investigate these molecular interactions. Additionally, the interplay between MSR1 and immunometabolism, as well as its clinical translational potential, requires deeper exploration. Future research should leverage multi-center clinical cohorts and single-cell spatial transcriptomics to dissect macrophage subset heterogeneity and facilitate precise mapping of the MSR1 regulatory network for potential clinical applications.

Conclusion

This study outlines the molecular landscape of irreversible pulpitis with a focus on macrophage-mediated efferocytosis. The findings highlight MSR1 as a key efferocytic regulator that facilitates the clearance of apoptotic cells and contributes to inflammation resolution. Functional loss of MSR1 led to impaired efferocytosis and aggravated pulp inflammation, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target for preserving pulp vitality through immune modulation.
