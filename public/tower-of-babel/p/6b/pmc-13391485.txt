Design of a fault‑tolerant‑metric‑aware, reversible n‑bit quantum arithmetic logic unit using IBM Qiskit
Authors: Agniswar Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quantum arithmetic and logic units (QALUs) are useful building blocks for reversible arithmetic subroutines, but broad-operation QALUs with explicit status outputs are rarely evaluated in a way that simultaneously emphasizes reversibility, reproducibility, hardware realism, and Clifford + T resource metrics. A reversible n-bit QALU implemented in Qiskit that supports 16 ALU-style operations is presented, including ADD, SUB, CMP, AND/OR/XOR and their negations, unary NOT, shifts, rotates, and operand passthrough, while preserving the input operands, writing the selected result to a separate result register, and emitting N, Z, C, V as well as EQ, LTu, LTs outputs. The implementation uses compile-time operation-specialized circuits rather than a runtime opcode-decoded super circuit, enabling clean verification and per-operation resource accounting. Exhaustive Aer verification at n = 4 covers all 256 input pairs for each of 16 operations (4096 cases total) and reports zero mismatches against a classical reference model. After decomposition to a Clifford + T-style basis, the arithmetic family (ADD, SUB, CMP) forms the dominant cost tier with T-count 86, CX-count 102–106, and depth 138 on the n = 4 instances, whereas logic and data-movement operations fall into lower-cost tiers. Hardware experiments on IBM Quantum are limited to n = 2 and use dynamical decoupling, gate twirling, and post hoc readout mitigation with mthree; the exhaustive 256-circuit campaign yields 91.41% raw correctness and 91.02% mitigated correctness, showing that suppression is useful, but that mitigation does not necessarily improve end-to-end accuracy at this scale. The contribution of the paper is therefore a reproducible Qiskit realization and evaluation framework for a flagged reversible QALU with Clifford + T-aware metrics, rather than a claim of a new asymptotically optimal arithmetic architecture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53845-1.

Introduction

Classical ALUs are multi‑operator units producing results and condition flags used by the control flow of von Neumann machines. Reproducing this capability in a quantum circuit is nontrivial because quantum operations must be reversible/unitary and cannot discard information without measurement1. The reversibility requirement connects directly to Landauer’s principle relating logical irreversibility to thermodynamic cost, and to Bennett’s demonstration that irreversible computations can be embedded reversibly (often at the expense of extra workspace and later “uncomputation”)2–6.

Reversible logic primitives (e.g., Toffoli/Fredkin/Peres‑style constructions) underpin many quantum arithmetic designs, since Toffoli is universal for classical reversible computation and maps cleanly to a quantum gate. Early quantum arithmetic networks for addition and modular arithmetic were provided by Vedral, Barenco, and Ekert, motivating adders as core building blocks for algorithms such as Shor’s7–10.

Ripple‑carry addition remains a central design point because of its linear depth and modest ancilla requirements. The Cuccaro–Draper–Kutin–Moulton (CDKM) “MAJ/UMA” ripple‑carry adder uses 2n-1 Toffoli gates and 5n-3 CNOTs (no incoming carry), with linear depth; variants support incoming carry and comparator‑style “high‑bit only” computations. Importantly, the CDKM paper explicitly notes that the “high bit only” configuration can be adapted into a comparator by subtracting and extracting the high bit10–12.

QFT‑based adders (Draper) avoid explicit carry bits by operating in a Fourier basis, at the cost of controlled phase rotations. Subsequent work surveys and extends QFT arithmetic, including modular and signed variants13–17. However, from a fault‑tolerant perspective, arbitrary rotations typically require compilation to Clifford + T sequences with T‑count scaling as a function of target approximation error (e.g., Ross–Selinger, Kliuchnikov–Maslov–Mosca). This often makes “rotation‑heavy” QFT adders less competitive on strict Clifford + T metrics unless approximations are coarse or dedicated synthesis/approximation is carefully optimized.

Because non‑Clifford operations dominate many fault‑tolerant cost models, modern arithmetic research focuses directly on T gates. Across this literature, a persistent gap is the combination of: (i) a broad ALU‑like function set, (ii) classical‑style flags and compare outputs, (iii) explicit Clifford + T (T‑count) accounting, and (iv) exhaustive correctness verification plus hardware validation with mitigation/suppression—all in a single, reproducible Qiskit implementation. This paper targets that intersection.

Research questions and contributions

The goal of this work is not to propose a new asymptotically optimal adder family, but to study whether a practical ALU-style reversible arithmetic block can be implemented and evaluated in a way that is simultaneously broad in functionality, explicit in status reporting, reproducible in software, and informative under Clifford + T-style resource metrics.

Accordingly, this paper addresses the following research questions.

Literature review

Early work on quantum arithmetic operations established two main paradigms: quantum ripple-carry adders based on reversible gate networks, and quantum Fourier transform (QFT) adders operating via phase rotations. Draper’s 2000 seminal work introduced addition in Fourier space, showing that two n-bit numbers can be added by applying a quantum Fourier transform, then performing controlled phase shifts proportional to the addend, and finally inverse transforming. This approach avoids carrying propagation in a computational basis, allowing addition in linear depth (though with many small rotation gates)21–24. Follow-up research expanded this to subtraction, multiplication and handling of signed numbers using QFT-based techniques. In parallel, reversible logic researchers developed ripple-carry adders using Toffoli (CCX) and CNOT gates that mimic classical binary addition with carries. Cuccaro et al. (2004) presented an n-bit ripple adder requiring only one ancilla (carry qubit) and achieving lower depth than earlier designs. Such adders use a majority and unmajority (MAJ/UMA) gate sequence to propagate carries, and they can be combined with other reversible circuits for a full arithmetic unit25–30.

Building reversible adders, reversible ALUs were proposed to integrate multiple operations. Thomsen, Glück, and Axelsen (2010) designed a reversible ALU that could perform five basic arithmetic/logic functions on two n-bit operands (e.g., addition, bitwise AND, OR XOR, etc.) within a single circuit31. Crucially, their design produced no garbage output and used no extra ancillas, by reusing the existing qubits for multiple purposes in different operations. The authors reported that this optimized ALU can be built with only 6n reversible gates (scaling linearly in bit-width), thanks to a novel V-shape circuit structure originally developed for adders. This result highlighted that in theory, highly efficient quantum-compatible ALUs are attainable; however, the abstract reversible gates in their design (multi-bit-controlled gates) need decomposition to elementary quantum gates when implemented on real hardware32.

Recently, Phillip et al. (2023) began implementing small-scale quantum ALUs on real quantum systems, demonstrated a prototype 2-bit quantum ALU using IBM Qiskit. Their ALU accepted a 2-bit input (with an extra carry bit) and could compute a limited set of functions (NOT, AND, OR, and a 2-bit addition)33. Although limited in width and functionality, this experiment showed that basic ALU operations can be realized on quantum circuits and hinted that a quantum ALU could even surpass a classical one in speed for certain tasks or enable new quantum operations. More advanced, Cakmak et al. (2023) introduced a Quantum Fourier Transform-based qALU and ran it on IBM’s quantum hardware. Their design showed a primitive Arithmetic Logic Unit (ALU) capable of performing two operations: an arithmetic addition and a logical NAND. The operation executed by the ALU is determined by a single control qubit, ∣S⟩. When ∣S⟩=∣0⟩, the Arithmetic logic unit adds n-bits of its inputs, and when ∣S⟩=∣1⟩, a bitwise NAND operation is performed. In principle, if the control qubit is in a superposition of ∣0⟩ and ∣1⟩, the ALU could execute both operations simultaneously in a quantum superposition, demonstrating the concept of quantum-controlled computation34. They implemented both a serial version (reusing a 1-bit adder sequentially) and a parallel version (employing an n-qubit QFT for addition) for up to 4-bit numbers and compared gate counts35. The parallel QFT approach, while using more qubits, reduced the depth of addition by exploiting the QFT’s ability to apply carries through phase rotations. Importantly, their experiments on a real IBM quantum processor indicated the qALU operations could be carried out with a high success probability despite hardware noise. This suggests the building blocks for quantum ALUs are on the right track36,37.

From the available research, it seems that the challenge of merging both arithmetic and logical functions within the same quantum circuit is indeed possible, yet every methodology, as it stands, seems to have its drawbacks. Reversible logic designs aim for minimal overhead (no ancillas, few gates) but can be complex to map to quantum gates38. QFT-based designs handle arithmetic elegantly yet require non-classical operations (rotations) that are susceptible to noise on present devices. Prior demonstrations have also been limited in the variety of operations. This work extends this frontier by constructing a more comprehensive quantum ALU and examining a hybrid approach (dual basis) to leverage the best of both worlds.

Methods and experimental procedure

Design objectives and operation-selection strategy

A central design choice in this work is to implement the QALU as a family of operation-specialized circuits generated at compile time, rather than as a single opcode-decoded reversible super circuit. This choice trades dynamic flexibility for interpretability and resource transparency. In particular, compile-time specialization avoids the large reversible multiplexing overhead that would otherwise be needed to coherently select among many operations inside one circuit.

This choice is appropriate for the present study because the main objective is not to design an instruction-decoded quantum processor, but to evaluate whether an ALU-like reversible building block with explicit status outputs can be implemented, verified, and resource-characterized within a modern Qiskit workflow.

Arithmetic subcircuits: ADD, SUB, and CMP

The arithmetic family is built around a ripple-carry construction consistent with the CDKM majority/unmajority (MAJ/UMA) design philosophy8,19. This choice was made because ripple-carry addition has linear depth, modest ancilla requirements, and a well-understood mapping into Toffoli/CNOT-dominated reversible logic.

For signed arithmetic, overflow is evaluated using the standard two’s-complement definition. The key point is that the arithmetic core provides not only a result register but also the internal information needed to derive ISA-style status outputs.

Logic, shift, rotate, and passthrough subcircuits

The non-arithmetic operations are computed directly into the out-of-place result register R while keeping A and B unchanged. The supported operations are AND, OR XOR, NAND, NOR XNOR, NOTA, SHL, SHR, ROTL, ROTR, PASSA, and PASSB.

These operations use reversible embeddings of the corresponding classical mappings on computational-basis states. Operations such as XOR, shifts, rotates, and operand passthrough are structurally lightweight because they are dominated by CNOT/X-style data movement, whereas AND/OR/NAND/NOR require additional multi-qubit logic and therefore occupy an intermediate cost tier after Clifford + T decomposition.

CMP is therefore an informative operation because it shows the marginal resource cost of explicit compare outputs once subtraction and flag generation are already present.

Resource extraction under Clifford + T-style compilation

To study future fault-tolerant relevance, each operation-specific circuit is transpiled to a Clifford + T-style basis and evaluated using three primary metrics: T-count, CX-count, and depth. In this work, T-count is defined as the total number of t and tdg gates after transpilation.

The compilation basis used for resource extraction is {cx, h, s, sdg, x, z, t, tdg} at optimization level 3, following the Qiskit transpiler workflow21,22. Because Toffoli-rich arithmetic and multi-controlled zero-detection logic dominate the non-Clifford portion of the circuits, these metrics provide a useful first-order view of future logical cost even though the present implementation does not realize full quantum error correction.

Classical reference model and exhaustive verification

Functional correctness is checked against a classical reference implementation for all supported operations. For each ordered input pair (A, B), the corresponding operation-specific QALU circuit is prepared on computational-basis inputs, executed, and measured; the measured result register and status outputs are then compared with the outputs of the classical reference model.

For n = 4, the exhaustive test space contains 22n = 256 ordered input pairs per operation. Across 16 supported operations, this yields 4096 operation-input cases in total. This complete truth-table verification is stronger than random sampling because every admissible input pair in the tested domain is checked.

Aer simulation and IBM hardware protocol

The exhaustive simulator campaign is carried out using Qiskit Aer with the matrix-product-state (MPS) method. This method is suitable for the present study because it allows deterministic simulation of the operation-specialized circuits while remaining compatible with the qubit counts used in the exhaustive campaign.

The IBM Quantum hardware study is restricted to n = 2 because the full QALU includes not only the operand and result registers, but also explicit flag outputs, comparison outputs, and ancilla overhead for arithmetic and zero detection. The hardware goal is therefore not large-n benchmarking, but an end-to-end validation that the same circuit family can be executed on a real backend under practical noise-management settings.

The hardware workflow consists of backend selection through the Qiskit Runtime service using least_busy, transpilation to backend-compatible circuits, exhaustive execution of the n = 2 circuit family, and post-processing with optional readout mitigation. Dynamical decoupling and gate twirling are enabled as noise-suppression techniques through runtime options, while mthree is used for post hoc mitigation of readout errors. Because these are suppression and mitigation methods rather than full quantum error correction, the hardware section should be interpreted as a NISQ-era validation study rather than as a demonstration of fault-tolerant computation.

Resource-cost tiers across operations

The compiled QALU operations separate naturally into three resource tiers. First, the arithmetic family ADD, SUB, and CMP forms the dominant cost tier, with T-count 86, CX-count 102–106, and depth 138 for the n = 4 instances. This is expected because these operations combine ripple-carry arithmetic with explicit status logic and therefore accumulate the largest number of Toffoli-derived non-Clifford layers.

Second, the Boolean family AND, OR, NAND, and NOR forms an intermediate tier with T-count 51 and depth 50–54. These operations do not require full carry propagation, but they still incur non-trivial multi-qubit overhead in the logic network and in the shared status-generation circuitry.

Third, the lightweight family XOR, XNOR, NOTA, SHL, SHR, ROTL, ROTR, PASSA, and PASSB occupies the lowest-cost tier, with T-count 23 and depth 42–43. These circuits are dominated by data movement and simple reversible embeddings rather than carry logic. The resulting tier structure is useful because it shows that the main non-Clifford bottleneck is not the existence of many ALU operations per se, but specifically the combination of carry propagation and shared multi-controlled flag logic.

A second observation is that CMP has the same T-count and depth as ADD/SUB, but only a small CX overhead relative to them. This indicates that once subtraction and flag generation are already present, exposing explicit comparison outputs (EQ, LTu, LTs) adds only modest extra overhead.

Interpretation. The dominant contributors to T‑count are (i) arithmetic Toffoli layers in ADD/SUB/CMP and (ii) the multi‑controlled X used to compute the Z flag. This is consistent with the general observation that Toffoli‑rich logic dominates non‑Clifford cost and motivates low‑T Toffoli/addition research (Jones; Gidney). The hardware study should be interpreted cautiously. The n = 2 experiments demonstrate that the proposed circuit family can be executed end-to-end on real IBM Quantum hardware and that the correct output remains the dominant outcome for most tested cases. However, the results do not show that readout mitigation universally improves performance: in the present campaign the mitigated correctness (91.02%) is slightly below the raw correctness (91.41%). This behavior is plausible in small-scale studies where residual gate errors, calibration drift, finite-shot statistics, and mitigation-model mismatch can offset the benefit of readout correction.

Accordingly, the main conclusion from the hardware section is not that mitigation is always beneficial, but that the QALU provides a useful benchmark for studying the interaction between structured reversible arithmetic circuits and practical NISQ-era suppression/mitigation workflows.

Comparative conclusions

The proposed QALU does not claim global dominance over prior designs on every metric. Ancilla-free reversible ALUs may remain better in qubit economy, while specialized low-T arithmetic circuits may remain better for a narrower arithmetic task. The contribution of the present work is instead the joint combination of a relatively broad ALU-style operation set, explicit NZCV and compare outputs, exhaustive verification in a public Qiskit workflow, and direct Clifford + T-aware metric reporting together with a small-scale hardware study.

Discussion

Novelty and design tradeoffs

The main novelty is system‑level integration under fault‑tolerant metrics: building a practical, verifiable QALU with flags and compare within Qiskit and quantifying its non‑Clifford cost after realistic compilation. Many prior ALU works optimize for reversible “quantum cost” metrics or propose ALU architectures without presenting Clifford + T T‑count data and exhaustive formal verification.

A key tradeoff is qubits vs. T‑count vs. control flexibility. Designs that integrate many operations into a single “opcode‑controlled” circuit may require large reversible multiplexers/control structures, increasing depth and ancilla use. By contrast, this work’s compile‑time operation selection produces separate circuits per operation, which is a natural fit for quantum algorithms that know the operation sequence at compile time. This mirrors how many quantum arithmetic blocks (e.g., adders) are used as subroutines rather than as dynamically opcode‑decoded units.

What the study adds beyond an implementation artifact

The main value of the present study is not the invention of a fundamentally new reversible arithmetic primitive, but the explicit integration of several aspects that are often reported separately: a relatively broad ALU-style function set, classical-style status outputs, exhaustive functional verification, hardware execution, and post-transpilation Clifford + T resource accounting. Framed this way, the QALU serves as a case study in system-level reversible arithmetic design under resource-aware compilation.

The results suggest two broader design lessons. First, the measured operation tiers show that explicit status reporting, especially zero detection, imposes a recurring non-negligible overhead across many operations. Second, the near-equality of ADD, SUB, and CMP costs indicates that once a subtraction-capable flagged arithmetic core is available, richer comparison functionality can be exposed at relatively small marginal cost. These observations are useful for future quantum-architecture work because they identify where optimization effort is likely to matter most: the arithmetic core and the shared multi-controlled flag logic.

Scalability and fault‑tolerant implications

From the CDKM ripple‑carry perspective, arithmetic depth scales linearly with n, and Toffoli count scales linearly as well; thus T‑count scales linearly when Toffoli is decomposed into Clifford + T. However, the literature provides strong evidence that significant constant‑factor improvements are possible:

Adder improvements: Gidney’s temporary logical‑AND yields 4n + O(1) T‑count adders, suggesting that the arithmetic core in this QALU could be swapped out to reduce T‑count asymptotically by ~ 2×.Toffoli improvements: Jones shows four‑T Toffoli constructions and discusses adding controls at 4n T rather than 8n, suggesting potential reductions for both Toffoli and multi‑control flag logic.MCX optimizations: relative‑phase Toffoli identities can reduce T/CX counts for multicontrolled gates, relevant to Z‑flag computation.

Thus, the current QALU should be seen as a platform architecture whose arithmetic and MCX submodules can be upgraded as synthesis improves.

Hardware results and mitigation limitations

The IBM hardware campaign demonstrates that even for small n, circuit families that include arithmetic and multi‑controlled structures can achieve > 90% correctness across exhaustive inputs. However, the lack of improvement under mthree mitigation in this particular run highlights a key limitation: mitigation efficacy depends on stable calibration, appropriate error models, and sufficient statistics; in some regimes mitigation can increase variance or amplify errors.

Noise suppression choices (DD and twirling) are theoretically well‑motivated (Viola–Lloyd; Wallman–Emerson) and exposed in IBM runtime options, but their interaction with circuit scheduling and backend specifics can produce variable outcomes.

Conclusion

This work presented a reversible n-bit QALU in Qiskit that supports a broad ALU-style function set together with explicit NZCV and comparison outputs. The design was verified exhaustively on Aer for n = 4 with zero mismatches over all tested operation-input cases and was executed on IBM Quantum hardware for n = 2 under practical suppression and mitigation settings. Beyond functional validation, the paper reported Clifford + T-aware metrics after transpilation and showed that the supported operations separate into clear arithmetic, Boolean, and lightweight data-movement cost tiers.

The main contribution is therefore a reproducible and resource-aware evaluation framework for a flagged reversible QALU, not a claim of universal optimality over all prior reversible-ALU architectures. In particular, the work clarifies that (i) arithmetic and zero-detection logic dominate the non-Clifford cost, (ii) explicit compare outputs can be added at modest marginal overhead once subtraction and flag logic are present, and (iii) NISQ-era suppression and mitigation techniques provide useful but limited support for hardware execution of structured reversible arithmetic circuits. Future work should replace the current arithmetic and multi-controlled subcircuits with lower-T constructions, broaden the comparison against modern arithmetic blocks, and push the evaluation toward larger logical-scale settings as hardware and synthesis tools improve.
