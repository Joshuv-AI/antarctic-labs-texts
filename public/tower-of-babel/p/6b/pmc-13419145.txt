Association of Health Literacy With Survival in Patients With Lung Cancer
Authors: Patrick Meyers, Palina Woodhouse, Emma Bradley, Stephen Deppen, Aimal Khan, Eric S Lambright, Sunil Kripalani, Kamran Idrees, Eric L Grogan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally, with survival influenced by stage at diagnosis, comorbidities, and sociodemographic factors. Health literacy, which impacts patients’ understanding of medical information and decision-making, has not been well studied in lung cancer survival outcomes.Research QuestionIn a retrospective cohort of patients diagnosed with lung cancer, how is health literacy associated with overall survival and cancer stage at diagnosis?Study Design and MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients diagnosed with lung cancer between 2010 and 2022 at a single institution. Health literacy was measured using the Brief Health Literacy Screen (BHLS). Patients were divided into low health literacy (BHLS score ≤ 9) and intermediate-high health literacy (BHLS score > 9) groups. Data on demographics, clinical characteristics, and treatment were collected. Logistic regression examined associations between health literacy and patient characteristics. Cox proportional hazard models evaluated the impact of health literacy on survival, adjusting for covariates.ResultsThe study included 3,828 patients with lung cancer, and 19.1% (n = 730) had low health literacy. Logistic regression indicated that older age, male sex, non-White race/ethnicity, and lower education were associated with low health literacy. Stage IV disease was associated with increased odds of low health literacy by 65% compared with stage I disease (OR, 1.65; P < .001). Low health literacy was independently associated with a 26% higher hazard of death (hazard ratio, 1.26; P < .001), and adjusted survival curves demonstrated worse overall survival for patients with low health literacy across all lung cancer stages.InterpretationOur results show that low health literacy is associated with late-stage diagnosis and reduced overall survival in patients with lung cancer. Interventions addressing limited health literacy for patients with lung cancer could alleviate these disparities.Key Words: cancer stage, health literacy, lung cancer, overall survival, social determinants of health

Take-Home Points

Study Question: In patients with lung cancer, how is health literacy—measured by the Brief Health Literacy Screen—associated with cancer stage at diagnosis and overall survival?

Results: Patients with low health literacy were significantly more likely to present with advanced-stage disease and had a 26% higher adjusted hazard of death than those with intermediate-high health literacy.

Interpretation: Our results show that health literacy is an important and potentially modifiable factor associated with both stage at presentation and survival in lung cancer, underscoring the value of routine literacy screening to identify patients who may benefit from targeted support across the cancer care continuum.

Lung cancer remains the second most common malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide.1 Despite advancements in early detection and treatment, patient outcomes, such as 5-year survival, remain poor, especially for those diagnosed at advanced stages. Social determinants of health, such as socioeconomic status, rurality, and race, have been shown to significantly impact access to guideline-concordant treatment and overall survival in patients with lung cancer.2 Additionally, patient characteristics like psychological and social risk factors have been identified as potential contributors to disparities in cancer outcomes.3,4

Health literacy, which has been defined as the “degree to which individuals have the capacity to obtain, process, and understand basic health information and services needed to make appropriate health decisions,” has emerged as a key social determinant of cancer-related outcomes.5,6 Approximately one-third of US adults have low health literacy,7,8 which is consistently associated with social determinants of health such as education, economic status, culture, and language.9,10 Limited health literacy among patients with cancer has been linked to delayed diagnoses, a reduced understanding of treatment options, and poor adherence to prescribed therapies.11 Furthermore, health literacy can negatively impact various stages of the cancer care continuum, from understanding and accessing screening services to navigating complex treatment regimens and follow-up care, exacerbating impacts on communication, comprehension, and shared-decision making—all of which are essential for effective oncologic management.12, 13, 14, 15 Although health literacy has been investigated in other malignancies, its association with stage at diagnosis and survival in lung cancer remains poorly characterized.

The purpose of this study is to examine the association between health literacy, as measured by a validated screening measure, the Brief Health Literacy Screen (BHLS), and 5-year overall survival among patients with lung cancer. Furthermore, we aim to determine the association of low health literacy with various patient characteristics including demographics, comorbidity status, and lung cancer stage and treatment. Given that low health literacy has been identified as a critical barrier to effective cancer care, understanding this relationship could help inform interventions designed to enhance patient education, communication, and engagement across the cancer care continuum—including screening, treatment, and surveillance.

Study Design and Methods

Study Design

This study is a retrospective cohort analysis conducted at a single tertiary-care academic institution and approved by the Vanderbilt University Medical Center institutional review board (No. 240455). Data were extracted from the electronic health records (EHRs) for patients diagnosed with lung cancer from 2010 to 2022. Health literacy was assessed as part of routine clinical care using the BHLS,16 a validated 3-item questionnaire measuring patients’ confidence with medical forms and difficulty understanding or reading health materials.17,18 The BHLS correlates strongly with established measures of functional health literacy, including the Short Test of Functional Health Literacy in Adults (S-TOFHLA) and Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine (REALM),17, 18, 19 and has been applied successfully in oncology settings, where brief health literacy screens predict treatment comprehension, adherence, and patient-reported outcomes.11,14,19

The BHLS was integrated into institutional workflows beginning in 2010 (following an earlier pilot), enabling near-complete capture in the population, with only 194 patients (4.8%) missing BHLS scores. Comparative analyses showed that patients with missing BHLS did not differ meaningfully in early mortality or major clinical characteristics (e-Table 1), minimizing concern for survivor bias, and were therefore excluded. Because BHLS administration occurs pragmatically during outpatient encounters, timing was not synchronized with the date of cancer diagnosis. Some patients had multiple assessments, whereas others had only 1. Consistent with evidence that adult health literacy is generally stable over time with gradual decline only in advanced age,6 we used each patient’s earliest available BHLS score.

BHLS responses (e-Appendix 1) were scored on a 5-point scale (range, 3-15), with higher scores indicating better health literacy. In accordance with prior validation work, patients were characterized as having low health literacy (BHLS score ≤ 9) and intermediate-high health literacy (BHLS score > 9).19 The primary outcome was overall survival, defined from cancer diagnosis to death, with censoring at last clinical encounter; the secondary outcome was cancer stage at diagnosis.

Study Population

All patients with diagnosed lung cancer irrespective of histology during the study period who had available BHLS scores were included. Patients without documented BHLS scores and with carcinoma in situ were excluded. A Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials diagram including study population details is included in Figure 1. Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, smoking status, education, insurance, cancer stage and type, treatment modalities obtained (surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy), and year of diagnosis, were extracted from the EHR. Insurance categories were defined as nonpublic insurance (private insurance, Veterans Health Administration Insurance, Tricare, and insurance not otherwise specified), Medicaid, Medicare, uninsured, and unknown (if no insurance status could be ascertained). Clinical and pathologic stages were reviewed and used for analysis. We deferred to pathologic stage when both clinical and pathologic stage were available. The Elixhauser Comorbidity Index was used to assess the burden of comorbidities within the cohort and includes important risk factors associated with lung cancer outcomes such as COPD and AIDS/HIV.20

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical characteristics between the 2 health literacy groups. Continuous variables (eg, age, pack-years) were reported as medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Covariate selection and model structure were guided by a directed acyclic graph to distinguish confounding from mediated pathways between health literacy and cancer outcomes (e-Fig 1). Logistic regression assessed associations between health literacy group (low vs intermediate-high) and covariates including age, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance status, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, cancer stage and histologic type, smoking status (currently smokes vs does not smoke), pack-years, and years of completed education. Temporal changes in staging, screening availability, and treatment were adjusted for by the inclusion of a linear year of diagnosis variable.

Model performance for the logistic regression model was evaluated using discrimination (C-index), calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow test and calibration plot), influence diagnostics (DFBETA), and overall fit. Full diagnostics descriptives are provided in e-Appendix 2, with the outputs provided in e-Table 2 and e-Figure 2.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to visualize overall survival differences by health literacy level and cancer stage, as calculated from the date of diagnosis to date of last contact in the EHR or death. Cox proportional hazards models estimated the association between health literacy and overall survival, adjusting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, comorbidity burden (Elixhauser Comorbidity Index), smoking status, pack-years, insurance status, cancer stage and type, and year of diagnosis. Education level was excluded from Cox modeling due to > 15% missingness. Because pack-years demonstrated a nonlinear association with lung cancer outcomes, a 5-knot restricted cubic spline of pack-years was incorporated into both logistic and Cox models. Effect modification by cancer stage was assessed by including a combined BHLS and stage interaction term in the Cox model and evaluating significance with a joint Wald test, demonstrating no evidence of interaction. Adjusted survival curves were generated from the Cox model.

Collinearity among covariates was evaluated using variance inflation factors without spline basis terms, so as not to introduce expected internal collinearity. All variance inflation factors were low (mean variance inflation factor, 1.06), indicating no meaningful collinearity (e-Table 3). Missingness was minimal (stage, 4.3%; year, 0.3%; education, 15.9%; all other variables complete); therefore, complete case analysis was used without imputation for all models. Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Shoenfeld residuals, with the assumption holding for health literacy. Full diagnostic description and analysis are included in e-Appendix 3 and e-Tables 4-5. Additionally, to differentiate the total association of health literacy with survival from effects mediated by stage and subsequent treatment, we estimated sequential Cox models adjusted first for preexposure covariates alone, then a model with stage, and finally a model with treatment added as an exploratory sensitivity analysis further described in e-Appendix 4.

After evaluating the study population, a subset analysis was performed on all patients who underwent surgery. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 18, StataCorp, 2025, with statistical significance defined as P < .05.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

The study included 3,828 patients with lung cancer, and 19.1% (n = 730) had low health literacy. The demographic characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. The median age at diagnosis was 66 years (interquartile range, 59-72) for the overall cohort, which was similar across both health literacy groups. Most patients in the low health literacy group (58.1%) and the intermediate-high group (51.4%) were male. Most were White patients, making up 83.2% of the low health literacy group and 89.1% of the intermediate-high health literacy group. Other racial and ethnic groups were represented at lower frequencies, with Black patients accounting for 11.6% and 8.7%, respectively, in the low and intermediate-high health literacy groups.

Factors Associated With Low Health Literacy

Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with low health literacy (BHLS score ≤ 9), with results summarized in Table 2. In the model, several factors emerged as significant associations to health literacy. Older age was associated with increased odds of low health literacy, with an OR of 1.02 per year increase (95% CI, 1.01-1.03; P < .001). Male sex was also associated, with male individuals having 28% higher odds of low health literacy than female individuals (OR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.05-1.55; P = .014). Lower educational attainment was significantly associated with low health literacy, with each additional year of education reducing the odds by 14% (OR, 0.86 per year increase; 95% CI, 0.83-0.89; P < .001), as was race/ethnicity with Black patients (OR, 1.47; 95% CI, 1.08-2.00; P = .016) and Hispanic patients (OR, 3.65; 95% CI, 1.61-8.26; P = .002) having higher odds of low health literacy compared with White patients. Additionally, insurance status was associated with health literacy, with the highest odds of low health literacy occurring among the uninsured compared with the nonpublic insurance group (OR, 2.71; 95% CI, 1.73-4.25; P ≤ .001). Patients diagnosed with stage IV lung cancer were 65% more likely to have low health literacy than those diagnosed at stage I (OR, 1.65; P < .001). The logistic regression model indicated no significant association between comorbidity burden (measured by the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index), lung cancer histology, smoking status, or smoking pack-years and health literacy.

Survival Analysis

Unadjusted Kaplan-Meier survival curves are included in Figure 2, depicting overall survival from the point of diagnosis of lung cancer, with low and intermediate-high health literacy groups stratified by stage. Across all stages, overall survival is higher at 5 years in the intermediate-high health literacy group, with differences in the point of differentiation between stages. For later stage disease, the 2 groups diverge at earlier time points, with stage 4 disease diverging between 6 and 12 months after diagnosis. Nonoverlapping confidence bands suggest separation in survival between groups, particularly in stage IV disease. These findings were consistent when the analysis was restricted to only patients who underwent surgical resection.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves display overall survival over 60 mo from the time of cancer diagnosis stratified by health literacy group (low vs intermediate-high) for each cancer stage (I, II, III, and IV). Each graph shows separate curves for patients with low health literacy (Brief Health Literacy Screen [BHLS] score ≤ 9) and high health literacy (BHLS score > 9), with 95% CIs (shaded areas) around the curves. Across all stages, patients with low health literacy demonstrate lower overall 5-y survival than those with high health literacy.

Cox proportional hazards modeling was performed to estimate the impact of health literacy on overall survival, adjusting for covariates such as age, race, sex, education level, cancer stage, and comorbidity burden. This demonstrated a significantly increased hazard of death in the low health literacy group compared with those with intermediate-high health literacy (hazard ratio [HR], 1.26; P < .001). In addition, advanced cancer stage, increasing age, and higher comorbidity burden (Elixhauser Comorbidity Index) were associated with a higher hazard of death and worse overall survival. Male sex was associated with a higher hazard of death than female sex (HR, 1.23; P < .001). Medicaid (HR, 1.24; P = .020), uninsured (HR, 1.34; P = .005), and unknown (HR, 1.65; P = .039) groups were also associated with a higher hazard of death compared with the nonpublic insurance group, whereas analysis of cancer histology demonstrated that small cell lung cancer (HR, 1.39; P ≤ .001) and lung cancer not otherwise specified (HR, 1.84; P ≤ .001) were also associated with a higher hazard of death than non-small cell lung cancer. Pack-years demonstrated a nonlinear effect on overall survival. These results are described in Table 3. There was no evidence of effect modification by stage (joint test of BHLS × stage interaction, χ23= 1.58, P = .664).

To better understand how health literacy influences survival, we estimated 3 sequential Cox models. A model (model A) adjusting only for preexposure covariates demonstrated a stronger association between lower health literacy and the hazard of death (HR, 1.39), reflecting the overall adjusted relationship. After including stage at diagnosis (model B)—our hypothesized mediator, and the model reflecting all variables in our full model in Table 3—the association attenuated but remained significant (HR, 1.26), consistent with partial mediation through later-stage presentation. In an exploratory model (model C) further adjusting for treatment receipt if ever received (surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation), the association attenuated further (HR, 1.18), suggesting that treatment engagement represents a downstream pathway and was thus not included in our final model. Discussion

In this study, we sought to assess the association between health literacy, as measured by the BHLS, and lung cancer outcomes in a large cohort of patients from a single academic institution. Our premise was that the BHLS functions as an easily deployed clinical tool that might capture patients at risk for poor clinical outcomes independent of traditional clinical risk factors, with the hypothesis that low health literacy scores on that tool are associated with worse overall 5-year survival among patients with lung cancer. The study findings indicate that low health literacy is prevalent in this tertiary care hospital population and is associated with significantly worse overall survival in a lung cancer cohort. Specifically, patients with low health literacy were more likely to present with advanced-stage disease and demonstrated decreased overall 5-year survival, even after adjusting for key demographic and clinical covariates, including age, sex, race, insurance status, cancer histology and stage, and a composite comorbidities score.

In our stage-adjusted model, lower health literacy was associated with a 26% higher hazard of death compared with intermediate-high health literacy. This finding aligns with previous studies that have shown a strong relationship between health literacy and outcomes in chronic diseases, including cancer. For example, limited health literacy has been consistently linked to worse outcomes, across a range of malignancies, likely due to reduced patient engagement, misunderstanding of treatment protocols, and lower adherence to follow-up care.11

Another striking finding was the strong association between low health literacy and advanced-stage diagnosis, with stage IV disease being 65% more likely to have low health literacy than stage I disease. Similar disparities have been observed nationally. One study found that despite a population-level shift toward earlier-stage detection after low-dose CT screening implementation, non-White and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups continued to present more often with stage IV disease.21 Our results indicate that limited health literacy may function as a parallel and modifiable contributor to delayed diagnosis. Early stage diagnosis is 1 of the most critical determinants of survival in lung cancer, and these findings underscore the need for interventions that specifically target health literacy as a modifiable risk factor associated with stage. We also found that low health literacy was more common among certain demographic groups, including older adults, male patients, non-White individuals, the uninsured, and those with lower educational attainment. These findings mirror national data, which suggest that individuals with these characteristics are more likely to experience difficulties in comprehending health information.22 Although health literacy correlates with several sociodemographic factors, collinearity diagnostics demonstrated no significant multicollinearity among included covariates.

Although we have not enumerated the causes of an association between health literacy and overall survival outcomes in this study, we have demonstrated that the BHLS can be deployed to capture patients at risk for poorer outcomes, like 5-year overall survival. Given our institutions high capture rate of low health literacy (19.1%) over a decade, this tool has proven useful for screening patients early in the cancer care continuum to ascertain patients that may be at higher risk and therefore require additional support. Future research will be needed to discover the underlying causes of worse survival outcomes that might be captured by health literacy screening.

Implications for Clinical Practice

Our findings suggest that routine screening for health literacy using tools like the BHLS should be considered in lung cancer care. Identifying patients with low health literacy early could enable targeted interventions, such as simplified communication, patient education, and frequent follow-up, which have been shown to improve treatment adherence and understanding of early detection’s importance.23 One potentially fruitful target is lung cancer screening. Individuals with limited health literacy may face barriers to understanding eligibility, navigating referrals, or completing low-dose CT screening, thereby increasing the likelihood of later-stage presentation. Integrating BHLS results into primary care workflows could help identify patients who may benefit from enhanced navigation or tailored educational materials to improve screening uptake. Furthermore, evidence-based strategies, including simplified language, visual aids, and teach-back methods, can enhance patient engagement and understanding and be used in this population.24,25 Incorporating health educators or patient navigators into care teams could address gaps in understanding and shared decision-making by guiding patients through complex systems and ensuring timely access to needed services.

This study adds to a growing body of evidence linking health literacy to health outcomes, particularly in oncology. Previous studies have demonstrated health literacy as an important factor associated with patient outcomes and patient-reported quality of care in cancers such as breast and colorectal cancer, where treatment adherence and understanding of complex treatment regimens are crucial.14 However, studies focusing on lung cancer have been relatively sparse, despite the fact that lung cancer presents unique challenges due to the complexity of its treatment and the need for careful monitoring of disease progression. Our study is among the first to comprehensively evaluate the impact of health literacy on lung cancer outcomes and reinforces the importance of addressing health literacy in this high-risk population.

Study Limitations

Although our study provides important insights, it is not without limitations. First, our data were derived from a single academic institution, limiting generalizability, particularly to more diverse populations or resource-limited environments. Second, the BHLS assessment was obtained as part of routine clinical workflow, rather than standardized relative to cancer diagnosis. Although adult health literacy is considered generally stable over the period described in this study, the lack of synchronized measurements induces variability, which may impact identified associations. However, sensitivity analyses comparing patients with and without BHLS documentation demonstrated no differences in stage distribution, early mortality, or major clinical characteristics, suggesting the risk of survivor bias, if present, is minimal (e-Fig 4, e-Table 1). Additionally, the BHLS captures only certain aspects of this complex construct. Health literacy is a multidimensional concept that encompasses not just the ability to read and comprehend health information, but also numeracy skills and health care system navigation. Future studies should consider using more comprehensive tools to capture the full scope of health literacy.

Another limitation is the retrospective nature of our study, which may be subject to residual confounding despite our use of multivariable adjustments. For example, we were unable to account for all possible social determinants of health, such as income level or access to health care facilities, which may have influenced both health literacy and lung cancer outcomes. We were also unable to capture variation in lung cancer screening patterns, surveillance adherence, or clinic presentation/no-show rates, which could contribute to differences in outcomes, particularly among early stage disease. Finally, we did not capture primary language or English proficiency, precluding assessment of potential misclassification of health literacy among patients whose first language is not English. Further work incorporating language understanding might provide more nuance to an understanding of health literacy and outcomes.

Interpretation

This study highlights the critical role that health literacy plays in shaping lung cancer outcomes. Low health literacy is associated with more advanced-stage diagnosis and worse overall 5-year survival, independent of other clinical and demographic factors. Addressing health literacy in clinical practice represents a key opportunity to improve patient outcomes, particularly in vulnerable populations. Interventions aimed at improving patient understanding, engagement, and navigation through the health care system are urgently needed to reduce disparities in lung cancer care and improve survival outcomes.
