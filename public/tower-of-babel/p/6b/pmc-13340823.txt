Serotype distribution of Streptococcus pneumoniae in children with invasive diseases in Türkiye, 2019–2025: An update in the 10th to 15th years of routine pneumococcal conjugate vaccination
Authors: Mehmet Ceyhan, Fatma Nur Öz, Melike Emiroğlu, Ufuk Hasdemir, Adem Karbuz, Nisel Yılmaz, Asuman Birinci, Yasemin Ay Altıntop, Betil Özhak Baysan, Cüneyt Özakın, Esra Özkaya, Murat Telli, Soner Sertan Kara, Akgün Yaman, Eda Karadağ Öncel, Ener Çağrı Dinleyici, Gülşen Hazırolan, İlker Devrim, Sabire Şöhret Aydemir, Deniz Bahar Akgün Karapınar, Hakan Uslu, Lütfiye Öksüz, Yasemin Zer, Candan Öztürk, Özlem Koyuncu Özyurt, Yelda Sorguç, Nezahat Akpolat, Özgen Alpay Özbek, Yücel Duman, Duygu Öcal, Faruk Aydın, Hüseyin Güdücüoğlu, Zeynep Çizmeci, Gülşen Hasçelik

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInvasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae remains a significant cause of pediatric morbidity. This study updates the serotype distribution and antibiotic resistance of pediatric IPD in Türkiye for 2019–2025, about a decade after 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13) was introduced in the National Immunization Program. We conducted a multicenter, hospital-based prospective surveillance between January 2019–April 2025 in children <18 y across 28 tertiary hospitals. S. pneumoniae isolates from sterile sites (blood, cerebrospinal fluid, pleural fluid) were confirmed by standard methods and serotyped using the Quellung reaction. Antimicrobial susceptibility was determined by gradient test as per CLSI criteria. A total of 203 samples were identified from 203 pediatric cases (median age 4 y, 56.2% boys). The clinical presentations were bacteremia/sepsis (57.6%) and meningitis (26.1%). The leading serotypes were 3 (14.3%), 14 (10.3%), 19F (7.9%), 19A (6.4%), 9N (4.4%), 15A (4.4%). PCV13 serotypes accounted for 46.8% isolates, while PCV7 and PCV10 serotypes were detected in 23.2% and 24.6%, respectively. Broader vaccines would increase coverage: PCV15 (46.8%) and PCV20 (56.7%). Antibiotic susceptibility (non-meningitis breakpoint) remained high for beta-lactams; 91.9% of isolates were penicillin-susceptible, with only 0.7% fully resistant, and cefotaxime resistance was rare (2.7%). Conversely, macrolide resistance was high, with 60.2% of isolates resistant to erythromycin. A decade after PCV13 implementation, vaccine serotypes – particularly 3, 14, 19F, and 19A – still cause nearly half of pediatric IPD, though non-vaccine serotypes such as 9N and 15A are rising. Higher-valent PCVs may improve protection, and continued surveillance is critical to guide vaccine policy and treatment strategies.KEYWORDS: Streptococcus pneumoniae, surveillance, invasive pneumococcal disease, children, serotype distribution, pneumococcal conjugate vaccines, vaccine coverage, antimicrobial resistance, Türkiye

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus) is a leading cause of serious invasive infections in children worldwide, including meningitis, sepsis, and pneumonia. Before widespread pneumococcal vaccination, an estimated 1.6 million deaths annually were caused by pneumococcal disease, with young children bearing a large burden.1 The introduction of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) has dramatically reduced invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) incidence due to vaccine-covered serotypes in many countries.2 In 2009, Türkiye incorporated PCV7 into its National Immunization Program (NIP) (4 doses at 2, 4, 6, and 12 months of age), and in November 2011 the program had changed to PCV13 based on local seroepidemiology.3 PCV13 includes serotypes 1, 3, 5, 6A, 7F, 19A in addition to those in PCV7 and PCV10, providing broader protection.4 Routine infant PCV immunization quickly led to declines in IPD caused by the original vaccine serotypes (e.g. 6B, 14, 19F) and overall childhood IPD in Türkiye.5 Following the introduction of the PCV-7 vaccine, serotype replacement has been observed at multiple sites around the world, with non-vaccine serotypes – including 1, 3, 5, 7F, and 19A – emerged to fill the ecological niches.6 Global and regional surveillances have documented the increasing prevalence of non-PCV13 serotypes such as 12F, 24F, 15B/C, 22F, and others in the post-PCV13 era.7,8 In Europe, for example, serotypes 12F and 24F have emerged as predominant causes of pediatric IPD in some settings that previously used PCV10, while serogroup 15 (15B/C) has surged in countries using PCV13.7,8

Türkiye’s own experience reflects these trends. The first years of our multicenter surveillance (2008–2014) showed that IPD cases caused by vaccine serotypes declined over time after vaccine introduction, while the proportion of non-vaccine serotypes – including 2, 8, 10, 15, 15C, and 23A – increased significantly (from 27.2% in 2008–2010 to 37.6% in 2011–2014, p = .045).1 A follow-up study in 2015–2018 found that PCV13 serotypes still accounted for 56.2% of pediatric IPD, with serotypes 19F, 1, and 3 being most common.5 This indicated that despite high vaccination coverage and reduced overall IPD incidence in young children, vaccine-serotypes (notably 19F and 3) continued to cause disease, and some previously rare serotypes (such as 10A, 11A, 12F, and 15B) had become more prominent.5 Meanwhile, emerging non-vaccine serotypes began to cause more cases. These shifts underscore the need for ongoing surveillance to inform vaccine strategy.5

Another critical aspect is antibiotic resistance in pneumococci. Historically, Türkiye had high rates of penicillin-nonsusceptible S. pneumoniae (PNSP) and macrolide resistance in pneumococcal isolates.9 Encouragingly, the widespread use of PCVs targeting key drug-resistant serotypes (such as 6B, 9 V, 14, 19A, 19F) has been associated with reduced penicillin resistance.10 In Türkiye, the proportion of penicillin-non-susceptible pediatric isolates fell from 33.7% in the pre-PCV7 era to 16.5% in 2011–2014 (post-PCV13).5 However, macrolide (erythromycin) resistance continued to be a concern; a recent study in Türkiye reported 42% macrolide resistance among invasive pneumococcal isolates even after PCV13 introduction.9 This may reflect continued circulation of resistant clones (e.g. those carrying ermB or mefA genes) in both vaccine serotypes and emerging non-vaccine serotypes. Surveillance of antibiotic susceptibility trends is therefore crucial for guiding empiric therapy of IPD in children.

To address serotype replacement, next-generation PCVs have been developed. A 15-valent PCV (PCV15, containing PCV13 serotypes plus 22F and 33F) and a 20-valent PCV (PCV20, adding 7 additional serotypes beyond PCV13: 8, 10A, 11A, 12F, 15B, 22F, 33F) have recently been licensed in most of the countries.11 PCV20 was approved for pediatric use in several countries after demonstrating non-inferior immunogenicity to PCV13 for shared serotypes and robust responses to the new serotypes.12,13 In Türkiye, PCV20 became available in 2023,9 but it has not yet replaced PCV13 in the NIP as of 2025. It is crucial to evaluate how much additional coverage could be provided by these expanded-serotype vaccines in Türkiye, and whether vaccine serotypes still comprise a significant fraction of disease. Such data can inform decisions on if or when to transition to a higher-valent PCV.

In this study, we present updated results from the Turkish nationwide pediatric pneumococcal surveillance for the years 2019–2025, focusing on serotype distribution, vaccine coverage, and antibiotic resistance patterns. We compare these findings to earlier periods to illustrate trends in serotype replacement and resistance. Our goal is to provide evidence on whether PCV13 remains effective in covering circulating strains and to assess the potential impact of introducing PCV15 and PCV20, thereby guiding immunization policy and clinical management of IPD.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study was conducted using data from an ongoing, national, multicenter, hospital-based surveillance study of IPD in children across Türkiye, which was initiated in 2008.1 The present study covers the period from January 2019 through April 2025. Pediatric patients (≤18 y old) presenting to participating hospitals with invasive S. pneumoniae infections whose cultures were positive for S. pneumoniae during routine laboratory testing were included in the study.

A network of 28 tertiary hospitals from all seven geographical regions of Türkiye contributed to the surveillance, similar to prior phases.1 These centers include university hospitals and children’s hospitals that serve a substantial proportion of the national population (approximately 60–65%) through tertiary care services.1 The study period (January 2019–April 2025) corresponds to an era of routine infant PCV13 immunization in Türkiye, before any switch to higher-valent PCVs. The study was approved by the Hacettepe University Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2019/17‒24, dated: 10.10.2019). Informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians for the use of clinical isolates and data.

Laboratory methods

Microbiological analyses were conducted at the Microbiology Laboratory of Hacettepe University Faculty of Medicine (Ankara, Türkiye) and Microbiology Laboratory of HelixLab Health Services Research & Development Inc. (Ankara, Türkiye).

Cases in which S. pneumoniae was isolated and identified from sterile body fluids – such as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), blood, or pleural fluid – were considered eligible. Repeated isolates obtained from the same patient were not included in the study. The isolates included in the study were confirmed as S. pneumoniae using alpha-hemolysis, Gram staining, optochin susceptibility, and lysis by sodium deoxycholate. Serogrouping was carried out using latex particle agglutination, while serotyping was conducted via the conventional Quellung reaction with commercial pool, group, type, and factor antisera (Statens Serum Institut, Copenhagen, Denmark). S. pneumoniae isolates were tested for susceptibility to penicillin, cefotaxime, and erythromycin using gradient test (E-test, AB Biodisk, Sweden), with results evaluated according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI-2024) guidelines.14 According to CLSI-2024 minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) breakpoints for parenteral penicillin were ≤2 µg/mL susceptible, 4 µg/mL intermediate, ≥8 µg/mL resistant for non-meningitis specimens, and ≤.06 µg/mL susceptible, ≥.12 µg/mL resistant for meningitis specimens. MIC breakpoints for cefotaxime were ≤1 µg/mL susceptible, 2 µg/mL intermediate, ≥4 µg/mL resistant for non-meningitis specimens and ≤.5 µg/mL susceptible, 1 µg/mL intermediate, ≥2 µg/mL resistant for meningitis specimens.14 Penicillin and cefotaxime susceptibilities were explicitly interpreted for the strains isolated from either CSF or other specimens.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using the PASW 18.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as numbers and percentages for categorical variables, and as mean, standard deviation, median, 25th percentile (Q1), 75th percentile (Q3), minimum, and maximum for numerical variables. Serotypes were additionally grouped according to vaccine coverage (7-valent, 10-valent, 13-valent, 15-valent, and 20-valent), and the derived coverage variables were analyzed as categorical variables. Similarly, antibiotic susceptibility results were analyzed as numerical variables and were also categorized according to guideline-based (CLSI14) classifications for categorical analysis. No comparative statistical analyses were performed; only descriptive results were provided. The findings are presented in tables and graphs.

Results

From January 2019 through April 2025, a total of 203 culture-proven IPD cases in children were identified in the participating centers. The annual case counts were 51 (25.1%) in 2019, 5 (2.5%) in 2020, 28 (13.8%) in 2021, 52 (25.6%) in 2022, 37 (18.2%) in 2023, 25 (12.3%), in 2024, and 5 (2.5%) in 2025. Over this surveillance period, 59.1% of cases (120/203) have been diagnosed in children aged 0–5 y, and 40.9% (83/203) in ages of 6–18 y. The overall median age was 4.0 y (Q1–Q3: 2–8 y), mean 5 ± 5 y (range, 0–17 y). Of the cases, 114 (56.2%) were male and 89 (43.8%) were female (male to female ratio = 1.28:1).

Of the 203 isolated S. pneumoniae, the majority were obtained from blood (116; 57.1%), followed by cerebrospinal fluid (53; 26.1%) and pleural fluid (14; 6.9%). Additional isolates were obtained from mastoid (9; 4.4%), ear (4; 2.0%), peritoneal fluid (3; 1.5%), vitreous fluid (2; 1.0%), joint fluid (1; 0.5%), and catheter (1; 0.5%). Discussion

This study provides an updated overview of the epidemiology of invasive S. pneumoniae in Turkish children in the late PCV13 era (January 2019–April 2025), extending our previous surveillance reports.1,5 The findings highlight both encouraging progress and ongoing challenges in pneumococcal disease prevention. In this study period, clinical patterns are similar to those reported previously, where bacteremia/sepsis was the most frequent IPD, followed by meningitis and pleuritis.1,5 Notably, the proportion of meningitis in our 2019–2025 series (26.1%) is slightly lower than that in 2015–2018 (31.1%).5

Serotype distribution of S. pneumoniae

In the present study, serotype 3 was the most prevalent, which caused 29 of the 203 cases (14.3%). Serotype 3 was especially dominant among young children; accounting for 24 of the 120 cases (20%) in the 0–5-y age group. This serotype is covered by PCV13; however, it continues to cause IPD. Serotype 14 (a PCV7 serotype) accounted for 8.3% of cases in the 0–5-y age group and 13.3% in the 6–18-y age group. Although we observed a notably high frequency of serotype 14 in our cohort, this finding contrasts with previous years and global data, where serotype 14 has generally declined following widespread PCV implementation. Despite high vaccination rates, persistence of serotype 14 – especially among older children – is difficult to explain; moreover, there is currently no direct evidence of local expansion or unique transmission dynamics. Further molecular epidemiological studies are needed to clarify whether such factors contribute to the continued circulation of serotype 14 in Türkiye and to better understand the impact of vaccine coverage rates on its distribution. The next leading serotype was 19F (another PCV7 serotype), which was observed at a higher frequency in older children (10.8%) than in under-5 y (5.8%), possibly reflecting residual circulation in cohorts born before widespread PCV or with waning immunity. Serotype 19A – which increased in many countries following widespread PCV7 vaccination and is included in PCV13 – was identified in 13 cases (6.4%). In our previous surveillance reports, the frequency of serotype 19A was 4.8% during the 2008–2014 period and 4.2% during the 2015–2018 period.1,5 Besides these, several non-PCV13 serotypes have emerged among the causes of IPD; with serotypes 9N and 15A accounting for nine cases (4.4%) each, respectively. Other notable serotypes in our series- each fond in ≤3% of isolates – included: 23F (n = 5), 23B (n = 6), 8 (n = 5), 15B (n = 6), 23A (n = 4), 11A (n = 5), and 7F (n = 2). We observed serotype 1 only in one case (0.5%) – a dramatic decrease compared to 2015–2018 when serotype 1 comprised 10.8% of cases.5 This likely reflects successful immunization, as serotype 1 is included in PCV13 and tends to disappear if vaccination coverage is high. Serotype 5 and 7F (included in PCV13) were also very rare, with only two cases of 7F and none of serotype 5.

Impact of vaccination and serotype replacement

A decade after PCV13 was included NIP, its target serotypes still account for nearly half of pediatric IPD cases in Türkiye (46.8% in 2019–2025). Serotype coverage of PCV13 remains substantial – notably including three of the top four serotypes causing disease (3, 14, 19F and 19A) – underscoring that vaccine-type disease has not been eliminated. According to our findings, serotypes 3 and 19A together accounted for 20.7% of cases in the current period (2019–2025), compared to 10.7% in 2008–2014 and 14.4% in 2015–2018, as reported in our previous studies.1,5 The current vaccine (PCV13) still matches nearly half of the circulating invasive strains in children, with similar coverage observed in older (6–18 y) and younger (0–5 y) age groups; 44.6% and 48.3%, respectively. By comparison, the less valent vaccines cover fewer isolates now; PCV7 serotypes covered only 23.2% of cases, and PCV10 serotypes 24.6%. PCV15 which adds 22F and 33F to PCV13 was calculated to cover 46.8% of cases, identical to PCV13 in our dataset. The additional serotypes in PCV20 that contributed to coverage were mainly 8, 10A, 11A, 12F, and 15B. In our series, serotype 8 (2.5%) and 11A (2.5%) are important contributors to PCV20 coverage. Serotype 12F was rare (0.5%), and 15B caused 3.0% of cases – the latter is included in PCV20. PCV20 vaccine will increase serotype coverage to 56.7%. In the present study, the additional PCV20 serotypes (8, 10A, 11A, 12F, 15B) accounted for 9.9% (20/203) of all identified serotypes.

Besides the increasing frequency of non-vaccine serotypes, vaccine serotypes also continue to be a problem for children in Türkiye despite routine and high-rate PCV13 vaccination. Several factors may contribute to these results. Serotype 3, the leading cause of IPD in this study, is included in PCV13 but is known to exhibit an incomplete vaccine effect; PCV13’s efficacy against serotype 3 disease is lower than other serotypes, as seen in multiple countries where serotype 3 has persisted or even increased post-PCV13.15,16 In our previously published studies, serotype 3 was also among the frequently observed serotypes (5.9% in 2008–2014 and 10.1% in 2015–2018),1,5 and in the present study, it was observed to persist with an increasing frequency (14.3% in 2019–2025). Hypotheses for the persistence of serotype 3 include high persistence carrier rates, its unique capsule biology (polysaccharide shedding) and clonal lineage dynamics. Because the capsule of serotype 3 is not covalently linked to peptidoglycan, it may be released, enabling evasion of antibody-mediated defense.15,16 Likewise, serotype 19F and 14 continue to cause IPD, possibly due to residual circulation in older unvaccinated children or high carriage prevalence. The presence of these vaccine types in breakthroughs underscores the importance of maintaining high vaccination coverage and possibly enhancing vaccine formulations or schedules (e.g. booster doses) to improve herd immunity.

Our data also demonstrate significant serotype replacement. Non-PCV13 serotypes now cause more than half (53.2%) of IPD in children. We observed serotypes like 9N and 15A among the top causes of IPD. Serotype 15A is particularly noteworthy; it belongs to the same serogroup as 15B/C but is not covered by current vaccines (PCV20 covers 15B but not 15A). Neither 9N nor 15A is included in PCV13, PCV15, and PCV20. This increase in serogroup 15 (particularly 15A) reflects the results of reports from Europe, where serotypes 15B/C have also increased in the post-PCV13 period.8 Our findings suggest Türkiye follow this trend with 15A, too. Other expanding serotypes in our series –23B, 23A, 8, 11A– reflect those seen in Europe and elsewhere after PCV13 rollout.7,17–20 Serotype 8, for instance, became the most common IPD serotype in adults in many European countries by the late 2010s.17 While serotype 8 still causes only 2.5% of pediatric cases in Türkiye, it must be taken account because of its invasive potential.17 In this context, PCV20 vaccine is important, as it includes serotype 8. Serotype coverages of PCV7 and PCV10 were 23.2% and 24.6%, respectively. These low values reflect the disappearance of serotypes 4, 18C and some others due to vaccination and replacement by serotypes not covered by PCVs. In our study, the virtual disappearance of serotype 1 by 2019–2025 (only one case) is an achievement likely attributable to vaccination. Serotype 1 was a significant cause of pediatric IPD (especially empyema) in the pre-vaccine era,5 but inclusion of serotype 1 in PCV10/13 has led to near elimination of this historically virulent serotype in many countries, including Türkiye. This outcome demonstrates how effective conjugate vaccines can be when a serotype is fully covered and the vaccine is widely administered.

None of our isolates were positive for serotype 22F or 33F, which are more commonly associated with adult IPD and certain geographic regions.17 Therefore, during this period PCV15 would have offered no additional advantage over PCV13 for pediatric IPD in Türkiye. In contrast, PCV20 (adding seven further serotypes beyond PCV13) would have covered 56.7% of cases (115/203). Thus, PCV20 could provide an estimated 10% additional coverage for IPD beyond that of PCV13, primarily by covering serotype 8 and 15B, which seem to be emerging globally.7,17

Our findings support the view that sustained surveillance remains essential. Shifts in serotype distribution can occur due to various reasons – vaccine implementation, antimicrobial use, and natural secular trends. The significant increase in non-PCV13 IPD from 37% (2011–2014) to 53.2% now confirms that serotype replacement has accelerated in the post-PCV13 decade.1 A comparable situation has been reported worldwide; for example, in the United States by 2018, only an estimated 30% of pediatric IPD cases were caused by PCV13 serotypes, while the eight most common non-vaccine serotypes accounted for 42% of cases.21 Western Europe has noted the emergence of serotypes like 24F and 12F as major pediatric pathogens after extensive use of PCV13.7 This may be due to the regional differences in circulating clones. However, serogroup 24 and some others could emerge in the future. Carriage studies may also aid in estimating the future serotype distribution of IPDs.

Implications for vaccine policy

Currently, PCV13 is still providing coverage for a significant proportion of IPD isolates in Turkish children, which is a strong argument for its continued use until a broader vaccine is replaced. Our analysis suggests that replacing PCV13 with the PCV15 (if considered) would yield no additional benefit for IPD prevention in Türkiye at this time – since none of our pediatric isolates were serotype 22F or 33F, which the additional serotypes covered by PCV15. This finding may reflect that serotypes 22F and 33F in Türkiye predominantly affect adults, as they have been noted in adult IPD surveillance22 and are less frequently associated with IPD in children. On the other hand, adopting PCV20 in the pediatric NIP could prevent an estimated additional 10% of IPD cases, increasing serotype coverage from 46.8% to 56.7%. Specifically, PCV20 would cover serotype 8 and 15B, which together caused about 4–5% of cases in our study, as well as some others (10A, 11A, 12F) that contributed smaller proportions. Although modest, the added benefit targets some serotypes with high potential impact, for instance, serotype 8, which is highly invasive and has been associated with outbreaks in some populations.17 Notably, PCV20 does not cover serotypes 15A and 9N, so that emerging serotypes would remain uncovered. Although PPV23 could theoretically cover more serotypes (61.6% according to data from this study), it is not suitable for routine use in young children due to poor immunogenicity in age <2 and lack of mucosal immunity/conjugation.23

Given the above, one could argue that PCV13 is still performing well in terms of covering the most pathogenic serotypes (our data show serotype 3, 14, 19A, 19F remain most common serotypes, all are covered by PCV13). Yet, the rising non-PCV13 fraction is concerning. Türkiye and other countries will need to discuss the cost-benefit of switching to PCV20. Although our data suggest that approximately 40% of IPD cases will still be attributable to serotypes not covered by PCV20 (including 9N, 15A, 23A, 23B, etc.), the transition to PCV20 will gradually increase serotype coverage. Future vaccines may need to include even more serotypes or utilize proteins common to all pneumococci to provide broader protection.24

Serotype replacement may also influence disease severity and clinical presentation. For instance, serotype 3 has been associated with complicated bacteremia and empyema.3,25,26 If serotype 3 remains common, IPD severity may not diminish with current vaccines. Emerging serotype 8 is known for causing increasing incidence of severe bacteremic pneumonia in adults and it spreads in children. On the other hand, some non-vaccine serotypes like 23B are less invasive (often colonizing rather than causing disease). Continuous monitoring will reveal whether the “new” serotypes are as virulent as the ones we’ve controlled.

Antibiotic resistance trends

Our results show a mixed but generally positive picture on antibiotic resistance trends. Studies have shown a decrease in penicillin resistance after PCV use,1,27–30 and our findings are consistent with this. Nevertheless, resistance rates have been reported to vary by region and over time, underscoring the need for close monitoring.27–30 Most pneumococcal isolates remain susceptible to third-generation cephalosporins aside from meningitis breakpoints.31,32 We had only two cefotaxime-resistant isolates (3.8% resistance), indicating cefotaxime is still a reliable empiric choice for severe pediatric infections. In the present study, 60% of invasive isolates were resistant to erythromycin. This has actually increased relative to some earlier local studies (16.1%–42%).33,34 This trend may be due to historically high macrolide use and the persistence of PCV13 serotypes like 14 and 19F (frequently macrolide-resistant) in Türkiye. Even in the post-vaccine era, serotype 14 remains among the top serotypes and is known to commonly carry macrolide resistance determinants.35 Our data underscore that macrolides would be unreliable for treating IPD in Turkish children. Interestingly, some studies have debated whether pneumococcal vaccination itself influences resistance trends independently of serotype changes.9 By removing certain serotypes, vaccines might make room for others that could be more or less resistant. In our study, the net effect has been beneficial for penicillin susceptibility.

Limitations

This study is descriptive in nature and did not aim to measure vaccine effectiveness or collect individual vaccination histories; its primary objective was to identify the serotypes causing disease in children during a defined period in the country. Notably, the surveillance covers a large proportion of pediatric IPD cases in Türkiye, but not all. Changes in serotype distribution observed during the COVID-19 pandemic period may be partially attributable to the pandemic’s influence on transmission dynamics and healthcare-seeking behavior. The effects of the pandemic may have influenced serotype distribution and annual trends, particularly the 2020–2021 data. This also makes interpreting trends throughout the entire period more difficult. Additionally, pneumococcal PCR-positive, culture-negative cases were not included in this analysis, as serotyping using the Quellung reaction as well as antibiogram was performed only on culture-positive isolates. Inclusion of only culture-positive cases may have introduced bias in serotype representation. The lack of data on vaccination status represents a limitation in assessing vaccine failure versus non-vaccination. The cost-effectiveness of PCV vaccines was not calculated in this study because reliable data to perform this calculation is not available. Nevertheless, the majority of the data being derived from confirmed cases from different regions of Türkiye and covering a long period are strengths of the present study. We believe that the data on serotype distribution and antibiotic resistance will contribute to the literature.

Conclusions

The introduction of PCVs in Türkiye has markedly changed the serotype distribution of pediatric IPDs. Vaccine serotypes (especially PCV7 types) have been successfully suppressed, but they were not disappeared. Serotype 3 and others are still causing an important number of cases, indicating a need for developing new vaccine strategies or formulations. Meanwhile, non-vaccine serotypes are an increasing source of IPD, emphasizing that higher-valent vaccines (like PCV20) are logical next steps to broaden protection. Our data provide a baseline expectation that PCV20 could modestly increase coverage of circulating strains, although serotype replacement will likely continue with types out of vaccine coverage (e.g. 15A). With regard to antimicrobial sensitivity, the declining penicillin resistance is a positive outcome possibly attributable to vaccine-driven shifts. However, macrolide resistance in IPD isolates has reached very high levels in Türkiye during the post-PCV era.

Ongoing surveillance is essential for guiding both immunization policy and empirical treatment. Data from 2019 to 2025 report will assist Turkish health authorities to develop new PCV policies and to give importance for maintaining high coverage with existing vaccines.
