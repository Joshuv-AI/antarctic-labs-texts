Humanistic care ability and its influencing factors among Chinese surgical nurses
Authors: Xiao‐Ju Teng, Miao‐Miao Yan, X U Yan‐Qun, L I Yuan‐Zheng, Xiu‐Bin Tao, Wei‐Zhen Zou, Xiao‐Ming Wang, Wenru Wang, Ying Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis paper aims to investigate the humanistic care ability among surgical nurses in China and identify its statistically significant influencing factors.DesignA cross‐sectional, descriptive and correlational design was used.MethodsA total of 210 surgical nurses were recruited from a tertiary public hospital in Wuhu, Anhui Province. Four with incomplete responses were excluded, leaving 206 participants for data analysis. Variables were measured using the general questionnaire, caring ability inventory (CAI), and General Self‐Efficacy Scale (GSES). Multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to identify the statistically significant influencing factors of participants' humanistic caring ability.ResultsThe overall average score of the CAI was 190.14 (SD = 19.24), and the average scores for the three dimensions of cognitive, courage, and patience were 70.46 (SD = 7.77), 62.12 (SD = 8.47) and 57.56 (SD = 6.02), respectively. The overall average score of the GSES was 26.05 (SD = 5.77). Professional attitude, perceived care from the department head nurse, perceived care from colleagues and self‐efficacy were the statistically significant factors influencing the humanistic care ability of surgical nurses (R 2 = 0.45, adjusted R 2 = 0.44, F = 40.64, p < 0.001).Keywords: China, GSES, humanistic care ability, influencing factor, public hospital, surgical nurse

INTRODUCTION

Nursing is a caring profession in which nurses play an active part in healing individuals, families and communities (Reed, 2006). Through therapeutic interaction, the nursing process is executed. Nurses play many roles. When a nurse attempts to comprehend a patient's problem, the conditions necessary to resolve it are beyond their technical expertise. In contrast, the patient requires not only advanced diagnostic and treatment technologies but also spiritual and psychological solace (Wang, 2020). The holistic and patient‐centered approach, with an emphasis on the individual rather than the disease, as the focus, has been advocated in the implementation of patient care (Moudatsou et al., 2020; Qian et al., 2018). In parallel, the Chinese Ministry of Health launched the ‘Quality Care Demonstration’ project in 2010 to call for integrating humanistic care into routine nursing care (Ma et al., 2013). Therefore, humanity care is one of the pivotal elements and core competencies in nursing practice.

Within the context of the therapeutic partnership between nurse and patient, humanistic care is the ability of the nurse to listen to the patient's wants and desires, comprehend their feelings and appreciate the value of their lives (Rogers, 1981). Humanistic care is vital in promoting patients' health and recovery and preserving their dignity (Smebye & Kirkevold, 2013). Surgical patients face a multitude of physical and psychological challenges, such as pain, fatigue, anxiety, hopelessness and depression. Studies have shown that most patients experience anxiety and apprehension before surgery, and postoperative discomfort and complications can lead to statistically significant psychological strain. (Pinto et al., 2016). Humanistic care in surgical nursing can play a crucial role in improving patient satisfaction and reducing patient‐provider disputes. By prioritizing compassion, respect and understanding of the patient's unique needs, humanistic care can help alleviate preoperative anxiety and improve post‐operative recovery (Khadaroo et al., 2015).

However, there are relatively few humanistic care curriculums in nursing education and less humanistic care training on the job in China, making it hard for Chinese public hospital nurses to advance their humanistic care practice (Cheng et al., 2017). In addition, with an aging population, the demand for healthcare is increasing, yet healthcare resources are stretched, and nurses may focus primarily on treatment tasks in an intense work environment at the expense of patient interactions. Consequently, patients often face healthcare staff who lack empathy and have a negative attitude (Radwin et al., 2019).

Existing research shows that Chinese nurses have varying levels of humanistic care ability, with some nurses scoring lower than nurses in Western countries (Deng et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2022). Demographic factors, including gender, age, marital status and previous work experience (Dong et al., 2023), along with social, economic and cultural factors, such as workforce shortages, increased workloads and the preponderance of medicine in the healthcare system, have been reported to affect the caring attitude and the level of humanistic care ability among the Chinese nurses (Jiang et al., 2015). Furthermore, quality care delivery is influenced by workplace culture, wherein elements including positive role models, empathy and communication skills are identified as crucial components as well (Cheng et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2013; Wang, 2020).

Self‐efficacy refers to one's conviction that they are capable of managing daily challenges and adapting to stressful situations (Schwarzer et al., 1997). In their study, Bu et al. (2022) found a positive relationship between organizational climate and humanistic practice competence, and self‐efficacy mediates this relationship. Similarly, Cheng et al. (2020) reported that self‐efficacy is one of the most critical factors influencing nurses' quality of clinical practices. Self‐efficacy affects the nursing practice environment and perceived organizational support (Cheng et al., 2020). Studies elsewhere reported that greater self‐efficacy boosts student nurses' confidence in providing patient care in complex clinical situations (McConville & Lane, 2006; Pike & O'Donnell, 2010). Therefore, self‐efficacy is another potential factor affecting a nurse's humanistic care ability in clinical practice.

Although various factors related to the ability of nurses to provide humanistic care have been identified in previous studies, few have thoroughly investigated the current status of humanistic care abilities among surgical nurses and the factors that influence them. Given the call for humanistic care in China's current healthcare system, the results of our study could aid in enhancing the humanistic care ability among Chinese public hospital nurses and improving the quality of patient care. Therefore, the aims of the study were to (1) investigate the level of humanistic care ability among surgical nurses in a Chinese public hospital and (2) identify the statistically significant factors associated with better humanistic care. Specifically, we explored the impact of self‐efficacy, demographic variables (such as age, education level and length of employment), and workplace factors (such as perceived care from the head nurses or colleagues) on their humanistic care ability.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

This study was conducted in a tertiary public hospital in Wuhu, a prefecture‐level city in Southeastern Anhui Province, China, with a population of around 3.6 million. A cross‐sectional, descriptive and correlational design was used. The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Medical Ethics Committee of the tertiary teaching hospital (Ethical Committee of REDACTED).

Participants

Power analysis was not performed to determine the sample size. Between August and November 2020, a total of 210 participants were recruited from the surgical department of the study hospital using convenience sampling. However, of the 210 questionnaires returned, 206 were valid, with no missing data. Therefore, the final data analysis was based on 206 participants. Participants were eligible for the study if they met the inclusion criteria: (1) had formal employment for at least 8 months, (2) had professional nursing credentials and (3) consented to participate and signed an informed consent form. They were excluded if they were: (1) on internship, (2) nurses coming to the department for advanced training or (3) rotating nurses.

Outcome measures

A general questionnaire

A general questionnaire was designed by the research team. The participants' sociodemographic characteristics, such as age, gender, marital status, the highest level of education, length of employment and professional rank, were collected in the first section.

The second section included the participant's professional attitude and the practice environment. Participants were asked to rank their attitudes toward the nursing profession on a scale of ‘dislike’, ‘neutral’ and ‘like’. The practice environment was evaluated based on the perceived level of care from their department head nurse and their colleagues, with one representing ‘very little’ to five representing ‘very much’. The higher the score, the greater the level of perceived care. In addition, participants were asked whether the nurse–patient conflict was present in their practice environment.

Caring ability inventory

Humanistic care ability was measured by the Chinese version of the caring ability inventory (CAI) (Nkongho, 2019; Xu, 2008). The CAI is one of the most widely used scales for measuring humanistic caring abilities. The scale had three dimensions: a cognitive dimension (14 items), a courage dimension (13 items) and a patience dimension (10 items). Each item was assessed on a 7‐point Likert scale, with one being ‘completely opposed’ and seven being ‘fully agree’. The higher the scores, the better the humanistic care ability. The scale has been validated in many countries and showed good psychometric properties (Rosanelli et al., 2016; Xu, 2008). The Chinese version of CAI has an excellent internal consistency with a Cronbach's α of 0.84 (Xu, 2008). The Cronbach's α of the CAI in this study is 0.887.

General Self‐Efficacy Scale

Participants' self‐efficacy was measured by the General Self‐Efficacy Scale (GSES) (Schwarzer & Jerusalem, 1995). The scale was developed to assess participants' general feelings of self‐efficacy with the aim of predicting an individual's ability to deal with daily difficulties and adjust to stressful events in life. It has been translated into different languages, including Chinese (Schwarzer et al., 1997; Zhang & Schwarzer, 1995). Participants were required to rate each item on a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (incorrect) to 4 (completely correct). The overall GSES score was the sum of all the items, ranging between 10 and 40. A higher score indicated better self‐efficacy (Zhang & Schwarzer, 1995). The scale has been used to measure the general self‐efficacy of nurses and showed good internal consistency with a Cronbach's α of 0.87 (Wang et al., 2001). The Cronbach's α of the GSES in this study is 0.924.

Data collection procedure

Potential participants were informed of the purpose and objective of the study, they were assured that their personal information would be kept confidential, and their participation was completely voluntary. The participant's written consent was obtained if they agreed to participate in the study. The questionnaire was distributed to eligible participants via a web‐based platform that could be accessed by computer or mobile device. The participants were instructed to complete the questionnaire independently. Their responses to the questionnaire were recorded anonymously. Each participant took approximately 7–8 min to complete the questionnaire.

Data analyses

Data were analysed by SPSS statistics version 25.0. The sociodemographic characteristics of the sample and outcome variables were described using descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation (SD) and range. The dependent variable was the humanistic care ability as measured by the CAI total score. Univariate regression analysis was first conducted to screen for possible factors associated with CAI total score. The statistically significant factors identified in the univariate analysis were inputted as the independent variables (IVs) in the multivariate linear regression analysis. The backward selection method was used to select the final model. Statistically significant levels were set at a p‐value of less than 0.05.

DISCUSSION

The current study investigated the level of humanistic care ability among surgical nurses in a Chinese public hospital and explored self‐efficacy and the demographic and workplace factors associated with better humanistic care. Our results indicated that self‐efficacy, professional attitude and care from the department head nurses and colleagues predicted humanistic care abilities.

In this study, our participants' mean CAI score was well below the threshold for good humanistic care ability. Correspondingly, the mean scores for the three dimensions, cognitive, courage and patience, were also very low. These results indicated that surgical nurses at our hospital lacked the necessary humanistic care ability, even though most hold a bachelor's degree or higher. Consistent with our findings, earlier studies have also reported poor humanistic care abilities among nurses in tertiary and community hospitals in China (Liu et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2021). Low humanistic care ability among Chinese nurses may reflect a gap in traditional nursing education and training in China, which continues to emphasize clinical skills and technical knowledge under the influence of the medical model, while humanistic care training receives less attention and is often treated as an ‘add‐on’ (Chen et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). In addition, the heavy workload in clinical practices further hinders the nurses' ability to communicate effectively with their patients (Chang et al., 2017). While these obstacles may be difficult to overcome in a short period, we explored the potential influencing factors of our nurses' caring abilities in the surgical setting.

In the univariate analysis, we found that the humanistic care ability of surgical nurses increased significantly with the increasing year of work. Guo and Qiu (2013) reported that nursing students with work experience had better care abilities than those who had never worked as a nurse before. The accumulation of clinical practice experience enhances humanistic care skills, particularly the cognitive and patient dimensions (Li et al., 2020). As their experience grows, nurses may develop a deeper understanding of the profession and the limitations of medical treatment, hence a greater appreciation of the importance of humanistic care beyond routine procedures.

We found that surgical nurses with a favourable view of the nursing profession are better at providing humanistic care, suggesting that a positive professional outlook is a prerequisite for humanistic care. Compared to Western countries, the societal status of the nursing profession in China remains low (Chen et al., 2021). Due to poor income, tremendous pressure and lower social standing, the profession is frequently viewed as undesirable (Chen et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2017). Nurses might not receive the respect they deserve from their patients or medical peers. Those with lower job satisfaction and a lower inclination to pursue nursing as a career had a lower sense of belonging and commitment to their work (Lyden et al., 2018; Shang et al., 2014). Nevertheless, evidence from the literature suggests that the perception of professional identity might evolve over time (Mao et al., 2021). A robust and sound career development structure and positive role models could sustain nurses' positive attitudes about their profession (Wang et al., 2022).

Furthermore, the results of our study also showed that humanistic care ability was positively correlated to the perceived care from the head nurse and colleagues. A head nurse's care can be viewed as a positive example of management's role modelling. It develops a culture of care inside the institution. Care and colleague support are essential sources of social support and peer cohesion. The personal experience of being cared for also fosters a sense of caring and compassion (Warshawski et al., 2018).

As predicted, our study also found a positive correlation between humanistic care ability and self‐efficacy. Self‐efficacy refers to a person's belief in his or her abilities and capacities to complete tasks successfully despite obstacles (Bandura et al., 1999). Similar to our finding, Xu et al. (2021) found that the caring efficacy of nurses affects their overall caring ability. Higher‐efficacy nurses can internalize their caring knowledge, use their communication skills more effectively to build relationships with patients, and demonstrate remarkable courage and patience when exhibiting caring behaviours (Xu et al., 2021). Previous research has demonstrated that simulation training can increase nurses' self‐efficacy in their ability and expertise to provide care (Hegland et al., 2017). Therefore, while considering in‐service training, nursing management may use simulation training to enhance the humanistic care competency of nurses.

In the multivariate regression analysis, professional attitude, perceived care from the head nurse and colleagues and self‐efficacy remained statistically significant, highlighting the significance of these factors. They were the most influential determinants of the humanistic care abilities of our surgical nurses. The departmental clinical nurse assessment system needs to incorporate the evaluation of humanistic care ability as part of performance evaluation. At the same time, well‐planned humanistic care training as a complementary component of continuing medical education programs and a focus on mentoring and modelling junior nurses by senior nurses in clinical practice are some strategies that could be employed to build a caring culture in the hospital.

Limitations

The strength of the study lies in its systematic approach to investigating the current status of humanistic care abilities among surgical nurses. It also analysed the various factors that influence these abilities. Nevertheless, our study had some limitations. First, the study was conducted only in a single hospital, and a formal power analysis was not performed prior to determining the sample size. Sampling is through convenient sampling and the sample size was relatively small. Therefore, the generalization of our findings has to be exercised with caution. Second, the study was conducted as a self‐assessment, which may have biased our results. And lastly, the provision of humanistic care is shaped by a variety of factors that operate on multiple levels, including those related to society, economics, culture and the workplace. However, the factors we have examined are not exhaustive, and future studies should seek to replicate our investigation by including more comprehensive sets of factors and expanding the scope of the research to multiple sites and larger sample sizes, as supported by power analysis.

CONCLUSION

Our study indicates that the humanistic care ability of surgical nurses in Chinese public hospitals was low. Professional attitude, perceived care from the department head nurse and colleagues, and the score of the GSES were the main determinants of the humanistic care abilities of our surgical nurses. Systemic initiatives that encompass broad‐scale changes to improve the culture of hospitals, policies and performance appraisal procedures of the departments and hospitals, along with providing education and training to enhance humanistic care abilities among surgical nurses, are necessary. A coordinated effort at multiple levels is required to ensure that surgical nurses have the skills and support they need to provide the best possible care to their patients. Therefore, the involvement of the department and hospital administration in implementing these initiatives is essential.
