Tissue sample taken by transbronchial biopsy in the diagnosis of tuberculosis; benefits-risks
Authors: Bilal Rabahoğlu, Nuri Tutar, Burcu Baran, Nur A. Yetkin, Fatma S. Oymak, İnci Gülmez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Tuberculosis (TB) poses a serious health risk in Turkey and globally and is often difficult to diagnose. It may be sometimes necessary to obtain bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and, in some cases, biopsy samples via bronchoscopy to acquire an adequate sample. Our aim is to assess the contribution of transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) alongside BAL in diagnosing TB. In addition, we will evaluate the risk of pneumothorax associated with TBLB and determine the overall risk–benefit ratio.Methods:Flexible fiberoptic bronchoscopy (FFB) reports performed for suspected TB between March 2011 and July 2018 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients who had both BAL and tissue samples taken via TBLB were included in the study. Of the 606 patients included, age, sex, the lung area where the biopsy was taken, BAL and biopsy AFB and culture results, and complications such as pneumothorax and chest tube application were recorded.Results:A total of 606 patients were included in the study. Of these, 391 patients were male (64.5%) and 215 were female (35.5%). A total of 37 (6.1%) patients had a positive culture for TB. Nineteen (59.4%) patients were positive on both BAL and tissue culture, while 5 patients were only positive on tissue culture. Pneumothorax developed in 34 patients (5.6%), 28 of whom required a chest tube.Conclusion:Using various modalities such as BAL and TBLB together for diagnosing pulmonary TB can be advantageous when appropriate, particularly given the absence of significant complications during the procedure. Our findings indicate that incorporating TBLB alongside BAL impacted the diagnosis of pulmonary TB.

INTRODUCTION

Although the incidence of tuberculosis (TB) is decreasing worldwide, it remains one of the most important causes of infection-related morbidity and mortality. In 2021, as reported by the World Health Organization (WHO), about 10.6 million people were infected, and 1.6 million deaths were caused by TB, making it one of the top ten causes of death.[1] Owing to its impact on public health, WHO developed a strategy named ‘End TB’ to help eliminate TB. According to this strategy, it is projected that TB can be eliminated by 2050 if new cases are prevented (through vaccination and treatment of latent TB cases), and if early diagnosis and proper treatment are implemented.[2]

Generally, the gold standards for diagnosing pulmonary TB are sputum smear and culture. Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smears are primarily used for quick and early diagnosis of TB. Despite having a high specificity (98–99%), AFB smears have low sensitivity (30–70%). The sensitivity yield of mycobacterial cultures is higher (80–85%) compared with AFB smears but may require a 3–8 week period to obtain results. Owing to certain difficulties (such as low bacterial yield or improper/lacking sputum), obtaining a diagnosis from sputum is not always possible.[3] Therefore, delaying the diagnosis of TB may lead to progression and public dissemination. As a next step, depending on local facilities and expertise, bronchoscopy may be considered a diagnostic tool for smear-negative TB. Using bronchoscopy may provide additional advantages, such as directly evaluating the lungs and obtaining suitable samples, such as lavage, bronchial washings, and biopsies if needed.[4]

In general, flexible fiberoptic bronchoscopy (FFB) is considered a safe procedure that may be performed for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes on both inpatients and outpatients. However, complications such as bronchospasm, bleeding, and pneumothorax may occur due to diagnostic sampling. According to studies, the rate of pneumothorax varies between 1 and 6%, whereas the risk of major bleeding (defined as >100 mL) varies between 0.12 and 7.5%.[5]

In WHO’s Global TB 2020 Report, Turkey’s estimated incidence rate for 2019 was 16/100,000, and the estimated mortality rate was 0.39/100,000. The estimated incidence rate in the Global TB 2021 Report was 15/100,000, and the estimated mortality rate was 0.55/100,000. In the city of Kayseri, where the study was conducted, 106 cases were diagnosed in the year 2020 alone.[6]

The objective of this study was to determine whether obtaining both bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and biopsy samples via transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) at the same time is necessary for the diagnosis of TB, considering the risk of pneumothorax due to the biopsy.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

This study was a retrospective analysis of all patients suspected of pulmonary TB due to symptoms and/or radiologic findings who were unable to provide sputum. It was conducted in the Erciyes University Medical Faculty Chest Diseases Department on patients who underwent FFB between March 2011 and July 2018. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of our institution (2024/143).

Patients older than 18 years who underwent FFB and from whom both BAL and TBLB samples were obtained were included in the study. Patients who underwent FFB for reasons apart from suspicion of TB, those receiving TB treatment, patients who were sputum smear positive for AFB, and pregnant patients were excluded.

All medical records of the patients in the specified time period were retrospectively reviewed and documented. We documented the patients from whom we obtained both BAL and TBLB samples. Demographics and the lung lobe from which both BAL and TBLB samples were obtained were recorded. In addition, after the biopsy was performed, the prevalence of pneumothorax was investigated after the procedure.

FFB was performed by a certified pulmonology specialist using local anesthesia (lidocaine 2%) and conscious sedation with fentanyl (25–50 mcg) and midazolam (1–5 mg). BAL and TBLB were directed by the radiological findings to the subsegmental bronchus. No local anesthesia (lidocaine 2%) was administered into the lung after passing the vocal cords to avoid affecting the specimens collected. To obtain BAL, a total of 100 mL of isotonic saline was delivered through the working channel port and collected into a trap using suction. Obtaining 30% of the BAL administered was considered adequate for analysis. We performed TBLB by passing the biopsy forceps through the working channel port and advancing them to the targeted subsegmental bronchus. After the biopsy was obtained, it was placed in isotonic saline for analysis. Since the gold standard for the diagnosis of TB is via culture, we sent the biopsy to the microbiology laboratory to culture the specimen for a definitive diagnosis. All samples were sent to the same microbiology laboratory in our institution. BAL and TBLB were examined for AFB smear by Ziehl-Neelsen staining, and for culture, they were inoculated on both liquid BACTEC and solid Lowenstein-Jensen media. The definitive (gold standard) diagnosis of TB was made based on BAL and/or biopsy culture positivity. By accepting BAL and biopsy culture as the gold standard for TB, we then calculated the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy accordingly.

Statistical analysis

We used SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA, version 22.0 for statistical analysis. The distribution of continuous variables was tested with the one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to determine whether the data were parametric. The homogeneity of variances was tested with Levene’s test. Descriptive statistics were used for demographics and other clinical features of the patients. Categorical variables were analyzed with the Chi-square test and reported as frequencies and group percentages. Frequency and percentage were computed to determine sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy using the standard formulas.

RESULTS

A total of 606 patient medical records were investigated from March 2011 through July 2018, from whom both BAL and TBLB samples were obtained. These patients were suspected to have pulmonary TB due to symptoms and/or radiologic findings [Figure 1a and b]. Of these patients, 391 (64.5%) were male and 215 (35.5%) were female. Their average age was 53.3 years (min: 18, max: 87). Out of these patients, 59 (9.7%) had at least one of the tests positive, either for AFB from BAL or biopsy smear, or from BAL or biopsy culture. A total of 37 (6.1%) patients had a positive culture for TB and were accepted as active tuberculosis patients and treated accordingly. Nineteen (3.1%) of these patients had a positive culture from both BAL and biopsy at the same time, whereas 13 (2.1%) were positive only from the BAL culture but negative on the biopsy culture, and five (0.82%) were positive only on the biopsy culture and negative on the BAL culture [Table 1]. The lobes from which TBLB samples were taken were as follows: 163 (26.9%) from the right upper lobe, 58 (9.6%) from the right middle lobe, 133 (21.9%) from the right lower lobe, 135 (22.3%) from the left upper lobe, and 117 (19.3%) from the left lower lobe [Table 2]. Pneumothorax developed in 34 (5.6%) patients, 28 of whom required a chest tube, while six recovered with oxygen therapy alone. Major bleeding (>100 mL) was observed in ten (1.7%) patients but was controlled during the procedure with local measures, and no surgical intervention was needed. No mortality was recorded among all patients included in the study.

DISCUSSION

As multiple studies have shown, especially in sputum-absent patients, FFB can be very helpful in providing alternative respiratory specimens for the diagnosis of TB; moreover, FFB is considered a cost-effective, safe, and simple procedure.[78] Some international studies have shown the diagnostic value of FFB in smear-negative or sputum-scarce patients to be variable, ranging from 47% to 90%. This wide variation may be explained by the different specimens taken (biopsy, culture, smear, and PCR), studied populations and study designs. Although FFB may be operator-dependent and the bacteriostatic effect of local anesthesia may lower the diagnostic value, it remains the single most effective procedure in sputum-negative patients.[4]

It is well known that not all patients with pulmonary TB will show AFB in their sputum smears. About 40–60% of patients with suspected pulmonary TB, whether due to clinical symptoms or radiological findings, may be sputum-scarce. Although sputum cultures have a higher sensitivity rate (80–85%) compared with sputum smears (30–70%), they may require a 3–8 week period to reach the clinician. Since controlling TB prompts early diagnosis, other diagnostic modalities, such as FFB, can be used.[9] Olteanu et al. concluded that combining TBLB with BAL offers the most comprehensive diagnostic approach for TB in patients with strong clinical and radiological suspicion, especially when three sputum AFB tests return negative or the patient is unable to provide a good quality sputum sample. They also noted that TBLB confirms about 17–60% of active TB cases and serves as an effective tool for quickly confirming a diagnosis in cases of smear-negative miliary TB with a diagnostic success rate ranging from 60% to 80%.[10] In a retrospective study conducted by Caymmi et al.,[11] it was concluded that when different modalities such as culture, biopsy, and Ziehl staining were used to diagnose TB, the diagnostic yield was about 50% when used alone. However, when these modalities were combined (direct examination, culture, and TBLB), the diagnostic sensitivity rose to about 80%. In our study, the BAL and biopsy cultures had sensitivities of 86.49% and 64.86%, respectively. These results highlight the importance of different diagnostic modalities (BAL and TBLB) in the diagnosis of pulmonary TB when no contraindications to the procedure are present.

Generally, after FFB is performed and TBLB is obtained, it is sent to pathology to check for granulomas.[1213] In this study, however, we chose to focus on the microbiological aspects of the biopsy because microbiological culture is considered the gold standard for diagnosing TB. Several studies have shown varying results when culturing biopsy specimens; however, it is believed that culturing the biopsy provides limited value even if lung tissue is successfully obtained.[314] Since it is known that local anesthetics inhibit the growth of respiratory pathogens, no local anesthesia was administered into the lower respiratory tract after passing the vocal cords in our study.[15] Out of the total 37 patients who had a positive culture, 24 (64.9%) were biopsy culture positive, and 32 (86.5%) were BAL culture positive. Nineteen (51.4%) of the total TB culture-positive patients were positive for both BAL and biopsy cultures at the same time, while five (13.5%) patients were positive only for biopsy culture. BAL remains a standard diagnostic tool for pulmonary tuberculosis TB, particularly in patients with sputum-negative or sputum-scarce disease. However, BAL alone may fail to detect all cases. In our study, 13.5% of culture-confirmed TB patients would have been missed if only BAL had been used. This underscores the incremental diagnostic value of adding TBLB.

FFB is generally considered a safe procedure, but complications such as pneumothorax and bleeding may occur more frequently than others. Of the 606 patients included in our study, pneumothorax developed in 34 (5.6%) and major bleeding in ten (1.7%) of the patients. Leiten et al.[5] showed in their study that the risk of pneumothorax varies between 1% and 6%, while the risk of major bleeding ranges from 0.12% to 7.5%. Jacomelli et al. and Caymmi et al.[1113] reported the risks to be 3% and 5%, respectively. In another retrospective study involving 26,895 patients who underwent biopsies via FFB, nearly half (41.37%) experienced varying amounts of bleeding, while massive bleeding was calculated at 1.47%.[16] The risks of pneumothorax and major bleeding in our study were consistent with those reported in other studies on this matter. Importantly, none of the complications in our study led to mortality or permanent disability, and all were managed with standard clinical measures, supporting the overall safety of the procedure when performed in experienced centers. While BAL remains the first-line diagnostic tool in sputum-negative TB, our findings and current literature support adding TBLB when BAL is inconclusive, especially in patients with strong clinical or radiologic suspicion, atypical presentations and high pre-test probability of TB. In our cohort, 13.5% of patients with positive TB cultures would have been missed if BAL alone had been used, supporting the adjunctive use of TBLB despite a 5.6% risk of pneumothorax. The added yield—demonstrated in both our study and external literature—may justify the minor but real increase in risk.

We faced several limitations in our study, such as its retrospective design and the relatively low number of patients who tested positive for pulmonary TB. In addition, the absence of pathological results may have further clarified the findings when combined with our specimens. Although the biopsy location was recorded at the lobar level, detailed segmental distribution of TBLB samples was not available in some patients in our retrospective data which may have added clarity to the study.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, using different modalities, such as BAL and TBLB simultaneously, to diagnose pulmonary TB may be beneficial when indicated, especially since no major complications were observed during the procedure. We found that adding TBLB to BAL influenced the diagnosis of pulmonary TB. Prospective and multicenter studies may be necessary to fully understand the role of TBLB in conjunction with BAL.

Key messages

To this day diagnosing TB may difficult. The combined use of different diagnostic modalities, such as BAL and TBLB, may be advantageous in the evaluation of suspected pulmonary TB, particularly when clinically indicated, as the procedures are not associated with significant complications.
