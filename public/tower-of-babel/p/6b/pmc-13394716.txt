Digital determinants of nutrition: the case for a distinct domain to guide health promotion research and policy
Authors: Kanika Agarwal, Louise Holly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDigitalization is shaping the environments, behaviours, and systems through which people access, choose, and produce food, yet nutrition has received limited attention within growing scholarly and policy debates on the digital determinants of health. This perspective argues that the digital transformation is shaping nutrition through pathways that are sufficiently distinct and far-reaching to warrant dedicated attention. We propose that there must be a concerted focus on addressing the digital determinants of nutrition (DDoN), defined as structural conditions that influence nutrition through shaping the characteristics, features, uses, and governance of digital technologies and ecosystems, in future research and policy action. Drawing on a narrative review of literature at the intersection of digital transformations and nutrition, we identify five major pathways through which digital technologies are currently shaping nutrition outcomes: precision nutrition solutions; digital nutrition education and information; digital food environments; digital marketing and content; and digitally transformed agriculture and food systems. Across these pathways, digital transformations present both significant opportunities and serious risks for nutrition, with structural risks often disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations. Establishing DDoN as a recognized domain within health promotion would enable a more coordinated, multi-sectoral response and help ensure that digital governance agendas account for their nutritional consequences.Keywords: nutrition, digital determinants, determinants of health, digital transformation, food environment, health promotion

Introduction: nutrition in an age of digital transformation

Within health promotion and public health, there is growing recognition that digital technologies are not neutral forces but powerful determinants of health. Digital transformations are reshaping nearly every aspect of life, including the environments, behaviours, and systems through which people access, choose, and produce food. Digital environments have therefore become closely entwined with the various factors that drive nutrition behaviour and outcomes (Gedrich 2003). The concept of digital determinants of health (DDoH), broadly defined as structural conditions that influence health through shaping the characteristics, features, uses, and governance of digital technologies and ecosystems, was introduced by the Lancet and Financial Times Commission on Governing Health Futures 2030 to capture these wide-ranging influences (Kickbusch et al. 2021).

Despite being a foundational pillar of health, nutrition has remained peripheral in DDoH scholarship and in broader discussions of digital governance. This omission is consequential because digital transformations are reshaping nutrition through mechanisms that extend well beyond the delivery of digital health interventions. These range from the algorithmic design of food delivery platforms and the rise of online ultra-processed food marketing, to the datafication of agriculture and the proliferation of nutrition misinformation on social media. These mechanisms are sufficiently distinct from those typically emphasized in DDoH frameworks and warrant dedicated attention.

This perspective makes the case for digital determinants of nutrition (DDoN) as a distinct domain and priority for future research and policy. We draw on a narrative review of literature at the intersection of digital transformation and nutrition, encompassing peer-reviewed studies, systematic reviews, and grey literature published between 2010 and 2026, to map five major pathways through which digital technologies are currently shaping nutrition outcomes. We then consider the implications for health promotion scholarship and practice.

Why nutrition warrants distinct attention within DDoH

DDoH operates at three levels: through digital interventions within healthcare systems; through the everyday use of digital technologies; and indirectly through the influence of the wider digital ecosystem (Kickbusch et al. 2021). Over recent years, a body of literature on DDoH has emerged, with a strong emphasis on how digital transformations affect health equity, health systems, and the broader social determinants of health (Chidambaram et al. 2024, Holly et al. 2025, Shaw et al. 2026). Within this corpus, nutrition has not featured prominently as an object of study although indirect references have been made to nutrition, diet, and food security as social determinants of health which are influenced by digitalization (Cuadros et al. 2025, Oliveira et al. 2025, van Kessel et al. 2025, Wade and Parks 2025). A search of the academic literature in June 2026 for the exact phrase ‘DDoN’ returned no results across PubMed, Web of Science, or Google Scholar. A combined search for the terms ‘digital determinants’ AND ‘nutrition’ returned only five relevant results (Mooney et al. 2025, Northcott et al. 2025, Jasani and Aghera 2026, Partridge et al. 2026, Zhao et al. 2026). This confirms that DDoN is neither an established concept nor a major area of focus within DDoH literature, despite the growing body of research on the diverse pathways digital transformations are influencing nutrition outcomes.

There are principled reasons to argue that nutrition merits dedicated attention and explicit prioritization within the ecosystem of digital determinants. First, while nutrition is closely connected to health, there is precedence for studying and responding to nutrition as a separate domain. Nutrition has a unique subject matter, encompassing food systems, dietary intake/requirements (interaction of nutrients with one another), and food behaviours, along with specific agricultural, economic, and environmental factors that influence them and lead to optimal health or adverse health outcomes (Agurs-Collins et al. 2024). Nutrition also has different endpoints from other health domains such as diet quality, food insecurity, nutritional status, breastfeeding, and child growth (Charmarbagwala et al. 2004).

Second, some of the root causes of malnutrition lie outside the conventional health domain including food systems, agricultural policy, and commercial food environments leading experts to consider determinants of nutrition separately from determinants of health more broadly (Harris and Nisbett 2021). The digital transformation is now deeply embedded in each of these domains in ways that health-focused DDoH scholarship has not fully captured.

Third, the intersection between digital and commercial determinants is particularly acute for nutrition because food availability and quality are largely influenced by commercial actors. Commercial determinants in the nutrition context refer to how industry strategies, including food production, pricing and marketing, shape dietary habits, and drive diet-related disease (Kalra et al. 2023). Digital platforms have transformed traditional physical market spaces into persuasive individualized digital environments through which food is marketed, discovered, purchased, and consumed. The commercial imperatives embedded in platform design, such as algorithmic amplification of popular but nutrient-poor options, data-driven targeted advertising, and dark patterns on food delivery apps, are not incidental features but structural drivers of poor nutrition that require dedicated governance responses (Kickbusch and Holly 2024).

Finally, the scale and pace of digitalization in food systems, agriculture, and everyday food behaviours is generating a set of nutrition-specific equity concerns that cut across multiple DDoH levels simultaneously. Digital divides in access to precision nutrition tools, unequal exposure to online food marketing, and the disruption of food supply chains by automation are all nutrition-relevant manifestations of the digital transformation that benefit from being analysed together under a coherent conceptual framework under the broader umbrella of DDoH.

Adapting one definition of DDoH, we define DDoN as structural conditions that influence nutrition through shaping the characteristics, features, uses, and governance of digital technologies and ecosystems, in future research and policy action (Holly et al. 2025). This definition signals that digital transformations are not neutral but operate within specific social, economic, and political contexts, and that they function as root causes of nutritional inequity as well as potential enablers of better nutrition.

Precision nutrition solutions

A rapidly expanding body of research explores the potential of digital technologies to deliver personalized nutrition support, spanning mobile applications, wearable devices, AI-driven dietary tracking, and microbiome- or genome-informed recommendation systems. Randomized controlled trials indicate that integrating data from wearables, food logs and gut microbiome sequencing can improve health outcomes (Zeevi et al. 2015, Bermingham et al. 2024). These tools can expand access to tailored dietary guidance beyond clinical settings.

However, the evidence base for digital nutrition interventions shows modest and often short-term benefits, with significant heterogeneity across study designs and populations (Kurniawan et al. 2025). Critically, digital nutrition interventions carry risks related to data privacy, algorithmic bias, and unequal access. For example, designers of digital interventions to tackle obesity can fail to consider systemic barriers such as income, limited connectivity, and low digital literacy thereby exacerbating inequalities (Partridge et al. 2026). A systematic review of digital nutrition interventions for adolescents found that no studies fully addressed the nine dimensions of DDoH identified by Chidambaram et al. (2024), suggesting that failure to account for digital determinants may cause these interventions to deepen rather than reduce nutritional inequalities (Mooney et al. 2025). This underscores the need for a more explicit DDoN lens in intervention design and evaluation.

Digital nutrition education and information

The abundance of nutrition-related content online provides unprecedented access to dietary information for many people. Digital exclusion hinders exposure to nutrition education, behaviour change communication, and welfare schemes, thereby reinforcing suboptimal nutrition practices among those at greatest risk of poor nutrition (Jasani and Aghera 2026). Barcode scanning applications such as Yuka and FoodSwitch have shown promise in enabling consumers to make more informed food choices (Dunford et al. 2014). Dietitians and nutritionists are increasingly using social media to share evidence-based guidance, and online platforms can support nutrition promotion at scale.

Yet, the same digital information environment creates significant risks. Social media is saturated with conflicting, unverified and commercially influenced nutrition claims. Qualitative studies find that many nutrition influencers lack formal qualifications and are financially incentivized by food industry actors (O’Connor et al. 2023). Unregulated online nutrition content can expose people, particularly those with lower levels of education and digital literacy, to harmful dietary advice, fad diets, and eating disorder-promoting content. In low- and middle-income countries, increased social media use has been associated with both lower stunting risk and higher rates of overweight in adolescents, highlighting complex and gender-differentiated effects of digital exposure on nutrition (Zhao et al. 2026). Governing the quality and safety of online nutrition information is a pressing challenge for digital platforms and policymakers alike.

Digital food environments

The food environment is an important nutrition-specific construct, with no direct comparator in most other health domains (Gaupholm et al. 2023). Online grocery shopping, food delivery applications, meal subscription boxes, and e-commerce platforms are transforming how food is accessed, purchased, and consumed. For some, these platforms expand access to diverse food options and can support adherence to healthy diets. Early evidence suggests that meal kits and online grocery services may improve short-term dietary quality for some users (Parry-Strong et al. 2023). However, access to these benefits is unevenly distributed, often requiring reliable digital infrastructure and sufficient data packages.

However, concerns are growing that digital food environments are systematically promoting poor nutrition choices, particularly among young people and lower-income communities. A national US survey found that one in four adolescents (aged 13–17 years) used mobile food delivery services, primarily to purchase fast food and sugar-sweetened beverages (Matsuzaki et al. 2025). The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the use of online food retail, associated with increased consumption of ultra-processed foods (Jia et al. 2024). The algorithms embedded in food delivery platforms amplify popular but nutrient-poor options; dark patterns in app design manipulate users into unintended purchases; and nutritional information is frequently absent or inaccessible on delivery platforms (Vanderlee and Sacks 2023). Some researchers have warned of the emergence of ‘cyber food swamps’: digital environments in which certain communities are particularly exposed to unhealthy food options (Zhang et al. 2024). Addressing the architecture and governance of digital food environments is therefore a health promotion priority.

Digital marketing and content

Digital marketing of food and beverages represents one of the well-evidenced and concerning DDoN pathways. Online channels, including social media, search engine optimization, influencer marketing, and in-game advertising, have become the primary means through which food companies reach consumers, especially children and adolescents who are less able to identify and critically evaluate marketing tactics. The harmful effects of unhealthy food advertising on children are well established (Elliott et al. 2025), and digital platforms have dramatically expanded the reach, targeting precision and opacity of such marketing. Studies with teenagers’ document that sugary beverages, fast food, and confectionery are the dominant food categories they encounter through digital advertising (Blanchard et al. 2023).

Social media platforms are also contributing to broader shifts in dietary norms and body image. There is mounting evidence that social media is part of the obesogenic environment, with its use associated with irregular meal patterns and higher consumption of unhealthy snacks, particularly among populations already vulnerable to poorer nutrition outcomes (Granheim et al. 2022). The use of highly visual platforms such as TikTok and Instagram is correlated with disordered eating symptoms in adolescents, including compulsive overeating and weight loss/control conditions (Blanchard et al. 2023). The commercial architecture of digital platforms, designed to maximize engagement and data capture rather than user wellbeing, is a structural driver of these outcomes. Restricting digital marketing of unhealthy foods and beverages, particularly to children, is a critical policy response that the health promotion community must champion (World Health Organization 2024).

Digitally transformed agriculture and food systems

Digital technologies, including the Internet of Things, big data analytics, and AI, are now deeply integrated into agricultural supply chains, food production, and distribution systems. Precision farming—using AI, GPS guidance, drone monitoring, and real-time environmental data to optimize crop yields and reduce waste—holds significant potential for improving food security, promoting nutrient-dense crop production, and reducing environmental impacts (Ghazal et al. 2024). Block-chain technology is being applied to improve food safety and traceability (Kamilaris et al. 2019). At present, such benefits are concentrated in higher resource settings.

At the same time, digitalization in food and agriculture systems carries equity risks. The high cost of AI tools and lack of digital infrastructure in rural and remote areas risks widening the divide between large-scale industrial farming and small-scale producers, with potential downstream consequences for the livelihoods of small farmers and their ability to purchase nutritious food (Hasteer et al. 2024). Automated food systems risk negative environmental impacts, and the digitally enabled concentration of power in global food supply chains raises systemic concerns about food sovereignty and resilience (Tzachor et al. 2022). These systemic DDoN operate largely upstream of individual consumer behaviour and require governance responses that go far beyond the health sector.

Implications for health promotion

The evidence across these five pathways demonstrates that digital transformations present both genuine opportunities and serious, structurally embedded risks for nutrition. Crucially, many of the risks are not conditional on individual choices but are outcomes of deliberate platform design, commercial data practices, and under-regulated digital market environments. As Table 1 illustrates, benefits and risks are not symmetrically distributed: structural risks often operate at population scale and disproportionately impact underserved populations, while benefits tend to be conditional on access and digital literacy. Prevailing digital divides mean that populations already at risk of poorer nutrition outcomes currently stand to gain least from digital transformations.

Many of the most significant DDoN operate at the level of corporate power, market structure and global governance, rather than as downstream behavioural concerns. A small number of technology and food/beverage companies exercise substantial influence over the digital infrastructures, including app stores, delivery platforms, social media algorithms, and agricultural data systems, through which nutrition-relevant decisions are now mediated. Platform monopolies and digitally enabled food businesses shape what foods are visible, affordable, and convenient, often with limited public oversight. These structures are increasingly international, as digital food and agriculture platforms operate across borders in ways that can outpace the regulatory capacity of national governments. Addressing DDoN therefore requires active engagement with the global political economy of digital platforms and digital food systems.

This analysis points to three inter-related implications for health promotion scholarship and practice.

First, the health promotion community needs to engage actively with digital governance as a nutrition issue. The governance of digital platforms, algorithms, data practice, and digital marketing is not simply a technology policy matter; it is a health promotion matter with direct consequences for population nutrition. Health promoters should be vocal advocates for stronger regulation of digital food marketing, particularly targeting children; for algorithmic transparency and accountability in food delivery platforms; and for digital design standards that support rather than undermine healthy food choices. This also entails engaging with upstream governance challenges such as platform regulation as nutrition concerns. The existing evidence base on unhealthy food marketing provides a strong foundation for these advocacy efforts (World Health Organization 2024).

Second, DDoN should be further incorporated into research and practice frameworks for addressing digital health interventions. The review by Mooney et al. (2025) demonstrates that digital nutrition interventions are routinely developed without systematic attention to the digital determinants shaping their reach and impact. Incorporating a digital determinants lens to intervention design, evaluation, and scale-up could help ensure that digital nutrition tools reduce rather than exacerbate nutritional inequalities. This includes attention to structural drivers of nutrition inequality such as commercial platform design, digital literacy, platform accessibility, data governance, and algorithmic accountability.

Third, nutrition professionals and the broader health promotion community should contribute to a more rigorous understanding of the DDoN. Such work could draw on existing DDoH typologies, including the dimensions identified by Chidambaram et al. (2024) and the multi-level model of van Kessel et al. (2025), while tailoring the analytical focus to the specific social, commercial, and political pathways through which digital transformations affect nutrition. Cross-disciplinary collaboration between nutrition scientists, digital health scholars, food systems researchers, and health promotion practitioners will be essential.

Addressing DDoN also requires explicit attention to equity. Future research should examine how DDoN are experienced differently across population groups defined by gender, age, socioeconomic status, geographic location, and digital access. In particular, the intersection of digital divides with nutrition inequity, where those with the least access to beneficial digital technologies are often most exposed to harmful digital food environments and marketing, demands targeted research and policy responses.

Conclusion

The digital transformation has become a major determinant of how people access, choose, produce, and consume food. Despite this, nutrition has not been prioritized within DDoH discourse. This perspective argues that the scale, specificity, and complexity of digital influences on nutrition justify the recognition of DDoN as a distinct domain and action area. DDoN does not seek to supplant DDoH but to deepen and focus on a domain—nutrition—where the stakes for population health and equity are exceptionally high and where dedicated conceptual attention is overdue.

The five pathways outlined in this perspective point to a distinct evidence base, spanning food environment research, agricultural digitalization, platform algorithms, and food marketing that does not map neatly onto existing DDoH categories and can be coherently understood as a domain in its own right. Explicitly naming this new domain the ‘DDoN’ provides a shared vocabulary that can drive research and policy prioritization, helping to consolidate this currently fragmented evidence into a recognizable field of inquiry for both nutrition and broader public health communities.

Beyond vocabulary, DDoN offers a lens through which debates about digital technology and food can be organized and deliberated, reframing questions that might otherwise be treated separately (platform regulation, food marketing, agricultural digitalization, and data privacy) as facets of a single, coherent policy domain with nutrition and equity at its centre. This mirrors the trajectory of other determinants, including commercial, political, and digital determinants, where naming and defining domains of emerging significance helped catalyse research, advocacy, and policy attention that might otherwise have remained dispersed.

Establishing DDoN as a recognized domain within health promotion could catalyse a more coordinated research agenda, strengthen advocacy for digital governance as a nutrition policy issue, and support the development of more equitable digital nutrition interventions. The nutrition community must engage actively in this agenda, both to ensure that digital transformation fosters equitable nutritional outcomes and to ensure that health promotion is shaping the digital environments in which people live, eat, and make dietary choices.
