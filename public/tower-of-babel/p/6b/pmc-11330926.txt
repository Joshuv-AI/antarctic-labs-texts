Comparative Effectiveness of Thrombectomy Devices in Deep Vein Thrombosis: A Step Forward
Authors: Matthew T Finn
Published: 2024

The abundance of novel devices for the treatment of deep venous thrombosis (DVT) has begun to feel overwhelming. Many devices now tout thrombolytic-free approaches to reduce the approximately 1% intracranial hemorrhage risk seen with catheter-directed thrombolysis.1 The rapid, iterative technological advancements for invasive thrombectomy have left clinicians with significant questions such as which device to use, when to use them, and whether to pair these devices with thrombolytic treatment.

There is currently little comparative effectiveness data to guide device strategy in invasive DVT treatment. A recent study evaluated mechanical thrombectomy (MT) vs catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT) using a pooled, propensity matching strategy, with thrombectomy patients taken from the CLOUT trial2 and CDT patients taken from the ATTRACT trial.3 The study showed that MT was associated with greater periprocedural thrombus reduction, shorter length of stay, and improved symptoms at 12 months when compared with CDT.4

In this issue of JSCAI, Mittleider et al5 report the results of a retrospective cohort study from the PINC AI database to determine comparative differences of 3 device types based on mortality, cost, length of stay, and 30-day readmissions. Multivariable analysis was used to adjust for potential confounders.

Patients included were those receiving MT for inferior vena cava and iliofemoral DVT without concomitant pulmonary embolism. Upper extremity and superior vena cava DVT were also excluded. The devices were divided into 3 groups: AngioJet ZelanteDVT (AJ), ClotTriever System (CT), and Indigo system (IN). After multivariate adjustment, the CT group was seen to experience lower in-hospital mortality than the AJ and IN groups. Rates of concomitant thrombolytic use were 61.6% for AJ, 8.9% for CT, and 37.8% for IN groups. Transfusion postprocedure was observed to be significantly higher for patients receiving AJ or IN. Cost data suggest lower charge per encounter with the CT device as well as shorter ICU and overall length of stay. The 30-day readmission rate ranged from 10% (AJ) to 14.6% (IN), while multivariate modeling predicted higher odds of readmission in the IN group.

This study represents a significant step forward in our understanding of the comparative effectiveness of available devices for treatment of DVT. Although this study must be viewed with the limitations of a retrospective database study and hypothesis generating, it does provide an important starting point from which prospective comparative trials can be built. The study suggests that there may be important differences in outcomes, complications, length of stay, and cost related to device type. In addition, this study signals that the movement away from thrombolytic therapy for the treatment of DVT may have real-world merit.

The bigger picture is that the indication for performing routine procedures in DVT remains somewhat unclear given the overall negative result of the ATTRACT randomized controlled trial.3 Although ATTRACT was performed prior to the routine availability of MT, no large, randomized trial has demonstrated the superiority of routine invasive strategies. Fortunately, the DEFIANCE randomized control trial6 will evaluate MT vs anticoagulation alone and provide further insight into the routine invasive treatment of significant DVT.

In conclusion, meaningful differences in outcomes based on device strategy for DVT likely exist, and the current competitive landscape of DVT therapies will drive progress in the treatment of these patients.
