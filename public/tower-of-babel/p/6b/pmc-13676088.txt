Epicardial adipose tissue radiomics complement calcium scoring in black adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Robert Okyere, Sai Rahul Ponnana, Prerna Singh, Santosh Sirasapalli, Ammar Hoori, Tao Hu, Tasveer Khawaja, Joseph Amoah, Michael Glidden, Ian Neeland, Salil Deo, Sadeer Al Kindi, David Wilson, Sanjay Rajagopalan
Published: 2027

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConventional markers of risk such as coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores are lower and less predictive in Black adults, despite a high prevalence of Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and Metabolic Syndrome We evaluated whether incorporation of epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) features capture prognostic signal beyond CAC and social deprivation index, in predicting major adverse cardiovascular events.MethodsAdults with T2DM undergoing non-contrast CT calcium scoring from the CLARIFY registry (NCT04075162), including 1024 non-Hispanic Black participants and 3072 propensity-matched non-Hispanic White participants were included. EAT was automatically segmented and radiomic features were extracted and selected using survival-based machine learning. Cox proportional hazards models were constructed using CAC, Social Deprivation Index (SDI), and EAT features alone and in combination. The primary endpoint was 3-point major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE-3P), defined as cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, or non-fatal stroke. Model discrimination, reclassification, and calibration were assessed.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of approximately 5 years, 206 (20%) Black participants and 422 (14%) matched White participants experienced MACE-3P In Black participants, the SDI+CAC+EAT model demonstrated the highest discrimination (C-index 0.62) and significantly improved reclassification compared with CAC alone (NRI) of 0.29 (95% CI 0.15–0.56; p < 0.0007). In contrast, the SDI+CAC+EAT model showed no improvement in discrimination when compared to CAC alone (C-index 0.67 v. 0.67) in the White cohort.ConclusionIn adults with T2DM, EAT may serve as a complimentary imaging marker when combined with CAC scores to provide incremental prognostic information or refine risk characterization.

Introduction

Coronary artery calcium (CAC) is a validated tool to assess subclinical coronary atherosclerotic burden on non-contrast computed tomography calcium score (CTCS) [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Addition of CAC to traditional risk factors including diabetes mellitus and metabolic syndrome, has been shown to improve risk classification of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) events [5]. While the prevalence of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) and related adverse cardiovascular outcomes are higher in Blacks when compared to other racial groups, surrogate markers of risk such as CAC scores have been reported to be lower [6,5,7]. Indeed, current risk-based cohort equations that explicitly incorporate race do not seem to perform as well in Blacks compared to whites[3,8,9] Thus, there is a need for improved alternative approaches in this population.

Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) is a “fascialess” fat depot between the visceral pericardium and myocardium, encompassing and extending along the major coronary vessels into the atrioventricular and interventricular grooves, collectively constituting about 20% of cardiac weight [10,11]. EAT is of similar embryonic origins as omental and mesenteric fat, evolving from splanchonopleuric mesoderm associated with the gut [[11], [12], [13]]. As a visceral fat depot, EAT is biologically active, secreting adipokines and is influenced by cellular inflammation [14,15]. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of EAT in atherogenesis and heart failure, via activation of adaptive and innate immune mechanisms [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Given the pathophysiologic relationship, driven by anatomic proximity of the EAT to the coronaries and adjacent myocardium, even simple measures such as EAT thickness, measured by cardiac computer tomography (CT), have been noted to be of prognostic significance in ASCVD [17].

We and others have developed deep learning algorithms that enable opportunistic fully automatic, accurate EAT segmentation from CT calcium score (CTCS) scans, together with feature extraction and analysis (e.g., morphological, Hounsfield unit (HU) intensity, spatial distribution and textural), enabling integration of risk information not captured by measures such as CAC or EAT thickness and/or volume [[20], [21], [22]]. Given the high rates of visceral adiposity and type 2 diabetes (T2D) in Blacks, and the previously reported underperformance of traditional risk prediction metrics such as CAC in this population, we hypothesized that EAT radiomic parameters (fat-omics) encode information regarding ASCVD risk in Blacks compared to Whites when combined with CAC scores alone. Accordingly, the goal of study was to evaluate if EAT radiomics meaningfully improves risk estimation in populations where CAC alone has known limitations.

Methods

Study population and design

A total of 40,851 patients (≥18 years) without known ASCVD were identified from the Community Calcium Scoring Assessment for Cardiovascular Risk Stratification Registry (CLARIFY, ClinicalTrials.gov NCT0407516224) at University Hospitals, Cleveland (UH) [23]. This study on de-identified data was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of UH.The IRB of UH waived informed consent for all studies utilizing anonymized CT scans. Patients underwent low-cost or no-cost CTCS for risk assessment between 2014 and 2020. Electronic health records were followed for incident MACE (myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization, stroke, or all-cause mortality) (Supplemental Table S1) with patients censored at last follow-up.

This retrospective cohort study included adults with T2DM identified by ICD-9 10th version codes for T2DM in the electronic health record. Where available, a proximal HbA1c value was additionally used to characterize glycemic control at the time of imaging (HbA1c available in 54.7% of the cohort; reported in Table 1) who self-identified as either non-Hispanic Black (a total of 1024 patients) or non-Hispanic White (a total of 3072 patients) [24]. Clinical characteristics and area level features such as Social Deprivation Index (SDI) data were estimated for all patients. Given the substantial difference in baseline clinical characteristics, propensity matching for clinical features (but not for SDI), was used in a 3:1 ratio based on age, gender, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), inflammatory diseases, and statin use (Table 1, Supplemental Figure S3). The 3:1 matching ratio was selected to maximize statistical efficiency and precision, while maintaining adequate covariate balance, given the larger pool of eligible White participants. Time-to-event was calculated from the date of the index CT calcium score to the first occurrence of any MACE-3P component or censoring at last follow-up (Fig. 4). Secondary endpoints included expanded composite outcomes—MACE-4P (MACE-3P plus coronary revascularization), MACE-5P (MACE-4P plus heart failure hospitalization)—as well as individual cardiovascular outcomes and all-cause mortality. Secondary endpoints were evaluated descriptively and using Kaplan–Meier analyses (Fig. 4).

In addition to composite endpoints, individual component events including cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, non-fatal stroke, coronary revascularization, and heart failure hospitalization were tabulated to clarify the relative contribution of each component to the overall composite risk (Table 1; Supplemental Table S1).

Social deprivation index (SDI)

The Social Deprivation Index (SDI) was linked to participants using residential ZIP code at the time of imaging. SDI is a composite measure reflecting area-level socioeconomic disadvantage. SDI was incorporated into multivariable models to account for structural and social determinants of health that may influence cardiovascular outcomes and potentially contribute to racial disparities in event rates.

SDI was not included in the propensity score matching because the matching strategy was designed to balance individual-level clinical characteristics between racial groups. Including SDI in the propensity score would risk overmatching and potentially obscure its independent contribution as a contextual exposure of interest. SDI was therefore evaluated as a separate predictor in Cox regression models.

The distribution of SDI values was summarized across racial groups using median and interquartile ranges and compared using non-parametric tests (Supplemental Table S4). To assess whether the association between SDI and outcomes differed by race, Cox models including a race-by-SDI interaction term were fitted (Supplemental Table S5). This interaction analyses between SDI and race were conducted to evaluate potential effect modification of neighborhood deprivation on cardiovascular risk.

CT imaging

All CTCS scans were electrocardiogram-gated, non-contrast acquisitions, with standardized scanner settings of 120kVp. Imaging was performed using either 64, 128, or 256 detector row scanners (Brilliance 64, Ingenuity CT, iCT 256, Ingenuity Core 128, Phillips Medical Systems, Netherlands; Optima CT 660, GE Healthcare, USA) between 2014–20 with a standard slice thickness of 2.5 mm, a nominal in-plane pixel spacing of 0.5 × 0.5 mm2.

EAT segmentation

DeepFat, a validated deep learning segmentation tool, was used for fully automated EAT segmentation from CTCS images and has been shown to be highly accurate compared to manual segmentation and good inter-operator repeatability (R > 0.97, p < 0.001) [[20], [21], [22]]. Pre-processing included: (1) applying an HU-attention window/level (350/40 HU) to focus on the pericardial sac and minimize numerical errors, and (2) using a novel look-ahead slab-of-slices strategy, that splits the heart into upper and lower halves, presenting an always increasing curvature of the sac to the “DeepLab-v3-plus” network for improved segmentation. An automated process by which a few segmentation metrics such as pericardial sac volume, EAT volume, and mean HU allowed removal of outliers using unsupervised clustering.

EAT feature selection and use in study

To identify epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) radiomic signatures associated with MACE, we first extracted 211 radiomic features from the automated EAT segmentation pipeline. These features spanned four domains: (1) morphological features capturing pericardial sac geometry and EAT thickness distribution; (2) attenuation-based features reflecting the Hounsfield unit (HU) distribution of voxels within the EAT-specific HU window of −190 to −30, which is specific to adipose tissue density and excludes myocardial (≈0 to +70 HU) and pericardial structures; (3) spatial features characterizing EAT distribution across positional quartiles (PQ, superior-to-inferior) and spherical regions (SR, radial distance from the myocardial surface to the pericardial boundary); and (4) a pericardial sac-level attenuation feature (SAC_MeanHU) reflecting mean HU across all voxels within the pericardial sac boundary regardless of tissue type, which encompasses EAT, myocardium, and cardiac chambers (see Supplemental Appendix for complete feature definitions and anatomical specificity).

These features were entered into a survival-based extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) model with a Cox proportional hazards loss function to model time-to-event outcomes. The model was trained using 10-fold cross-validation, and feature importance was ranked using the XGBoost gain metric.

For visualization and descriptive comparison, we examined the top 30 radiomic features associated with MACE based on feature importance ranking. These features were subsequently displayed using percent change and Cohen’s d to highlight differences between Black and matched White participants.

For downstream radiomics modeling, feature refinement was performed by retaining only variables with gain importance >0.09. This yielded a final set of 24 radiomic features, which were used in Cox proportional hazards models evaluating the incremental prognostic value of EAT radiomics beyond CAC and SDI in the T2DM cohort. The final retained features included morphological, attenuation-based, and spatial descriptors, allowing assessment of whether higher-dimensional EAT phenotypes improve cardiovascular risk estimation beyond conventional measures.

Evaluation of EAT radiomics in predicting MACE

We developed and compared six Cox proportional hazards regression models to evaluate the additive prognostic value of SDI, CAC, and EAT features in predicting MACE among individuals with T2D. Each model incorporated a distinct combination of predictors, including: (1) SDI alone, (2) CAC alone, (3) EAT alone, (4) SDI + CAC, (5) CAC + EAT, and (6) the comprehensive model integrating all three domains (SDI + CAC + EAT; hereafter referred to as the Full Model). This pre-specified six-model structure was designed to enable direct quantification of the incremental contribution of each predictor class—SDI, EAT, and the combination—above CAC alone, which served as the clinical reference standard.

CAC was modeled as a continuous log-transformed variable using log(1 + CAC score) in regression analyses to account for right-skewed distribution. Within each race-stratified matched cohort, participants were randomly divided into training (75%) and validation (25%) sets. The primary performance metric was Harrell’s C-index at 5 years, calculated in the validation cohort. Time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was evaluated at 5-year horizons using inverse probability of censoring weighting. Calibration at 5 years was assessed using bootstrap resampling with 100 iterations.

Signal enhancement metrics

Measures such as changes in discrimination, integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and net reclassification improvement (NRI) were used to quantify whether EAT radiomics contain additional prognostic information beyond traditional variables. These analyses were intended to assess signal enrichment rather than to establish a clinically deployable risk prediction model. IDI was calculated as the difference in discrimination slopes between models, defined as the change in the mean predicted probability among participants who experienced events, minus the change among those who remained event-free.

We computed category-free (continuous) NRI to avoid dependence on arbitrary risk thresholds. Continuous NRI quantifies the proportion of participants with events assigned higher predicted risk and the proportion without events assigned lower predicted risk under the expanded model compared with the reference model.

All reclassification metrics were calculated at the 5-year time horizon given the cohort had a median follow-up duration of 4 years, using inverse probability of censoring weighting to account for right-censoring. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were derived using 1000 bootstrap resamples.

Results

Study cohort characteristics and outcomes

Central illustration and Supplemental Figure S1 illustrate the overall study design while Table 1 provides the demographic and clinical characteristics of the black patients in CLARIFY. Mean age was similar between Black cohort and propensity-matched White group (61.29 ± 9.50 vs 61.49 ± 8.91 years). Women comprised 61% of Black patients and 59% of matched White patients. Current or former smoking was present in 48% and 46%, respectively. Body mass index, triglycerides, blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, inflammatory disease prevalence, and statin use were comparable between matched groups (Table 1).

Median CAC score was 16 (IQR 0–162) in Black patients and 56 (IQR 0–312) in matched White patients. The distribution of CAC categories is shown in Table 1. Additionally, T2D Black patients had a higher multi-ethnic study of atherosclerosis (MESA) calculator-based ASCVD and preventing risk of atherosclerotic disease events (PREVENT-Full model) risk, despite having lower CAC scores (Fig. 2). Given the higher prevalence of multiple risk factors in Blacks, we performed propensity matching for these factors in a 3:1 ratio, given that we were interested in the importance of EAT features in blacks that were operational, outside the influence of traditional risk factors. CAC scores in blacks were lower compared to the non-black population in the propensity matched group, as well in every category of CAC (Table 1). MACE and all-cause mortality were also higher in Blacks when compared to the overall T2D population, including those in the propensity-matched cohort (Table 1), supporting prior observations from other cohorts, that have also reported lower calcium scores even after controlling for traditional risk factors [9].

The median follow-up was 4.04 years in Black patients and 4.11 years in matched White patients. In the Black cohort, 206 events of MACE-3P occurred, including 91 myocardial infarctions, 78 strokes, and 80 deaths. Corresponding event counts for the matched White cohort are shown in Table 1 and Supplemental Table S1, which detail event frequencies by endpoint component. Neighborhood deprivation differed markedly by race. Black participants had substantially higher SDI values compared with both matched and unmatched White participants (median 68 vs 27 and 26, respectively; p < 0.001; Supplemental Table S4), consistent with greater area-level socioeconomic disadvantage. In Cox models evaluating neighborhood deprivation, higher SDI was associated with increased MACE risk (HR 1.01 per 1-unit increase, p < 0.001). Black participants had higher risk compared with White participants at comparable SDI levels (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.05–1.68, p = 0.02). However, the race-by-SDI interaction was not statistically significant (p = 0.40) (Supplemental Table S5).

Model performance and incremental contribution of SDI and EAT

At the 5-year follow-up, the CAC-alone model demonstrated modest discrimination in the Black cohort (C-index 0.574; AUC 0.58, 95% CI 0.48–0.68). Comparing the six pre-specified models allows direct attribution of incremental prognostic signal to each predictor class. In the Black cohort, addition of SDI to CAC alone (SDI+CAC model) increased the C-index to 0.588 (Δ+0.014 over CAC alone), indicating a modest contribution of socioeconomic deprivation beyond imaging. In contrast, addition of EAT radiomic features to CAC alone (CAC+EAT model) increased the C-index to 0.609 (Δ+0.035), a meaningfully larger increment that reflects the unique prognostic information contained in higher-dimensional adipose phenotyping. The Full Model combining all three predictors achieved the highest discrimination (C-index 0.618; AUC 0.66, 95% CI 0.57–0.76), with an increment of Δ+0.044 over CAC alone. These model-level comparisons are summarized in Table 2 and Supplemental Table S2.

In the matched White cohort, CAC alone demonstrated substantially stronger baseline discrimination (C-index 0.666; AUC 0.69, 95% CI 0.63–0.75), and neither the addition of SDI (SDI+CAC C-index 0.669, Δ+0.003) nor EAT features (CAC+EAT C-index 0.669, Δ+0.003) nor the full combination (C-index 0.669, Δ+0.003) yielded meaningful improvement. This race-specific pattern indicates that the additional prognostic signal contributed by EAT radiomics is not simply a reflection of socioeconomic deprivation captured by SDI, but represents a distinct imaging-derived dimension of cardiometabolic risk that is particularly informative in Black adults with T2DM.

In the Black cohort, addition of SDI and EAT features to CAC resulted in an Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) of 0.14 (95% CI 0.13–0.33; p = 0.00047) and a continuous Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) of 0.29 (95% CI 0.15–0.56; p = 0.000699) compared with CAC alone. In the matched White cohort, the corresponding IDI was 0.07 (95% CI 0.06–0.16) and NRI was 0.25 (95% CI 0.14–0.38), both statistically significant but of substantially smaller magnitude (Table 3). The greater reclassification improvement in Black participants suggests that EAT-based features refine risk categorization in a population where CAC-based categorization is least reliable.

The full model in both Black and White cohorts demonstrated the closest alignment to the reference line, indicating good calibration across the risk spectrum with a slight deviation at higher predicted risk (Fig. 5). The CAC-only model in the Black cohort was poorly calibrated, showing risk underestimation at higher decile and risk overestimation at lower decile. Among the top radiomic features, SAC_MeanHU—reflecting mean attenuation across the entire pericardial sac, including both EAT and myocardial contributions—showed the largest percent change between racial groups (Fig. 3, Supplemental Figure S2). EAT-specific attenuation features, including positional quartile- and spherical region-stratified HU probabilities in the higher attenuation ranges (−70 to −50 and −50 to −30 HU), were also significantly elevated in Black participants, consistent with greater EAT inflammatory activity in this group (see Supplemental Appendix for feature definitions).

Discussion

The main findings of the study suggest that the addition of top EAT radiomic features beyond CAC and SDI improved discrimination, reclassification, and calibration in Black adults with T2DM, with substantially greater benefit compared to matched White participants. Importantly, model-level decomposition reveals that the imaging-derived signal from EAT features (ΔC-index +0.035 for CAC+EAT vs. CAC alone in the Black cohort) exceeds the incremental contribution of SDI alone (ΔC-index +0.014 for SDI+CAC vs. CAC alone), suggesting that the observed improvement is driven primarily by imaging rather than socioeconomic variables. Although SDI was independently associated with MACE (HR 1.01 per 1-unit increase, p < 0.001; Supplemental Table S5), consistent with prior reports [25], and Black participants carried higher residual risk at comparable SDI levels (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.05–1.68), the race-by-SDI interaction was non-significant (p = 0.40). This implies that the benefit of EAT radiomics in Black participants is not attributable to SDI acting as an unmeasured confounder, but rather reflects unique adipose phenotypic information not captured by area-level socioeconomic measures.

These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that Black adults tend to have lower CAC scores despite higher cardiovascular event rates after controlling for traditional risk factors [[1], [2], [3], [4],6,5,7,9]. Black participants in this study had substantially higher SDI values than both matched and unmatched White participants (median 68 vs. 27; p < 0.001), reflecting greater neighborhood-level socioeconomic disadvantage. However, the race-specific improvement in model performance with EAT radiomics—observed in the Black cohort but not in the matched White cohort—suggests that imaging-derived adipose phenotypes capture biologic risk dimensions not adequately reflected by calcified plaque burden or area-level deprivation.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that comprehensively evaluated EAT-derived features in Black T2DM when compared to white adults and also evaluated incorporation of these features as predictors of MACE. Previous research evaluated simpler metrics, such as EAT volume, max thickness, and mean HU attenuation, which have shown to have limited predictive power compared to fat-omics [8,26,27]. While other advanced assessments of CT calcium score for MACE prediction have been reported in the literature on smaller cohorts [28,29], in our study, the incorporation of SDI to CAC and EAT to CAC each independently improved discrimination in Blacks but not in matched white patients. The incorporation of all 3 features demonstrated improved risk estimation for MACE independent of other clinical risk factors (Supplemental Tables S2 and S3) in the Black cohort, and were predominantly attenuation-based. In Black participants, the CAC-only model showed evidence of miscalibration, whereas the full model incorporating SDI and EAT radiomics aligned more closely with observed risk across the estimation range. This observation is consistent with the broader premise that reliance on CAC alone may underestimate risk in some groups with T2DM. At the same time, calibration improvement in a derivation cohort should be interpreted cautiously. Such findings are useful for hypothesis generation and model refinement, but they do not establish generalizability across settings, scanners, referral patterns, or populations.

Our findings also build on earlier work examining simpler EAT measures such as thickness, mean attenuation, or total volume [30,8,31,26,27,[20], [21], [22]]. While these measures have shown associations with cardiometabolic disease, they provide only a limited description of a complex depot. Radiomic analysis allows a richer characterization of morphology, attenuation distribution, and spatial organization. The present study suggests that this higher-dimensional representation may be especially informative in a subgroup where conventional imaging metrics underperform. A number of features were related to attenuation/textural features that may embed information related to inflammation. For instance, PQ3 and SR4 Pro-70–50 (Fig. 1), correspond to higher attenuation fat closer to the 3rd position quartile and the 4th spherical region which anatomically correspond to superior and immediately adjacent EAT sections to the heart. The accumulation of higher HU values in EAT adjacent to the heart, likely indicates metabolically active inflammation of EAT that are not adequately reflected by calcified plaque burden or area-level deprivation, and consequently translate into higher cardiovascular risk in Blacks [20]. A number of epidemiologic studies show a higher predilection for metabolic risk factors including obesity, insulin resistance, and T2D in Blacks [6,5,7] which may drive these differences. Visceral adiposity is highly prevalent in Black patients and indeed in this study, a crude measure of adiposity, BMI, was higher in Blacks. An in-depth assessment of EAT patterns by race has not been previously undertaken. A prior study only looked at ethnic differences and their association with EAT mainly focused on EAT thickness [8].

Although modeling analyses demonstrated modest improvements in discrimination and reclassification metrics when EAT radiomics were incorporated, these results should not be interpreted as establishing a clinical risk prediction tool. The present analyses were intended to evaluate whether EAT radiomic features encode prognostic information beyond conventional anthropometric and imaging measures. Because the models were developed and evaluated within a single observational cohort without external validation, the results should be considered hypothesis generating and indicative of a potentially informative imaging phenotype rather than a validated risk assessment framework. Future studies incorporating external validation cohorts and prospective testing will be required to determine whether EAT radiomics can meaningfully augment established cardiovascular risk stratification approaches.

The intended eventual clinical application of this study is risk refinement during routine CAC scanning in Black adults with type 2 diabetes. Patients with low or intermediate CAC scores who are reclassified into higher-risk categories by EAT-enhanced models may benefit from earlier or more intensive preventive strategies, including aggressive lipid lowering, blood pressure control, and cardiometabolic therapies. Further investigations into the use of CAC scores and EAT in all populations is warranted to better expand its use and to better identify the unique risk-enhancing features.

Our study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, the study was conducted in a single-health-system registry in Northeast Ohio, which may introduce referral bias; the CLARIFY program offered low- or no-cost screening, potentially enriching the sample with socioeconomically diverse patients but also limiting generalizability to other health systems and geographic settings.

Second, although propensity matching balanced multiple clinical covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors remains possible. HbA1c was available in approximately 54.7% of the cohort and is reported descriptively in Table 1. Among those with available values, Black participants had modestly higher mean HbA1c compared with matched White participants (7.25 ± 1.76 vs. 6.82 ± 1.32; p < 0.001), consistent with known racial disparities in glycemic control. However, because HbA1c was missing in approximately 45.3% of the cohort, given the real-world registry setting, the assumption of missingness completely at random cannot be verified, and adjustment using only the 54.7% of participants with available values risked introducing selection bias into the propensity model and Cox regression estimates. Accordingly, differential diabetes severity or glycemic control between racial groups remains a source of residual confounding in the present analysis. Additional diabetes severity metrics—including duration of diabetes and antidiabetic medication use (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists)—were also not uniformly captured and could not be adjusted for. Future prospective studies should incorporate complete, standardized diabetes severity data to enable more robust adjustment for these confounders.

Third, the CLARIFY registry did not protocolize clinical management following CTCS. The higher proportion of Black participants with CAC=0 (39%vs. 27% in matched White participants) raises the possibility that some individuals may have been de-risked based on CAC findings alone, potentially leading to de-escalation of preventive therapy and influencing downstream event rates and model calibration. This represents an uncontrolled confound that cannot be quantified from available data, and it underscores the clinical concern motivating this study—namely, that reliance on CAC as a gatekeeper for preventive therapy may result in systematic undertreatment of high-risk Black adults.

Fourth, SDI is an area-level measure and does not capture the full spectrum of individual social adversity, lifetime structural exposures, or within-neighborhood heterogeneity [32]. The results should not be interpreted as fully accounting for the social determinants of health disparities in this population.

Fifth, automated EAT segmentation, while validated, is subject to measurement error and scanner-related variability that may affect radiomic features. All scans were acquired on a limited set of standardized scanners (120 kVp, 2.5 mm slice thickness), which partially mitigates this concern within the registry, but external applicability to other imaging protocols requires further validation.

Sixth, the study is restricted to adults with T2DM. The biological mechanisms linking EAT inflammation to cardiovascular risk—particularly the higher HU attenuation features observed in Black participants—may be especially prominent in the context of diabetes-associated metabolic dysregulation and should not be assumed to generalize to non-diabetic populations without confirmatory data. Prior work from this registry using the DeepFat segmentation pipeline in unselected adults has demonstrated prognostic signal for EAT radiomics in broader cohorts [[20], [33]], but whether the race-specific pattern of incremental benefit observed here replicates outside the T2DM context remains to be determined.

Finally, and most importantly, the present analysis lacks external validation. Models were developed and evaluated within a single observational cohort using internal train-test splits. The findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and reflective of incremental prognostic signal, not evidence for a validated clinical risk prediction tool.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study shows that the addition of fat-omics-based EAT features to CAC can improve cardiovascular risk estimation and reclassification in Black adults and may prove as useful markers for assessing and monitoring the progression of subclinical cardiovascular disease and risk of MACE.
