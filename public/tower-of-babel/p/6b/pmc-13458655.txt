Longitudinal circulating tumour DNA identifies patients at high risk of upstaging and recurrence in non‐muscle‐invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Can Aydogdu, Betty Wang, Sean Thomas McSweeney, Gabriela Diaz, Taeris Guzman, Sahab Ram Dewala, Rakesh Arya, Mikayla Baer, Amanda Nizam, Christopher Wee, Shalini Moningi, Alberto Pieretti, Christopher J Weight, Rebecca Campbell, Mohit Sindhani, Jihad Kaouk, Robert Abouassaly, Laura Bukavina

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate whether longitudinal tumour‐informed circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) testing identifies patients at increased risk of upstaging and recurrence in high‐risk non‐muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC).Patients and MethodsA cohort of 52 patients with high‐risk NMIBC underwent serial plasma ctDNA testing using a personalised tumour‐informed assay. The ctDNA was obtained at initial presentation and at approximately 3‐month surveillance intervals during appropriate routine clinical NMIBC therapy and follow‐up. Introduction

Non‐muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) accounts for most newly diagnosed bladder cancers and continues to present major therapeutic challenges. Recurrence is frequent and every fifth patient with high‐risk disease progresses to MIBC despite intravesical therapy [1, 2, 3]. Emerging artificial intelligence‐based pathology biomarkers show promise in identifying patients who may not respond to intravesical therapy such as BCG [4], but they currently lack prospective validation and do not guide decisions on treatment escalation. Cystoscopy and urine cytology remain the cornerstone of surveillance and are effective for assessing local bladder disease, but they provide limited insight into the underlying biological aggressiveness of tumours that appear non‐invasive at presentation or their potential for occult systemic spread.

Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) provides a minimally invasive approach to assess tumour‐derived DNA in plasma and has demonstrated prognostic and predictive utility in MIBC, including identification of molecular residual disease and selection for perioperative systemic therapy [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. In contrast, the role of ctDNA in NMIBC is not well defined. Data on its prevalence, biological relevance, and potential to guide management in earlier stage disease are limited.

We previously showed that ctDNA was detectable in about one third of patients with high‐risk NMIBC and that positivity was associated with adverse pathology and disease progression despite non‐muscle‐invasive staging [11], suggesting that some clinically defined NMIBC already reflects biologically aggressive disease.

This study evaluated a larger retrospective real‐world cohort with longer follow‐up to clarify the prevalence and clinical significance of ctDNA detection across BCG‐naïve and BCG‐exposed high‐risk NMIBC. The primary objective was to determine the rate of ctDNA positivity, with secondary objectives assessing its association with clinical or pathological upstaging and genomic features linked to ctDNA shedding.

Patients and Methods

Patients

We conducted a multicentre retrospective cohort study of consecutive adult patients with high‐risk NMIBC who underwent plasma ctDNA testing at Cleveland Clinic Ohio and Cleveland Clinic Florida between August 2024 and November 2025. The ctDNA testing was incorporated into routine clinical practice during this period and was applied broadly to consecutive patients rather than restricted to those with suspected advanced disease. The ctDNA testing was routinely offered to nearly all eligible patients with high‐risk NMIBC as part of clinical care; final testing depended on patient consent, clinician discussion, and successful assay completion. During the study period, testing was increasingly adopted as part of routine management of high‐risk NMIBC at our institution. Eligible patients had high‐risk NMIBC per AUA criteria [12]. Repeat transurethral resection of bladder tumour (TURBT) was performed in 35 of 52 patients. In all, 17 patients did not undergo repeat TURBT because 14 proceeded directly to neoadjuvant chemotherapy and/or radical cystectomy (RC), two had Ta high‐grade disease or carcinoma in situ (CIS) with adequate detrusor muscle in the initial specimen, and one did not undergo repeat TURBT for an unknown reason. All patients underwent cross‐sectional staging with CT or MRI as part of clinical evaluation. Patients were categorised as BCG‐naïve, BCG‐exposed, or BCG‐unresponsive per United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) criteria [13]. Given the limited cohort size and real‐world treatment patterns, patients with prior BCG exposure were grouped as BCG‐exposed/BCG‐unresponsive for subgroup reporting. Subtype histology/divergent differentiation was permitted if disease remained NMIBC. Comprehensive genomic profiling (Altera™, Natera, Inc., Austin, TX, USA) was available for a subset of patients. Patients with prior MIBC, upper tract, or metastatic disease were excluded. All management followed AUA and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines [12, 14, 15]. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (24‐921).

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients with detectable ctDNA in the overall cohort and within BCG‐exposure subgroups while showing concurrent non‐muscle‐invasive disease on pathology. Secondary endpoints included rates of clinical upstaging, pathological response at RC, and genomic differences according to ctDNA status. Clinical upstaging was defined as identification of clinical T stage (cT) ≥2 or node‐positive disease based on cross‐sectional imaging performed after referral to our institution, acknowledging that some cases may represent occult advanced disease present at initial presentation. When RC was performed, final pathological stage was recorded, with pathological complete response defined as pathological T stage (pT)0N0 and non‐muscle‐invasive residual disease defined as ≤pT1N0. Analyses of RC outcomes including ctDNA trajectories before and after surgery were considered exploratory because surgery was performed at clinician and patient discretion. Main triggers included exhaustion of intravesical therapy, high‐risk T1 disease with aggressive variant histology, discordant pathology and imaging suggesting advanced disease, and patient preference.

Tumour‐Informed ctDNA Assay

All patients underwent serial tumour‐informed ctDNA analysis using the personalised Signatera™ assay (Natera Inc.). For assay design, whole‐exome sequencing of formalin‐fixed‐paraffin‐embedded tumour tissue and matched normal DNA identified clonal somatic variants that were used to design an individualised multiplex PCR‐based next‐generation sequencing panel. Whole‐exome sequencing results and selected variants are not returned with the clinical ctDNA report. Plasma cell‐free DNA was then analysed for these variants to a depth of ~100 000×. A sample was classified as ctDNA‐positive when at least two patient‐specific tumour‐derived variants were detected, corresponding to an analytical specificity >99.5% and a limit of detection of 0.01% variant allele frequency. After the first sample obtained at presentation, ctDNA testing was performed at approximately 3‐month intervals during routine follow‐up. The median (interquartile range) number of samples obtained per patient was three (two–four).

Comprehensive Genomic Panel

Comprehensive genomic profiling was performed using the Altera™ tumour–normal whole‐exome sequencing platform (Natera Inc.). The assay uses hybrid‐capture sequencing with boosted coverage of ~440 clinically relevant cancer genes to detect single nucleotide variants, insertions/deletions, copy number alterations, and selected structural rearrangements. Tumour RNA sequencing was used to assess fusions. Tumour mutational burden, programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) combined positive score, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) immunohistochemistry were also reported. Genomic features were compared between ctDNA‐positive and ctDNA‐negative groups.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarised clinical characteristics, ctDNA findings, and genomic alterations. The prevalence of ctDNA‐positivity was estimated for the overall cohort and BCG‐defined subgroups. Associations between ctDNA status and clinical or pathological outcomes were assessed using odds ratios (ORs) and Fisher's exact tests; Receiver operating characteristic analyses were performed to assess the discriminatory performance of initial ctDNA status for predicting clinical and pathological upstaging. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative PV (NPV), and area under the curve (AUC) were calculated. The 95% CIs for AUC estimates were calculated using the DeLong method, while CIs for sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were calculated using the Wilson score method. Time‐to‐event outcomes were analysed using Kaplan–Meier, log‐rank, and Cox models (hazard ratios [HRs], 95% CIs); follow‐up was estimated using reverse Kaplan–Meier; gene‐level comparisons used Barnard exact tests, as exploratory analyses, no multiplicity adjustment was applied. Multivariable logistic regression and Cox models were initially considered; however, given the limited number of outcome events, adjustment for multiple covariates was not statistically reliable and carried a high risk of overfitting. Therefore, only univariable analyses were performed. A two‐sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using Python, version 3.14.2 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA).

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 52 patients with high‐risk NMIBC were included, with a median follow‐up of 8.6 months (95% CI 6.7–10.2 months). The cohort was predominantly male (83%), White (88%) and with a history of tobacco exposure (65%). Just over half of patients were BCG‐naïve at presentation, whereas 16 (30%) met criteria for BCG‐unresponsive disease. Index tumours were almost uniformly T1 high grade at initial TURBT, and concomitant CIS was present in 18% of cases. Prior intravesical treatment consisted mainly of BCG and/or intravesical chemotherapy, and 21 patients (40%) ultimately underwent RC (Table 1). Longitudinal ctDNA Outcomes

Overall, ctDNA was detectable in 17 of 52 patients (33%), including eight of 29 BCG‐naïve patients (28%) and nine of 23 BCG‐exposed or BCG‐unresponsive patients (39%). As shown in Fig. 1, longitudinal trajectories of ctDNA, treatments, and clinical events demonstrated that many patients with sustained ctDNA‐negativity during surveillance did not develop progression over the follow‐up period. In contrast, ctDNA‐positive patients frequently showed early or persistent detection of ctDNA, and in several cases ctDNA‐positivity preceded clinically evident recurrence or progression.

Clinical Upstaging and Pathological Outcomes After RC

The ctDNA status was closely associated with patterns of identification of clinical upstaging. Although all patients initially presented with cT1N0 disease, those with detectable ctDNA were much more likely to be clinically upstaged during evaluation, often to cT3 or cT4 (Fig. 2A). Overall, clinical upstaging occurred in 10 of 17 (59%) ctDNA‐positive patients compared with five of 35 (14%) ctDNA‐negative patients (P = 0.002), corresponding to 8.57‐fold higher odds of upstaging in the ctDNA‐positive group (95% CI 2.22–33.15). ctDNA positivity demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance for predicting clinical upstaging overall (AUC 0.74), with stronger discrimination among patients managed without RC (AUC 0.82). Performance characteristics are summarised in Table S2.

Among patients who proceeded to RC, ctDNA‐positive individuals were enriched for advanced pT and pN stages, with many harbouring muscle‐invasive or node‐positive disease (Fig. 3A). Pathological upstaging was observed in seven of nine (78%) ctDNA‐positive patients vs one of 12 (8%) ctDNA‐negative patients (P = 0.002; OR 38.5, 95% CI 2.92–508.46), and ctDNA positivity showed strong discriminatory performance for pathological upstaging (AUC 0.86), with high sensitivity, specificity, and NPV (Table S2), despite four of six (67%) clinically upstaged patients receiving perioperative systemic therapy following multidisciplinary evaluation for suspected advanced disease (Fig. 2A). Pathological complete response was achieved in none of ctDNA‐positive patients compared with five of 12 (42%) ctDNA‐negative patients (P = 0.045), and residual NMIBC (≤pT1N0) was present in two of nine (22%) vs 11 of 12 (92%), respectively (P = 0.002) (Fig. 2B). Thus, ctDNA‐positive patients undergoing RC were far more likely to have muscle‐invasive or node‐positive pathology, whereas ctDNA‐negative patients more commonly had pT0 or non‐muscle‐invasive residual disease.

Perioperative ctDNA Dynamics

Perioperative ctDNA dynamics were assessable in 17 patients with paired measurements before and after RC. Most exhibited stable ctDNA status across the two time points (Fig. 3B). All patients who were ctDNA‐negative preoperatively remained negative after surgery (10/10), which identifies a subgroup with consistently low detectable molecular tumour burden. Among those who were ctDNA‐positive before RC, five of the seven (71%) patients remained positive postoperatively, suggesting residual ctDNA despite resection of the bladder and lymph nodes, while two of the seven (29%) converted to ctDNA‐negative, compatible with molecular clearance. Notably, no patient with a negative pre‐RC ctDNA assay developed new postoperative ctDNA‐positivity during follow‐up. Considering all evaluable postoperative samples, 14 of 20 patients were ctDNA‐negative, yielding a postoperative negativity rate of 70%.

Distant and Intravesical Recurrence

Clinically, distant recurrence occurred in three of 17 (18%) ctDNA‐positive patients and in none of the ctDNA‐negative patients (log‐rank P = 0.01); the median lead time from first ctDNA‐positivity to distant recurrence detected on conventional imaging was 81 days, based on a limited number of events. Intravesical recurrence was also more frequent among ctDNA‐positive patients (three of 17 [27%] vs two of 35 [6%] for ctDNA‐negative), although we were unable to demonstrate a statistically significant difference (HR 2.90, 95% CI 0.48–17.40; P = 0.24) (Fig. 2C).

Genomic Association by ctDNA Status

Comprehensive genomic profiling was available for 29 tumours (Fig. S1). Among ctDNA‐negative cases, pathogenic fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) alterations were identified in seven of 18 tumours and in none of 11 ctDNA‐positive tumours (P = 0.022) (Table S3). TP53 mutations were detected in six of 18 ctDNA‐negative tumours and in eight of 11 ctDNA‐positive tumours (P = 0.049). RB1 and RBM10 alterations were present in five of 18 and four of 18 ctDNA‐negative tumours, respectively, and absent in ctDNA‐positive tumours (P = 0.08 and P = 0.11). CDKN2A and PIK3CA alterations were identified in two of 18 and six of 18 ctDNA‐negative tumours and in four of 11 and one of 11 ctDNA‐positive tumours (P = 0.12 and P = 0.15). Tumour mutational burden was evaluable in all sequenced tumours and showed a similar distribution across ctDNA groups.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the largest cohort of patients with high‐risk NMIBC who have undergone longitudinal tumour‐informed ctDNA testing with a commercially available assay. ctDNA was detectable in one third of patients, indicating that a substantial proportion of clinically non‐muscle‐invasive tumours already exhibit molecular features consistent with aggressive biology.

Although ctDNA assays are not yet standard in NMIBC, in our practice they were applied broadly to consecutive patients with high‐risk disease rather than selectively to those with suspected understaging. The distribution of clinicopathological features in this cohort is consistent with typical high‐risk NMIBC populations (Table 1). Nevertheless, unmeasured biases inherent to retrospective real‐world adoption cannot be fully excluded.

Circulating tumour DNA‐positivity was strongly associated with both clinical and pathological progression. Patients with detectable ctDNA had a markedly higher likelihood of clinical upstaging to muscle‐invasive or node‐positive disease, occurring in 59% of ctDNA‐positive patients vs 14% in ctDNA‐negative patients, who rarely progressed. Among those who underwent RC, ctDNA‐positivity was associated with adverse pathological features, including high rates of pathological upstaging and absence of pathological complete response (78% vs 8%). Distant recurrences occurred exclusively in ctDNA‐positive patients, and ctDNA detection preceded radiographic progression on conventional imaging by a median of 81 days in several cases.

Additionally, ctDNA positivity showed the strongest discrimination for pathological upstaging at RC (AUC 0.86) and for clinical upstaging among patients managed without immediate RC (AUC 0.82), supporting ctDNA as a marker of occult advanced disease rather than solely reflecting imaging‐based suspicion (Table S2).

The increasing availability of bladder‐sparing and salvage intravesical therapies has broadened management options for patients with high‐risk NMIBC. On the downside it has also created clinical uncertainty regarding when definitive treatment should be pursued. Surveillance remains anchored in cystoscopy, cytology, and periodic cross‐sectional imaging, all of which are optimised for detection of visible mucosal or radiographic disease but have limited sensitivity for micrometastatic spread. In our cohort, two of three ctDNA‐positive patients who underwent RC had imaging classified as cT1N0 prior to surgery yet were found to have ≥pT2 and/or node‐positive disease on final pathology, whereas this discordance was not observed among ctDNA‐negative patients. In these cases, ctDNA positivity identified aggressive tumour biology that may not be fully captured by standard clinical assessment and may reflect either occult advanced disease or limitations of clinical staging.

These observations highlight the need for biomarkers that refine risk stratification beyond conventional staging. When ctDNA is already positive, delaying definitive management through repeated salvage intravesical therapy risks missing a window for potentially curative intervention. Conversely, patients who remain ctDNA‐negative longitudinally demonstrated favourable pathological features and rarely progressed, suggesting that ctDNA‐negativity may help support continued bladder preservation in selected individuals. Integrating serial ctDNA testing into surveillance may therefore help tailor the timing and intensity of therapy, identifying patients who warrant early escalation to RC or systemic approaches and distinguishing them from those who may safely continue bladder‐sparing strategies.

Perioperative ctDNA dynamics further support its role as a marker of global tumour burden. All patients who were ctDNA‐negative before RC remained negative postoperatively, representing a biologically favourable subset with low metastatic risk. In contrast, most ctDNA‐positive patients remained positive after RC, suggesting residual micrometastatic disease despite removal of the bladder and regional lymph nodes. Only a minority converted from positive to negative, consistent with molecular clearance following surgery [16]. Notably, there were no instances of new postoperative ctDNA‐positivity among patients who were negative preoperatively. Together, these patterns indicate that baseline ctDNA status carries important prognostic information and that persistence of ctDNA after RC may identify patients who could benefit from intensified systemic surveillance or adjuvant therapy [7].

Genomic profiling provides additional biological context. ctDNA‐positive tumours were enriched for TP53 alterations, which are commonly associated with more aggressive, basal‐like tumour biology and progression toward muscle‐invasive disease. In contrast, ctDNA‐negative tumours more frequently carried FGFR3 alterations, a hallmark of luminal‐papillary tumours typically associated with favourable NMIBC biology [17]. These observations reinforce that ctDNA‐positivity reflects differences in underlying tumour biology rather than technical variability.

Our findings build upon a growing but still limited literature on ctDNA in NMIBC. Earlier studies have been constrained by very small cohorts, retrospective designs, and bespoke, labour‐intensive assays applied to biobanked specimens, often with incomplete clinical annotation. Investigators such as Birkenkamp‐Demtröder et al. [18] first demonstrated the feasibility of detecting plasma ctDNA in NMIBC. More recent prospective efforts, including the series by Kagathur et al. [19] and our prior real‐world analysis [11], have linked ctDNA‐positivity to progression risk. A recent study by BinHumaid et al. [20] reported ctDNA‐positivity in about 11% of patients with high‐grade pT1 NMIBC and showed that ctDNA‐positive status was strongly associated with shorter overall and disease‐specific survival. The assay was tissue or urine informed and relied on a limited set of targets with very small serum inputs, factors that likely reduced sensitivity, yet ctDNA still identified a clearly high‐risk subgroup not captured by standard clinical features. Finally, a recent study by Ben‐David et al. [21] evaluated 45 patients with high‐risk NMIBC who all proceeded to RC and found that preoperative ctDNA positivity was strongly associated with pathological upstaging, nodal involvement, and worse recurrence‐free survival after surgery, with 66.7% of ctDNA‐positive patients upstaged to ≥pT2 disease compared with 13.3% of ctDNA‐negative patients. However, that study specifically addressed risk stratification within a pre‐selected RC population, whereas our study asks a different clinical question by including patients managed both with bladder‐preserving strategies and RC, allowing assessment of ctDNA as a tool for earlier treatment escalation and longitudinal surveillance across the broader high‐risk NMIBC population. The present study extends this work by examining a larger, clinically annotated cohort with longitudinal sampling across both BCG‐naïve and BCG‐exposed/BCG‐unresponsive disease and by integrating ctDNA with detailed staging, RC outcomes, and genomic profiling.

This study has limitations. The sample size, although the largest to date, remains modest and limits subgroup analyses. Management decisions and imaging schedules were not protocolised, introducing potential confounding. Although ctDNA testing was broadly applied in clinical practice, unmeasured selection biases inherent to retrospective adoption cannot be fully excluded. In addition, the limited number of recurrence and upstaging events precluded reliable multivariable modelling, limiting assessment of the independent predictive value of ctDNA after adjustment for potential confounders. While patients were treated across multiple institutions within a single health system, the cohort was predominantly White and reflects referral patterns of a tertiary referral centre with a high proportion of complex and high‐risk cases, which may limit generalisability to broader NMIBC populations. Comprehensive genomic profiling was available only for a subset of tumours. Given the limited sample size and multiple gene‐level comparisons, these genomic findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive. Follow‐up remains relatively short for a disease that may progress over several years and is insufficient to fully characterise recurrence dynamics; longer observation will be needed to determine whether early ctDNA patterns predict long‐term metastasis‐free and cancer‐specific survival.

Despite these limitations, the findings have important clinical implications. ctDNA is detectable at clinically meaningful rates in high‐risk NMIBC and identifies a subgroup with aggressive tumour biology, high likelihood of clinical and pathological upstaging, and early recurrence. In an evolving treatment landscape with increasing reliance on bladder preservation and salvage intravesical therapy, ctDNA may offer a complementary tool for risk stratification that may help guide decisions regarding escalation to RC or systemic therapy. Prospective studies incorporating ctDNA‐guided management strategies, potentially alongside urine‐based molecular assays, are warranted to determine whether early identification of high‐risk disease can improve outcomes.

Conclusion

In this real‐world cohort of high‐risk NMIBC, ctDNA was detectable in one third of patients and identified a subgroup with a significantly higher risk of clinical and pathological upstaging and distant recurrence, while ctDNA‐negative patients rarely progressed. Several ctDNA‐positive individuals had recent imaging without nodal disease yet were found at RC to have advanced tumours, indicating that ctDNA was often the only early marker of occult spread. These findings highlight limitations of standard surveillance and support further evaluation of ctDNA as a tool for earlier therapeutic escalation in prospective studies.
