Acoustic Tweezers‐Assisted Biomaterial Molding in Petri Dishes (TAMP) for Engineering Glioma Tissues With Controlled Cellular and Extracellular Matrix Architectures
Authors: Yingshan Du, Jiali Li, Hui Liu, Julio P. Arroyo, Luyu Bo, Bowen Cai, Teng Li, Zhide Wang, Chongpeng Qiu, Rafael Davalos, Joseph Rich, Zhenhua Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTissue engineering has shown great potential for manufacturing tissue replacements and developing physiologically relevant tissue models. However, conventional tissue engineering strategies often face challenges in fabricating tissues simultaneously incorporating controlled cellular and extracellular matrix (ECM) architectures. Here, we introduce Acoustic Tweezers‐Assisted Biomaterial Molding in Petri Dishes (TAMP), enabling tissue fabrication with controlled cellular and ECM architectures in Petri dishes, specifically, producing constructs with customized ECM geometries and various internal cellular architectures, including parallel elongated cell bundles, arrays of interconnected cell spheroids, and lattice‐like cellular networks. TAMP leverages a portable acoustic array that delivers standing waves into a Petri dish or a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold, thereby enabling a unique “dual‐control” mechanism. The acoustic field arranges internal cells into parallel line‐like and lattice‐like patterns, while the PDMS mold defines the overall ECM geometry. Our approach was demonstrated by arranging micro‐objects into various patterns within different‐shaped molds and fabricating glioma tissues with various unique internal cellular architectures, including parallel elongated glioma tissue bundles, lattice‐like glioma tissue networks, and chains of interconnected glioma spheroids. We anticipate the TAMP technique will lead to portable, easy‐to‐operate tools for engineering tissues with controlled cellular and ECM architectures for biomedical research, disease modeling, and drug testing.

Introduction

Tissue engineering that can recapitulate physiologically relevant features of native tissues has shown great potential for manufacturing tissue replacements for regenerative medicine [1, 2, 3], as well as for developing tissue models for drug screening [4, 5] and disease modeling [6, 7, 8]. Ideally, engineered tissues should incorporate not only extracellular matrix (ECM) architectures but also cellular architectures that mimic those found in native tissues, as these architectures are critical for recapitulating the material properties, mechanical behaviors, and physiological functions of native tissues. For instance, oriented skeletal muscle fibers enable anisotropic material properties and directional contraction, critical for musculoskeletal motion control. Similarly, myocardial syncytia oriented in multiple directions within the ventricular wall of the heart provide mechanical reinforcement along different axes and enable efficient contraction for blood pumping. Even in tumors (e.g., breast cancer [9, 10, 11], pancreatic cancer [12, 13, 14], and glioma [15, 16, 17]), oriented or streamline‐like cellular architectures associated with collective cell migration have been observed and are considered hallmarks of malignant progression [18, 19].

To fabricate tissues with controlled internal cellular architectures, existing strategies typically exploit nanotopography [20, 21, 22, 23], post‐fabrication mechanical stretching [24, 25], biochemical cues [26, 27, 28], as well as externally applied electromagnetic fields [29, 30, 31, 32]. Among these approaches, groove substrate‐based nanotopography utilizes physical confinement to guide cell growth and fabricate tissues with oriented cells. Post‐fabrication mechanical stretching exploits strain‐induced reorientation of cells within prefabricated tissues that initially contain randomly oriented cells. Magnetic techniques employ externally applied magnetic fields to drive the directional motion of cells labeled with magnetic‐responsive particles, thereby controlling cellular orientation. Given the success of these strategies in arranging cellular architectures, they still face several challenges. For instance, microgroove‐based methods require cells to grow on customized grooved substrates, time‐consuming processes for fabricating grooved substrates, and different substrates for different cellular arrangements. Mechanical stretching, as a post‐processing approach, cannot be directly integrated into the initial tissue fabrication process and is limited to reorienting cells, making it difficult to generate more complex cellular architectures. Magnetic techniques require pre‐fabrication labeling of cells with exogenous magnetically responsive particles, which are difficult to remove from the fabricated tissues and may compromise cell viability, alter cellular phenotypes, and affect tissue properties. Additionally, it remains challenging to precisely control magnetic field patterns, thereby limiting the capability of creating complex cellular organizations such as interconnected spheroid arrays and cellular networks.

Acoustics‐based cell manipulation technologies, such as acoustic tweezers, have garnered substantial interest for the development of contactless, biocompatible devices for cell separation, patterning, alignment, concentration, and sorting [33, 34, 35, 36]. Recently, acoustic technologies have demonstrated great potential in arranging cell distributions for engineering various tissues [34, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42], such as neural [43], muscle [44], cardiac [45], and vascular tissues [46] for regenerative medicine and tissue model development. For instance, utilizing standing surface acoustic waves generated by interdigital transducers, Kang et al. [46] aligned endothelial cells in a microfluidic chamber to manufacture oriented vascular structures. Leveraging standing bulk acoustic waves generated by thickness‐resonance‐mode piezoelectric transducers, Armstrong et al. [47] aligned myoblasts in customized ink chambers to manufacture anisotropic muscle tissues. In addition to engineering‐oriented tissues through acoustic cell alignment, standing waves can generate potential wells to aggregate cells into clusters for developing breast cancer spheroids [48] and fibroblastic tumor microtissues [49]. Despite the capabilities of existing acoustics‐assisted tissue engineering methods, there is a lack of portable and easy‐to‐operate acoustic devices that can be readily adopted by biomedical researchers to fabricate complex tissues with controlled cellular and ECM architectures. Previous acoustic approaches typically required customized fluidic chambers for cell arrangement, cannot be readily implemented in Petri dishes (a commonly used laboratory supply), and face challenges to simultaneously control the ECM geometries and internal cellular arrangements. In addition, the functions and applications of acoustics‐assisted tissue engineering need to be extended. For example, no acoustic methods have yet recapitulated the anisotropic features observed in gliomas, which are essential for modeling glioma invasion.

In this study, we present an acoustics‐assisted tissue engineering approach, namely Acoustic Tweezers‐Assisted Biomaterial Molding in Petri Dishes (TAMP), which addresses the limitations of previous acoustic methods and offers the following key features. First, TAMP offers the capability to fabricate customized tissues with simultaneous control over ECM geometries and internal cellular architectures, for example, fabricating tissues with parallel cell bundles, organized arrays of interconnected cell spheroids, and lattice‐like cellular networks. Second, TAMP employs a portable, easy‐to‐use acoustic array device that is compatible with commonly used Petri dishes, enabling tissue engineering directly within Petri dishes. This capability minimizes contamination risks and eliminates the need for specialized microfluidic chambers that are typically required in prior acoustic approaches [50, 51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56]. Third, TAMP enables the fabrication of uniquely architected glioma tissues, including parallel elongated glioma tissue bundles, interconnected and differently oriented cell bundles, lattice‐like glioma tissue networks, and spheroid arrays with reinforced protrusion‐based inter‐spheroid connections, such as ring‐shaped networks and long chains of interconnected spheroids. Therefore, TAMP not only fills the gap in acoustics‐assisted glioma tissue engineering but also achieves architected glioma tissue models with features that have not been realized using conventional fabrication methods, such as nanotopography [57, 58, 59], magnetic alignment [60], and mechanical loading [61]. With these capabilities, we anticipate that TAMP will facilitate the development of portable, easy‐to‐use, multifunctional devices for fabricating architected biomimetic tissues with controlled cellular architectures and ECM geometries for biomedical research, disease modeling, and drug testing.

To achieve the aforementioned tissue engineering capabilities, in particular, TAMP leverages a customized portable acoustic array that can route acoustic waves through the bottom wall of a Petri dish positioned at the center of the array, thereby generating standing waves within the dish and enabling contactless, label‐free patterning of cells preloaded into the dish. Notably, effective cell patterning can still be achieved when cells are loaded into an open‐top polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold placed inside the Petri dish. Accordingly, when a cell‐laden collagen solution is loaded into the mold for tissue fabrication, TAMP provides a unique “dual‐control” mechanism: the mold physically defines the overall tissue geometry, while the acoustic field simultaneously controls the internal microscopic cellular architecture. After fabrication of a collagen hydrogel containing acoustically arranged cells, the cell‐laden hydrogel is subsequently cultured for tissue development. To validate our TAMP technique, our acoustic array module was characterized and demonstrated by arranging microscale objects preloaded into Petri dishes or PDMS molds into various configurations, including parallel line‐like patterns and lattice‐like patterns. Furthermore, the TAMP approach was successfully demonstrated by fabricating glioma tissues with various cellular architectures, such as parallel elongated glioma tissue bundles, lattice‐like glioma tissue networks, and chains of interconnected glioma spheroids. Notably, these distinct tissue morphologies were found to correlate with the initial cell arrangements established by acoustic patterning prior to culture.

Results and Discussion

Mechanisms of TAMP

The TAMP approach leverages an array of piezoelectric transducers to generate bulk acoustic waves in a Petri dish for arranging cell distributions during the in‐Petri‐dish biomaterial molding process, thereby achieving the fabrication of cell‐laden biomaterials, which have both customized geometries that depend on the mold geometries, as well as customized internal cell arrangements that depend on the generated acoustic fields. To achieve such tissue fabrication functionality, we designed a setup (illustrated in Figure 1a,b) composed of (i) a glass‐bottom Petri dish, (ii) a customized PDMS mold placed inside the dish, (iii) a piezoelectric transducer array surrounding the dish, (iv) an ultrasound coupling layer (e.g., a thin deionized water layer) under the dish and in contact with the transducers, as well as (v) a 3D printed fixture to hold the positions of the dish and transducer array. Detailed processes for building this setup with the aforementioned key components are presented in the Experimental Section. Note that the PDMS mold and transducer array in the setup are all customizable and interchangeable, thereby enabling the fabrication of cell‐laden biomaterials with various desired matrix material geometries and internal cellular arrangements. For example, as shown by test setup photos in Figure S1, the piezoelectric transducer array can be configured in several ways, including configurations with two orthogonal pairs of transducers, two transducer pairs with an intersection angle of 60°, and three transducer pairs with a 60° intersection angle between adjacent pairs.

(b) Working mechanism of in‐Petri‐dish acoustic cell patterning: acoustic waves generated by transducers outside the Petri dish propagate through the coupling layer and the dish bottom into the collagen‐cell solution, where counter‐propagating waves form standing waves with periodically distributed potential wells at pressure nodes to trap and pattern cells. (c) Mechanism of acoustics‐assisted tissue engineering: randomly distributed cells in a collagen solution are patterned by acoustic waves during the collagen gelation, yielding a collagen hydrogel construct containing an immobilized, acoustically arranged internal cell pattern. (d) Fabrication of tissues with different ECM shapes and internal cellular architectures. Different PDMS molds (i.e., none, triangular, hexagonal, circular) can be used to control the hydrogel shapes, while the acoustic array configurations, transducer ON/OFF states, and operation frequencies can be used to adjust the internal cell patterns. For example, when only exciting one pair of transducers, hydrogel constructs containing parallel line‐like cell patterns can be fabricated (see the two schematics on the left). When exciting two transducer pairs with 90° and 60° intersection angles, hydrogel constructs containing lattice‐like cell patterns can be fabricated (see the two schematics on the right). The collagen hydrogels with patterned cells are subsequently cultured for tissue formation, thereby obtaining tissues containing controlled ECM shapes and internal cellular architectures.

To implement our TAMP‐assisted molding approach for the fabrication of customized tissues containing acoustically patterned cells, we followed key steps summarized below. In the first step, a prefabricated, customized PDMS mold was placed inside a Petri dish, followed by adding a cell‐laden solution into the mold's chamber. Note that this study focuses on using the collagen‐cell solution, as collagen is a key ECM composition in native tissues and allows for achieving better biomimetic physiological environments and cell growth compared to other matrices, such as the photopolymerizable gelatin methacrylate (GelMA) matrix material used in UV exposure‐based bioprinting. Detailed processes for preparing the cell‐laden collagen solution can be found in the Experimental Section.

In the second step, one pair (or multiple pairs) of piezoelectric transducers was excited to generate acoustic waves in the cell‐laden collagen solution, thereby applying acoustic radiation forces to arrange randomly distributed cells in the collagen solution into organized distributions such as cells aligned along parallel lines (see Figure 1d, left) and cells distributed in a lattice‐like pattern (see Figure 1d, right). Different from most bulk acoustic wave‐based cell patterning devices [44, 47, 62, 63, 64], which require transducers to be placed in direct contact with the cell‐laden solution to generate in‐solution acoustic waves, or to be physically bonded to the side walls of the solution chamber to transmit waves into the solution, our device employs transducers positioned apart from the Petri dish. This design offers advantages such as a reduced risk of solution contamination, simplified operation without the need to physically attach transducers to the chamber side walls, and the ability to easily replace the Petri dish while reusing the transducers for different experiments. As illustrated by the mechanism schematic in Figure 1b, our approach leverages a thin coupling layer (e.g., water), which is under the dish and in contact with the transducers placed on the sides. Through this coupling layer, transducers can transmit into the dish's glass bottom and then into the cell solution inside the dish. Within the solution, the interaction of acoustic waves propagating in counter directions creates standing waves, which have periodically distributed potential wells at pressure nodes functioning as noncontact microscale tweezers to trap cells and arrange their distribution. After the collagen gelation in ∼5 min, a hydrogel construct containing acoustically arranged cells is fabricated (illustrated in Figure 1c). Additionally, during acoustic cell arrangement, the piezoelectric transducers generated a small amount of heat that slightly increased the temperature of the collagen‐cell suspension, for example, from 23°C to ∼26.7°C when four transducers were excited using inputs with the same voltage (14 V) and frequency (3.2 MHz). This temperature rise barely affected cell viability and final hydrogel storage modulus [65, 66, 67], while it accelerated the gelation process (i.e., the transformation from a solution to a hydrogel), consistent with previous studies that found collagen undergoes entropy‐driven self‐assembly when the temperature of a collagen solution rises slightly above 25°C [68, 69].

In the third step, the fabricated collagen hydrogel, which contains acoustically arranged cells, is transferred to an incubator for culture and tissue formation. Note that the TAMP tissue fabrication process (from adding the collagen‐cell solution to cell patterning and tissue culture) can be completed within the same Petri dish. Moreover, the TAMP approach allows for fabricating various tissue constructs, which have desired ECM geometries by customizing the PDMS molds, as well as versatile internal cell patterns by changing the input signals and/or arrangements of acoustic transducers. As illustrated in Figure 1d, tissue constructs with triangular, hexagonal, and circular geometries can be fabricated by using different PDMS molds to shape the collagen hydrogel. Moreover, when exciting a pair of transducers, a parallel line‐like pattern with cells aligned along pressure node lines can be constructed. When exciting two orthogonal pairs of transducers, our approach can create a rectangular lattice‐like pattern composed of cells grouped in numerous clusters, which are trapped in acoustic potential wells distributed on a 2D rectangular grid. When using two transducer pairs with a crossing angle of 60°, an oblique lattice‐like pattern of cell clusters can be constructed. With the aforementioned features, we believe that our TAMP approach can facilitate the fabrication of anisotropic tissues, the construction of tumor models, and the investigation of the effects of acoustically arranged cell patterns on tissue formation.

In‐Petri‐Dish and In‐Mold Cell Patterning

A key feature of our TAMP approach is acoustically arranging cells suspended in pre‐gel solutions, which are preloaded into a Petri dish with or without a PDMS mold, without direct contact between the acoustic transducers and the cell‐laden solutions. To clarify how the acoustic waves generated outside the Petri dish are transmitted into the culture medium layer, acoustic‐structure interaction finite element simulations are performed with a two‐dimensional (2D) model in the x–z plane (Figure 2a). The model consists of a culture medium layer, a glass layer, and an ultrasonic coupling layer. Prescribed pressure boundary conditions are applied to the two lateral sides of the coupling layer to represent counter‐propagating acoustic excitation from the external transducers. The simulation shows that the prescribed pressure excitation forms standing waves in the coupling layer and excites a periodic out‐of‐plane velocity field in the thin glass layer (Figure 2b). This glass layer acts as a leaky acoustic coupling pathway between the coupling layer and the culture medium layer. As a result, a periodic pressure field is formed in the culture medium layer. The cut‐line profiles extracted above and below the glass layer (Figure 2c) show that the pressure in the culture medium is moderately reduced relative to that in the coupling layer, indicating substantial acoustic transmission through the thin glass layer. Another feature is that our TAMP approach allows for arranging cells into various organized distributions, such as parallel line‐like and lattice‐like patterns, by changing the number of excited transducer pairs and the transducer excitation signals. To validate these features, a TAMP platform with two orthogonally arranged transducer pairs was used to arrange U251 glioma cells. As illustrated by the test setup in Figure S2, a dual‐channel function generator was adopted to generate two signals at frequencies fx and fy , and these signals are then amplified and applied to piezoelectric transducers arranged along x‐ and y‐ axes, respectively. By controlling the ON/OFF of the function generator's two channels and changing their frequencies, our TAMP platform can generate different acoustic fields, as shown in Figure 2d–h, predicted by analytical simulations (see Section S1 for the simulation method). Therefore, the TAMP platform can arrange cells in different distributions, as confirmed by the acquired microscopy images in Figure 2i–m.

For example, when only exciting the transducer pair along the y‐axis with the frequency fy set to 3.2 MHz, the generated standing acoustic waves have pressure node lines along the x‐direction (see blue regions in Figure 2d, top) and they apply acoustic radiation forces (vector fields in Figure 2d, bottom) on cells to translate them to pressure node lines, thereby aligning cells along the x‐direction and constructing a parallel line‐like cell pattern (see Figure 2i). The spacing between adjacent lines of aligned cells is ∼248 µm, agreeing with the half‐wavelength spacing between pressure node lines at the test frequency of 3.2 MHz. By increasing the excitation frequency to 5.4 and 7.5 MHz, denser distributions of pressure node lines are generated (see acoustic fields in Figure 2e,f), leading to smaller cell alignment spacings of ∼146 and ∼106 µm, respectively (see cell patterns in Figure 2j,k). Interestingly, at the highest excitation frequency of 7.5 MHz, the microscopic image in Figure 2k (top) shows multiple rows of single cells, like multiple parallel‐arranged pearl chains. Additionally, by increasing the initial cell seeding density, more cells are trapped along pressure node lines, thereby forming larger‐width lines of aligned cells, as shown by the microscopic images in Figure 2i–k (bottom). We believe the above demonstrated in‐Petri‐dish cell alignment function can facilitate the fabrication of oriented cell bundles and anisotropic tissues.

When the two transducer pairs along x‐ and y‐axes are excited at frequencies fx = 3.20 MHz and fy = 3.18 MHz, respectively, our TAMP platform generates standing waves with an array of potential wells distributed in a square lattice‐like pattern (see Figure 2g, top). The resulting acoustic radiation force vectors point to the center of each potential well (see Figure 2g, bottom); consequently, cells originally distributed near the well should be grouped at the well's center, forming a cell cluster. As confirmed by the acquired microscopic image in Figure 2l, cells assemble into multiple clusters distributed in a square lattice‐like pattern, agreeing with the potential well distribution. The dynamic process of standing acoustic wave‐induced patterning of micro‐sized objects in a Petri dish can be seen from Movie S1. When increasing the excitation frequencies to fx = 5.40 MHz and fy = 5.41 MHz, the generated standing waves have more potential wells with smaller sizes distributed in a lattice‐like pattern with smaller spacings in x‐ and y‐ directions (see acoustic field in Figure 2h, top), thereby constructing more cell clusters with smaller sizes trapped by acoustic potential wells (see microscopic image in Figure 2m, top). Additionally, when increasing the initial cell seeding density, more cells are trapped in individual potential wells, thereby constructing larger cell clusters, as shown by the microscopic images in Figure 2l,m (bottom). Beyond the above demonstrated square lattice‐like cell patterns, other lattice‐like patterns (e.g., pattern in Figure S3) can also be constructed by changing the excitation frequencies of the two transducer pairs. We believe the above demonstrated functions, in‐Petri‐dish cell patterning and cell cluster construction, can facilitate the fabrication of cell spheroids and the study of the inter‐spheroid interaction.

We further demonstrated the ability to achieve acoustic patterning of micro‐objects preloaded into PDMS molds with different shapes, such as hexagonal and circular inner geometries. A collagen solution containing dispersed Fe3O4 microparticles (30 µm, Alpha Chemicals) was used for the demonstration, as the acoustically constructed patterns of Fe3O4 microparticles can more easily be observed without microscopic imaging, compared to cell patterns. Figure 2n shows a fabricated circular‐shaped collagen hydrogel containing Fe3O4 microparticles in a parallel line‐like pattern constructed by standing acoustic waves generated from a pair of transducers along the y‐axis. Figure 2o shows a fabricated hexagonal‐shaped collagen hydrogel, which contains Fe3O4 microparticles in a lattice‐like pattern constructed by exciting both pairs of transducers. These particle patterns are less ideal than the simulated potential well distributions, possibly attributed to particle–particle interaction, sedimentation, acoustic streaming, and mold boundary‐induced acoustic reflections/scattering. The results in Figure 2 confirm a critical feature of our TAMP approach: micro‐objects in a collagen solution can be effectively arranged by acoustic waves even when a PDMS structure and a Petri dish are present between the solution and the acoustic transducers. This is because our TAMP approach sends acoustic waves into the solution through the Petri dish's thin glass bottom and the liquid coupling layer under the dish. The results presented in this subsection demonstrate that our TAMP approach can generate acoustic waves to arrange micro‐sized objects (e.g., cells and microparticles) in Petri dishes and PDMS molds into various distributions, such as parallel line‐like and lattice‐like patterns, and to fabricate collagen hydrogels containing different internal patterns of micro‐objects. In the following, our TAMP approach was further demonstrated by fabricating glioma tissues with various acoustically achieved cellular architectures, such as anisotropic glioma tissues containing multiple parallelly arranged glioma cell bundles, a lattice‐like interconnected network of glioma cells, as well as arrays of interconnected glioma spheroids.

Fabrication for Oriented Collagen‐Matrix Glioma Tissues

Our TAMP approach was applied for the fabrication of oriented collagen‐matrix glioma tissues containing parallel‐aligned, elongated glioma tissue bundles. First, collagen hydrogel constructs containing parallel line‐like cell patterns were fabricated, while one pair of acoustic transducers was excited to generate in‐Petri‐dish standing acoustic waves and align U251 glioma cells suspended in a collagen solution. Then the fabricated hydrogel constructs were cultured for cell growth and tissue formation. Through microscopic imaging, immunofluorescent staining, and Fourier transform‐based image analysis, we analyzed the fabricated glioma tissues to better understand the acoustic effects on the cell distribution, viability, and elongation, tissue morphology, as well as glioma cell bundle formation and orientation. Detailed procedures for the collagen‐cell solution preparation, tissue culture, and those characterization methods are presented in the Experimental Section.

Before using the collagen‐glioma cell suspension for tissue fabrication, we first evaluated the effect of acoustic waves generated by our TAMP platform on cell viability, by exposing cells to standing acoustic waves, culturing the cells, and then conducting the live/dead cell viability assays at 24 and 48 h. The excitation voltage of acoustic transducers and the acoustic exposure duration were set to 14 V and 2 min, respectively, which were typically used in our TAMP fabrication method. As shown by the viability analysis results in Figure 3e, glioma cells in both the control (acoustic transducers OFF) and experimental (acoustic transducers ON) groups exhibited high cell viability (>90%), indicating a negligible effect of acoustic waves on cell viability.

Then we demonstrated the TAMP‐based fabrication of a collagen hydrogel construct containing a parallel line‐like pattern of acoustically aligned glioma cells, and analyzed the effect of acoustic cell alignment on glioma cell elongation. We utilized a pair of transducers to generate standing acoustic waves to arrange glioma cells in a 2.5 mg mL−1 collagen solution. Compared to the control group (acoustics OFF) with cells randomly distributed (see microscopic image in Figure 3a, left), the test group with acoustic transducers turned ON shows that glioma cells within the collagen matrix were successfully aligned by the generated standing acoustic waves to form multiple periodically distributed lines nearly in parallel (see Figure 3c, left). After 3 days of culture, the effect of acoustic cell alignment on glioma cell elongation direction was analyzed by comparing the cell distributions and morphologies between the control and test groups. In the control group, cells remained randomly distributed and extended in various directions within the collagen hydrogel (see Figure 3b, left). In contrast, in the acoustics‐ON group, cells remained aligned and exhibited directional elongation along the acoustic alignment direction, predominantly (see Figure 3d, left). For further comparison, the acquired microscopic images were processed through multi‐dimensional Fourier transform to obtain their wavenumber spectra, which were used to analyze the overall tissue directionality and cell arrangement periodicity. In the acoustics‐ON group (Figure 3c,d, right), the spectra exhibit symmetrically arranged high‐intensity spots at 90° and 270°, indicating that the cell arrangement has periodicity along the 90° (or 270°) direction and overall directionality with cells oriented along the 0° (or 180°) direction. In contrast, the spectra of the control group (Figure 3a,b, right) do not show these high‐intensity spots, indicating the absence of overall periodicity or tissue directionality.

To further assess the effects of acoustic cell alignment on glioma tissue formation and morphology, the fabricated cell‐laden hydrogels were cultured on days 3 and 7, followed by characterization through immunofluorescent staining, fluorescent microscopic imaging, and Fourier transform‐based directionality analysis. Figure 3f,h presents fluorescent microscopy images of fabricated glioma tissues in the control and experimental groups (without and with acoustic waves), respectively. In these images, the nuclei were labeled by DAPI (blue) staining. The glioma‐specific glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) was labeled by a polyclonal GFAP fluorescent antibody (green) to reveal the glioma cell cytoskeleton and analyze the formation of elongated and reinforced protrusions. In the control group without using acoustic waves, glioma cells in the collagen matrix remained randomly and loosely distributed with no obvious elongation up to Day 3 (see Figure 3f). On Day 7, although some cells exhibited protrusions in random directions, they didn't form long interconnected structures or directional cell bundles (see Figure 3g). In contrast, in the acoustics‐ON group on Day 3 (see Figure 3h), cells initially aligned along pressure node lines formed multiple long, oriented bundles of interconnected cells. This observation indicates that acoustic cell alignment can facilitate the formation of cell bundles with enhanced directional intercellular connections and improved overall directionality compared with the control group without acoustic alignment. The acquired confocal images on Day 7 (Figure 3i) show more and longer protrusions connecting cells along the acoustic alignment direction. Some protrusions also bridge adjacent cell bundles (see Figure 3i), thereby forming a large glioma cell network composed of interconnected cell bundles, implying the potential for achieving enhanced communications in tissues constructed using our TAMP approach. To characterize tissue directionality (or anisotropy), we applied a multi‐dimensional Fourier transform to the acquired fluorescent microscopic images. The resulting wavenumber spectra for the acoustics‐ON group (Figure 3h,i, right) exhibit directional high‐intensity regions (i.e., symmetrically arranged high‐intensity spots), indicating the successful fabrication of directional (or anisotropic) tissues. In contrast, the control group does not show clear directionality (Figure 3f,g, right). Additionally, the overall directionality of the fabricated tissue can be quantitatively determined as the direction orthogonal to the high‐spectrum‐intensity direction. For example, when the high‐intensity spectral direction is 0° (or 180°), as shown in Figure 3h, the corresponding overall tissue directionality is 90° (or 270°).

The acquired confocal images reveal that cells are densely packed to form multiple cell bundles, as indicated by the densely arranged nuclei stained with DAPI (blue). The GFAP staining (green) reveals the formation of long cell protrusions mostly oriented along the bundle's axial direction, enabling intercellular physical connections. The cross‐sectional and 3D views confirm that cells are not only arranged in a 2D plane but also distributed in the thickness direction of the collagen matrix, indicating that the glioma tissue fabricated using our acoustic approach forms a 3D cellular architecture. For further characterization of the tissue morphology, we analyzed the image intensity distributions along two paths illustrated by the red lines in Figure 4d. For Path 1 along a cell bundle's axial direction, the intensity profile (Figure 4e, left) shows relatively high values in the cell bundle region, indicating densely packed cells. The intensity profile (Figure 4e, right) along Path 2 shows four peaks corresponding to the positions crossing the four cell bundles. The average spacing between adjacent peaks is measured at 146 µm, which agrees with a half wavelength of the generated acoustic waves at 5.4 MHz. In contrast, cells in the control‐group tissue fabricated without using acoustic waves are randomly distributed (see Figure 4f), and their protrusions are irregularly oriented without forming parallelly arranged long cell bundles like those in Figure 4g, containing protrusions oriented in nearly the same direction. The directionality analysis results for these two samples, obtained through multidimensional Fourier transform, are compared in Figure S4, further confirming clear directionality in the acoustics‐ON group and the absence of overall directionality in the control group.

Interestingly, we found that the acoustic cell alignment can promote the formation of longer and wider (or more robust) cellular connections compared to the control group without using acoustic waves. For example, the widths of cell bundles in the acoustics‐ON group are generally wider than cellular connection widths in the control group. To quantitatively compare the differences between tissue morphologies of acoustics‐ON and control groups, we measured the widths of cellular connections at fifteen positions (indicated by red lines in Figure S5) selected from each confocal image. As shown in the box plot (Figure 4h), the cellular connection widths in the control group range from 13.7 to 53.9 µm with an average of 32.4 µm, while in the acoustics‐ON group, the fabricated cell bundles have increased widths, ranging from 38.0 to 77.7 µm with an average of 50.6 µm. Additionally, we measured the lengths of the cell bundles fabricated using our acoustic approach and found that the lengths range from 642.0 to 2911.1 µm with an average of 1510.3 µm (see Figure 4i); however, such long cell bundles were not observed from the control group and previous tissue engineering studies [70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75].

Lattice‐Like Tissue Networks and Interconnected Spheroid Arrays

Besides tissues with parallelly aligned cell bundles, we fabricated and characterized a tissue containing interconnected cell bundles in lattice‐like architectures (see Figure 5a). To fabricate this tissue, standing acoustic waves generated by two transducer pairs with an intersection angle of 60° were used to arrange cell distributions before gelation, and then the fabricated collagen hydrogel containing patterned cells was cultured for tissue network formation. The acquired confocal images on Day 3 (see Figure 5a–c) confirm the successful fabrication of a 3D cellular architecture composed of multiple cell bundles oriented in two primary directions (e.g., 90° and 150°). The image intensity profiles along two paths (illustrated by red lines in Figure 5d) both show multiple high‐intensity regions at locations where the paths cross cell bundles, as shown in Figure 5e, further evidence confirming the fabrication of cell bundles in different orientations when using standing waves generated by two pairs of transducers. Moreover, the GFAP staining (green in Figure 5a) reveals long cell protrusions along these two directions, enabling physical connections among the cell bundles to form an interconnected cellular network. The 3D rotational visualization of glioma tissues (see Movie S2 and S3) engineered by our TAMP approach clearly exhibits in‐plane and out‐of‐plane intercellular connections, confirming the formation of a 3D interconnected cellular network.

We also demonstrated that our TAMP approach could facilitate the fabrication of numerous cell spheroids and the formation of cell protrusions critical for constructing inter‐spheroid physical connections (see Figure 5f,g). To fabricate these tissues, cells seeded in a collagen solution were grouped into multiple clusters distributed in a square lattice‐like configuration by using acoustic potential wells generated by two orthogonal pairs of transducers. After gelation, hydrogels containing patterned cell clusters were constructed and then cultured for 3 days for the formation of cell spheroids with protrusions that enable physical connections between adjacent spheroids. The acquired confocal images on Day 3 (see Figure 5f) confirm the fabrication of multiple spheroids distributed in a square lattice‐like configuration, as well as the generation of elongated protrusions from spheroids, allowing for the construction of inter‐spheroid physical connections. Interestingly, the protrusions from one spheroid can extend toward neighboring spheroids in different directions, thereby creating a ring‐shaped cell network with multiple interconnected spheroids, as shown in Figure 5f (right). The protrusions can also physically connect a row of spheroids to create a long chain (length: ∼975 µm) of interconnected spheroids (123.7 ± 58.1 µm, n = 9), like multiple keys joined by bridges, as shown by the acquired high‐magnification bright‐field microscopic images in Figure 5g. Moreover, these images reveal the formation of multiple protrusion‐based bridges between adjacent spheroids, critical for achieving reinforced inter‐spheroid connections.

Additionally, experiments were performed to demonstrate that the TAMP‐fabricated anisotropic hydrogels containing aligned cell bundles enable directional intercellular communication and small‐molecule transport. For these experiments, NIH3T3 fibroblasts, which are commonly used in gap junction studies [76, 77], were employed. During cell ink preparation, donor cells were preloaded with Calcein‐AM, a low‐molecular‐weight fluorescent tracer capable of passing through functional gap junctions. The Calcein‐loaded donor cells were then mixed with non‐labeled recipient cells at a ratio of 1:4 in the collagen precursor solution prior to TAMP fabrication. The TAMP approach was then used to construct hydrogels containing multiple parallelly aligned bundles composed of interconnected cells. Microscopic images (Figure 5h, bright‐field images overlaid with fluorescence images) were acquired after 0, 12, 24, and 48 h of culture. To minimize photobleaching artifacts, independent samples were imaged at each time point using identical microscopy settings. At 0 h, a small number of Calcein‐positive donor cells were randomly distributed within the acoustically constructed cell bundles. As the culture time increased to 12, 24, and 48 h, an increasing number of cells within the bundles exhibited green fluorescence, and the fluorescence regions became increasingly continuous along the bundles. The time‐dependent expansion of the Calcein signal along the cell bundles suggests progressive intercellular transfer of low‐molecular‐weight molecules through physically connected cells. The directional evolution of fluorescence along the aligned bundles suggests that the TAMP‐constructed cellular patterns may facilitate studies of cell–cell communication in engineered anisotropic tissue models.

The above demonstration and characterization results confirm that our TAMP approach can construct glioma tissues with various unique cellular architectures, such as 3D cellular networks with interconnected, differently oriented cell bundles, as well as spheroid arrays with reinforced protrusion‐based inter‐spheroid connections. It has been known that intercellular physical contacts are critical in facilitating signal transduction, providing cell growth/migration guidance, as well as supporting tissue‐level functionalities [78, 79]. Therefore, we expect that our engineered tissues with reinforced protrusion‐based physical connections potentially enable enhanced signal transduction. Additionally, we believe that our TAMP approach can contribute to the development of oriented glioma models containing aligned long cell bundles, as well as functional tissues with 3D cellular networks and interconnected spheroids.

Conclusion

This study presents an acoustics‐assisted tissue engineering approach, namely TAMP, which enables the fabrication of structured tissues with simultaneous control over cellular architectures and tissue geometries directly in commonly used Petri dishes, for example, tissue constructs with customizable overall shapes and various internal cellular architectures, such as elongated parallel cell bundles, arrays of interconnected spheroids, and lattice‐like interconnected cellular networks. To achieve this tissue fabrication capability, in particular, TAMP leverages a portable, easy‐to‐use, reconfigurable acoustic array surrounding a Petri dish, which is capable of delivering acoustic waves into an open‐top PDMS mold placed within the Petri dish to arrange the distribution of cells in a solution preloaded into the mold. This functionality enables TAMP‐based tissue fabrication through a unique “dual‐control” mechanism, in which the PDMS mold physically defines the overall tissue geometry, while acoustic waves regulate the internal microscopic cellular architecture. Accordingly, by using PDMS molds with different shapes, tissues with distinct overall geometries can be fabricated. In parallel, by tuning the acoustic array input signals and/or changing the arrangement of piezoelectric elements to modify the generated acoustic fields, various internal cellular arrangements can be achieved, including parallel line‐like and lattice‐like cell patterns.

To characterize and demonstrate the TAMP approach, first, we showed that its acoustic array can generate standing acoustic waves in Petri dishes and PDMS molds to achieve in‐Petri‐dish and in‐mold cell patterning. By adjusting the input signals and configurations of the acoustic array, cells were arranged into a variety of organized distributions, including parallel line‐like and lattice‐like patterns. These cell patterns closely match the potential well distributions predicted by analytical simulations, indicating the feasibility of predicting the acoustically arranged cell patterns. Second, the TAMP approach was demonstrated by fabricating oriented tissues, such as parallel, elongated glioma tissue bundles. To fabricate these tissue bundles, glioma cells were aligned and arranged into parallel line‐like patterns using standing acoustic waves, followed by culture for cell growth and tissue formation. Notably, we found that the initial cell alignment induced by acoustic waves facilitates the formation of elongated tissue bundles with reinforced protrusion‐mediated cell‐cell connections. Our observed single bundles exhibit lengths of up to 2911.1 µm, which exceed those reported in previous tissue engineering studies [70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75]. Third, using the TAMP approach, we successfully fabricated glioma tissues with unique internal cellular architectures, including lattice‐like networks with interconnected, differently oriented cell bundles, ring‐shaped networks of interconnected glioma spheroids, and long chains of interconnected glioma spheroids. To the best of our knowledge, such glioma tissue architectures have not been achieved in previous studies [80, 81, 82, 83]. Interestingly, in our fabricated glioma tissues, we observed that protrusions from individual glioma spheroids can extend toward neighboring spheroids in different directions, forming ring‐shaped networks of interconnected spheroids. These protrusions can also physically connect a series of spheroids to create long, interconnected spheroid chains, resembling multiple bridged keys. Such structured glioma tissues may facilitate the investigation of inter‐spheroid signal transduction. We believe that our TAMP approach can contribute to the development of oriented glioma models featuring aligned, elongated cell bundles, as well as functional tissues with 3D cellular networks and interconnected spheroids. The interconnected cellular networks may provide structural pathways for glioma cell–cell communication and collective invasion. The aligned cell bundles and protrusion‐based intercellular bridges can potentially guide directional migration and facilitate signal propagation between neighboring cells or spheroids. We anticipate that the cellular structures engineered using our TAMP approach will provide useful in vitro models for studying glioma invasion dynamics, calcium‐wave propagation, gap‐junction‐associated molecular exchange, and therapeutic responses. Further functional assays will be performed to investigate these processes in future studies.

The TAMP approach distinguishes itself from previous physical field‐assisted methods. Compared with magnetic field‐assisted biofabrication methods that require labeling cells with magnetically responsive particles to manipulate cells [29, 30, 31, 32], the TAMP approach is free of any labeling particles, which are difficult to remove from fabricated tissues and may potentially affect cell phenotype and tissue properties. Compared with methods assisted by optical tweezers [84, 85, 86, 87], bulk wave acoustic tweezers, although offering a lower cell arrangement resolution, use a simple, easy‐to‐operate system with better scalability and penetration depth. Compared with other acoustic methods, the TAMP approach, although unable to perform selective cell manipulation like acoustic vortex beam‐based methods [88] or construct complex cell patterns like acoustic holography [89], emphasizes the use of a portable, easy‐to‐operate, Petri‐dish‐compatible configuration for simultaneous control of ECM geometry and internal cell architecture. Additionally, this work extends acoustic tissue engineering approaches to glioma tissue engineering and the construction of long chains of interconnected spheroids.

As validated by our experiments, our introduced TAMP approach offers several critical features. First, it enables the fabrication of customized tissues with simultaneous control over internal cellular architectures and ECM geometries. Second, both acoustic cell patterning and tissue fabrication can be performed directly within Petri dishes, thereby minimizing contamination risks and eliminating the need for specialized microfluidic chambers that are usually required in prior acoustic cell patterning approaches [50, 51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56]. Third, TAMP enables the fabrication of uniquely architected glioma tissues, including parallel elongated glioma tissue bundles, 3D cellular networks with interconnected, differently oriented cell bundles, and spheroid arrays with reinforced protrusion‐based inter‐spheroid connections (such as ring‐shaped networks and long chains of interconnected spheroids). We expect that the ability to achieve tissues with oriented cell bundles can facilitate the development of anisotropic tissues with direction‐dependent properties and functionalities. In addition, we anticipate that our engineered tissues with customized cellular architectures and reinforced protrusion‐based physical connections may enable enhanced signal transduction, guided cell growth and migration, and tailored tissue‐level functions, as intercellular physical contacts play critical roles in facilitating signal transduction, providing guidance for cell growth and migration, and supporting tissue‐level functionalities [78, 79].

Building on this work, future studies will focus on further validating the functional properties of the fabricated architected glioma tissues, including tumor signaling activation, glioma invasion dynamics, and responses to therapeutic interventions. We also plan to apply TAMP to fabricate a range of engineered tissue models, such as neural tissue models, blood–brain barrier models, and cardiac muscle models. In the long run, we anticipate that this study will facilitate the development of portable, easy‐to‐use, multifunctional acoustic platforms for the biofabrication of architected tissues with highly customizable ECM geometries and internal cellular architectures for biomedical research, disease modeling, and drug testing.

Experimental Section

Design and Operation of the TAMP Platform

Our TAMP‐based biofabrication approach leverages an acoustic module with multiple pairs of piezoelectric transducers and PDMS molds with customized shapes, as illustrated in Figure 1a (also see Figure S1 for device photos). Each piezoelectric transducer (Steiner & Martins, Inc.) has dimensions of 20 × 15 × 2.1 mm and resonance frequencies of 3.20, 5.44, and 7.50 MHz, characterized by a network analyzer (E5061B, Keysight Technologies). Multiple pairs of transducers are installed on a 3D‐printed customized holder that has a center well for placing a glass‐bottom Petri dish with a diameter of 35 mm (NEST Biotechnology). Under the Petri dish, a 2‐mm‐thick deionized‐water layer was maintained as the acoustic coupling medium, enabling acoustic waves generated by the side‐positioned piezoelectric transducers to propagate through the coupling layer and dish bottom into the collagen‐cell solution. Note that by using different 3D‐printed holders, the piezoelectric transducers can be arranged in different configurations, such as two transducer pairs with crossing angles of 60° and 90°, as shown by the photos in Figure S1a and S1b, respectively.

The process for fabricating a PDMS mold is illustrated in Figure S6. The base polymer and curing agent were thoroughly mixed at a ratio of 10:1, followed by degassing to remove bubbles generated during mixing. The degassed PDMS solution was then poured into a Petri dish, where a solid mold with a customized shape was pre‐attached to the dish's bottom. The Petri dish containing the solid mold and PDMS solution was then placed in an oven set to 80°C. After overnight curing, a PDMS mold with a customized‐shape center well was obtained. Prior to using the PDMS mold for biomaterial fabrication, it was sterilized with 75% ethanol followed by 60 min ultraviolet irradiation. The sterilized PDMS mold was then plasma‐treated and attached to a Petri dish's glass bottom.

To perform TAMP‐based tissue fabrication, we followed a procedure illustrated in Figure S7. A collagen‐cell solution was added to the PDMS mold. The standing acoustic waves were generated to arrange cell distributions. After collagen gelation (∼ 5 min), a cell‐laden hydrogel construct was fabricated, having both a customized ECM geometry and an acoustically arranged internal cell pattern. During the acoustic cell arrangement process, if only exciting a pair of transducers, cells in the collagen solution can be arranged into a parallel line‐like distribution. When using two transducer pairs, cells can be grouped into multiple clusters distributed in a lattice‐like pattern. To excite a pair of transducers, we used a continuous sinusoidal signal generated by a function generator (AFG3022C, Tektronix) and then amplified to 14 V using a portable amplifier (DPA‐2698, JUNTEK), as shown in Figure S2. The acoustic pressure inside the Petri dish was characterized using a hydrophone. At 3.20 MHz, the pressure measured in the patterning region was approximately 30–50 kPa. This test condition achieved effective cell trapping and patterning, while maintaining high cell viability. During acoustic patterning, the temperature of the collagen‐cell suspension was monitored using a thermal camera (TIM‐400, Micro‐Epsilon). Under a representative condition with four transducers excited at 14 V and 3.2 MHz, the temperature increased from ∼23°C to ∼26.7°C. The excitation frequency was selected from the transducer's three resonance frequencies of 3.20, 5.44, and 7.50 MHz, at which the generated acoustic waves have different wavelengths and the arranged cell patterns have different spacings. Additionally, when the excitation frequency slightly offsets from a resonance frequency, the transducer can still be excited, as there is a small bandwidth at each resonance frequency, allowing for acoustic wave generation.

Finite‐Element Acoustic–Structure Interaction Simulation in the x–z Plane

Acoustic–structure interaction simulations were performed in COMSOL to reveal acoustic transmission through the glass bottom of the Petri dish, using a simplified 2D model in the x–z plane consisting of a culture medium layer, a glass layer, and an ultrasonic coupling layer. The culture medium and ultrasonic coupling layer were modeled using pressure acoustics in the frequency domain, while the glass layer was modeled as an isotropic linear elastic solid. Acoustic‐structure coupling was applied at the culture medium‐glass and glass‐coupling medium interfaces. The density and sound speed of liquid domains were set to 1000 kg m−3 and 1480 m s−1. The glass layer's density, Young's modulus, and Poisson's ratio were set to 2230 kg m−3, 64 GPa, and 0.22. Prescribed pressure boundaries were applied on the two lateral sides of the coupling layer to represent the acoustic excitation generated by the external transducers. Air and polystyrene impedance boundaries were used to approximate the liquid‐air interface and the surrounding Petri‐dish structure, respectively. Low‐reflecting boundaries were applied at the edges of the glass layer. The model was solved at 3.18 MHz, and the pressure field in the fluids and the vertical velocity of the glass layer were extracted for analysis.

Prediction of Acoustic Fields

To assist the design and optimization of acoustic transducer arrays for cell patterning and to predict the generated acoustic fields and their arranged cell patterns, we used an analytical modeling approach in Section S1. With this approach, we can simulate the acoustic pressure fields, Gor'kov potential fields, and acoustic radiation force fields generated by an array of multiple transducer pairs arranged in different directions (see Figure S8) when exciting the array with different inputs. As the secondary acoustic radiation force is not included in the model, it cannot predict cell–cell interactions or the cell aggregation process.

Collagen‐Cell Solution Preparation

To prepare a collagen solution containing live cells, initially, the pH of a 200 µL collagen stock solution (5 mg mL−1) was adjusted by adding 15 µL of 0.1 N sodium hydroxide (NaOH). Subsequently, the neutralized collagen solution was then mixed with 150 µL of cell suspension in 1× phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) (low concentration ∼1×106 cells mL−1, high concentration ∼5×106 cells mL−1) and 35 µL 10× PBS to obtain a final collagen concentration of 2.5 mg mL−1 with a pH in the range of 7.2–7.4. All preparation steps were performed on ice. Human glioblastoma U251 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM, Genesee) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin‐streptomycin (30‐002‐CI, Corning), and 1% GlutaMAX in an incubator maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2. The U251 cells used in this study were sourced from the Biomedical Engineering Department at Virginia Tech.

Fabrication of Glioma Tissues Containing Patterned U251 Cells

The collagen‐cell solution was kept on ice to prevent gelation of the collagen and then transferred into a glass‐bottom Petri dish, which was then placed at the center of the TAMP platform. Prior to cell patterning, the deionized water (i.e., a coupling layer between acoustic transducers and the Petri dish) was also cooled to prevent collagen gelation before turning on acoustic waves. The excitation frequency of the piezoelectric transducer pairs was tuned to their working frequencies. An excitation amplitude of 14 V was applied to trap cells at the acoustic pressure nodes. Cell patterns were formed and maintained within the acoustic field for ∼5 min during the collagen gelation process, which was triggered by the slight temperature increase induced by acoustic waves. The control‐group tests with randomly distributed cells were performed without using acoustic waves. After the gelation of the collagen solution, an engineered collagen hydrogel construct containing U251 cells was cultured in a culture medium for 7 days, with the medium replaced every 3 days.

Tissue Staining

Engineered glioma tissues were rinsed with 1× PBS to remove residual culture medium, followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Sigma) for 10 min at room temperature. After fixation, tissues were permeabilized and blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.1% Triton X‐100 for 30 min at room temperature. Samples were then incubated overnight at 4°C with a polyclonal anti‐GFAP antibody (1:100 dilution) (BioLegend). The following day, tissues were washed twice with 1× PBS and counterstained with DAPI prior to imaging.

Cell Viability

Cell viability was assessed using the live/dead viability assay kit (Invitrogen). Briefly, samples were incubated with 4 µM calcein‐AM to label viable cells and 4 µM ethidium homodimer‐1 (EthD‐1) to label dead cells for 45 min. Cell viability data were acquired using the Cell Counter plugin in ImageJ (Version 1.54j, NIH). The percentage of live cells was calculated as the ratio of live cells to the total number of cells. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Image Analysis

Engineered glioma tissues were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 800, Zeiss) equipped with 5× and 10× objective lenses. GFAP‐Alexa Fluor 488 fluorescence was excited using an argon laser at 488 nm, while DAPI fluorescence was excited using a UV laser at 350 nm. All experiments were independently performed at least three times. Each experimental group included at least six samples. Stacked images were generated by overlaying the z‐axis slices of engineered glioma tissues. Fluorescence intensity was quantified from the maximum intensity projection of the z‐stack images. The width and length of cell bundles were measured in ImageJ and then analyzed using the Statistics Toolbox in MATLAB (Version 2023b, MathWorks). To evaluate the overall tissue directionality, the microscopy image was processed using a multidimensional Fourier transform and then analyzed in the wavenumber domain. The detailed procedure and flow diagram of this analysis method are provided in Section S2 and Figure S9, respectively.
