Carbohydrase enzyme and polyethylene glycol altered the chemical composition, growth performance and apparent nutrient digestibility of Prosopis juliflora pods meal diets fed to Boschveld chicken breed
Authors: P Tshiambara, CK Lebopa, HK Mokoboki, HS Mudau, KE Ravhuhali
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study was designed to evaluate how treating Prosopis juliflora pods with polyethylene glycol and carbohydrase enzyme will affect its chemical composition and how the inclusion of treated P. juliflora pods in Boschveld chicken's diets will affect growth performance and nutrient digestibility. Before P. juliflora pods were administrated to the diets, they were pre-treated with enzyme, PEG and a combination of both and analysed for their nutrient content. Five diets, namely PJPM1: commercial standard pullet grower diet as a control; PJPM2: commercial standard grower diet with untreated 30 % of P. juliflora; PJPM3: commercial standard grower diet with PEG treated 30 % of P. juliflora; PJPM4: commercial standard grower diet with carbohydrase enzyme treated 30 % of P. juliflora; PJPM5: commercial standard grower diet with PEG and carbohydrase enzyme treated 30 % of P. juliflora. A total of 300 six-week-old Boschveld chickens were used in this trial. After 42 days of feeding trial, three Boschveld chickens per replicate (n = 90) were randomly selected for the determination of nutrient digestibility. Results on the chemical composition of raw P. juliflora pods meal, pre-treated with enzyme, PEG and combination of both show significant difference (P <0.05) in CP, Ash, NDF, CF, EE, Hemicellulose, NFC and NFE. Only overall feed intake shows significant different (P <0.05) in all dietary treatments and all the measured parameters of nutrient digestibility. Enzyme and PEG positively contribute to the chemical composition, growth performance, and nutrient digestibility of diets containing P. juliflora fed to Boschveld chickens.

Introduction

In South Africa, the poultry industry makes up the greatest portion of the agricultural economy, accounting for about half of all animal products (SAPA, 2016). Among various poultry divisions, broiler production is the most popular. On the other hand, indigenous chickens have also been reported to play a major role in alleviating poverty and food security in most developing countries. However, Nondzutha et al. (2020) indicated that poultry has been shown to have a major positive impact on household well-being and income generation. One of the most well-known dual-purpose breeds created for South Africa and other regions of Africa is the Boschveld chicken breed. This breed was developed by crossing the Venda, Matabele and Ovambo chicken breeds (Bosch, 2011). According to Bosch (2011), farmers require a durable chicken that can feed itself, stay alive, and produce enough meat and eggs until slaughtered. One advantage of this breed is that farmers can use less feed as they can consume non-conventional diets on a free range (Okoro et al., 2017).

The production of livestock has significantly depended on conventional feed resources over the past years, and there is massive competition for these resources between humans and animals. Sawal et al. (2004) reported that an increase in population growth negatively affects grazing land due to an increase in urbanization to accommodate human population growth; as a result, the availability of conventional feed resources decreases. The use of non-conventional feeds such as plant pods, plant leaves, and factory by-products for livestock has long been documented by studies (Shamsi et al., 2012; Correddu et al., 2020; Chuang et al., 2021). In arid and semi-arid regions there are a variety of non-conventional feed sources for chickens that have been searched and used for over some years (Gómez-Guillén et al., 2011; Martens et al., 2012). One of the plant species that has been investigated by researchers in both ruminant and non-ruminant production is Prosopis juliflora (Khobondo et al., 2019; Almeida et al., 2022)

Prosopis juliflora is a shrub or tree in the Fabaceae family native to Mexico, South America, and the Caribbean (Burkart, 1976; Asfaw & Thulin, 1989; Pasiecznik et al., 2012). It has spread to other regions of the world, including Africa, Asia, and Australia, where it is now considered an invasive species (Shackleton et al., 2014; Hussain et al., 2021). Study by Pasiecznik et al. (2002) has brought attention to the invasion of P. juliflora on millions of hectares of rangeland in most parts of the world. In South Africa, Prosopis species have been reported to be found in provinces such as the Western Cape, Free State, Northern Cape, and North-West (Zachariades et al., 2011; Shackleton et al., 2014; Ravhuhali et al., 2021) where it has invaded about 1.8 million hectares of land (Van den Berg et al., 2013). Prosopis juliflora consists of fruits or pods that have been documented to be used as animal feeds in arid and semi-arid regions where there is feed scarcity. These pods contain a considerable amount of carbohydrates, sugar, and protein that can be used by livestock (Chaturvedi & Sahoo, 2013; Patnaik et al., 2017; Pasiecznik et al., 2022). Variations in the nutritional content of whole Prosopis pods (crude protein varied from 7.1 to 18.5 %, ether extract from 0.4 to 7.3 %, crude fibre from 12.3 to 30.8 %, nitrogen-free extract from 46.3 to 71.1 %, and the ash content from 1.4 to 7.15 %) have been observed by several researchers (Sawal et al., 2004; Choge et al., 2007; King'ori et al., 2011).

The study by Al-Marzooqi et al. (2015) reported that the inclusion of P. juliflora pod meal at a 5 % level in broiler diets does not affect the bird's performance. Again, Girma et al. (2011) investigate the inclusion of P. juliflora pod meal on broiler diets and find that a 30 % inclusion level has an impact on the bird's feed intake and growth performance. Kidane Hintsa et al. (2020) reported that P. juliflora pods can be included in up to 30 % of the diets of layers without having any effect on feed utilization, performance, egg quality, or the health status of the birds, whether the Prosopis is treated for secondary compounds or given as raw. However, the study by Meseret et al. (2011) found that P. juliflora pods can be included up to 20 % in layers' rations, but when the inclusion level reached 30 %, the egg mass, weight, and feed to egg mass ratio decreased. Broilers and layers chickens are shown to ingest different quantity of P. juliflora which can depend on factors such as the nutritional composition, inclusion levels, breed type, and gut adaptation. Kaminiska (1979) study found that layers' longer intestinal length than broilers allow them to utilize dietary fibre more efficiently. As a result, the inclusion of P. juliflora at the level of 30 % has been reported to be effectively used in layers but affects broiler chicken performance. Prosopis juliflora pod meal has 3 % higher gross energy than maize, but a lower apparent metabolic energy than maize (Al-Marzooqi et al., 2015). This shows that P. juliflora had a higher amount of indigestible fibre. Sawal et al. (2004) reported that the decrease in the apparent metabolic energy value of P. juliflora pod meal had a major effect on the digestibility of poultry due to the fibre content present. On the other hand, Abd El Tawab and Khattab (2018) highlighted that whenever plant species are available, secondary compounds are present and these tend to interfere with feed intake, digestibility and protein utilization. Over the years, various techniques including the use of polyethylene glycol (PEG) and enzyme have been reported to reduce tannin concentration and fibre content in leaves and pods used as animal feed ingredients (Makkar et al., 2009; Bhat et al., 2013; Mudau et al., 2022; Mhlongo et al., 2022). Several authors have found that the utilization of PEG and enzyme in the breakdown of non-starch polysaccharides found in plant cell walls and the deactivation of the high tannin content found in dietary feed resulted in increasing digestibility and enhancing nutritional availability (Alipour & Rouzbehan, 2007; Salem et al., 2000). Due to paucity of the information regarding the chemical composition and apparent nutrient digestibility of Boschveld chickens fed P. juliflora pods meal with carbohydrase enzyme and polyethylene glycol. This study aimed to understand how these treatments affect its chemical composition and Boschveld chicken performance. Hypothesizing that pre-treating P. juliflora pods with PEG and enzyme will affect its chemical composition and addition of the treated P. juliflora pods meal to Boschveld chicken diets will affect their growth rate and nutrient digestibility. Therefore, the current study was designed (1) To investigate how treating Prosopis juliflora with polyethylene glycol and carbohydrase enzymes alters its chemical composition, and (2) To evaluate the effect of this treated pods on the digestibility and growth performance of Boschveld chickens.

Materials and method

Description of the study area

The experiment was carried out at the Research Farm (Molelwane) of the North-West University with geographical coordinates of 25°40.459′ S and 26°10.563′ E, situated in the North West province of South Africa. The prevailing average temperature ranges from 28°C in summer and 16°C in winter. The area receives the average annual rainfall 450 mm (Enebe & Babalola, 2020).

Description of the experimental birds

Three hundred six-week-old Boschveld birds were purchased from Boschveld Ranching (PTY) LTD, located in Limpopo, Bela-Bela, South Africa. The Boschveld chicken is well noted for its light red-brown coloration with white feathers in between that assist with camouflage protecting them from predators (Bosch, 2011). The same author further highlighted that the breed was developed by crossing three Indigenous breeds, namely the Venda (50 %), Ovambo (25 %), and Matabele (25 %), for their different features. The breed is claimed to have captured the egg production potential of the Venda breed, the body size of the Matabele breed, and the hardiness of the Ovambo breed (Bosch, 2011). The same author further emphasizes that Boschveld chickens are good for meat and egg production; roosters may weigh up to 1.7 kg at 12 weeks and 2.6 kg at 20 weeks, while hens can weigh up to 1.5 kg at 12 weeks and 1.7 kg at 20 weeks. Okoro et al. (2017) state that the Boschveld breed has the benefit of being able to eat homemade rations on a free range. The breed begins egg production at 24 weeks old, and they lay eggs for two and a half years (Bosch, 2011). Another outstanding attribute of this kind of chicken is its capacity to resist severe temperatures, both hot and cold. This breed reaches sexual maturity in 136 days (20 weeks); the mature weight of the Boschveld females is about 2.3 kg and 3.5 kg for males. The use of the experimental birds was approved by the Faculty of Natural and Agricultural Sciences Research Committee and ethically cleared by the Ethics Committee with ethics number NWU-00,816–23-A5.

Sample collection

Prosopis juliflora fruits (pods) that are mature or ripe (yellow) were manually harvested from their trees in Montshioa area located in Mafikeng municipality, the Ngaka Modiri Molema local municipality in the North-West province and transported to the experimental farm for processing. The pods were then spread out on a black plastic sheet for fourteen days to thoroughly dry and minimize moisture content. Any spoiled pods and extraneous substances were eliminated. After drying, the pods were hummer milled through a 1 mm sieve to make Prosopis juliflora pod meal for proximate analysis. Other feed ingredients were purchased from Opti feeds (PTY) LTD (Lichtenburg, South Africa).

Chemical analysis

Prosopis juliflora (raw and treated pods) and the formulated diets were analysed for dry matter through oven-drying, and ash content was measured using incineration in a muffle furnace. The crude protein (CP), crude fibre (CF), ether extract (EE), dry matter (DM), ash, and organic matter (OM) content standard of AOAC (2012). The EE and CF was analysed using Ankom method. The CP was analysed using the Kjeldahl method, and neutral detergent fibre (NDF), acid detergent fibre (ADF), acid detergent lignin (ADL), hemicellulose, and cellulose was assessed using the Van Soest et al. (1991) procedure. The percentage of Non-fibrous Carbohydrates (%NFC) was calculated using the formula: %NFC=100−(%CF+%CP+%ASH+%NDF). The dry matter digestibility (DMD%) was determined with the formula: DMD%=88.9−(0.779×%ADF). To estimate digestible energy (DE, kcal/kg), the regression equation provided by Fonnesbeck et al. (1984) was applied: DE(kcal/kg)=0.27+0.0428(DMD%). The DE values were then converted to metabolizable energy (ME) using the formula from Khalil et al. (1986): ME(Mcal/kg)=0.821DE(Mcal/kg). The formula for predicting total digestible nutrients (TDN) was adopted from Bath & Marble (1989). Condensed tannins (CT) were analysed using the standard procedure described by Porter et al. (1986). The concentrations of total phenolics and total tannins of the samples were analysed using the Folin-Ciocalteau method (Makkar, 2003). All experiments were performed in sextuplicate for chemical analyses to ensure accuracy.

Enzymes and PEG treatments

The study used a multi-component carbohydrase enzyme, Ronozyme®VP (DSM Nutritional Products, South Africa) to break down carbohydrates in feed ingredients. Ronozyme®VP improves nutrient release by degrading the complex non-starch polysaccharide (NSP) structure, reducing viscosity. It contains activities such as endo-1, 3 (4) glucanase (50 fungal-glucanase units/g), hemicellulase (primarily mannanase), pentosanase, and trace amounts of pectic substance-hydrolyzing. The enzyme was included at recommended standard rate of 500 g per ton of feed. To neutralize tannins content in Prosopis pods, polyethylene glycol (PEG, molecular weight 4000) was used at a concentration of 2.5 % (250 g in 5 litres of distilled water), following the standard procedure described by Van Niekerk et al. (2020). In this study, the control diets consisted of untreated Prosopis pods, which were subjected to the same handling and processing conditions, but without the addition of PEG and enzyme. All treatment conditions, including temperature, humidity, and processing time, were standardized across both enzyme and PEG treated and control diets to ensure consistency. However, for the control diets, the pods were only soaked in distilled water. Diets formulation

Isonitrogenous and isoenergetic experimental diets were formulated where P. juliflora pod meal was included at a recommended level of 30 % (300 g kg-1) (Kidane Hintsa et al., 2020) (Table 1). Before inclusion in the diets, P. juliflora was pre-treated with polyethylene glycol and carbohydrase enzymes. Prosopis juliflora was treated with 500 g/ton of carbohydrase enzyme. PEG (250 g kg-1) was dissolved in 5000 ml of water and used to treat 5 kgs (Van Niekerk et al., 2020) of P. juliflora pod meal. The carbohydrase enzyme was targeting the fibre content, while the PEG was binding the tannins in P. juliflora. The five dietary treatments were formulated as follows: (i) PJPM1 = a commercial standard pullet grower diet as a negative control; (ii) PJPM2 = a commercial standard grower diet with untreated 30 % of P. juliflora; (iii) PJPM3 = a commercial standard grower diet with PEG treated 30 % of P. juliflora; (iv) PJPM4 = a commercial standard grower diet with carbohydrase enzyme treated 30 % of P. juliflora; (v) PJPM5 = a commercial standard grower diet with PEG and carbohydrase enzyme treated 30 % of P. juliflora. Growth performance

In this study the birds were randomly assigned to 30 pens that were distributed to the five diets, and each dietary treatment was replicated six times. The study was a completely randomized design (CRD) with the pen (holding 10 birds each) as the experimental unit, replicated six times, giving a total of 30 pens. All the diets met the minimum amino acid requirement of Boschveld birds at this phase of growth (6–12 weeks). Standards grower were used as they are recommended for the Boschveld breed (Bosch, 2011). Treatment diets were supplemented with 0 g/ton, PEG and 500 g/ton of carbohydrase enzymes. The experiment lasted for 42 days, during which feed and water were offered on an ad libitum basis.

The live weight of chicks was recorded at the beginning of the experiment to determine the initial body weight, and consequent weekly measurements were done to estimate the average weekly weight gain (AWG). The collection and measurement of feed refusals were done daily before giving fresh feeds during the experiment to estimate feed intake per pen. The body weight gain and feed intake data were used to calculate the average weekly gain. This weekly data has been used to calculate the overall growth performance, feed intake (FI), and feed convention efficiency (FCE) of the chickens, which were determined at the end of the study.

Nutrient digestibility

After the 42 days of the feeding trial, a total of 90 chickens (3 birds per pen) were subjected to a digestibility trial. The birds were given a three-day adjustment period before faecal collection, which was done for seven days. The excreta were collected from each replicate in the morning every day, wrapped, and stored at −15 °C. Feed offered and feed refusals were weighed to calculate feed consumed. The samples were then analyzed for their proximate composition using the AOAC methods described above. Apparent digestibility of the nutrients was calculated according to Bashar et al. (2010) using the following equation:Apparentdigestibility=Nutrientintake−faecalnutrientNutrientintakeX100

Statistical analysis

Data on chemical analyses, apparent nutrient digestibility and overall growth performance were analysed using one-way ANOVA (GLM PROC) in SAS (2010) software. The following model was employed for the analysis:Yij=μ+Di+Eij

Where;

Yij = the response variable, μ = Overall population mean, Di = Treatment's effect, and Eij = Random error term associated with observation ij which is assumed to be normally and independently distributed.

Data on weekly feed intake, body weight gain, feed convention efficiency and overall growth performance were analysed using the repeated measures procedure of the Statistical Analysis System (SAS), version 9.4 of 2010. The following statistical linear model were used:Yijk=μ+Di+Wj+(D×W)ij+Eijk

Where;

Yijk = response variable, μ = population mean, Di = effect of diets, Wj = effect of week (chickens age), (D × W) ij = effect of interaction between diets and week, Eijk = random error associated with observation ijk. Separation of means was compared using the probability of difference option in SAS (2010) software. Significance for all statistical tests was declared at P< 0.05.

Ethical clearance

All experimental procedures were approved by the Faculty of Natural and Agricultural Sciences Research Committee, and the use of animals were ethically cleared by the Ethics Committee with ethic number NWU-00,816–23-A5.

Results

Nutritional composition of P. juliflora pods meal pre-treated with enzyme and PEG

Results in Table 3 show the nutritional composition of P. juliflora pods meal pre-treated with enzyme and PEG. Among the parameters measured from all treatments, results show significant differences (P < 0.05) in crude protein (CP), ash, neutral detergent fibre (NDF), crude fibre (CF), ether extract (EE), hemicellulose, non-fibre carbohydrates (NFC), nitrogen-free extract (NFE), phenol and total tannins. The CP content was 97.52 g kg-1 DM for the PJPEGENZ group and 106.96 g kg-1 DM for the PJR group with treatment PJR showing the highest (P < 0.05) CP content and treatment PJPEGENZ showing the lowest (P < 0.05) CP content. PJPEGENZ shows the highest (P < 0.05) ash content (34.45 g kg-1 DM), while PJR, PJPEG and PJENZ had the lowest (P < 0.05) ash content. The highest (P < 0.05) NDF concentration was observed at PJENZ, while treatment PJPEG was showing the lowest NDF concentration. The CF content of the treatments was 198.52 g kg-1 DM for the PJENZ group and 220.79 g kg-1 DM for the PJPEGENZ group, with treatment PJPEGENZ showing the highest (P < 0.05) CF content (220.79 g kg-1 DM) and PJENZ showing the lowest (P < 0.05) CF content (198.52 g kg-1 DM). The highest (P < 0.05) hemicellulose content (148.32 g kg-1 DM) was observed at treatments PJR and PJENZ, while the lowest (P < 0.05) hemicellulose content (111.54 g kg-1 DM) was observed at PJPEG. The ME of P. juliflora pods meal was 2.41 Mcal DM for the PJENZ group and 2.49 Mcal DM for the PJR group. No significant difference (P > 0.05) was observed in condensed tannin concentration in all treatments. However, a significant difference (P < 0.05) was observed in the concentration of phenol and total tannin in all treatments. The highest (P > 0.05) phenol concentration (0.7446) was observed in treatment PJPEGENZ, while treatment PJR and PJENZ had the lowest (P > 0.05) phenols concentration (0.324 % DM). Discussion

Chemical composition

Prosopis juliflora pods have long been investigated due to their considerable amount of nutritive content, such as sugar and carbohydrates, which are beneficial for both human and animal consumption (Ruiz-Nieto et al., 2020; de Lemos et al., 2023). The dry matter, known as the proportion of feed materials that remain after moisture removal, ranged from 896.425 to 902.541 g kg-1 DM in the current study. These findings are in agreement with the range reported by de Melo Cavalcante et al. (2022) on P. juliflora pods. As a general rule, dry matter is important due to its ability to provide correct measurement of feed quantity and the level of nutrients available to be offered. Whereas moisture content contributes to the weight of feed but not to the diet's nutrient concentration levels. The results of the current study on the ash content ranged between 23.742 to 34.450 g kg-1 DM. Similar results were reported by de Melo Cavalcante et al. (2022) and Negreiros et al. (1992), who documented ash contents of 30.9 g kg-1 DM and 32.0 g kg-1 DM in P. juliflora pods, respectively. The speculation is that the use of a combination of both PEG and enzyme had a positive effect when compared to all treatments due to their contribution to increased ash content. The amount of ash content in poultry diets represents the inorganic minerals such as calcium, potassium, iron, sodium, magnesium and others (Bukar & Saeed, 2014). Even though the mineral concentration levels were not assessed, an increased ash content from PJPEGENZ can play an important role in promoting balanced growth in chickens. However, the findings of ash content in the present study were below the findings reported by Al-Soqeer et al. (2023), whose ash content was 73.7 g kg-1 DM. It has, however, been reported that excess ash content in poultry diets may cause bone, kidney, and joint problems in growing chickens (Afolabi et al., 2021). Therefore, the ash content of P. juliflora pod meal in the current study is not within the range that can cause any harm or defects to Boschveld chickens. The CP content obtained in the current study of 97.521–106.966 g kg-1 DM is within the ranges obtained by other authors, such as King'ori et al. (2011) and Choge et al. (2007), who obtained the range of 71.0–162.0 g kg-1 DM and 71.0–185.0 g kg-1 DM. However, the pre-treatment of P. juliflora with either PEG, enzyme, or both enzyme and PEG resulted in lower CP content in the present study. These findings are in line with those of a study by Mhlongo et al. (2022), who reported a decrease in the crude protein of grape pomace when treated with fibrolytic enzyme. The decrease in CP content when P. juliflora was pre-treated with PEG, enzyme, or both enzyme and PEG in this study is still unknown. Although there are slight decreases in the CP content of the current observation, the findings of CP content in this study are still within the range reported by the NRC (1984) of 7–17 % CP for improving the growth rate of chickens of heavy breeds such as Boschveld chickens. Therefore, the results of the current study confirm that indeed P. juliflora pod meal contains the CP content that can improve the growth rate of chickens such as Boschveld breeds.

The pods of P. juliflora are known to contain high CF content in their cell walls, and this fibre indicates the portion of the feed that is not usable for the animals (Ruiz-Nieto et al., 2020). Although dietary fibre in chickens is known to limit digestion and the absorption of nutrients, CF plays an important role by stimulating digestion and the production of important intestinal bacteria (Zhang et al., 2018; Jha & Mishra, 2021). A research study by Varastegani and Dahlan (2014) indicates that the fibre content in the complete ration for chickens should be below 70 g kg-1 DM. Furthermore, a study by Choge et al. (2007) reported the CF content of P. juliflora pod ranging from 123.0 to 280.0 g kg-1 DM. Therefore, the CF content range of 198.520–220.792 g kg-1 DM obtained in the current study is within the range reported by Choge et al. (2007). However, the CF content of P. juliflora is above the standard required for poultry, and therefore, rendering the treatment of fibre content before administration to the chicken is crucial. The pre-treatment of P. juliflora pod simultaneously with both enzyme and PEG (PJPEGENZ) showed the highest CF content, while the pre-treated P. juliflora pod with fibre degrading enzyme showed the lowest CF content. The current study results on enzyme treatment are in agreement with the findings by Mhlongo et al. (2022) study, which reported a decrease in the CF content of grape pomace when pre-treated with the fibrolytic enzyme. The findings suggest that carbohydrase enzyme (Ronozyme®VP) has the ability to degrade the non-starch polysaccharide found in the cell wall of the P. juliflora pods. The hemicellulose content of P. juliflora pre-treated with enzyme, PEG and combination of both enzyme and PEG were below the range documented by King'ori et al. (2011). These results indicate the positive impact that PEG and enzyme had in reducing the hemicellulose content in the P. juliflora pods meal. The reduction in hemicellulose can be attributed to the enzymatic breakdown of complex polysaccharides into simpler sugars, which enhances digestibility (Laca et al., 2019). Polyethylene glycol (PEG), on the other hand, is known for binding to tannins and neutralizing their anti-nutritional effects, allowing more efficient access to fibre components like hemicellulose (Kumanda, 2019). According to Khalil (2015), when used in combination, PEG and enzyme treatments synergistically improve the degradation of structural carbohydrates, further reducing hemicellulose content and improving the nutritional value of the pod meal for poultry. The NFE content recorded in the current study range from 621.0 to 650.0 g kg-1 DM is within the ranges of 463.0 to 653.0 g kg-1 DM obtained in a study by King'ori et al. (2011). This consistency with previous findings suggests that the carbohydrate content in P. juliflora pods remains relatively stable across different conditions. The study by Singh and Kim (2021) indicates that nitrogen-free extract (NFE) represents the easily digestible carbohydrates, which are a critical source of energy for poultry. The findings reported by Lawrence et al. (2023) and Mohite and Sharma (2024) documented that the high NFE content can be attributed to the breakdown of complex carbohydrates during pre-treatment processes, such as drying or enzymatic degradation, which enhances the availability of simple sugars and starches. These treatments improve the digestibility of the feed and provide a readily available source of energy, crucial for maintaining chicken productivity in nutrient-deficient environments. The range in EE content (18.889 to 26.221 g kg-1 DM) of the current study is in line with the findings of 13.0 to 42.6 g kg-1 DM reported by Sawal et al. (2004). Kathirvel and Kumudha (2011) state that the lower fat level in P. juliflora pod meal is an indication of the high carbohydrate content that the animal can utilize. Metabolizable energy content recorded in the current study (2.418 to 2.494 Mcal/kg) was within the range reported by Silva et al. (1989) while the DE content (2.945 to 3.038 Mcal/kg) was below the range recorded by these authors. The findings by Noblet et al. (2023) emphasize that the metabolizable energy (ME) values are particularly important for poultry, as they represent the energy available to the birds after accounting for losses in faeces, urine, and gases. Additionally, poultry, being monogastric animals, rely heavily on high-quality energy sources for efficient growth and production (Bikker & Jansman, 2023). According to the findings reported by de Lemos et al. (2023) highlighted that the slight decrease in DE content may be attributed to factors such as the fibrous nature of P. juliflora pods, which can lower the digestibility of certain components. However, the processing treatments applied (e.g., enzymatic or PEG) likely helped to mitigate these effects, ensuring that the ME remains sufficient to meet the nutritional needs of poultry. By optimizing the pre-treatment of feed, the energy availability can be improved, making P. juliflora a viable feed ingredient for poultry production.

Regarding anti-nutritional factors, studies have highlighted that the use of enzymes and PEG in the breakdown of non-starch polysaccharides found in plant cell walls, and deactivation of the high tannin content found in dietary feed resulted in increased digestibility and enhanced nutritional availability (Adeola & Bedford, 2004; Alipour & Rouzbehan, 2007; Salem et al., 2000). Anti-nutritional factors are secondary compounds found in plants fruit or leaves, and their presence in poultry feed inhibits the nutrient utilization by the animals, which then affects the animal productivity (Salim et al., 2018). Levels of phenols (0.64–0.74 % DM) and total tannins (0.069–0.34 % DM) obtained in the current study are lower than those obtained by Srinivas and Chaturvedi (2023), who reported phenol content of 2.098 % DM and total tannin content of 1.868 % DM. The results of the current study indicate that the pre-treatment of P. juliflora pods with enzyme or PEG contributed to the reduction of total tannins concentration. The results are in line with the findings by Mlambo et al. (2007) who highlighted that PEG has the ability to bind tannins while releasing proteins and other feed components for absorption and digestion. Suggesting chickens were able to obtain enough nutrients whenever there's PEG and enzyme inclusion, as PEG and enzyme were able to reduce phenols and tannins, as observed on growth performance. In this study, the results on condensed tannin concentration indicate that both the enzyme and PEG had no effect on the condensed tannin concentration. The current results are in contrary to the findings by Van Nieker et al. (2020), who reported a decrease in the condensed tannins content when grape pomace was being pre-treated with the inclusion of PEG.

Overall growth performance, feed intake and feed convention efficiency

The inclusion of P. juliflora pods meal (30 %) in the diets of Boschveld chickens improved the feed intake, body weight gain of the birds when compared to the dietary treatment without P. juliflora pods meal (Figs. 1 and 2). Feed intake is the amount of feed that the animal ingested, and adequate feed intake is important for animal development and healthy growth (Forbes, 2007). However, the dietary treatments with P. juliflora pods pre-treated with PEG and enzyme (PJPM3 to PJPM5) generally shows the highest value of the overall feed intake when compared to dietary treatment containing raw P. juliflora (PJPM2). These results are in line with the findings by Choudhary et al. (2005) on the impact of multienzyme in broiler diets. The authors indicated that birds’ performance can be improved when P. juliflora is treated with enzyme in broiler diets, and this could be ascribed to the breakdown of the insoluble non-starch polysaccharides found in the P. juliflora pod cell walls and the deactivation of the anti-nutritional factors found in the plant pods by both PEG and enzymes (Adeola & Bedford, 2004; Alipour & Rouzbehan, 2007). Studies reported that high fibre and anti-nutritional factors found in most plant pods can contribute to low digestibility of substrates, and this may lead to the suppression of nutrient utilisation, which has a direct effect on feed conversion ratio and weight gain (De Vries et al., 2012; Banti & Bajo, 2020), which is in line with the findings of the current experiment. The type of chicken breed, the neutralisation of anti-nutritional factors, and the breakdown of the insoluble non-starch polysaccharides found in the Prosopis pod cell walls by both PEG and enzymes to unlock nutrients might have contributed to the improvement in weight gain and feed conversion efficiency of the birds in the current study. The performance of the Boschveld chickens in diets containing non-conventional diets or P. juliflora attributes shows that Boschveld chickens have the ability to utilize low-quality feed ingredients and yield good results. Apparent nutrients digestibility

Nutrient digestibility refers to the amount of nutrients ingested by the animals from the feed given. The results of the current study indicate that treating P. juliflora with both PEG and enzyme (PJPEGENZ) resulted in the highest crude protein digestibility compared to the other treatment diets. These results are in agreement with those obtained in a study by Khan et al. (2006), where it was observed that enzyme inclusion improved the crude protein digestibility. The lowest crude protein digestibility recorded in the control with no enzyme or PEG inclusion indicates that more nutrients are utilized by chickens with the addition of supplements that help with the digestion of the ingredients. This authenticate that, in this study, chickens were able to ingest the greater amount of crude protein in diets containing treated and untreated P. juliflora pods meal when compared to control diets. Furthermore, the results of the current study indicate that the pre-treatment of the P. juliflora pod meal with carbohydrase enzyme contributed to the low level of both the dry matter and ether extract digestibility of the dietary treatments, indicating that less organic matter was ingested by the chickens. Contrary results were reported in a study by Khan et al. (2006), where supplementation of soybean-corn diet with an enzyme improved the dry matter, and ether extracts nutrients digestibility of the broiler's diets when compared to the control diets. The crude fibre digestibility in all treatments from this study is below 50 %, ranging between 33.56 % and 45.43 % DM. The control diet in the current study had the highest crude fibre digestibility, indicating that the diets contain ingredients that have no crude fibre content like the one of P. juliflora. Whereas, a dietary treatment containing P. juliflora pod meal pre-treated with enzyme resulted in the lowest crude fibre digestibility when compared to the other treatment diets. The current study results are similarly to the findings by Al-Marzooqi et al. (2015), who reported that supplementation of enzyme to the diets containing P. juliflora did not improve the crude fibre digestibility. Furthermore, the results of the current study indicate that the pre-treatment of the P. juliflora pods meal with PEG resulted in high NDF digestibility but low ADL digestibility when compared to the pre-treatment with enzyme. Furthermore, current study results attest that carbohydrase enzymes have the potential to improve the ADL digestibility. The findings of this study correspond with those of Khattak et al. (2006), who observed that exogenous enzymes, such as carbohydrase enzymes, promote NSP breakdown and reduce internal viscosity, which enhances gut function and accelerates nutrient digestibility.

Conclusion

This study highlights that P. juliflora pods treated with enzyme or PEG did not enhance crude protein content, but however effectively reduced crude fibre and total tannin contents, thereby improving fibre digestibility. Treated P juliflora pods with enzyme or PEG had a positive impact on its chemical composition resulting in improved growth performance and nutrient digestibility. However, limitations such as the focus on a single chicken breed may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, variations in environmental conditions and feed composition could affect the results. Future research should aim to validate these findings in larger, more diverse poultry populations and explore the long-term impacts of enzyme and PEG supplementation on nutrient absorption and overall health. Investigating alternative chemical and nutritional modifications of P. juliflora could further optimize poultry diets. Integrating these insights into breeding programs for poultry strains that can efficiently utilize high-fibre and high-tannin feeds will enhance feed efficiency and overall poultry production both locally and globally. Cost implication is also a limiting factor as it was not integrated into the current study.
