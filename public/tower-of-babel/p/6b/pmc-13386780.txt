Differential associations of active and passive digital modalities with health perception biases among older adults
Authors: Haowen Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapid digitalization positions internet use as a critical social determinant of health. However existing literature largely views internet engagement as a single uniform activity. It overlooks how specific types of digital use correlate with health perception bias which is the evaluative mismatch between subjective self-rating and objective physical status.MethodsThis research analyzed data from the 2020 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. This nationally representative survey used a multi-stage stratified probability sampling strategy. Missing data were handled via Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations. Using Python and Stata software the study employed Latent Profile Analysis to categorize objective physical health. Binary logistic regression and Propensity Score Matching were then used to analyze the associations between specific digital modalities and health perception biases.ResultsThe analysis revealed divergent associations across digital activities. Active information-oriented news reading and social-oriented chatting inversely correlated with pessimistic bias and positively correlated with optimistic bias. In contrast passive entertain-oriented video consumption strongly associated with higher odds of pessimistic bias among objectively healthy older adults. Education moderated these statistical relationships. The positive association between video use and pessimism was weaker among highly educated individuals while associations across both active and passive types were stronger among those with lower education. Additionally, these specific associations were highly sensitive to gender and marital status.ConclusionsThe empirical correlates of the digital environment vary significantly by specific usage patterns and socioeconomic backgrounds. The digital divide extends beyond basic connectivity to include how different digital activities associate with subjective health evaluations. Recognizing that these observational correlations intertwine with lifelong personal trajectories policymakers should focus on fostering content-specific digital literacy and designing tailored digital environments that carefully accommodate the diverse preexisting evaluative tendencies of an aging society.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07705-2.Keywords: Health perception bias, Types of digital use, Latent Profile Analysis, Active aging, Digital literacy

Backgrounds

Subjective health evaluation is a central indicator of well-being in later life. Specifically, health perception bias which is the mismatch between an individual’s actual physical condition and their subjective health rating strongly correlates with their overall quality of life [1, 2]. In the current digital era, the internet serves as a primary platform where older adults access health information and find standards for social comparison [3, 4]. Understanding how this digital environment shapes the subjective well-being of older adults has become an increasingly critical public health issue.

Drawing on the existing literature different digital activities are theorized to relate to distinct functional and behavioral patterns. Activities such as information-oriented news reading and social-oriented chatting represent active digital engagement. These functional types require sustained attention and systematic information processing [5, 6]. They also align with the practical goals of older adults to acquire useful health knowledge and maintain social connections [7, 8]. By actively securing these multidimensional resources older adults are theorized to build a strong protective buffer against the physical challenges of aging [9, 10]. Therefore, active digital participation is hypothesized to associate empirically with more positive health perceptions a pattern often interpreted through the theoretical lens of cognitive and social reserve.

Conversely passive digital behaviors exhibit entirely different functional characteristics. Entertain-oriented video consumption particularly through short video applications relies heavily on algorithmic system interactivity and involves passive information reception [11, 12]. Furthermore, literature grounded in social comparison theory explains that these platforms frequently show highly idealized images of successful aging and wealthy lifestyles. Evaluating personal physical decline against these idealized virtual lives is theorized to foster unfavorable self-evaluations and media-related health concerns [13, 14]. Viewed through this interpretative lens this passive digital environment is logically anticipated to associate with a pessimistic health perception bias.

However existing research on the digital divide often treats internet use as a single uniform activity. Most studies focus simply on basic internet access or total screen time [15, 16]. This broad approach fundamentally overlooks how specific digital modalities systematically relate to the distinct health evaluation patterns mentioned above. As a result, the current literature presents conflicting findings regarding the consequences of internet use among older adults. Some studies link general digital engagement to active aging and positive subjective health [10, 17], while others associate it with increased distress and negative health appraisals [18, 19]. To address these mixed findings and bridge this research gap studies must move beyond the simple binary of internet access to examine the behavioral associations between specific digital activities and health perception biases.

Although the nature of our secondary survey data precludes the direct measurement of these underlying cognitive and evaluative processes the theoretical perspectives discussed above provide a crucial interpretative lens. Acknowledging this empirical boundary, we propose the analytical framework illustrated in Fig. 1 to systematically test the observable behavioral associations. This model focuses strictly on the statistical relationships between specific digital modalities and health perception biases. Furthermore, our framework incorporates individual differences as critical boundary conditions. Educational attainment represents a key proxy indicator for cognitive reserve and digital literacy among older adults. Individuals with more formal schooling generally possess higher cognitive capacity to process complex health data through information-oriented news. They also demonstrate lower susceptibility to the negative media effects associated with algorithmic video feeds [20, 21]. For older adults with limited educational backgrounds engaging in social-oriented chatting or passive video watching often correlates with immediate emotional support and mental diversion that might be scarce in their offline lives [22–24]. Therefore, as depicted in the model education empirically moderates the main pathways.

Importantly, we explicitly acknowledge that these associations are correlational rather than causal. It is highly possible that lifelong paths such as objective health trajectories education and personality traits established in early adulthood like inherent optimism or pessimism might actually drive later-life interests and dictate what type of internet content older adults are likely to access. Our cross-sectional design cannot disentangle these causal pathways thus we approach the findings strictly as observational correlates.

Guided by the aforementioned theoretical perspectives, the overarching objective of this empirical study is to examine how different digital activities correlate with health perception biases among older adults. To achieve this, we address three specific research questions. First, how do active and passive digital engagements, specifically information-oriented news, social-oriented chat, and entertain-oriented video, associate with pessimistic and optimistic biases across distinct physical health baselines? Second, how does educational attainment moderate these statistical relationships? Finally, because lifelong social roles and immediate household environments closely relate to subjective well-being, we explore whether these observed correlates vary by gender and marital status. This structured empirical approach provides a detailed understanding of digital aging.

By answering these research questions, this study provides distinct empirical, theoretical, and practical contributions to the literature on digital aging. Empirically, the study conceptualizes and measures a health perception bias matrix, which we explicitly present as a novel conceptual and empirical contribution to quantify evaluative mismatches rather than relying on standard evaluation measures. This novel measurement provides a clear perspective to separate objective physical decline from subjective evaluation, allowing researchers to precisely quantify the mismatch between actual health and perceived well-being. Furthermore, the study supplies robust behavioral evidence distinguishing the statistical outcomes associated with passive video consumption from those linked to active digital engagements. Theoretically, the research advances the digital divide literature by breaking away from the traditional view of internet use as a single uniform activity. Rather than attempting to validate unmeasured psychological mechanisms, we offer an empirical analytical framework that systematically categorizes functional digital modalities and explains their heterogeneous behavioral associations with health perception biases. Practically, these findings supply specific behavioral evidence for designing digital environments tailored for older adults. The research informs targeted digital literacy programs to help older adults critically navigate algorithmic content, while fully recognizing that these digital habits are deeply intertwined with long-standing personality traits.

Methods

Data source and study sample

Data were derived from the 2020 wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). To ensure national representativeness, CHARLS used a multi-stage, stratified probability-proportional-to-size sampling method. The sampling frame covered 150 county-level units and 450 village-level or urban community units across 28 provinces in China. Within each primary sampling unit, households were randomly selected through mapping and listing procedures, and age-eligible individuals were then interviewed. The 2020 wave is well-suited for this research because it records specific types of internet use, including reading news, chatting online, and watching videos. This level of detail allows for a detailed analysis of digital behaviors among older adults.

In this study, we applied specific selection criteria to focus on the older population. First, we restricted the sample to respondents aged 65 years and older. To ensure a robust analysis, we excluded observations with missing data on key demographic variables, subjective health assessments, and specific internet behaviors. Following these procedures, the final analytical sample included 7,289 individuals.

Measures

Dependent variables

The conceptualization of our dependent variable is based on the biopsychosocial model and theories of subjective aging. These theories suggest that self-rated health is an active psychological process rather than a direct reflection of physical illness [1, 2]. Because differences between objective status and subjective evaluation indicate psychological adaptation, we built a two-dimensional framework that crosses objective physical health with subjective health assessments to quantify this mismatch.

To measure physical health, we used 42 objective health indicators covering seven key areas. These areas include physical functioning, activities of daily living, instrumental activities of daily living, chronic diseases, health behaviors, geriatric syndromes, and cognitive status. The definitions and measurement standards for each indicator are shown in Supplementary Table S1. In the original dataset, we excluded respondents under 65 years of age and those with missing data on key demographic variables and subjective health assessments. After these exclusions, 29.42% of the cases had at least one missing value among the objective health indicators. To reduce potential bias, we handled these missing values using multiple imputation by chained equations. This process generated five imputed datasets for the latent profile analysis. We implemented both the multiple imputation and the latent profile analysis using Python 3.11.

We chose latent profile analysis over traditional frailty indices to firmly align with the theoretical assumptions of the biopsychosocial model [25]. Traditional cumulative deficit models simply count the number of health deficits, which assumes that all symptoms contribute equally and independently to health decline [26]. Conversely, the biopsychosocial framework conceptualizes older adult health not as a simple sum of isolated illnesses but as a holistic and multidimensional state involving complex symptom clustering. Therefore, applying latent profile analysis provides a person centered theoretical fit. By identifying underlying health typologies based on the joint probability distributions of the 42 indicators, this statistical method accurately captures the holistic physiological baseline [27–29]. Establishing this precise holistic baseline is theoretically necessary to quantify the psychological mismatch inherent in health perception bias. The interconnections among these health markers, shown in the network analysis in Supplementary Figure S1, further support the need for this profile-based classification.

To create the 2 × 2 cognitive bias matrix for our analysis, we dichotomized the objective physical status. We combined the pre-frail and frail subgroups into a single vulnerable category and compared it with the healthy cohort. Subjective health was measured using a standard self-rated health question. To match the objective categories, we recoded the responses into a binary variable. Participants who reported very good, good, or fair health were coded as having good subjective health. Those reporting poor or very poor health were coded as having poor subjective health. By crossing the binary objective physical status with the binary subjective assessment, we created a 2 × 2 matrix with four exclusive health perception typologies. These typologies reflect the match or mismatch between an individual’s actual physical condition and their subjective evaluation.

Based on our analytical strategy, we focused on two main types of cognitive mismatch as the dependent variables. The first is pessimistic bias in the healthy cohort. This includes individuals who are objectively healthy but report poor subjective health. The second is optimistic bias in the frail cohort. This includes individuals who are objectively frail but report good subjective health. This subgroup approach allows us to analyze the factors associated with health underestimation and overestimation across different physical baselines. It provides a clear foundation for the logit models.

Independent variables

We measured different digital behaviors using a multi-response question from the CHARLS 2020 survey about specific internet activities. To address the complexity of digital engagement among older adults, we treated internet use not as a single activity but as distinct functional types. This approach differentiates media consumption by user motivations and the different levels of cognitive load required. Rather than simply describing what older adults do online, this method categorizes their behaviors by the psychological mechanisms and cognitive demands associated with each activity.

Following this framework, we constructed three distinct binary variables. The first type, news, was coded as 1 if respondents selected reading news. We classified this as information-oriented use because processing text-based news requires high cognitive effort, working memory engagement, and systematic information processing. This active engagement relates to the motivation to reduce environmental uncertainty and acquire useful knowledge. This distinguishes it from passive consumption. The second type, chat, was coded as 1 if respondents selected chatting. This social-oriented use involves active interpersonal communication aimed at building social capital and maintaining meaningful relationships. These relationships are important for psychological resilience and reducing isolation in later life. The third type, video, was coded as 1 if respondents selected watching videos. We categorized this as entertain-oriented use because current digital video consumption relies heavily on heuristic processing. This is especially true for the short videos common among older adults in China. It provides immediate sensory gratification and passive entertainment. It also requires minimal cognitive effort compared to reading or active two-way social interactions.

For each of these three types, respondents who did not select the activity were coded as 0. In addition, individuals who reported not using the internet at all were coded as 0 across all three variables. They serve as a baseline non-user group in the analyses. These three functional types are not mutually exclusive. This reflects the reality that older adults can engage in multiple digital activities at the same time. By using this categorization, we move beyond simple binary measurements of internet access to examine the specific correlates of different digital behaviors.

Covariates

To account for potential confounders, we adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics commonly associated with both digital use and health outcomes. Gender was coded as 1 for male and 0 for female. Age was measured in continuous years. We included a quadratic term (age squared) to account for potential nonlinear relationships between aging and health perception. Socioeconomic status was represented by education, measured in continuous years of schooling. Marital status was dichotomized into married or partnered (coded as 1) and widowed, divorced, separated, or never married (coded as 0). Residential location was dichotomized, with rural residence coded as 1 and urban residence coded as 0. Finally, we included region fixed effects in all regression models to control for unobserved spatial differences and regional economic disparities.

Statistical analysis

To examine the associations between different types of digital use and health perception biases, we used binary logistic regression models stratified by objective physical health. This approach fits our focus on two binary outcomes within specific subgroups. We estimated one model for pessimistic bias in the healthy group and a separate model for optimistic bias in the frail group. The coefficients were exponentiated to obtain odds ratios. These ratios show the relative likelihood of observing the cognitive bias associated with a one-unit change in the predictor.

To test the robustness of these baseline findings and address potential selection bias inherent in observational survey data, we conducted Propensity Score Matching [30, 31]. By balancing the distribution of observed covariates between internet users and non-users across different types of use, this established technique helps clarify the specific associations of digital engagement [15, 32, 33]. However, it is important to explicitly acknowledge the methodological limits of this approach. While Propensity Score Matching effectively reduces selection bias based on observed covariates, it inherently cannot account for unobserved confounders [34, 35]. As a result, the findings should be interpreted strictly as robust observational associations rather than causal relationships. Finally, to explore the patterns underlying these associations, we introduced interaction terms into the baseline models to test the moderating role of education. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 17.0.

Results

The final analytical sample included 7,289 respondents aged 65 and older. Based on the latent profile analysis, 3,152 individuals were classified into the healthy cohort, while 4,137 individuals were classified into the frail cohort. Conversely, within the frail cohort, a large proportion of older adults (53.1%, N = 2,197) showed an optimistic bias, reporting good subjective health despite objective physical decline, compared to the 1,940 individuals who consistently reported poor health.

Different patterns of digital use emerged across the health perception groups. Generally, individuals showing a pessimistic bias reported lower rates of internet use across all types compared to their consistent counterparts in the healthy cohort. For example, the rate of reading news was 14% among the consistent good group but only 8% among the pessimistic bias group. In contrast, within the frail cohort, individuals with an optimistic bias showed higher digital engagement than those in the consistent poor group. Specifically, the rates of reading news, chatting online, and watching videos among the optimistic bias group were 12%, 10%, and 12%, respectively. These rates were consistently higher than those in the consistent poor baseline group.

Sociodemographic characteristics also varied across the cognitive bias groups, reflecting underlying social differences. In the healthy cohort, individuals with a pessimistic bias tended to have fewer years of schooling (2.94 years versus 3.84 years) and were more likely to live in rural areas (75% versus 66%) compared to those with consistent good health perceptions. Similarly, within the frail cohort, the optimistic bias group had more education, with an average of 3.49 years of schooling compared to 3.04 years for the consistent poor group, along with a lower proportion of rural residents. These baseline differences in digital behaviors and sociodemographic profiles highlight the need to account for these covariates in the regression models to clarify the associations between digital use and health perception biases.

Clear sociodemographic differences emerged between digital users and non-users across all three types, reflecting a digital divide. Compared to non-users, individuals reporting digital activities were younger, mostly male, and more likely to be married. The largest differences were observed in socioeconomic status and residence. For example, news users had an average of 8.17 years of schooling compared to only 2.88 years among non-users. Similarly, while approximately 71% of non-users lived in rural areas, this proportion dropped to roughly 30% to 38% among digital users. Regarding health perception groups, non-users showed a higher rate of the Consistent Poor typology compared to users. These baseline differences in sociodemographic characteristics highlight the importance of accounting for these covariates in the regression analyses.

To test the robustness of these baseline associations and reduce potential selection bias, we re-estimated the models using Propensity Score Matching (Table 3). Specifically, we used a one-to-one nearest neighbor matching approach to balance the user and non-user samples based on individual sociodemographic characteristics and regional locations. The matched-sample estimates confirmed the primary findings. In the healthy cohort (Model A), the negative associations of news (OR = 0.355, p = 0.003) and chat (OR = 0.468, p = 0.049) with pessimistic bias not only remained significant but grew stronger. Video consumption maintained its strong positive association (OR = 2.164, p = 0.027). In the frail cohort (Model B), the associations linking news (OR = 1.530, p = 0.037) and chat (OR = 1.480, p = 0.034) to higher odds of optimistic bias remained stable. Overall, this approach provides observational evidence that distinct functional types of digital use associate with different patterns of health perception bias.

Exploratory analyses of educational moderation and subgroup heterogeneity

To further explore the boundary conditions of the primary findings, we conducted exploratory analyses on the moderating role of education. We first investigated education as a moderator by adding interaction terms between specific digital activities and a binary education indicator into the baseline models. This indicator was defined as the completion of junior high school or above, representing nine or more years of formal schooling. Figure 3 presents these interaction results, showing specific moderation patterns across the two educational groups. In the healthy cohort (Model A), lower education strengthened both the positive and negative associations of digital use. The positive association between entertain-oriented video use and pessimistic bias was strong among older adults with less education, while this correlation was much weaker for those with more education. Similarly, the inverse correlations linking information-oriented news and social-oriented chat to lower chances of pessimism were more visible among the less educated. This pattern suggests that these active digital types are theorized to correspond to a stronger psychological buffer for individuals with lower socioeconomic status.

Discussion

Divergent correlates of active and passive digital modalities

Addressing our first research question regarding how active and passive digital engagements associate with health perception biases across distinct physical baselines the most important finding of this study is the clear difference in how various digital activities correlate with health evaluations. This finding challenges the uniform view of internet use among older adults. Across both the healthy and frail groups active digital use showed consistent protective correlations. Specifically reading news and chatting online were linked to lower chances of pessimism among the healthy and higher chances of optimism among the frail. This consistent statistical co-occurrence suggests that active digital participation aligns well with specific preexisting behavioral motivations to seek useful information and fulfill the need for social connection [36–38]. Actively searching for news requires processing complex information. Rather than actively changing cognitive states this digital behavior theoretically reflects a higher level of existing cognitive engagement. At the same time frequent online chatting often co-occurs with robust existing social networks and a strong underlying sense of belonging [39–41]. Therefore, the observed association between active digital behaviors and positive health evaluations may theoretically reflect an underlying sense of control over their daily lives. This perceived sense of control corresponds closely in the literature to a narrower evaluative gap between actual physical health and subjective appraisals [42, 43].

In sharp contrast watching entertainment videos showed a strong positive association with pessimistic bias in the healthy group. Passively watching short videos frequently exposes older adults to recommended content depicting highly idealized images of successful aging or wealthy lifestyles. Evaluating personal circumstances against these visually appealing but unrealistic standards is theorized to involve upward comparisons. For objectively healthy older adults this theoretical upward comparison provides a crucial interpretative lens for the observed statistical correlation as it strongly aligns in the literature with unfavorable self-evaluations and media-related health concerns [44–46]. Consequently, older adults prone to pessimism may be more likely to internalize these perceived flaws and rate their own health negatively. The repeated display of idealized lives in short videos correlates uniquely with pessimistic biases a statistical pattern indicative of a shared susceptibility to negative media effects among specific healthy older adults [47, 48].

Interestingly this negative association between watching videos and health perception was completely absent in the frail group. The correlation with optimistic bias remained statistically insignificant for these individuals. This non-significant finding highlights an important limit of passive digital entertainment. When older adults face real physical decline their fundamental needs and behavioral patterns likely shift. They typically demonstrate a reliance on robust alternative resources such as useful health knowledge from news or genuine support from close social ties. The brief sensory stimulation provided by short videos does not correspond to the deep cognitive and social reserve often necessary to maintain an optimistic outlook in the face of actual physical weakness [49–51]. Therefore, passive video watching fails to emerge as a meaningful statistical correlate of positive health perception once actual physical frailty begins.

The complex moderating role of educational attainment

Turning to our second research question we explored how educational attainment moderates these statistical relationships. Beyond the main findings education emerged as a key moderator that shaped the observed associations between digital use and health perception. In the healthy cohort lower education strengthened the protective correlations of reading news and chatting online. Older adults with less formal schooling often lack diverse offline information sources and social connections. For this group active digital platforms are theorized to serve as prominent behavioral indicators of seeking informational and social resources. Accessing daily news or maintaining online social ties may reflect a compensatory strategy that is rare in their offline lives [3, 24, 52]. As a result, these active behaviors show a stronger empirical correlation with lower pessimism among less educated individuals.

Conversely lower education also strengthened the positive correlation between watching videos and pessimistic bias in the healthy cohort. Short video platforms often present users with a massive amount of fragmented and exaggerated content. Older adults with less education may lack the basic digital literacy needed to filter this heavy flow of information. This statistical relationship can be theoretically interpreted through the lens of communication overload and related evaluative distress [12, 53–55]. Lacking sufficient cognitive reserve to process idealized online stories these individuals are statistically more likely to report media-related pessimistic evaluations.

In the frail cohort education showed a different moderating pattern for active digital use. The positive correlation between reading news and optimistic bias was mostly seen among highly educated older adults. This finding suggests a pattern of cognitive matching. Highly educated older adults generally possess the skills to process complex health information which theoretically aligns with their tendency to report positive health evaluations despite physical decline [56–58]. In contrast the less educated frail group showed a stronger positive correlation between online chatting and optimistic bias. When facing actual physical weakness individuals with less education may exhibit a greater reliance on the direct support and strong social ties provided by digital communication rather than complex health information [59, 60].

Furthermore, education showed a clear crossing pattern for video use among frail respondents. Passive video watching correlated with higher chances of optimism among the less educated but correlated with lower chances of optimism among highly educated individuals. For frail older adults with lower social status engaging with entertaining videos might manifest as a form of digital escape. This passive activity is hypothesized to co-occur with a need for temporary distraction from the difficult realities of physical decline [61–63]. However, for highly educated individuals dealing with frailty the sharp contrast between the active online world and their own physical limits corresponds to a wider evaluative mismatch. This clear awareness of the contrast potentially explains why passive entertainment fails to correlate with maintaining a positive view of their health [64, 65].

Demographic sensitivities and the leveling phenomenon

Finally in response to our third research question exploring whether these observed correlates vary by demographic characteristics the subgroup analysis showed that gender and marital status significantly shape the associations in the healthy cohort. Male respondents showed a unique dual sensitivity to digital modalities. The positive association of reading news and the vulnerability correlation of watching videos were notably stronger among men. This statistical difference can be theoretically interpreted through the lens of broader social changes after retirement. Compared to women older men often experience a sharper loss of professional identity and social roles [66–68]. In addition, older women typically maintain wider offline social networks that provide consistent support. As a result, men may exhibit a greater behavioral reliance on the digital world for social connection. Their health perceptions correlate more closely with news reading which theoretically reflects a continued desire for social participation [69]. At the same time this specific digital reliance theoretically co-occurs with a higher likelihood of reporting frustration and unfavorable comparisons associated with the idealized lifestyles shown in short videos [48, 70].

A specific sensitivity also emerged regarding marital status in the healthy cohort. The positive correlation between video use and pessimistic bias was statistically significant only among married individuals. This empirical finding highlights the complex dynamics of a shared household environment. Spouses living together often consume similar digital content and discuss mutual health concerns. When recommended videos introduce health related topics this shared environment likely facilitates the mutual resonance of media-related health concerns. The initial worry associated with passive video watching corresponds to a shared pessimistic appraisal about their health [71–73]. Unmarried individuals lack this immediate daily interaction which theoretically coincides with a lower likelihood of mutual resonance regarding pessimistic biases.

In stark contrast to the healthy cohort the frail cohort showed remarkable consistency across all demographic groups. The correlations linking active digital use to optimistic bias remained similar regardless of gender or marital status. This surprising uniformity points to a profound leveling phenomenon associated with physical decline. When older adults face the clear reality of actual physical deterioration their basic need for alternative coping resources appears to correspond to an overriding of traditional social and demographic differences. Seeking helpful health knowledge through news or finding support through chatting theoretically emerges as a universal behavioral pattern among this specific demographic [3, 74–76]. In the context of actual physical frailty, the positive behavioral associations of active digital use cross demographic boundaries. The protective statistical associations linked to these active behaviors operate consistently across different subgroups. This uniformity highlights a shared behavioral response to the universal challenges of aging and physical vulnerability.

Theoretical and empirical contributions

By distinguishing our methodological applications from our conceptual findings this study provides essential theoretical and empirical insights for the literature on digital engagement and health perception among older adults. Theoretically we advance the digital divide framework by moving beyond the traditional metric of basic internet access. Our empirical analytical framework demonstrates that the behavioral correlates of digital technology are not uniform but depend fundamentally on the specific functional modality. Rather than attempting to validate unmeasured internal mechanisms we propose that active digital engagement may theoretically reflect existing cognitive and social reserves that co-occur with positive health evaluations. Conversely passive algorithmic consumption can be theoretically interpreted as a behavioral pattern that systematically associates with pessimistic health appraisals.

Empirically this study expands the literature on subjective aging by conceptualizing and measuring the health perception bias matrix. This novel empirical approach moves the research field beyond traditional measures of simple self-rated health. It allows researchers to precisely quantify how specific digital behavioral patterns systematically associate with the evaluative mismatch between objective physical decline and subjective appraisal. Ultimately these theoretical and empirical insights confirm that digital aging is a highly differentiated process. They highlight that the subjective health evaluations of older adults in the digital era are profoundly intertwined with the active or passive nature of their technology use rather than simply being a result of general internet access.

Practical implications for calibrating digital engagement

The findings offer practical implications for guiding digital engagement among older adults. Acknowledging that evaluative tendencies are deeply rooted in lifelong histories these recommendations focus on aligning digital environments with diverse behavioral patterns rather than attempting to alter fundamental personal outlooks. At the community level programs should avoid broadly restricting internet access. Instead, local senior centers could offer practical digital skills workshops. These workshops would train older adults especially those with less education to critically navigate exaggerated online content and curate their digital feeds in ways that suit their specific informational needs and preexisting dispositions. At the household level younger family members can act as digital guides. They can assist older adults in customizing their online platforms ensuring that the digital environment accommodates their existing evaluative baselines while facilitating active social connections. This tailored approach empirically aligns with a more balanced evaluation of personal health.

Furthermore, the technology sector must advance age friendly digital environments beyond basic visual adjustments like larger text. Platform developers should design algorithms that respect the diverse lifelong trajectories observed among the aging population. Because passive video watching strongly co-occurs with pessimistic health appraisals among specific subgroups applications could implement customizable content controls that empower users to break the cycle of endless algorithmic feeds. Additionally digital platforms could introduce senior focused curation tools. For specific demographic groups such as older men these specialized options would prioritize authoritative medical knowledge and allow users to limit their exposure to unrealistic depictions of aging. By aligning software architecture with the preexisting evaluative tendencies and behavioral patterns of older adults these targeted changes allow digital platforms to serve as supportive tools for navigating later life.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First and most importantly, the nature of our secondary survey data precludes the direct measurement of internal cognitive and evaluative mechanisms. While we draw upon established theoretical frameworks to interpret our findings our dataset does not contain specific scales to quantify variables like cognitive overload or relative deprivation. Therefore, the mechanisms discussed in this study serve strictly as preliminary interpretative lenses to explain the observed behavioral correlations rather than directly tested empirical variables. Future research should employ primary data collection with validated scales to directly test these potential mediating pathways.

Second, the discussion must strictly acknowledge that the observed associations are correlations and not necessarily causal. We must explicitly note that while Propensity Score Matching can reduce selection bias based on observed covariates it inherently cannot establish causality. It is highly plausible that health status and preexisting optimistic or pessimistic views actually affect internet use and the specific content older adults are interested in accessing. These individuals possess a long-life history and their fundamental evaluative traits may have already been established in their early adulthood. Moreover, there can be lifelong paths between health status education and personality traits such as inherent optimism and pessimism leading to later-life interests. These lifelong trajectories rather than the digital environment itself likely dictate what type of internet content people access based on their life history. We cannot assume that simply modifying the content and use of the internet will inherently make people optimistic or pessimistic. A cross-sectional design cannot answer these questions and further longitudinal or experimental research is strictly needed before making assumptions about causal pathways or the true effectiveness of interventions.

Third, we must acknowledge the inherent limitations in our categorization of the independent variables. While we theoretically classified digital modalities into distinct information-oriented social-oriented and entertainment-oriented categories contemporary digital platforms frequently exhibit functional convergence. In practice these online activities substantially overlap. For example, watching short videos is primarily an entertainment-oriented activity but it can also involve significant information acquisition such as viewing health tutorials or daily news clips. Similarly social chatting applications routinely serve as active conduits for sharing news articles. Our classification relies on the primary self-reported functional use of the respondents and represents a necessary theoretical simplification of highly complex digital behaviors. This simplified categorization cannot fully capture the multifaceted nature of modern internet use where a single application fulfills multiple diverse needs simultaneously. Future studies using objective digital tracking app-level data and detailed content analysis are necessary to disentangle these overlapping functions.

Finally, using self-reported survey data for both digital engagement and health status introduces potential recall bias and social desirability bias. Respondents might underestimate their passive screen time and overestimate their reading of authoritative news. In addition, subjective health evaluations can reflect temporary emotional states rather than enduring cognitive appraisals.

Conclusions

This study broadens the understanding of digital aging by moving beyond the simple perspective of basic internet access. By classifying digital engagement into specific functional modalities our empirical analysis demonstrated that active information-oriented and social-oriented uses consistently correlate with more positive health perceptions across older adult populations. Conversely passive entertainment-oriented video consumption is strongly associated with pessimistic biases particularly among objectively healthy older adults.

As extensively discussed, these findings provide important theoretical and empirical contributions to the literature on digital engagement and subjective health evaluations. By conceptualizing the functional usage divide and applying Latent Profile Analysis to precisely isolate health perception biases this research establishes that the empirical correlates of digital technology are fundamentally not uniform. Crucially we reiterate that these associations reflect complex statistical co-occurrences intertwined with lifelong trajectories rather than simple causal pathways. Ultimately this study provides a valuable behavioral perspective for public health initiatives. The correlational evidence emphasizes the critical need to foster content-specific digital literacy and design tailored digital environments that carefully accommodate the diverse preexisting evaluative tendencies of an aging society.
