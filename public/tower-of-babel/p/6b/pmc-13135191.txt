Understanding Supply-Side Implementation Barriers Affecting Child Nutrition Outcomes in the Take-Home Ration Scheme: A Qualitative Study From Madhya Pradesh, India
Authors: Arun Kokane, Harijith K R, Junaid Mushtaq, Nikita Rawat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChild undernutrition persists as a critical public health challenge across India despite the extensive infrastructure of the Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) program; child undernutrition remains persistently high, especially in Madhya Pradesh, where stunting affects 35.5% of children. The Take-Home Ration (THR) component, intended to provide supplementary nutrition to women and young children, continues to underperform due to interconnected supply-chain, governance, and community-level challenges. This study aimed to examine supply-side implementation barriers and assess the current status of THR distribution and Social and Behaviour Change Communication (SBCC) activities under ICDS in Madhya Pradesh, India, from a multi-stakeholder perspective.MethodsThis qualitative, multi-method study combined Net-Map supply chain analysis and semi-structured key informant interviews with 15 stakeholders spanning state, district, block, and community levels in Hoshangabad district, Madhya Pradesh, India. Thematic analysis using Braun and Clarke's framework identified systemic barriers and adaptive practices across seven organisational hierarchies.ResultsNetwork mapping revealed that the State Nutrition Committee, manufacturing divisions, and Anganwadi Centres function as non-substitutable backbone actors whose dependencies create systemic vulnerabilities. Identified constraints included multi-tiered supply chains causing cumulative delays, prolonged government payment cycles restricting manufacturer capacity, and product-community misalignment between fortification requirements and taste acceptability. Additional barriers comprised weak inter-departmental coordination, limited frontline capacity, and inadequate community engagement due to opportunity costs and digital connectivity gaps. Locally generated adaptive solutions-such as product modifications and community recipe innovations-remained institutionally disconnected from higher governance systems.ConclusionStrengthening THR implementation requires a coordinated systems approach emphasising fiscal accountability, decentralised decision-making, and context-specific, culturally grounded social and behaviour change communication. Institutionalising mechanisms that integrate frontline-derived learning into policy adaptation is essential for achieving resilient nutrition program delivery and improved outcomes.

Introduction

Child undernutrition is a significant public health challenge in India, as India accounts for nearly one-third of the global burden of stunting and wasting among children under five years of age [1]. Despite this, data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) reveal that Madhya Pradesh faces high nutritional challenges for children. Presently, the prevalence of stunting is at 35.5%, wasting at 19.3%, and underweight at 32.1% [2]. The Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) scheme, which was launched in 1975, currently operates in about 1.4 million Anganwadi Centres (AWCs) nationwide, representing the most extensive integrated program for early childhood care and development ​[3].

The Take-Home Ration (THR) component of ICDS is implemented to provide ready-to-eat or ready-to-cook supplementary nutrition to children aged 6-36 months and pregnant and lactating women from vulnerable households [4]. Along with the THR distribution system, ICDS and SBCC strategies are implemented for improving feeding practices to enhance program utilisation [5]. ICDS services have expanded significantly over the past years, but some evaluations suggest that implementation challenges continue to limit the program's effectiveness in addressing child undernutrition ​[6].

Despite various changes and continuous research on the ICDS program improvement, significant implementation gaps remain in understanding the complex interplay between THR provision, SBCC strategies, and community feeding practices from the perspectives of diverse stakeholders across the implementation hierarchy [7]. The previous studies on the Take-Home Ration (THR) program have concentrated mainly on quantitative indicators or the perspectives of a single group of stakeholders. This narrow focus has limited the understanding of the complex challenges and opportunities within the THR system. Moreover, there is still little evidence on how current THR formulations and SBCC strategies fit with the actual feeding habits, household practices, and cultural preferences of families in rural areas.

This study sought to address existing knowledge gaps by employing a qualitative, multi-stakeholder approach to examine the implementation of the THR program in Madhya Pradesh. Specifically, it aimed to explore supply-side stakeholders' perspectives on operational, governance, and human resource challenges influencing the THR supply chain, as well as to assess the current status of THR distribution and SBCC activities under ICDS in Madhya Pradesh, India. The study had two specific objectives: (1) To explore supply-side stakeholders' perspectives on operational, governance, and human resource challenges influencing the THR supply chain; and (2) To assess the current status of THR distribution and SBCC activities under ICDS in Madhya Pradesh, India.

Materials and methods

Study design and context

This qualitative study was conducted as the formative research component of a larger cluster randomised controlled trial (cRCT) examining the effectiveness of improved THR combined with behaviour change communication interventions to enhance nutritional outcomes for children across six states in India [8]. This qualitative research specifically identified supply-side implementation barriers and facilitators affecting the quality, utilisation, and effectiveness of the THR scheme in Madhya Pradesh. The study was conducted in Hoshangabad district, located in the central region of Madhya Pradesh, India. AWCs and associated administrative structures across two blocks in the district were purposively selected as study sites.

Study population and sampling strategy

Using purposive sampling with maximum variation, we recruited key informants representing all organisational levels from state-level policy formulation to community-level service delivery. The sampling framework was designed to capture diverse stakeholder perspectives on the challenges and facilitators of the THR supply chain. We recruited 15 key informants across seven distinct stakeholder categories: Anganwadi Workers (AWWs, n=8), Child Development Project Officers (CDPOs, n=2), District Program Officers (DPOs, n=1), State Program Officers (SPOs, n=1), THR Manufacturing Unit Managers (n=1), United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) Program Representatives (n=1), and Non Government Organizations (NGO) State Program Coordinators (n=1). Participants were eligible if they held direct or indirect responsibility for THR program planning, implementation, monitoring, or utilisation for ≥6 months at the time of data collection. The sample size of 15 was determined a priori based on the purposive maximum variation sampling strategy, designed to ensure representation across all seven stakeholder categories spanning the full implementation hierarchy. Rather than being guided by iterative saturation testing, the sampling framework prioritised comprehensive tier coverage to enable a systems-level perspective.

Data collection methods

Data collection was conducted over four months (April-August 2025) using a sequential two-step mixed-methods approach that combined supply chain mapping with in-depth qualitative interviews.

Step 1: Net-Mapping of THR Supply Chain

To systematically trace THR flows from policy formulation through distribution, net-mapping exercises were conducted in two separate sessions. The first session included four participants comprising district and block-level officials, and the second session included one state-level official. Using large sheets of paper and colour-coded markers (blue for information flows, red for financial flows, green for food commodity flows), participants identified all relevant actors from national-level policy bodies (e.g., the Secretary, Government of India) through to frontline workers and beneficiaries. The net-mapping protocol followed a structured five-step process: (1) mapping the supply chain flow; (2) identifying key actors at each stage; (3) clarifying the roles of each actor; (4) linking actors through directional arrows indicating the nature of the relationship (food, information, or financial flows); and (5) assessing influence through participatory scoring. Sequenced actors according to their formal roles, responsibilities, and organisational hierarchy. Mapped directional arrows indicating pathways and relationships for data, funds, and commodities. Influence scores were assigned using a 1-5 star rating scale based on pre-defined criteria: each actor's contribution to ensuring THR quality, timeliness of delivery, efficiency of fund transfer, and capacity for adaptive program management. These criteria were explained to participants prior to scoring. Ratings were assigned through facilitated group consensus, with the research team recording all agreements and areas of discussion. A trained research team member recorded detailed notes documenting points of agreement, disagreement, and key insights. Maps were photographed and systematically analysed to identify critical gaps, bottlenecks, and key stakeholders for in-depth investigation.

Step 2: In-Depth Key Informant Interviews

Based on findings from the supply chain mapping, semi-structured key informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted with the 15 identified stakeholders. The network mapping findings directly informed the content and focus of the interview guides in a deliberate, sequential manner. Specifically, actors who received high influence ratings and identified bottleneck nodes during the mapping exercise informed the development of targeted probes within the interview guides. For example, the five-star influence rating assigned to manufacturers prompted specific probes in the manufacturer guide around payment systems and production constraints, while the identification of financial flow disruptions during mapping generated explicit probes on government payment delays across multiple stakeholder guides. Separate, contextually tailored interview guides were developed for each stakeholder category, informed by the comprehensive literature review on THR implementation challenges, consultation with nutrition and implementation science experts, and preliminary findings from the broader intervention trial.

Interviews were conducted in Hindi (the local language) by trained research staff. The data collection team was stratified by organisational level: two project scientists with extensive qualitative research and nutrition expertise conducted interviews with state- and district-level officials; project technical staff holding Master's degrees in nutrition science conducted interviews with Anganwadi Workers and block-level coordinators. This strategy ensured contextual appropriateness and rapport with diverse participant groups. Each interview lasted 20-40 minutes. All interviews were digitally audio-recorded following written informed consent; interviews were supplemented with detailed field notes documenting non-verbal communication, environmental context, and emerging themes requiring follow-up.

Data management and analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim in Hindi by trained transcribers and translated into English by bilingual qualitative researchers familiar with the local socio-cultural context. To ensure translation accuracy, 20% of transcripts were back-translated and cross-verified. Data were analysed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's framework [9], combining deductive codes with inductive codes emerging from the data. Coding was performed manually by two independent researchers, who reviewed transcripts line by line to identify key ideas and concepts. Independent coding enhanced objectivity and minimised individual bias.

Codes were subsequently organised into a hierarchical framework aligned with the study objectives, covering three major domains: operational challenges (procurement, storage, logistics), governance barriers (policy clarity, coordination, accountability), and human resource constraints (training, motivation, capacity). The codebook was developed iteratively: beginning with a deductive framework derived from the three study domains and expanded inductively as new codes emerged from the first three transcripts, which were independently coded by both researchers before the full codebook was finalised. A comprehensive codebook was developed to maintain consistency across analyses. The research team held bi-weekly meetings to review coding discrepancies, refine categories, and ensure analytical rigour through consensus. In instances of persistent coding disagreement, the principal investigator reviewed the contested codes as a third reviewer and provided a deciding interpretation. During qualitative analysis, emergent themes were synthesised into coherent narratives that illustrated variations across stakeholder groups, such as differences in perspectives between frontline workers and district officials.

For the integration of Net-Map and thematic analysis, interview narratives were cross-referenced with network influence scores during the analytical phase. For example, the five-star rating assigned to manufacturers during mapping was corroborated by interview accounts describing how payment delays to manufacturers cascaded disruptions across the entire supply system. One notable discrepancy observed between methods was that the District Program Officer received a moderate influence rating (three stars) during network mapping, yet interview accounts from AWWs and CDPOs suggested that DPO-level responsiveness to field-level complaints was perceived as lower still in practice, pointing to a gap between formal positional influence and practical operational responsiveness.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institute Human Ethical Committee, All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS) Bhopal (approval number: IHEC-LOP/2024/P24/044). The data collection team obtained written informed consent from all participants prior to data collection. The study maintained participant confidentiality through anonymisation; all transcripts and recordings were identified by participant codes rather than names and stored on password-protected, encrypted drives. Participants retained the right to refuse or withdraw from the study at any time without consequence.

Backbone Actors and Hierarchical Dynamics

The THR supply chain operates through a vertically integrated yet interdependent hierarchy. Three actors form its operational backbone: the State Nutrition Committee, Division/Manufacturers/Vendors, and Anganwadi centres (AWCs), each translating national policy intent into tangible community benefit through distinct but complementary functions.

State nutrition committee (strategic policy hub): The State Nutrition Committee, comprising senior officials, AIIMS nutrition experts, and UNICEF advisors, functions as the central policy and coordination node. It establishes food standards through age- and group-specific Recommended Dietary Allowances (RDA), approves weekly THR recipes, and formulates cost-sharing ratios between central and state governments. The Committee consolidates district-level demand forecasts and communicates procurement targets directly to manufacturers. Upward flows of field feedback and implementation reports enable continuous program refinement. This dual-direction flow of information positions the Committee as the principal mechanism aligning national policy with state execution.

Division/manufacturers/vendors (commodity production and quality assurance): The manufacturing division operationalises state directives through procurement, production, and quality control. Raw materials (wheat, rice, soya) are acquired through closed-tender processes, ensuring quality and price stability. Multi-tiered quality assurance, including in-house and external laboratory testing, ensures product safety and compliance. Financial inflows from state accounts sustain production, while outflows include certified THR consignments, invoices, and quality reports. The manufacturer's role in converting policy prescriptions into standardised, consumable rations situates it as the operational core of the supply chain.

Anganwadi centers (frontline service and data nodes): AWCs represent the program's community interface, where policy outcomes manifest as household-level nutrition support. Through the Poshan Tracker digital platform, AWC workers register beneficiaries, generate localised demand, and record THR distribution. They verify consignments, maintain storage records, and collect beneficiary feedback on product use and acceptability. These real-time data streams support demand forecasting and inform upstream recipe or operational modifications. The AWC's proximity to communities and its dual role in service delivery and data generation confer both formal and informal influence within the system.

Intermediate Coordination and Oversight Actors

Block-Level Authorities coordinate logistics between districts and AWCs. Their responsibilities include route scheduling, receipt verification, storage management, and reimbursement of transport costs. Their four-star influence reflects substantial operational control within the distribution tier. District Program Officers provide fiscal oversight and program monitoring. They compile bills, authorise fund transfers, and review implementation reports from blocks and AWCs. Their role is supervisory rather than operational, resulting in moderate influence. GOI Secretary provides overarching policy direction and national monitoring. This role involves issuing Supplementary Nutrition Program guidelines, approving cost-sharing ratios, and reviewing compliance data from states.

Resource Flows and System Integration

Three interlinked flows sustain the THR system: Information flows (blue) connect all levels, translating policy downward and transmitting demand and feedback upward. These loops enable adaptive management and evidence-based decision-making. Financial flows (red) originate from state treasuries to fund production, transport, and AWC-level operations. Timely disbursement is essential to avoid production or distribution delays. Food commodity flows (green) trace the physical journey of THR from raw material procurement to manufacturing, warehousing, block distribution, and ultimately community-level consumption. Together, these flows create a vertically integrated but tightly coupled system whose resilience depends on synchronisation across nodes, as shown in Figure 1.

Key findings from multi-stakeholder perspectives on THR implementation challenges

Thematic analysis identified six overarching themes encompassing supply-side and demand-side dimensions of take-home ration (THR) implementation challenges. These themes encapsulated distinct but interconnected barriers across the program ecosystem: structural supply chain inefficiencies, product-community fit challenges, governance coordination gaps, workforce capacity deficits, community engagement limitations, and actionable stakeholder recommendations. Each theme contained between two and four sub-themes, capturing the nuanced perspectives of different stakeholder groups. Below, we present the major findings organised by theme, with illustrative quotes from representative participants. Supply chain complexity and governance gaps were the most cross-cutting themes, raised consistently by participants across all seven stakeholder levels. Product-community fit challenges were most prominently highlighted by AWWs and the THR manufacturer. Community engagement and SBCC barriers were predominantly surfaced by AWWs and development partners. While formal frequency counts are not presented consistent with Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis framework, which prioritises depth of interpretation over quantification, the differential salience of themes across stakeholder groups is noted to assist readers in contextualising the findings. Although some themes share conceptual territory (for example, governance gaps and community engagement limitations both reflect institutional fragmentation at different levels), they were maintained as distinct themes because they address qualitatively different aspects of the program ecosystem: one at the inter-departmental governance level and the other at the community-facing service delivery level, as shown in Table 3.

Supply chain complexity and operational barriers

The take-home ration supply chain operates through a multi-layered administrative structure spanning state procurement offices, district aggregation facilities, block-level distribution hubs, and frontline Anganwadi centres. This hierarchical distribution pathway, while designed for centralised oversight and standardisation, generated recurrent operational bottlenecks at manufacturing, transport, and distribution stages. Three interconnected sub-themes characterise these supply-side barriers.

Multi-Level Supply Chain Structure and Distribution Flow Challenges

The THR distribution pathway involves multiple administrative tiers with distinct approval procedures and handoff points. A THR manufacturing manager described the operational process: "Our process starts from Samuh with the board of directors from three districts (Harda, Betul, Narmadapuram). Orders come from the Mahila Bal Vikas office in Bhopal and then to our manufacturing plant".

Each handoff across administrative levels introduced cumulative processing delays before products reached end-distribution points. Last-mile vulnerabilities further compromised product integrity during transit and unloading. The extended timeline from order placement to final delivery undermined responsiveness to supply fluctuations and created persistent inventory management challenges. Communication gaps between state procurement offices and block-level distribution hubs resulted in forecasting mismatches where demand signals did not align with supply availability, forcing redistribution from centres with surplus stock to those experiencing shortages. This reactive rather than proactive supply management pattern created cyclical availability gaps.

Financial and Payment System Bottlenecks

Government payment delays and deferred financing emerged as critical constraints limiting manufacturing capacity and production continuity. Manufacturers reported substantial pending receivables from the government that restricted their ability to procure raw materials from suppliers who demanded upfront payment. A THR manufacturing manager explained: "Suppliers demand payment upfront. We currently have ₹3-4 crore tied up in pending payments from the government".

These extended payment cycles, spanning multiple months between product delivery and government remittance, placed manufacturers under capital constraints that either disrupted production schedules or forced them to absorb costs through retained earnings, reducing their operational flexibility. The systemic mismatch between government fund availability and raw material procurement windows meant that even when funds were eventually disbursed, the opportunity to purchase raw materials at optimal market prices had often passed.

Quality Control Procedures and Storage Limitations

Quality assurance mechanisms existed at production facilities, including in-house testing of nutritional parameters and external verification through NABL-accredited laboratories. However, restricted storage capacity at manufacturing sites forced just-in-time procurement operations where manufacturers could maintain minimal buffer stocks. A THR manufacturing manager noted: "We cannot store more than 20-25 tonnes of any raw material. When supplies run out, we immediately issue a new purchase order".

This storage constraint reduced system resilience to supply disruptions and left the program vulnerable to raw material shortages that could interrupt production. At distribution endpoints, quality assurance failures undermined community acceptance. Contamination during transit, particularly sand in fortified premixes, prompted families to reject rations, directly reducing program effectiveness. Infrastructure inadequacies at service delivery sites compounded these quality concerns, where Anganwadi centres lacked secure storage facilities with appropriate climate control, exposing THR stocks to moisture, pests, and temperature fluctuations that degraded product quality before consumption.

Product acceptance and utilisation patterns

Community acceptance and household utilisation of take-home ration products exhibited substantial variation driven by sensory characteristics, preparation requirements, availability of time and resources, and household consumption dynamics. These demand-side factors directly determined whether target beneficiaries actually consumed prescribed rations and whether nutritional intake reached intended levels.

Taste Preferences and Sensory Acceptance Issues

Sensory characteristics, such as taste, post-storage odour, and texture, strongly influenced consumer acceptance of THR products and repeat consumption patterns. Multiple THR items were reported to develop off-flavours after storage, with khichdi-based products perceived as bitter, and rancid odours attributed to oil oxidation during extended storage periods. An Anganwadi worker (AWW Village 4, Block 2) described: "Khichdi smells bad after storage, especially due to excess oil. Khichdi tastes bitter".

This sensory degradation reflected a fundamental tension in product formulation: ingredients necessary for nutritional fortification (particularly soya protein to enhance amino acid profiles) contributed to unpalatable sensory profiles, reducing consumption despite improved nutritional adequacy. Monotonous daily consumption of identical products further reduced appeal and adherence. Communities preferred product variety, yet increasing formulation diversity required manufacturers to develop, test, and obtain approval for multiple products, a resource-intensive process with delayed regulatory timelines. The design dilemma persisted; stronger fortification improved nutritional value but compromised sensory acceptance, while taste-optimised formulations risked reducing nutritional density and program effectiveness.

Household Consumption Dynamics and Target-Group Sharing

Rations designated for specific vulnerable groups, children, pregnant women, and lactating mothers, were routinely shared across entire households rather than consumed by intended beneficiaries. An Anganwadi worker (AWW Village 3, Block 2) reported: Families roast halwa mix and make laddoos or barfi. But many use it for the whole family".

This intra-household sharing behaviour reflected rational household decision-making within contexts of food scarcity and cultural norms of equitable kinship-based food distribution. From program perspectives, sharing diluted the intended nutritional dose to individual vulnerable members, reducing targeting efficiency. However, from a household perspective, focusing on nutrition for a single household member while others remained undernourished conflicted with household food security strategies and social obligations. Working mothers faced additional utilisation constraints, as time requirements for daily preparation conflicted with their livelihood activities. Women engaged in agricultural or daily wage labour lacked consistent opportunity for daily THR preparation, leading them to prepare larger quantities less frequently, which inadvertently encouraged whole-household sharing rather than individual targeted consumption.

Preparation Methods and Recipe Guidance Limitations

Packaging provided only minimal and generic preparation instructions, limiting adaptation to local cooking practices, available fuel sources, and household preferences. Frontline workers routinely employed locally appropriate modifications, enriching khichdi with peanuts or ghee and adjusting cooking methods to available equipment, yet these practical adaptations were neither documented on packaging nor standardised across delivery points. A THR manufacturing manager confirmed: "The pouch instructions only state mixing with hot water or milk. No local recipes are provided".

This gap between standardised product design and contextual preparation realities constrained product acceptability and consistent nutrient delivery. The absence of localised recipe guidance was a missed opportunity to leverage frontline workers' knowledge and community preferences to enhance product utilisation. Communities lacked confidence to modify preparation methods despite possessing traditional knowledge of complementary ingredients and cooking techniques that could enhance both palatability and nutritional bioavailability.

Systemic coordination and governance gaps

The take-home ration program was implemented across multiple government departments with distinct reporting structures, targets, and operational instructions. This institutional fragmentation created governance challenges affecting program coherence, information flow, and adaptive management capacity.

Inter-Departmental Coordination Challenges and Structural Silos

Implementation responsibilities were distributed across departments, with unclear accountability and distinct performance metrics. A state program officer described: "Everyone has their own target, their own system, their own instruction". This departmental fragmentation generated redundant data collection systems, conflicting operational priorities, and inconsistent messaging to beneficiaries and implementation staff. However, positive collaboration examples existed at the local level, where Anganwadi workers, Auxiliary Nurse Midwives, and Accredited Social Health Activists worked as integrated teams on reproductive health and child survival initiatives. These instances of effective cross-sectoral cooperation demonstrated that collaboration was operationally feasible, yet such practices remained ad hoc and dependent on individual initiative rather than embedded within formal program structure. The absence of systemised coordination mechanisms prevented scalable replication of successful collaborative models.

Supervision and Monitoring System Effectiveness

Supervisory capacity was severely inadequate relative to program scale. An Anganwadi worker (AWW Village 1, Block 1) reported: "The supervisor comes once in two to three months. CDPO comes maybe once in five to six months". With approximately 97,000 anganwadi centres across the state, this supervisory visit frequency was insufficient for real-time troubleshooting, performance feedback, or adaptive program management. Digital monitoring tools partially compensated for in-person supervision limitations through the Poshan and Sampark apps, which achieved coverage of 70-80% of centres where internet connectivity permitted. However, unreliable connectivity in remote areas and inconsistent staff training on app functionality limited digital platform uptake precisely where alternative supervision mechanisms were weakest. This created accountability gaps, with the most geographically remote and potentially underserved populations receiving the least supervisory oversight and support.

Technology Integration Challenges and Digital System Barriers

Digital monitoring systems faced substantial operational hurdles that reduced functionality and user adoption. An Anganwadi worker (AWW Village 3, Block 1) noted: "Face capture delays discourage beneficiaries; Jio network is unreliable here". Poor network coverage, latency in biometric authentication features, and inadequate user training led to partial or inconsistent platform utilisation. Staff training on app updates was frequently inadequate, with workers uncertain about evolving platform functionality. These technical barriers impeded efficient data reporting and demotivated staff engagement with digital systems. The digital divide created by unequal internet access paradoxically disadvantaged remote populations who simultaneously faced the weakest traditional supervision mechanisms, concentrating program oversight challenges in the most difficult-to-serve geographies.

Capacity building and human resource constraints

Frontline workforce capacity emerged as a critical implementation bottleneck. Anganwadi workers, who serve as the primary interface between the program and communities, faced educational and training deficits that constrained service quality and program effectiveness.

Training Gaps and Educational Limitations Among Frontline Workers

Educational attainment among Anganwadi workers varied substantially, with many possessing only primary-level formal education. An Anganwadi worker (AWW Village 4, Block 2) reflected: "Many of us are 5th pass. We need basic, practical training regularly". Workers consistently requested recurrent, hands-on skill development opportunities focused on nutrition counselling, anthropometric measurement, and THR preparation techniques, ideally two to three times annually, to build and maintain competence. Program administrators acknowledged chronic underinvestment in training budgets, with funding for in-person skill development absent for extended periods. This training deficit directly limited the quality of community-facing services, constrained workers' ability to provide tailored guidance on product utilisation and dietary behaviour change, and reduced their confidence in program messaging. Specific skill gaps, including anthropometric assessment procedures and contextualised THR recipe preparation, remained unaddressed despite being identified as essential for effective program delivery.

Infrastructure and Resource Deficits Affecting Service Delivery

Physical infrastructure at many Anganwadi centres fell substantially short of program requirements. An Anganwadi worker (AWW Village 1, Block 2) reported: "Our Anganwadi has not been constructed. We operate from the school kitchen". Many centres operated from temporary or shared premises without dedicated space, adequate climate control, or secure storage facilities. The absence of dedicated THR storage exposed ration stocks to moisture, pests, and temperature fluctuations, creating food safety risks that could undermine community trust. The lack of child development materials and basic equipment reduced centres' appeal as community gathering spaces and constrained their capacity to deliver comprehensive early childhood development services. These infrastructure deficits, compounded by limited resource allocations for maintenance and supplies, created barriers to service quality that persisted independent of worker effort or commitment, representing structural rather than individual implementation challenges.

Community engagement and social behaviour change communication

Community mobilisation and behaviour change communication (SBCC) initiatives employed diverse strategies and communication channels, though their reach and cultural appropriateness varied substantially across urban and rural contexts and socioeconomic groups.

Communication Strategies and Tool Diversity Across Implementation Levels

Behaviour change communication initiatives deployed multiple delivery channels, flipbooks, radio jingles, short videos, and household counselling, reflecting pragmatic attempts to accommodate varied community access patterns and communication preferences. A development partner (an NGO program officer) representative explained: "We developed an anaemia-fortification flipbook and radio jingles to raise awareness among people in the easiest possible way".

However, digital channel effectiveness was highly context-dependent. Urban and peri-urban populations with access to smartphones, television, and internet connectivity could engage with YouTube-based content and WhatsApp-facilitated messaging. Rural populations with limited digital infrastructure found these channels inaccessible, creating unequal program exposure across geographic and socioeconomic strata. This fragmented channel approach, while reflecting adaptation to local infrastructure realities, risked reinforcing existing health communication inequities, with advantaged populations receiving digitally enhanced messaging while disadvantaged populations remained underexposed to behaviour change interventions.

Language and Cultural Adaptation Challenges

Standardised behaviour change materials frequently employed state-level languages that diverged substantially from local community dialects, creating comprehension gaps between program messaging and community understanding. A CDPO (Block Administrative Official Block 1) noted: "Beneficiaries often do not understand standard Hindi. They speak in Malwa, in Bundeli". This language misalignment represented a significant barrier to the effectiveness of behaviour change and contributed to perceived program irrelevance among communities. Conversely, frontline workers who communicated in local dialects and employed culturally grounded messaging examples achieved substantially better community receptiveness and engagement. The mismatch between standardised institutional materials and local communication contexts remained inadequately addressed despite frontline evidence that linguistic and cultural localisation substantially improved intervention effectiveness and community trust.

Community Resistance and Engagement Dynamics

Opportunity costs substantially limited household participation in program activities, particularly among economically vulnerable populations. An Anganwadi worker (AWW Village 2, Block 1) reported: "Labourers skip Village Health Nutrition Day (VHND) to avoid losing ₹300-₹350 daily wages". Daily wage losses deterred participation among labour-dependent households, reducing program exposure precisely among the most nutritionally vulnerable populations. This economic barrier reflected competing demands for immediate survival income and access to health services, rather than structural constraints or community indifference. Frontline workers employed adaptive engagement strategies, including brief, targeted household visits during non-work hours, to compensate for barriers to formal participation partially. These adaptive practices reflected both program commitment and recognition of structural constraints, though they necessarily reduced the comprehensiveness of behaviour change exposure compared to formal group sessions.

Stakeholder-specific implementation recommendations

Participants across all stakeholder groups, including manufacturing managers, Anganwadi workers, block and district administrators, and program officers, offered operationally grounded improvement suggestions reflecting their direct implementation experience and frontline knowledge of program operations.

Supply Chain and Production Enhancement Strategies

Manufacturing stakeholders proposed targeted interventions addressing identified financial and logistical constraints. A THR manufacturer recommended: "If Mahila Bal Vikas gives us some advance money, then we will get some things for making or supplying". Partial advance payments to manufacturers, contingent on production capacity and past performance, were proposed as mechanisms to stabilise raw material procurement and reduce production interruptions caused by delayed government remittance. Enhanced secondary packaging specifications were suggested to reduce contamination and breakage during distribution. Expedited approval pathways for product recipe modifications were recommended to increase manufacturer responsiveness to field-derived feedback on product acceptance and community preferences. These recommendations reflected recognition that supply chain reliability and product responsiveness required systemic support beyond individual manufacturer capacity.

Program Design and Service Delivery Improvements

Administrative stakeholders recommended systemic modifications to strengthen workforce quality and digital infrastructure. Merit-based recruitment coupled with competency assessment of Anganwadi workers was proposed as a mechanism to improve frontline service quality and program delivery consistency. Upgrading digital infrastructure at fair-price shops and distribution points was recommended to strengthen THR inventory tracking and reduce stock discrepancies. Product diversification, introducing varied THR flavours and formats, was suggested to reduce monotony and enhance acceptability across different population segments. Incorporating short, locally tailored video messages into behaviour change efforts was proposed as a strategy to leverage digital channels while maintaining cultural and linguistic relevance through community-based production.

Community-Level Operational Refinements

Frontline workers offered practical suggestions grounded in direct household engagement and implementation experience. An Anganwadi worker (AWW Village 4, Block 1) recommended: "Separate packets for pregnant women and children will help avoid misuse". Packaging modifications, particularly separate sachets designated for children and pregnant women, were suggested to facilitate targeted distribution and reduce intra-household sharing while acknowledging household decision-making autonomy. Component separation (maintaining sugar and oil as distinct ingredients rather than premixing) was recommended to allow households flexibility in product preparation, enabling taste adjustments and preserving palatability during storage. These community-derived suggestions reflected a sophisticated understanding of household consumption dynamics and represented low-cost, implementable modifications to enhance both targeting efficiency and product utilisation.

Discussion

This multi-stakeholder qualitative analysis reveals the intertwined supply, demand, and governance obstacles that persistently weaken take-home ration (THR) program implementation under India's Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS). Using network mapping, this study reveals that the State Nutrition Committee, manufacturers, and Anganwadi centres are linchpins of program success, their central roles conferring both authority and responsibility. However, this concentrated influence produces systemic vulnerabilities when these backbone actors experience disruptions; the consequences quickly ripple through all layers of the system, highlighting how single points of failure fundamentally threaten program continuity.

This study examined implementation barriers from stakeholder perspectives and did not directly measure nutritional outcomes such as stunting, wasting, or program utilization rates. Any associations between the identified supply-side barriers and child nutritional outcomes referenced in this discussion are inferred from the existing literature and should not be interpreted as empirically established causal relationships within this study's design. Conclusions are appropriately framed as stakeholder-derived, context-specific insights rather than system-wide definitive findings.

These results not only reaffirm concerns seen in earlier evaluations, including chronic supply chain breakdowns, quality assurance shortfalls, and limited frontline workforce capacity, but also deepen understanding of how these issues emerge from the program's structural design. For example, prior evidence from Maharashtra highlighted contamination in ration packets, poor storage conditions, and limited community awareness [10]. Similar investigations in Rajasthan and Jharkhand reported acute ration stockouts linked to supplier payment delays, findings that align closely with financial constraints and implementation lags observed by manufacturers in this study's setting [11]. Rather than reflecting isolated incidents, such patterns surface across diverse geographies and stakeholders, suggesting these are foundational weaknesses in program architecture rather than exceptions.

The hierarchical structure in the network mapping is not inherently problematic; centralisation can bring oversight and efficiency. However, a lack of redundancy or adaptable feedback mechanisms can hinder resilience and adaptation, compounding the system's fragility. For instance, as documented here and in evaluations employing participatory network approaches, essential communication and resource flows often move in parallel silos, distanced from the ground realities and needs uniquely understood by frontline staff [12]. Without formalised channels to convey practical feedback on product use and household realities upward, frontline actors remain unable to influence significant aspects of program design, such as recipe alterations or packaging changes, irrespective of their acute awareness of what works locally [13].

Similarly, while multi-tiered administrative supply chains are intended to reinforce quality and accountability, they often introduce cumulative logistical delays, poorly managed inventories, and stifled responsiveness at the local level. Evidence from comparable nutritional interventions in Bangladesh, for instance, shows that buffer stocks, decentralised supply chain management, and prompt procurement approvals directly improve system resilience and availability [14]. The large-scale payment delays reported by Indian manufacturers, as echoed in public sector procurement research, significantly dampen suppliers' flexibility, forcing them either to curtail production or to rely on reserves, both of which ultimately risk stockouts and program interruptions [15]. This financial-logistical misalignment created inefficiencies that cascaded through the production system, preventing consistent ration availability at distribution endpoints.

Quality assurance, critical for both safety and community trust, continues to be undermined by field-level challenges [16]. Although production facilities maintain robust nutritional and laboratory verification, the realities of rural transport and insufficient local infrastructure expose products to unacceptable risks of moisture, temperature swings, and pest contamination. Anganwadi workers reports of contamination, such as finding sand in rations, highlight the practical disconnect between formal quality protocols and everyday delivery. Studies from large-scale food fortification programs confirm that robust transport, infrastructure, and storage solutions are as important as technical quality control [17].

Another pivotal finding is the persistent disconnect between formulating optimally fortified products and achieving community acceptance. While the inclusion of soya protein and other nutrients is mandated for child health, these same ingredients frequently undermine product palatability, ultimately reducing uptake. Controlled trials and systematic reviews acknowledge that nutrient-fortified products do not always outperform unfortified options in acceptability, particularly when distributed under non-ideal field conditions that accentuate flavour and texture degradation over time. The underlying lesson is clear without engaging households in iterative product development and prioritising sensory considerations alongside nutritional value, intended health impacts may never be realised [18,19].

Intra-household ration sharing, a recurring observation in food-insecure settings, further complicates targeting. Culled from both this and broader studies, household rations intended for children or mothers are commonly shared among all family members, particularly when household food insecurity is high and social norms discourage selective allocation [20]. It raises sensible questions about the appropriateness of recipient-focused targeting models and supports consideration of more flexible, household-oriented approaches that align with local values while maximising nutritional benefits.

Preparation guidance also warrants rethinking. Uniform, generic instructions provided on packages are often disconnected from regional food cultures and household practices, missing clear opportunities to integrate frontline expertise and community foodways into program delivery. Research in both Indian and African contexts underscores that nutritional impact depends as much on cultural adaptability and practical utilisation as on the nutrient profile itself. In this study, Anganwadi workers routinely modified recipes to local tastes, but these innovations remain undocumented, limiting their broader adoption and missing a chance to standardise best practice [21].

Governance constraints, fragmented cross-sector coordination, sporadic supervision, and challenges with digital monitoring affect every level of service delivery. While the expansion of digital platforms has improved coverage in well-connected areas, both this analysis and global digital health reviews demonstrate that these gains are undermined by unreliable internet, inadequate training, and gaps in infrastructure, particularly in remote regions where oversight is needed most [22]. These same regions are often the least supervised due to logistical barriers, exacerbating inequities in program reach.

Workforce capacity stands as another crucial lever for impact. Anganwadi workers, often with primary education and little ongoing training, are asked to deliver increasingly technical interventions without the support or upskilling necessary for high-quality service provision. Global and Indian evidence strongly support investments in practical training, competency-based recruitment, and workforce expansion not only to improve service delivery but to foster motivation, social recognition, and retention [23].

Stakeholder-sourced recommendations, ranging from advance payments to manufacturers and improved packaging to diversified rations and merit-based worker recruitment, are both practical and grounded in lived experience. Critically, evidence from this study suggests that the identified barriers are structurally interdependent, and that isolated, single-component interventions may have limited impact without coordinated systems-level reform. Experience from participatory action research and health system strengthening globally affirms that, without meaningful routes for distributed knowledge to inform adaptive design, implementation will always lag behind aspiration [24].

Despite limitations of geographic and sample breadth and the inherent biases that come from close programmatic involvement, these findings offer a compelling, actionable set of priorities. Stabilising procurement by combining advance payments with robust payment systems, strengthening logistics and secondary packaging, investing in recurrent workforce training, supporting product diversification, and enhancing community engagement through locally adaptive communication and ingredient packaging all have immediate feasibility and apparent impact [25,26]. However, only coordinated pursuit of these reforms, rather than piecemeal or siloed efforts, will harness potential synergies and address the system’s interconnected weaknesses [27].

Future research should prioritise real-world, longitudinal assessments of reform impact; the integration of coverage and process data using mixed-methods; geographic expansion to illuminate universal versus context-specific challenges; participatory co-design approaches to fuel local innovation; and economic modelling to generate compelling investment cases. Ultimately, the preponderance of evidence, locally and globally, converges on a central lesson [28]: technical solutions are vital, but only transformative when they are unlocked by responsive governance, empowered frontlines, and a design ethos that truly values lived expertise as much as formal protocols.

Strengths and limitations

This qualitative study employs rigorous mixed-methods, combining network mapping and in-depth interviews across seven organisational tiers, capturing systemic interdependencies among supply, governance, and community engagement that are rarely documented in THR evaluations. Maximum variation purposive sampling of 15 stakeholders and independent dual coding enhance analytical credibility. The novel application of Net-Map methodology within the THR context represents a specific methodological contribution of this study: by visually and participatorily revealing structural vulnerabilities and non-substitutable backbone actors, Net-Map surfaced systemic interdependencies that conventional interview-only designs may not readily expose.

However, geographic restriction to the Hoshangabad district limits generalizability across Indian states with differing administrative structures. It is acknowledged that Hoshangabad district's administrative and infrastructural context may differ from states with substantially higher or lower NFHS-5 nutritional burden indicators, and findings should be interpreted within this geographic boundary. The cross-sectional design cannot capture seasonal variations in availability and storage degradation, while potential social desirability bias may influence government and manufacturer responses, given the research team's prior program involvement. The inability to directly observe implementation processes, rather than relying solely on stakeholder accounts, restricts triangulation with actual supply chain delays and quality failures.

The over-representation of Anganwadi Workers (n=8, 53%) means findings may disproportionately reflect frontline service delivery perspectives, with higher-level governance and fiscal viewpoints captured through only four administrative and development partner participants, who are less comprehensively represented. Future studies should deliberately oversample higher-level stakeholders to achieve a more balanced distribution. The study relied entirely on stakeholder self-reports and did not incorporate direct observational methods, document review (e.g., program monitoring reports, government payment records), or beneficiary-level validation. The absence of these triangulation sources means reported barriers could not be independently verified against programmatic records or beneficiary experiences. Member checking was not formally conducted; however, the research team's prolonged engagement with the study setting and the corroboration of findings across multiple stakeholder groups served as a partial compensatory mechanism for establishing confirmability. No quantitative frequency analysis of themes was undertaken, consistent with Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis framework; however, this means formal comparisons of theme prominence cannot be made. Nutritional outcomes were not directly measured within the design of this sub-study; however, these parameters are being assessed as part of the Nutritional Status Enhancement of Children through Behaviour Change Communication and Take-Home Ration (NECCTAR) trial, and associations between the identified barriers and child nutritional status are interpreted in that broader study context.

Conclusions

This multi-stakeholder analysis demonstrates that the Take-Home Ration program's limited effectiveness arises from structural fragilities embedded within its supply chain and governance architecture. Excessive centralisation, fiscal bottlenecks, fragmented inter-departmental coordination, and chronic workforce deficits collectively erode accountability and operational reliability. Key nodes, the State Nutrition Committee, manufacturing units, and Anganwadi Centres, function as non-redundant actors whose interdependencies generate systemic vulnerability. Evidence from this study suggests that the identified barriers are structurally interdependent, and that isolated, single-component interventions may have limited impact without coordinated systems-level reform. Misalignment between product fortification standards and household sensory preferences further diminishes utilisation, while frontline innovations such as localised recipes, adaptive community engagement, and contextualised product modifications remain excluded from formal policy learning cycles. Advancing THR effectiveness requires a paradigmatic shift from hierarchical compliance to adaptive governance that institutionalises frontline knowledge. Priorities include fiscal predictability, decentralised decision-making, cross-sectoral coordination, and culturally responsive behaviour change communication. Embedding these reforms can strengthen supply chain resilience, enhance program acceptability, and accelerate sustainable reductions in childhood undernutrition across resource-constrained settings.
