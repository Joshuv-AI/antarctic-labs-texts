A genome-wide analysis of ascorbate oxidase gene family and hormonal activity in mango exposed to enhanced UV-B radiation
Authors: Hassam Tahir, Yijia Gao, Muhammad Sajjad, Minjie Qian, Ling Wei, Muhammad Zeeshan Ul Haq, Ashar Tahir, Muhammad Aamir Farooq, Kaibing Zhou, Feng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAscorbate oxidase (AO), an apoplastic enzyme from the multi-copper oxidase family, is vital in maintaining redox homeostasis, particularly under environmental stress conditions like enhanced UV-B radiation. Our research group has already reported that AO activity was inhibited under enhanced UV-B radiation stress, which may enhance the activity of ascorbate levels in mango fruit. Although the AO family has been studied in other crops, its role in Mangifera indica remains unexplored.ResultsThis study conducted a comprehensive genome-wide analysis of the AO gene family in mango, which includes the identification and functional characterization of the AO family in the mango genome, including its structural features, regulatory elements, and expression patterns under enhanced UV-B radiation. A total of 19 MiAO gene family members were identified, each consisting of two to five exons, and they were distributed across 10 different chromosomes. Phylogenetic analysis showed a closed relationship of AO genes between mango and tomato, suggesting shared ancestry. Promoter analysis identified 21 cis-regulatory elements involved in several biological processes, including signalling, growth, phytohormone, development, and stress responses. Furthermore, hormonal analysis under UV-B indicated a significant reduction in MeJA, SA, and GA3 levels. However, the expression analysis demonstrated that MiAO5, MiAO6, MiAO7, MiAO8, and MiAO9 were significantly downregulated under enhanced UV-B radiation stress. These results indicated that hormone level reduction may positively regulate the expression of AO family member’s gene expression, which may increase the accumulation of ascorbic acid, resulting in ROS scavenging.ConclusionThese results shed light on MiAO’s role in mango under UV-B’s, offering a foundation for breeding UV-B-tolerant mangoes and refining cultivation techniques. Subsequent research will expand on this to pinpoint the specific contributions of individual MiAO genes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07452-3.

Background

Mango (Mangifera indica L.), belonging to the Anacardiaceae family, is a major tropical fruit crop grown in more than 110 countries [1]. It is one of the five most essential fruit species globally, producing 40 million metric tons and a key tropical cash crop [2, 3]. Major mango-producing countries include India, China, Thailand, Indonesia, Mexico, Philippines, Pakistan, Nigeria, Guinea, and Brazil [4]. India is the largest producer, with approximately 18 million tons, followed by China [1]. China ranks as the world’s second-largest mango producer, with approximately 349,000 hectares dedicated to cultivation and an annual output of 3.3 million tons valued at USD 2.87 billion [5, 6]. Major mango-growing regions in China include Hainan, Guangxi, Yunnan, and Sichuan [7]. With its fertile soil and tropical climate, Hainan Island provides ideal conditions for cultivating various tropical crops, including mangoes. Mango production accounts for 34.66% of all fruit cultivation in Hainan Province, which is the leading mango-producing area in China [8]. Despite its economic importance, mango production faces several environmental challenges affecting yield [3]. These challenges include drought, salinity, extreme temperatures, high light intensity, and UV radiation that significantly impact plant survival and productivity [9]. UV-B radiation 280–315 nm, primarily from ozone depletion, has become a considerable plant abiotic stressor. These radiations can negatively affect plant growth and development, posing challenges to their overall health and productivity [10]. Prolonged UV-B exposure reduces fruit quality and yield due to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage, which affects enzyme activity, pigment levels, and photosynthetic tissues [7]. UV-B exposure induces physiological, biochemical, and molecular alterations, increasing oxidative stress, which hinders development, diminishes biomass, and modifies blooming patterns [11, 12].

An enzyme ascorbate oxidase (AO), found in the apoplast, facilitates the oxidation of ascorbic acid (AsA) to monodehydroascorbate (MDHA), which is then reduced to dehydroascorbate (DHA). The DHA is conveyed to the cytoplasm, where dehydroascorbate reductase (DHAR) converts it back into AsA, preserving redox equilibrium [13]. AO, a glycosylated blue multicopper oxidase, is a member of the copper oxidase family and is prevalent in higher plants [14, 15]. Its concentration is elevated in rapidly proliferating tissues and immature fruits [16, 17], and it is essential for cellular proliferation, cell wall metabolism, and the plant’s ability to respond to stress [18, 19]. The expression of the AO gene is stimulated by light and suppressed in darkness, with this daily regulatory pattern being independent of circadian rhythm in Nicotiana tabacum [20]. The expression of AO is elevated in roots and immature fruits [21, 22]. In A. thaliana, AO genes regulate iron transport, whereas in N. tabacum, AO expression is highest during daylight and in juvenile tissues [23]. The overexpression of AO genes facilitates cellular elongation [24]. In transgenic tomatoes, reduced AO expression elevates AsA levels in fruits and enhances yield [25, 26]. The activity of AO modulates cell division and the cell cycle by reducing DHA synthesis, affecting plant development and stress responses [27, 28], whereas AO genes control ROS homeostasis in plants. Consequently, they may directly or indirectly link to plants’ growth and developmental processes and react to diverse environmental challenges [29]. However, our previous study demonstrated that exposure to enhanced UV-B stress resulted in the downregulation of AO genes, which consequently elevated the level of ascorbate in fruit, thereby strengthening their ability to mitigate ROS [7].

Plant hormones, including abscisic acid (ABA), auxin, cytokinins (CK), jasmonic acid (JA), ethylene, and brassinosteroids (BR), are critical regulators of plant growth, development, and stress responses [30]. The UV-B radiation interacts with these hormonal pathways, affecting physiological and molecular processes essential for adaptation to UV-B stress. Stress-responsive hormones like salicylic acid (SA), ABA, JA, and ethylene enhance plant resilience, while growth-promoting hormones like CKs and gibberellins (GA) support the development [30, 31]. The functions of these hormones vary based on tissue type, environmental conditions, and developmental stage [32]. For instance, UV-B induces morphological changes resembling those caused by disruptions in auxin metabolism and transport [33], as oxidative damage from UV-B exposure has been shown to disrupt auxin homeostasis [34]. The ABA enhances UV-B tolerance by promoting biosynthesis, as evidenced by the sensitivity of ABA-deficient mutants [31], further highlighting its importance in stress adaptation. Ethylene negatively impacts UV-B protection by reducing flavonoid accumulation, as ethylene-deficient mutants exhibit enhanced UV-B tolerance [35, 36], suggesting that ethylene negatively influences UV-B stress tolerance by modulating flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. The SA and JA regulate UV-B stress responses [37]. Meanwhile, JA and SA modulate UV-B responses, and higher JA levels are correlated with increased resistance. In contrast, elevated SA levels are linked to greater susceptibility, likely due to SA’s inhibitory effects on enzymes like ascorbate peroxidase and catalase [38, 39]. The regulation of AO expression exemplifies phytohormone-mediated control. Growth suppressors, such as SA, inhibit its expression [20, 40]. This regulation occurs through complex transcriptional and translational mechanisms [21, 41]. Overall, plant hormones exhibit intricate interactions in growth regulation and stress reactions. Understanding their interplay under UV-B stress conditions is vital for strategies to develop plant resilience in changing environmental surroundings.

Genome-wide identification of AO gene families in diverse species, including A. thaliana, Oryza sativa, Sorghum bicolor, Zea mays, and Glycine max, has elucidated their evolutionary distribution and functional roles [20, 28], yet remains constrained in mango. This study aimed to find the dominance and chromosomal distribution of MiAO genes in mango, a vital tropical agricultural crop. Despite the acknowledged role of ascorbate oxidase in growth and stress responses, comprehensive investigations of this gene family in mango are lacking. To uncover their functional diversity, we analyzed the exon-intron structure, gene-regulatory networks, physicochemical parameters of encoded proteins, and evolutionary relationships of MiAO genes. Furthermore, we investigated their involvement in hormonal signaling pathways, including those mediated by IAA, GA3, ABA, SA, meJA, and ethylene, to elucidate their contributions to growth, development, and stress tolerance. Transcriptome data were analyzed to profile gene expression, with qRT-PCR validation confirming specific gene expression patterns. These results pose novel insights into the potential functions of MiAO genes in stress tolerance and hormone-mediated regulation, laying the groundwork for deeper exploration of their significance in mango under biotic and abiotic stressors.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and mango sampling

The experiment was conducted in a 15-year-old Tainong 1 mango orchard in Sanya, Hainan (18°25′ N, 109°46′ E), with an annual rainfall of 1700 mm and an average temperature of 25 °C. Four 40 W UV-B lamps emitting 313 nm radiation at 96 kJ·m−2·d−1 (Beijing Electric Light Source Research Institute, China) were mounted 30 cm above the canopy to simulate a 15% increased UV-B. The lamps followed a sunrise-to-sunset cycle with automatic shutdown during rainfall. Ten trees were divided into treatment and control groups. Five fruits per tree were sampled every 10 days from 30 to 91 days after flowering (d). Samples were processed on-site and stored at − 80 °C.

Identification of the AO gene family in the mango genome

The MiAO (LOC123218301) protein sequence was examined using the BLASTP tool to determine its family associations within the mango reference genome database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/?term=mango). Ultimately, 19 members of the mango AO family were identified. To gain functional insights, the domains of these gene families were analyzed using InterPro for domain annotation. Mango AO protein sequences were further queried against the A. thaliana and tomato genomes using the NCBI database, leading to the identification of 3 Arabidopsis and 16 tomato members. Additionally, the physicochemical parameters of the mango protein were calculated by the ProtParam program (https://web.expasy.org/protparam) [42]. Predicted features included the total amino acid count, relative molecular Weight (Mw), isoelectric point (pI), and grand average of hydropathy (GRAVY), providing detailed information about the protein’s biological attributes.

Conserved motifs, domains, and gene structure analysis of AO gene family

The conserved motifs of selected proteins were examined using the NCBI CDD batch (Conserved Domains Database and Resources). and the MEME online tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme). Additionally, proteins were analyzed using the MEME Suite, revealing 10 common motifs [43]. The conserved domains were assessed using the Batch CD-Search web-based tool to produce associated domain files [44]. The resulting data were visualized using TBtools-II v2.096 [45].

Chromosome distribution, evolutionary analysis (Ka/Ks), and collinearity analysis

The Ka/Ks ratios and tandem duplications for AO family gene were accurately calculated using Mega 11. A Ka/Ks ratio higher than one, equal to one, or less than one indicates positive, neutral, and purifying selection [46]. Additionally, the divergence time for these gene pairs was determined using the formula “T = Ks/2λ × 10−6”, using a λ value of 1.5 × 10−8 for dicots to compute the duplication time in millions of years [47]. The homology analysis of AO genes in Mangifera indica, and A. thaliana was conducted using MCscan X, a plug-in of TBtools-II v2.096, with default settings [48].

Phylogenetic relationship of AO family

Mango evolutionary relationships of gene families with those from Arabidopsis and Tomato were examined through multiple sequence alignment using the MUSCLE algorithm. A phylogenetic tree was then made by the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method in MEGA 11 software [49]. A bootstrap analysis with 1000 replicates was conducted, while default settings were applied for all other parameters. The phylogenetic tree was visualized and annotated to facilitate comparative analysis using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/).

Identification of cis-acting elements in the promoter regions

Selected gene families in the 2000-base pair upstream region were retrieved and exposed to cis-acting element analysis by the PlantCare database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [50]. Additionally, cis-acting elements were formulated using the TBtools-II software v2.096.

Determination of hormonal content

Mango abscisic acid (ABA), ethylene, Methyl jasmonate (MeJA), Gibberellic acid (GA), Salicylic acid (SA), and Indole-3-Acetic Acid (IAA) concentrations in fruit pulp were determined using plant ELISA kits from Shanghai Yuanju Biotechnology Center, Shanghai, China. A 50 mg pulp sample was centrifuged for 15 min at 4000 rpm after homogenization (1.0 g in 9 mL PBS). Micro ELISA Stripplates were supplemented with standard solutions and supernatants. Standard wells included 50 µL of standard, while sample wells included 10 µL of sample + 40 µL of diluent. The plates were incubated for 60 min at 37 °C after 100 µL of HRP-conjugate reagent was inserted and cleaned. The 50 µL of Chromogen Solutions A and B were added, keeping the mixture out of the light. After 15 min, adding 50 µL of Stop Solution caused the color to change from blue to yellow. To check the hormone concentrations, the absorbance was calculated at 450 nm.

The qRT-PCR analysis

Ten DEGs from the AO gene family were selected for qRT-PCR analysis to validate the RNA-seq results. Primers were designed using Primer Premier 6 and synthesized by Shanghai Bioengineering Co., Ltd. (China) (Table S1). Total RNA was extracted and transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript II Kit. qRT-PCR was conducted on a qTOWER3 system with SYBR Master Mix using actin as the reference gene. To ensure consistency with RNA-seq data, gene expression levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method [51].

Statistical analysis

ANOVA (One-way) was conducted to evaluate the statistical differences among the experimental groups, allowing for the identification of significant variations. GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 was used for data visualization to generate precise graphical representations of the results. In addition, a heatmap illustrating the expression profiles of DEGs was created using TBtools-II (v2.096) [45].

Results

Identification and characterization of AO gene family members in Mango

A total of 19 AO family members were identified in the Mangifera indica genome and designated as MiAO1 to MiAO19. The physicochemical parameters of the corresponding MiAO proteins, including molecular weight and isoelectric point (pI), were analyzed (Table 1). The results showed that the MiAO proteins ranged in length from 538 to 590 amino acids. Among them, MiAO1, MiAO6, and MiAO10 were the shortest, each comprising 538 amino acids, while MiAO19 was the longest, with 590 amino acids. The molecular weight of MiAO proteins varied from approximately 59.92 to 66.74 kDa. Based on their pI values, eight proteins were classified as acidic (pI ≤ 7), while eleven were basic (pI ≥ 7). Stability analysis revealed that 16 MiAO proteins had an instability index below 40, indicating they are stable, whereas three exhibited an instability index above 40, classifying them as unstable. Additionally, the negative GRAVY values for all MiAO proteins suggest that they are predominantly hydrophilic.

Biological features of the AO gene family

Conserved motifs, domains, and gene structure analysis

The MEME online analysis tool detected 10 motifs within the 19 AO protein sequences. In this context, a motif refers to a short, conserved amino acid sequence pattern that often represents functionally or structurally important regions of a protein. Statistical analysis indicated the presence of 10 conserved motifs (motifs 1–10) among the 19 MiAO protein family members (Fig. 1a). These conserved motifs exhibited noticeable variation across different gene clusters. For example, MiAO14 to MiAO19 included 7 conserved motifs, whereas the remaining genes displayed between 8 and 10 motifs. Motifs 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, and 8 were found in all MiAO protein examined, suggest a high level of conservation for specific motifs, which may be crucial for vital biological functions. Integrating the phylogenetic tree with motif analysis revealed distinct patterns of motif distribution across various branches of the MiAO family protein, highlighting their evolutionary divergence and functional specialization. Figure 1b presents the analysis results of the 19 identified MiAOs protein domains. All proteins included all three superfamilies, including the Cupredoxin superfamily, Cu-oxidase_2 superfamily, and Cu-oxidase_3 superfamily. Analyzing the gene structure is essential for investigating the structural diversity of duplicated genes. The organization of introns and exons offers important perspectives on the diversity and similarities among AO protein family members across various classes in mango. Using the mango genome, we analyzed the structural composition of 19 AO proteins to determine the positions of exons and introns. The organization of exons and introns and their respective counts are essential evolutionary indicators for gene families. A protein and exon-intron structure map of the MiAO family was constructed to further evaluate structural characteristics. Figure 1c illustrates that MiAO16, MiAO11, MiAO12, and MiAO13 have one intron, whereas the other 15 feature two introns. Furthermore, five MiAO members possess five exons, while MiAO11 includes only two exons, and the remaining members display between six and eight exons.

Chromosome distribution, evolutionary analysis (Ka/Ks), and collinearity of 19 MiAOs genes

The chromosomal locations of the MiAO gene family members were examined within the mango genome, and gene duplication events were also examined. The research found that the 19 MiAO genes were irregularly distributed over 10 of the 20 chromosomes (Fig. 2). Chromosome 16 had the highest density, with 6 genes, followed by Chromosome 1, which included 3 genes. Furthermore, chromosomes 2 and 11 included 2 genes, whereas chromosomes 4, 6, 9, 10, 15, and 19 each had 1 gene.

The Ka/Ks ratio is a critical metric for understanding the evolutionary dynamics of coding sequences, offering insights into the type of selection pressure acting on genes after duplication. To elucidate the selection patterns of the MiAO genes, we analyzed the Ka/Ks ratios for all homologous gene pairs (Table 2). Our analysis identified 106 pairs of homologous genes, all exhibiting a Ka/Ks ratio of less than 1. This indicates that these genes have been subjected to negative (purifying) selection, which preserves their functional integrity by removing deleterious mutations. The estimated divergence times for these gene pairs spanned a wide range, from 0.98 to 107.18 million years ago (MYA), reflecting the complex evolutionary history of the MiAO gene family. These results suggest that the duplication events contributing to the expansion of this gene family occurred across distinct evolutionary periods. However, Gene duplication is a fundamental mechanism driving the diversification of plant genomes. It occurs through various processes, including segmental and tandem duplication, and plays a vital role in the evolution of gene families.

To explore the duplication patterns of the MiAO genes in the mango genome, we have identified 15 gene pairs that were classified as tandem repeats, indicating that segmental duplication has been the predominant mechanism shaping the evolution of this gene family (Fig. 3a). The collinearity analysis of the genomes of Mangifera indica, and A. thaliana revealed 25 syntenic relationships between Mangifera indica and A. thaliana (Fig. 3b). Notably, several AO genes specifically, MiAO1, MiAO4, MiAO5, MiAO6, MiAO7, MiAO8, MiAO9, MiAO10, MiAO11, MiAO12, MiAO14, and MiAO19 demonstrated close relationships with homologous genes in A. thaliana, indicating their potential significance in the evolution of the AO gene family. The comparative analysis of AO genes between mango and A. thaliana is crucial for elucidating interspecific genetic relationships and predicting gene functions. This understanding enhances insights into genetic diversity and paves the way for potential applications in plant biology and improvement programs.

Phylogenetic study of AO family members

A comprehensive phylogenetic analysis of 38 AO protein sequences derived from mango, tomato, and A.thaliana revealed their classification into three distinct groups (Fig. 4). Group 1 comprised 19 genes, including 9 from mango and 10 from tomato, representing the largest and most conserved group, which suggests strong evolutionary conservation and potentially similar functional roles in these species. Group 2 contained 13 genes, with contributions from all three species (6 from mango, 4 from tomato, and 3 from A. thaliana), indicating a moderate level of divergence and possible species-specific adaptations. Group 3 consisted of 6 genes, including 4 from mango and 2 from tomato, suggesting a more specialized subgroup. Notably, certain MiAO displayed close evolutionary relationships with tomato, for example, MiAO5 shares a close evolutionary relationship with SlAO3, and MiAO9 clusters closely with SlAO2 within Group 1. These findings imply that certain AO protein in mango and tomato may have originated from a common ancestor and could perform similar physiological roles, particularly in stress response and redox regulation, reflecting evolutionary conservation in the AO protein family.

Cis-acting component analysis of the AO family promoter

Cis-acting elements are a distinct class of DNA sequences located in the promoter region of genes, playing a crucial role in the regulation of transcription. These elements modulate gene expression by interacting with transcription factors, either promoting or suppressing transcriptional activity [52, 53]. In this study, the promoter sequences of 2000 bp upstream from 19 MiAO genes were analyzed to identify regulatory elements. After filtering out uncharacterized and low-confidence elements, three primary categories of cis-acting elements were identified: abiotic stress response, plant hormone response, and growth and development response elements (Fig. 5). Within the abiotic stress response category, four types of regulatory elements were identified, associated with light, anaerobic conditions, drought, and low temperatures. Notably, light-responsive motifs were the most abundant, including GT1, I-box, chs-CMA1a, GA, GATA, G-box, MRE, and AE-box, accounting for 79.7% of all light-responsive elements. Additionally, ATCT and Box 4 motifs constituted 80.8% of the light-responsive elements, while ARE (13.2%), MBS (2.5%), and LTR (3.4%) were identified as anaerobic, drought, and low-temperature response elements, respectively. The prevalence of light-responsive elements suggests a potential role of the MiAO gene family in plant photomorphogenesis. Six types of hormone-related regulatory elements were identified, corresponding to gibberellin, methyl jasmonate, auxin, abscisic acid, and ethylene signaling pathways. The most abundant motifs included ABRE (15.3%), SARE (8.9%), AuxRR-core, TGA-box (7.0%), ERE (35.2%), GARE (3.8%), and TGACG (29.5%). Among these, ERE and TGACG were the most prevalent, accounting for 35.2% and 29.5% of all hormone-related elements, respectively. Additionally, six growth- and development-related cis-acting elements were identified and associated with circadian regulation, endosperm expression, meristem expression, MYBHv1 binding sites, seed specificity, and zein metabolism. These elements included circadian (12.5%), GCN4 (25%), CAT-box (21.8%), CCAAT-box (6.2%), RY-element (9.3%), and O2-site (25%). The MiAOs gene members are thought to play an important role in many developmental processes, hormone control, and stress response mechanisms, indicating their potential significance in these biological activities.

Quantitative changes in hormones

The hormone content analysis in mango pulp revealed significant differences between the enhanced UV-B treatment and the control (CK) at various days after flowering(d) (Fig. 6). ABA levels were significantly lower at 40 d of Enhanced UV-B exposure compared to CK. Ethylene level showed non-significant difference than that of CK. Similarly, GA levels were significantly reduced under Enhanced UV-B treatment at both 40 d and 91d. In contrast, IAA levels were significantly higher in the Enhanced UV-B treatment at 40 d but were not as significant as CK at 91d. SA levels were significantly lower in the treatment group at 91d. However, at 40 d, the levels of MeJA were significantly lower under Enhanced UV-B treatment compared to CK.

Expression analysis of MiAO genes under enhanced UV-B stress

To study the effects of Enhanced UV-B treatment on mango pulp, 19 MiAOs genes associated with the AsA metabolism pathway were analyzed using transcriptome data as shown in the heatmap (Fig. 7). Results revealed that 10 MiAO genes exhibited significant expression changes when compared to the CK group. By the 40th day of enhanced UV-B treatment, seven genes were upregulated. Meanwhile, the expression of three genes was downregulated. However, by the 91 st day, the expression of nine MiAO genes was downregulated.

Validation by qRT-PCR

To investigate the role of MiAO genes within the AO gene family in mango fruit, we screened 10 genes (MiAO1, MiAO5, MiAO6, MiAO7, MiAO8, MiAO9, MiAO10, MiAO14, MiAO15 and MiAO19), through transcriptome analysis at different developmental stages to understand better physiological function that the AO gene family play important role in mango fruit pulp under Enhanced UV-B stress (Fig. 8). Expression levels were assessed using qRT-PCR at 30, 40, and 91d. Distinct activation and inhibition patterns were observed at different developmental stages. The expression of MiAO1, MiAO5, MiAO6, MiAO8, MiAO9, MiAO10, MiAO14, and MiAO15 showed significant upregulation in the enhanced UV-B stress group at 40d. While MiAO10, MiAO14, and MiAO15 showed downregulation expression at 40d. At 91 d, the expression pattern of MiAO5, MiAO6, MiAO7, MiAO8, and MiAO9 showed significant downregulation under enhanced UV-B treatment as compared to the CK group. These findings highlight the differential expression patterns of MiAO genes in response to enhanced UV-B stress, suggesting their potential roles in regulating fruit development and stress responses during mango maturation.

Discussion

Bioinformatic analysis of ascorbate oxidase gene family

Ascorbate oxidases (AOs) are apoplastic enzymes that maintain redox homeostasis by catalyzing the oxidation of AsA into dehydroascorbic acid (DHA) [54]. These enzymes are integral to plant responses against abiotic and biotic stresses and are also involved in cell wall metabolism and cell expansion. In the apoplast, AOs are crucial for ROS-mediated innate immunity through modulation of the redox state [21]. Due to their wide-ranging functions, AOs are considered key contributors to plant physiological processes and defense mechanisms. Studies across various plant species have revealed diverse numbers of AO genes, reflecting their functional significance. For instance, 8 AO genes were identified in Gossypium hirsutum [28], 9 in Citrus sinensis [55], 5 in rice, 3 in A. thaliana, 7 in Glycine max, 6 in Sorghum bicolor [20], 14 in wheat [56] and 12 in Beta vulgaris [57]. The study of conserved motifs and domains is crucial as they reveal structural conservation and determine protein function [58]. In this study, ten conserved motifs (1–10) were identified among 19 MiAO gene family members, with variations observed in a cluster-specific manner (Fig. 1a). The distinct distribution patterns of these motifs indicate possible evolutionary divergence and functional specialization within the MiAO gene family (Fig. 1b). The predicted functional domains of MiAOs, Cu_Oxidase1, Cu_Oxidase2, and Cu_Oxidase3, align with prior studies [20, 28, 57] and are part of cupredoxin domains, consistent with findings in A. thaliana, O. sativa, Z. mays, and S. bicolor [20].

The structural diversity observed within the MiAO gene family suggests an overall conserved exon–intron organization, which may reflect functional divergence or species-specific evolutionary events (Fig. 1c). The presence of both single- and dual-intron structures, along with variable exon counts, is consistent with patterns reported in AO genes in cotton species and Beta vulgaris exhibit similar exon and intron organization patterns, as previously documented by Pan et al. [28] and Skorupa et al. [57]. The observed variations in exon number are likely the result of evolutionary exon insertion or deletion events, as proposed by Xu et al. [59]. Additionally, intron phases, which denote the positions of introns relative to codon boundaries, further underscore the conserved nature of eukaryotic gene structures [60].

The number of gene family members in various species is influenced by multiple factors, including natural selection, genome duplication, and tandem duplication events [61]. Previous studies have demonstrated that tandem repeat events during evolution often contribute to the expansion of gene families, increasing the number of gene members and enhancing genetic diversity [61, 62]. Such gene duplications can result in functional differentiation, leading to the emergence of novel functions and adaptations. The evolutionary analysis of the MiAO gene family in mango suggests that these genes have undergone strong purifying selection, which preserves essential biological roles (Table S2). Similar findings have been reported in AO gene families of other plant species, indicating that selective pressure has maintained the structural and functional integrity of these genes throughout evolution [63]. Collinearity analysis revealed 25 syntenic relationships between Mangifera indica (Fig. 3b). Several MiAO genes (MiAO1, MiAO4–MiAO12, MiAO14, and MiAO19) showed strong associations with homologs in these genomes, indicating their evolutionary significance. The observed collinearity between M.indica and A.thaliana reinforces the notion of conserved genomic regions, suggesting that core members of the AO gene family may share a common ancestral origin [64]. Moreover, the phylogenetic analysis and presence of divergent members in the AO family suggest possible functional differences, even though the genes are largely conserved (Fig. 4). This pattern is similar to other species, where gene duplication and changes in function have helped them adapt over time [65]. Collectively, these findings highlight the evolutionary significance of AO genes in mango and suggest their potential utility in stress tolerance and metabolic regulation. The clustering of homologous MiAO proteins is attributed to their high sequence homology, indicating their conserved nature throughout evolution. AO proteins form distinct sub-clusters within both clusters, likely due to their independent evolutionary trajectories. Notably, AO proteins in dicots appear to share a common ancestral lineage.

Cis-acting elements are crucial for regulating biological processes, including hormonal responses, abiotic stress adaptation, and developmental pathways [66]. Auxin-induced promoters with AuxRE and DR5 motifs regulate hormone responses [67], while light-responsive promoters often contain G-box, AT-rich sequences, GT1-motif, and I-box elements [68]. CATGTG and CACG motifs are linked to drought stress responses [69]. Plant hormones such as IAA, GA, SA, ABA, and MeJA play vital roles in growth, development, and stress adaptation [70–72]. In this study, we extracted 2000 bp promoter sequences for 19 MiAO coding sequences and identified cis-acting elements, focusing on stress and hormonal response elements (Fig. 5).

Hormonal regulation and relative gene expression of MiAO genes

Plant hormones are central to the entire plant life cycle, playing synergistic or antagonistic roles under abiotic stress. Enhanced UV-B interferes with hormonal metabolism and transport [34, 73]. Overall, IAA and GA promote plant growth among hormones, whereas JA, ABA, and SA inhibit it (Fig. 6) [73, 74]. In our study, we identified cis-acting elements associated with ABA, GA3, IAA, MeJA, and SA in the promoters of MiAOs genes. Both ABA and SA induced differential expression, highlighting their role in hormonal regulation. Additionally, ABA and MeJA were found to play a key role in regulating MiAO expression, further emphasizing their importance in the plant’s hormonal network [75]. UV-B exposure resulted in decreased levels of ABA and GA3 in mango pulp, aligning with findings from studies on Rhododendron chrysanthemum, which also showed suppressed hormonal activity under UV-B stress [76]. This reduction can hinder normal physiological processes such as fruit ripening and growth. The ratio between ABA and GA3 is crucial for regulating growth and development [77]. Typically, ABA acts as a growth inhibitor, while GA promotes growth [78]. Under UV-B stress, the increased ABA/GA3 ratio enhanced stress tolerance but led to smaller fruit due to the reduced effect of GA3 on growth [79]. IAA levels also declined at 40 days, likely due to UV-B-induced inhibition of auxin biosynthesis, as reported in previous studies [80]. Ethylene activity remained non-significant, suggesting a diminished role in fruit ripening. However, the ethylene/IAA ratio was lower at 40 days, likely preventing fruit shedding, while a higher ratio at 91 days promoted fruit maturation. Contrary to the typical increase in MeJA and SA levels under UV-B stress [34, 81, 82], our study found a decrease in both hormones, possibly contributing to ROS accumulation. Similar MeJA and SA level reductions were observed in UV-B-treated carrots [83], suggesting a weakened defense response and increased oxidative damage. Our findings reveal the complex hormonal changes triggered by UV-B stress, impacting growth, fruit development, and stress adaptation. These insights could help in improving stress resilience in fruit crops.

Biotic and abiotic stresses significantly impact agriculture by impairing plant growth and reducing productivity. Both stress types trigger ROS accumulation in the cytoplasm and apoplast, leading to cellular damage and physiological [56, 84–86]. Ascorbate (AsA) is a vital antioxidant that scavenges excess ROS, but AO, an apoplastic enzyme, counteracts this defense by oxidizing AsA into DHA, negatively affecting stress responses [28]. To understand the role of MiAO genes in stress responses, we analyzed their expression under UV-B treatment. Our results showed significant downregulation of MiAO5, MiAO6, MiAO7, MiAO8, and MiAO9 at 91 days under UV-B stress compared to the control (Fig. 8). This suggests suppressing AO expression enhances AsA accumulation, increasing ROS scavenging capacity. Consistent with our findings, previous studies also reported reduced AO gene expression under UV-B stress, supporting the hypothesis that AO downregulation facilitates higher AsA levels for oxidative damage mitigation [7, 15]. Similar trends were observed in tobacco, where transgenic plants expressing AO exhibited higher photosynthetic rates under high light irradiance [87]. In tomatoes and lettuce, suppression of AO led to increased AsA levels and improved stress tolerance under UV-B stress [25, 88]. The ascorbate-glutathione cycle plays a crucial role in ROS detoxification, with AsA and glutathione maintaining redox homeostasis [21, 89]. However, AO activity disrupts this balance by oxidizing AsA, exacerbating ROS accumulation [20]. Studies in Arabidopsis thaliana and tobacco showed that AO suppression improved stress tolerance, with AO mutants exhibiting higher AsA/DHA ratios under salinity stress [90]. Conversely, AO overexpression reduced ROS scavenging, highlighting its detrimental effects under stress conditions [91]. AO expression is also regulated by growth suppressors like SA, which inhibit its expression [20, 21, 41]. Under UV-B stress, AO gene downregulation increases AsA levels, creating a more reduced apoplastic environment. As oxidative damage increased in the later stages of fruit growth and development under enhanced UV-B radiation, the contents of MeJA, SA and GA3 decreased significantly. qRT-PCR results showed that genes such as MiAO5, MiAO6, MiAO7, MiAO8 and MiAO9 were significantly downregulated under enhanced UV-B radiation stress, which was consistent with the increased accumulation of ascorbic acid. This indicates that MeJA, SA and GA3 positively regulate the expression of genes such as MiAO5, MiAO6, MiAO7, MiAO8 and MiAO9. These findings suggest that the MiAO gene family is key in coordinating hormonal and stress responses, enhancing plant adaptation to abiotic and biotic stresses. This study provides insight into the complex regulatory mechanisms underlying plant stress responses and highlights the significance of cis-acting elements in enhancing stress resilience.

Conclusions

MiAO gene family investigation in mango highlights their critical roles in stress tolerance and plant developmental processes. We examined significant variability in the physical properties, conserved motifs, domains, structure, and evolutionary relationships of these genes, with other species. The identification of diverse cis-acting elements linked to development and hormone signaling further indicates a complex regulatory network. The presence of conserved Cu_oxidase domains and motifs underscores the evolutionary conservation of the MiAO gene family. Notably, the downregulation of MiAO genes during fruit development under UV-B stress was associated with a decrease in AO activity. This reduction in AO activity likely limits the oxidation of AsA to DHA, resulting in elevated levels of AsA. Increased AsA accumulation enhances the plant’s capacity for ROS scavenging, thereby mitigating oxidative damage. However, Fluctuations in key hormones, including ABA, ethylene, MeJA, GA3, and SA, under UV-B stress further suggest a regulatory link between MiAO gene expression, hormone signaling, and metabolic processes. These findings indicate that reduced AO expression supports the maintenance of redox homeostasis by bolstering antioxidant defenses during stress. We found the pivotal role of the MiAO gene family in improving mango fruit quality and enhancing stress resilience, particularly under UV-B conditions. Functional characterization of these genes, including overexpression studies, will provide deeper insights into their roles and potential applications for enhancing crop tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses, ultimately contributing to higher yields and improved stress resilience.
