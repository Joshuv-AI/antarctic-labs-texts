Timing and Reasons for Supportive Care Referral in Head and Neck Cancer: A Prospective Study From India
Authors: S. Gayatri, Anuja Damani, Aswathi Praveen, Krithika Rao, Arun Ghoshal, Shreya Nair, Shirley Lewis, Umesh Velu, Naveen Salins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatients with head and neck cancer (HNC) receiving curative‐intent radiotherapy (RT) or concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) experience high symptom burden and acute treatment‐related toxicities. Supportive Care (SC) can mitigate these effects; however, the timing and documented triggers for referral are insufficiently characterized in routine practice.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, observational longitudinal cohort study (April 2023–March 2024) at a tertiary cancer center in India. Consecutive eligible adults with HNC receiving curative‐intent RT/CCRT were enrolled (n = 96). Referral timing (days from RT initiation) and documented reasons were extracted from the electronic medical record (EMR). Symptom burden (ESAS‐r [Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale‐Revised]), quality of life (FACT‐HN), and toxicities (NCI CTCAE v5 [National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events Version 5]) were assessed at the first SC visit and at five follow‐up visits over 8 weeks. Missing follow‐up outcomes were imputed using the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method. Changes across visits were assessed using the Friedman test, with Kendall's W reported as an effect size.ResultsThe mean age was 56.35 years (SD 12.83); 70 (72.9%) were men. Half of the cohort lacked a documented referral reason in the EMR. Referrals occurred predominantly during the second and third treatment weeks (mean 19.2 days from RT initiation; SD 12.66; range 0–71 days). The timing of referral correlated strongly with the RT fractions received (Spearman's ρ = 0.942, p < 0.001). Pain improved from a median of 5 to 2 (χ2 = 141.68, p < 0.001; W = 0.30). Anxiety improved from a median of 3 to 2 (χ2 = 54.51, p < 0.001; W = 0.11). Total FACT‐HN improved from a median of 83.7 to 96.7 (χ2 = 79.88, p < 0.001; W = 0.17).ConclusionsSC referrals for HNC patients are commonly reactive during peak acute toxicity periods rather than proactive. Structured referral triggers and improved EMR documentation may enhance timely symptom control and quality of life during curative treatment.

Introduction

Head and Neck Cancers (HNC) include malignant conditions arising from the squamous lining of the head and neck, including the oral cavity, lips, pharynx, nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, and parotid glands. According to the GLOBOCAN 2020 estimate, HNC is the seventh most common cancer. There are nearly nine million new cases per year, which accounts for around 4.5% of all newly reported cancers worldwide [1]. In addition to this, HNCs account for approximately four million deaths annually, representing around 4.6% of global cancer fatalities. In HNC, the treatment plan, including surgery, radiation targets, dosing, and systemic therapy indications, is determined by the disease's specific location, stage, and pathological findings [2] For patients with early‐stage HNC, either surgery or RT alone is typically recommended; however, for regionally or locally advanced diseases, a combined approach using multiple treatment modalities is usually advised [2]. Radiotherapy (RT) and chemo/immunotherapy for HNC often cause adverse effects that impact quality of life [3].

Issues faced by HNC patients are multifaceted, such as pain, fatigue, dry mouth, sleep disturbances, anxiety, financial difficulties, depression, weakness, and numbness [4]. Despite the advances in RT, there is a significant impact on QoL [3], the severe symptoms experienced by HNC patients, such as mucositis, pain, and xerostomia, can lead to social isolation, emotional distress, and a decline in quality of life [5, 6] Consequently, addressing these challenges is critical in the holistic Supportive Care (SC) of individuals with HNCs. While existing research offers valuable insights into the SC needs and the positive effects of SC interventions on their well‐being [7, 8], there remains a notable gap in understanding the specific reasons and optimal timing for SC referrals during RT or concurrent chemoradiotherapy. A retrospective review in 2016 explored patterns of referral of HNC patients to outpatient Palliative Care (PC) services, identifying pain and symptom management as the most common reason for referral, and found that addressing the symptom burden led to more effective Advanced care planning [9] In the Indian setting, a phase III randomized controlled trial involving 212 participants demonstrated the benefits of a multidisciplinary approach that included palliative care, a psycho‐oncologist, a social worker, a dietitian, a physiotherapist, and an oral hygienist [6]. Similarly, a non‐randomized time‐series study involving 134 HNC patients found that interventions such as exercise therapy, educational sessions, and information booklets reduced symptom burden [10]. A scoping review of 97 studies highlighted the positive impact of various interventions—pharmacological, physical, psychosocial, and rehabilitative—on patients' QoL [7], and that the provision of these services begins at diagnosis, continues through the duration of cancer treatment, and adapts as needed into survivorship or end‐of‐life care.

In our hospital, these SC services for oncology patients are provided by a specialized Palliative Medicine and SC department comprising physicians, nurses, and other multidisciplinary professionals. Need‐based referrals to the department of Palliative Medicine and SC are initiated by the treating oncologists. Despite evidence supporting early SC, there is limited data from India on when and why referrals occur during curative treatment for HNC. This study addresses this gap. This study aims to identify the timing and key reasons for referrals to Early Palliative and SC for patients receiving CTRT or RT with curative intent, providing critical insights for developing a robust referral protocol.

Methods

Study Design and Setting—This hospital‐based, prospective, longitudinal cohort study was conducted in the outpatient and inpatient settings of the Department of Palliative Medicine and SC at a tertiary hospital over 12 months, from April 1, 2023, to March 30, 2024. The study focused on SC services, including symptom assessment and management, treatment for radiation‐related side effects, identification of care needs for patients and caregivers, and coordination of referrals to the appropriate multidisciplinary team.

Participants and Sampling—A purposive sampling technique was used to select eligible patients, all of whom provided informed consent before enrolment. This method was chosen to ensure that patients receiving curative‐intent treatment were included in the study. The inclusion criteria consisted of both outpatients and inpatients over the age of 18 who had histopathological evidence of head and neck cancers (HNCs) and were undergoing definitive intent RT or concurrent chemoradiotherapy. Patients were excluded if they were scheduled for palliative intent RT, had been referred before the initiation of treatment or the study period, or had undergone surgery or other systemic therapies.

Sample Size

As this was a prospective observational study with a descriptive primary objective, the sample size was based on precision for estimating proportions. Assuming a conservative referral proportion of 50%, a 95% confidence level, and 10% absolute precision, the required sample size was 96 patients. This approach is consistent with observational head and neck cancer SC studies [10].

Outcome Measures—The primary outcomes were to identify documented reasons for SC referrals and to record the timing of these referrals by calculating the days from the initiation of RT to the referral. Symptoms were assessed using the healthcare professional‐assisted Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale‐Revised (ESAS‐r) (Appendix 1) [11] and the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy‐Head & Neck (FACT‐HN) questionnaire (Appendix 2) in Kannada during initial consultations and follow‐up visits. Mucosal and skin toxicity due to radiation treatment were recorded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI CTCAE Version 5) (Appendix 3) at multiple follow‐up intervals [12].

Data Sources and Measurements—Demographic data and patient history were obtained from routine patient assessment charts. Skin and mucosal toxicities were graded using the NCI CTCAE Version 5. The ESAS‐r questionnaire evaluated the severity of nine common cancer‐related symptoms, including pain, fatigue, and anxiety, rated on a scale from 0 (no symptom) to 10 (worst severity). The ESAS tool has demonstrated validity and reliability in previous studies, making it suitable for routine symptom assessment. The FACT‐HN questionnaire, a self‐administered tool, assesses the quality of life (QoL) in HNC patients, covering physical, social, emotional, and functional well‐being. Each item is rated on a scale of 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating better QoL. The Kannada version was developed and is available with permission.

Bias Mitigation—To minimize potential reporting bias from self‐reported questionnaires, participants were allowed sufficient time (at least 1 h) to complete them, with the primary investigator available to provide clarifications as needed. Additionally, selection bias was reduced by enrolling consecutive eligible patients throughout the study period.

Statistical Analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using Jamovi (version 2.5.6). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages; continuous variables were summarized using mean (SD) or median (IQR), as appropriate [13]. Changes across repeated follow‐ups were analyzed using the Friedman test; Kendall's W was reported as an effect size. Missing follow‐up outcomes were addressed using LOCF imputation [14]. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 135 patients screened, 96 were recruited, with 86 completing all six follow‐up visits. All 96 participants were included in the analysis (Figure 1).

CONSORT flow diagram.

The age of participants ranged from 18 to 90 years, with an average age of 56.48 years, and a standard deviation of 12.83. Among the recruited participants, (72) 72.9% were men and 27.1% were women. Charlson's comorbidity score was used to record the premorbid status of the patients undergoing treatment, with an average score of 3.80, a standard deviation of 1.48, and a range of 2–9.

The most frequent sites of cancer among patients referred were the oral cavity, pharynx (including the nasopharynx, oropharynx, and hypopharynx), and larynx. The distribution of malignancies across these sites was as follows: 64.6% oral cavity, 14.6% oropharynx, 11.5% larynx, and the least common being 2.1% salivary glands.

The predominant histopathology was squamous cell carcinoma, with 50% moderately differentiated, 37.5% well differentiated, and 12.5% poorly differentiated or other histologic types, such as salivary gland adenocarcinoma. Patients presented with Stage 2 and 3a malignancies. Among them, 67% had undergone prior surgery, followed by planned RT and chemotherapy. Specifically, 56.3% of participants received adjuvant RT alone, while the remainder 43.8% were undergoing definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy.

Among the ninety‐six participants in the study, 50% lacked a documented reason for referral to SC in their EMR. For those with recorded reasons n = 48, the most prevalent was symptom management, followed by SC for acute radiation‐induced toxicities and their associated symptoms. Notably, there was only a single referral for conflict resolution and shared decision‐making.

The timing of referral to SC is defined as the duration from the start of RT to the initial SC referral. The average time was 18 days, with a standard deviation of 12.40 days (0–71 days). Further, analysis of the frequency distribution of fractions of RT received at the time of referral indicated that most referrals occurred during the second week of treatment (Table 1).

Using Karl Pearson's correlation coefficient, a remarkably high positive correlation was observed between the Fractions of RT received and the timing of referral (r = + 0.943: p < 0.001: Statistically very highly significant correlation observed) (Figure 2).

Karl Pearson's correlation between fractions of radiotherapy received and timing of referral to Supportive Care.

The average pain score on a 0 to 10 scale upon referral was 4.92 (standard deviation (SD) = 1.73), while tiredness scored 2.21 (SD = 2.18), nausea scored 1.27 (SD = 1.82), lack of appetite scored 1.35 (SD = 1.73), depression scored 2.32 (SD = 2.25), and anxiety scored 3.02 (SD = 1.95). Drowsiness and shortness of breath were reported with the lowest scores. In physical well‐being, respondents had an average score of 18.49 (SD = 4.55), with a range of 5–26. Social and familial aspects of well‐being were reflected in an average score of 18.42 (SD = 4.35), ranging from 5.80 to 28.00. Emotional well‐being, on average, yielded a score of 17.46 (SD = 3.12), with a range of 6 to 23. Functional well‐being, encompassing daily activities and tasks, was measured at a mean score of 9.88 (SD = 4.78), with a range of 3 to 28. Additional concerns were apparent, with an average score of 18.81 (SD = 4.18), ranging from 7 to 28. Lastly, the composite measure of Total FACT‐HN yielded an average score of 83.09 (SD = 14.09), ranging from 47.30 to 108.80, indicating overall well‐being across multiple dimensions.

Across follow‐up visits, symptom burden decreased significantly. Pain improved from a median of 5 at baseline to 2 by Week 8 (Friedman's χ 2 = 141.68, p < 0.001; Kendall's W = 0.30). Anxiety improved from a median of 3–2 (χ 2 = 54.51, p < 0.001; W = 0.11). No significant changes were observed in drowsiness (χ 2 = 2.70, df = 5, p = 0.747) or shortness of breath (χ 2 = 3.16, df = 5, p = 0.675) across the follow‐up visits. These findings collectively suggest dynamic shifts in symptomatology throughout follow‐up, underscoring the importance of ongoing monitoring and management of symptom burden and acute treatment‐related toxicity (Figure 3a). Total FACT‐HN improved from a median of 83.7–96.7 (χ 2 = 79.88, p < 0.001; W = 0.17). A statistically significant difference (p < 0.001) in components of the FACT‐HN tool was noted (Figure 3b).

Twenty‐eight individuals (29.2%) experienced no mucositis. Twenty‐nine individuals (30.2%) reported Grade 1 mucositis, while 31 (32.3%) noted Grade 2 mucositis. A smaller subset of participants (8.3%, 8 individuals) experienced Grade 3 mucositis. Forty‐five participants (46.9% of the population) exhibited Grade 1 radiation dermatitis. Conversely, the remainder of the population either presented with no dermatitis (45.8%) or Grade 2 dermatitis (7.3%).

The maximum grade of mucositis was observed during the third week of follow‐up assessment, followed by substantial improvement in subsequent evaluations (χ 2 = 54.2, df = 5, p < 0.001). Conversely, concerning radiation‐induced dermatitis, the highest score was recorded during the fourth weekly visit. However, a subsequent improvement in the grade of dermatitis was noted throughout follow‐up assessments. (χ 2 = 64.7, df = 5, p < 0.001) (Figure 3c).

Discussion

In this study, we found that half of the participants referred to the Department of Palliative Medicine and SC lacked clear documentation of the referral reason, highlighting systemic gaps rather than isolated documentation lapses. The gap in referral to PC has been researched before in the literature; one such study is a qualitative systematic review and meta‐aggregation that has explored the lack of mutual communication as a potential disabler for PC/SC referral [15, 16]. Studies have investigated referral patterns among these patients; however, they acknowledge the need for further research to understand the contextual factors underlying the lack of referral to PC/SC.

Incomplete documentation may hinder care coordination and continuity, underscoring the importance of standardized referral processes and clearer communication between services. Contributing factors may include limitations in the Electronic Medical Record (EMR), high clinical workload, and fragmented referral pathways. Interventions such as structured referral templates, mandatory minimum data fields, and EMR‐based prompts could enhance documentation quality, clarify clinical intent, and promote earlier, goal‐concordant integration of SC [17, 18, 19]. Previous studies have reported that patients are often unaware of the reason for referral to PC services, underscoring the importance of clear communication; although this was not directly assessed in our study, it remains an important consideration for future research [15].

Approximately half of the referrals were made early during treatment, based on high symptom burden and distress scores, due to acute radiation‐induced toxicities [20, 21]. This timing is consistent with the 2024 ASCO guideline update, which emphasizes the importance of incorporating PC services early in treatment [22] and of providing needs‐based referrals to PC services early in the disease trajectory. Recent prospective data from Southeast Asian cohorts confirm a high symptom burden and quality‐of‐life impact among patients treated with RT or chemoradiotherapy for head and neck cancer, with oropharyngeal, neurosensory, psychological, and gastrointestinal symptom clusters significantly affecting quality of life throughout treatment [23, 24]. These data, in the context of LMIC challenges such as limited RT access and high treatment‐related toxicity, emphasize the value of proactive, needs‐based referral triggers embedded early in multidisciplinary workflows. Compared with existing ESMO [25] and NCCN frameworks [2, 26] that advocate structured, trajectory‐driven referral, the predominantly reactive referral pattern observed in this study highlights an implementation gap, reinforcing the argument for protocolized early PC referral pathways tailored to high‐symptom, high‐toxicity treatment phases.

The predominantly reactive referral pattern highlights an opportunity to embed proactive PC/SC triggers into routine Head and Neck Cancer Radiotherapy and Concurrent Chemoradiotherapy workflows. A statistically significant improvement in overall symptom burden and in components of the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy‐ Head & Neck (FACT‐HN) was observed during curative treatment. Similar findings were observed in a 2023 single‐center, non‐RCT time‐series study by S. G. Nayak et al. on the effectiveness of the Comprehensive Intervention Program [7]. This study revealed a statistically significant improvement in QoL, fatigue, and psychosocial distress in the intervention arm. These results align with our study, which demonstrated significant improvement in symptom burden and FACT tool components across six follow‐ups in SC. However, such improvements in an observational setting cannot be attributed exclusively to SC interventions, as symptom trajectories during curative treatment are also influenced by natural recovery from acute toxicities and disease response to ongoing oncologic therapy. While structured SC is recognized to enhance symptom monitoring, timely intervention, and patient‐reported outcomes, the absence of a comparator group limits the ability to isolate its independent effect from background recovery and treatment‐related improvement.

Consistent with our findings that most referrals were initiated 2–4 weeks after the initiation of radiation treatment, two other studies reported the highest grade of mucositis during the third week of treatment, followed by significant improvement in subsequent evaluations [20, 33]. Regarding radiation‐induced dermatitis, an initial exacerbation was noted, with peak severity occurring during the fourth weekly visit, in line with previous studies [34].

Strengths‐ This study addressed both symptoms and treatment‐related toxicities. The study's findings can help identify critical time points when SC is most needed for proactive symptom management interventions. A key strength of the study is that nearly 90% of patients were consistently followed for 8 weeks, providing robust, comprehensive data on their progress and outcomes.

Limitations—Documentation of the referral reason was completed for only half of the patients. SC interventions were primarily focused on symptom control and the management of treatment‐related adverse effects.

The study's observational design, without a control group, makes it difficult to determine causation and draw definitive conclusions about the relationship between the variables. The observed changes in symptoms and quality of life might be influenced by factors beyond the SC intervention, such as natural disease progression or concurrent treatments. Also, we have limited data on the outcomes of patients receiving routine care outside the study, and they may have suffered from a lack of referral.

While our study did not specifically analyze symptom burden with the site of malignancy or radiation field, we acknowledge that these factors may significantly influence SC needs. Future research focusing on site‐specific symptom profiles and dosimetry variables could offer more nuanced insights into optimizing PC integration. Although this study did not evaluate specific SC interventions, we recognize the clinical value of identifying which patients benefit most from targeted symptom management strategies (e.g., mouth rinses, analgesics, hydration).

This study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other oncology settings with different referral pathways or PC integration models. Although the study prospectively captured referral timing and determinants, it did not include patient‐reported experience measures, such as satisfaction with care or understanding of the referral purpose. Future multicentre studies incorporating patient‐centered outcomes would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of SC integration in head and neck oncology.

Conclusion

The primary reasons for SC referrals were focused on managing symptoms and addressing acute radiation‐induced toxicities. These referrals were most frequently made during the second and third week of treatment, aligning with the peak in symptom burden and the onset of acute radiation‐induced toxicities. As treatment progressed and SC involvement increased, there was a noticeable improvement in symptoms, particularly toward the completion of treatment, accompanied by a reduction in the severity of mucosal and skin‐related toxicities. More efforts are needed to improve documentation and to incorporate a multidisciplinary, patient‐driven, holistic approach to treatment, including physiotherapy, occupational therapy, a rehabilitation team, and psychosocial oncology for patients, and to consider the inclusion of caregiver support within the current healthcare framework.
