Characteristics of paraspinal muscle in asymptomatic population with pelvic anteversion: correlation between bony alignment and paraspinal muscle
Authors: Yi Zhao, Siyu Zhou, Yudong Zhao, Zhuoran Sun, Weishi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnteverted pelvis (AP) subgroup is unusual subtype in asymptomatic population characterized by low pelvic incidence (PI), high lumbar lordosis (LL), and a sloped sacrum. However, the contribution of paraspinal muscles (PSM) to this specific alignment has not been clarified. The purpose of this study was to determine if the AP subgroup was associated with different characteristics in the cross-sectional area (CSA) and fat infiltration (FI) of PSM.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 168 asymptomatic volunteers. MRI was used to assess the CSA and FI of the multifidus (MF), erector spinae (ES), and psoas (PS), and the ES and MF were added up as lumbar extensor muscles (LEM). Sagittal parameters were measured via whole-spine radiographs, and were compared in AP and non-AP subgroup. Correlation analysis was applied between sagittal parameters and PSM. Further, an age, sex and PI matched control group of AP group was built to compare the PSM. Linear regression analysis was applied to explore the potential influence factor of PSM.ResultsIn AP subgroup, larger LL, sacral slope (SS) and smaller PI were discovered(P < 0.001). In the general cohort, PI, SS and LL were found to be positively related to the CSA and fCSA of LEM (P < 0.05). Comparing to control group, AP individuals showed significantly larger CSA and fCSA in MF (CSA 0.82 ± 0.17vs 0.7 ± 0.14, P < 0.001; fCSA 0.57 ± 0.15vs 0.47 ± 0.12, P < 0.001) and LEM (CSA 1.57 ± 0.45vs 1.36 ± 0.35, P = 0.016; fCSA 1.07 ± 0.38vs 0.89 ± 0.31, P = 0.017) at L5-S1, while no statistical significance was found in PS. Regression analysis indicated that age(β=-0.184, P = 0.014), pelvic anteversion(β = 0.293, P = 0.008), and PI(β = 0.399, P = 0.004) were independent predictive factors of LEM CSA. For LEM fCSA, pelvic anteversion (β = 0.272, P = 0.013) and PI (β = 0.356, P = 0.011) were also independent predictive factors.ConclusionsAsymptomatic individuals with AP possess significantly stronger LEM at the lumbosacral junction. The findings suggest that LEM could play a critical role in maintaining lumbar curvature and pelvic rotation in AP subgroup. This study highlights the importance of considering the individualized bone-muscle match when analyzing sagittal balance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06345-3.Keywords: Spinopelvic alignment, Paraspinal muscle, Pelvic anteversion, Sagittal balance, Asymptomatic population

Introduction

In recent decades, the strong correlation between the restoration of sagittal balance and clinical outcomes of lumbar spine surgery has been widely reported [1–5]. In surgical planning, the anatomical parameter, pelvic incidence (PI), was considered an essential reference for its stability in adulthood and close correlation to other sagittal parameters [6, 7]. In the mainstream opinion, a larger PI indicates larger ideal lumbar lordosis (LL) with relatively sloped sacrum and vice versa [2, 4]. However, in recent years, an unusual anteverted pelvis (AP) subtype in asymptomatic population was noticed, which links the large LL and sloped sacrum with relatively small PI [8, 9]. The existence of AP subgroup revealed that different patterns of PI-LL match could exist in asymptomatic population. Further exploring the mechanism of pelvic anteversion could be necessary for deeper understanding of sagittal balance.

As far as we know, the concept of pelvic anteversion in asymptomatic population is initially proposed by Laouissat et al. [9] as a modification of the original Roussouly type 3 [10], which is defined as sacral slope (SS) >35° with low-grade PI and low or negative pelvic tilt (PT). In their study, 16% of western population was divided into the AP subgroup, while higher proportion (26.2%-30.3%) was found in Asian cohort [8, 10, 11]. Further, Zhou et al. [11] demonstrated that there is larger capacity of pelvic retroversion in AP subgroup from standing to sitting, indicating the different characteristics of spinopelvic motion between the groups. Previous results revealed the morphological and functional characteristics of AP subgroup, while the mechanism of pelvic anteversion remains unclear. To improve our knowledge of pelvic anteversion, considering the potential role of bony, soft tissue or neuromuscular factors is necessary [11, 12].

In recent years, the role of paraspinal muscle (PSM) in maintaining sagittal balance has been widely concerned both in patients with lumbar diseases and asymptomatic cohort [13–16]. In patients with degenerative spinal kyphosis, Xia et al. [13] reported that cross-sectional area (CSA) of lumbar extensor muscles (LEM) was positively related to PI, LL and SS, indicating the potential influence of LEM on maintaining lumbar curvature and pelvic position. Moreover, in asymptomatic population, Meakin et al. [14] showed that larger volume of LEM is associated with a larger lordosis in L4-S1. Those results revealed that the PSM could participate in the adjustment of lumbar curvature and pelvic rotation, while if such effect plays a role in the formation of pelvic anteversion remains unclear.

The present study was performed to explore the role of PSM morphology in AP subgroup in asymptomatic population. Specifically, the Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) was used to evaluate the CSA and fat infiltration (FI) of PSM. This study would firstly characterize the relation between sagittal parameters and PSM, and further clarify the role of these muscle characteristics in the context of pelvic anteversion. It was hypothesized that the unique spinopelvic alignment of the AP subgroup would be associated with distinct paraspinal muscle features.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a single-center cross-sectional study. All participants provided their written informed consent. The study was applied under the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of the conducting institution (IRB00006761-M2023727).

Participants

Volunteers were recruited from a single medical center and the local community. Before participating in this study, all subjects would undergo a history taking and physical examination. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age ≥ 18 years. (2) scoring < 10 on the Oswestry Disability Index [17]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) radiological disorders indicating spinal scoliosis, vertebral fracture, spondylolisthesis, sagittal imbalance. (2) history of chronic neck or low back pain (3) history of prior spinal or lower extremity diseases (4) history of radicular symptoms (5) history of neuromuscular disorders (6) Pregnancy or other contraindications of radiological examination.

Radiographic evaluation of spinopelvic alignment

All the subjects underwent the standardized anteroposterior and lateral whole-spine radiography in standing posture. Participants were instructed to assume an upright posture, flexing their shoulders and elbows to place their fingertips on the ipsilateral clavicle, and they were also directed to look straight ahead to keep the head in a neutral position. The distance between the participant and the tube was 180 cm. Then the following sagittal parameters were measured in the PACS system (Picture Archiving and Communication System, GE Healthcare, USA). The parameters included as follows: (1) spinal parameters: LL, thoracic kyphosis (TK), L4-S1 lordosis, lumbar tilt (L1T), lumbar apex; (2) pelvic parameters: PI, PT and SS; (3) global parameters: sagittal vertical axis (SVA), T1 pelvic angle (TPA). The protocols of measuring the sagittal parameters were shown in Fig. 1. Pelvic anteversion was defined as PT < 9° and SS/PI >0.80 [8, 11].

Protocols of measuring sagittal parameters

Paraspinal muscle evaluation on MRI

All enrolled subjects had undergone MRI of lumbar spine. MRIs were obtained with Signa HDxt 3.0T (General Electric Company, Boston, MA, USA). The slice thickness was 3 mm with a 3-mm gap between each slice and the axial MRI was parallel to the inferior endplate of the vertebral body. The multifidus (MF) and erector spinae (ES) were measured bilaterally and separately from T2- weighted images at the intervertebral disc of L3-L4, L4-L5 and L5-S1 level [18–20]. The Psoas major (PS) was measured in the same way at the intervertebral disc of L3-L4 and L4-L5 (Fig. 2). The LEM was defined as the sum of MF and ES in this study.

The measurement of muscle parameters. The FI was segmented by threshold method. The area with number 1, 2, 3 indicate MF, ES and PS respectively

The Image J software (version 1.51, National institute of Health, USA) was used to analyze the muscle parameters. The CSA of MF, ES and PS were measured on each level to evaluate the muscle size. To adjust for variations in body size among participants, the CSA of each muscle was normalized by dividing the absolute muscle area by the CSA of the corresponding intervertebral disc. A thresholding technique based on differences in the pixel signal intensities of muscle and fat was used to separate the tissues [21]. The FI of MF, ES and PS measured by the thresholding technique, which is defined as the percentage of fat area in the total muscle area, and the functional cross-sectional area (fCSA) was calculated as CSA×(100%-FI). Further, considering the physiological asymmetry of PSM (detailed in the Supplementary Materials), the mean values of the left and right sides were calculated for each muscle as follows: (1) mean CSA (mCSA): the average value of CSA on both sides; (2) mean fCSA (mfCSA): the average value of fCSA on both sides; (3) mean FI (mFI): (1-mfCSA/mCSA) ×100%. The total CSA and total fCSA were defined as the sum of mCSA and mfCSA of all segments.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was applied using SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). All statistics were in form of mean ± standard deviation. Descriptive analysis was performed to evaluate the demographic data. T-test and chi-square test were used to compare the demographic data, radiological parameters and muscle parameters in different groups. Pearson’s correlation analysis was applied to analyze the correlation between sagittal parameters and muscle parameters. Propensity Score Matching was used to build a 1:1 age, sex, PI-matched control group of AP group. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed on the entire cohort (N = 168) to explore the influence factors of LEM. Statistical significance was set as P < 0.05.

Characteristics of paraspinal muscles in AP and control group

For further analysis, an age, sex, and PI-matched control group was established. The age, PI and sex were 46.5 ± 12.4 years, 44.2 ± 7.8°, and men/women 18/27 in control group, which showed no statistical difference comparing to AP group.

The comparison of muscle parameters between the matched groups revealed significant differences primarily at the L5-S1 level (Table 4). Compared to the control group, the AP group exhibited a significantly larger mCSA (1.57 ± 0.45 vs. 1.36 ± 0.35, P = 0.016) and mfCSA (1.07 ± 0.38 vs. 0.89 ± 0.31, P = 0.017) of LEM at L5-S1 level. This difference in LEM was mainly attributed to the MF, which also presented significantly larger mCSA (AP vs. control group 0.82 ± 0.17 vs. 0.70 ± 0.14, P < 0.001) and mfCSA (AP vs. control group 0.57 ± 0.15 vs. 0.47 ± 0.12, P = 0.001) at L5-S1 level. In contrast, no significant inter-group differences were observed in the ES or PS.

Discussion

This study aimed to clarify the role of PSM morphology in the unique spinopelvic alignment of the asymptomatic AP subgroup. The principal finding of the study was that compared to the age, sex, and PI-matched control group, asymptomatic AP individuals presented significantly larger and more robust LEM, which was mainly driven by the MF at the L5-S1 level. Furthermore, the multivariable regression analysis further substantiated this relationship, identifying both pelvic anteversion and PI as independent predictors of LEM CSA or fCSA. These findings collectively suggest the potential correlation between PSM morphology and different patterns of spinopelvic alignment, highlighting the potential role of PSM characteristics in the maintenance of sagittal alignment.

Previous studies have reported a unique AP subgroup in asymptomatic population, characterized by large LL and highly sloped sacrum with relatively small PI [8, 9]. With the unusual spinopelvic alignment, the AP subgroup further presented special patterns in spinopelvic motion, as larger LL decrease and pelvic retroversion were found in AP subgroup from standing to sitting [11]. In the present study, 45 of 168 (26.8%) of the participants were recognized with pelvic anteversion, which presented a similar prevalence and sagittal balance pattern to previous studies [8, 9, 11]. In this study, the relationships between PSM and spinopelvic alignment were carefully evaluated, and the role of PSM in pelvic anteversion was further analyzed.

Our analysis of baseline PSM characteristics revealed significant sex-based differences in muscle morphology, which is consistent with the existing literature [22–25]. In our cohort, males generally exhibited larger fCSA and lower FI than females. While this finding aligns with previous studies, it is notable that the magnitude of this sex-based variance in our cohort was less pronounced than has been previously reported [22–25]. Such discrepancy was attributed to our methodology of normalizing muscle CSA by the intervertebral disc area. This normalization likely decreased the effects of overall body size, which differs between sexes, thereby providing a more direct comparison of intrinsic muscle quality.

As an important functional complex, the relationship between muscle and bony alignment has been widely investigated. In patient cohorts, a close correlation between PSM degeneration and the development of sagittal malalignment has been established [13, 15, 16, 18, 19, 26–28]. While these studies highlight the role of muscles in pathology, few studies focused on asymptomatic population. Meakin et al. [14] have demonstrated the positive correlation between L4-S1 lordosis and volume of LEM, while their study focused primarily on local spinal curvature. The present study expands on these findings by incorporating a comprehensive evaluation of both global and regional sagittal parameters.

The analysis in this study revealed weak to moderate correlations between the size of LEM and key spinopelvic parameters, including PI, SS, and LL. Collectively, these correlations suggest an interplay between the size of the LEM and sagittal parameters. Building upon previous studies in patient populations [13, 15, 16, 18, 19, 26–28], our findings further demonstrate that LEM characteristics are also closely associated with pelvic rotation and lumbar curvature in asymptomatic individuals. In contrast, for PS, poor correlation to sagittal parameters was found, indicating the different roles between LEM and PS. Such results were consistent with the previous studies focusing on PSM function, as LEM including MF and ES was partially attached to the posterior of lumbar vertebra and sacrum, which contributes to the stability of lumbar curvature and pelvis rotation in standing [29, 30].

To further show the role of PSM in pelvic anteversion, an age, sex and PI-matched control group was built. The trend of larger mCSA was found in LEM in AP group, and statistical difference was found at L5-S1 segment. This finding was further supported by multiple linear models, which identified age, pelvic anteversion and PI as independent influence factors of LEM size. Those results suggested a close relation between pelvic anteversion and LEM, while the causal factor is unclear. On the one hand, stronger LEM could take part in the formation of pelvic anteversion, which is associated with larger LL with anteverted pelvis. On the other hand, stronger LEM in AP group could be the result of the unusual bony alignment. As the AP subgroup presented highly sloped sacrum, a higher shear stress along the intervertebral space could be generated, and stronger LEM was matched to provide sufficient resistance force preventing spondylolisthesis, especially at L5-S1 level [29, 30]. With the possible hypotheses, one important concept is that bony alignment and LEM affect each other and together participate in the maintenance of sagittal balance. Therefore, when judging sagittal balance, it is necessary to consider bone-muscle match comprehensively instead of respective measurement, as a suitable LEM for non-AP group may not be strong enough for AP subgroup. Such variations in bone-muscle match patterns could further lead to the prevalence and development of lumbar generative diseases, which need to be further examined in future studies.

Several limitations in this study should be noticed. Firstly, the baseline data suggests potential heterogeneity between our AP and non-AP groups. While our use of PSM and multivariable regression controlled for key confounders, the influence of unidentified confounding variables cannot be entirely ruled out. Future research with larger samples and more comprehensive data is needed to further investigate the characteristics of this unusual AP subgroup. Secondly, the present study was performed in single ethnic, which could be with potential bias in other ethnics, which requires future verification. Thirdly, only MRI-based muscle evaluation was applied, which could be single-dimensional. Future studies should explore other muscle evaluation parameters, including three-dimensional muscle volume analysis, the semi-quantitative Goutallier classification for fat infiltration [31], and further functional assessments of muscle strength and endurance. A multi-faceted approach like this would contribute to a more in-depth understanding of the interaction between musculature and bony alignment.

Conclusion

This study explored the relation between PSM and the spinopelvic alignment in asymptomatic population. In this cohort, a positive correlation between LEM and PI was discovered, and stronger LEM was found in AP group at lower lumbar segment. These results suggest that the differences among various sagittal balance subtypes extend beyond bony alignment, and that characteristic variations in PSM size must also be considered. Therefore, future lumbar surgical planning could potentially benefit from a comprehensive approach that evaluates the match between a patient’s PSM characteristics and their specific sagittal balance pattern, rather than focusing solely on bony alignment.
