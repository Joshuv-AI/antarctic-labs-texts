Gaze dynamics toward familiar and unfamiliar faces in prosopagnosia
Authors: Adi Mizrachi, Oryah Lancry-Dayan, Yoni Pertzov, Galia Avidan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecognizing familiar faces is typically fast and automatic in neurotypical individuals but is markedly impaired in congenital prosopagnosia (CP) – a lifelong face recognition deficit without apparent brain injury. While previous research has focused on deficits in face perception or gaze to facial features in CP, it remains unclear whether individuals with CP can use facial familiarity, either explicitly or implicitly, to guide gaze dynamics in goal-directed tasks. Here, we investigated whether familiarity modulates gaze behavior in individuals with CP using two tasks previously validated in neurotypical populations: a face memorization task and a visual search task, each involving displays with both familiar (famous) and unfamiliar faces. The familiar faces in these tasks were categorized as either explicitly or implicitly visually recognized, based on participants’ ability to identify them by both image and name or only by name (respectively). Although individuals with CP performed worse than controls across both tasks (exhibiting lower accuracy and slower response times) they nonetheless showed preserved guidance of gaze by facial familiarity. When familiarity was explicit, their gaze patterns closely resembled those of neurotypical controls, with attention adjusted according to task demands: spending less time on familiar faces during memorization and more time on them during visual search. Notably, gaze differences also emerged for implicitly familiar faces, which, despite not being consciously recognized by image, elicited distinct viewing patterns compared to unfamiliar faces. These findings suggest that individuals with CP retain residual familiarity representation that can guide gaze even without explicit recognition. Introduction

For most of us, recognizing and identifying faces is a seamless and effortless part of everyday social interactions, often executed even without conscious thought. However, for individuals with congenital prosopagnosia (CP) this seemingly simple task presents a profound challenge. CP, also referred to as developmental prosopagnosia (DP), is a neuropsychological disorder marked by severe impairments in the ability to learn, recognize, and process faces. Despite having normal intelligence, intact low-level visual abilities, and typical exposure to faces, individuals with CP struggle with faces due to a failure in developing the cognitive and neural mechanisms essential for normal face recognition1–6.

To better understand the origins of face recognition deficits in CP, some studies have focused on the early stages of face processing in CP individuals, either by examining general face processing metrices7 or by analyzing their reliance on specific facial features8–10. However, since the core difficulty in CP lies in recognizing familiar faces in everyday life, research that extends beyond general face processing is highly relevant. In particular, comparing how individuals with CP process familiar versus unfamiliar faces may offer deeper insights into the nature of their impairment.

In neurotypical individuals, research has shown that familiar and unfamiliar faces are processed differently11,12. A common way to investigate this distinction is by analyzing gaze behavior, particularly focusing on fixations (the periods when the eyes remain relatively stable). Research has demonstrated that both personally familiar and famous faces typically elicit fewer fixations, fewer returns to previously viewed regions, and a more restricted sampling of facial features compared to unfamiliar faces13. In addition, there is evidence that neurotypicals look more on external (e.g. hair- and jaw-line of the face) versus internal features (e.g. eyes, nose, and mouth) when faces are unfamiliar14.

Similar research in CP individuals is relatively sparse. A single-case study of an individual with CP revealed distinctions between familiar and unfamiliar faces15: the CP participant responded faster to famous faces, made more consecutive fixations on familiar faces, and sampled more regions of unfamiliar faces. Notably, even when the participant failed to explicitly recognize a face, his eye movement patterns resembled those of controls who successfully identified famous faces, showing the pattern of reduced sampling of familiar faces. Although these findings are based on a single case, this latter result supports the notion that CP individuals exhibit some implicit, rudimentary processing of familiar faces. This notion was also suggested by a different study that relied on reaction time and accuracy as dependent measures rather than eye movements16, and is further supported by electroencephalography (EEG) evidence demonstrating covert recognition in CP17.

While these previous findings highlight the impact of familiarity on how both neurotypical individuals and those with CP scan single faces, recent studies in neurotypical populations suggest that familiarity can also guide attention when multiple faces are present. To that end, these studies have shifted from the traditional focus on scanning patterns of individual faces to examining how eye movements vary in more complex visual contexts involving the presentation of multiple faces, some familiar and some not18–22.

One such study employed a memorization task in which participants viewed a display of four faces, followed by a single test face. They were asked to indicate whether the test face had appeared in the preceding display. During the initial presentation of the four faces, participants first directed their gaze toward the familiar face but quickly shifted gaze toward the unfamiliar ones, suggesting that while familiarity captures initial attention, unfamiliar faces are prioritized when memory encoding is required20. A similar study found that familiar objects elicited faster first fixations, whereas unfamiliar objects drew longer and more frequent gaze visits21. This modification of gaze based on familiarity allows observers to optimize the allocation of their limited processing resources. Since participants are tasked to encode the faces, directing more attention to unfamiliar faces and objects, that are harder to encode23, can help them achieve better performance in the task.

This pattern of results, however, is expected to change when observers face a different set of task demands. For example, when searching for someone familiar, we would anticipate preference towards the familiar face rather than unfamiliar ones. Indeed, in a visual search task where participants were asked to locate a familiar face among several unfamiliar ones, they consistently fixated on the familiar face earlier than would be expected by chance. This suggests efficient guidance of gaze towards a familiar face, when it is task relevant19.

These two lines of research demonstrate that face familiarity can both attract and repel gaze, depending on the viewer’s current goals. However, it remains unclear whether – and to what extent – individuals with CP, who are profoundly impaired in recognizing familiar faces, can still leverage familiarity in such contexts. Hence, in the current study, we tested individuals with CP in the memorization and visual search paradigms described above. To further examine the potential role of implicit familiarity processing, famous faces were categorized as either explicitly recognized (i.e., when participants recognized a famous person upon seeing both their image and name) or implicitly recognized (i.e., when participants recognized the person’s name but not their face). We use the term “implicitly recognized” to refer to faces that are implicitly perceived as familiar based on visual processing, despite the absence of explicit, consciously accessible recognition. For simplicity, we use the shorter term “implicitly recognized” throughout the paper.

There are two possible scenarios. One possibility is that without explicit recognition, CPs truly fail to process familiar faces, and their gaze behavior aligns with their self-report, showing no indication of implicit familiarity processing. An alternative possibility is that even when CPs cannot explicitly identify a face, some residual familiarity would still shape their gaze, revealing an implicit process not captured by conscious report. Clarifying which of these scenarios holds is important for understanding whether recognition can guide gaze even without conscious awareness.

Our objectives for the study were twofold. First, we aimed to investigate whether similar to neurotypicals, participants with CP could leverage familiarity to efficiently deploy gaze towards and away from familiar faces19–21. Second, we sought to determine whether implicit familiarity is sufficient to guide gaze of CP individuals. Finding such evidence would provide further support to the notion that individuals with CP exhibit implicit recognition of familiar faces, even if they are unable to explicitly recognize them.

Method

Participants

A sample of thirteen individuals with CP was initially recruited for the study. This number is consistent with a power analysis conducted using G*Power24, with α = 0.05 (one-tailed) and desired power = 0.80. The effect size was based on the smallest significant effect reported in a previous study21 (d = 0.74) employing one of the tasks used here, which indicated a required sample size of N = 13.

Five participants were excluded for the following reasons: one due to eye-tracking calibration issues (average validation error > 1° of visual angle), one for scoring above the cutoff of 29 on the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ), and three for recognizing all faces in the final face questionnaire, which prevented comparison between explicit and implicit conditions. The final sample therefore included 8 participants diagnosed with CP. Given the rarity of CP, this sample size is consistent with previous behavioral studies involving CP7,8,10,25.

Yet, we acknowledge that the sample size may limit the sensitivity of our statistical analyses to detect small effects. To address this limitation and the fact that our key inferential tests employ mixed-design ANOVAs and paired-samples t-tests, we conducted additional sensitivity power analyses (see “Power and sensitivity analyses” below) to estimate the smallest effect size that could be reliably detected with the current sample. This provides important context for interpreting the observed effects and helps determine whether non-significant results are more likely attributable to insufficient power or reflect a genuine absence of an effect. Importantly, all tasks were also piloted with neurotypical participants (7 per task) to confirm replication of the original findings1920,21, and to ensure that the effects could be detected even with a small sample size.

The CP participants were matched with 8 controls of similar age and gender (CP group: 4 males; age range 24–56, M = 35, SD = 13.1; Control group: 4 males; age range 25–58, M = 34.7, SD = 14.4). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision.

We note that CP subject #2 had mild, monocular amblyopia, according to self-report. Yet he showed a clear CP-like impairment profile based on our diagnostic criteria alongside an intact CCMT score, indicating preserved object recognition despite his amblyopia and no obvious general visual perception difficulties. The calibration for this participant was conducted using the eye with normal vision. Additionally, CP subject #3 reported that she was born with a congenital heart defect. We acknowledge these unique characteristics of developmental impairments despite not being directly related to CP. Controls were recruited from the community and were matched to CP based on age, gender and handedness. Handedness was determined by self-report.

All participants were required to sign a consent form prior to joining the study and were rewarded with a payment in exchange for their participation. Ethical approval for all the experiments was provided by the ethical committee of the Psychology Department at Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, Israel. All procedures were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

To accommodate participants’ needs, experiments were conducted by the same experimenter (A.M) in Prof. Galia Avidan’s lab in the Psychology Department at Ben-Gurion University of the Negev or in the lab of a colleague, Prof. Bat-Sheva Hadad, Department of Special Education, Faculty of Education, University of Haifa. The experimental setup and equipment were similar in both testing locations. Five CP subjects were tested in the lab at the University of Haifa, while the remaining CPs and all control subjects were tested in the lab at Ben-Gurion University of the Negev.

Power and sensitivity analyses

We conducted sensitivity analysis24 for our main statistical tests. These analyses estimate, for our actual sample size (8 CP participants and 8 matched controls), α = 0.05 and desired power of 0.80, the minimum effect sizes that our design could reliably detect (see Table S1 in the Supplementary Materials).

The results indicate that the present study is well powered to detect large effects but has limited power to detect small-to-medium effects. Given the limited power of the present study to detect such effects, we interpret these latter findings with caution.

CP diagnosis

Screening of CPs was conducted based on our previous studies using diagnostic tests and a semi-structured interview25,26. The tests include a Famous Identity Test, a Cambridge Face Memory Test27, and a Cambridge Car Memory Test for control nonface stimuli28. We diagnosed participants as having CP if both CFMT and FFMT scores were more than 1.6 standard deviations below the mean of previously reported norms27,28. In the semi-structured interview, we asked participants about past brain injuries and possible developmental impairments to exclude acquired prosopagnosia (AP) as well as about their difficulties with face processing. CCMT served as a control measure to verify that object memory was intact. While an atypical CCMT score would not have excluded a participant from the CP group, a normal CCMT score strengthens the interpretation that the impairment is face-specific rather than reflecting a broader object-recognition difficulty.

Notably, individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) also tend to have difficulties with face processing and show distinctive eye movement patterns toward faces29. Thus, prior to participation, subjects were asked to fill out the adult’s Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ) questionnaire that examines autistic traits30. Individuals who scored above the autistic cutoff31 of 29 were asked to complete the tasks in this study for possible future projects, but their data was not included in this current study. Eye-tracking

Eye movements were recorded using the Eyelink 1000 system (SR Research Ltd, Canada) by tracking pupil and corneal reflection using a CCD video camera that was placed on the desk in front of the participants. We used a chin rest to minimize head movements. All tasks were presented on a 52 × 35 cm LCD screen, positioned at a viewing distance of 65 cm from the participants’ eyes. This setup subtended a visual field of approximately 42.8° horizontally and 30.1° vertically. Prior to the recordings, we performed the standard nine-point calibration and validation process, provided by SR research. The calibration procedure started with the appearance of a single dot in the center of a black computer screen, followed by the appearance of dots in 9 different locations on which participants were requested to fixate. After the 9-points calibration procedure, a validation process was performed in a similar manner. The experiment began when the average validation error across the 9 points was less than 1 degree of visual angle. Otherwise, the calibration process was repeated. Calibration and validation were conducted at the beginning and middle of every task and eccentricity was measured from the center of the face stimulus. Afterward, participants saw a screen with the relevant instructions for each task. Participants were told that they should maintain a high level of attention and concentration while exploring the stimuli as naturally as possible32.

Procedure

Participants completed the two tasks in a fixed order, with the memorization task first (Fig. 1A), followed by the visual search task (Fig. 1B). At the end of the experiment, they filled out questionnaires to assess their familiarity with the celebrities presented in both tasks (Fig. 1C). The same photograph of each celebrity was used across the familiarity questionnaire, the memorization task, and the search task.

(A) The memorization task. Participants were required to memorize a set of four faces and then indicate, via key press, whether a single face had appeared in the previous four-faces display. (B) The search task. Participants were instructed to search for a familiar face without prior knowledge of its specific identity. Each trial began with a central fixation cross, which participants were required to fixate on before starting the task. Once the cross disappeared, they initiated the search. Upon locating the familiar face, participants were instructed to fixate on it and press the space bar. (C) Recognition questionnaires, based on face recognition and name recognition. We defined recognition as explicit familiarity if a person was identified in both questionnaires (i.e., based on a picture and a written name) and implicit familiarity if a person was recognized only in the name-based questionnaire. For privacy reasons, bars are placed over faces in the illustration, these bars were not present in the original study.

The memorization task (Fig. 1A)

The task began with a parallel display of four faces (5000 ms), followed by a blank screen with a central fixation point (3000 ms), a single face display (2000 ms), and another blank fixation interval (5000 ms) to prevent location bias33,34. In the parallel display, each famous face appeared in four trials, once in each location of the display (top right, top left, bottom right, bottom left). The experiment included 64 trials, with eight familiar faces of Israeli celebrities and 56 unfamiliar faces. Participants were instructed to press one of two buttons (Q for “no” and P for “yes”) to indicate whether the face presented in the single display had also appeared in the preceding parallel display. The face that appeared in the single display could be of an Israeli celebrity or an unfamiliar face, who either appeared or did not appear in the parallel display. This design ensured that all faces were fully counterbalanced20,21. In the parallel display, faces subtended approximately 6.7° × 10.0° of the visual field and were displayed at an eccentricity of approximately 12.0° from fixation. In the single display, the face captured approximately 10.5° × 16° of the visual field.

The visual search test (Fig. 1B)

The task began with ten practice trials, where participants were shown five colored circles. Four circles were identical, and one had a different color. Participants were instructed to look at the odd-colored circle and press the space bar. Each display began with a central fixation cross, and participants were told to start searching only once the cross disappeared. After correctly identifying the odd circle in three out of five trials (not necessarily consecutively), participants could proceed to the main task by pressing the space bar.

In the main task, participants were presented with a parallel display of five faces, including one of an Israeli celebrity, arranged evenly around a central fixation cross. After 500 ms, the cross disappeared, and participants were asked to locate the familiar face, look at it and press the space bar to proceed. The familiar face choice was determined based on the face that was being fixated at the time the participant pressed the space bar. Faces in the visual search task subtended approximately 3.1° × 6.4° and were presented at eccentricities ranging from 6.6° to 9.8° from the central fixation. All familiar faces from the memorization task appeared in the search task.

Questionnaires (Fig. 1C)

Following the two tasks, participants were asked to complete a famous faces recognition questionnaire which included the same famous faces that appeared throughout the experiment (note that this is a different version of a famous faces questionnaire than the one used for diagnosis). This questionnaire included the following questions regarding every face: Do you recognize this person? Can you write down their name? What is the relevant context in which this image is familiar to you? The participants were informed that they could answer “I don’t know” to the question, in case the face or its context were unknown to them. To assess familiarity with the celebrities independent of visual recognition, participants completed a separate questionnaire in which they identified celebrities by their printed names rather than their images16. These questionnaires were used to differentiate between explicit and implicit recognition. Explicit recognition was defined as answering “Yes” to both questions (“Do you recognize this person?” and “Do you recognize this name?”). Implicit recognition was defined as answering “No” to the face-recognition question but “Yes” to the name-recognition question. Participants were also asked to provide contextual details about each celebrity when viewing both the image and the written name (see Flowchart S1 in the Supplementary Materials). However, the ability to provide additional details was not a requirement for categorization of a face as explicitly or implicitly recognized. Rather, a “Yes” response to the recognition question was sufficient to classify a face/name as recognized, even if participants could not recall further information.

Apparatus and stimuli

The apparatus and stimuli used in both tasks were similar to those in previous experiments. For more details on the memorization task and visual search task, see20,21 and19 respectively.

Data acquisition and exclusion criteria

We used the famous faces recognition questionnaire that was administered at the end of the experiment to determine which famous faces were completely unfamiliar to the participants (i.e., not recognized by name and image) and removed these trials from the analyses of the memorization and visual search, this was done for both CP (8.2%) and controls (1.6%). We also excluded trials where celebrities were recognized by image but not by name (1.8% in CPs, 3.7% in controls), as well as those with faulty recognition, such as incorrect names or wrong contextual details (3.9% in CPs, 0.5% in controls). While cases in which participants offered no additional information were treated leniently and could still be credited as correct, explicit misidentifications or recognition errors could not be reliably classified as unfamiliar, implicit, or explicit recognition, and therefore excluded from the analyses. See Supplementary Table S4 for the number of trials per participant in each recognition category (Unfamiliar, Implicit Familiarity, Explicit Familiarity, Recognized by image only, and Mistakes in Recognition).

Additionally, in the visual search task, we found that some faces were familiar to control participants by name only; these were excluded from the main analysis (10 trials overall) and were examined separately (see Supplementary Figure S1). In the original study19, trials were excluded if (a) participants shifted their gaze before the fixation cross disappeared, (b) more than 20% of gaze samples were missing, or (c) participants responded without looking at the familiar face in the visual search task. In the present study, we adopted a more liberal criterion for data loss: trials were excluded only when more than 50% of gaze samples were missing (resulting in ~ 0.5% of trials excluded, compared to a substantially higher proportion had we applied the stricter 20% cutoff, 2% of trials). In addition, criterion (a) was not applied, although criterion (c) was retained. This adjustment was made to preserve as much data as possible given the inherently small CP sample, while still ensuring acceptable data quality.

Data analysis

To assess the influence of familiarity on gaze deployment, we followed the approach used in previous studies19–21 and conducted a visit analysis. A visit is defined as all consecutive fixations within the same area (i.e. face) before an eye movement is made outside of that area. Visits were captured during the simultaneous display of faces, in each task. We measured three aspects of visits: the time until the first visit on a face, mean number of visits on a face and mean visits duration on a face20,21. These measures were calculated separately for unfamiliar and familiar faces. We further distinguished between faces recognized by both name and image in the final questionnaires and those recognized by name only, allowing us to measure explicit and implicit recognition, respectively.

Because each display featured one familiar face and several unfamiliar faces, we needed to account for this imbalance in our analysis. In the memorization task, we addressed this by averaging the measures across the three unfamiliar faces. However, this approach was not suitable for the visual search task, as its completion depended on locating the familiar face. This characteristic of the task could introduce a bias, particularly for the time of first visit, which would naturally be earlier for unfamiliar faces than for familiar ones due to the requirement to terminate the task once a familiar face is detected. To mitigate this bias, instead of averaging across all unfamiliar faces, we selected a predefined unfamiliar face for comparison (specifically, this face was a German celebrity used in the original study, see19 for more details). Importantly, in approximately 20% of trials, this predefined unfamiliar face was not fixated at all, introducing a bias toward earlier fixation times for unfamiliar faces. To correct this, we assigned the first visit time of the unfamiliar face in these trials to the maximum trial duration, effectively simulating a scenario in which the unfamiliar face would have been the next to be fixated if the trial had not ended due to finding the familiar face.

This study was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/hpfng/?view_only=386d3c0631f842e6aef7493e1202a354), where all data, analysis scripts, and materials are openly available.

Results

For detailed means and standard deviations for each visit measure in the memorization and visual search tasks, see Tables S2 and S3 in the Supplementary Materials, respectively.

Is familiarity-guided gaze deployment similar between individuals with CP and neurotypicals?

To address this question, we compared gaze patterns of controls and CPs in the two tasks, focusing on gaze behavior towards unfamiliar faces and explicitly recognized familiar faces. To that end, we employed a two-way ANOVA with group [CP / control] as a between-subjects factor and familiarity [explicitly familiar / unfamiliar] as a within-subjects factor, for each gaze measure (time of first visit, number of visits and visit duration, see Methods section for more details).

CP and neurotypical participants exhibited similar effects of familiarity-guided attention when examining the number of visits (Fig. 2A) and visit duration (Fig. 2B). These measures reflected how gaze behavior, influenced by familiarity, is flexibly adjusted to meet task demands. In the memorization task, familiarity was significantly related to a reduced number of visits (F(1,14) = 41.75, p < .001, = 0.75) and to shorter visits (F(1,14) = 21.25, p < .001, = 0.60), reflecting the tendency to allocate less visual attention towards familiar faces during encoding. In the search task, familiarity was significantly related to an increased number of visits (F(1,14) = 19.91, p < .001, = 0.59) and to longer visits (F(1,14) = 62.07, p < .001, = 0.82), reflecting the tendency to allocate more attention towards the target familiar faces during search.

Results are presented separately for each group, with color saturation indicating familiarity level: explicit (dark blue), implicit (medium blue), and unfamiliar (light blue). Error bars represent ± SE. *p < .05, **p < .01, ***p < .001.

In the memorization task, no main effect was found for group for the number of visits (F(1,14) = 4.58, p = .050, = 0.25), but was observed for the visit duration: (F(1,14) = 9.47, p = .008, = 0.40). In the search task, a significant main effect of group was observed for visit duration (F(1,14) = 17.99, p < .001, = 0.56) and for the number of visits (F(1,14) = 4.76, p = .047, = 0.25). Moreover, in the search task a significant interaction between group and familiarity emerged for the number of visits (F(1,14) = 4.79, p = .046, = 0.26), whereas the interaction for visit duration did not reach significance (F(1,14) = 3.44, p = .085, = 0.20). To explore the source of this interaction, we conducted t-tests to compare familiar and unfamiliar faces within each group. This analysis showed that controls made significantly more visits to familiar faces (t(7) = -5.65, p = < 0.001, d = -0.84) but not CP participants (t(7) = − 1.40, p = .20, d = − 0.28).

The analysis of time of first visit yielded no main effect of familiarity in the memorization task (F(1,14) = 3.93, p = .067, = 0.22) but a significant main effect in the search task (F(1,14) = 12.16, p = .004, = 0.46). Importantly, despite the differences in tasks, the direction of the effect was similar between tasks: participants fixated the familiar face faster than the unfamiliar ones (Fig. 2C). There was no main effect of group in the search task (F(1,14) = 3.52, p = .082, = 0.20) nor in the memorization task (F(1,14) = 0.02, p = .889, = 0.00). In the memorization task, there was a significant interaction between group and familiarity (F(1,14) = 8.78, p = .010, = 0.39).

To explore the source of this interaction, we conducted t-tests to compare familiar and unfamiliar faces within each group. This analysis showed that the first visit time to familiar faces was significantly earlier for CP participants (t(7) = 2.58, p = .036, d = 0.57) but not for neurotypical participants (t(7) = − 1.70, p = .132, d = − 0.11). Thus, only the CP participants displayed the previously described initial preference for the familiar face during the memorization task29,30, while the controls did not. In the search task, no significant interaction was observed between familiarity and group (F(1,14) = 0.22, p = .644, = 0.02).

Overall, we found that when CP participants and controls could explicitly recognize familiar faces, their gaze behavior toward familiar and unfamiliar faces followed a qualitatively similar pattern. Importantly, CP participants demonstrated the ability to flexibly adjust the allocation of gaze based on current task demands and leverage familiarity to enhance efficient deployment of overt attention.

Is implicit familiarity sufficient to drive modifications of gaze behavior?

To address this question, we focused on the gaze patterns of CP participants and compared their responses to implicitly recognized, explicitly recognized and unfamiliar faces. The distinction between explicit and implicit recognition was based on participants’ responses in the debriefing questionnaire, where they indicated whether the faces were recognized by both image and name or only by name (see Methods and Supplementary Flowchart S1.). Since implicit recognition could also occur in the typical population (i.e., the ability to recognize someone by name but not by image), most participants in the control group exhibited some instances of implicit familiarity in the search task but to a lesser extent (1.02% in control compared to 37.86% in CP participants). The analysis of these cases in the search task is presented in the Supplementary Materials and reveals a pattern similar to the one described for CP. Such cases were even more rare in the memorization task that included only eight celebrities, yielding such analysis meaningless.

Based on the division according to familiarity type, we computed a one-way ANOVA with a within-subject factor of familiarity type [explicit familiarity / implicit familiarity / unfamiliar] for each measure. In the memorization task, there was no significant effect of familiarity for time until the first visit (F(2,14) = 1.60, p = .236, = 0.19) or visit duration (F(2,14) = 0.83, p = .455, = 0.11), but a significant main effect emerged for number of visits (F(2,14) = 5.20, p = .021, = 0.43; see Fig. 2A–C). To further investigate the effects of familiarity, we extended the analysis by conducting two follow-up t-tests: one comparing explicitly and implicitly recognized faces, and another comparing implicitly recognized faces with unfamiliar ones.

The paired t-test comparisons revealed no significant differences between implicit and explicit familiar faces for any of the measures in the memorization task, including time until the first visit (t(7) = − 0.13, p = .902, d = − 0.07), visit duration (t(7) = − 0.93, p = .383, d = − 0.47), and number of visits (t(7) = − 0.40, p = .698, d = − 0.20).

The comparison between implicitly familiar faces and unfamiliar ones also showed no significant differences for time until the first visit (t(7) = 1.55, p = .166, d = 0.78) or visit duration (t(7) = − 0.14, p = .896, d = − 0.05). However, implicitly recognized faces were revisited significantly less often than unfamiliar faces (t(7) = 2.51, p = .041, d = 0.79).

In the search task, the ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of familiarity type for visit duration (F(2,14) = 27.61, p < .001, = 0.80) and number of visits (F(2,14) = 8.54, p = .003, = 0.55), but not for time until the first visit (F(2,14) = 2.94, p = .086, = 0.30). Once again, we followed these effects with comparisons between implicitly recognized faces and explicitly familiar or unfamiliar faces.

These comparisons showed a significant difference for visit duration (t(7) = 5.49, p = .001, d = 0.67) between explicitly and implicitly familiar faces, with longer duration for the explicitly familiar faces compared to the implicitly familiar ones. For number of visits, CP participants revisited explicitly familiar faces more often than implicitly familiar ones (t(7) = − 2.41, p = .047, d = − 0.38). For the time until the first visit, CP did not show a significant difference between explicitly and implicitly familiar faces (t(7) = -0.5, p = .6 d = -0.06). Comparing implicitly familiar faces and unfamiliar ones revealed that implicitly familiar faces attracted fewer visits (t(7) = − 4.88, p = .002, d = − 0.60) but longer viewing durations (t(7) = − 2.93, p = .022, d = − 0.52). No significant difference was found for first visit time (t(7) = 1.46, p = .188, d = 0.28).

These findings indicate that while CP participants cannot explicitly report many familiar faces as such, they still process familiarity at an implicit level. This effect is evident across different task demands, indicating that implicit familiarity can, to some extent, influence gaze behavior and support the efficient allocation of overt attention.

Analyses of accuracy and RTs

To complete the analyses, we also examined the performance in the two tasks in relation to the questions addressed above: (1) Will the effects of familiarity on performance be similar for CP and neurotypical participants? and (2) Will implicit familiarity be sufficient to impact performance?

To address the first question, we examined whether the groups’ performance in the memorization task (i.e., correctly reporting that a face had appeared in the previous parallel display) was better than chance (50%). A one-sample t-test showed that accuracy was significantly above chance for controls when the face to report was familiar (t(7) = 21.39, p < .001, d = 7.56) and unfamiliar (t(7) = 11.15, p < .001, d = 3.94). CP participants showed a similar pattern and performed significantly above chance for familiar (t(7) = 11.88, p < .001, d = 4.20) and unfamiliar faces (t(7) = 3.55, p = .009, d = 1.90).

Additionally, to investigate possible differences in performance between the groups, we employed a two-way ANOVA with group [CP / control] as a between-subjects factor and familiarity of the face being reported on [explicitly familiar / unfamiliar] as a within-subjects factor (Table 3). This analysis revealed a significant main effect of familiarity for accuracy (F(1,14) = 9.36, p = .008, = 0.40) and response time (RT; F(1,14) = 15.67, p = .001, = 0.53). A main effect of group was found for accuracy (F(1,14) = 6.12, p = .027, = 0.30) but not for RT (F(1,14) = 3.42, p = .086, = 0.20), suggesting that CP participants had relatively compatible response time to controls. The interaction between familiarity and group was not significant for accuracy (F(1,14) = 1.11, p = .311, = 0.07) or RT (F(1,14) = 0.76, p = .397, = 0.05).

In the search task, we conducted one-sample t-tests to determine whether participants identified explicitly familiar faces as the search target significantly more often than would be expected by chance (20%). Controls (t(14) = 18.83, p < .001, d = 4.86) as well as CPs (t(7) = 19.27, p < .001, d = 6.81) performed well above chance. To compare between CP and controls on accuracy and RT, we employed paired-sample t-tests. The results revealed that controls were more accurate (t(7) = 2.99, p = .020, d = 1.71) and had faster RTs (t(7) = − 2.63, p = .034, d = − 1.55) compared to CP participants (Table 3).

So far, we have shown that while CP participants generally perform worse than neurotypicals in tasks involving face recognition when faces are explicitly recognized, they still perform better than chance. The outstanding question is whether similar patterns will emerge when familiarity could not be explicitly reported.

To explore this question, we first examined whether accuracy was higher than chance when the familiar face was only implicitly recognized by CP participants. Interestingly, performance was better than chance only in the search task (t(7) = 7.45, p < .001, d = 2.63) but not in the memorization one (t(7) = − 0.28, p = .789, d = − 0.10).

To further examine the impact of implicit familiarity on performance, we compared accuracy and RT of CP participants when the familiar faces were explicitly and implicitly recognized (Table 3). Thus, we conducted a one-way ANOVA with factor of familiarity type [explicit / implicit / unfamiliar] in the memorization task and a paired-sample t-test [explicit / implicit] for the visual search task.

In both tasks, familiarity type had a significant effect on accuracy. In the memorization task, CP participants were more accurate when the reported face was explicitly familiar compared to implicitly familiar (t(7) = 3.63, p = .008, d = 1.60), and also more accurate for unfamiliar faces than implicitly familiar (t(7) = 2.60, p = .036, d = 0.98). Similarly, in the search task, CP participants were more accurate for explicitly recognized familiar faces compared to implicitly familiar ones (t(7) = 9.24, p < .001, d = 4.45).

For the reaction time measure, in the memorization task, the main effect of familiarity type was not significant (F(2,14) = 2.93, p = .086,, = 0.30). Paired comparisons showed insignificant differences between explicitly familiar and implicitly familiar faces (t(7) = − 2.10, p = .073, d = − 0.63), and between unfamiliar and implicitly familiar faces (t(7) = − 0.98, p = .359, d = − 0.32). In the search task, participants responded faster to explicitly familiar faces compared to implicitly familiar ones (t(7) = − 3.06, p = .018, d = − 0.39).

Our sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S1), showed that the present design is sufficiently powered to detect only relatively large effects (e.g., partial η² ≈ 0.12 for the familiarity main effect and the familiarity × group interaction, partial η² ≈ 0.30 for the between-group effect, and partial η² ≈ 0.36 for the one-way ANOVA). Hence, the interpretation of our findings should be viewed in light of these statistical considerations. The main familiarity effects observed in the behavioral measures exceeded these thresholds, providing strong evidence for robust familiarity-guided performance differences. In contrast, several group effects and group × familiarity interactions yielded effect sizes close to or below the detectable range, and thus their statistical significance, or lack thereof, should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

The goal of the current study was to examine whether familiarity can guide dynamics of gaze in CP and whether this guidance persists even for implicitly recognized faces. To this end, we employed two visual tasks previously used with neurotypical participants, a memorization task and a visual search task, while tracking eye movements19–21. Unlike earlier studies that focused on general face processing7 or explored viewing patterns of isolated facial features8–10, we analyzed gaze behavior in response to arrays containing multiple faces.

Familiarity-Guided effects on gaze deployment in CP

In both tasks, when participants could explicitly recognize the familiar face, individuals with CP exhibited flexible gaze behavior patterns similar to those of neurotypicals. In the memorization task, which required encoding of both familiar and unfamiliar faces, both groups spent less time looking at familiar faces, as indicated by fewer and shorter visits. This finding replicates previous studies in neurotypical populations20,21 and reflects a strategic allocation of attentional resources, prioritizing unfamiliar faces that are more challenging to encode. In contrast, in the search task, in which participants were instructed to locate a familiar face, gaze behavior shifted to favor the familiar face, as evidenced by earlier visits and longer viewing durations. This demonstrates that participants in both groups were able to flexibly adjust their gaze behavior to meet the demands of the task.

Interestingly, modifications of gaze behavior were also evident when familiarity was implicit. In this case, gaze allocation towards famous faces that were not explicitly recognized in the final debriefing, differed from those of unfamiliar faces. This was reflected in fewer visits in the memorization task, as well as more frequent and prolonged visits in the visual search task. Notably, these patterns resemble those observed for explicitly recognized faces, suggesting that implicit familiarity can still influence visual attention, even in the absence of conscious recognition.

Despite showing gaze behavior patterns similar to neurotypical individuals, performance of participants with CP was overall poorer in both tasks, demonstrating lower accuracy (and slower reaction times in the search task) even for explicitly recognized faces. This suggests persistent difficulties in retrieving familiar faces from both short-term memory (as required in the memorization task) and long-term memory (as required in the search task). These results align with previous findings in CP, which point to deficits in encoding faces and also difficulties in maintaining and updating face representations in working memory35.

Nevertheless, despite their overall reduced performance, individuals with CP were still able to leverage familiarity to support task performance. This implies that familiarity continues to confer a measurable advantage, even in the context of impaired performance relative to neurotypical controls. This advantage, however, was reduced or even disappeared when familiarity was only implicit. In both tasks, performance was poorer for implicitly recognized faces compared to explicitly recognized ones. In the memorization task, performance for implicitly familiar faces was so low that it was not significantly better than chance. This suggests that, for individuals with CP, familiarity-guided attention in cases of implicit familiarity may function as a kind of “honey trap”: the face evokes a subtle sense of familiarity, strong enough to engage the attentional system’s bias toward unfamiliar faces, yet insufficient to enhance retrieval. As a result, attention is directed away from these implicitly familiar faces, which may hinder their encoding and lead to difficulties retrieving them from short-term memory.

Surprisingly, only individuals with CP exhibited the previously reported effect of earlier fixation on familiar items21. Prior studies21 have attributed this effect to the “personal significance” of familiar items, suggesting that its absence in the control group may be due to the use of celebrity faces rather than personally familiar faces or acquaintances. The presence of this effect in the CP group raises intriguing questions about their sensitivity to the emotional or personal value of faces, which could be further explored in future research.

Indications of implicit familiarity in gaze patterns

Our finding of differences in the gaze behavior patterns of individuals with CP between implicitly familiar and unfamiliar faces supports the idea that while individuals with CP may not explicitly identify well-known, familiar faces, they nevertheless exhibit implicit processing, as evident by measures of accuracy and RT16 and eye movement patterns15,36. For example, a case study revealed that a CP participant exhibited faster responses to famous faces, increased number of consecutive fixations on familiar faces, and greater region sampling compared to unfamiliar faces. Notably, even in instances where explicit recognition failed, his eye movement patterns aligned with those of control participants who successfully identified the faces15. Similarly, another study reported improved matching performance and accuracy for famous over unfamiliar faces, even when familiar faces were not explicitly recognized16. In contrast to previous eye-tracking studies on individuals with CP15, the present study examined gaze patterns while multiple faces were presented. Unlike studies that present a single face at a time, this approach provides a unique opportunity to examine how individuals with CP prioritize between multiple faces, rather than focusing solely on specific facial features. Moreover, it offers a more ecologically valid perspective, capturing the challenges CP individuals face in real-world situations, such as navigating social interactions in a room full of people.

The finding that implicit familiarity influences eye movements in individuals with CP aligns with previous research in neurotypical populations, where eye-tracking has been used to uncover evidence of implicit processing37–41. For example, one study demonstrated that when viewing manipulated scenes, observers fixated longer on the manipulated regions, even when they were unaware of the change41. Other studies have shown that after pairing faces with scenes, subliminal presentation of the scene elicits longer viewing of the learned face39, or that pairing a stimulus with an aversive outcome leads to modifications in gaze behavior toward the aversive stimulus even before explicit learning occurs38. Here, we extend this line of research by leveraging the unique characteristics of CP to demonstrate that even when face identity could not be reported explicitly, they still influence gaze deployment.

Limitations

A potential concern is that our categorization of explicit and implicit recognition relied on responses from the final questionnaire, whereas recognition may have shifted over the course of the experiment. The more plausible scenario is that some faces initially went unrecognized during the tasks but became familiar through repeated exposure, leading to correct recognition in the final questionnaire. By contrast, the reverse scenario (accurate recognition during the tasks but failure to recognize the same faces in the questionnaire after multiple exposures) appears to be unlikely.

One potential source of the mentioned mismatch (where faces unfamiliar during the trials were reported as familiar in the questionnaire) is that participants may have interpreted the instructions of the questionnaire as referring to familiarity gained during the task rather than prior knowledge. To minimize this risk, we explicitly emphasized that the questionnaire concerned familiarity with the faces prior to the experiment.

Importantly, even if such cases persisted, they would not undermine the results. Faces that were not recognized during the task but were reported as familiar in the final questionnaire would have been classified as explicitly familiar, potentially introducing some noise into this category. Nevertheless, explicitly familiar faces still elicited reliably distinct patterns from unfamiliar ones, indicating that the explicit category predominantly reflected genuine familiarity. In participants with prosopagnosia, these faces produced patterns similar to controls while remaining clearly distinct from unfamiliar faces, further supporting the conclusion that explicit familiarity guides visual attention, even in the presence of potential classification noise.

Additionally, a key limitation of the present study is the small sample size. Although our initial power analysis indicated that 13 participants would be required, practical constraints reduced the final sample to 8. This inevitably lowers statistical power and limits the ability to detect small effects. Our sensitivity analyses showed that the design was sufficiently powered to detect only relatively large effects. Accordingly, the robust familiarity effects observed here, whose effect sizes exceeded the detectable threshold (partial η² ≈ 0.12), could be considered reliable. In contrast, some group differences and group × familiarity interactions fell within the lower end of the detectable range, meaning that their nonsignificant outcomes should not be taken as evidence of no effect. Instead, these results likely reflect limited sensitivity to small-to-medium effects. Nonetheless, the findings remain meaningful in several respects: (i) the observed effects consistently aligned with a priori predictions based on previous studies, grounded in theory and supported by the control group; (ii) the distinction between implicit and explicit familiarity emerged across tasks and measures, suggesting a reliable pattern rather than an isolated result; and (iii) effect sizes and confidence intervals are reported, enabling readers to assess the magnitude and precision of the results beyond significance testing. Taken together, while the limited sample size is an important constraint, the converging evidence supports the view that implicit familiarity is sufficient to guide visual attention in complex displays. Future research could build on these findings as a starting point for clarifying and refining how implicit familiarity operates, and how it may differ from explicit familiarity.

Conclusions

The current study presents a novel, fine-grained analysis of eye movement patterns in individuals with CP as they view familiar and unfamiliar faces within multi-face displays. This approach addresses two previously unresolved questions: Can individuals with CP use facial familiarity to guide their gaze similarly to neurotypical individuals? And is implicit familiarity alone sufficient to drive this effect? The affirmative findings to both questions offer new insights into the mechanisms underlying CP, deepen our understanding of how familiarity functions in this population, and demonstrate that attentional guidance can remain intact, even for implicitly familiar faces.
