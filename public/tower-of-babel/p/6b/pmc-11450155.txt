Profile of antimicrobial use and potential use of the defined daily dose in neonatology
Authors: Cinara Rejane Viana Oliveira, Izabel Almeida Alves, Alfredo Dias de Oliveira-Filho, Ney Boa-Sorte
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
One of the measures for monitoring microbial resistance is the calculation of the defined daily dose of antimicrobial agents. For this calculation, the weight of an adult of 70 kg is used as a standard, so that application in neonatology is not possible. The aim of this study is to describe the use profile and calculate the defined daily dose (DDD) of antimicrobials in a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) of a public hospital in the interior of Bahia, Brazil. From March 2020 to December 2021, the medical records of 712 newborns admitted to a NICU between September 2018 and June 2020 were analyzed. A total of 410 newborns diagnosed with neonatal sepsis were included. The most used antimicrobials per patient were gentamicin (408/410; 99.5%), ampicillin (407; 99.3%), amikacin (29; 7.1%) and oxacillin (21; 5.1%), with a mean (SD) treatment duration of 9.8 (3.9) days. The most commonly used combination of antimicrobials was ampicillin with gentamicin, which was used in 406 patients (99.0%). The values for neonatal DDDs were on average 26 times lower than those for adult DDDs. The neonatal DDDs were similar to those observed in other studies. Ampicilin and cefepime were the antimicrobials for which the greatest differences were observed in neonatal DDDs compared with adult DDDs, which differed mainly between maintenance doses, reflecting the lack of international standards in neonatology. Standardization of DDDs as a surveillance measure has the potential to clarify the pattern of antimicrobial use in neonatal patients worldwide and, in particular, to prevent indiscriminate use and bacterial resistance.

Introduction

Antimicrobials are drugs that are frequently prescribed for hospitalized neonatal patients. They account for 75% of drugs prescribed in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs)1,2. Despite the high use of antimicrobials in neonatology, there is no standardized assessment of their use3.

The globally recommended unit of measurement for antimicrobial consumption by the WHO is the Defined Daily Dose (DDD)4. With this tool, it is possible to assess, in a standardized way, the consumption of antimicrobials in hospitals3. For the calculation of the DDD to be carried out, the weight of an adult of 70 kg is used as a standard, while, to express the average daily maintenance, the unit of measure gram (g) is used, thus, its application ends up being unfeasible. in pediatrics and neonatology4,5.

In neonatology, given the lack of a large variation in weight among newborns, some studies enable the possibility of applying the DDD, and the precursor study was carried out by Liem et al., in 20106. The creation of a standard for calculating neonatal DDD (nDDD) is necessary to measure consumption in this population, which favors the increase of research and the comparison between different health units, making it possible to draw a real profile of ATM use and direct actions to control their indiscriminate use7.

The indiscriminate use of antimicrobials in the hospital environment can lead to ineffective treatment, in addition to an increase in the mortality rate, prolongation of the disease, longer stay in the hospital environment, as well as higher financial expenses4. For neonates, this concern is even greater, as due to the inherent physiological conditions of neonates, such as the dynamic development of organs, changes in body composition, the evolution of gene/enzyme expression, and comorbidities, they can influence the efficacy and toxicity of the drug2.

Consumption and antimicrobial resistance data are needed, especially in developing countries, where public sector control and reporting are still weak8. This issue becomes even more relevant outside the large urban centers and the most populous cities, with very little data on this issue in medium-sized cities in Brazil.

Considering that the increase in bacterial resistance in neonatology is a reality that affects the entire population, as well as the lack of research in the area, it is necessary to reflect on the implementation of strategies aimed at reducing the incorrect and indiscriminate consumption of antibiotics. And, among these strategies, there are studies of antimicrobial consumption in neonatology.

In this context, processes that adopt standardized measurement units for TMJ consumption in neonatology should be instigated. Thus, the objective of this study was to describe the use profile and calculate the Defined Daily Dose (DDD) of antimicrobials in a NICU of a public hospital in northeast of Brazil.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted from March 2020 to December 2021 in a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) of a high-complexity hospital in a medium-sized city in the state of Bahia, northeastern Brazil. This hospital serves as a reference for medical, surgical, pediatric, and obstetric care in the NICU, adult ICU and neurosurgery and covers 37 municipalities with an estimated population of 586,000 people.

This study was carried out following the ethical guidelines of the norm contained in the CNS Resolution nº 466/12, which is based in the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the State University of Bahia (UNEB), review number 4.463.864/2020, and by the Institutional Review Board of the study hospital.

Sample selection

The medical records of all 712 newborns consecutively admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit between September 2018 and June 2020 were reviewed. To be included in the study, neonates had to have a diagnosis of early or late sepsis in their medical records and had to have completed antimicrobial use in any unit of the hospital between September 2018 and June 2020. Newborns whose medical records showed that they had been transferred from another department that was already using antimicrobials, who had not completed antimicrobial treatment at the study hospital or those who had been on the same antimicrobial for more than 30 days were excluded.

Data collection and variable settings

A form prepared by the authors was used for data collection, which included information on maternal history, obstetric history, and clinical and laboratory features of newborns about known risk factors for neonatal sepsis and antibiotic use. All data were obtained from the medical records of the newborns included in the study.

In the study hospital, sepsis was defined as indicated in the Latin American Sepsis Consensus9. In brief a clinical syndrome characterized by a potentially fatal organ dysfunction caused by an unregulated host response to the infection. The consensus recommends taking into account the clinical criteria, the medical team’s and parents’ assessment of the severity of the illness and the presence of comorbidities in order to recognize sepsis at an early stage. Additionally, consensus recommend starting antibiotic treatment as soon as possible, within the first three hours after diagnosis9.

All antimicrobial agents used by the patients were analysed according to type, average duration of treatment, frequency of association per patient and classification according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification (ATC).

Comparison of the arithmetic average doses of antimicrobials found with dosage recommendations from (inter)national sources.

To calculate the DDD, the arithmetic average dose in mg/kg/day was first determined.

The arithmetic average dose found was also compared with the recommended dose given in three databases: Thompson Micromedex®, Red Book and the recommendation of the Brazilian Society of Pediatrics (Sociedade Brasileira de Pediatria in portuguese). The Thompsom Micromedex® and Red Book databases are international reference databases that are considered the gold standard. The database of the Brazilian Society of Pediatrics is a reference database in Brazil. The purpose of using this database was to analyze whether the dosages in the neonatal unit where we conducted the study were compatible with the standardized dosages recommended in Brazil.

Data analysis

To calculate the neonatal DDD (DDDn) in grammes (g) for each antimicrobial, the average weight of all patients (which was 2 kg in our study) was multiplied by the recommended dose for each antimicrobial (mg per kg). The DDDn was compared with the adult DDD, which is based on 70 kg.

The most used antimicrobials were gentamicin, ampicillin, amikacin, and oxacillin, respectively, by 408 (99.5%); 407 (99.3%); 29 (7.1%), and 21 (5.1%) patients.

Regarding the duration of treatment with antimicrobials, the mean (SD) duration of treatment was equal to 9.8 (3.9) days, ranging from 1 to 33 days of use, with a median (25th–75th ) of 10. 0 (9.0–11.0) days. Among neonates who used more than two antimicrobial regimens (N = 31), this average was 18.9 (5.5) days, with a median (25th -75th ) of 19.0 (14.0–23.0).

The antimicrobials that were most used associated were ampicillin with gentamicin, in 406 prescriptions (99.0% of neonates). With regard to use blood samples of the 410 patients, 178 (43.4%) underwent a blood culture test, and of these, only two microorganisms were isolated (Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is one of the first to be conducted in Brazil and that deals with the application of DDD in neonatology. In addition, this study determined an average dose of antimicrobials for hospitalised neonatal patients. The determination of the DDDn allowed a comparison with the dose defined by the WHO for adults and with the DDDn values observed in other studies. In general, the DDDn are similar to each other, regardless of the country where the study was conducted, which of course all differ from the DDD for adults2–4,6,7,10.

Detailed knowledge of antibiotic use, both quantitative and qualitative, is essential for implementing effective strategies to reduce the overuse, underuse, and inappropriate use of antibiotics, thereby addressing the threat of resistant microorganisms. Various methods can quantify antibiotic use in hospitals, but the Defined Daily Dose (DDD) is the most commonly used unit. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends the DDD for comparing medication use across different settings, underscoring its value for this purpose4,6.

Although the DDD is not ideal for quantifying antibiotic use due to significant variations in body weight, especially in the pediatric population, it can be useful in the neonatal population. In this age group, due to the narrower range of body weights compared to pediatric patients and the difficulty in accessing individual data, neonatal DDDs can serve as an appropriate unit of measurement6.

The DDD is an estimate of consumption. For neonatal patients, using a standard weight of 2 kg, rather than 70 kg, to calculate the DDD provides a more accurate measurement of antimicrobial consumption, rather than applying a standard that does not accurately reflect consumption in the neonatal population.

Neonatal sepsis is a common syndrome in neonatal intensive care units. In this study, 57.5% of patients were diagnosed with neonatal sepsis. Furthermore, neonatal sepsis has a mortality rate exceeding 50% in low-income countries and between 15% and 30% in high-income countries. Globally, approximately 30 million cases occur each year, leading to about 8 million deaths11. Due to these factors, we chose to include only patients with neonatal sepsis in this study4,6.

The clinical and sociodemographic results of the patients showed that more than 65% of the patients were premature and had a low birth weight. Prematurity, together with low birth weight, is the main risk factor for the development of neonatal sepsis.

The most commonly used antimicrobials in NICUs, mainly for empirical treatments, were ampicillin, amikacin, and gentamicin. According to the Brazilian Society of Pediatrics, this situation is justified by the low induction of resistance and the high sensitivity of gram-negative rods to amikacin, as well as its wide availability and low cost12.

For treatment with antimicrobial agents to be effective, it must have a certain duration depending on the case. Some factors can influence this duration, such as the result of the culture and the severity of the infection7. A study by Shane et al. emphasizes that the duration of treatment in early-onset sepsis in combination with ampicillin and aminoglycoside should be 10 days13. Bacteremia caused by group B streptococci and uncomplicated meningitis should be treated for 14 days. For complicated infections, treatment should be extended to 21–28 days13. In the present study, the median duration of treatment was 10 days, with an interquartile range of only 2 days and an average of 9.8 days. For those using a second antimicrobial regimen, indicating a more severe condition, the mean/median was 19 days, which is consistent with the literature7,13.

One of the factors associated with the long duration of treatment with antimicrobials is the lack of blood cultures or incorrect blood cultures, which makes it difficult to identify the microorganism and consequently causes a longer duration of treatment. In our study, less than 50% of the newborns had blood cultures performed. However, the blood cultures that were positive confirmed the presence of microorganisms associated with neonatal sepsis. The factors that may have influenced the large number of negative blood cultures are the collection at the wrong time and the lack of standardization in the diagnosis of sepsis4,13. This circumstance could speak in favour of an excessive and overtimed use of antimicrobial agents.

The association of antimicrobial agents varies depending on the type of sepsis. In our study, gentamicin was most combined with ampicillin, which is the most commonly used combination of antimicrobials for the treatment of early neonatal sepsis14.

When analysing the comparison between the doses of antimicrobials used in the NICU of the service studied, it was found that these were very close to the doses found in the three databases. The largest differences in doses were found for the antimicrobials ampicillin and cefepime, which were found to be overdosed according to the databases, and for piperacillin and inhibitor, which were found to be underdosed.

After calculating the difference between the DDD for adults given by the WHO and the DDDn, on average 26 times lower values were found compared to the DDD for adults. The largest differences were found for amoxicillin, which was 49 times lower, for piperacillin and inhibitor 43.4 times lower, for vancomycin 37.3 times lower and for amikacin 25.6 times lower.

The pioneering study that correlated the difference between adult and neonatal DDD values was prepared by Liem et al., in 2010. In this study, a weight of 2.0 kg was used as a basis, the same value used in the present study, after rounding6.

As has been shown, the DDDn for most antimicrobials is close to the values found in this work. The largest differences in DDDn are mainly explained by differences in maintenance doses, as there is no international standard in neonatology.

One limitation relates to the definition of sepsis. We use a Latin American consensus definition that emphasizes the clinical assessment of the treatment team. As many countries in Latin America have limited resources, it is recommended to initiate treatment without waiting for laboratory test results. This circumstance emphasizes the need to use strategies to measure ATM consumption, which is very important.

Among the known pharmacokinetic variations in neonates, high pH can be highlighted, showing relative achlorhydria, a condition that directly affects absorption15, high body water, and low protein concentration, affecting drug distribution16. Regarding biotransformation, the hepatic microsomal enzymatic system is immature, a situation caused by the lack of enzymatic induction15. Finally, excretion is affected, as the renal clearance mechanism is compromised17.

These findings reinforce the possibility of standardizing the DDD for neonatology. The objective of standardizing the DDD in neonatology is to realistically estimate the use of antimicrobials in patients around the world. Therefore, this study showed, using real doses of antimicrobials, that the methodology proposed by Liem and collaborators can be reproduced. Furthermore, our study only included septic patients, which provides an estimate of the types of antimicrobials used and the average daily maintenance dose in this type of infection. It is necessary to validate this method in future studies, since this proposal can estimate the consumption of antimicrobials in neonatology, in addition to allowing the correlation of bacterial resistance data at a global level, promoting standardization and rational use.

Conclusion

The difference between neonatal DDD and adult DDD recommended by the WHO was mainly based on the weight of the patients, and in neonatology, in addition to weight, pharmacokinetic factors should also be taken into account.

The antimicrobials for which the greatest differences in neonatal DDDs compared to adult DDDs were observed mainly showed differences between maintenance doses, due to the lack of international standards in neonatology.

The standardization of DDDn together with other forms to measure the use of antimicrobials (Length of Therapy – LOT, Prescribed Daily Doses – PDD, Days of Therapy – DOT) and management actions of the Antimicrobial Stewardship Programme (ASP) has the potential to clarify the pattern of antimicrobial use in neonatal patients around the world, preventing indiscriminate use and in particular bacterial resistance.
