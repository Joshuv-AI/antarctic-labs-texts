Language, culture, and ethnicity data collection in pediatric healthcare in Canada: a national environmental scan
Authors: Victor Do, Amanda S Newton, Rebecca Gokiert, Geoff D C Ball
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesCanada's pediatric healthcare systems serve an increasingly ethnoculturally diverse population, yet the standardized collection of language, culture, and ethnicity (LCE) data remains inconsistent. Without reliable data, health systems cannot identify inequities, monitor disparities, or design targeted interventions. Our purpose was to examine current LCE data collection practices across pediatric healthcare settings in Canada and identify perceived barriers, enablers, and opportunities to strengthen equitable data infrastructure.MethodsA cross-sectional environmental scan was conducted between January and February 2026 using an anonymous online survey of pediatric healthcare clinicians and staff across Canada. The survey included closed- and open-ended questions on LCE data collection practices, tools, effectiveness, data use, training, and organizational supports. Quantitative data were analyzed descriptively; qualitative responses underwent inductive content analysis.ResultsA total of 237 participants from diverse pediatric settings completed the survey. Nearly 60% reported that their organization does not collect LCE data, and 56% were unsure of the effectiveness of existing practices. When collected, LCE data were primarily limited to preferred language and used to identify interpreter needs. Formal training and organizational policies were rare (both 3%). Perceived barriers included a lack of training, time constraints, and the absence of standardized tools. Qualitative findings highlighted strong conceptual support for LCE data collection but emphasized its conditional value, ethical complexity, and need for participatory approaches.ConclusionLCE data collection in Canadian pediatric healthcare remains fragmented and underutilized. Advancing equitable data infrastructure will require standardized protocols, targeted training, participatory co-design, and clear accountability for data use.Keywords: Language, Culture, Ethnicity, Data collection

Background

Canada's pediatric healthcare systems operate within an ethnoculturally diverse society. The 2021 Canadian Census reported over 450 ethnic or cultural origins, with more than five million individuals speaking a language other than English or French and nearly one million unable to conduct a conversation in either official language (1,2). This diversity underscores the importance of culturally responsive healthcare systems. Yet, linguistic and racial inequities persist, reinforcing differential access to care, experiences of discrimination, and disparate health outcomes among racialized and linguistically diverse children and families (3,4).

Structural racism and language barriers create systemic obstacles to equitable care (5). Studies internationally showed that children and families facing communication barriers are at higher risk for medical errors, lower patient satisfaction, and poorer clinical outcomes (6,7). These inequities may remain invisible at the point of care within healthcare institutions since standardized and comprehensive collection of language, culture, and ethnicity (LCE) data is inconsistent across pediatric settings (8,9). Without reliable and accurate LCE data, health systems cannot monitor disparities, evaluate interventions, or implement evidence-informed strategies for equitable and culturally safe care (8,9).

The absence of widely accepted standardized tools for collecting LCE data represents a critical gap in health information infrastructure (10). These include question scripts within electronic health records (EHRs) and standardized intake forms. Equally important are organizational procedures, workflows, policies, and staff training to support systematic and respectful data collection (11). If implemented meaningfully, these mechanisms may help identify inequities, monitor service delivery quality, and improve patient-centred care.

Initiatives across Canada have begun advancing this agenda. In British Columbia, the 2022 Anti-Racism Data Act authorizes the collection and use of demographic data to address systemic inequities, supported by an Anti-Racism Data Committee to guide culturally safe practices (12). Ontario's Health Equity Impact Assessment (HEIA) tool supports the identification of potential equity impacts in policy and service development (13). While important, these efforts vary in scope and implementation, and evidence of their integration into pediatric health care remains limited. Most provinces lack comparable legislative frameworks, providing an important comparative context.

Systematic LCE data collection is inherently complex, as language, culture, and ethnicity are socially constructed, dynamic, and context dependent. Although race, ethnicity, culture, and language are often grouped within sociodemographic data initiatives, they represent distinct constructs. Race reflects socially constructed systems of categorization that shape exposure to racism and inequitable social conditions (14). Ethnicity generally refers to shared ancestry, heritage, or cultural traditions, while culture encompasses values, beliefs, practices, and identities that influence how individuals understand and experience health and health care (15). Language reflects communication preferences and abilities that may directly affect access, safety, and quality of care. While race-based data collection has appropriately received substantial attention within health equity initiatives, less is known about how pediatric healthcare organizations collect and use language, culture, and ethnicity information. Variability in institutional interpretation, alongside ethical, technical, and relational challenges, can hinder standardized adoption. A comprehensive understanding of current practices, barriers, and opportunities for LCE data collection in pediatric health care is therefore needed.

This study aimed to examine current LCE data collection practices across pediatric health care settings in Canada and identify perceived barriers, enablers, and opportunities to strengthen equitable data infrastructure.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional multi-method environmental scan using an online survey to examine current tools, processes, and practices for collecting LCE information in pediatric health care. The survey was conducted between January and February 2026.

Survey development

Survey development followed Burns’ systematic approach to instrument design for healthcare research (16), progressing from concept definition to pilot testing to support content validity and internal consistency. Drawing on a preceding scoping review and literature on sociodemographic and language data collection (17,18), the research team identified key domains related to LCE data collection and use. The initial survey included 25 items with demographic questions. Interest-holders, including pediatric clinicians and health equity researchers (n = 5), provided feedback to ensure content validity and contextual relevance. A pilot test with 10 pediatric clinicians assessed usability, comprehension, and flow, informing revisions (e.g., shortening for ease of completion) prior to deployment.

The final survey consisted of 18 items (4 demographic, 11 closed-ended, and 3 open-ended), with an estimated completion time of 10 minutes. Topics included scope and methods of data collection, tools and systems used, barriers and facilitators, and perceived utility (see Supplementary Appendix A for a copy of our survey).

Setting and participants

A purposive, cross-sectional sampling strategy targeted pediatric healthcare providers across diverse Canadian settings, including tertiary hospitals, outpatient clinics, and community health centres. Participants were expected to have direct responsibility for, or working knowledge of, how LCE data are collected, recorded, or used within their organization. Recruitment occurred through national and provincial pediatric professional networks, institutional contacts, and health equity-focused organizations. We sought geographic and professional diversity by disseminating the survey across multiple provinces and to clinicians, administrators, and allied health professionals working in pediatric settings. Although recruitment was purposive in targeting individuals expected to have knowledge of organizational LCE data collection practices, participation was voluntary and self-selected, resulting in a sample with elements of convenience sampling.

Data collection

Data were collected anonymously via REDCap, a secure, web-based platform hosted by the Women and Children's Health Research Institute at the University of Alberta (19). Recruitment occurred through professional listservs, institutional networks, and direct invitations to pediatric health professionals and organizations, including the Pediatric Inpatient Research Network. Because dissemination occurred through overlapping distribution channels and secondary forwarding by contacts, the total number of individuals who received the survey could not be determined and a response rate could not be calculated. The survey remained open for 6 weeks, with two reminder emails to the listservs/contacts at 2-week intervals. Participation was voluntary, and completion of the survey implied consent.

Data analysis

Qualitative data underwent an inductive content analysis (20) to identify themes surrounding contextual nuances, challenges, and improvements. Following data cleaning and de-identification reviewers performed initial open coding on the raw text to ensure inter-rater reliability. Discrepancies in coding were resolved through consensus-based discussion to enhance interpretative rigor. Finally, a convergent triangulation approach (21) was employed to integrate these qualitative insights with quantitative descriptive results, providing a ‘thick description’ that contextualized and explained statistical variances within the primary findings.

Ethics

Ethics approval was obtained from the University of Alberta REB (Pro00153025). Participants provided implied consent through survey completion. Data were stored securely in REDCap and reported in aggregate to preserve anonymity and confidentiality.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 237 respondents completed the survey. Participants represented all major metropolitan regions of Canada, with the largest proportions from Ontario (25%), Alberta (22%), and Quebec (14%), followed by British Columbia (11%) and Saskatchewan (9%). The Atlantic provinces and Manitoba comprised the remaining responses (19%), with no respondents from our three territories. Respondents worked across diverse healthcare settings, most commonly pediatric outpatient clinics (49%) and acute care settings (34%). Over one-half (54%) had worked in their current setting for 1 to 5 years, with an additional 32% reporting 6 to 10 years of experience, suggesting familiarity with local clinical processes. LCE data collection practices

Respondents reported limited and inconsistent collection of LCE data (Table 2). Nearly 60% indicated that their organization did not collect LCE data, and 4% were unsure. Qualitative responses reinforced this, with participants expressing uncertainty about whether data were collected, how they were documented, or their accuracy. Data collection was often described as informal, inconsistent, or conflated with interpreter requests rather than a systematic practice. When collected, preferred language was the most common variable (32%), while ethnicity (6%), race (3%), culture (1%), and language proficiency (1%) were rarely captured. Among respondents indicating LCE data collection (∼40%), it most often occurred at registration or check-in (34%). Qualitative accounts described this as a front-line administrative task, often disconnected from broader clinical or organizational workflows.

Roles, tools and system gaps

Registration clerks (30%) and unit clerks (21%) were most often responsible for collecting LCE data, with limited involvement from physicians (9%) and nurses (5%). The most commonly reported tools were electronic health record (EHR) forms (26%) and direct verbal questioning (18%). Qualitative findings suggested this variability contributed to fragmented practices, with unclear roles, limited training, and minimal integration into clinical decision-making.

Perceived effectiveness and use of LCE data

Perceived effectiveness of LCE data collection was marked by substantial uncertainty. Over one-half of respondents (56%) selected ‘I don't know’, and among those who provided ratings, most were mid-range, with few rating tools as highly effective (3%). This uncertainty was echoed qualitatively, with participants reporting limited awareness of how LCE data were used or whether they informed care. When collected, use was primarily limited to identifying interpreter needs (29%). Use for broader purposes, such as tailoring care (7%), discharge planning (2%), quality improvement (3%), or population health and research (2%), was uncommon (Figure 1); however, respondents expressed strong conceptual support for LCE data to improve culturally safe and equitable care, though these benefits were largely described as aspirational rather than realized.

Cascade of reported language, culture, and ethnicity (LCE) data collection and use across pediatric healthcare settings. While 40% of respondents reported some LCE data collection in their setting, reported use narrowed substantially beyond interpreter identification, with minimal integration into clinical tailoring, discharge planning, quality improvement, or research activities.

Training, policies, and barriers

Formal support for LCE data collection was limited. Nearly all respondents (97%) reported no training, and only 3% identified formal organizational policies, with over half unsure whether such policies existed. Key barriers (selected from a predefined list on survey) included lack of training (43%), time constraints (43%), unclear data use (40%), and absence of standardized tools (40%). Privacy and ethical concerns (36%), lack of organizational mandate (33%), and perceived reluctance from families (32%) were also common. Qualitative findings highlighted concerns related to trust, potential data misuse, and ethical implications, particularly for culturally and ethnically sensitive information. Without clear purpose and safeguards, LCE data collection was sometimes perceived as performative or potentially harmful.

Conditional value and ethical reflexivity

Across the quantitative and qualitative findings, respondents emphasized that the value of LCE data collection depends on meaningful use. Collecting data without a clear application was viewed as ineffective.

Participants consistently recommended system-level improvements, including standardized protocols, clear articulation of purpose, staff training, and integration into clinical workflows to reduce burden. A prominent qualitative finding was the need for participatory approaches, engaging clinicians, administrative staff, patients, families, and communities in co-design.

Indigenous-specific considerations were emphasized, particularly the importance of data sovereignty and appropriate governance. Respondents noted that Indigenous identity data require distinct, principled approaches supported by policy, infrastructure, and community engagement.

Recommendations for improvement

Respondents offered several recommendations to improve data collection. Key suggestions included the development of standardized, system-wide protocols; clear articulation of why data are being collected and how they will be used; education and training to support culturally safe conversations; and integration of LCE data collection into existing workflows to minimize burden. Many also highlighted the need for mandates, policy guidance, EHR, infrastructure, and safeguards to ensure ethical and effective implementation.

Discussion

Our study highlighted a substantial gap between the recognized importance of LCE data collection and its realization in pediatric health care in Canada. Across jurisdictions and roles, respondents expressed strong support for LCE data as a mechanism to advance culturally safe, equitable care. However, this support coexists with uncertainty, inconsistency, and limited institutional infrastructure, resulting in fragmented and underutilized data practices. These findings suggest that the key challenge is not whether LCE data should be collected, but how to do so effectively, ethically, and in ways that meaningfully inform care and system improvement.

A central contribution of this study is its insight into how LCE data collection effectiveness is understood. The high proportion of “I don't know responses, alongside qualitative uncertainty about how effectiveness could be defined, points to a conceptual gap. LCE data collection appears not to be operationalized as a coherent system-level process, but rather as a diffuse set of activities unevenly embedded across registration, clinical workflows, and documentation practices.

These findings align with implementation science literature, which emphasizes that the success of data initiatives depends not only on discrete tools, such as EHR fields or intake forms, but also on the broader sociotechnical system (22). Standardized question scripts, validation procedures, staff roles, governance structures, and feedback loops are essential components of effective data collection (10,23). In their absence, respondents are unable to assess whether data are accurate, complete, or actionable (10,23). Effectiveness should therefore be conceptualized as an end-to-end process encompassing purpose definition, collection, access, use, and evaluation. Our findings also highlight the conceptual complexity of collecting sociodemographic information in health care. Respondents reported collecting language, race, ethnicity, and culture inconsistently, reflecting broader uncertainty regarding how these constructs should be defined and operationalized. Although these variables are frequently grouped together within health equity initiatives, they capture different dimensions of identity and experience. Language may identify communication needs, ethnicity may provide insight into shared heritage and traditions, race reflects socially constructed systems of power and stratification, and culture encompasses beliefs, practices, and worldviews that influence healthcare interactions.

The near absence of targeted training emerged as a critical finding. Respondents described discomfort and uncertainty in asking LCE-related questions, reflecting the inherently relational nature of these data. Unlike biomedical variables, LCE data are shaped through interpersonal interactions influenced by power, trust, and historical experiences of discrimination (24). This lack of training may undermine both data quality and patient trust (25). Respondents raised concerns that poorly conducted data collection could cause harm, particularly for structurally marginalized communities. These concerns align with critiques of equity data initiatives that prioritize extraction over relationship building (10,23). Training must extend beyond technical instruction to include culturally safe communication, ethical reflexivity, and clarity on how data will be used to benefit patients and communities.

The narrow use of LCE data, primarily to identify interpreter needs, further underscores the gap between aspiration and practice. While interpreter access is essential, LCE data were rarely used to inform quality improvement or population health analysis. This limited application reinforces perceptions that data collection is symbolic rather than substantive.

Respondents’ emphasis on conditional value is particularly notable. Many indicated that collecting LCE data without clear use cases or the ability to act on findings is not only ineffective but potentially harmful (24). This reflects growing recognition that equity data initiatives can become performative if not embedded within accountability and improvement structures (26). Data that are collected but not used risk eroding trust among both staff and families, particularly in contexts shaped by historical misuse of information.

Participatory approaches emerged as a critical strategy for strengthening LCE data practices. While administrative data linkages can offer efficiency and population-level insights, they are limited in capturing lived experience, language preferences, and culturally meaningful identities (27). In contrast, participatory approaches enable contextual adaptation, transparency, and shared ownership (28), and are particularly important for Indigenous identity data, where principles of data sovereignty, governance, and self-determination must guide all stages of data collection and use (29).

Engaging interest-holders in co-design is both an ethical and practical imperative. Co-designed processes are more likely to be trusted, consistently implemented, and meaningfully used (26). They also create opportunities to clarify purpose, address concerns about misuse, and align data practices with the priorities of communities most affected by inequities (28).

Taken together, these findings suggest several implications. Health systems should move beyond focusing on the presence of LCE data fields to investing in the processes that make data collection effective, including standardized protocols, training, clear mandates, and feedback mechanisms. Training should be prioritized as a core component, recognizing the relational and ethical dimensions. Finally, participatory co-design with relevant interest-holders should be central to future efforts. Future research should explore how families and communities experience LCE data collection, including how trust is built or undermined. Longitudinal and implementation-focused studies are also needed to examine how improved processes influence data quality, use, and health equity outcomes.

Limitations

First, although recruitment was purposive, participation was voluntary and self-selected, and therefore the final sample may be more accurately characterized as a convenience sample of individuals with an interest in or awareness of health equity and sociodemographic data collection.

Second, although geographic representation was broad, respondent distribution was uneven across provinces, settings, and roles. Alberta, Ontario, and Quebec were more heavily represented, and physicians comprised the majority of respondents. Third, the study was designed to describe practices and perceptions rather than verify them. Although participants were encouraged to have knowledge of institutional LCE data practices, eligibility was self-identified and not formally verified. Responses likely reflect a range of familiarity, from detailed operational knowledge to partial awareness. The study did not include direct review of institutional policies, EHR configurations, or data quality metrics, and reported practices may differ from formal procedures. Finally, findings reflect a single point in time. Given ongoing policy developments and evolving equity initiatives in several provinces practices may change over time.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that while there is widespread recognition of the importance of LCE data in pediatric healthcare, current practices fall short of enabling their effective and ethical use. Addressing this gap requires reframing LCE data collection as a participatory, relational, and process-driven endeavor rather than a technical task. By centring interest-holder engagement, investing in training, and embedding data collection within accountable systems of use, pediatric healthcare settings can move beyond symbolic data practices toward meaningful action on health equity.
