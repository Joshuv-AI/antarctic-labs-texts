Challenging behaviour by patients or close contacts, as experienced by healthcare workers in adult general and university hospital care: a scoping review protocol

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHealthcare professionals in general and academic hospitals increasingly face challenging behaviour from patients and their close contacts, particularly in acute care settings. Ranging from aggression to socially inappropriate actions, such behaviour is understood as interactional and context-dependent. Yet research largely focuses on verbal and physical aggression, leaving other forms and hospital context underexplored, despite concerns about staff safety, workforce sustainability and care quality.Methods and analysisThis scoping review aims to map literature on challenging behaviour in adult general and university hospital care (types of behaviour, prevalence, associated risk and protective factors, contextual influences, management and prevention strategies, definitions, conceptualisations of challenging behaviour and their implications for research, practice and policy). We will include adult patients and their close contacts (eg, family members, informal caregivers) (≥18 years) receiving somatic care in general or university hospitals. Psychiatric, forensic, prison, long-term care, rehabilitation and specialised geriatric settings will be excluded. The review focuses on behaviour within patient-provider or close contact-provider interactions. Peer-reviewed studies of any design and grey literature (eg, guidelines, policy documents, toolkits) published in English from 2010 onwards will be included. Searches will be conducted in MEDLINE, Embase, APA PsycINFO, CINAHL, Web of Science, Scopus and the Cochrane Library, supplemented by grey-literature searches in Google, Overton and relevant organisational websites. Data will be charted on study characteristics, types and prevalence of behaviour, associated factors, contextual features and management and prevention strategies and synthesised narratively and in tables.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required as the review uses publicly available sources. Although formal ethics review was not required, the authors considered the broader ethical dimensions of the work, including the potentially sensitive and stigmatising nature of the concept of challenging behaviour, the role of contextual and organisational factors and the responsible involvement of stakeholders and experts by experience in interpreting and contextualising the findings. Findings will be disseminated through open-access publication, conference presentations, stakeholder meetings, local/national practice and policy networks. Stakeholder collaboration supports safe, effective care, enhances staff safety and supports high-quality, patient-centred care and workforce sustainability.Trial registration numberDOI10.17605/OSF.IO/GA3EX.

Introduction

Acute hospital settings are increasingly under pressure due to rising patient acuity,1 workforce shortages and organisational demands.2 Also, hospital healthcare professionals are increasingly confronted with challenging behaviour such as workplace violence from patients and their close contacts.3 4 This issue receives considerable and growing attention due to its impact on staff well-being, job satisfaction and workforce retention.3 However, a broader spectrum of behaviours challenges healthcare delivery.5 6 These may include, but are not limited to confusion, controlling or demanding conduct, sexually inappropriate remarks and socially inappropriate behaviour. In patients, such behaviours can reflect cognitive impairment, fear, frustration or unmet needs.7 Disruptive interactions involving patients, close contacts and healthcare professionals can compromise care quality, safety and equitable access.3 4 Considering rising care demands, persistent workforce shortages and growing expectations for employers to safeguard staff, the need for effective strategies to recognise and manage such behaviours is increasingly urgent.3

The term ‘Challenging behaviour’ (CB) was originally introduced in the context of intellectual disability as a non-pathologising alternative to ‘problem behaviour’. It refers to culturally abnormal behaviour that threatens safety or significantly restricts access to community facilities.4

Earlier work largely conceptualised CB as a manifestation of individual pathology, including conditions such as dementia, delirium, autism, substance withdrawal and traumatic brain injury.812 CB is described to be particularly common in healthcare settings characterised by clinical vulnerability, stress, trauma or limited staff support.8 More recent perspectives understand CB as arising through interactions between patients, close contacts, healthcare professionals and the care environment.1317 This evolution reflects a shift towards viewing CB as a transdiagnostic phenomenon influenced not only by individual determinants, but also by contextual determinants, including environmental stressors, care practices and resource limitations. This interactional perspective shifts attention towards potentially modifiable organisational and contextual influences.

However, important uncertainties remain. Studies use varying definitions, labels and thresholds for what constitutes CB; differ in the contextual factors they consider; and report a wide range of consequences for patients, close contacts, healthcare professionals, teams and organisations. Research has predominantly focused on community,13 educational,18 forensic14 and psychiatric settings, whereas adult general hospital care remains comparatively underexplored. Acute hospital settings may be particularly relevant because of high patient acuity, rapid turnover, somatic complexity, multidisciplinary involvement and organisational pressure.19 Mapping these conceptualisations, contextual factors and reported consequences is therefore necessary to clarify the scope of the field.

Despite growing recognition of the broad and context-dependent nature of CB, most studies and reviews continue to focus narrowly on verbal and physical aggression,16 leaving other forms of challenging behaviour under-represented. Consequently, insight into non-aggressive CB and its contextual determinants remains limited. Reported prevalence varies widely due to inconsistent terminology and heterogeneous study designs.20 21 Although risk factors and interventions—such as de-escalation training and environmental modifications—have been identified, the evidence remains fragmented and strongly context-specific.22 23

The immediate impetus for this scoping review arose from two hospital-wide improvement programmes on challenging behaviour at Rijnstate Hospital and Radboud University Medical Center. These programmes highlighted the need for a more comprehensive and hospital-specific evidence base on challenging behaviour to guide practice and policy.

In acute hospital settings, important knowledge gaps remain regarding the identification, frequency, affected populations and management of challenging behaviours beyond aggression. Uncertainties also persist about how CB is conceptualised in these settings—particularly which behaviours are included, how contextual factors are considered and which outcomes are emphasised. Given the urgency of the issue and growing public and political concern about staff safety and service disruption, there is a need to consolidate the evidence base. This scoping review therefore aims to map how challenging behaviour by hospitalised adult patients and their close contacts is defined, described and addressed in the literature, including reported behaviours, contributing factors and proposed interventions.

Aim and objectives

Aim

This scoping review aims to map existing evidence on the types, prevalence, associated factors, contexts and management and prevention strategies of challenging behaviour in adult general and university hospital care. In addition, it aims to clarify how challenging behaviour is defined, labelled and operationalised across the literature, particularly in relation to aggression and violence.

Objectives

To identify and categorise types of challenging behaviour reported in adult general and university hospital care, including population/stakeholder, reported prevalence and associated risk and protective factors.

Methodology and methods

Design

A scoping review design was selected because the literature on challenging behaviour in adult general hospital care is conceptually and terminologically fragmented. Existing reviews predominantly address aggression and workplace violence, while less is known about the broader range of behaviours, populations, contexts, associated factors and organisational responses included under or adjacent to the concept of challenging behaviour. A scoping approach is therefore appropriate for systematically mapping the conceptual breadth and characteristics of the available literature, clarifying how challenging behaviour is defined, labelled and operationalised and identifying gaps in the evidence base. This mapping may provide a foundation for subsequent explanatory, intervention-focused or evaluative reviews.24

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. First, no formal quality appraisal will be conducted. The review will therefore map the range and nature of the available literature, but will not assess the methodological robustness of individual studies or the strength of evidence for specific interventions or outcomes. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as a descriptive mapping of the available literature rather than as evidence of effectiveness or causal relationships. Second, the transferability of the findings may be limited for populations, settings or healthcare systems that are under-represented or not represented in the included sources. This is particularly relevant because terminology, manifestations and organisational responses to challenging behaviour may vary across countries, languages and healthcare systems. Nevertheless, the review may identify knowledge gaps and clarify current approaches, thereby informing subsequent research, clinical practice and organisational policy.

Review registration

The review protocol was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) on 23 August 2024 (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/GA3EX). Ethical approval was not required as the review is based solely on the analysis of publicly available secondary data. During the development, several clarifications and refinements were made to the originally registered protocol, including more detailed eligibility criteria, the inclusion of grey literature, the specification of hospital settings and the incorporation of stakeholder consultation in line with recent JBI guidance. A summary of these changes is presented in online supplemental file 1. The core aim and methodological framework of the review remain consistent with the original registration.

The protocol was registered on the OSF to provide a time-stamped public record of the planned methods and any subsequent amendments. Journal publication provides a more detailed, peer-reviewed, citable and widely accessible account of the review rationale and methods, enabling methodological scrutiny and increasing awareness of the planned review.

Review team

The review team consists of a multidisciplinary group of clinicians and academics, including a professor and head of department in academic psychiatry (IT), a psychiatrist and medical manager of an acute and intensive care division and medical lead of a hospital-wide CB programme (MVS), and a psychiatrist and care programme lead for hospital and consultation-liaison psychiatry (PMK), as well as a biomedical information specialist (WMB) and a research student (LB). Joris van de Klundert (JvdK), professor of Health Services Management and Organisation, served as an advisor in the development of this protocol.

PMK conceived the review together with MVS, JvdK and IT, drafted the protocol and will lead the conduct of the scoping review. LB contributed to refining the research questions and methods and will support study selection and data charting. WMB designed and refined the search strategies and will run and document all database searches. IT and MVS provided methodological and clinical input throughout and will supervise the review and interpretation of the findings. All authors critically revised the manuscript and approved the final version. PMK is guarantor.

Patient and public involvement

A professional expert-by-experience (people with lived experience as patients or family members), employed at Rijnstate Hospital, contributed to the initial framing of this review as part of the hospital’s Challenging Behaviour Programme. The protocol was then developed collaboratively across academic and clinical institutions.

In line with recent JBI guidance on co-creation through consultation,29 structured input will be embedded in the action research component of the CB programme. This includes multidisciplinary stakeholder teams from five hospital units (emergency department, internal medicine, neurology, psychiatry and gastroenterology), comprising hospital managers, nurses, medical specialists, resident physicians and policy officers.

These teams will participate in two focus group sessions at key stages of the review process. The first focus group session will refine our review questions to ensure that our research questions are closely aligned with practical needs. A second focus group session will check our preliminary interpretation of findings, and identification of implications for clinical and organisational practice.

During and after the review, we will also consult multidisciplinary clinical teams to discuss the relevance, feasibility and applicability of the results in routine hospital practice. The results of the review will be disseminated to these stakeholders in accessible formats.

Information sources

To ensure comprehensive coverage of the relevant literature, a structured search will be conducted across the electronic bibliographic databases: MEDLINE ALL (via Ovid), Embase (via Elsevier), APA PsycINFO (via Ovid), CINAHL (via EBSCO), Web of Science, the Cochrane Library (via Wiley) and Scopus. The search will include publications from 2010 to present and will be limited to studies published in English. This pragmatic cut-off was chosen to capture approximately the last 15 years of contemporary hospital practice and policy at the time the review was designed. As the review aims to map current terminology, practice-based guidance and organisational responses to challenging behaviour, rather than to provide a historical account of the concept, this period was considered appropriate. The time frame also supports feasibility and reproducibility given the breadth of terminology, settings, evidence types and grey literature covered by the review.

In addition to peer-reviewed literature, we will consult grey literature to capture practice-based evidence, policy documents and relevant institutional reports. This includes targeted searches in Google and Overton. This approach allows us to incorporate non-indexed but potentially influential sources of evidence. A manual reference list screening of included articles and of relevant systematic reviews will be conducted to identify additional studies.

Search strategy

The review team’s biomedical information specialist (WMB) has developed an initial search strategy in Embase in close collaboration with the rest of the team; this strategy will be used as the basis for the other databases (online supplemental file 2). Draft strategies will be iteratively piloted, discussed and revised until they are judged to achieve an appropriate balance between sensitivity and specificity.

Database-specific filters will be applied, where appropriate, to exclude non-relevant populations, in line with the eligibility criteria summarised in table 1.

Grey literature search procedure

We will use targeted searches in Google, selected organisational websites and Overton (see online supplemental file 2 for detailed search strings). For each predefined search string in Google, we will screen records in order of relevance and will stop screening for that search string when a block of 50 consecutive records yields no new eligible documents; at that point, we will consider further screening for that string unlikely to identify additional eligible sources and will not extend screening to subsequent pages.

In Overton, we will iteratively refine search strings based on key terms used in the database searches and in initially identified policy documents, and we will screen the resulting records in order of relevance using the same eligibility criteria as for the database searches.

For each included grey-literature source, we will record the organisation, year of publication or last update, document type and geographical scope and we will summarise the key content in a separate table.

Timing of the search

The searches were developed in July 2024 and will be updated shortly before submission.

Study selection

All records retrieved from the bibliographic databases will be exported to EndNote. Records will first be deduplicated using the method described by Bramer et al.31 The deduplicated EndNote library will then be used for the pilot title/abstract screening (see below). After completion of the pilot and any necessary refinements of the eligibility criteria and decision rules, the deduplicated set of records will be imported into Covidence for full screening. Grey-literature records (eg, documents identified via Google, Overton and organisational websites) will be documented in a separate log and, where possible, also imported into Covidence. Where direct import is not feasible, grey-literature sources will be screened manually, following the same eligibility criteria and decision rules as for the database records.

Title and abstract (or equivalent) screening

Two reviewers (PMK and LB) will independently screen titles and abstracts (or equivalent summaries for grey-literature sources) against the predefined eligibility criteria. To ensure consistency, a pilot screening of 100 records will be conducted in EndNote using the deduplicated library. If an inter-rater agreement of ≥80% is achieved, the reviewers will proceed with the full screening in Covidence. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion and, if needed, consultation with the broader research team. Disagreements will first be discussed between PMK and LB using the predefined eligibility criteria. If consensus cannot be reached, a third senior reviewer (MVS or IT) will adjudicate. Reasons for exclusion at full-text stage will be recorded in Covidence and reported in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.

Full-text screening

All records deemed potentially eligible will be retrieved in full text and assessed for inclusion by the same two reviewers (PMK and LB). At this stage, exclusions will be documented with reasons. This procedure will be applied to both peer-reviewed articles and grey-literature documents. The full screening and selection process will be presented using a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram, detailing the number of records identified, screened, included and excluded separately for database and grey-literature sources.

Conceptual framing and scope considerations

To avoid duplicating existing systematic reviews and to maintain the broader focus of this scoping review, primary studies exclusively addressing aggression or violence will not be routinely included unless they explicitly use or examine the broader construct of challenging behaviour or address aggression/violence alongside other forms of challenging behaviour.

Data charting process

We have developed a preliminary data-charting form (online supplemental file 3) based on the objectives of the review, the JBI guidance and initial familiarity with the literature. Data will be charted on author, year, country, title, type of study/source of evidence, number of studies included (for reviews), type of challenging behaviour, context, population/stakeholder, prevalence, risk and protective factors, management strategies, prevention strategies, definitions of aggression and violence where relevant and quantification of challenging behaviour. The form will be piloted independently by at least two reviewers on a purposive sample of approximately 10 included studies representing distinctive designs and settings. We will independently pilot the form for grey literature. The review team will then meet to discuss the clarity, relevance and completeness of the forms and will make iterative refinements as needed. Any substantive changes to the data-charting forms after the pilot phase will be documented.

During piloting, clarity will be assessed by whether reviewers interpret each field consistently; relevance by whether each field contributes directly to the review objectives; and completeness by whether the form captures the information needed to describe study characteristics, behaviour types, populations/stakeholders, context, prevalence, associated factors and management/prevention strategies. Fields that are unclear, redundant or insufficient will be revised before full data charting.

Data charting will be conducted primarily by one reviewer and checked by a second reviewer for accuracy and completeness. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer.

Methodological quality assessment

In line with JBI guidance for scoping reviews, we will not conduct a formal assessment of the methodological quality or risk of bias of the included studies, because our objective is to map the breadth and nature of the existing evidence rather than to produce pooled effect estimates or to support direct comparisons of intervention effects. We will, however, describe key study characteristics (such as design, sample size and setting) and will summarise any major limitations reported by the authors where relevant to the interpretation of the findings.

Synthesis of results

Charted data will first be organised according to the predefined data-charting categories, including definitions and labels of challenging behaviour, behaviour types, populations/stakeholders, care contexts, prevalence, associated factors and management or prevention strategies. We will then group related findings into descriptive themes and compare these themes across source types, populations/stakeholders and hospital contexts. Summary tables and figures will be used to structure the evidence, indicate the relative prominence of identified themes and highlight key patterns, gaps and implications for research, clinical practice and organisational policy.

Results will be presented in text, tables and figures. Text will be used to describe the main descriptive themes, patterns and gaps identified across the included literature. Tables will summarise study and source characteristics, including publication year, country, source type, population/stakeholder group, care context, behaviour type, definitions or labels used and reported management or prevention strategies. Figures may be used to visualise the distribution of literature across behaviour types, care contexts or source types and to present a conceptual overview of how challenging behaviour is defined, contextualised and addressed in adult hospital care. The final format will be adapted to the nature and volume of the included sources.

Data availability

All data generated by the review will be extracted from publicly available sources. Search strategies, screening flow and charted data will be reported in the article and online supplemental files. No individual participant data will be used.

Discussion

This scoping review protocol describes the planned methods for mapping literature on challenging behaviour by adult patients and close contacts in general and university hospital care. The review will examine how challenging behaviour is defined, labelled and operationalised and will map reported behaviour types, contexts, populations, associated factors and management or prevention strategies.

As this is a scoping review, no formal quality appraisal will be conducted. The review will therefore map the range and nature of the available literature, but will not assess the methodological robustness of individual studies or the strength of evidence for specific interventions or outcomes. In addition, the findings may have limited transferability to populations, settings or contexts not represented in the included sources. Nonetheless, by identifying key gaps and clarifying current approaches to challenging behaviour in adult hospital care, the review may inform future research, clinical practice and organisational policy. We therefore hope to contribute to staff well-being, job satisfaction and workforce retention.

Restricting inclusion to English-language publications may limit the comprehensiveness of the evidence map and may exclude relevant non-English policy, practice-based or empirical literature. This is particularly relevant for a concept such as challenging behaviour, where terminology and organisational responses may vary across countries and languages. In addition, the focus on high-income countries may limit transferability to low- and middle-income settings, where hospital resources, staffing models, safety infrastructures, legal frameworks and organisational responses to challenging behaviour may differ substantially.
