Medication-induced central sleep apnea: beyond the unification of concepts, seizing the opportunity to precision medicine
Authors: Sébastien Baillieul, Jean-Louis Pépin, Renaud Tamisier, Johan Verbraecken
Published: 2024

Central sleep apnea (CSA) in adults encompasses a group of sleep-related breathing disorders, categorized into six distinct entities in the third edition of the International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD-3) [1]: primary CSA, CSA with Cheyne-Stokes Breathing (CSB), CSA due to a medical disorder without CSB, CSA due to high altitude periodic breathing, treatment-emergent CSA, and CSA due to a medication or substance. The latest entity, medication-induced CSA, should gain greater attention as CSA is a potentially serious and underrecognized adverse reaction of several medications, being associated with potential long-term adverse consequences through sleep fragmentation and intermittent hypoxia [2].

In this issue of SLEEP, Javaheri et al. [3] reviewed the evidence and the related mechanisms of medication-induced CSA. Importantly, the authors also raise awareness of the crucial importance of a careful retrieval of patients’ medication in every single case of CSA. Indeed, in the absence of a careful history, medication-induced CSA could be misclassified as primary CSA, with potential therapeutical implications. The list of medications that may induce CSA as a potentially serious and underrecognized adverse reaction includes opioids, baclofen, valproic acid, sodium oxybate, gabapentin, and ticagrelor [3].

One of the strengths of the article by Javaheri et al. [3] is to offer, based on a scrutinous review of preclinical and clinical evidence, an attempt to unify the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of medication-induced CSA. This approach is coherent with recent papers offering a refinement of the nosological classification of the ICSD-3 [1], taking into account the heterogeneity of CSA in terms of phenotypes, underlying etiology, treatment response, outcomes, as well as pathophysiological concepts [2, 4–6].

Among the medications inducing CSA, opioids are the most well-recognized and prevalent cause of medication-induced CSA. As reminded by the authors [3], medication-induced CSA represents a major public health issue in the context of what is described as “opioid epidemics”, with opioid use disorder affecting over 16 million individuals worldwide and causing one overdose death every 20 minutes [7]. Most opioid-related deaths occur during sleep due to opioid-induced respiratory failure [3, 8]. From a pathophysiological point of view, opiates and opioids are known depressants of the pontomedullary respiratory center, exerting a direct influence on neurons of the pre-Bötzinger complex, and the cranial and bulbospinal motor neurons of the diaphragm and the upper airway muscles [9, 10], predisposing to hypoventilation and upper airway obstruction [11]. Opioids also reduce breathing frequency and influence the rhythmogenesis in the pre-Bötzinger complex [12, 13]. Less known is their effect on central and peripheral chemoresponsiveness, as they dampen the hypercapnic ventilatory response and the loop gain, while methadone additionally increases the hypoxic response [3]. From the diagnosis, etiological, and pathophysiological perspectives, full polysomnography (PSG), the gold standard for CSA diagnosis, has its rightful place [5, 14]. In adults [1], the polysomnographic criteria defining CSA are ≥ 5 apneas or hypopneas per hour of sleep (AHI), with more than 50% being central events. PSG allows the mandatory identification of hypopneas of central origin, as they are of critical importance for avoiding misdiagnosis and underestimation of CSA prevalence [4, 5, 14]. PSG is also crucial for the precise description of the polysomnographic patterns of flow and respiratory effort, which may help identify the underlying and sometimes overlapping etiologies of CSA, a key step in CSA care. The most archetypical pattern, pathognomonic of chronic heart failure, is CSB. Another typical pattern easily identifiable on PSG is ataxic breathing, tied with opioid-induced CSA. Observable chaotic changes in amplitude and breathing frequency associated with sleep-related respiratory events of central origin result from opioid-induced alterations in breathing frequency and rhythmogenesis [12, 13].

The mean prevalence of CSA associated with chronic opioid use reached 24% [15], while for methadone, prescribed for pain management, CSA prevalence reached 30% [16]. Despite its frequency, CSA is not universally present in patients treated with opioids, nor follow a predictable dose-dependent phenomenon [17]. This raises the question of individual susceptibility to opioid-induced CSA, a question insufficiently emphasized by the authors in their review. Specific endophenotypic traits may explain the interindividual variability in medication-induced CSA occurrence, but this remains to be demonstrated. In the pathophysiological description of medication-induced CSA, the other overlooked parameter in the article is the impact of medications on sleep. The sleep-related consequences of medications are pleiotropic and are not restricted to CSA only. Moreover, the repercussions of medications on sleep may play a role in the pathophysiology of sleep-related respiratory disturbances, by modulating, for example, the respiratory arousal threshold [8].

Opioids are commonly prescribed for pain control, a condition associated with sleep disturbances [17, 18]. Both single doses and chronic administration of oral opioid medications can significantly affect sleep architecture and arousal threshold [17, 18]. Genetics may play a role in the susceptibility to individual sleep-related alterations to opioids as patients with the OPRM1 118-GG genotype showed an increase in sleep problems and worsening sleep patterns while taking opioids [19].

Besides opioids, CSA has also been reported under treatment with baclofen, valproic acid, and sodium oxybate, which all interfere with gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). It has been hypothesized that they may act on inspiratory neurons [2, 20–22]. Ticagrelor, an antiplatelet agent, by increasing ventilatory drive, may induce CSA both in naïve patients and CPAP-treated OSA patients [23, 24]. The two mechanisms underlying Ticagrelor-induced CSA are their direct inhibition of P2Y12 receptors in the central nervous system, and the stimulation of pulmonary vagal C-fibers, inducing hyperventilation [25–31].

Thus, the review by Javaheri et al. [3] offers, by deciphering the underlying mechanisms of medications-induced CSA, the identification of key medication-specific endotypic and phenotypic traits and their related neurological mechanisms. As highlighted by the authors, diagnosis of medication-induced CSA is of critical importance as it may lead to appropriate treatment, that relies on withdrawal of the medication when possible, and if not on positive airway pressure devices including continuous positive airway pressure or adaptive servo-ventilation.

From the perspective of precision medicine for CSA, future studies should undertake an endophenotypic approach, as developed in obstructive sleep apnea [32]. If the prevalence of CSA is far lower than the one of obstructive sleep apnea, the pathophysiological knowledge of CSA’s underlying mechanisms as well as the tools available to characterize the endophenotypic traits developed in OSA may ease the research process [33].

Beyond the identification of the underlying CSA putative etiology and pathophysiology, progress has to be made in the characterization of the prognostic value of CSA, especially in the case of medication-induced CSA. As reminded by Javaheri et al. in their review [3], CSA is a potentially serious and underrecognized adverse reaction of medications, supposed to be along with respiratory depression one of the key mechanisms of lethal consequences of opioids. Therefore, it is critical to recognize medication-induced CSA early, through better identification of endophenotypic predisposing traits, particularly because discontinuation of the offending agents has been shown to eliminate CSA and potentially the associated life-threatening risk. The identification of phenotypical traits of CSA associated with worse prognosis may help substantiate treatment indications and the development of potentially new therapeutic approaches, including invasive options.

Therefore, large systematic studies are needed and should be focused on the following key objectives: clarifying the prevalence of CSA with each medication; better depicting the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of medication-induced CSA, by integrating the multi-directional and integrated relationship of respiratory-specific phenotypic traits and sleep consequences of opioids; developing consensus on phenotypic traits of medication-induced CSA, including sleep disturbances, CSA occurrence and severity and prognosis as core outcome measures. This approach may lead to the identification of biomarkers, risk factors, and phenotypes for medication-induced CSA, and finally to more personalized and precision care.
