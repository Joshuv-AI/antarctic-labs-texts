Comment On “The Effect Of Modular Training On Self‐Management, Disease, And Treatment Adaptation In Individuals With Hypertension”
Authors: Anushka, Mousumi Saha, Subhasish Chatterjee
Published: 2026

To The Editor,

A recently published randomized controlled trial by Keşer et al. on “the effect of modular training on self‐management, disease, and treatment adaptation in individuals with hypertension” caught my attention. The authors address an essential component of patient education and self‐management in this study [1].

However, there are a few methodological and reporting issues with this study that must be discussed because they may have an impact on the validity, transparency and interpretation of the findings.

Firstly, it appears that the experiment was registered retrospectively after data collection was completed. This raises questions about selective outcome reporting and decreases transparency because the original study procedure and the predetermined outcomes cannot be confirmed. The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors strongly advises prospective trial registration in order to guarantee the legitimacy and transparency of research [2].

Secondly, without using objective clinical measures, the study only uses self‐reported questionnaire‐based outcomes (HSES, MMS, ACIS). This could introduce social desirability bias and cause the effectiveness of the intervention to be overestimated. The objective outcomes such as pill counts, pharmacy refill data, or computerized medication adherence monitoring can be included. All of these might enhance the internal validity of the findings and yield more trust worthy data [3].

Thirdly, participant dropouts and the handling of missing data are not clearly reported in the study. This makes it more difficult to determine whether every participant was included in the final analysis and this limits the evaluation of attrition bias. The reporting of transparent participant flow and missing data handling are essential to ensure transparency and validity of the study [4].

Fourthly, there is no CONSORT flow diagram in the study to show participant progression. It is unclear how many people were screened, excluded, and lost to follow‐up. This hinders the transparency and makes it challenging to evaluate the possible source of bias and completely comprehend the participant flow. A clear flow diagram would increase the reproducibility and clarity of the study [4].

Lastly, although randomization was mentioned, the allocation concealment method was not described. Group assignment must not be predictable without adequate allocation concealment, and this might lead to selection bias. Allocation method must be transparent in order to ensure the study's internal validity and reliability [5].

By resolving these problems, the study's overall credibility and scientific rigor will increase. We expect that these remarks will enhance methodological transparency and improve the understanding of the study findings.
