Impact of exercise-induced fatigue on the risk of stress fractures in the tibia during smash landing in female badminton players
Authors: Jing Ma, Binyong Ye, Bin Zhang, Zhanyang He, Gongju Liu, Chuimei Sheng, Houwei Zhu, GuanLiang Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the effects of exercise-induced fatigue on the risk of tibial stress fractures by comparing biomechanical parameters during backhand-side smash landings in elite female badminton players before and after fatigue. Thirteen elite female badminton players performed smash-landing trials before and after fatigue. The AnyBody Modeling System was used for inverse dynamics analysis, and finite element analysis was conducted using the AnyFE2Abq plugin to compute tibial stress and strain. After fatigue, the backhand rearcourt jump smash (BRJS) and the backhand lateral jump smash (BLJS) landing movements showed significantly increased peak tibial acceleration (p = 0.003; p = 0.008), vertical average loading rate (p = 0.024; p = 0.040), and vertical instantaneous loading rate (p = 0.007; p = 0.029. Moreover, the simulated tibialis anterior muscle force significantly decreased after fatigue (p = 0.004; p = 0.046). Finite element analysis further revealed markedly increased tibial stress and strain after fatigue, with peak stress and strain reaching multiple times the pre-fatigue values (peak stress: BRJS anterior medial, 224.0%; BRJS posterior, 213.2%; BLJS anterior medial, 348.6%; BLJS posterior, 522.8%; peak strain: BRJS, 241.1%; BLJS, 243.7%). Exercise-induced fatigue increased tibial acceleration and vertical loading rates during smash landings in elite female badminton players. Introduction

Stress fractures (SF) are insidious overuse injuries that are frequently difficult to diagnose in their early stages and are often identified only after symptoms worsen, typically requiring prolonged rehabilitation and incurring substantial medical costs1,2. In sports medicine, SF account for approximately 10% of all sports-related injuries and are particularly prevalent among athletes engaged in high-intensity repetitive activities1,3. Tibial stress fractures (TSF) are among the most common forms of SF and are generally regarded as fatigue-related rather than acute injuries4. According to the mechanical fatigue framework proposed by Edwards et al.5, repeated subthreshold mechanical loading induces the progressive accumulation of bone micro-damage; when damage exceeds the bone’s intrinsic repair capacity, microcracks propagate and may ultimately lead to structural failure and SF formation6. Notably, increases in strain magnitude and strain rate markedly reduce bone fatigue resistance, suggesting that larger and more rapidly applied loads may accelerate fatigue damage accumulation7.

However, most existing TSF research has focused on running8 and military training9, which are predominantly characterized by repetitive locomotor cycles, whereas badminton involves non-cyclical, high-demand actions that may impose distinct tibial loading patterns. Epidemiological evidence indicates a high burden of stress fractures in elite badminton, with the tibia commonly affected1,10. Among badminton-specific tasks, smash landings are considered particularly relevant because peak vertical ground reaction forces can reach approximately 3–5 times body weight and are accompanied by braking and lateral shear, producing complex three-dimensional loading of the tibia11–13. Two frequently performed techniques, the backhand rearcourt jump smash (BRJS) and the backhand lateral jump smash (BLJS), typically involve non-dominant-leg landings and pronounced movement asymmetry, including asymmetric upper–lower limb coordination and increased trunk lateral flexion and hip abduction14,15. Owing to these distinctive loading characteristics, BRJS and BLJS represent suitable tasks for investigating tibial mechanical responses in badminton-specific landings.

Sex differences are an important consideration in TSF risk. TSF and related stress injuries are reported more frequently in female athletes than in males, including observations of approximately two-fold higher rates of bilateral TSF in females and a similar disparity in collegiate athletics, as well as a markedly higher risk of skeletal stress reactions among female military recruits16–20. In addition, exercise-induced fatigue is widely recognized as a key modifiable risk factor for TSF. Biomechanically, fatigue may increase impact-related demands and reduce shock attenuation21,22; consistent with this, fatigue has been associated with higher vertical ground reaction force loading rates, greater dynamic loading, and progressively larger impact-related acceleration amplitudes during locomotor or landing tasks23–26. Despite this rationale, direct evidence for fatigue-related changes in tibial strain remains inconsistent, with studies reporting increases27,28, no change, or even decreases following fatigue29–31. These discrepancies likely reflect heterogeneity in fatigue protocols, task characteristics, and assessment approaches32–35, and may also be influenced by the difficulty of experimentally capturing spatially detailed tibial stress–strain patterns during dynamic sport movements27,29,31. Finite element (FE) modelling enables three-dimensional, region-specific estimation of tibial stress–strain under physiologically relevant loading, and because muscle forces substantially influence tibial loading pathways36, integrating musculoskeletal modelling with FE analysis may provide additional insight into fatigue-related, region-specific tibial mechanics during demanding landings.

Therefore, this study integrated kinematic and kinetic analyses with musculoskeletal modelling and FE analysis to quantify the effects of fatigue on landing biomechanics, ankle muscle forces, and tibial stress–strain responses during BRJS and BLJS. Given that fatigue has been linked to higher loading rates and reduced shock attenuation, and that muscle forces modulate tibial loading pathways21–26, we hypothesized that fatigue would increase impact-related landing metrics and, via altered ankle muscle force-generation patterns, lead to higher FE-estimated tibial stress and strain.

Research subjects and methods

Research subjects

An a priori power analysis was conducted using GPower (version 3.1; University of Düsseldorf, Germany) for a two-tailed paired-samples t-test. Assuming a large effect size (Cohen’s dz = 0.85) based on a previous GPower-based paired t-test sample size justification, with α = 0.05 and power = 0.80, the required sample size was 13 participants37. A total of 13 elite female badminton players from China were recruited (mean age: 21.20 ± 1.90 years; height: 167.10 ± 4.10 cm; weight: 57.30 ± 5.10 kg; BMI: 20.54 ± 1.57 kg/m²). Participants had no history of cardiovascular diseases in the past year, normal lower limb alignment, and no musculoskeletal injuries requiring medical intervention in the past six months. The Finite Element (FE) model, boundary conditions, and loads were based on a representative athlete (age: 20 years, height: 165 cm, weight: 53 kg), with characteristics that matched the average values of the study group. The experiment was conducted in the same venue and environmental conditions, and all participants used standardized equipment (same brand of rackets, clothing, and footwear). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Normal University (approval number: ZSRT2023171) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants signed written informed consent forms.

Testing methods and procedures

Kinematic and kinetic data collection

The experimental personnel performed three-dimensional spatial calibration on the infrared high-speed motion capture system (Motion Analysis Raptor-4, USA) equipped with eight cameras, and calibrated and optimized three Kistler 3D force plates (model 9287B, Switzerland, size 90 cm × 60 cm × 10 cm). Marker trajectories were sampled at 200 Hz, and ground reaction forces were sampled at 1000 Hz. The data acquisition system synchronized the infrared motion capture system with the Kistler force plates to ensure synchronized collection of kinematic and kinetic data. The initial contact during landing was determined by identifying the first frame where the ground reaction force exceeded 10 N. The analysis range covered the entire period from initial ground contact to when the knee flexion reached its maximum value. Hip, knee, and ankle joint angles were calculated using a Cardan rotation sequence of X–Y–Z (flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, and internal/external rotation).

Research procedure.

Diagrams illustrating the BRJS and BLJS movements.

The fatigue induction protocol used in this study was based on the badminton-specific speed test by Madsen et al.39, with the addition of randomization in technical movements. Participants started from the center of the court and, based on screen instructions, quickly performed front-court net shots and high backcourt clears, then rapidly returned to the center of the court. Each set consisted of 15 repetitions, with 15 s of rest between sets (Fig. 1c). To ensure the participants’ movement speed, the fastest action speed frequency was set using a metronome prior to the fatigue induction. If participants failed to perform the movements according to the metronome rhythm, they were required to do five vertical jumps. If the average height of the five jumps did not exceed 70% of their maximum jump height, fatigue levels were assessed using the Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale and heart rate monitoring. Fatigue induction was considered successful when the participants did not consecutively exceed 70% of their maximum jump height in five vertical jumps, had an RPE score ≥ 17, and their heart rate exceeded 85% of their maximum heart rate40–42. After fatigue induction, participants performed BRJS and BLJS movement tests (Fig. 1d). The validity of the trials was assessed by two badminton referees and two coaches based on criteria directly related to task and data validity, including correct execution of the intended movement (BRJS or BLJS), proper contact of the landing foot with the force plates, and completion of the movement without obvious loss of balance, slipping, or additional support steps.

AnyBody model construction

This study constructed a personalized three-dimensional lower-limb musculoskeletal model based on the Lower Extremity model in the AnyBody Modeling System (v7.4; AnyBody Technology A/S, Denmark) from the AMMR model repository. Kinematic data in C3D format were imported to drive the musculoskeletal inverse-dynamics analysis, together with synchronized three-dimensional GRF, center of pressure, and ground reaction moments measured by the force platform. Through code editing, muscles and bones irrelevant to the present landing task were removed; the model retained the trunk and lower limbs while the upper extremities were excluded. The model was scaled to each participant using body height, body mass, and skeletal geometry. Each bone contained mass properties, joint centers, and muscle insertion points, and scaling was applied to mass properties and node positions43.

These scaling assumptions reflect that muscle force is proportional to cross-sectional area, mass is proportional to volume, and bones are uniformly scaled in all directions. Muscle–tendon dynamics were represented using the classic three-element Hill-type muscle model44, which is widely used in inverse-dynamics-based musculoskeletal simulations and provides stable and physiologically reasonable muscle force estimates for dynamic tasks. This formulation is appropriate for the present study, as the focus was on estimating muscle force contributions to tibial loading rather than on detailed muscle contraction mechanics.

Inverse dynamics was then performed to compute net joint moments and joint reaction forces of the lower extremity. Muscle forces were subsequently estimated by resolving the required joint moments using AnyBody’s built-in muscle recruitment optimization under dynamic equilibrium and physiological constraints as defined in Eq. (2). The required joint moments were obtained from the inverse-dynamics solution based on the measured kinematics and external loads rather than being prescribed as additional moment constraints at a specific joint. Joint moments considered for muscle force estimation followed the default degrees of freedom of the model, including hip flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, and internal/external rotation moments; knee flexion/extension moment; and ankle plantarflexion/dorsiflexion and inversion/eversion moments. The simulated forces of the gastrocnemius medialis, gastrocnemius lateralis, soleus, and tibialis anterior were extracted for statistical analysis.

Tibia model construction

To enable subject-specific finite element modeling while limiting the need for multiple CT scans and minimizing radiation exposure, a representative athlete was selected for tibial CT-based model construction and FE simulation, based on loading-related characteristics that were close to the group mean. Sasimontonkul et al.45 pointed out that the external load on the distal tibia comes from the peak vertical ground reaction force (VGRF), while the internal load comes from the plantar flexors, particularly the soleus muscle force (SMF). Therefore, in this study, the PVGRF and SMF of all test subjects were normalized and used only as selection criteria to identify a representative athlete whose loading-related characteristics were closest to the group mean. Finite element analysis was subsequently performed using the complete, subject-specific AnyBody inverse-dynamics solution exported to Abaqus via AnyFE2Abq (Fig. 3). Importantly, PVGRF and SMF were used only as criteria for selecting the representative participant and were not used as the sole inputs to the FE model. The FE loading conditions were derived from the complete AnyBody inverse-dynamics solution and exported via AnyFE2Abq, rather than being limited to a single muscle force.

The bilateral tibias of the representative athlete were scanned using spiral CT (SOMATOM Force, Germany) to obtain geometric data of the tibia. During the scan, the participant lay supine with both legs naturally relaxed, ensuring that the tibia was centered in the scanning field of view and remained still. The scan layer thickness was 5 mm, generating a total of 717 slices, which were saved in DICOM format. The DICOM data were imported into Mimics 22.0 (Materialise NV, Belgium) for processing, where the lower limbs were segmented using the dynamic region growing algorithm based on tissue grayscale thresholds to extract the tibial portion. A 3D model of the tibia was constructed and saved as an .stl file format (Fig. 1e). Since the .stl file output from Mimics software only contains geometric information, it was reverse-engineered in Geomagic Studio 2013 (Geomagic Incorporated, USA) and converted into a solid model that can be recognized by CAD/CAE software. The coordinate axes were adjusted in Geomagic Studio to ensure the tibia model aligned with the skeletal model exported from AnyBody. Finally, continuous scaling was applied to ensure a perfect match of the tibia model in AnyBody software, followed by surface treatment to generate a defect-free solid model (Fig. 1f).

Finite element analysis

In Abaqus software, the material parameters for the tibia model were defined under a linear-elastic assumption. Although bone exhibits heterogeneous and anisotropic behavior, a homogeneous isotropic material definition was adopted in the present study because the CT data were used for geometry reconstruction only and were not processed through the quantitative calibration and validation required for reliable HU-to-density-to-modulus mapping. In the absence of such calibration, CT-based heterogeneous assignment, including separate cortical–trabecular material definitions, could introduce substantial uncertainty; therefore, a single effective uniform modulus was used for the tibial FE simulations, and cortical and trabecular regions were not assigned different elastic properties. The Young’s modulus was set to 12 GPa, Poisson’s ratio to 0.3, and density to 1600 kg/m³46,47. Next, the tibia model was meshed using second-order tetrahedral elements to maintain the tibia’s anatomical features and irregular curves.

In finite element analysis, muscle loads have a significant impact on the simulation results. Therefore, the tibia model was imported into AnyBody and analyzed using the default AnyFE2Abq workflow. The FE loading inputs exported from the complete musculoskeletal solution consisted of three-dimensional intersegmental joint reaction forces and moments obtained from inverse dynamics, together with tibia-related muscle attachment forces available in the model, according to the default AnyFE2Abq mapping. Here, joint reaction force and joint reaction moment refer to the resultant intersegmental force and moment required to satisfy dynamic equilibrium of adjacent segments in inverse dynamics, and they are distinct from joint contact forces distributed across the articulating surfaces; therefore, the absolute magnitudes of the finite element simulation results may be affected. The AnyFE2Abq plugin exported the required loads and boundary conditions (Fig. 1g)43,48. Muscles that do not insert on the tibia were not applied as direct tibial attachment forces; however, their mechanical effects were inherently reflected in the exported joint reaction forces and moments, thereby supporting the objective of characterizing the three-dimensional stress/strain distribution of the tibia. Finally, the tibia model, loads, and boundary conditions were imported into Abaqus CAE 2020 for the final finite element analysis (Fig. 1h).

Boundary conditions followed the default AnyFE2Abq export. The distal tibial region was constrained in three dimensions to remove rigid-body motion, and no reaction forces or moments were applied at the distal end. The proximal tibial region was loaded using the exported knee joint reaction force and moment acting on the tibial segment, applied through the AnyFE2Abq-mapped reference point and coupling constraints to the proximal tibial surface. In the present implementation of the default AnyFE2Abq workflow, the FE mapping and boundary-condition export were performed for a single bone model; therefore, the tibia was modeled as the primary load-bearing structure and the exported knee joint reactions were applied to the tibia. The fibula was not included in the current FE model, which should be considered when interpreting the absolute magnitudes of stress/strain. No additional non-physiological constraints were imposed at the knee beyond those required for numerical stability. The FE analysis was performed over the landing phase, starting at initial contact and ending at peak knee flexion, with the exported loads applied as a time-varying history across this phase. Peak stress and peak strain were identified as the maximum values occurring at any time increment within the landing phase for subsequent analysis.

Statistical methods

This study compared pre-fatigue and post-fatigue outcomes using paired analyses and did not statistically compare the two technical movements, as the two movements are irreplaceable in competitive practice. Normality of each variable was assessed prior to hypothesis testing. For variables meeting the normality assumption, paired-sample t tests were used; otherwise, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied. All statistical tests were conducted at a significance level of α = 0.05. Given the small sample size, effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were reported to enhance interpretability, where an effect size of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 represents small, medium, and large effects, respectively. A formal multiple-comparison correction was not applied, given that each outcome variable was examined using a single, predefined paired comparison between pre- and post-fatigue conditions.

Research results

Model validation results

According to relevant literature, under standing conditions, the typical axial compressive load on the tibial plateau joint surface is approximately 700 N, which primarily acts on the area above the tibial plateau and the upper region of the epiphysis50. Based on this, this study set the finite element loads and boundary conditions, with a region 2.5 cm above the distal tibial ankle joint surface constrained by six degrees of freedom to simulate actual physiological restrictions. The finite element simulation results show that the peak stress in the tibial shaft is 48.234 MPa, which falls within the tibial stress reference range reported in existing literature (47.05–52.93 MPa), indicating that the tibial finite element model established in this study has good biomechanical validity and verification accuracy in terms of mechanical response51 (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Main findings

This study investigated fatigue related changes in landing biomechanics and tibial stress strain responses during BRJS and BLJS using an integrated musculoskeletal and FE framework. The main findings were that fatigue significantly increased impact related metrics, including PTA, VALR, and VILR, in both movements, whereas PVGRF and sagittal plane lower limb ROM showed no significant pre post differences. Musculoskeletal simulations indicated reduced tibialis anterior force after fatigue in both tasks, with an additional reduction in gastrocnemius lateralis force during BRJS. Importantly, FE analysis demonstrated markedly higher tibial stress and strain after fatigue, with stress strain concentrations primarily located in the anterior medial and posterior regions of the tibial shaft, particularly within the middle and distal thirds. Together, these findings suggest that fatigue is associated with a more demanding mechanical environment for the tibia during high demand badminton landings.

Biomechanical mechanisms

FE stress strain distribution

From the finite element analysis results, it is evident that the tibial stress-strain values during the execution of BRJS and BLJS in a fatigued state were significantly higher than before fatigue. Tensile stress was concentrated on the anterior medial tibia, while compressive stress was concentrated on the posterior tibia, with the compressive stress on the posterior side being higher than the tensile stress on the anterior side. This is consistent with previous research findings, suggesting that female badminton players are more likely to experience TSF on the posterior side of the tibia during BRJS and BLJS36.

According to the “mechanical loading theory”52, bone tissue can maintain dynamic equilibrium between absorption and remodeling within a strain range of 100–1500 µε. When the strain range increases to 1500–3000 µε, the bone enters a high remodeling state, and the risk of micro-crack formation rises. Beyond 3000 µε, the repair mechanisms are less effective, the accumulation rate of micro-damage increases, and the risk of TSF significantly increases. Previous in vitro fatigue studies and modeling predictions suggest that cortical bone subjected to cyclic loading enters a high-risk fatigue regime when strain magnitudes approach or exceed approximately 3000 µε27,53, whereas at strain levels up to 1500 µε, fatigue failure does not occur even after tens of millions of cycles54. In the present study, post fatigue peak tibial strain exceeded pre fatigue values, and in BRJS it approached or exceeded commonly cited high risk thresholds. This pattern suggests that fatigue may increase mechanical conditions associated with micro damage accumulation when these landings are repeatedly performed.

To better interpret the pronounced increase in peak tibial stress observed in the finite element analysis, we provide in the Supplementary Tables the input loads used for the simulations for the representative participant at the time point corresponding to peak stress under both tasks before and after fatigue (Tables S1–S4). For BRJS, the vertical joint reaction forces at the ankle and knee increased after fatigue, indicating a higher level of weight-bearing load transmitted along the tibia. Coronal-plane components also increased, accompanied by larger joint reaction moments and changes in the sign of selected components. Because the tibial shaft is a slender structure, peak principal stress and strain are typically dominated by bending; when lateral force components and moment components increase or reverse direction, the loading state is more likely to shift from predominantly axial compression to axial compression superimposed with stronger lateral bending, thereby amplifying tensile strain and stress on one cortical surface. In addition, muscle forces were redistributed at the peak-stress time point, with reduced contributions from posterior thigh muscles and increased contributions from posterior lower-leg muscles. This redistribution may alter the composition of joint reaction force components and increase tibial net bending and shear coupling, providing a mechanical explanation for the marked rise in peak stress during BRJS.

For BLJS, vertical joint reaction forces similarly increased after fatigue, while coronal-plane components increased and sagittal-plane shear components became larger, suggesting that the loading state at peak stress shifted from simple weight bearing toward a stronger combination of compression, lateral bending, and anteroposterior shear. Moreover, the patellofemoral joint reaction force increased substantially, indicating an enhanced effective loading associated with the knee extensor chain, which may further exacerbate local shear conditions in the proximal tibia. The muscle inputs were consistent with this pattern, with greater reliance on the plantarflexor group for weight support and deceleration and increased lateral stabilizer activity for frontal-plane control, together providing load-input evidence for the more pronounced posterior stress concentration and the amplified peak strain observed after fatigue.

It should be noted that the loads reported in Tables S1–S4 are internal inputs for the representative participant at the peak-stress time point and include muscle and inertial effects; therefore, they are not directly comparable with the group-mean outcome measures reported below, and differences in magnitude between these two levels of analysis are not contradictory.

External force mechanisms

External forces mainly come from body weight and GRF, which represent the external loads experienced by the athlete. TSF has been linked to both the magnitude and cumulative exposure of these loads, which may be reflected by body weight, PVGRF, VALR, VILR, or combinations of these variables16. Body weight acts on the proximal tibia, generating compressive forces and anterior shear forces, whereas GRF applies compressive and posterior shear forces at the distal tibia. In the present study, PVGRF did not differ significantly between pre-fatigue and post-fatigue conditions during BRJS or BLJS, which is consistent with the meta-analysis by Zadpoor et al.55 on the effects of lower-limb muscle fatigue on GRF. However, Madigan et al. reported a decrease in PVGRF after fatigue during a drop-jump task56, whereas other studies have suggested increases in PVGRF with neuromuscular fatigue57. Such discrepancies may be related to participant characteristics, task execution, fatigue protocols, and the degree of fatigue. In predictable tasks, pre-planned stabilization strategies may allow sufficient time for muscle recruitment, which could contribute to unchanged PVGRF despite fatigue58. This mechanism may explain why there was no significant change in PVGRF in this study, and may also relate to the lack of significant differences in lower limb joint ROM before and after fatigue. Although PVGRF was unchanged, PVGRF during running and jumping can exceed several times body weight during landing59, and the resulting posterior shear from GRF may dominate the anterior shear from body weight, contributing to a posterior bending moment at the tibia. Importantly, both VALR and VILR were higher after fatigue, consistent with Nicol et al.60. VILR reflects the maximum rate of GRF increase in early landing, whereas VALR represents the average rate of load increase from initial contact to the peak. Concurrent increases in both metrics indicate a faster rise of vertical loading within a shorter time window. When loading rate is high, muscles and soft tissues may have less time to buffer and absorb energy, potentially increasing the proportion of load transmitted to weight-bearing bones such as the tibia16. Because TSF is a fatigue-related injury and loading rate is a key factor influencing bone fatigue resistance, higher loading rates may increase susceptibility to fatigue-related micro-damage when such landings are repeated16. However, loading rate was not modeled as a rate-dependent material or inertial effect in the present FE simulations; accordingly, VALR and VILR were interpreted as observational metrics of impact-rise characteristics rather than explicit inputs to the finite element model.

PTA is an indirect indicator of tibial impact exposure and provides an intuitive estimate of tibial loading intensity16. The vertical component of PTA has been used to distinguish TSF cases and to predict TSF with reasonable accuracy16. In the present study, PTA increased after fatigue. Although the magnitude of increase was small, repetitive landings may amplify the cumulative effect of impact exposure. Prior work has reported 1.361 fold higher odds of TSF per 1 g increase in PTA16. Here, PTA is interpreted as an observational indicator of impact exposure rather than a direct determinant of the FE input loads.

Internal force mechanisms

Internal forces, particularly muscle forces, play a crucial role in shaping tibial loading. Previous studies have indicated that plantar flexor and dorsiflexor muscles, as antagonistic groups, can coordinate their contractions to modulate tibial bending by converting non-axial bending components into more axial loading, thereby reducing shear stress, enhancing structural stability, and providing a degree of mechanical protection61. From a functional perspective, the triceps surae contributes to a posterior bending moment at the tibia, whereas the tibialis anterior resists posterior bending. Because plantar flexor muscles must support body weight during landing, their force output is typically greater than that of the dorsiflexors, resulting in a larger posterior bending demand.

The musculoskeletal simulation results of the present study showed that tibialis anterior force decreased significantly under fatigue during both BRJS and BLJS, whereas gastrocnemius lateralis force decreased after fatigue only during BRJS. Mizrahi et al.62 similarly observed that the integrated electromyographic activity of the tibialis anterior decreased after prolonged exercise, while gastrocnemius activity remained relatively unchanged, suggesting differences in fatigue resistance between anterior and posterior muscle groups. Such asymmetry may further increase the imbalance between anterior and posterior muscle forces under fatigue. As a result, a greater proportion of bending demand that would normally be modulated by active muscular control may be borne by the tibial structure, potentially increasing bending-related stress and strain. Importantly, tibial stress/strain reflects the net bending moment arising from GRF characteristics, joint posture (moment arms), and force distribution across multiple muscles, rather than any single muscle force alone. Although EMG was not collected and a muscle-driven forward-dynamics model was not used, fatigue-related effects may still be reflected indirectly in our inverse-dynamics simulations because post-fatigue kinematics and ground reaction forces alter joint moment demands and moment arms. Consequently, the estimated muscle forces may shift accordingly to satisfy these post-fatigue mechanical demands.

In summary, the fatigue-related increases in tibial stress and strain observed in the FE analysis appear to reflect combined changes in external impact characteristics and internal force sharing during landing (Fig. 8). Although PVGRF and sagittal-plane lower-limb ROM did not differ significantly between conditions, VALR and VILR increased after fatigue, indicating a faster rise of vertical impact loading. PTA also increased, suggesting greater impact exposure at the tibia; here, PTA is interpreted as an observational indicator of impact transmission rather than a direct determinant of the FE input loads. In parallel, fatigue altered ankle muscle force distribution, and the relative reduction in dorsiflexor force may diminish the capacity to modulate tibial bending demands. Thus, the coexistence of higher-rate external loading and altered muscle force sharing may shift tibial loading toward a less favorable mechanical environment. It should also be noted that the biomechanical variables reported in this study, such as GRF- and muscle-related measures, represent group-averaged outcomes across participants. In contrast, the finite element analysis was conducted using a representative individual-specific tibial model, with both the boundary conditions and loading derived from the inverse-dynamics results of the same individual. As a result, the relationship between changes in group-averaged biomechanical metrics and the magnitude of peak stress predicted by the finite element model may not be strictly proportional. Individual-specific loading patterns and anatomical characteristics may therefore contribute to an amplified stress response that is not fully reflected by group-level mean values.

Practical implications

The findings suggest that fatigue management and maintenance of landing control may be important for reducing high rate impact exposure during badminton smash landings. Training strategies emphasizing shock attenuation under fatigued conditions may help limit increases in VALR, VILR, and PTA. Conditioning programs targeting dorsiflexor endurance and neuromuscular coordination may also be relevant, given the observed reduction in tibialis anterior force. Monitoring impact related indicators during training may help identify periods of elevated mechanical exposure and inform training load management.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample included only female athletes, which may limit generalizability to male athletes or other performance levels. A further limitation of this study is that the finite element modeling was based on a single representative participant in order to minimize CT-related radiation exposure, which limits the generalizability of absolute stress–strain estimates across individuals. In addition, while experimental biomechanical variables were analyzed at the group-averaged level, the finite element results reflect an individual-specific loading condition. This discrepancy may contribute to differences between group-level biomechanical trends and the magnitude of stress predicted by the finite element model. In addition, the tibia was modeled as a linear elastic, homogeneous, isotropic structure without differentiating cortical and trabecular bone properties, which may influence the absolute magnitudes of stress and strain. Finally, fatigue was induced acutely under laboratory conditions and may not fully represent cumulative or long-term fatigue during training or competition.

Future work

Future studies should examine whether fatigue related loading patterns generalize across larger and more diverse samples, including male athletes and different skill levels. Extending FE analyses to multiple subject specific geometries or alternative modeling approaches that reduce radiation exposure would strengthen generalizability. Incorporating more detailed bone material heterogeneity and subject specific muscle tendon properties, and where feasible combining modeling with neuromuscular measurements, may further refine understanding of fatigue related tibial loading mechanisms during badminton landings.

Conclusion

This study examined fatigue related changes in landing biomechanics and tibial stress and strain responses during BRJS and BLJS in elite female badminton players using an integrated musculoskeletal and finite element framework. Fatigue was associated with increased impact related loading characteristics, whereas peak vertical ground reaction force and sagittal plane lower limb joint range of motion showed no significant differences between conditions. Finite element analysis further demonstrated markedly elevated tibial stress and strain after fatigue, with stress and strain concentrations primarily located in the anterior medial and posterior regions of the tibial shaft.Overall, these findings indicate that fatigue is associated with a less favorable mechanical environment for the tibia during demanding badminton landing tasks and provide biomechanical insight into potential mechanisms underlying fatigue related tibial stress injury risk.
