GLP‐1‐Based Therapies in Type 1 Diabetes: Emerging Evidence on Cardiovascular, Renal, and Safety Outcomes
Authors: Anastasios Tentolouris, Djordje S. Popovic, Alexandros L. Liarakos, Alexandros Briasoulis, Nikolaos Papanas, Theocharis Koufakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIndividuals living with type 1 diabetes (T1D) are at increased risk for cardiovascular (CV) and renal complications, yet therapeutic strategies specifically targeting cardiorenal risk reduction in this population remain limited. The role of glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1)‐based therapies in T1D remains unclear.MethodsWe conducted a review of published studies examining the CV, clinical, and renal effects of GLP‐1‐based therapies in people with T1D, while also evaluating safety outcomes and future research priorities.ResultsThree observational real‐world studies evaluating GLP‐1‐based therapies in adults with T1D were identified. Overall, these studies demonstrated clinically meaningful reductions in all‐cause mortality, hospitalisation, heart failure, and composite CV outcomes. Renal benefits were also observed, including lower risks of early estimated glomerular filtration rate decline and end‐stage kidney disease. Importantly, no consistent increase in diabetic ketoacidosis or severe hypoglycemia was identified, supporting a generally reassuring safety profile. These findings challenge the traditional view of GLP‐1 receptor agonists as therapies of predominantly glycemic relevance in T1D and support their potential role in cardiorenal risk reduction. Nevertheless, the currently available evidence is entirely observational and warrants confirmation through prospective randomised studies specifically designed for T1D populations.ConclusionCurrent real‐world evidence suggests that GLP‐1‐based therapies in T1D may provide clinically meaningful CV and renal benefits with a reassuring safety profile; however, adequately powered randomised trials are needed to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Approximately 9.2 million individuals worldwide were living with type 1 diabetes (T1D) in 2024, according to estimates from the International Diabetes Federation [1]. Among them, 19.8% were younger than 20 years, 68.6% were between 20 and 59 years of age, and 11.8% were aged 60 years or older, highlighting that most people with T1D are adults [1].

Despite advances in insulin therapy, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), and automated insulin delivery systems (AID), adults with T1D continue to face a substantially elevated risk of cardiovascular (CV) and renal complications and have a markedly reduced life expectancy, estimated to be approximately 13 years shorter than that of the general population [2, 3]. This burden is amplified by the growing prevalence of obesity in T1D, which has emerged as an important and evolving clinical challenge [4, 5]. The prevalence of overweight and obesity in T1D is now comparable to that of the general population and reaching up to 60% in some adult cohorts [6, 7]. Excess adiposity in people living with T1D has been associated with greater difficulty achieving glycemic targets, as well as an increased risk of both microvascular and macrovascular complications [4, 8].

GLP‐1‐based therapies are extensively used in the management of type 2 diabetes (T2D) [9]. Large‐scale CV randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have demonstrated that these agents significantly reduce CV risk both in individuals with established CV disease and in those at elevated CV risk [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16], as well as risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression among those with T2D and established CKD [17]. In addition to their cardiorenal protective properties, GLP‐1‐based therapies exert a broad range of pleiotropic effects [18, 19, 20, 21].

Interest in GLP‐1 therapies use as adjunct insulin therapy in T1D has grown in recent years, with studies mainly focusing on their effects on glycemic and metabolic endpoints. Agents such as liraglutide [22, 23, 24, 25, 26], semaglutide [27, 28], and tirzepatide [29] have shown encouraging metabolic benefits in this population. However, despite these promising findings, data regarding the impact of GLP‐1‐based therapies on CV and renal outcomes in T1D remain limited.

Three recent large‐scale real‐world studies have substantially expanded current knowledge regarding the cardiorenal effects of GLP‐1‐based therapy in T1D [30, 31, 32]. Together, these studies included very large patient populations and used robust methodologies, including propensity score matching and target trial emulation, to reduce confounding and bias.

The present review aims to critically examine the available evidence on the CV and renal outcomes associated with GLP‐1‐based therapy in T1D, while also evaluating glycemic and safety outcomes, identifying current knowledge gaps, and outlining future directions for research and clinical practice.

Metabolic Effects of GLP‐1‐Based Therapies in T1D

GLP‐1‐based therapies exert a range of glycemic actions in adults with T1D, although these effects are generally modest and secondary to the metabolic and weight‐related benefits that have driven clinical interest in this population. The available evidence derives primarily from RCTs of liraglutide [22, 23, 24, 25, 26], with a growing body of data on semaglutide [27, 28] and tirzepatide [29].

Liraglutide has been most extensively studied as an adjunct to insulin in T1D [22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. The two largest RCTs—ADJUNCT ONE [n = 1398; 52 weeks, body mass index (BMI) ≥ 20 kg/m2] and ADJUNCT TWO (n = 835; 26 weeks, BMI ≥ 20 kg/m2)—demonstrated dose‐dependent but modest reductions in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) of approximately 0.3%–0.5% compared with placebo, accompanied by clinically meaningful weight loss of 4.9–5.1 kg and reductions in total daily insulin dose (TDD) of approximately 8%–10% at the highest evaluated dose of 1.8 mg/day [22, 23]. Consistent findings were reported in smaller RCTs, including the Lira‐1 and Lira Pump trials (BMI > 25 kg/m2), where liraglutide 1.8 mg reduced HbA1c by 0.41%–0.5% from baseline, improved time in range (TIR) by approximately 12 percentage points, and reduced bolus insulin requirements [24, 25]. Complementary evidence from a body composition RCT demonstrated that liraglutide significantly reduced fat mass by 3.4 kg, driven predominantly by visceral adipose tissue loss, without affecting lean mass [26]. Across these trials, glycemic improvement with liraglutide is consistent but limited in magnitude; the most reproducible benefits are reductions in body weight and insulin requirements rather than HbA1c per se.

Semaglutide has shown comparable or slightly better glycemic benefits in a smaller number of studies [27, 28]. In the ADJUST‐T1D RCT (n = 72; 26 weeks, BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2), once‐weekly semaglutide 1 mg significantly improved TIR by 8.8 percentage points and reduced HbA1c by 0.3% versus placebo, with a mean weight loss of 8.8 kg [27]. In a crossover RCT evaluating semaglutide as an adjunct to AID systems, TIR improved by 4.8 percentage points without increasing time below range, suggesting compatibility with closed‐loop insulin therapy [28]. Real‐world evidence further supports these findings: a 12‐month observational study in adults with overweight or obesity reported weight loss of approximately 13%, a modest HbA1c reduction of 0.4%, and a 13.6% reduction in TDD [33].

Tirzepatide, a dual GLP‐1/glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), has produced the most pronounced weight and insulin‐related effects reported to date in T1D. In a 12‐week phase 2 RCT (n = 24), tirzepatide 5 mg once weekly produced a mean weight loss of 10.3 kg (8.8% of body weight), reduced TDD by approximately 35%, and improved HbA1c by 0.4% compared with placebo [29]. Pooled data from six observational studies further confirmed these signals, reporting mean HbA1c reductions of 0.68%, weight reductions of 12.4 kg, TDD reductions of 25.9 units, and a 14.8 percentage point increase in TIR [34]. However, the evidence base for tirzepatide in T1D remains limited and is currently based predominantly on observational data, while the phase 3 SURPASS‐T1D‐1 trial is ongoing [35]. Main study outcomes include changes in HbA1c and body weight, alongside CGM‐derived efficacy metrics such as TIR, evaluated at week 40.

In summary, GLP‐1‐based therapy in adults with T1D is associated with modest but consistent improvements in HbA1c and TIR, alongside more pronounced and reproducible reductions in body weight and insulin requirements. Although these glycemic benefits are clinically meaningful, they are not the primary driver of interest in GLP‐1‐based therapy for T1D, which is increasingly focused on broader cardiometabolic risk reduction. Importantly, most clinical studies enrolled broadly defined populations spanning a wide BMI range, including individuals with normal BMI.

The earliest of these, published by Garg et al., was a retrospective cohort study that used the TriNetX United States database to compare 4212 adults with T1D receiving GLP‐1‐based therapy against 4212 propensity score‐matched controls not receiving such treatment [30]. After adjusting for demographics, BMI, HbA1c, and key comorbidities, GLP‐1 use was associated with a markedly lower hazard of all‐cause mortality [hazard ratio (HR) 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.18 (0.11–0.30), p < 0.0001]. This magnitude of effect was accompanied by significantly lower odds of all‐cause hospitalisation [odds ratio (OR) (95% CI): 0.30 (0.20–0.45), p < 0.0001] and emergency department visits [OR (95% CI): 0.56 (0.43–0.71), p < 0.0001]. Importantly, this study did not assess individual CV endpoints such as myocardial infarction, stroke, or heart failure, which limits the specificity of its CV conclusions [30].

Building upon these observations, Tentolouris et al. (2026) conducted a propensity score‐matched cohort analysis using the TriNetX Global Health Research Network, which includes data from approximately 146 million patients across 111 healthcare organisations worldwide [31]. The study enrolled 4088 adults with T1D and BMI ≥ 20 kg/m2 per group and applied a six‐month lag period before outcome accrual to minimise immortal‐time bias. The matching strategy was more extensive, encompassing cardiometabolic risk factors, concomitant CV medications, renal function parameters, and lipid profiles. In time‐to‐event analyses, GLP‐1‐based therapy was associated with a 33% lower hazard of all‐cause mortality [HR (95% CI): 0.67 (0.46–0.98), p = 0.038]. Of particular importance, the study demonstrated a substantial reduction in the risk of heart failure [HR (95% CI): 0.38 (0.21–0.67), p = 0.001], with events occurring in 0.4% of the GLP‐1 group compared with 1.6% of controls. The composite adapted‐MACE outcome—encompassing heart failure, myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, cardiac arrest, and coronary revascularisation—was also significantly reduced [HR (95% CI): 0.61 (0.40–0.94), p = 0.025], with heart failure accounting for most events in both groups. No significant reduction was observed for cerebral infarction [HR (95% CI): 0.89 (0.44–1.83), p = 0.755], while myocardial infarction events were too infrequent to permit reliable individual outcome analysis. The risk of all‐cause hospitalisation was also reduced [HR (95% CI): 0.70 (0.51–0.96), p = 0.025].

The TriNetX‐based studies have important limitations that should be acknowledged. Longitudinal data on key cardiometabolic variables, including HbA1c, BMI, lipid profile, and insulin dose, were not consistently available beyond baseline, limiting assessment of potential mediating mechanisms and more detailed characterisation of outcomes. In addition, the platform lacks granular information regarding treatment adherence, duration of exposure, insulin regimen, and treatment switching, introducing the possibility of exposure misclassification. Although propensity score matching achieved good balance across multiple clinically relevant covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as diabetes duration, disease severity, and lifestyle characteristics cannot be excluded. Furthermore, identification of T1D relied on ICD‐10 coding, raising the possibility of diagnostic misclassification. Additional limitations include the relatively low baseline CV risk profile of the study populations and relatively small numbers of certain CV events, particularly stroke and myocardial infarction, which may affect statistical power and limit generalizability to higher‐risk populations. Furthermore, as with all retrospective observational studies, causal inference cannot be established because of the potential for residual confounding, indication bias, and other inherent methodological limitations. Moreover, real‐world studies evaluating obesity‐related therapies are inherently vulnerable to “healthy user” and prescribing biases. In clinical practice, GLP‐1‐based therapies may be preferentially prescribed to individuals who are more motivated, more engaged in self‐management, and more adherent to lifestyle measures and concomitant treatments, while being used less frequently in patients perceived as frail, poorly compliant, or at higher risk for adverse events. Although propensity score methodologies can effectively balance measured baseline characteristics, they cannot fully adjust for these unmeasured behavioural, socioeconomic, and clinician‐driven prescribing factors. A further important consideration is that the two studies reviewed here (Garg et al. and Tentolouris et al.) were derived from the TriNetX platform, albeit from different networks (United States versus Global) and non‐identical cohorts. This partial overlap in data source limits the independence of the available evidence, and the apparent consistency of findings across studies should therefore be interpreted with corresponding caution.

The most methodologically rigorous evidence comes from Xu et al. (2026), who employed sequential target trial emulation using national electronic health record data from the Optum Labs Data Warehouse, encompassing 174 678 individuals with T1D who contributed 6 092 537 person‐trials across 135 sequentially emulated trials between January 2013 and March 2024 [32]. This design applies the principles of RCTs to observational data, effectively eliminating immortal‐time bias and enabling the evaluation of hard long‐term endpoints. Propensity score overlap weighting achieved excellent covariate balance across a broad array of clinical and laboratory variables. Initiation of GLP‐1‐based therapy was associated with a statistically significant 15% reduction in MACE—defined as a composite of acute myocardial infarction, stroke, and all‐cause mortality—compared with non‐initiation [HR (95% CI): 0.85 (0.77–0.95); five‐year risk 4.3% versus 5.0%, risk difference −0.7%]. Among individual MACE components, myocardial infarction was significantly reduced [HR (95% CI): 0.79 (0.66–0.95)], while reductions in stroke [HR (95% CI): 0.93 (0.78–1.10)] and all‐cause mortality [HR (95% CI): 0.84 (0.71–1.00)] did not individually reach statistical significance. Hospitalisation for heart failure was additionally reduced by 18% [HR (95% CI): 0.82 (0.71–0.94)], aligning with findings from the Tentolouris study [31]. Notably, use of GLP‐1‐based therapies was associated with a 28% lower risk of major adverse liver events [HR (95% CI): 0.72 (0.60–0.85)], which is of particular relevance given that hepatic steatosis is increasingly recognised among people with T1D. Patients initiating GLP‐1‐based therapy were also significantly more likely to achieve clinically meaningful weight loss thresholds of 5%, 10%, and 15% from baseline [32].

The strength of the reported associations varied considerably across studies, with several estimates accompanied by relatively wide confidence intervals. This heterogeneity likely reflects important differences in study methodology, including the use of propensity‐score matching versus sequential target‐trial emulation, as well as variation in the matching strategies and covariates selected for adjustment, sample size, baseline CV risk, follow‐up duration, event rates, and outcome definitions. These observations highlight the limitations of observational data and underscore the need for prospective RCTS.

Encouragingly, several ongoing clinical trials are currently evaluating the potential cardiometabolic effects of GLP‐1‐based therapies in individuals with T1D. The phase 2 RESET1 RCT is designed to enrol 60 adults with T1D, aged 25–70 years, with disease duration ≥ 2 years, living with overweight (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2), HbA1c ≥ 7%, and at least one CV risk factor. Participants are randomised to semaglutide up to 1.0 mg weekly or placebo for 26 weeks [36]. The primary endpoint is carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity, a marker of arterial stiffness, while secondary mechanistic evaluations include insulin sensitivity assessed by hyperinsulinemic‐euglycemic clamp and incretin/pancreatic hormone responses during a mixed‐meal tolerance test. Another ongoing trial, T1‐DISCO (Type 1 Diabetes Impacts of Semaglutide on Cardiovascular Outcomes), is examining the effects of semaglutide on vascular function, renal outcomes, and insulin sensitivity in adults with T1D [37]. Major vascular endpoints include changes in aortic pulse wave velocity as an indicator of central arterial stiffness, along with carotid‐femoral and carotid‐radial pulse wave velocity measurements reflecting peripheral arterial stiffness. Secondary endpoints include alterations in insulin requirements and renal vascular resistance.

Taken together, the available real‐world evidence indicates that GLP‐1‐based therapy in T1D is associated with consistent and clinically meaningful reductions in mortality, hospitalisation, and composite CV outcomes (Figure 1). The heart failure signal is particularly robust, with significant and consistent reductions reported by both studies [31, 32]. The biological plausibility of the observed heart failure benefit is supported by several complementary mechanisms attributed to GLP‐1‐based therapies [38]. Clinically meaningful weight reduction, particularly through preferential loss of visceral and epicardial adipose tissue, may attenuate obesity‐related neurohormonal activation, reduce cardiac mechanical loading, and moderate adverse myocardial remodelling, processes that are especially relevant to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction [38]. In parallel, GLP‐1‐based therapies exert anti‐inflammatory and vasculoprotective effects, including reductions in pro‐inflammatory cytokines and vascular inflammation, which may contribute to improved myocardial function [38, 39]. Moreover, these agents favourably influence multiple metabolic and hemodynamic parameters implicated in heart failure pathophysiology, including blood pressure, lipid profile, endothelial function, natriuresis, and insulin sensitivity [38]. Importantly, many of these mechanisms may also contribute to the broader CV and renal benefits observed with GLP‐1‐based therapies and are not specific to heart failure alone [40]. Dual GLP‐1/GIP receptor agonists such as tirzepatide may further extend these benefits through enhanced adipose‐tissue insulin sensitivity, improved lipid handling, and greater weight reduction [40]. Direct mechanistic evidence in T1D is currently limited, and these hypotheses are largely extrapolated from studies in T2D, obesity, and broader cardiometabolic populations.

However, all currently available data are observational and therefore cannot establish causality. Importantly, the ongoing RCTs in T1D are primarily focused on surrogate markers of CV disease, such as arterial stiffness and insulin sensitivity, rather than hard CV outcomes comparable to those evaluated in the landmark CV outcome trials conducted in T2D. Consequently, adequately powered RCTs specifically designed to assess major CV endpoints in individuals with T1D are still needed to validate these promising findings.

Kidney Outcomes of GLP‐1‐Based Therapies in T1D

Evidence regarding the renal effects of GLP‐1‐based therapies in T1D is more limited than the CV data and currently derives from two of the three studies reviewed here [31, 32]. The study by Garg et al. did not assess renal‐specific outcomes; the authors explicitly acknowledged that changes in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) could not be evaluated within the TriNetX database framework used [30].

Tentolouris et al. included CKD incidence and exploratory eGFR–based endpoints as efficacy outcomes. The primary CKD analysis, defined using ICD‐10 codes, showed a numerically lower event rate in the GLP‐1 group compared with controls (1.2% versus 2.3%), corresponding to a relative risk of 0.51 (95% CI: 0.36–0.73); however, in time‐to‐event analysis, the difference did not reach statistical significance [HR (95% CI): 0.84 (0.58–1.21), p = 0.343]. The authors attributed this to the low burden of CKD at baseline, reflected by a mean eGFR of approximately 96 mL/min/1.73 m2, which substantially limited statistical power to detect a kidney‐specific treatment effect. In contrast, a pre‐specified exploratory analysis examining incident eGFR decline below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, a threshold defining stage G3a CKD and representing clinically meaningful kidney function deterioration, demonstrated a significant 34% reduction in risk among GLP‐1‐treated patients who had not previously experienced such values [HR (95% CI): 0.66 (0.51–0.86), p = 0.002; 2.0% versus 5.2%]. A further exploratory analysis targeting eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 showed a non‐significant trend in the same direction [HR (95% CI): 0.68 (0.44–1.05), p = 0.079]. These exploratory findings, while requiring prospective confirmation, suggest that GLP‐1‐based therapy may attenuate early loss of kidney function in T1D, even in populations without advanced renal impairment at baseline. A key limitation was the absence of urinary albumin measurements, which are fundamental to CKD staging and to the assessment of the antiproteinuric effects demonstrated by GLP‐1‐based therapies in RCTs in T2D [17].

The most direct evidence for a renal benefit comes from Xu et al., who included end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD), defined as initiation of dialysis or kidney transplantation, as a co‐primary efficacy outcome. Over a median follow‐up of 38 months, GLP‐1 RA initiation was associated with a 19% lower hazard of ESKD compared with non‐initiation [HR (95% CI): 0.81 (0.69–0.95); five‐year risk 1.6% versus 1.9%, risk difference −0.3%]. The magnitude of this risk reduction is comparable to that reported in the FLOW trial, which demonstrated a 24% reduction in a composite kidney outcome with once‐weekly semaglutide in adults with T2D and established CKD [17]. Subgroup analyses stratified by age and baseline HbA1c demonstrated consistent ESKD reductions regardless of glycemic status at entry, HR 0.69 in those with HbA1c < 8% and HR 0.84 in those with HbA1c ≥ 8%, suggesting that the renal benefit is not primarily mediated through improved glycemic control alone. As in the Tentolouris study [31], albuminuria data were unavailable, limiting characterisation of the underlying renal pathophysiology and precluding the assessment of early proteinuric changes [32].

Overall, the available real‐world data suggest a potentially meaningful renoprotective effect of GLP‐1‐based therapies in T1D, most convincingly demonstrated for early eGFR decline and ESKD. The absence of albuminuria data across all three studies is a common and important limitation that will need to be addressed in future work. The ongoing RT1D trial (NCT05822609), which evaluates the effects of semaglutide on kidney oxygenation, albuminuria, and eGFR in T1D, is expected to provide more granular mechanistic and clinical renal data [41]. Additionally, among the secondary outcomes, RESET1 RCT will assess the effect of semaglutide on the change in the urinary albumin‐creatinine ratio [36] while the impact of semaglutide on the change in the renal vascular resistance is among the secondary endpoints in T1‐DISCO [37].

Safety Profile of GLP‐1‐Based Therapies in T1D

When GLP‐1‐based therapies were first evaluated as adjunct insulin therapy in T1D, early data from RCTs raised important safety signals, particularly regarding diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hypoglycemia. In the ADJUNCT ONE and ADJUNCT TWO trials, the rate of hyperglycemia with ketosis was approximately two‐fold higher with liraglutide 1.8 mg compared with placebo, an effect most plausibly attributable to relative insulin under‐dosing in the context of insulin dose reduction, and potentially compounded by reduced caloric intake and dehydration related to gastrointestinal adverse effects such as nausea and vomiting [22, 23]. Τhe available evidence regarding hypoglycemia risk with GLP‐1‐based therapies in T1D remains inconsistent, with studies reporting increased rates of hypoglycemia [22, 23], reduced risk [24] or no significant difference compared with control groups [25, 27]. No DKA events were reported in the available semaglutide RCTs [27, 28], and tirzepatide data did not identify major safety signals, though follow‐up was short in duration [29]. These observations across agents and study designs underscored the importance of structured insulin management and close monitoring when initiating GLP‐1‐based therapy in T1D, and motivated careful evaluation of these outcomes in larger, real‐world populations.

The three real‐world studies reviewed here provide broadly reassuring safety data [30, 31, 32]. A consistent and clinically important finding across all three was the absence of an increased risk of DKA with GLP‐1‐based therapy, an outcome of particular relevance given prior concerns raised by sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 (SGLT2) inhibitor use in T1D and the theoretical possibility that insulin dose reductions accompanying GLP‐1‐based therapy initiation might predispose patients to ketosis. Garg et al. reported no significant differences in DKA risk with GLP‐1 single/dual RA treatment across the non‐obese and class I obesity subgroups, while event counts in individuals with class II and III obesity were too low to permit reliable assessment of this outcome [30]. This was supported by Tentolouris et al., who observed no significant difference in DKA incidence in time‐to‐event analysis [HR (95% CI): 0.71 (0.46–1.08), p = 0.105], despite a numerically lower event rate in the GLP‐1 group on the relative risk scale (0.8% versus 1.8%; relative risk 0.45, 95% CI: 0.30–0.68, p < 0.001). Xu et al. similarly demonstrated no increased risk of hospitalisation for DKA [HR (95% CI): 0.83 (0.76–0.90); five‐year risk 6.0% versus 7.1%] [32]. Taken together, these real‐world findings are consistent with contemporary randomised trial data using CGM and AID, which have reported no excess DKA with GLP‐1‐based therapy.

Regarding hypoglycemia, the evidence across the three studies is equally reassuring, with none reporting an increased risk. Garg et al. found no significant difference in hypoglycemia rates between groups [OR (95% CI): 0.93 (0.61–1.43), p = 0.747] [30]. Both Tentolouris et al. and Xu et al. identified statistically significant reductions in hypoglycemia risk associated with GLP‐1 therapy [31, 32]. Tentolouris et al. reported a 28% lower hazard of hypoglycemia in the GLP‐1 group [HR (95% CI): 0.72 (0.55–0.95), p = 0.021; 2.1% versus 4.5%], while Xu et al. reported an 18% reduction in hospitalisation for severe hypoglycemia events [HR (95% CI): 0.82 (0.72–0.94); five‐year risk 2.2% versus 2.6%]. The lower hypoglycemia rates observed across these studies may reflect insulin dose reductions in patients initiating GLP‐1 therapy, heightened clinical monitoring, and more proactive self‐management, a pattern consistent with evidence from clinical trials demonstrating reductions in total daily insulin requirements with GLP‐1‐based adjunct therapy in T1D.

Gastrointestinal adverse events are the most commonly reported side effects of GLP‐1 therapy in clinical trials; however, the real‐world data from these observational studies present a more nuanced picture. Garg et al. reported lower odds of laxative [OR (95% CI) 0.52 (0.43–0.63), p < 0.001] and prokinetic prescription [OR (95% CI): 0.74 (0.57–0.96), p = 0.024] in the GLP‐1 group, with no significant difference in antiemetic prescriptions, a pattern the authors attributed to likely selection bias, whereby clinicians may have preferentially initiated GLP‐1 therapy in patients considered less susceptible to gastrointestinal intolerance [30].

Tentolouris et al. reported fewer than ten pancreatitis events per group (approximately 0.2%), with no meaningful difference between groups [31]. Xu et al. reported no statistically significant increase in composite gastrointestinal adverse events, encompassing biliary disease, pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, and gastroparesis, among GLP‐1 RA initiators [HR (95% CI): 1.05 (0.99–1.13); five‐year risk 9.9% versus 9.7%] [32].

A further safety consideration is gastroparesis, which is more common in T1D than in T2D [42, 43]. Given that GLP‐1‐based therapies slow gastric emptying, there is a theoretical concern that they may exacerbate gastroparetic symptoms and further complicate the synchronisation of prandial insulin administration with nutrient absorption.

In summary, the currently available evidence suggests that the safety profile of GLP‐1‐based therapy in T1D is generally reassuring. Importantly, no consistent increase in the risk of DKA or severe hypoglycaemia has been observed, which is particularly relevant given that these complications have historically represented the major safety concerns limiting the use of adjunct glucose‐lowering therapies in T1D. However, the lack of adjudicated safety events in retrospective database studies should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. In addition, the neutral‐to‐favourable gastrointestinal safety profile reported in real‐world studies, compared with some RCTs, may reflect slower dose escalation and closer clinical monitoring in routine practice. Overall, these findings support the potential clinical feasibility of GLP‐1‐based therapies as adjunctive treatment in selected adults with T1D. However, adequately powered RCTs with rigorous adjudication of safety outcomes and longer‐term follow‐up are still required to more definitively establish the safety profile of these agents in T1D.

An important practical consideration relates to the interaction between GLP‐1‐based therapy and insulin titration in T1D. Initiation of GLP‐1‐based therapies is commonly associated with a reduction in total daily insulin requirements, typically in the range of 10%–25%, reflecting weight loss, improved insulin sensitivity, delayed gastric emptying, and suppression of postprandial glucagon secretion [44]. Inadequate insulin dose adjustment may increase the risk of hypoglycemia, whereas overly aggressive insulin reductions may predispose to hyperglycemia and ketosis. Consequently, the timing and magnitude of insulin titration are likely to have a substantial impact on both efficacy and safety outcomes in routine clinical practice and may partly account for the heterogeneous DKA and hypoglycemia signals reported across studies. To support clinicians, a recent international consensus report provides practical guidance on the safe use of GLP‐1‐based therapies in individuals with T1D, including structured insulin‐titration and monitoring strategies designed to minimise the risks of ketosis and hypoglycemia [45]. Accordingly, careful insulin adjustment, supported by frequent CGM‐based monitoring and individualised titration strategies, including optimisation of basal rates and bolus parameters in individuals using AID systems, is essential when GLP‐1‐based therapies are introduced as adjunctive treatment in T1D [45].

Special Populations and Subgroups

The real‐world studies reviewed in this paper enrolled broadly defined adult populations with T1D, and direct evidence in specific subgroups remains limited. Nonetheless, several groups merit particular consideration when evaluating the risk–benefit profile of GLP‐1‐based therapies in T1D.

Children and Adolescents

All three real‐world studies reviewed here restricted enrolment to adults aged 18 years or older, and none of the pivotal GLP‐1‐based therapies trials in T1D have included paediatric participants. As a result, evidence on the efficacy and safety of GLP‐1 use in children and adolescents with T1D is limited [46]. This is a significant gap, given the rising prevalence of overweight and obesity among young people with T1D and the recognised association between excess adiposity and poorer glycemic control, accelerated CV risk factor accumulation, and reduced quality of life in this age group [47]. The theoretical concern regarding DKA risk may be particularly relevant in younger individuals, who are already at higher intrinsic risk of ketoacidosis compared with adults. Until adequately powered paediatric studies are conducted, GLP‐1‐based therapies in children and adolescents with T1D cannot be recommended outside of research settings.

Elderly Individuals

Older adults with T1D represent a growing and clinically complex population. Hypoglycemia unawareness, reduced renal function, sarcopenia, polypharmacy, and a high burden of comorbid CV disease are common challenges in this group [48]. The reduction in hypoglycemia risk observed across the real‐world studies reviewed here may be of particular benefit in elderly patients, for whom hypoglycemia carries heightened risks of falls, cognitive impairment, and CV events. Conversely, the weight loss associated with GLP‐1‐based therapies warrants careful consideration in older adults, where unintended reduction in lean mass may exacerbate sarcopenia and functional decline [49]. None of the three studies discussed herein reported subgroup analyses specifically stratified by older age, and dedicated studies evaluating GLP‐1‐based therapies in elderly individuals with T1D are currently lacking [30, 31, 32].

Individuals With Established Cardiovascular Disease or Chronic Kidney Disease

In T2D, CV outcome trials have consistently demonstrated that GLP‐1‐based therapies confer the greatest absolute risk reduction in individuals with established CV disease, elevated CV risk, or CKD, where baseline event rates are highest [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. A similar pattern may apply in T1D, but direct evidence is currently limited. The real‐world studies reviewed here included patients across the CV risk spectrum but were not designed or powered to evaluate outcomes specifically in subgroups with pre‐existing CV disease or CKD. Notably, Tentolouris et al. reported a mean baseline eGFR of approximately 96 mL/min/1.73 m2, indicating a predominantly preserved renal function population, which may have attenuated the observed kidney‐specific treatment effect. For patients with established CKD, impaired ketone excretion may theoretically increase DKA risk, and dose adjustments require careful consideration. Prospective studies with dedicated enrolment of T1D patients with pre‐existing CV disease or CKD are needed to define the benefit–risk profile in these higher‐risk subgroups.

Individuals Using Hybrid Closed‐Loop Insulin Systems

The increasing use of hybrid closed‐loop AID systems in T1D introduces a distinct clinical context for GLP‐1‐based therapies. A crossover RCT demonstrated that adding semaglutide to an AID system improved TIR by 4.8 percentage points without increasing hypoglycemia, suggesting potential complementarity between the two therapeutic approaches [28]. Similarly, in the ADJUST‐T1D RCT involving 72 adults living with T1D, obesity, and AID use, semaglutide 1 mg weekly for 26 weeks significantly improved the composite endpoint of achieving > 70% TIR, < 4% time below range, and ≥ 5% weight loss compared with placebo (36% vs. 0%, p < 0.001) [27]. Semaglutide was also associated with reductions in HbA1c (−0.3%) and body weight (−8.8 kg), alongside an 8.8 percentage‐point increase in TIR, without excess severe hypoglycemia or DKA.

GLP‐1‐based therapies may reduce postprandial glucose excursions and overall insulin requirements, which could improve AID system performance; however, the consequent reduction in insulin needs may also require recalibration of the algorithm's basal and bolus delivery to avoid suboptimal glucose control. The ongoing SEMA‐AP trial is specifically evaluating the efficacy and safety of weekly semaglutide as an adjunct to closed‐loop therapy in T1D, and its results are awaited with interest [50]. Until these data become available, clinicians initiating GLP‐1‐based therapies in patients using AID systems should apply careful monitoring and anticipate the need for insulin dose adjustments within the device settings [45].

Current Gaps and Future Directions

The evidence reviewed in this paper highlights both the promise and the current limitations of GLP‐1 RA therapy in T1D. Several important gaps remain and will need to be addressed to define the role of these agents more precisely in clinical practice.

Lack of Dedicated Cardiovascular Outcome Trials

The most important gap in the current evidence base is the absence of randomised CV RCTs specifically designed for individuals with T1D. Although the real‐world studies reviewed herein are methodologically robust, they remain observational and therefore cannot establish causality. In T2D, landmark CV outcome trials provided the definitive evidence supporting the use of GLP‐1‐based therapies for CV risk reduction in high‐risk populations and subsequently shaped contemporary guideline recommendations [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. To date, no equivalent trial has been conducted in T1D. Designing such a study poses substantial challenges.

Although CV disease is traditionally viewed as a complication predominantly affecting people with T2D, accumulating evidence suggests that the long‐term CV burden in T1D may be equally substantial and, in older adults, may even exceed that observed in T2D. A recent nationwide Swedish cohort study demonstrated that the relative CV risk associated with T1D and T2D varies substantially by age [51]. While individuals with T2D had a higher risk of CV events before the age of 50 years, those with T1D experienced a greater CV burden and higher mortality after the age of 60 years, largely attributable to their longer diabetes duration. These findings underscore the cumulative impact of lifelong exposure to diabetes and highlight the importance of early and sustained CV risk‐factor management throughout the lifespan of people with T1D. Encouragingly, dedicated CV outcome initiatives are now emerging in T1D. The ongoing Steno 1 study is enrolling 2000 high‐risk adults with T1D to evaluate whether a multifactorial intervention strategy incorporating semaglutide, sotagliflozin, and finerenone can reduce CV events, CKD progression, heart failure hospitalisations, and mortality compared with standard care [52].

Renal‐Specific Endpoint Trials

A related and equally important gap is the absence of dedicated renal outcome trials in T1D. The current observational evidence provides early signals of a renoprotective effect but lacks the granularity of purpose‐built kidney trials. Notably, urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio, a key biomarker of early diabetic kidney disease, was unavailable across all three studies reviewed here. The ongoing RT1D trial will evaluate kidney oxygenation, albuminuria, and eGFR with semaglutide in T1D, but remains small and mechanistic in scope [41]. Larger, event‐driven trials are ultimately required to establish the renoprotective profile of GLP‐1‐based therapies in T1D on firm grounds.

Emerging Therapeutic Agents

The therapeutic landscape is evolving rapidly. Tirzepatide has produced the most pronounced weight and insulin‐related effects reported to date in T1D and is currently under evaluation in the phase 3 SURPASS‐T1D‐1 RCT [35]. Oral semaglutide, which offers the convenience of once‐daily tablet administration and may improve long‐term adherence, has not yet been studied in T1D but represents a logical candidate for future evaluation. Beyond these, next‐generation agents, including triple agonists targeting GLP‐1, glucose‐dependent GIP, and glucagon receptors simultaneously, are in clinical development and may deliver additional cardiometabolic benefits, though their role in T1D remains entirely unexplored.

An additional unresolved question is whether the cardiorenal benefits observed in the real‐world studies reviewed here represent a true class effect of GLP‐1‐based therapies or whether meaningful differences exist between individual agents. All three studies pooled multiple agents in a single exposure group and were not powered for between‐drug comparisons, so the relative contribution of each molecule cannot be determined. In T2D, GLP‐1‐based therapies as a class have shown broadly consistent cardiorenal benefits, but differences in receptor affinity, half‐life, route of administration, and ancillary mechanisms, including GIP co‐agonism with tirzepatide, may translate into varying magnitudes of effect. Dedicated head‐to‐head comparisons and adequately powered trials of individual agents in T1D will be needed before firm conclusions can be drawn about agent‐specific cardiorenal protection.

Immunomodulatory Role and Beta‐Cell Preservation

A potentially transformative area of future research in T1D relates to the possible immunomodulatory and β‐cell–preserving properties of GLP‐1‐based therapies. Beyond their established metabolic effects, GLP‐1 RAs have demonstrated anti‐inflammatory actions in both preclinical and early clinical studies [53]. A recent meta‐analysis of preclinical data showed that GLP‐1 therapies exert significant anti‐apoptotic effects on pancreatic β‐cells across experimental models, supporting their potential role in preserving β‐cell mass and function [54]. However, the substantial heterogeneity among available studies, together with unresolved concerns regarding possible long‐term β‐cell functional exhaustion, necessitates cautious interpretation of these findings. Importantly, residual β‐cell function, reflected by detectable C‐peptide secretion, has been consistently associated with improved glycemic control, lower rates of hypoglycemia, and reduced risk of DKA in T1D [55].

To date, however, clinical evidence supporting a β‐cell‐preserving effect of GLP‐1‐based therapies in T1D remains limited. In a 52‐week phase 2 RCT of adults with newly diagnosed T1D and residual β‐cell function, treatment with albiglutide did not significantly attenuate the decline in stimulated C‐peptide levels compared with placebo, although a transient improvement in peak C‐peptide was observed during follow‐up [56]. These findings suggest that any potential β‐cell‐preserving effect in humans is likely to be modest and remains unproven.

Nevertheless, whether GLP‐1‐based therapies can preserve or enhance residual β‐cell function, particularly in adults with newly diagnosed or adult‐onset T1D who more frequently retain endogenous insulin secretion, remains a compelling scientific question and an important target for future clinical investigation [57].

Conclusion

The three real‐world studies reviewed herein provide consistent evidence that GLP‐1‐based therapies in T1D are associated with reductions in all‐cause mortality, CV events, heart failure, hospitalisation, and kidney disease progression, alongside a generally reassuring safety profile with respect to DKA and hypoglycemia. These findings challenge the traditional perception of GLP‐1‐based therapies as agents of predominantly glycemic relevance in T1D and support their potential role in cardiorenal risk reduction in this high‐risk population. Important limitations include the observational nature of the available studies, the absence of adjudicated events, limited albuminuria data, heterogeneous follow‐up duration, and the possibility of residual confounding despite rigorous statistical adjustment. In addition, evidence remains sparse in key subgroups, including older adults, children and adolescents, and individuals with established CV or CKD. The next decade will be critical in defining the role of GLP‐1‐based therapies in T1D. Dedicated CV RCTs, renal outcome studies, and long‐term safety trials are needed to confirm these promising findings and guide future clinical recommendations. It must be emphasised that, to date, no randomised outcome trial has demonstrated CV or renal benefit of GLP‐1‐based therapies in T1D; accordingly, the cardiorenal advantages described in this review should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than established, and current clinical use should not be predicated on assumed outcome benefits. Given that obesity represents a major driver for the use of adjunctive GLP‐1‐based therapy in individuals living with T1D, the risk‐benefit profile of these agents currently appears most favourable in adults living with overweight or obesity, insulin resistance, and elevated cardiometabolic risk, particularly those with established or high risk of CV disease or CKD. In such populations, the potential benefits in terms of weight reduction, glycemic improvement, and cardiorenal protection are more likely to outweigh the risks of gastrointestinal adverse effects and the need for careful insulin titration. By contrast, the rationale for widespread use of GLP‐1‐based therapies in lean individuals living with T1D remains less well defined, as the expected metabolic and cardiorenal benefits are less certain, while the potential for unintended loss of lean body mass may be more clinically relevant.
