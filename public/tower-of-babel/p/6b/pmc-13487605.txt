Hard Exudates in Diabetic Macular Edema: Automated OCT Quantification and Longitudinal Response to Faricimab
Authors: Giovanni Scalabrin, Francesco Perego, Matteo Capobianco, Daniele Veritti, Enrico Borrelli, Pasquale Viggiano, Giacomo Boscia, Francesco Bandello, Rosangela Lattanzio, Maria Vittoria Cicinelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo develop and validate an automated OCT-based pipeline for hard exudates (HEs) quantification and to characterize their longitudinal evolution in eyes with diabetic macular edema (DME) treated with faricimab.DesignRetrospective, single-center observational study.SubjectsTreatment-naïve and previously treated DME eyes initiating intravitreal faricimab with ≥2 visits and a minimum follow-up of 3 months.MethodsAll patients underwent best-recorded visual acuity (VA) assessment and serial OCT imaging (Spectralis HRA system, Heidelberg Engineering). A custom ImageJ-based pipeline was used to quantify HE across volumetric datasets. Extracted metrics included total lesion count, square-root–transformed area, lesion density, and spatial distribution. Faricimab was administered using a treat-and-extend regimen following a 3-monthly injection loading phase.Main Outcome MeasuresAgreement with manual grading assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients and Bland–Altman analysis; longitudinal changes in VA, central subfield thickness (CST), and HE metrics modeled using linear mixed-effects models with evaluation of nonlinear trajectories via natural cubic splines and interaction effects with clinical and treatment-related predictors.ResultsEighty-three eyes from 65 patients (mean age 64 ± 10 years) were included, of which 72 (87%) presented with HE at baseline. Automated quantification showed good to excellent agreement with manual grading (intraclass correlation coefficient 0.85–0.92). Over a median follow-up of 13.5 months, all HE metrics decreased significantly (P < 0.001), following nonlinear trajectories characterized by an initial plateau and subsequent accelerated decline. Higher faricimab injection frequency during the first year was associated with faster HE resolution (β = –1.06 per injection per month, P = 0.02). Hard exudate burden remained positively associated with CST over time (β = 0.34 μm per unit √HE area, P < 0.001) and with worse VA at any given time point (β = 2.34 × 10–4 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution per unit √HE area, P = 0.048), without significantly influencing the rate of functional recovery.ConclusionsAutomated OCT-based quantification enables reproducible assessment of HE burden and its longitudinal dynamics in DME. Diabetic macular edema (DME) is the leading cause of vision loss in working-age individuals with diabetes mellitus and represents a major global health burden.1 It arises from disruption of the inner blood–retinal barrier, leading to the accumulation of fluid, plasma proteins, and lipids within the retinal tissue.2 Among its structural manifestations, hard exudates (HEs) are a hallmark feature. These lesions consist of focal intraretinal deposits of precipitated lipoproteins and lipid-laden macrophages, typically localizing to the outer retinal layers and appearing as well-demarcated yellowish deposits on ophthalmoscopy and color fundus photography.3 Their extent is commonly graded using standardized systems such as the ETDRS classification.4

Beyond their descriptive value, HEs have important clinical implications. Greater HE burden has been associated with worse visual acuity (VA), independent of retinal thickness.5 In advanced stages, their progressive accumulation and centripetal migration may lead to subretinal fibrosis6 and permanent central vision loss.7

Intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy is the current standard of care for center-involving DME.8 While its effects on retinal thickness and visual outcomes are well established, its impact on lipid-related biomarkers such as HE remains less consistently defined.9,10 Faricimab (Vabysmo; Roche/Genentech) is a first-in-class bispecific antibody targeting both VEGF-A and angiopoietin-2, thereby modulating vascular permeability and inflammation. In the phase III YOSEMITE trial and RHINE trials,11,12 as well as the RHONE-X extension,13 faricimab demonstrated visual outcomes noninferior to aflibercept with extended durability, supporting dosing intervals up to 16 weeks.

Post hoc analyses of YOSEMITE/RHINE further showed greater reductions in hyper-reflective foci (HRF, ≥20 μm to ≤50 μm in diameter) with faricimab compared with aflibercept, suggesting a broader effect on inflammatory and permeability-driven components of retinal pathology.14 Hyper-reflective foci have been proposed as an early manifestation of lipid extravasation and may represent precursors of larger HE.15 However, the YOSEMITE/RHINE analysis focused on HRF rather than established HEs and was primarily designed to compare biomarker responses between treatment arms. Consequently, the longitudinal behavior of HE, factors associated with HE burden and resolution, and their relationship with structural and functional outcomes remain poorly characterized. In addition, quantitative assessment of HE on OCT is not standardized, limiting reproducibility and longitudinal evaluation across studies.

To address these gaps, we developed a semiautomated image analysis pipeline based on sequential ImageJ macros for retinal layer segmentation and HE quantification on structural OCT B-scans. We applied this approach to a cohort of patients with DME treated with intravitreal faricimab at a single academic center. This study aimed to validate the proposed quantification method, to characterize the longitudinal evolution of OCT-derived HE metrics (area, count, and density) during treatment, and to evaluate the relationship between baseline HE burden and visual outcomes over follow-up.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

The present study was conducted within the SMERALD registry an institutional observational database that permits retrospective review of electronic health records from January 2013 onward and ongoing prospective data collection through 2031. Consecutive patients with DME treated with intravitreal faricimab at our institution between October 202316 and March 2026 were identified through retrospective chart review. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of IRCCS Ospedale San Raffaele (SMERALD-V3; approval date November 1, 2021). Given the retrospective nature of the present analysis, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Inclusion criteria were (1) age ≥18 years; (2) diagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus; (3) DME confirmed on spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT); (4) initiation of intravitreal faricimab; and (5) availability of ≥2 visits with a minimum follow-up of 3 months.

Exclusion criteria included (1) macular edema or vision loss due to causes other than DME; (2) significant media opacity precluding adequate OCT acquisition; (3) ocular surgery within 6 months before treatment initiation; and (4) recent intravitreal therapy (anti-VEGF within 1 month or dexamethasone implant within 4 months before baseline). Both eyes of each patient were included if eligible; for each participant, electronic clinical records from all available visits were reviewed.

Faricimab was administered according to a treat-and-extend protocol, consisting of a loading phase of 3 or 4 (up to December 2024) monthly injections followed by individualized interval extension based on anatomical and functional response, at the discretion of the treating physician, up to a maximum interval of 24 weeks. The date of the first injection was defined as the baseline visit.

Data Collection

Baseline variables included age, sex, type and duration of diabetes, glycated hemoglobin, diabetic retinopathy stage (nonproliferative or proliferative), duration of DME, and prior DME treatments. Additional variables included history of vitrectomy, macular laser photocoagulation, panretinal photocoagulation, intravitreal steroids, peripheral vasculopathy (defined as diabetic foot disease, lower limb amputation, or documented peripheral arterial disease), and diabetic kidney disease.

Visual acuity was obtained from routine clinical records and converted from decimal notation to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution units for analysis. Formal refraction was not systematically performed at every visit; therefore, VA values represent the best-recorded VA documented at each timepoint. Retinal thickness in the central 1 mm (central subfield thickness [CST], μm) and the presence of subretinal fluid (SRF) were assessed on SD-OCT.

OCT Acquisition

All SD-OCT scans were acquired using the Spectralis HRA system (Heidelberg Engineering) with a standardized macular protocol consisting of ≥19 horizontal B-scans, covering a 20° × 15° field (approximately 6.0 × 4.6 mm). Automatic real-time image averaging was used for all acquisitions, with a minimum of 25 frames averaged per B-scan. Follow-up scans were obtained using the device’s follow-up mode to ensure precise spatial registration across visits. For each eye, the same acquisition protocol (field of view, number of B-scans) was maintained across all follow-up visits. Native lateral resolution was approximately 5.7 μm/pixel, and axial resolution was 3.87 μm/pixel.

Image Processing and HE Segmentation Pipeline

A custom automated pipeline was developed in ImageJ (Fiji, National Institutes of Health) and implemented as 2 sequential macros for retinal layer segmentation and HE quantification. Full algorithmic details are provided in Supplementary Materials 1-2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org.

Preprocessing and Spatial Normalization

All OCT volumes were cropped to a standardized region of interest (1016 × 496 pixels) and resampled using bicubic interpolation. Volumes containing >19 B-scans were downsampled to 19 equidistant slices. All images were converted to 8-bit grayscale.

Retinal Pigment Epithelium Segmentation

The retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) was segmented on each B-scan using a score-based greedy tracking algorithm with bidirectional forward and backward passes. For all volumes, segmentation was initialized using 3 operator-defined reference points (left, center, and right) placed on the first B-scan, which defined a piece-wise linear depth prior propagated across columns; from the second slice onward, the mean RPE depth of previously segmented slices served as the seed. At each column, the optimal boundary candidate maximized a composite score incorporating vertical gradient strength, local brightness, penalties for adjacent hyperreflective structures, an asymmetric intercolumn jump constraint, and a running-mean deviation term. In 3-dimensional-aware mode, deviations from the corresponding position in the preceding slice were additionally penalized to enforce interslice continuity. The forward and backward profiles were fused and smoothed using a 9-column moving average filter.

To improve robustness in regions affected by HE-related shadowing, candidate RPE locations were penalized when highly reflective structures persisted above the presumed RPE, a pattern inconsistent with normal OCT anatomy. Additional penalties were applied when local reflectivity above the candidate boundary exceeded that of the candidate pixel itself, reducing the likelihood of tracking shadowing artifacts from hyper-reflective structures. Intercolumn jump constraints and 3-dimensional continuity penalties further discouraged abrupt local deviations. No manual correction of the RPE boundary was performed.

Internal Limiting Membrane Segmentation

The internal limiting membrane (ILM) was segmented using the same bidirectional framework, initialized from the RPE profile of each slice. The scoring function emphasized positive vertical gradients at the vitreoretinal interface and incorporated geometric constraints relative to the RPE (70–160 pixels above; upper bound adapted dynamically from the fourth slice onward based on running mean retinal thickness), a strict intercolumn jump limit (≤3 pixels), and curvature regularization (≤2 pixels per column change in slope). The fused profile was smoothed using a weighted 5-tap filter.

Retinal Mask Generation

A binary retinal mask was generated between the ILM and RPE boundaries for each B-scan. The inner boundary incorporated a laterally adaptive offset below the ILM (12% of local retinal thickness temporally, 30% nasally) to exclude the hyper-reflective retinal nerve fiber layer, while the outer boundary was set 20 pixels above the RPE to exclude the outer retinal bands (external limiting membrane, ellipsoid zone, and interdigitation zone). Morphological dilation and erosion (radius 3 pixels) were applied to close small discontinuities. Masks were visually inspected and manually corrected when necessary, and large macular vessel cross-sections were excluded. The final mask was applied to the OCT volume via a logical AND operation to generate the masked retinal stack (OCT_masked) used for HE detection.

Detection and Quantification of HEs

Hard exudate detection was performed pixel-wise using a ring-based local contrast method. To correct for artifacts caused by detached hyaloid or vitreous opacities, the true ILM depth was re-estimated as the shallowest position with ≥10 consecutive pixels above 60 intensity units, smoothed with a local maximum and moving average filter (half-width 4 pixels).

A pixel was flagged as an HE candidate if it met all of the following criteria: absolute intensity ≥180 units; mean intensity within a central disk (radius 2 pixels) exceeding the surrounding annular ring (inner radius 4, outer radius 10 pixels) by ≥25 units; spatial clustering of ≥16 pixels within a ±3-pixel neighborhood; and ≥6 pixels with intensity >210 within a ±4-pixel neighborhood (vessel discrimination filter). The binary candidate map was refined by median filtering (radius 1.5 pixels) and morphological opening, followed by connected–component analysis retaining objects of 10–3000 pixels2 and circularity 0.10–1.0 (Fig 1, Supplementary Video 1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

For each volume, the following metrics were extracted: total HE count, cumulative lesion area (pixels2), mean lesion size, number of affected B-scans, and mean lesion density per affected slice. Spatial distribution was characterized by classifying slices as central (30%–70% of total volume depth, corresponding to slices 7–13 of 19) or peripheral, with lesion counts and areas reported separately for each region and expressed as a central-to-peripheral ratio.

Pixel-to-Physical Unit Conversion

Area-based HE metrics were converted from pixels2 to μm2 by applying an anisotropic conversion factor of 5.61 × 3.87 ≈ 21.7 μm2 per pixel2, derived from the postnormalization pixel dimensions. Count-based and density-based metrics were retained in their native units. Area-based metrics were square root transformed to stabilize variance, following established precedent for skewed lesion area data.14

Pipeline Validation

To assess agreement between automated and manual HE quantification, a subset of 56 OCT volumes at 2 follow-up timepoints (visits 2 and 4) was independently graded by a trained observer. The grader delineated individual HE foci using the freehand selection tool in ImageJ, recording lesion area in pixels2 for all hyperreflective deposits visually consistent with HE and meeting a minimum size of 10 pixels2.

The validation analysis was performed after retinal segmentation and masking (OCT_masked stack). Therefore, the reported agreement reflects the performance of the HE detection step within the predefined retinal region of interest rather than the entire pipeline.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Continuous variables are reported as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and mean (standard deviation) where appropriate. Exploratory differences between eyes with and without HE at baseline were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Agreement between automated and manual HE measurements was assessed for total lesion count, total HE area, and square-root–transformed HE area using intraclass correlation coefficients (2-way mixed-effects model, absolute agreement, and single measures) and Bland–Altman analysis. Intraclass correlation coefficient values were interpreted according to the criteria proposed by Koo and Li: <0.50 poor, 0.50–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good, and >0.90 excellent reliability.17

At baseline, associations between HE metrics and clinical variables were explored using Pearson correlation for continuous variables. Associations with baseline HE burden (square-root–transformed area) were further evaluated using linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept for patient IDs to account for intereye correlations. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariable analysis were entered into a multivariable model.

Longitudinal trajectories of HE metrics (area, lesion count, density, and central-to-peripheral ratio) were modeled using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts for eye nested within patient IDs. This specification was supported by substantial intraclass correlation at the patient level (intraclass correlation coefficient range 0.44–0.67 across metrics). Time was modeled as a continuous variable (months from baseline). To assess potential nonlinearity, natural cubic spline models (df = 3) were compared with linear models using likelihood ratio tests.

To evaluate factors influencing the rate of HE change, interaction models were fitted between time and each candidate predictor, including demographic, systemic, and treatment-related variables (age, glycated hemoglobin, DME duration, prior injections, CST, maximum treatment interval, total faricimab injections in the first year, diabetes type, treatment-naïve status, kidney disease, peripheral vasculopathy, SRF, diabetic retinopathy type, macular laser, and steroid use). For parsimony and interpretability, interaction effects were assessed using linear time terms rather than spline-based models. Individual-level rates of HE change were estimated by fitting eye-specific linear regression slopes of square-root–transformed HE area over time. These slopes were then correlated with clinical variables using Spearman correlation coefficients.

Concurrent longitudinal associations between HE burden and CST and VA were assessed using time-varying mixed-effects models, in which square-root–transformed HE area was included as a time-varying covariate alongside time. Interaction terms between time and HE burden were tested to evaluate whether these associations changed over follow-up.

All statistical tests were 2-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Results of linear mixed-effects models are presented as regression coefficients (β) with standard errors (SEs). No correction for multiple comparisons was applied to exploratory interaction analyses; these results should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis generating.

Results

Study Population

A total of 83 eyes from 65 patients were included (mean age 64 ± 10.2 years; 31 females, 37%). At baseline, 72 eyes (87%) presented with HE, whereas 11 eyes (13%) did not. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were broadly comparable between groups, with no significant differences in age, sex distribution, systemic comorbidities, diabetes-related variables, or prior treatment exposure. Baseline VA and CST were also similar.

Notably, SRF was observed exclusively in eyes with HE (17/72, 24%) and in none of the HE– eyes. Similarly, kidney disease was present only in the HE group (9/72, 13%) and absent in the HE– group. Full baseline characteristics are reported in Table 1.

Validation of the Automated HE Detection Pipeline

Agreement between automated and manual HE quantification was good to excellent across all primary metrics. The intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.85 (95% confidence interval, 0.76–0.91) for total lesion count, 0.91 (95% confidence interval, 0.84–0.94) for total lesion area, and 0.92 (95% confidence interval, 0.87–0.96) for square-root–transformed area (Supplementary Figure 1A-C, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated negligible systematic bias for lesion count (bias = 0.0; limits of agreement ±36.6, Supplementary Figure 1D, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org) and a small negative bias for raw area (bias = –2447 μm2; limits of agreement –37 684 to +32 789 μm2, Supplementary Figure 1E, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), with the magnitude of disagreement increasing proportionally with lesion size. Agreement improved after square-root transformation (bias = –2.10; limits of agreement –83.2 to +79.0, Supplementary Figure 1F, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), supporting the use of this scale for subsequent analyses.

Baseline HE Characteristics

Among the 72 eyes with HEs at baseline, lesion burden showed wide variability. Median total lesion count was 30 (IQR 13–68), and median total lesion area was 25 700 μm2 (IQR 8450–65 200). Hard exudates were distributed across a median of 11/19 B-scans (IQR 7–16), with a median lesion density of 2.47 lesions per affected B-scan (IQR 1.60–4.60) (Table 2).

Hard exudate metrics demonstrated strong internal consistency, with total lesion count, central and peripheral counts, total area, and lesion density highly intercorrelated (r = 0.75–0.98, all P < 0.001).

Greater baseline HE burden was associated with worse retinal structure and function. Higher lipid deposition correlated with greater retinal thickening, with CST showing consistent positive associations across all HE metrics (r = 0.36–0.47, all P ≤ 0.001), and with worse VA, with VA correlating modestly but significantly with HE burden (r = 0.31–0.33, all P ≤ 0.005) (Supplementary Figure 2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Factors Associated with HE Burden at Baseline

In univariable analyses (Supplementary Table 1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), SRF (β = 87.0, P = 0.002) and CST (β = 28.4 per 100 μm, P = 0.013) were the strongest predictors of baseline HE area, with additional borderline associations for treatment-naive status (β = 45.7, P = 0.06) and prior injection number (β = –2.71, P = 0.08).

In the multivariable model, SRF remained the strongest independent associate of greater HE area (β = 71.8, SE = 29.5, P = 0.02), followed by treatment-naive status (β = 56.5, SE = 23.9, P = 0.02) and greater CST (β = 24.3 per 100 μm, SE = 11.6, P = 0.04) (Fig 2).

Longitudinal HE Reduction and Treatment Response

Median follow-up was 13.5 months (IQR 11.1–19.9). The median number of injections in the first year was 6 (IQR 4–6). Most eyes achieved treatment interval extension: 89% reached ≥8 weeks, 43% ≥12 weeks, and 19% ≥16 weeks, including 6% reaching the maximum 24-week interval. Only 11% of eyes remained below 8 weeks.

Mixed-effects models demonstrated a significant reduction in all HE metrics over time. Square-root–transformed HE area decreased by –4.88 units per month (SE = 0.77, P < 0.001), total lesion count by –2.08 lesions per month (SE = 0.36, P < 0.001), and lesion density by –0.11 per affected B-scan per month (SE = 0.018, P < 0.001). The central-to-peripheral ratio also declined (β = –0.062 per month, SE = 0.020, P = 0.002), indicating progressive redistribution away from the central macula (Supplementary Fig 3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

For HE area and lesion count, spline models provided a significantly better fit than linear models (area: χ2(2) = 10.76, P = 0.01; count: χ2(2) = 9.32, P = 0.01), indicating nonlinear trajectories. Both showed an initial plateau during the first 3–4 months, followed by accelerated decline (Fig 3A–E). In contrast, the central-to-peripheral ratio followed a linear trajectory (χ2(2) = 0.60, P = 0.7), suggesting a steady spatial redistribution (Fig 3F).

Factors Associated with the Rate of HE Reduction

Two predictors significantly modulated the rate of HE decline. Greater prior treatment exposure before faricimab initiation was associated with a slower reduction in HE burden (β = +0.22 per injection per month, SE = 0.084, P = 0.008). Conversely, a higher number of faricimab injections in the first year was associated with faster HE resolution (β = –1.06 per injection per month, SE = 0.458, P = 0.02) (Fig 4A). A borderline interaction was observed for treatment-naïve status (β = –2.91, SE = 1.51, P = 0.05), suggesting a trend toward faster HE reduction in naïve eyes.

The black line indicates the linear regression fit with 95% confidence interval (shaded); the dashed horizontal line marks the zero-slope reference. A higher injection frequency was associated with a faster rate of HE reduction (Spearman R = –0.31, P = 0.01), consistent with the corresponding mixed-effects interaction analysis. (B and C) Concurrent population-level longitudinal trajectories of HE burden and functional (B) or structural (C) outcomes, estimated from time-varying mixed-effects models. In both panels, the red solid line represents √(total HE area) (left y-axis), overlaid with best-corrected visual acuity (VA, logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution [logMAR]; blue dashed line, right y-axis) in (B) and central subfield thickness (CST, μm; orange dashed line, right y-axis) in (C). Shaded bands indicate 95% confidence intervals. Progressive HE reduction paralleled a modest gain in VA and a marked decrease in CST, illustrating the close co-evolution of lipid clearance, anatomical restoration, and functional improvement under dual VEGF-A/Ang-2 inhibition. Ang-2 = angiopoietin-2; HE = hard exudate.

Association of HE Burden with VA and CST over Follow-Up

Over the follow-up, both VA and CST improved significantly alongside HE reduction. Visual acuity improved at a rate of –0.0035 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution per month (SE = 0.0013, P = 0.009), corresponding to an approximately 2.1 ETDRS-letter gain per year.18 Central subfield thickness decreased by –7.88 μm per month (SE = 1.09, P < 0.001), corresponding to a reduction of ∼95 μm per year.

In time-varying mixed-effects models, greater concurrent HE burden was associated with higher CST at any given timepoint (β = 0.34 μm per unit √HE area, SE = 0.071, P < 0.001). A significant interaction between HE area and time (β = –0.029 μm per unit √HE area per month, SE = 0.009, P = 0.002) indicated that eyes with higher HE burden experienced a steeper CST reduction over time.

Similarly, greater concurrent HE burden was associated with worse VA at any given timepoint (β = 2.34 × 10–4 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution per unit √HE area, SE = 1.18 × 10–4, P = 0.048). The interaction between HE burden and time was not statistically significant (β = –2.00 × 10–5, P = 0.10), indicating that the rate of VA improvement over follow-up did not differ significantly according to HE burden.

Discussion

In this retrospective clinical study, we characterized the longitudinal trajectory of HE in eyes with DME treated with faricimab using an automated OCT-based quantification pipeline.

Four main findings emerge. First, greater HE burden at baseline was associated with higher CST, the presence of SRF, treatment-naïve status, and worse VA. Second, faricimab treatment was associated with a significant reduction in all HE metrics over time, following a nonlinear trajectory characterized by an early plateau and subsequent acceleration. Third, the rate of HE reduction was modulated by prior treatment exposure and treatment intensity during follow-up. Fourth, HE burden remained closely associated with CST and, to a lesser extent, VA over time. Eyes with greater HE burden exhibited higher CST values throughout follow-up and a steeper reduction in CST over time.

Morphologically, HEs range from discrete punctate foci to confluent plaques or circinate rings surrounding microaneurysms or areas of edema.19 In the present study, greater HE burden at baseline was associated with worse VA and increased retinal thickness. These associations suggest that eyes with greater lipid deposition tended to exhibit more severe structural and functional impairment within this faricimab-treated cohort. However, because treatment allocation was not randomized and many eyes had received prior anti-VEGF therapy before switching to faricimab, selection bias cannot be excluded and these findings should be interpreted within the context of the population studied. Beyond overall burden, the spatial distribution of HE appears to be clinically relevant. Srinivas et al20 reported that central HE burden, rather than total HE area, was more strongly associated with VA at 12 months, underscoring the importance of foveal involvement in determining visual prognosis. In our cohort, higher HE burden was associated with greater retinal thickness at any given time point, indicating that HE accumulation reflects ongoing vascular leakage. Bressler et al21 similarly showed that eyes with baseline HE experienced greater reductions in CST after anti-VEGF therapy, suggesting that HE may identify a predominantly permeability-driven phenotype of DME.

A similar, albeit weaker, relationship was observed with VA, with higher HE burden associated with worse VA but not significantly associated with the rate of functional recovery. Notably, the magnitude of visual improvement was modest, consistent with clinical setting results of predominantly nontreatment-naïve DME patients.22 In addition, although greater HE burden was associated with worse VA at any given timepoint, it was not significantly associated with the rate of visual improvement over follow-up.

The independent association between SRF and greater HE burden is noteworthy. Subretinal fluid in DME reflects disruption of the outer blood–retinal barrier and is typically associated with more pronounced vascular hyperpermeability.23,24 Under these conditions, increased leakage of lipoproteins into the retinal tissue may favor their subsequent precipitation as HE. The association with treatment-naïve status is similarly coherent: eyes without prior anti-VEGF exposure have not yet undergone vascular stabilization and the downstream clearance of lipid deposits, allowing HEs to accumulate over time.

The observed biphasic trajectory, characterized by an initial plateau followed by accelerated decline, likely reflects a temporal dissociation between fluid resorption and lipid clearance, and may represent a fundamental feature of HE dynamics under anti-VEGF therapy. Although retinal thickness typically improves rapidly after treatment initiation, HE resolution is delayed. The initial plateau seen in the present study likely reflects continued lipid precipitation during early fluid resorption, whereas the subsequent acceleration phase corresponds to progressive phagocytic clearance of deposited lipids. This pattern aligns with prior observations.20,25 Similar dynamics have been reported across different anti-VEGF agents,9,10 suggesting that this represents a class effect of VEGF inhibition rather than a drug-specific phenomenon.

The dual inhibition of VEGF-A and angiopoietin-2 by faricimab may provide additional mechanistic relevance. Angiopoietin-2 destabilizes the vascular endothelium and promotes inflammatory cell recruitment, processes implicated in both vascular leakage and lipid deposition.26 In post hoc analyses of YOSEMITE/RHINE, faricimab was associated with greater reductions in HRF than aflibercept, suggesting a broader effect on inflammatory and permeability-related retinal changes.14 However, HRF and HE may represent different stages of the same pathological continuum, with HEs reflecting more established lipid deposition. Although the YOSEMITE/RHINE analysis focused on HRF, our study extends these observations by evaluating the longitudinal behavior of HE, including their burden, spatial distribution, and relationship with structural and functional outcomes. Although direct comparisons cannot be made, the consistent and progressive reduction in HE observed in our cohort suggests that the effects of faricimab may extend beyond fluid resolution to include modulation of lipid-related retinal changes.27 Furthermore, higher injection frequency in the first year was associated with faster HE reduction. These findings suggest that early and adequately intensive treatment may be important not only for fluid control but also for modulating longer-term lipid deposition.

From a methodological standpoint, this study highlights the potential of automated OCT-based HE quantification. Most prior approaches have relied on color fundus photography or semiquantitative grading, which are limited by projection artifacts and reduced sensitivity to small or deeper lesions.4,5,28 More recent techniques using en face OCT29 or multicolor imaging30 have improved detection but remain limited in volumetric assessment. By operating directly on structural OCT volumes with layer-specific constraints, our pipeline enables reproducible and scalable quantification of HE burden. Agreement with manual grading was substantial to excellent, particularly for square-root–transformed area, which showed improved homoscedasticity compared with raw measurements. The observed proportional bias for larger lesions likely reflects increasing boundary uncertainty in confluent deposits and further supports the use of transformed metrics for analyses. The dense pixel-wise outputs generated by our pipeline may provide a foundation for future supervised learning approaches to HEs segmentation.31,32

This study has several limitations. The retrospective single-center design limits generalizability, and the absence of a comparator arm precludes direct assessment of relative efficacy versus other anti-VEGF agents. Systemic lipid parameters were not consistently available, precluding evaluation of their contribution to HE burden.33 The number of follow-up timepoints was limited, restricting the precision of individual trajectory modeling. The validation analysis was performed on masked OCT volumes. As a result, the reported agreement reflects the performance of the HE detection algorithm within the predefined retinal region of interest and does not constitute an independent validation of the segmentation step. In addition, manual and automated quantification were performed on identically preprocessed OCT volumes; therefore, the impact of interpolation, slice resampling, and volume downsampling on absolute HE measurements was not independently assessed. Finally, although developed on Spectralis SD-OCT data with standardized acquisition parameters, the pipeline is structurally transferable to other OCT platforms; however, absolute intensity thresholds and spatial calibration may require adjustment to account for device-specific resolution and signal characteristics.

In conclusion, faricimab treatment in clinical-setting DME was associated with a significant, nonlinear reduction in OCT-quantified HE burden over time. Baseline HE burden was associated with markers of greater disease severity within this cohort and remained associated with both CST and VA throughout follow-up. These findings support the role of HEs as a dynamic biomarker of disease activity and treatment response and highlight the value of automated OCT-based quantification for the longitudinal assessment of lipid-related changes in DME.

Supplementary Figure S1

Validation of the automated hard exudate (HE) detection pipeline against manual grading. Agreement between automated and manual HE quantification was assessed on a subset of 56 OCT volumes independently graded by a trained observer. (A–C) Scatter plots of automatically versus manually measured values for total HE lesions count (A), total HE area in μm² (B), and square-root–transformed total HE area (C). (D–F) Corresponding Bland–Altman plots showing the difference between automated and manual measurements (Auto – Manual) versus their mean for total lesion count (D), total HE area in μm² (E), and square–root–transformed total HE area (F). The solid horizontal line represents the mean bias, and the dashed horizontal lines indicate the upper and lower limits of agreement (±1.96 SD). Agreement was good to excellent across all metrics (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.850 for total count, 0.905 for raw area, and 0.924 for √area). A small negative proportional bias was evident for raw area measurements, with larger lesions tending to be underestimated by the automated pipeline; this proportional bias was markedly attenuated after square-root transformation.

Correlation matrix of baseline HE metrics and clinical variables. Pairwise associations among automated HE metrics and demographic, systemic, and ocular variables in the 72 eyes with HE at baseline. The diagonal shows the kernel density distribution of each variable. The lower triangle displays bivariate scatter plots, and the upper triangle reports the corresponding Pearson correlation coefficients. Significance levels are indicated as ∗∗∗ P < 0.001, ∗∗ P < 0.01, ∗ P < 0.05, and P < 0.10.

Supplementary Figure S3

Longitudinal trajectories of automated HE metrics over the follow-up period. Population-level trajectories of the four automated HE metrics estimated from linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts for eye nested within patient, across 83 eyes from 65 patients followed for a median of 13.5 months under intravitreal faricimab therapy. (A) Square-root–transformed total HE area (μm); (B) total HE lesion count; (C) mean HE density per affected B-scan; (D) central-to-peripheral HE count ratio. In each panel, the solid line represents the estimated mean trajectory and the shaded band the 95% confidence interval; gray dots depict individual eye observations at each visit, with time expressed in months from baseline. All four metrics decreased significantly over follow-up (all P < 0.001 for the time effect), with total HE area, lesion count, and density describing progressive reduction in overall HE burden, and the central-to-peripheral ratio capturing a parallel spatial redistribution of residual HE away from the central macula.
