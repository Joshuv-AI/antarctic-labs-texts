Revisiting the survival paradox between stage IIB/C and IIIA colon cancer
Authors: Cheng Yu, Zhiliang Shi, Guoqiang Zhou, Xu Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Patients with stage IIB/C (T4a-bN0) colon cancer often exhibit worse survival rates compared to those with stage IIIA (T1-2N1, T1N2a) colon cancer. This study re-evaluates the survival paradox using the latest Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) data (released on April 17, 2024) to inform potential revisions to the staging criteria. Utilizing SEER data with 8th edition TNM staging criteria, 4692 colon cancer patients diagnosed between 2018 and 2021 were analyzed with chi-square test. Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis were employed to assess factors associated with cancer-specific survival (CSS) and overall survival (OS). The 3-year CSS rates for stage IIB and IIC were 73.1% and 70.3%, respectively, whereas stage IIIA had a substantially higher CSS rate of 91% (P < 0.001). Similarly, the OS rates were 64.9% and 63.0% for stage IIB and IIC, respectively, compared to 83.1% for stage IIIA (P < 0.001). Multivariate analyses revealed stage IIIA patients had significantly lower risks of cancer-specific mortality (hazard ratio (HR) = 0.374, 95% CI: 0.296–0.473, P < 0.001) and overall mortality (HR = 0.575, 95% CI: 0.483–0.685, P < 0.001) compared to stage IIB patients. The upcoming 9th edition of the AJCC staging system should address this paradox by integrating advanced diagnostic markers and emphasizing the aggressive biology of T4 tumor, providing more accurate prognostic information and guiding more effective treatment strategies for colon cancer patients.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most common malignant tumor and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with an estimated 1.8 million new cases and 881,000 deaths in 2018 alone1,2. The tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system, developed by the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC), is the cornerstone for assessing prognosis, categorizing patients, and determining the most effective treatment regimens for CRC3. Typically, higher-stage malignancies correlate with poorer survival outcomes compared to lower-stage tumors3,4.

However, a paradoxical survival phenomenon has been observed in CRC patients within the current TNM staging system, where patients with stage IIB/C (T4a-bN0) often exhibit worse survival rates compared to those with stage IIIA (T1-2N1, T1N2a)5. For instance, the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate for stage IIB/C CRC is between 50.4 and 65%, while it ranges from 72.1 to 81% for stage IIIA6. This discrepancy raises concerns about the adequacy of the current staging system and its implications for treatment planning, some studies suggest that the TNM staging system may need adjustments.

Several factors have been proposed to explain this survival paradox. Stage IIB/C tumors may have more aggressive biological behavior than those in stage IIIA, despite the absence of lymph node involvement7. Another factor contributing to this survival paradox is the adequacy of lymph node retrieval during surgical resection which can impact staging accuracy, potentially leading to understaging and consequently poorer outcomes in patients with stage IIB/C CRC. Variations in the application and efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy for stage IIB/C compared to stage IIIA patients might also contribute to the observed survival differences5,8. In addition, it is reported that the presence of positive surgical margins is more common in stage IIB/C patients. These positive margins are associated with higher recurrence rates and poorer survival outcomes, thus partially explaining this survival paradox9.

The TNM staging system underwent significant revisions with the seventh and eighth editions, yet these adjustments have not fully addressed the survival paradox for non-metastatic CRC10. The TNM system continues to place significant emphasis on nodal involvement (N stage) in determining overall prognosis, sometimes at the expense of adequately considering the impact of tumor invasion depth (T stage), which we believe is the main reason for unsatisfactory staging system. Notably, the introduction of new subcategories, such as N1c for tumor deposits and the subdivision of T4 into T4a and T4b, aimed to refine prognostic precision, but the paradox persists5. Understanding the prognostic implications of this paradox is critical for optimizing treatment strategies. For instance, patients with stage IIB/C CRC might benefit from more aggressive treatment approaches, which are typically reserved for higher stages, if their poorer prognosis is better recognized and managed.

In this study, we aim to re-evaluate the survival outcomes of patients with stage IIB/C and stage IIIA colon cancer using the latest Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) data with 8th edition TNM staging criteria to determine if the survival paradox persists. We all know that AJCC is preparing to release the next edition (9th edition) of the CRC staging system. We hope that the updates in this edition will fully address the issues we are concerned about, thereby providing better guidance for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of CRC.

Methods

Data source

The SEER database, curated by the National Cancer Institute, was utilized for this study. SEER encompasses approximately 34.6% of the U.S. population and provides detailed information on cancer incidence, survival, and prevalence. The dataset includes patient demographics, tumor characteristics, treatment modalities, and follow-up data. Since SEER is an open database providing anonymous information, ethical approval or patients informed consent was not required for our study.

Study population

Using SEER*Stat software (version 8.3.4), eligible cancer patients were identified from the latest SEER database (released on April 17, 2024). The criteria for enrollment included colon cancer(with a clear pathological diagnosis of adenocarcinoma or mucinous adenocarcinoma/signet ring cell carcinoma), stage IIB/C or stage IIIA, diagnosed between 2018 (starting using the 8th edition of the AJCC TNM staging system) and 2021 and positive histology. In addition, patients with unknown No. of lymph node examined, unknown race or without surgical resection were excluded from the present study. The flow diagram of cases selection were shown in Fig. 1. Patient with unknown No. of lymph node examined, unknown race or without surgical resection were excluded from the study population. Finally, only stage IIB/C or stage IIIA colon cancer patients were selected as a target population for our investigation.Fig. 1Patients flow diagram from the SEER database.

Patients flow diagram from the SEER database.

Variables and outcomes

The primary endpoint was cancer-specific survival (CSS), defined as the time from diagnosis to colon cancer-related death. The secondary endpoint was OS, defined as the time from diagnosis to the death from any cause. This study focused on comparing the demographic and therapeutic characteristics and outcomes of these patients, particularly examining factors influencing OS and CSS. The following variables were analyzed: age at diagnosis (stratified into ≤ 65 and > 65 years), gender (male and female), race (categorized as white, black, and other), No. of lymph nodes examined (divided into < 12 and ≥ 12), histology (adenocarcinoma versus mucinous adenocarcinoma/signet ring cell carcinoma), the chemotherapy administration (dichotomized into yes and no/unknown) and tumor stage (stage IIB/C and stage IIIA).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics. Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables between patients diagnosed with stage IIB/IIC and stage IIIA colon cancer. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each variable were calculated using Cox regression, with multivariate Cox regression analysis employed to identify independent prognostic factors. Only factors considered to be statistically significancant (P value < 0.20) in the univariate Cox analysis would be included in the multivariate Cox analyses. And colon cancer patients were subjected to multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression to determine factors independently associated with OS and CSS. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to visualize survival differences, and the log-rank test was employed to assess statistical significance. All analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 23 statistical software (IBM Corporation). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient selection and characteristics

At first, a total of 63,421 colon cancer patients diagnosed between 2018 and 2021 were identified from the SEER database. Finally, 4692 patients meeting the inclusion criteria were selected as a target population for our investigation, including 1806 stage IIB, 1142 stage IIC and 1744 stage IIIA colon cancer patients (Fig. 1).

For OS, significant factors included age, gender, race, No. of lymph nodes examined, chemotherapy and tumor stage in the multivariate analysis (P < 0.05, Table 5). Notably, stages T1N1 and T2N1 were associated with significantly better OS compared to stage T4aN0 (HR = 0.481, 95% CI: 0.363–0.637, P < 0.001 for stage T1N1; HR = 0.615, 95% CI: 0.502–0.753, P < 0.001 for stage T2N1). In addition, the OS rates of stage T1N1 and stage T2N1 colon cancer patients were significantly higher than stage T4aN0 and stage T4bN0 colon cancer patients (3-year OS rates for stage T4aN0 vs. stage T4bN0 vs. T1N1, vs. stage T2N1, 64.9% vs. 63.0% vs. 86.8% vs. 81.5%, P < 0.001, Fig. 3C). The T1N2a stage did not show a significant difference compared with T4aN0 stage in the multivariate Cox analysis and Kaplan–Meier OS analysis (P > 0.05), this could possibly be due to the relatively small sample size of stage T1N2a colon cancer patients (n = 58).

Discussion

The survival paradox between stage IIB/C and stage IIIA colon cancer has been widely reported5,11,12. However, no one has yet reassessed the survival paradox using the newly released SEER data with the latest TNM 8th edition staging system. In light of the upcoming publication of the 9th edition of the AJCC CRC staging system, we urge the new staging system to pay attention to this phenomenon. Our findings highlight the survival paradox, where patients with stage IIB/C colon cancer exhibit worse survival outcomes compared to those with stage IIIA colon cancer. This paradox persists despite adjustments in the staging system aimed at improving prognostic accuracy, underscoring the need for further revisions in the upcoming 9th edition of the AJCC staging system. Our analysis reveals a significant disparity in survival rates between stage IIB/C and stage IIIA colon cancer patients. The 3-year CSS rates for stage IIB and IIC were 73.1% and 70.3%, respectively, whereas stage IIIA had a substantially higher CSS rate of 91%. Similarly, the OS rates mirrored this trend, with stage IIB and IIC at 64.9% and 63.0%, respectively, compared to 83.1% for stage IIIA. These findings are consistent with previous studies that reported similar survival discrepancies.

Several factors potentially contribute to the survival paradox. One critical factor is the aggressive biological behavior of stage IIB/C tumors9,13. Although these tumors lack lymph node involvement, their T4 classification indicates extensive local invasion, which is associated with a higher likelihood of local recurrence and poorer outcomes14. In the year 2000, the colorectal working group reclassified T4 into two subcategories: T4a, indicating tumor penetration of the surface of the visceral peritoneum, and T4b, indicating direct tumor invasion or histological adherence to other organs or structures15. Studies have shown that T4 lesions, particularly T4b, which invade or adhere to other organs or structures, carry a worse prognosis than earlier T stages16. This may explain why stage IIB/C tumors exhibit more aggressive behavior and consequently worse survival outcomes than stage IIIA tumors, which have less local invasion but involve regional lymph nodes. The adequacy of lymph node retrieval during surgery is another important consideration. However, patients with stage II disease often have fewer lymph nodes examined, leading to potential understaging and worse outcomes17. This issue of inadequate lymph node assessment may result from surgical or pathological practices, or from the inherent difficulty in retrieving nodes from tumors with extensive local invasion. Chemotherapy also plays a significant role in survival outcomes. Our data indicate that a higher proportion of stage IIIA patients received chemotherapy (71.4%) compared to stage IIB/C patients (46.6%). This disparity in treatment approaches highlights the need for guidelines that ensure stage IIB/C patients receive appropriate adjuvant therapy. Current guidelines recommend the examination of at least 12 lymph nodes for accurate staging18. Although our study confirms that examining ≥ 12 lymph nodes and the chemotherapy administration are associated with improved CSS and OS, the survival paradox still persists even after adjusting for No. of lymph nodes retrieved and the chemotherapy administration in multivariate Cox regression analysis. Therefore, T4 lesions are the most significant factor leading to this phenomenon. The T4 category, particularly T4b, indicates advanced local invasion, which correlates with poorer outcomes19. Further subclassifying T4b based on the extent and location of invasion might provide better prognostic information and guide treatment strategies more effectively. For instance, distinguishing between T4b tumors that invade different structures or organs could help tailor surgical and adjuvant treatment approaches more precisely, potentially improving survival outcomes for these patients. The 9th edition of the CRC staging system should focus on elucidating this survival paradox and further emphasize the aggressive biological characteristics of T4 tumors themselves and the adverse prognosis they entail.

Emphasizing the importance of thorough lymph node examination in staging guidelines could help reduce understaging and improve survival outcomes. Ensuring that surgical and pathological practices adhere to the recommended examination of ≥ 12 lymph nodes is crucial. This might involve additional training for surgeons and pathologists, as well as institutional policies that mandate compliance with lymph node examination guidelines. Enhanced quality control measures could ensure that all patients receive the most accurate staging possible. The inclusion of tumor deposits in the staging criteria, as introduced in the 8th edition with the N1c subcategory, should be refined and perhaps expanded to better capture their prognostic significance. Tumor deposits have been associated with worse outcomes and might require more aggressive treatment. A more detailed classification of tumor deposits, considering their size, number, and location, could improve prognostic accuracy and treatment planning. Research has shown that patients with tumor deposits have survival outcomes similar to those with nodal involvement, supporting the need for their careful evaluation and appropriate classification in the staging system20. Recognizing the poorer prognosis of stage IIB/C colon cancer patients is essential for optimizing treatment strategies. These patients might benefit from more aggressive treatment approaches, including adjuvant chemotherapy, which are typically reserved for higher-stage diseases. Tailoring treatment based on a comprehensive evaluation of tumor characteristics, including molecular and genetic markers, could improve outcomes. For instance, patients with high-risk stage II disease (e.g., T4 tumors, lymphovascular invasion, or poorly differentiated histology) would benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy similar to stage III patients, thus potentially improving their survival rates16.

In the future, researches should focus on validating the proposed modifications to the TNM staging system through large-scale and prospective studies. Such efforts are essential to ensure the accuracy and clinical relevance of these changes, particularly in diverse colon cancer patient populations. Additionally, exploring the underlying biological mechanisms driving the aggressive behavior of stage IIB/C tumors could uncover new therapeutic targets and strategies21,22. Understanding the genetic and molecular differences between stage IIB/C and IIIA tumors could lead to the development of targeted therapies that address the specific characteristics of these tumors, thereby improving patient outcomes.

Therefore, the present study is a necessary step in providing updated evidence that the survival paradox between stage IIB/C and stage IIIA colon cancer remains unresolved. By using the latest SEER data, we offer a contemporary perspective that is directly relevant to the upcoming revisions in the 9th edition of TNM staging system. The clinical significance of our findings lies in their potential to influence these revisions, ultimately leading to improved prognostic accuracy and treatment strategies for colon cancer patients.

Our study, while providing valuable insights, has several limitations. As a retrospective analysis using the SEER database, it is subject to selection bias. The absence of detailed molecular and genetic data in SEER limits our ability to consider important prognostic factors that might influence survival outcomes. Additionally, the study’s findings, based on a U.S. population, may not be fully generalizable to other settings. Lastly, our study reaffirms the survival paradox but does not propose or evaluate a new staging system. Future prospective studies are needed to address these limitations and validate our results in more diverse populations.

Conclusions

While significant progress have been made in colon cancer staging and treatment, the survival paradox between stage IIB/C and stage IIIA patients persists, underscoring the need for continued refinement of staging criteria and treatment protocols. By integrating advanced diagnostic markers and emphasizing the aggressive biology of T4 tumor, the upcoming revisions to the AJCC staging system can help resolve these discrepancies, providing more accurate prognostic information and guiding more effective treatment strategies for colon cancer patients. These efforts are essential for improving the overall management and outcomes of CRC, ensuring that all patients receive the most appropriate and effective care based on their specific disease characteristics.
