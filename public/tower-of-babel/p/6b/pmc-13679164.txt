Advancing assisted reproductive technology in Africa: a narrative review on the promising role of molecular biology and molecular techniques
Authors: Foster Kyei, Clement Napo, Collins Nabawo, Emmanuel Opoku Asante, Benjamin Nyarko, Isaac Kofi Adu, Cara Obenewaa Aidoo, Victoria Essien, Prince Amoah Barnie, Emmanuel Owusu Ansah, Kwadwo Fosu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAssisted Reproductive Technology (ART) has significantly improved fertility outcomes globally, yet its full potential remains underutilized. In Africa, persistent barriers including high costs, limited access, inadequate infrastructure, lack of awareness, and sociocultural and ethical concerns impedes its utility. This review explores the promising role of molecular biology and advanced molecular techniques in overcoming these challenges and advancing ART on the continent. Techniques such as preimplantation genetic testing (PGT), non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing, mitochondrial replacement therapy (MRT), and single-cell RNA sequencing offer opportunities to enhance embryo selection, minimize genetic and epigenetic risks, and improve pregnancy success rates. Furthermore, innovations like biomarker discovery, liquid biopsy, and genomic screening can personalize treatment strategies and improve reproductive outcomes. We also provide a narrative on the progress made in expanding ART services, improving affordability through low-cost protocols, and building local expertise through training and collaborations. While challenges persist, especially in regulation and access to ART, the integration of molecular innovations in a phased manner has the potential to transform ART if implemented within appropriate infrastructural, ethical, and policy frameworks. This review underscores the need for sustained investment, policy reform, and interdisciplinary collaboration to bridge existing gaps and ensure that ART technologies are accessible, safe, and effective for all individuals and couples affected by infertility in the region.Keywords: Assisted reproduction, Infertility, Africa, Cultural, Molecular Techniques

Introduction

Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) has given a very appreciable number of couples around the world the ‘right’ to be parents. It is estimated that more than 180 million couples in developing countries suffer from primary or secondary infertility. In Africa alone, a meta-analysis study showed that the pooled proportion of both primary and secondary infertility was 49.91% and 49.79%, respectively, in a Chi-square analysis. Some married couples are facing social backlash on their inability to conceive and bear children. ART presents a relieving opportunity for such individuals to bear children [1]. At least 8 million children have been engendered through ART and the impact keeps on increasing with time [2]. The prevalence of infertility further underscores the significance of ART, affecting nearly 1 in 6 adults [3]. The potential of molecular biology and techniques in advancing ART in Africa is a promising area of research.

The potential for molecular biology and its associated techniques to further revolutionize reproductive outcomes has been recognized by researchers such as Wilmut et al. [4] and Brasch-Andersen et al. [5], who emphasize the power of these methods in addressing reproductive health challenges. Oloyede & Giwa-Osagie [6] and McDowell et al. [7] further underscore the critical need for these advancements in Africa, a continent where infertility rates are notably high and conventional ART methods have often yielded limited success. However, the successful integration of these sophisticated techniques into African ART practices is not without its challenges. While the core principles of ART remain universal, its implementation in Africa encounters distinct challenges such as limited access and inadequate facilities [6]. Despite these challenges, the potential of molecular biology and its associated techniques in advancing assisted reproductive technology in Africa is clear, and further research and implementation efforts are needed in this area.

Standardized definitions for ART procedures, such as those outlined in the International Glossary on Infertility and Fertility Care, have been essential for establishing a common language for reporting ART outcomes (Zegers‐Hochschild et al., 2017). For the first time, terms from embryology and andrology as well as definitions like "fertility care" and "fertility awareness" have been added to the lexicon. In addition, the term "infertility" has been broadened to encompass a greater range of disorders influencing a person's or a couple's ability to conceive. Although infertility is still defined as a condition marked by the inability to achieve a clinical pregnancy, it now recognises that infertility is not always caused by an illness, thereby presenting the idea of a functional impairment that can result in a handicap.

The integration of microfluidics in ART has demonstrated the potential to enhance techniques like intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) and address infertility challenges [8]. Research focusing on the molecular aspects of ART procedures has emphasized the association between ART and adverse perinatal outcomes, underscoring the importance of meticulous clinical and laboratory protocols [9]. As ART continues to evolve, incorporating molecular insights and techniques will be critical in improving success rates and advancing reproductive healthcare in Africa.

In recent years, the landscape of fertility treatments has undergone transformational changes across the globe, with particular emphasis placed on advancing ART within African contexts. This review aims to explore the pivotal role that molecular biology and its associated techniques have played in revolutionizing ART practices throughout the continent. By delving into this dynamic intersection between cutting-edge science and societal needs, we seek to highlight the potential benefits and challenges inherent to implementing these innovative approaches in diverse African settings.

This review is grounded in the recognition that infertility affects millions worldwide [2], including many Africans who face unique barriers such as limited access to healthcare services, cultural stigmas surrounding reproduction, and insufficient resources dedicated to research and development [10]. As a result, there exists a pressing need to develop tailored solutions that address local concerns while leveraging global advancements in molecular biology and related technologies.

In this study, we aim to examine various aspects of integrating molecular biology into ART practice in Africa, including the application of next-generation sequencing (NGS), genomic imprinting analysis, preimplantation genetic testing (PGT), and other emerging methods. We will also discuss the importance of collaborations among researchers, clinicians, policymakers, and community stakeholders in fostering sustainable growth and equitable distribution of these novel tools. While frontier technologies such as CRISPR and mitochondrial replacement hold long-term promise, their translation to African settings requires careful staging given current infrastructural and regulatory limitations [11]. Near-term molecular innovations such as non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), biomarker-based embryo selection, and low-cost genomic screening are more feasible starting points.

Challenges currently facing assisted reproductive technology in Africa

Assisted Reproductive Technology faces numerous challenges globally. These challenges can be particularly pronounced in Africa and sub-Saharan Africa, where they are socioeconomically, politically, religiously, and culturally founded [12].

Lack of awareness and education

The lack of awareness and education surrounding infertility and ART also presents a substantial challenge. Research suggests that general knowledge about fertility is low to moderate, highlighting the urgent need for public education and fertility awareness initiatives [13]. This limited awareness contributes to low utilization rates of available ART options. Furthermore, insufficient educational programs for both the general public and healthcare providers impede a comprehensive understanding and acceptance of ART [14]. Low media coverage and public interest in arts and culture, as noted in the context of artistic expression, may also contribute to a lack of information dissemination regarding ART [15].

Regulatory, ethical and religious concerns

Many African countries lack comprehensive regulatory frameworks governing ART, leading to variations in the quality and safety of services provided [16]. Ethical concerns, such as the use of donor gametes and surrogacy, can complicate the implementation of ART in different cultural contexts [17, 18]. Some religious organizations believe that reproductive technologies are a deviation from traditional sexual practice and that they diminish the reproductive process by separating the unitive and procreative aspects of sexual activity. They also believe that bringing a third party into the process is a violation of the sanctity of marriage and the family [19]. Within Islam, while IVF using a couple's gametes is generally permissible, the use of third-party donors is typically prohibited [20]. Christian perspectives on IVF are varied, with the Catholic Church strongly opposing ART, while Protestant, Anglican, Orthodox, and Coptic churches show varying acceptance, mostly rejecting third-party donations. Judaism encourages procreation, but Orthodox sects prohibit sperm/egg donation and surrogacy. Chinese and Japanese cultural approaches, shaped by Confucianism and Shintoism, allow ART with specific legal restrictions. Religious beliefs continue to shape ART access and acceptance worldwide [21, 22]. Integrating molecular technologies without appropriate regulation risks widening health inequities. National and regional ART guidelines must therefore include ethical oversight of gene-editing, donor use, and data privacy, while ensuring affordability and inclusion.

Resource limitations

Inadequate infrastructure, such as poorly equipped laboratories and clinics, can impact the quality of ART services [23]. A shortage of trained specialists, including reproductive endocrinologists, embryologists, and genetic counselors, limits the availability of high-quality care [24]. Also, one of the points of contention in the IVF discussion is the question of resource allocation. Gametes are essential to the process and are often donated. There should be a way to prioritize oocyte recipients [25], according to those who oppose IVF for postmenopausal women. Postmenopausal women who remain childless because of their own free decisions made earlier in life (such as postponing pregnancy until they have established their job) should not be permitted to receive oocytes, according to some. It has been argued that oocytes are a limited resource, and hence, recipients should be prioritized based on clinical need. Taken together, research, policy infrastructure, and public education are believed to play a crucial role in dealing with how resource deficits are curtailed in ART practice.

Cost and limited accessibility

The use of assisted reproductive technology to treat infertile individuals or couples is often regarded as a significant biomedical intervention around the world. The availability, quality, and delivery of infertility treatment services, however, differ significantly across high-income countries (HICs) and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3]. ART has been around for more than 4 decades, but most individuals in settings with poor resources still cannot access it [26]. ART procedures are often expensive, making them inaccessible to a large portion of the population in many African countries [24]. The limited availability of ART clinics, especially in rural and underserved areas, restricts access to these services [17, 18].

Cultural and social barriers

Cultural beliefs and social stigmas associated with infertility can discourage individuals and couples from seeking ART [14]. In some communities, there is a preference for natural conception, and assisted reproduction may be viewed with suspicion or disapproval [15]. In African cultures, fertility often extends beyond the individual. It is deeply rooted in the community, and childless unions may be viewed as incomplete. In some cases, infertility is frequently attributed to women, leading to significant stigma, feelings of guilt, shame, and social isolation [27]. The interwoven nature of cultural and religious systems in many African communities further shapes perceptions and experiences of infertility. Traditional beliefs and practices heavily influence how infertility is understood and managed [28]. In the black community in South Africa, for instance, women are often held responsible for infertility, while male infertility remains a taboo subject [29].

Genetic and epigenetic risk

Persistence of genetic defects in children produced using ART

Many couples who resort to ART have underlying genetic or chromosomal abnormalities that can be passed on to their offspring [30]. These pre-existing genetic defects may be perpetuated in the resulting children, increasing the risk of inherited disorders.

ART, also, is often sought by older parents who have a higher risk of transmitting genetic mutations due to increased age-related mutations in their gametes [31]. This can lead to a higher prevalence of chromosomal abnormalities and monogenic disorders in children born through ART.

High risk of epigenetic defects in children produced from ART

The ART process can lead to changes in DNA methylation and other epigenetic mechanisms, increasing the risk of genetic disorders [32]. These epigenetic modifications may affect gene expression and contribute to the development of various health conditions in ART-conceived children. Procedures like ICSI involve direct manipulation of gametes, increasing the potential for genetic and epigenetic abnormalities [33]. The bypassing of natural selection processes during ICSI can lead to the propagation of genetic defects. Despite preimplantation genetic testing (PGT), some genetic and epigenetic defects might still be present due to the limitations of current screening technologies [34]. The inability to detect all genetic abnormalities can result in the transfer of embryos with unidentified genetic defects.

Association between DNA methylation and ART child development [35, 36]

Children conceived via ART often exhibit different DNA methylation patterns, which can influence gene expression and developmental outcomes [35, 36]. These epigenetic changes may contribute to variations in growth, metabolism, and potential long-term health risks. The observed methylation changes in ART-conceived children have been associated with a range of developmental outcomes, including growth, metabolic regulation, and increased susceptibility to certain health conditions [35, 36]. The long-term implications of these epigenetic alterations are still being investigated.

Contextual feasibility of molecular techniques in Africa

Despite the structural and sociocultural barriers outlined above, the integration of molecular techniques into African ART practice remains feasible when aligned with local capacity and implemented incrementally. Feasibility in this context does not imply immediate or universal adoption, but rather the strategic introduction of molecular approaches that are scalable, cost-conscious, and compatible with existing clinical infrastructure. Phased implementation, targeted diagnostics, and regional capacity-building initiatives demonstrate how molecular innovations can be adapted to African health systems without intensifying existing constraints.

ART successes, progress and potential in Africa

Opportunities to enhance reproductive healthcare in Africa using assisted reproductive technology are developing. As full-scale success is still only evident in a small number of nations, increasing funding, research, and making friendly policies have significant potential for wider advantages. Below, with the help of successful nations as models, we examine some of the main ways that ART might improve reproductive healthcare in Africa.

Expanding access and infrastructure

While affordability issues remain, access to ART services has expanded over the past two decades in Africa [11, 37, 38]. For instance, South Africa, Egypt, and Nigeria have established multiple fertility centers, catering to both local and international patients. As of 2021, a report by Horton et al. [39] indicates that Egypt, with the highest number of fertility centres, had 110, followed by Nigeria with 98, and then South Africa with 40. This expansion has been driven by various factors, including increased investment, public–private partnerships (PPPs), and the recognition of the importance of addressing infertility as a public health issue [40]. Notwithstanding, in a recent study, every fertility expert concurred that to address access in the area, government support for assisted reproductive services in the public sector was required. Additional recommendations included lowering drug expenses by employing sedatives for oocyte retrievals rather than general anaesthetics and modest stimulation procedures [41]. The development and deployment of point-of-care molecular diagnostic tools could improve access by extending diagnostic capabilities beyond centralized clinics to more remote and underserved areas. This would align with the observed expansion of ART services in countries like South Africa, Egypt, and Nigeria, potentially reaching a larger proportion of the population. The concept of mobile ART laboratories, staffed by trained personnel from a central clinic, also presents a promising avenue for improving access in regions with limited infrastructure.

Improved affordability and financial accessibility

As a lack of data on some IVF interventions is thought to be associated with ART treatment cost, historically, this impediment has been a significant barrier to access in Africa. The success of low-cost IVF techniques and the expansion of ART services are also thought to be significantly hampered by the continent's shortage of qualified embryologists [11]. However, recent efforts to improve affordability and financial accessibility have yielded positive results. To alleviate financial burdens, some African fertility clinics have adopted cost-effective ART methods [42]. These include natural cycle IVF and mild ovarian stimulation protocols, which reduce medication usage and associated costs [3, 43]. Also, molecular techniques that enhance embryo selection, like PGT, could potentially reduce the overall cost of IVF. By increasing the likelihood of successful implantation in the first cycle, this could decrease the need for multiple treatment rounds. Such approaches not only lower expenses but also minimize patient discomfort and health risks linked to conventional stimulation methods. ​While these initiatives mark progress, the affordability of ART in many African countries remains a significant challenge. Direct medical costs for infertility treatments often exceed the average annual income, leading to unaffordability and potential financial hardship for those seeking such services. Continued efforts to implement supportive policies, expand financial assistance programs, and adopt cost-effective clinical practices are essential to enhance ART accessibility across Africa [3].

Increased success rates in ART

The application of ART in Africa has also seen significant advancements in terms of technical capabilities and improved outcomes. Researchers and clinicians have adapted and optimized ART techniques to address the unique challenges and needs of the African context, such as the higher prevalence of infectious diseases and genetic factors [44]. This has led to improved success rates, increased live birth rates, and better overall outcomes for ART patients in Africa [17, 18] (Table 1). A study by Archary et al. [43] found that multiple delivery rates exceeded 20%, with preterm births more frequent in twins and triplets (59.5%) compared to singletons (21.9%). Following a fresh embryo transfer, the clinical pregnancy rate (CPR) per embryo transfer is 37.3%,following a frozen embryo transfer, at 37.8%. Meanwhile, in autologous cycles, the cumulative CPR per aspiration was found to be 41.9% [43]. Amidst these positive observations, a 5-year study observed stable rates across success parameters, highlighting other social determinants like improving pregnancy follow-up as important factors to consider [46]. Additionally, advances in techniques and skills through continuous medical education and partnerships with developed countries have also gradually raised ART success rates in Africa [37, 48, 49]. Live birth rates similar to global standards have been documented, challenging notions of higher treatment failure rates [48]. However, further improvements are still required to match developed country levels [37].

Capacity building and skill development for the advancement of molecular ART in Africa

Another notable success of ART in Africa has been investment in capacity building and skill development among healthcare professionals. Initiatives to train and upskill clinicians, embryologists, and other ART specialists have been instrumental in improving the quality and consistency of ART services across the continent [39]. This has helped to address the shortage of skilled personnel, which was previously a significant barrier to the widespread adoption of ART.

The promising role of molecular biology and molecular techniques in ART advancement

Molecular biology and molecular techniques have significantly advanced assisted reproductive technology, enhancing both the efficiency and success rates of reproductive procedures. While PGT-A has been shown to improve outcomes in selected populations, meta-analyses reveal limited benefit in younger women or low-resource contexts [50, 51]. Thus, implementing such technology in African ART centres should be guided by robust cost-effectiveness and clinical benefit assessments.

Preimplantation genetic testing (PGT)

PGT for aneuploidy (PGT-A)

Preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) is a valuable tool in assisted reproductive technology (ART) that helps identify chromosomal abnormalities in embryos before they are transferred to the uterus. This technique increases the chances of a successful pregnancy by selecting genetically normal embryos for transfer, thus reducing the risk of miscarriage and improving the likelihood of a healthy live birth [52].

PGT for monogenic/single gene defects (PGT-M)

Preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic/single gene defects (PGT-M) is another application of molecular techniques in ART. PGT-M allows for the detection of specific genetic disorders in embryos, preventing the transmission of inherited diseases and improving the chances of a healthy pregnancy [53].

In the African ART context, the strategic use of PGT may help offset structural challenges such as high treatment costs, limited availability of repeat ART cycles, and late presentation of infertility cases. Prioritizing the transfer of embryos with higher implantation potential or free of known inherited disorders improves cost-effectiveness and reduce emotional and financial strain on patients. Its relevance is further heightened in regions where genetic counselling and long-term care for congenital disorders are limited, positioning PGT as a preventive rather than corrective intervention.

Non-Invasive Prenatal Testing (NIPT)

Non-Invasive Prenatal Testing (NIPT) is an emerging molecular technique that enables the early detection of genetic abnormalities in fetuses through maternal blood samples. This approach reduces the need for more invasive procedures, such as amniocentesis, which carries a higher risk of complications [54]. NIPT has the potential to improve prenatal care and decision-making in the African context, where access to specialized healthcare resources may be limited. Its non-invasive nature aligns well with resource-limited settings by minimizing procedure-related risks and reducing dependence on highly specialized personnel. As laboratory capacity and referral networks expand across parts of the continent, NIPT could serve as an important bridge between ART services and routine antenatal care, supporting earlier risk stratification and referral.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is a powerful molecular technique that analyzes gene expression patterns in individual cells. In the context of ART, scRNA-seq has advanced understanding of embryo development and cellular heterogeneity thereby improving the chances of a successful pregnancy [55]. However, its direct clinical application in African ART settings remains limited. While scRNA-seq is currently confined to research and highly specialized laboratories, its primary value lies in the translational insights it provides rather than routine clinical use. Insights from this technology can inform simplified embryo assessment tools and culture strategies that are more feasible for use in African fertility clinics. Over time, such translational benefits may contribute to improving embryo selection efficiency without requiring widespread adoption of the technology itself.

CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing

CRISPR/Cas9 is a revolutionary gene editing technology that holds promise for correcting genetic defects in human embryos. This technique allows for the precise modification of specific DNA sequences, potentially enabling the correction of inherited genetic disorders before embryo implantation [56]. However, the use of CRISPR/Cas9 in human embryos is still under intense ethical and regulatory scrutiny due to concerns about safety, efficacy, and potential unintended consequences [57].

In Africa, discussions around CRISPR/Cas9 in reproductive medicine are largely prospective and policy-oriented rather than clinical. The technology raises important considerations related to equity, governance, and ethical oversight, particularly in regions where regulatory frameworks for advanced reproductive technologies are still emerging. Engaging African scientists, ethicists, and policymakers early in these debates is essential to ensure that future decisions reflect regional values, health priorities, and social contexts.

Mitochondrial Replacement Therapy (MRT)

Mitochondrial Replacement Therapy (MRT), also known as "three-parent IVF," is a technique that aims to prevent the transmission of mitochondrial diseases. By replacing the defective mitochondria in the mother's egg with healthy mitochondria from a donor, MRT can help avoid the inheritance of mitochondrial disorders [58]. This approach can be particularly beneficial in the African context, where the prevalence of mitochondrial diseases may be higher due to genetic factors or limited access to specialized healthcare. However, the use of MRT also raises ethical concerns and is subject to regulatory approval in many countries.

Biomarker discovery

The identification of reliable molecular biomarkers for key aspects of assisted reproductive technology, such as embryo viability, endometrial receptivity, and ovarian reserve, holds significant potential to personalize and optimize ART protocols [59]. Mounting evidence indicate that understanding the unique molecular signatures associated with these critical factors can help healthcare providers tailor treatment strategies to individual patients. This improves the chances of successful embryo implantation and pregnancy [60]. In effect, this approach can be particularly beneficial in the African context, where access to specialized fertility services may be limited, by helping to maximize the efficiency of ART procedures. Biomarker-based approaches hold particular promise for improving efficiency in African ART programmes, where maximizing outcomes per treatment cycle is critical. Population-specific biomarker research is therefore essential to avoid reliance on data generated predominantly in non-African cohorts.

Epigenetic studies

Epigenetic factors, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin remodeling, play a crucial role in embryo development and implantation [61]. Understanding these epigenetic mechanisms can provide valuable insights into the complex processes that influence fertility and successful pregnancy outcomes [62]. Epigenetic studies in the African context may reveal unique patterns or deviations that could inform the development of interventions to improve embryo development and implantation, ultimately leading to better reproductive health outcomes.

Sperm and oocyte selection

Molecular techniques, such as assessment of genetic and epigenetic markers, can help in the selection of high-quality sperm and oocytes, which is crucial for improving fertilization rates and embryo quality [63, 64]. These advancements can be particularly beneficial in the African context, where there may be limited access to specialized fertility services or a higher prevalence of genetic or epigenetic factors that can affect reproductive success. Healthcare providers can optimize the chances of successful fertilization and embryo development, ultimately enhancing the overall outcomes of ART procedures by employing these advanced selection methods.

Liquid biopsy

The analysis of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) present in maternal bodily fluids, such as blood, has emerged as a powerful tool for monitoring pregnancy health and detecting potential complications early on [65]. This non-invasive approach, known as liquid biopsy, can provide valuable insights into the fetal and placental health status, allowing for timely interventions and improved maternal and neonatal outcomes [66]. In the African context, where access to specialized prenatal care may be limited, liquid biopsy could be a transformative technology, enabling more comprehensive monitoring of pregnancy and early detection of potential issues, thereby reducing maternal and infant mortality and morbidity.

Genomic screening

Comprehensive genetic screening of both partners undergoing assisted reproductive technology (ART) can help identify potential genetic incompatibilities or predispositions to inherited disorders, which is crucial for guiding donor selection or alternative treatment approaches [67]. This level of genetic analysis can inform decision-making and personalize treatment plans, ultimately improving the chances of successful conception and healthy pregnancy outcomes [68]. In the African setting, where genetic diversity is high and certain genetic disorders may be more prevalent, comprehensive genomic screening can be particularly valuable in ensuring the selection of appropriate gametes or embryos, and in identifying alternative reproductive options, such as the use of donor gametes or preimplantation genetic testing, when necessary.

Phased integration of molecular techniques in Africa

Based on the infrastructural, systemic, and social setbacks faced by Africa, phased integration of ART as a potent solution to infertility challenges is recommended.

Near-term (1–5 years)

In the short term, it is possible to think of molecular advances that meet the needs of African infertility while minimizing the discrepancies in accessibility and other social concerns. Affordable, scalable molecular diagnostics such as PGT-A, NIPT, microbiome profiling, and biomarker discovery are a key starting point with their integral role in optimizing embryo selection and early diagnosis [50, 51, 54].

Medium-term (5–10 years)

Population-level initiatives like carrier screening or research-based consortia similar to Mackenzie’s Mission. The Mackenzie’s mission is a three-phased intervention that aims to screen willing participants for their reproductive genetic carrier status while assessing the acceptability and feasibility program, which comes at no cost. Such interventions will positively impact awareness, which is also a key indicator for penetration, while improving one's fertility chances [69].

Long-term (> 10 years)

Emerging and ethically debated methods such as CRISPR/Cas9, MRT, and single-cell sequencing are introduced only after strong regulatory oversight and equitable access mechanisms are in place. Techniques as these are expected to obviously take longer times to receive widespread adoption in several African countries. A gradual and or long-term integration span is proposed for these technologies that have ethical, social, and cultural debates surrounding them [56, 57].

Balancing cost, benefit, and equity in molecular ART

The integration of molecular techniques into Assisted Reproductive Technology holds clear potential to improve diagnostic precision, treatment efficiency, and reproductive outcomes. However, in African contexts, the value of these innovations must be assessed not only in terms of clinical benefit, but also through the lenses of affordability, equity, and health system sustainability. Balancing these dimensions is essential to ensure that molecular ART advances enhance reproductive care without reinforcing existing social and economic disparities.

While molecular advances such as preimplantation genetic testing, non-invasive prenatal testing, and biomarker-based embryo assessment can improve embryo selection and reduce long-term treatment burden, their benefits are unevenly distributed when access is determined primarily by ability to pay [3, 70, 71]. In African countries where the cost of a single ART cycle exceeds the average annual household income, the unregulated introduction of high-cost molecular technologies risks creating two-tier fertility systems that will only be the privilege of urban and higher-income populations [3]. Equity considerations therefore require that molecular innovations be evaluated not only for effectiveness, but also for their potential impact on access and inclusion.

Ethically debated and resource-intensive molecular tools, such as CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing and mitochondrial replacement therapy (MRT), should remain confined to research, policy deliberation, and long-term planning until robust regulatory oversight, public engagement, and equitable access mechanisms are established [72]. Their premature introduction could widen the gap between elite urban patients and the majority of infertile couples who already struggle with affordability and access [11].

Public-sector involvement and policy leadership are critical to ensuring that molecular ART does not exacerbate reproductive health inequities. Integrating selected molecular services into public or subsidized fertility programmes, supported by transparent pricing structures and regional collaboration, can help distribute benefits more fairly. Public–private partnerships and regional research consortia can also play a pivotal role in improving cost-effectiveness by pooling resources, sharing molecular laboratory infrastructure, and promoting collaborative training programs [73]. Strengthening these cooperative efforts aligns with recent trends reported by the African Network and Registry for ART (ANARA), which highlight the importance of resource-sharing and standardized practices across fertility centres [43, 70, 71]. Leveraging digital health tools and telemedicine can also extend the reach of molecular diagnostics and counselling to underserved regions, promoting inclusivity and continuity of care.

Recommendations for advancing molecular integration in ART

The integration of molecular techniques into ART holds immense potential for improving reproductive outcomes, reducing the risk of genetic disorders, and personalizing infertility treatments [74]. Combining advanced molecular techniques like pre-implantation genetic testing, non-invasive prenatal testing, CRISPR/Cas9, biomarker discovery, epigenetic Studies, Sperm and Oocyte Selection, mitochondrial analysis, single-cell sequencing, genomic screening, etc. can make ART more accurate, efficient, and successful while also lowering the risk of genetic disorders and improving reproductive outcomes. Future developments in molecular diagnostics and genomics will continue to shape the future of ART, giving hope to millions of people hoping to overcome infertility and become parents. In the intriguing field of assisted reproductive technology, artificial intelligence holds great promise for carrying out laborious and repetitive activities with high reproducibility, minimal error, record keeping, and mechanical judgment [75, 76]. Broadly, with the use of AI tools together with different molecular techniques, it may be possible to develop and choose the ideal embryo for implantation in the future, standardizing conception rates globally.

While challenges persist, notable successes in access, improved affordability and financial accessibility, success rates, social awareness, economic impact, and capacity building and skill development demonstrate ART’s viability and long-term potential to transform reproductive healthcare in Africa if systemic issues are addressed. Future progress requires maintaining these gains through continued policy support, innovation, and regional collaborations.

This review is limited by the evolving nature of ART registry data in Africa and the scarcity of region-specific cost-effectiveness studies. Additionally, while molecular innovations are promising, most remain in early research or pilot phases within high-income contexts.

Conclusion

Advances in assisted reproductive technology have improved fertility outcomes in Africa, yet structural barriers continue to limit equitable access and sustained impact. Molecular biology offers powerful tools to enhance ART effectiveness, but their value in African settings depends not on rapid adoption, but on responsible, phase integration aligned with local capacity, ethical oversight, and health system readiness. Prioritizing scalable and cost-effective molecular approaches such as genomic screening and biomarker-driven diagnostics can improve clinical outcomes while safeguarding affordability and equity. More complex and ethically sensitive technologies should follow only after robust regulatory frameworks and inclusive access mechanisms are established. With coordinated investment in infrastructure, workforce development, and policy reform, molecular innovation can strengthen ART as a patient-centered and socially responsive component of reproductive healthcare in Africa, ensuring progress that is both scientifically sound and contextually appropriate.
