A pH-responsive miR-146a-loaded Gel/MSN nanocarrier enhances anti-inflammatory efficacy in allergic rhinitis through pyroptosis suppression
Authors: Limei Cui, Yujuan Yang, Saike Hou, Guangkuo Wang, Hongfei Zhao, Yan Hao, Yakui Mou, Yu Zhang, Xicheng Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAllergic rhinitis (AR) is characterized by Th2-dominant inflammation. MicroRNA-146a (miR-146a) is a critical regulator of immune balance in AR, yet its therapeutic translation is severely limited by poor stability and inefficient local delivery. This study aimed to construct a pH-responsive chitosan-coated mesoporous silica (Gel/MSN) nanocarrier for the efficient intranasal delivery of miR-146a, and to evaluate its therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms in AR.MethodsThe Gel/MSN platform was fabricated and characterized for its structural properties, miR-146a loading efficiency, and pH-triggered release profile. For in vivo evaluation, the nanocomposite was administered intranasally to an AR rat model. Therapeutic efficacy was assessed via behavioral scoring, serum IgE measurements, and the analysis of Th2 cytokines and pyroptosis-related mediators in the nasal mucosa. Biocompatibility was systematically evaluated through 15-day subacute toxicity studies and in vitro cellular assays.ResultsThe synthesized Gel/MSNs possessed a well-defined mesoporous structure with a high specific surface area, enabling efficient miR-146a loading and sustained release under weakly acidic conditions. In vivo, Gel/MSN-miR146a exhibited excellent biocompatibility and markedly alleviated acute nasal symptoms in AR rats, significantly outperforming free miR-146a in reducing serum IgE and Th2 cytokine levels. Mechanistically, the nanocarrier enhanced local miR-146a bioavailability, effectively downregulating the IRAK1/TRAF6/NF-κB axis. Crucially, this treatment strongly suppressed the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins (NLRP3, Caspase-1, GSDMD) and effector cytokines (IL-1β, IL-18). Rescue experiments with the pyroptosis activator nigericin reversed the ameliorative effects of Gel/MSN-miR146a, confirming that pyroptosis suppression is essential for its anti-AR efficacy.ConclusionsWe successfully developed a biocompatible, pH-responsive Gel/MSN nanocarrier for the efficient intranasal delivery of miR-146a. This platform exerts potent anti-AR effects via a dual mechanism: inhibiting Th2-driven inflammation and suppressing pyroptosis, offering a highly promising and translatable nanotherapeutic strategy for clinical AR management.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08272-1.Keywords: Allergic rhinitis, miR-146a, Mesoporous silica nanoparticles, pH-responsive hydrogel, Pyroptosis

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is one of the most prevalent chronic respiratory diseases, with a median prevalence of 18.1%, reaching up to 50% in certain countries [1, 2]. Despite receiving standard treatments-including pharmacotherapy and immunotherapy-some patients still exhibit poor symptom control, primarily due to the complex pathophysiology of the disease [3]. Therefore, it is essential to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms of AR, identify key factors capable of regulating multiple inflammatory pathways, and develop novel therapeutic agents targeting its pathogenesis.

MicroRNA (miR)-146a has been identified as a key immunoregulatory molecule with potent anti-inflammatory properties, playing important roles in tumor biology, immune regulation, and inflammatory disorders [4, 5]. Furthermore, beyond its role in adaptive immunity, miR-146a is a recognized negative regulator of innate immune signaling, capable of suppressing central inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB [6, 7]. Our previous studies demonstrated that miR-146a exerts anti- inflammatory effects in AR by targeting STAT5b, thereby promoting the differentiation and functional activity of regulatory T cells [8]. Other studies on the pathogenesis of AR have shown that miR-146a-5p disrupts the Smad3/GATA-3 interaction, upregulates IFN-γ, and promotes Th1 differentiation of CD4+ T cells [9]. In addition, miR-146a-5p enhances transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) production and inhibits Th2 cell polarization [10]. Collectively, accumulating evidence supports miR-146a as a promising therapeutic target for the clinical management of AR. Notably, pyroptosis, a lytic and pro-inflammatory form of cell death, contributes to the pathogenesis of AR by triggering the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 [11–13]. These cytokines, in turn, activate type II innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) and Th2 cells, promoting type 2 inflammatory responses and amplifying local immune activation [14]. Separately, in other inflammatory diseases, miR-146a has been established as a key negative regulator of pyroptosis [15]. However, whether miR-146a can modulate pyroptosis in the context of AR remains an open question.

MiRNA-based therapeutics hold great promise for treating inflammatory diseases including AR, yet their clinical application remains challenging due to poor cellular uptake, rapid degradation, and short half-lives. These properties necessitate efficient delivery systems that can protect miRNAs and enable localized, controlled release. The nasal route offers a noninvasive pathway for direct delivery to inflamed mucosa, but its mucus barrier and mildly acidic environment (pH 5.5–6.5, even lower in AR) demand pH-responsive carriers with strong stability and retention [16]. Existing nanocarriers, including liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, have shown limited success due to instability and rapid release [17]. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs), featuring large surface area, tunable porosity, and modifiable surfaces, provide a promising platform for controlled and stimuli-responsive drug delivery [18].

Therefore, this study was designed to construct a novel pH-responsive nanocomposite platform based on amine-modified MSNs (NH2-MSNs) encapsulated within a chitosan hydrogel for intranasal miR-146a delivery and to evaluate its therapeutic efficacy and safety in AR. This delivery platform, Gel/MSN-miR146a, can respond to the characteristic low-pH microenvironment in the nasal cavity, manifested by increased solubility of chitosan and protonation-induced release of loaded miR-146a from the aminated MSNs. Capitalizing on this system, we further sought to investigate the previously unverified role of miR-146a in regulating pyroptosis and to directly compare the anti-pyroptotic efficacy of our nanocomposite against free miR-146a.

Methods

Synthesis of NH2-MSNs

MSNs were synthesized by dissolving 0.5 g of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) in 60 mL of deionized water at 60 °C for 30 min. After adding 4 mL of 2 M NaOH, 1.2 mL of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) was injected dropwise and reacted at 60 °C for 24 h. The precipitate was centrifuged (10,000 ×g, 10 min), washed thrice with ethanol/water (1:1 v/v), vacuum-dried at 60 °C, and calcined. For amino- functionalization, 0.1 g of MSNs were dispersed in 50 mL of deionized water (pH 4.0). APTES was added dropwise at 80 °C under reflux. After temperature equilibration, the mixture was refluxed at 80 °C for 4 h, washed with ethanol until neutral pH, and the colloidal fraction was isolated by centrifugation (8,000 ×g, 15 min) followed by vacuum drying at 45 °C to obtain NH2-MSNs. This modification serves two critical purposes: (1) to provide positive charges for electrostatic binding of negatively charged miR-146a; (2) to confer pH-responsive properties to the nanocarrier, as amine groups undergo protonation in acidic environments.

Preparation of miR-146a loaded NH2-MSNs

MiR-146a was loaded into NH2-MSNs via electrostatic adsorption. NH2-MSNs were dispersed in nuclease-free water, mixed with synthetic miR-146a mimics and incubated under gentle agitation at 25 °C for 1 h. Subsequent centrifugation (13,000 ×g, 10 min, 4°C) separated unbound miRNAs, with supernatant discarded. The pellet was resuspended in nuclease-free PBS (pH 7.4) and centrifuged again (13,000 ×g, 10 min, 4°C) for washing. The final MSN-miR146a complex was reconstituted in nuclease-free PBS or water at 5 mg/mL nanoparticle concentration for downstream applications.

Physicochemical characterization of nanoparticles

The internal structure of the material was observed using a JEOL JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope (TEM, Tokyo, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV. The morphology of the samples was characterized using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Specific surface area was calculated by the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method using a Micromeritics TriStar II Plus 3030 automated surface area and porosity analyzer. Samples were degassed under vacuum at 200 °C for 6 h, cooled to room temperature, and then analyzed by nitrogen adsorption at 77 K. Pore size distribution was determined using the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) model. For dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements, nanoparticles were redispersed in nuclease-free water and subjected to physiochemical characterization. Hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index were determined via dynamic light scattering using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS at 25 °C with 173° backscatter detection. The zeta potential was measured in the same instrument via laser Doppler velocimetry with folded capillary cells.

Fluorescent labelling of miRNA and microscopic analysis

Synthetic miR-146a was fluorescently labeled with Cy5™ Mono NHS Ester (Ruixi Biotech) by incubation at 25 °C for 2 h with light protection. Unbound dye was removed using Hieff NGS® RNA Cleaner (12602ES01, YEASEN) per manufacturer protocol. Purified Cy5-miR-146a was prepared as 50 μM stock in nuclease-free water, aliquoted in amber tubes, and stored at −80 °C.

Determination of loading efficiency

To determine loading efficiency, different ratios of NH2-MSNs nanoparticles and Cy5-labeled miR-146a were mixed at 25 °C as described before. Flow cytometric analysis (MoFlo XDP, Beckman) detected Cy5+ nanoparticles using 640 nm excitation, with unlabeled NH2-MSNs as negative control. Loading percentage was calculated as: (FL-positive particles/total particles) × 100%.

Preparation of Gel/MSN-miR146a complexes

Chitosan powder (85% deacetylation degree, Ruixi Biotech) was dissolved in 0.5% (v/v) acetic acid/nuclease-free water under magnetic stirring 300 rpm for 2 h to prepare 1.5% (w/v) stock solution. Preformed MSN-miR146a (as described before) was mixed with chitosan solution at 25 °C under constant 200 rpm stirring. Sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP; T6383, Sigma-Aldrich) solution (0.1% w/v in nuclease-free water) was added dropwise at 0.3% (w/v) ratio to crosslink the complex. Resultant nanocomposite gel was vortexed (1,200 rpm × 30 sec) and aged at 25 °C for 8 h.

In-vitro controlled release of miRNAs

Post-synthesis complexes were washed thrice with nuclease-free PBS (pH 7.4, 13,000 ×g, 10 min) to remove unbound miRNAs. Release profiles were assessed under RNase inhibitor (N8080119, Thermo Fisher)-supplemented buffers: 0.01 M PBS (pH 8.0), 0.01 M PBS (pH 7.4), 0.1 M Sodium acetate (pH 6.0). Identical hydrogel masses were dialyzed (10 kDa MWCO, SnakeSkin) against 100 mL buffer at 33 °C with orbital shaking. Aliquots (1 mL) were sampled at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h with buffer replacement. Post-centrifugation, supernatants were analyzed using SpectraMax® i3x.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from clinically confirmed AR patients and age/sex-matched healthy controls following written informed consent. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital Affiliated to Qingdao University (Approval No: 2022–419), in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Male C57BL/6J mice and Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (6–8 weeks, SPF-grade) were procured from Jinan Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. (License No: SCXK[Lu]2023–0002). Animals were housed in specific pathogen-free SPF conditions at the Central Laboratory of Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital, with environmental parameters maintained according to Chinese National Standards GB 14,925–2010 (environmental requirements) and GB 14,924.3–2010 (nutritional standards).

Cell culture procedures

PBMCs were isolated via density gradient centrifugation (Ficoll-Paque™ Plus, GE Healthcare). Human Nasal Epithelial Cells (HNEpCs, OriCell Cat# H0-1301) and primary CD4+ T cells (Jinyuan Biotech) were commercially sourced. HNEpCs were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco Cat# C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell Cat# FSD500). CD4+ T cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco Cat# 11875500BT) with 10% FBS. For miR-146a knockdown, HNEpCs were transduced with lentiviral vectors expressing miR-146a-specific shRNA (Genepharma) at an MOI of 20 for 48 h. To model allergic inflammation, cells were stimulated with 40 μg/mL Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus group 2 allergen (Der p 2; Greer labs Cat#XPB82D3A2.5) for 24 h. All cultures were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ humidified atmosphere.

Animal models of allergic rhinitis

Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were sensitized via intraperitoneal injection of 1 mL saline suspension containing 0.3 mg ovalbumin (OVA) and 30 mg aluminum hydroxide adjuvant on days 1, 7, and 14. From days 21 to 27, daily bilateral intranasal challenge with 20 μL OVA solution (10% w/v) per nostril was performed. Nasal rubbing frequency was quantified during 15-min post-final challenge. AR-confirmed rats received bilateral intranasal administration for 7 consecutive days: 50 μL gelatin-loaded MSN (Gel/MSN) encapsulating miR-146a (2 mg/kg), 50 μL free synthetic miR-146a mimic (2 mg in saline), or 50 μL saline vehicle. For pyroptosis activation experiments, an additional treatment group was included in which AR rats received Gel/MSN-miR146a combined with nigericin. Concurrent with intranasal Gel/MSN-miR146a administration, 20 μL of 10 μM nigericin was administered intranasally to each nostril daily for 7 consecutive days. Nasal rubbing was monitored throughout treatment; specimens were collected post-euthanasia.

C57BL/6 mice were sensitized by intraperitoneal injection of 200 μL suspension containing 25 μg OVA and 2 mg aluminum hydroxide adjuvant in saline on days 1, 7, and 14. After a 7-day interval, bilateral intranasal challenge with 20 μL OVA solution (40 mg/mL in saline) per nostril was administered daily from days 21 to 27. AR-associated behaviors were video-recorded immediately post-final challenge. Phenotype-confirmed mice were randomized to receive bilateral intranasal delivery of either 50 μL AAV5-hsa-miR-146a-5p (1 × 101 3 viral particles/mL) or equivalent control AAV (empty vector). Post-7-day intervention, behavior was reassessed prior to euthanasia for collection of nasal mucosa and peripheral blood.

Nasal mucosal retention assessment

SD rats were randomized into two groups receiving intranasal administration of either 50 μL free miR-146a (2 mg) or Gel/MSN-miR146a (equivalent miR-146a content). At predetermined intervals (6, 24, 48 h post-administration), rats were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation. Nasal tissues were dissected sagittally along the septum, and imaged using an IVIS SpectrumCT system (PerkinElmer).

Transfection efficiency and cellular uptake

HNEpCs and primary CD4+ T cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured for 12 h in complete medium. miR-146a (2 μg/mL) was complexed with Gel/MSN or used as free mimic. Cells were incubated with complexes for 12 h at 37°C/5% CO₂. Fluorescence microscopy (ECHO Revolve) captured images.

Cell viability assay

HNEpCs and primary CD4+ T cells were seeded in 96-well plates and pre-cultured for 12 h. Cells were treated with: Gel/MSN nanocomposite (50 μg/mL), Gel/MSN-miR-NC (negative control, 50 μg/mL carrier + 50 nM scramble microRNA), Gel/MSN-miR 146a (50 μg/mL carrier + 50 nM miR-146a). After 48 h incubation, 10% (v/v) CCK-8 reagent (K1018, ApexBio) in serum-free medium was added. Following 1.5 h incubation at 37 °C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using SpectraMax® i3x.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

PBMCs and HNEpCs were harvested 48 h post-transfection with Gel/MSN-miR NC or Gel/MSN-miR146a. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol™ (15596026, Invitrogen) and quantified via NanoDrop™ ND-2000. Nasal mucosa tissues were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, homogenized in TRIzol using Tissuelyser (Jingxin) at 60 Hz for 1 min, then processed identically. Reverse transcription of 2 μg RNA employed HiScript® III RT SuperMix (R323, Vazyme), for miRNA, miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (11148ES50, Yeasen) was used, qPCR reactions (20 μL) used ChamQ™ SYBR Master Mix (Q331, Vazyme) on Bio-Rad CFX96 under: 95°C/30 s; 40 cycles of 95°C/5 s, 60°C/30 s. Endogenous controls: β-actin (mRNA) and U6 (miRNA). Species-specific primer sequences are tabulated in Table 1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Peripheral blood was collected from experimental groups into non-anticoagulant tubes and allowed to clot at 4 °C for ≥4 h. Serum was isolated by centrifugation (12,000 ×g, 10 min, 4°C). Murine nasal tissues were snap-frozen, then homogenized in PBS using a tissue homogenizer (Jingxin) at 60 Hz for 1 minute. Protein levels of IL-1β (EK301B, MULTI SCIENCE; ERC007.48, NeoBioscience), IL-18 (EK318, MULTI SCIENCE; ERC010.48, NeoBioscience), GSDMD (JL46371, JONLINBIO), IgE (M250110-117a, NeoBioscience; D731065, BBI), IL-4 (JYM0011Mo), and IL-5 (JYM0191M, jymbio; JL 20,895, JONLINBIO) in serum were quantified using commercial ELISA kits per manufacturer protocols. Absorbance readings at 450 nm were acquired on SpectraMax® i3x, with analyte concentrations calculated against standard curves.

Western blot analysis

HNEpCs with miR-146a knockdown were lysed in RIPA buffer (R0010, Solarbio) with discontinuous ultrasonication (3 s on/5 s off, 40% amplitude, 1 min on ice), incubated 30 min on ice, then centrifuged (12,000 ×g, 15 min, 4 °C; Eppendorf 5418 R). Murine nasal tissues were flash-frozen, homogenized in RIPA using grinder (60 Hz, 1 min; jingxin), and processed identically. Protein supernatants were quantified via BCA kit (PC0020, Solarbio), denatured in 4× Loading buffer (95 °C, 5 min), and 20 μg/lane separated by 12% SDS-PAGE (Mini-PROTEAN Tetra, Bio-Rad). Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (IPFL00010, Millipore), blocked in 5% non-fat milk/TBST (0.1% Tween-20) for 2 h at 25 °C, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: IRAK1 (ab238, Abcam, 1:1000), TRAF6 (ab40675, Abcam, 1:1000), phospho-NF-kappaB p65 (Ser536)(p-NF-κB p65, 3033, CST, 1:1000), NLRP3 (ab263899, Abcam, 1:1000), GSDMD (PA5-116815, Thermo fisher, 1:1000), IL-1β (16806–1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), Caspase-1 (22915–1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), GAPDH (60004–1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:2000). After TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at 25 °C. Signals were developed with SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS (34579, Thermo Scientific) and imaged on Tanon 5200 Multi (Tanon). Band intensities were quantified by Image J (v1.53, NIH).

Histopathological evaluation

Rat nasal cavities were excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; BS044, Biosharp) for 24 h at 25 °C. After decalcification in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4; E117, Solarbio) for 21 days at 4 °C, tissues were paraffin-embedded and sectioned coronally at 3 μm thickness. Consecutive sections were stained with Hematoxylin & Eosin (H&E) and Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining. For H&E staining, 5 μm-thick deparaffinized sections stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (5 min) and eosin (3 min) for eosinophil quantification in lamina propria. For PAS staining, Deparaffinized sections oxidized in 0.5% periodic acid (15 min), stained with Schiff’s reagent (20 min, RT) to quantify goblet cell density (mucin-secreting cells) in respiratory epithelium.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software 10.0, commencing with normality assessment via Shapiro-Wilk test (α = 0.05). For comparisons between two groups, Student’s unpaired t-test (two-tailed) was applied to parametric data, for paired measurements paired t-test was employed. Multi-group comparisons employed one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for single-factor experiments or two-way ANOVA with Šidák’s multiple comparisons for factorial designs. Data derived from a minimum of three independent biological replicates are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Preparation and characterization of chitosan-coated mesoporous silica nanoparticles

NH2-MSNs and Gel/MSN-miR146a were synthesized via chemical methods. Morphological characterization was performed using transmission electron microscopy and scanning electron microscopy, confirming uniform nanostructures (Fig. 1A). Pore structure properties of the NH2-MSNs were characterized by nitrogen adsorption-desorption analysis. The material exhibited a BET specific surface area of 272.65 m2/g (Fig. 1B). The BJH adsorption average pore diameter was calculated to be 18.26 nm (Supplemental Fig. 1). These results confirm that the as-synthesized NH2-MSNs possess a well-defined mesoporous structure, large specific surface area, and high pore volume. Given that mature miR-146a has a molecular weight of 7.14 kDa and a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 2.2 nm, these amino-modified mesoporous silica nanoparticles provide sufficient internal space for efficient nucleic acid loading, making them highly suitable as effective gene delivery carriers. MiR-146a or scrambled control miRNA was complexed with NH2-MSNs at varying ratios in nuclease-free water. After a 60-min incubation at room temperature and centrifugation to remove unbound miRNA, zeta potential and hydrodynamic diameter were measured (Fig. 1C). The results indicated that the zeta potential gradually decreased with an increasing amount of loaded miR-146a. Furthermore, the binding of negatively charged miR-146a to the surface and interior pores of the NH2-MSNs via electrostatic interactions increased the thickness of the particle’s hydration layer and induced mild inter-particle cross-linking. This led to a moderate increase in the hydrodynamic size and a bigger size distribution. The fluorescence intensity of Cy5-labeled MSN-miR146a complexes was quantified by flow cytometry (Fig. 1D). Results demonstrated efficient miRNA loading by NH2-MSNs, with optimal loading efficiency achieved at a 1:1 miRNA:carrier mass ratio.To facilitate intranasal administration, the complexes were subsequently incorporated into a chitosan-based hydrogel. pH-responsive release profiles of Gel/MSN-miR146a were evaluated in buffers at different pH values. Sustained miR-146a release occurred at pH 6.0, while minimal release was observed at pH 7.4 and 8.0 (Fig. 1E).This pH-dependent behavior is likely because, at neutral pH, the positively charged NH2-MSNs stably bind to the negatively charged miR-146a via electrostatic interactions. However, under weakly acidic conditions, the surface amine groups undergo marked protonation, which disrupts these electrostatic interactions. Meanwhile, the chitosan matrix also becomes protonated and solubilized. Together, these two events trigger the efficient, pH-responsive release of miR-146a. For in vivo tracking, SD rats received intranasal administration of either Gel/MSN-miR146a or free miR-146a. Analysis using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) revealed prolonged nasal retention of Gel/MSN-miR146a with detectable fluorescence signals persisting at 48 h post-administration. In contrast, free miR-146a showed rapid clearance (only weak fluorescence detectable at 4 h) (Fig. 1F). The pH-responsive release profile and prolonged nasal retention of this system highlight its pharmacokinetic advantages for local nasal delivery. Critically, no fluorescent signals were detected in heart, liver, lung, spleen, or kidney tissues at any timepoint, indicating an absence of systemic accumulation(Supplemental Fig. 2). This qualitative biodistribution profile demonstrates the favorable local targeting capability of the Gel/MSN platform, which minimizes systemic exposure and potential off-target effects.

Biosafety evaluation of chitosan-mesoporous silica nanoparticles in vivo and in vitro

To assess the biosafety of Gel/MSN-miR146a, wild-type SD rats were divided into two groups receiving intranasal administration of either Gel/MSN-miR146a or saline (control) every other day for 15 days. Histopathological examination by H&E staining revealed no significant pathological alterations in major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney, of treated animals compared to controls on day 15 (Fig. 2A). H&E and PAS staining of the rat nasal mucosal showed that the nasal mucosal epithelium remained structurally intact, with no pathological changes such as significant inflammatory cell infiltration or goblet cell hyperplasia (Fig. 2B). These findings confirmed that intranasal administration of Gel/MSN-miR146a results in favorable local tissue compatibility and no detectable mucosal toxicity. Complete blood count analysis confirmed that all hematological parameters – including white blood cells, red blood cells, hemoglobin, and platelets – remained within normal physiological ranges (Fig. 2C). Combined with the organ distribution results in Supplemental Fig. 2, these data collectively demonstrate the favorable in vivo safety of Gel/MSN-miR146a. For in vitro assessment, HNEpCs and human CD4+ T-lymphocytes were treated with Gel/MSN-miR146a. Fluorescence microscopy after 48 h demonstrated successful cellular uptake of Cy5-labeled nanoparticles in both cell types, accompanied by significant upregulation of miR-146a expression (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, CCK-8 assays confirmed no significant cytotoxicity for either blank Gel/MSN carriers or Gel/MSN-miR146a complexes at therapeutic concentrations (Fig. 2E). Taken together, the results from the 15-day in vivo subacute toxicity study and in vitro cellular experiments indicate that the Gel/MSN-miR146a platform exhibits favorable biocompatibility at the therapeutic dose and within the specified administration cycle.

Chitosan-mesoporous silica nanoparticles effectively reduce AR symptoms by suppressing type 2 inflammation

AR model rats were treated with either free miR-146a or Gel/MSN-miR146a via intranasal administration (Fig. 3A). After 7 consecutive days of treatment, behavioral assessment revealed that both free miR-146a and Gel/MSN-miR146a significantly reduced nasal scratching frequency compared to untreated controls. Notably, no statistically significant difference in nasal scratching frequency was observed between the Gel/MSN-miR146a and free miR-146a groups (Fig. 3B). This indicates that both treatments provide comparable efficacy in alleviating acute behavioral symptoms of AR. Importantly, while acute behavioral symptom relief was comparable, the superiority of the Gel/MSN-miR146a platform became evident at the molecular and biochemical levels, demonstrating a substantially stronger inhibitory effect on systemic and local inflammatory markers. ELISA analysis of peripheral blood demonstrated significantly decreased serum IgE levels in both treatment groups, with Gel/MSN-miR146a exhibiting greater reduction than free miR-146a (Fig. 3C). Notably, IL-5 levels in serum were significantly lower in the Gel/MSN-miR146a group than in the free miR-146a group (Fig. 3D). Similarly, Gel/MSN-miR146a treatment markedly reduced IL-13 and TNF-α expression in nasal mucosa compared with free miR-146a (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, histopathological evaluation via H&E and PAS staining confirmed amelioration of AR-induced pathological changes in the nasal mucosa for both treatment groups (Fig. 3F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that while free miRNA is sufficient for acute behavioral symptom management, the Gel/MSN-miR146a delivery system possesses a superior ability to suppress Th2 cytokines and modulate the underlying molecular inflammatory profile in AR.

Chitosan-mesoporous silica nanoparticles effectively suppress pyroptosis activation

This efficacy prompted us to investigate whether our platform conferred a broader anti-inflammatory profile by targeting upstream initiating events. qPCR analysis was performed to quantify miR-146a expression levels in nasal mucosal tissues following treatment. The results showed that miR-146a expression was increased by 1.81 ± 0.53-fold in the free miR-146a group, whereas a significantly higher upregulation (2.94 ± 1.02-fold) was observed in the Gel/MSN-miR146a group (Fig. 4A). To further elucidate the regulatory role of miR-146a, we examined the protein levels of its downstream targets. As shown in Figs. 4B and 4C, compared with the free miR-146a group, Gel/MSN-miR146a induced a more pronounced downregulation of IRAK1 and TRAF6, and more effectively inhibited the phosphorylation of NF-κB p65. Collectively, these data indicate that the Gel/MSN-miR146a delivery system can effectively enhance the bioavailability of miR-146a, thereby strengthening its downstream immunomodulatory effects.

Given the established role of pyroptosis as a potent amplifier of inflammation, we assessed its activation status in the nasal mucosa of AR rats. ELISA quantification revealed that the Gel/MSN-miR146a group exhibited significantly greater reduction in pyroptosis effector cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 compared to the free miR-146a group (Fig. 4D). However, the protein levels of key pyroptotic mediators, including NLRP3, GSDMD, and Caspase-1, were significantly downregulated in both treatment groups compared to the AR model group, with no statistically significant difference observed between the two treatment modalities (Figs. 4E,4F). This apparent discrepancy likely occurs because the tissue levels of terminal cytokines (IL-1β, IL-18) reflect cumulative downstream processes—including cleavage efficiency, amplification cascades, and clearance dynamics—rather than solely upstream protein expression, thus providing a more accurate measure of the pathway’s final functional output.

To firmly establish a causal relationship between the therapeutic effect of Gel/MSN-miR146a and pyroptosis inhibition, we conducted a rescue experiment in AR rat models by administering Gel/MSN-miR146a in combination with nigericin, a specific activator of pyroptosis. Notably, the therapeutic benefits of Gel/MSN-miR146a were completely abrogated by nigericin treatment. Specifically, compared to treatment with Gel/MSN-miR146a alone, nigericin administration led to a significant increase in nasal scratching frequency (Fig. 5A), a marked rebound in serum IgE and IL-5 levels (Fig. 5B), and a significant upregulation of IL-13 and TNF-α mRNA expression in the nasal mucosa (Fig. 5C). Pathological examination revealed that administration of the pyroptosis activator nigericin reversed the ameliorative effects of Gel/MSN-miR146a on nasal epithelial architecture and goblet cell hyperplasia, and restored pathological injury in the nasal mucosa to levels comparable to that in the AR model group (Fig. 5D). Taken together, these results directly confirm that the therapeutic efficacy of Gel/MSN-miR146a in AR is dependent on its ability to inhibit the pyroptosis pathway, providing definitive experimental evidence for the causal link between pyroptosis suppression and the anti-AR effects of our nanoplatform.

miR-146a inversely correlates with pyroptosis markers in clinical samples and AR mouse models

Given the demonstrated inhibitory effect of miR-146a on pyroptosis in our therapeutic experiments using the AR rat model, we next sought to determine whether this regulatory association is clinically relevant and conserved across species. To address this, we investigated the expression levels of miR-146a and pyroptotic mediators in PBMCs isolated from AR patients, as well as in an independent OVA-induced AR mouse model. In the PBMCs of AR patients, miR-146a expression was significantly decreased compared with healthy controls, whereas IL-1β, IL-18, and GSDMD mRNA levels were markedly increased (Fig. 6A). Similarly, in the OVA-sensitized AR mouse model, reduced miR-146a expression and protein levels of IL-1β, IL-18, and GSDMD were observed in both PBMCs and nasal mucosa tissues (Fig. 6B,C). Correlation analysis further revealed a significant inverse relationship between miR-146a expression and pyroptosis-related markers in nasal mucosa of AR mouse model (Fig. 6D). The inverse correlation between miR-146a and pyroptotic markers observed in both human clinical specimens and mouse models is consistent with the functional outcomes from our AR rat therapeutic model. This cross-species consistency strongly reinforces the conclusion that miR-146a serves as a functional negative regulator of pyroptosis in allergic rhinitis, further validating the clinical relevance of our earlier mechanistic findings.

Discussion

MiR-146a, a key modulator of adaptive immune responses and T-cell activation, is one of the most highly expressed miRNAs in regulatory T (Treg) cells. [19] Despite its well-recognized therapeutic potential, the clinical translation of miR-146a is severely limited by its rapid degradation in biological fluids and poor nasal mucosal retention. In this study, we developed a novel nanodelivery system tailored to the acidic nasal microenvironment of allergic rhinitis (AR) patients for the stable and targeted delivery of miR-146a. Experimental validation confirmed that the Gel/MSN-miR146a platform exhibits favorable physicochemical properties, excellent biosafety, and sustained anti-inflammatory efficacy in alleviating AR symptoms and type 2 inflammation. Consequently, it demonstrates an improved therapeutic profile that may facilitate future translational potential compared to free miR-146a, although comprehensive preclinical evaluations are still required. Capitalizing on this effective platform, we further uncovered a previously unreported dimension of miR-146a function in AR: its role as a functional negative regulator of pyroptosis.

It is well established that free RNA molecules are highly susceptible to degradation and often fail to reach disease sites efficiently [20]. Researchers are currently exploring various nanocarriers to deliver RNA, protect it from degradation, improve clinical outcomes at lower doses, and fully realize its therapeutic potential [21]. Most existing studies on nasal miRNA nanodelivery focus on crossing the blood–brain barrier for neurological disorders [22]. Although some research has explored airway inflammation—such as using miR-143a–overexpressing MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-EVs) to restore nasal barrier function in allergic rhinitis—reports remain scarce, underscoring the significance of advancing miRNA nanodelivery strategies for the upper airway [23].

MiR-146a has also been reported as an important therapeutic target for airway delivery. For instance, Su et al. utilized PEG–PLA nanoparticles to deliver miR-146a for AR treatment. Their composite gel/NPs/miR-146a formulation was designed to release miR-146a in response to a specific nasal temperature of 33 °C. However, nasal temperature fluctuates with environmental conditions, which may lead to inconsistent performance of such temperature-sensitive gel materials [24]. In a lower airway study, Adel et al. adsorbed miR-146a onto nanoparticles composed of poly (glycerol adipate-co-ω-pentadecalactone) (PGA-co-PDL) incorporating the cationic lipid DOTAP (dioleoyltrimethylammonium propane) to reduce IRAK1 expression in a COPD animal model [25]. In addition, in studies of group 2 innate lymphoid cell (ILC2)-dominant allergic airway inflammation, MSC-EVs overexpressing miR-146a were shown to partially prevent inflammatory cell infiltration and mucus hypersecretion in the lungs [26].

In our study, we focused on the low-pH microenvironment of the AR nasal cavity (pH 5.5–6.5) as a unique trigger for controlled drug release. Our pH-sensitive release system is based on a synergistic dual-component mechanism combining an amine-modified MSN core and a chitosan gel coating, a strategy widely recognized for controlled drug delivery in nanomedicine [27–29]. Our results demonstrated that the Gel/MSN-miR146a platform exhibited a favorable pH-responsive release profile, with significantly greater release of miR-146a observed at pH 6.0 compared to pH 7.4 and 8.0. At physiological pH, amine groups on the MSN pore surface are only partially protonated, allowing strong electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged miR-146a. Additionally, the chitosan coating remains in a collapsed state, acting as a physical barrier to prevent premature miRNA leakage. In the slightly acidic AR nasal microenvironment, both the amine groups on the MSN surface and the chitosan coating undergo extensive protonation. This triggers two synergistic effects that promote rapid miR-146a release: first, increased electrostatic repulsion between the positively charged amine groups and the partially protonated miR-146a phosphate groups; and second, the swelling of the chitosan gel coating due to charge repulsion, which opens the mesopore entrances and facilitates miRNA diffusion. This synergistic pH-responsive design ensures preferential miR-146a release at the target site, maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects. These findings are consistent with recent advances in pH-sensitive mesoporous silica nanodevices [30–32], and our approach extends these concepts to nasal miRNA delivery, providing a versatile platform for treating inflammatory airway diseases.

The rational design of our nanocarrier directly translated into superior therapeutic efficacy compared to free miR-146a. While both treatments effectively managed acute behavioral symptoms without statistically significant differences, the nanoplatform exhibited distinct superiority in modulating the underlying inflammatory microenvironment. Quantitative qPCR analysis confirmed that Gel/MSN-miR146a delivery resulted in 2.3-fold higher levels of mature miR-146a in the nasal mucosa than free miR-146a, and concomitantly induced a more pronounced downregulation of miR-146a’s downstream target genes. This enhanced mucosal bioavailability and the subsequent downregulation of inflammation-regulatory genes led to marked reductions in key AR biomarkers, including IgE, IL‑5, IL‑13, and TNF‑α, in the AR rat model.

Another important finding of this study is the identification of the key mechanism underlying miR-146a’s therapeutic efficacy in AR. Gel/MSN-miR146a exerts its anti-inflammatory effects through a dual-suppression mechanism: it not only mitigates downstream Type 2 adaptive immune responses by inhibiting cytokines such as IL-13 and TNF-α, but also blocks upstream innate immune inflammation by regulating the pyroptosis pathway.

Our data showed that both free miR-146a and Gel/MSN-miR146a treatments significantly downregulated the expression of key upstream pyroptotic proteins (NLRP3, GSDMD, and Caspase-1) in AR rat models, indicating that both formulations effectively inhibit the core initiation step of the pyroptosis pathway-a critical mechanistic basis for alleviating AR symptoms. However, the Gel/MSN-miR146a platform was significantly more effective at reducing the levels of terminal pyroptotic effector cytokines, IL-1β and IL-18. As previously noted, the tissue levels of these terminal effectors are governed by complex downstream amplification cascades and clearance dynamics rather than just upstream protein expression [33, 34].

We attribute this superior downstream inhibition to the unique physical properties of our nanoplatform. Leveraging the mucoadhesive properties of chitosan, the Gel/MSN-miR146a platform achieves prolonged nasal retention (>48 hours) and sustained miR-146a release. This allows for a continuous blockade of the pyroptosis-mediated inflammatory amplification cascade within the AR microenvironment, resulting in significantly greater inhibition of terminal effector cytokines compared to free miR-146a, which is rapidly cleared after a single administration.

Furthermore, the in vivo rescue experiment using the specific NLRP3 activator nigericin provides definitive causal evidence that the therapeutic efficacy of Gel/MSN-miR146a is critically dependent on the inhibition of NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis. This conclusion was further validated in two independent experimental systems: human clinical samples and an independent OVA-induced AR mouse model. This cross-species validation not only strengthens the scientific rigor of our findings that miR-146a negatively regulates pyroptosis in AR, but also supports the potential clinical relevance of our study, providing a strong foundation for future translational investigations.

Notably, the Gel/MSN platform also exhibited an excellent safety profile. Recent clinical data indicate that numerous MSN-based nanomaterials have advanced to Phase I and II clinical trials, demonstrating substantial translational potential [35–37]. Naturally derived chitosan also offers significant advantages for clinical translation due to its excellent biodegradability, low immunogenicity, and strong mucoadhesive properties, which are particularly beneficial for our nasal delivery application [38]. Qualitative in vivo imaging in our study showed that Gel/MSN-miR146a was primarily retained in the nasal mucosa following intranasal administration, with no detectable fluorescence signal in major organs (including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) up to 48 hours post-administration, indicating minimal systemic exposure. Additionally, the 15-day repeated-dose toxicity study demonstrated normal hematological parameters and no pathological changes in major organs, preliminarily ruling out acute and subacute toxicity risks.

Our study has several limitations. First, the MSNs used in this study had a size range of 70–100 nm, therefore, it is essential to further investigate whether variations in MSN size affect loading efficiency and cytotoxicity. Furthermore, achieving precise control over MSN size during large-scale production may present manufacturing challenges [39]. Second, in terms of safety evaluation, while this study completed qualitative biodistribution analysis and a 15-day subacute toxicity study confirming the short-term safety of the platform, quantitative pharmacokinetic parameters (e.g., nasal retention half-life, mucosal clearance rate) and 90-day long-term toxicity studies (including immunotoxicity, reproductive toxicity, and genotoxicity) remain to be completed to establish a comprehensive safety profile meeting clinical submission requirements. Finally, the precise molecular mechanisms through which miR-146a regulates pyroptosis require further elucidation. Collectively, these uncompleted evaluations are critical prerequisites to advance this platform toward clinical translation.

Conclusion

In summary, the developed pH-responsive Gel/MSN-miR146a platform not only effectively alleviates AR symptoms but also establishes a clear mechanistic link between the amelioration of AR pathology and pyroptosis suppression. This innovative nanocomposite addresses the key limitations of free miRNA delivery in preclinical AR models, and represents potential preclinical candidate for AR management. Future studies will focus on establishing a comprehensive pharmacokinetic profile, evaluating the long-term biosafety of this delivery system and elucidating the precise molecular interactions between miR-146a and the pyroptosis signaling cascade.
