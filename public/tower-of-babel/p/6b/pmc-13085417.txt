Serum and CSF neurofilament light chain and glial fibrillary acidic protein levels in idiopathic intracranial hypertension
Authors: Nik Krajnc, Markus Ponleitner, Omar Keritam, Jan Philipp Nolte, Martin Bertich, Nina Müller, Christoph Stapf, Sina Zaic, Stefan Macher, Wolfgang Marik, Klaus Novak, Christian Wöber, Berthold Pemp, Gabriel Bsteh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is a neurological disorder marked by increased intracranial pressure (ICP). Neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) are established biomarkers of neuroaxonal degeneration and astroglial activation that typically show strong serum-cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) concordance. However, their behaviour in IIH and the influence of increased ICP on CSF-serum relationship remains poorly understood. The aim of this study was to investigate serum and CSF NfL and GFAP levels in IIH, examine their CSF-serum coupling, and assess associations with ophthalmological outcomes.MethodsIn this prospective observational study, newly diagnosed people with IIH (pwIIH) underwent baseline serum and CSF sampling. NfL and GFAP levels were quantified using single-molecule array (Simoa→) technology, with age-, BMI-, and sex-adjustment. CSF-to-serum quotients (QNfL, QGFAP) and albumin-normalized indices were calculated. Outcome measures included: papilledema degree, visual acuity, perimetric mean deviation on automated perimetry, peripapillary retinal nerve fibre layer and macular ganglion cell layer thickness measured by optical coherence tomography, and arachnoid optic nerve sheath diameter measured by transbulbar sonography.ResultsThirty pwIIH (mean age 34.1 [SD 10.0], 100.0% female, mean CSF opening pressure [OP] 34.1 cmCSF [3.9], median BMI 35.4 kg/m2 [IQR 32.7–40.8]) were included. We did not observe any relevant associations between fluid biomarkers and ophthalmological outcomes at any time point. Notably, CSF NfL and GFAP levels did not correlate with respective serum levels. In contrast, serum NfL and GFAP levels were inversely associated with CSF OP, and QNfL and QGFAP showed positive associations with CSF OP (QNfL: β = 9.28; 95% CI 2.82, 15.73; p = 0.007; QGFAP: β = 13.00; 95% CI 0.12, 25.88; p = 0.048).ConclusionsNeither NfL nor GFAP levels seem particularly promising as biomarkers for prediction of ophthalmological outcomes in IIH. However, the observed dissociation between serum and CSF biomarkers supports altered CSF dynamics, rather than neuroaxonal or astrocytic injury, as the dominant pathophysiological drivers in the active phase of IIH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02323-0.

Introduction

Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is a neurological disorder characterized by elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) and a substantial risk of irreversible visual impairment. Although its precise pathophysiology remains incompletely understood, accumulating evidence suggests impaired cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) absorption and increased outflow resistance may play an important role in ICP elevation [1, 2]. Visual impairment in IIH arises from pressure-related injury to the optic nerve, as elevated ICP is transmitted along the surrounding subarachnoid space, leading to axoplasmic flow stasis, papilledema, and, in severe or prolonged cases, axonal loss [3].

Neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) are well-established biomarkers of neuroaxonal injury and astroglial activation, respectively, and can be quantified in both CSF and serum. In most neurological disorders, serum levels are assumed to reliably reflect CSF levels, supporting their use as minimally invasive biomarkers of central nervous system (CNS) pathology [4, 5]. This assumption, however, relies on intact CSF circulation and preserved CSF-blood exchange, conditions that may be disrupted in disorders characterized by elevated ICP and altered CSF dynamics.

In IIH, emerging evidence suggests that this assumption may not hold. Previous studies have reported associations between CSF NfL levels and CSF opening pressure (OP), whereas corresponding serum levels showed weaker or absent relationships [6, 7]. A similar pattern has been described for GFAP, with elevated CSF but not serum levels in people with IIH (pwIIH) [7]. These observations raise the possibility that altered CSF dynamics affect the clearance and distribution of CNS-derived proteins, thereby limiting their interpretability of both CSF- and serum-based biomarkers in this disease context.

Importantly, if IIH were to result in true neuroaxonal damage or astroglial activation beyond transient axoplasmic flow disturbance, this would be expected to manifest as increased release of NfL and/or GFAP, with corresponding associations to both functional and structural ophthalmological outcomes. However, the absence of such correlations in a given cohort would not necessarily exclude the presence of axonal injury, as biomarker detectability may depend on disease severity, duration, and the spatial extent of damage. Accordingly, fluid biomarker alterations in IIH may reflect a spectrum ranging from transient pressure-related effects to structural injury, the relative contribution of which remains to be clarified.

Against this background, the present study aimed to systematically characterize CSF and serum NfL and GFAP levels in newly diagnosed pwIIH, assess the degree of coupling between compartments, and examine their associations with structural and functional ophthalmological outcomes. By integrating biomarker profiling with clinically relevant visual measures in the context of altered CSF dynamics, this work seeks to clarify the extent to which NfL and GFAP provide meaningful insight into optic nerve injury during the active phase of IIH.

Methods

Study cohort and study design

For this prospective observational study, we included untreated, newly diagnosed pwIIH according to Korsbaek criteria from an ongoing prospective observational cohort study (Vienna Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension Biomarker study [VIIH-BIO]), jointly conducted by the Department of Neurology and the Department of Ophthalmology at the Medical University of Vienna starting in January 2021 [8]. Briefly, standardized VIIH-BIO case reports include demographic data, body weight, disease specific parameters, as well as documentation of diagnostic and therapeutic procedures. All examinations are performed by specialized neurologists and neuro-ophthalmologists. All pwIIH are treated in accordance with best practice based on the Austrian consensus recommendations [9].

Symptom duration was defined as the time between the onset of symptoms and the diagnostic lumbar puncture (LP). Data were collected within a 7-day window prior to LP, designated as baseline (M0), and subsequently at three (M3), six (M6), and twelve (M12) months of follow-up. For the purposes of this study, patients diagnosed with IIH without papilledema (IIH-WOP) were excluded from subsequent analyses.

Lumbar puncture

A standardized diagnostic LP was performed using a Quincke type point, 20GA 3.50 IN, 0.9 × 88 mm (Braun Melsungen AG, Germany) spinal needle with a spinal manometer (Rocket Medical®, UK). LP was performed in the left lateral decubitus position with the legs repositioned after dural puncture to reduce abdominal compression. A stabilized CSF OP reading was recorded as fluctuations in the manometer settled. After measuring CSF OP, CSF was drained until a target of 10–15 cm CSF was reached (maximum of 50 ml CSF drained). CSF closing pressure was recorded prior to removal of the spinal needle.

Ophthalmological assessment

Fundoscopy was executed using the Frisén staging scale to rate papilledema severity and categorize the swelling of optic discs from stage 0 (no papilledema) to stage 5 (severe papilledema) [10]. Based on papilledema severity, patients were stratified into two groups: mild papilledema, corresponding to Frisén grades 1–2, and moderate to severe papilledema, defined as Frisén grade ≥ 3. This dichotomization was performed due to the limited number of patients with higher Frisén grades, which precluded statistically meaningful analyses across the full ordinal scale.

Best-corrected visual acuity was assessed using Sloan charts at distance after subjective refraction. Results were given in logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) [11].

Automated visual field testing (Humphrey Field Analyzer, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany), using 30 − 2 Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm (SITA) standard protocols, was performed, quantifying the mean deviation (MD) in decibels (dB) of all test locations compared to age-matched controls and defining abnormal perimetry as an MD lower than − 2.0 dB [10, 12].

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging was performed without pupil dilation in both eyes of each person using spectral-domain OCT (Spectralis OCT, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany; software Heidelberg eye explorer software version 6.9a) adhering to the OSCAR-IB quality control criteria and describing findings in accordance with the APOSTEL criteria [13, 14]. For peripapillary retinal nerve fibre layer (pRNFL) measurement, a 12° (3.4 mm) ring scan centred on the optic nerve head was used (1536 A-scans, automatic real-time tracking [ART]: 100 averaged frames) [15]. Macular ganglion cell layer (GCL) thickness was measured by means of a 20°×20° volume scan (centred on the fovea with 512 A-scans and 25 B-scans aligned vertically with 16 averaged frames). GCL thickness was defined as the mean layer thickness of the four inner and outer quadrants of the circular grid centred around the foveola corresponding to the 3 and 6 mm rings as defined by the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study, excluding the central 1 mm diameter zone from the volumetric scan [16]. Image processing was semiautomated using the built-in proprietary software for automated layer segmentation and manual correction of obvious errors. For statistical analyses, the eye with greater functional impairment, as indicated by a more severe visual field defect, was selected for each participant.

Transbulbar sonography (ABSolu, Quantel Medical, Cournon d’Auvergne, France) was performed after topical anaesthesia with oxybuprocaine eye drops. Optic nerve sheaths were qualitatively assessed by transverse and longitudinal B-scans using a 20 MHz annular probe placed temporally on the eyeball to verify the presence of a bat sign indicating perineural CSF congestion. For quantitative measurement of arachnoid optic nerve sheath diameter (AONSD) standardized amplitude modulation (A-scan) echography with tissue sensitivity settings was performed, placing the 8 MHz parallel beam A-scan probe on the temporal eye equator in primary gaze position [17, 18]. Standardized A-scan echography enables a more accurate measurement of AONSD than calliper measurements from B-scans, which lack standardized settings and are influenced by the applied signal gain [19]. At least two measurements were taken within 3 mm of the posterior bulb wall, and the highest was documented as the diameter.

Neurofilament light chain and glial fibrillary acidic protein levels

NfL and GFAP measurements were performed by an investigator (MP) blinded to outcome measures. After thawing the samples for 60 min, the multiplex assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions and protocol using the Simoa Neurology 2 Plex B assay kits, calibrators, and consumables in the Simoa SR-X Analyzer (Quanterix, Lexington, MA, USA). Briefly, duplicate calibrators and controls, and single samples were equilibrated to room temperature and dispensed in 96-well plates. Subsequently, incubation with detectors and paramagnetic bead solutions was performed according to the kit instructions. Washing and incubation were undertaken in the Simoa microplate incubator and washer. Sample readout and concentration analysis were executed on the Quanterix SR-X Analyzer as previously desribed [20]. Standardized z-scores for sNfL and GFAP, adjusted for age and body mass index, as well as sex in the case of GFAP were calculated [21]. BMI values exceeding 66.1 were capped at 66.1 to align with the reference cohort.

The NfL quotient (QNfL) was calculated as CSF NfL level divided by sNfL level, and the GFAP quotient (QGFAP) as CSF GFAP/sGFAP. To account for the blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, we derived the NfL index as QNfL normalized to the albumin quotient (Qalb), and analogously computed the GFAP index as QGFAP/Qalb.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, patient consents, and reporting

The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Medical University Vienna (ethical approval number: 2216/2020). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study adheres to the reporting guidelines outlined within the ‘Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement’.

Data availability

Data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request by a qualified researcher and upon approval by the ethics committee and the data-clearing committee of the Medical University Vienna.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R (Version 4.5.0). Categorial variables were expressed in absolute frequencies and percentages, continuous variables were reported depending on their distribution as mean and standard deviation (SD) or as median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Continuous variables were tested for normal distribution by the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Univariate comparisons were performed using Pearson’s chi-square test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Univariate correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficient, as appropriate.

Linear regression models were used to assess the association between fluid biomarkers (independent variables: CSF NfL, sNfL, QNfL, NfL index, CSF GFAP, sGFAP, QGFAP, GFAP index) and outcome measures at baseline (dependent variables: pRNFL thickness, GCL volume, AONSD), adjusting for age, BMI, and CSF OP. For longitudinal analyses, linear mixed-effects models were estimated to evaluate the association between repeated measures of outcome measures and each fluid biomarker over time, accounting for within-subject correlation and adjusting for covariates (age, BMI, CSF OP). To evaluate the relationship between CSF OP (independent variable) and fluid biomarkers (dependent variables: CSF NfL, sNfL, QNfL, NfL index, CSF GFAP, sGFAP, QGFAP, GFAP), linear regression analyses were conducted, adjusting for age and BMI. Given the modest sample size, analyses examining associations between fluid biomarkers and ophthalmological outcomes were considered exploratory.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings by refitting the models with a random intercept at the patient level and incorporating data from both eyes, thereby accounting for inter-eye correlation within individuals. Correction for multiple testing was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate. The significance level was set at a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

MRI venography (MRV) showed unilateral or bilateral venous sinus stenosis (combined conduit score [CSS] ≥ 2) in 88.2%, with 11.8% exhibiting sinus hypoplasia (CSS 4). Prognostic value of fluid biomarkers

CSF levels of NfL and GFAP showed no correlation with any of the ophthalmological outcome parameters at any time point (Suppl. Table 2). In multivariable models, CSF NfL and GFAP levels were still not associated with any of the ophthalmological outcomes at any time point (Suppl. Table 3). A borderline significant association between CSF GFAP levels and baseline pRNFL thickness did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for multiple testing and was not seen at subsequent timepoints.

Looking at serum biomarker levels, neither absolute sNfL and sGFAP levels nor their corresponding z-scores were significantly correlated with any of the ophthalmological outcomes at any time point (Suppl. Table 2). Similarly, in multivariable linear regression models, none of the serum biomarkers exhibited a significant association with ophthalmological outcomes at any time point (Suppl. Table 3).

Also, neither the quotients (QNfL, QGFAP) nor the NfL and GFAP indices showed correlation with any of the ophthalmological outcomes at any time point (Suppl. Table 2). In linear regression models, QGFAP was negatively associated with baseline pRNFL thickness, which did not survive adjustment for multiple testing, whereas QNfL and the indices were not associated with ophthalmological outcomes at any timepoint (Suppl. Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses incorporating inter-eye correlation yielded consistent results across all models.

Correlation between serum and CSF levels of NfL and GFAP

CSF NfL levels did not correlate significantly with sNfL (rs=0.05, p = 0.791). Likewise, CSF GFAP levels did not correlate with sGFAP (rs= -0.02, p = 0.936). Consistently, CSF NfL and GFAP levels were not associated with their respective serum counterparts in multivariate models (Fig. 2).

CSF levels of NfL and GFAP did not correlate significantly with CSF OP (CSF NfL: r = 0.02, p = 0.937; CSF GFAP: r = 0.17, p = 0.356), whereas serum levels seemed to be inversely, although non-significantly correlated with CSF OP (sNfL: rs= -0.35, p = 0.061; sGFAP: rs= -0.27, p = 0.150). Notably, in multivariable models, higher CSF OP was independently associated with lower sNfL (β= -1.54; 95% CI -2.72, -0.37; p = 0.012) and sGFAP levels (β= -19.74; 95% CI -37.4, -2.07; p = 0.03), explaining 21% and 15% of the variance, respectively.

Correspondingly, CSF OP positively correlated with QNfL (rs=0.46, p = 0.01), QGFAP (rs=0.38, p = 0.038), NfL index (rs=0.39, p = 0.031), and GFAP index (rs=0.41, p = 0.023). Of note, in multivariable models, both QNfL and QGFAP were associated with CSF OP (Fig. 2). This association was preserved when accounting for BBB integrity as the NfL index was positively associated with CSF OP, whereas this relationship was not observed for the GFAP index (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this study, we systematically evaluated NfL and GFAP levels in paired CSF and serum samples from a prospectively collected cohort of newly diagnosed pwIIH, integrating longitudinal structural and functional ophthalmological outcomes. Despite preliminary evidence supporting a potential role for NfL and GFAP as biomarkers in IIH, the relationship between CSF and serum levels and the influence of ICP remains incompletely understood [22].

Against this background, the principal finding of the present study is that neither CSF nor serum NfL and GFAP levels were meaningfully associated with ophthalmological outcomes at any time point. Notably, this lack of association is concordant with the largely preserved structural integrity of the optic nerve in our cohort, as reflected by GCL thickness that predominantly remained within the normal range, arguing against widespread or clinically overt neuroaxonal damage during the active disease phase, although subtle or highly localized injury cannot be excluded. In addition, the relatively mild papilledema severity and short symptom duration in our cohort may have limited the extent of detectable neuroaxonal injury at the time of sampling. Importantly, although patients with moderate-to-severe papilledema demonstrated worse perimetric mean deviation at baseline, best-corrected visual acuity and GCL thickness did not differ between the groups, suggesting that functional impairment may, at least in part, reflect pressure-related and potentially reversible mechanisms rather than established neuroaxonal loss. In contrast, serum NfL and GFAP levels demonstrated inverse associations with CSF OP, whereas NfL and GFAP quotients and indices showed positive associations with CSF OP. Importantly, these associations were confined to serum biomarkers and were not observed for their CSF counterparts, indicating that elevated quotients and indices are driven predominantly by reduced serum levels of CNS-derived proteins (i.e., a smaller denominator), rather than by increased CSF levels (i.e., a larger numerator). It should be noted that fluid biomarkers such as NfL and GFAP primarily reflect global CNS injury and inherently lack spatial resolution. Consequently, subtle, focal, or early damage confined to the optic nerve head may not be adequately captured by CSF or serum concentrations, a phenomenon well recognized in other localized optic neuropathies, where biomarker elevations may remain modest despite clinically meaningful structural injury.

Still, this pattern suggests that the distribution of CNS-derived biomarkers in IIH may be substantially influenced by altered CSF dynamics and impaired CSF-blood exchange, rather than by the extent of neuroaxonal or astrocytic injury. Notably, among patients with available MRV data, the majority demonstrated relevant venous sinus stenosis, a finding compatible with altered venous outflow and supportive of altered CSF dynamics contributing to the observed biomarker compartmentalization. BBB integrity is unlikely to play a major role in this context, as it is considered intact in IIH, which was further confirmed in our cohort by normal albumin quotients and the congruent behaviour of biomarker quotients and indices, which correct for BBB integrity. Importantly, our study was not designed to directly assess CSF outflow resistance, and therefore mechanistic inferences regarding the primacy of altered CSF dynamics must remain tentative. We did not directly assess intermediate mechanisms of CSF biomarker egress, such as venous sinus stenosis severity or glymphatic transit; therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as indirect evidence suggesting altered clearance kinetics rather than definitive proof of altered CSF dynamics.

This observed dissociation between CSF and serum biomarkers represents a key conceptual implication of our findings. In most neurological disorders, serum NfL and GFAP are regarded as reliable surrogates of CSF levels; [23] however, this assumption presupposes intact CSF circulation and preserved clearance pathways. In IIH, elevated ICP and altered CSF dynamics likely hinder the efflux of CNS-derived proteins into the systemic circulation. As a consequence, CSF levels may increase due to impaired clearance, while serum levels remain disproportionately low, resulting in artificially inflated CSF-to-serum quotients and indices [6, 7, 24]. Consequently, CSF biomarkers may overestimate, while serum biomarkers may underestimate, the degree of underlying CNS injury, thereby distorting biomarker-based inferences during the active disease phase.

Consistent with this interpretation, we did not replicate earlier reports linking CSF NfL levels to papilledema severity [24]. This discrepancy may, at least in part, reflect differences in cohort composition, as our study included only a small number of patients with moderate to severe papilledema, whereas prior reports may have included patients with more advanced optic nerve involvement. Even where such associations have been described, impaired CSF clearance may confound biomarker-phenotype relationships, reflecting altered CSF dynamics rather than true neuroaxonal degeneration. Moreover, the absence of associations between fluid biomarkers and ophthalmological outcomes further undermines their prognostic relevance in IIH.

Taken together, our findings support two overarching conclusions. First, altered CSF dynamics appears to substantially influence biomarker distribution in IIH, profoundly altering the clearance kinetics of NfL and GFAP. Second, NfL and GFAP do not constitute reliable markers of disease severity or visual outcome during the active phase of IIH, when CSF dynamics dominate biomarker behaviour. In this context, a meaningful diagnostic or prognostic role of sNfL or GFAP levels in IIH appears limited, given their dependence on CSF OP and the extent of underlying neuroaxonal injury rather than disease-specific mechanisms. However, future prospective studies with larger sample size are certainly warranted in this regard.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Notably, the exclusively female and predominantly obese cohort closely mirrors the typical epidemiological profile of IIH, thereby strengthening the clinical representativeness of our findings within the classical disease phenotype. However, extrapolation to atypical populations, including male patients or individuals with normal BMI, should be undertaken with caution and requires validation in more heterogeneous populations. Although IIH is a rare disorder, the modest sample size limits statistical power to detect small to moderate associations; accordingly, these analyses should be regarded as exploratory. While this study is based on a prospective cohort with closely monitored, standardized follow-up, it remains observational, and treatment regimens - despite adherence to best practice guidelines - varied between individuals, introducing potential confounding. Furthermore, the absence of repeated lumbar punctures precluded assessment of longitudinal biomarker trajectories in relation to ICP normalization. Moreover, the dichotomization of papilledema severity was necessitated by the small number of patients with higher Frisén grades, which limited the ability to analyse the ordinal scale in a statistically robust manner and may have reduced sensitivity to detect subtle gradations across severity levels. Although z-score truncation for BMI values exceeding 66.1 may have introduced minor distortion, the small number of affected participants renders any such effect negligible. Despite these limitations, the rigorously standardized data acquisition, multimodal ophthalmological phenotyping, and concurrent assessment of paired CSF and serum biomarkers substantially strengthen the validity of our conclusions.

Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrate a robust and reproducible dissociation between serum and CSF NfL and GFAP levels in active IIH that is closely linked to elevated ICP. These findings are consistent with altered CSF dynamics influencing biomarker distribution in IIH and therefore caution against interpreting elevated CSF biomarker levels during active disease as definitive evidence of widespread neuroaxonal or astrocytic injury. Consequently, both CSF and – critically, given their greater clinical practicality - serum NfL and GFAP levels exhibit limited prognostic utility in the acute phase of IIH.
