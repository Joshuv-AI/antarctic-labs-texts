Dietary diversity, adequate vitamin A-rich food consumption and their associated factors among lactating mothers in South Ethiopia: a food frequency questionnaire-based study
Authors: Mehert Kitaw Gebretsadik, Darik Temesgen Assefa, Samuel Alemu Bamboro, Nigus Kabtu Belete, Mustafa Glagn Abdilwohab, Muluken Bekele Sorrie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLactating mothers in low-income countries frequently experience low dietary diversity and inadequate vitamin A-rich food consumption. This can lead to micronutrient deficiencies, which increase the risk of intergenerational malnutrition and mortality. Regardless of this, there is limited research on dietary diversity and vitamin A-rich food consumption among lactating mothers in the study area.ObjectiveTo assess the prevalence of dietary diversity, adequate vitamin A-rich food consumption and associated factors among lactating mothers in Arba Minch Zuria district, Southern Ethiopia.Study designA community-based cross-sectional study design was employed.Setting and participantsThis study was conducted among 410 randomly selected lactating mothers from the Arba Minch Zuria district, Southern Ethiopia.Primary and secondary outcomesThe data were collected by trained data collectors through a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire. Bivariate logistic regression was conducted to select candidate variables, and a multivariable logistic regression model was used to assess the association. A p value<0.05 with corresponding 95% CI was used to test statistical significance.ResultsThe prevalence of minimum dietary diversity was 44.1% (95% CI 39.3% to 48.9%); family size (adjusted OR (AOR)=1.92; 95% CI 1.09 to 3.397), meal frequency (AOR 3.43, 95% CI 1.69 to 6.95), home gardening (AOR=3.56; 95% CI 1.99 to 6.37), postnatal care follow-up (AOR=2.73, 95% CI 1.54–4.837) and food security (AOR=2.067; 95% CI 1.237 to 3.45) were significantly associated with dietary diversity among lactating mothers. Adequate consumption of vitamin A-rich food was 24.6% (95% CI 20.5% to 28.9%); educational status (AOR=3.604; 95% CI 1.635 to 7.94), antenatal care follow-up (AOR=2.73; 95% CI 1.03 to 7.28) and home gardening (AOR=2.21; 95% CI 1.11 to 4.34) were significantly associated with it.ConclusionNearly half of the lactating mothers in the study area achieved the minimum dietary diversity; family size, meal frequency, home gardening, postnatal care and food security were factors significantly associated. Almost one-fourth of the mothers consumed adequate vitamin A-rich food; educational status, antenatal care follow-up and home gardening were significantly associated with it.Keywords: Nutrition assessment, Nutrient deficiencies, Dietary patterns

Introduction

According to the UNICEF conceptual framework of maternal and child undernutrition, eating a poor-quality diet is an immediate cause of undernutrition that leads to short-term and long-term health problems. Diversification of diet is one of the most proven nutritional interventions for overcoming undernutrition that aims to reduce micronutrient deficiencies (MNDs).1 Among the segment of the population, lactating mothers (LM) in low-income and middle-income countries frequently experience low dietary diversity (DD) since their daily diets mainly consist of starchy staples with little to no fresh fruit, vegetables and animal-source food consumption.2

Lactation is one of the most complex and nutritionally demanding phases in a woman’s life cycles. Adequate maternal nutrition is crucial to rebuild their body stores of nutrients depleted during pregnancy and conserve nutrient stores to ensure breast milk supply without compromising maternal nutrition reserves.3 LMs produce 700–800 mL of milk per day, which requires an additional 550 kcal of energy (two additional meals) per day, and also the requirements for some micronutrients, including vitamin A (VA), are increased by 50% or more.4 Since it is impossible to obtain micronutrients from just one or two food groups, increasing the consumption of a diversified diet from different food groups during the lactation period plays a significant role in preventing maternal and child MNDs.1

VA, retinol and pro-VA carotenoids are normal components of human milk. The VA content of a mother’s breast milk depends on the mother’s VA status. Thus, the recommended dietary intake of VA in LM is 1300 µg per day, which is higher than the 770 µg per day recommended for pregnant women. Consequently, LMs and children who persistently consume inadequate VA-rich foods are at high risk for vitamin A deficiency (VAD).5 6

Out of 1.76 billion women of reproductive age (WRA), anaemia affects 528 million (30%), and 19 million of them suffer from VAD. The prevalence of MNDs in at least one of the three micronutrients (iron, zinc and folate) is estimated to affect 1.2 billion (69%) of non-pregnant WRA.7,9 The prevalence of micronutrient inadequacy from dietary sources was the highest (>40%) for VA, calcium, niacin, vitamin B-6, vitamin B-12, iron and/or zinc.4

A community-based cross-sectional study conducted in Ghana and South Africa found that 43% and 25% of mothers achieved the minimum dietary diversity of women (MDD-W).10 11 Similarly, a high magnitude of VAD has been demonstrated in different countries across Africa; about 40% of LM in Makhaza, Zimbabwe, and 38% in Zambia are VA-deficient. Additionally, 10% of women in Kenya were severely VA deficient.12 13

In Ethiopia, among the total WRA, only 7% of women met the minimum recommended five or more food groups per day; only 0.1% of WRA adhered to the Ethiopian Food-Based Dietary Guideline (EFBDG) recommendation to consume foods from all six food groups daily.14 Another study revealed that 48.8% and 24.5% of satisfied MDD-W in the Ataye district, North Shoa zone, and the Gedeo Zone, Southern Ethiopia, respectively.15 Another study conducted in Gondar Town, Ethiopia, showed that adequate consumption of VA-rich foods was 38.94%.16

In contrast, a study conducted in Bahar Dar, Ethiopia, revealed that the overall prevalence of micronutrient intake inadequacy across 12 nutrients was 39.9%, and particularly inadequate intake of VA was 98.2%.6 Based on retinol adjusted for inflammation, the national prevalence of VAD among WRA in Ethiopia was found to be 3.4%, and it was categorised as a mild public health problem, with a 4.6% prevalence in Southern Ethiopia.17

Due to the substantial rise in MND over the past two decades, WRA has gained considerable attention.18 The Ethiopian government has implemented the National Nutrition Program One and National Nutrition Program Two to enhance the nutrition status of WRA, including LM.19 The EFBDG also announced 11 key messages with guiding principles to take a diverse diet of six food groups for adults in a day. Moreover, the guideline states additional recommendations for pregnant women and LM.20

Despite these efforts, a significant number of mothers, particularly in rural areas, are impacted by MNDs.15 In addition, only a few studies highlighted the issue of DD and VAD among LM in Ethiopia, particularly in this study area. Therefore, this study aims to assess the prevalence of DD and adequate VA-rich food consumption in the Arba Minch surrounding district, Gamo Zone, Southern Ethiopia. Furthermore, the study sought to identify associated factors, including socio-demographic and economic characteristics, maternal decision-making autonomy, household (HH) food security, nutritional knowledge and obstetric healthcare-related characteristics among LM in the study area.

Methods

Study design, period and setting

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from 2 July to 5 August 2023 in the Arba Minch surrounding district, Gamo Zone, Southern Ethiopia. The Arba Minch surrounding district is one of the rural administrative districts in the Gamo Zone. The district has 20 kebeles, with an estimated total population of 131 890, out of which 66 208 are females. The district produces various essential crops, including maize, sorghum, wheat and barley. Additionally, Moringa stenopetala and kale are among the most consumed staple vegetables, with banana and mango being particularly popular fruits.

Source population and study population

All LMs who had a child or children <2 years old in the Arba Minch surrounding district.

Sample size calculation and sampling procedure

Sample size determination

The sample size for assessing the prevalence of MDD-W (first objective) was determined with the single population proportion formula, considering a 95% CI (Z=1.96), a 5% margin of error and a MDD-W prevalence of 47.8% obtained from a community-based cross-sectional study in Angecha district, Southern Ethiopia.21 The sample size for the second objective was calculated by using a 50% prevalence of adequate VA-rich food consumption.

Sampling techniques

A simple random sampling (SRS) technique was employed for this study. Initially, 5 kebeles were randomly selected from the 20 kebeles in the Arba Minch surrounding district using the lottery method. Subsequently, a complete list of LMs’ child pairs was obtained from the health post registration book in the selected kebeles to serve as sampling frame. Following the proportional allocation of the sample size to each kebele, individual study participants were selected using the SRS computer-generated numbers.

Data collection methods

Data collection instruments and procedures

Structured and pretested interviewer-administered questionnaires were used to collect data from LM, covering information on socio-demographic and economic factors, HH-related factors, maternal healthcare and feeding practice-related factors. The tools were prepared and uploaded to Open Data Kit (ODK), a smartphone-based application for data collection.

Qualitative food frequency questionnaires (FFQs) were developed from Helen Keller International’s (HKI) weekly FFQ tool; after adapting the tool to the local context, adequate consumption of VA-rich foods among LM in the area was assessed. Only major sources of VA-rich foods were taken into consideration (t100 RE), and from the total of 28 different food items in the HKI tool, 21 food items that are available and consumed in the study area were included in this study. The VA-rich food items in this study include plant source foods (dark green leafy vegetables (DGLVs), carrot, pumpkin, mango, papaya, sweet potato and apricot), and animal sources like fish, egg with yolk, butter and organ meat.22

A qualitative FFQ, which contained 50 food items that were commonly consumed in the study area, was used to assess MDD-W.23 The list of food items was also developed based on an extensive interview of the data collectors, focus group discussions with 1–5 women network leaders and health extension workers (HEWs) who were from the study area and who knew the culture, language and types of foods commonly consumed.

These 50 food items were categorised into 10 food groups based on MDD-W, and participants were asked to report the consumption frequency of each food item in the 10 food groups in the past week. Then, the weekly FFQ was converted into a 24-hour intake by selecting the highest score of food items consumed from each food group. The food item with the highest weekly frequency was converted into 1 day (24-hour period), and this process was similarly done for the 10 food groups.24 The daily converted numbers were added to get the minimum of 0 and the maximum score of 10.

Food security status was assessed using a complete form of the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS), which was developed and validated by the Food and Nutrition Technical Assistance.25

In this study, nutritional knowledge of the LMs was computed based on 10 knowledge questions using a mean score. Mothers scoring above the mean were classified as having good knowledge, while those below had poor knowledge.15

Operational definitions and measurement

Minimum dietary diversity of women

It is an indicator/tool that uses 10 main food groups to measure the nutrient adequacy of women (LMs in this case): an LM who had consumed five or more food groups over 24 hours was categorised under adequate MDD-W.23

Adequate VA-rich foods consumption

Consumption of animal sources of VA-rich foods for >4 days per week or consumption of a weighted source (total consumption of animal and vegetable sources of VA-rich foods) for >6 days per week.22

Food-secure mothers

A mother who experiences none of the food insecurity (access) conditions or just experiences worry, but rarely in the past 4 weeks.

Food-insecure mothers

Mothers who are unable at all times to access sufficient food to lead an active and healthy life; they are further classified as mild, moderate and severely food insecure based on the HFIAS.25

Data quality assurance

The questionnaire was initially developed in English and translated into Amharic. To ensure its consistency, it was translated back into English. Pretest was carried out on 22 (5%) of the study sample, and after the pretest, internal consistencies of the tools were assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The data were collected by five trained college students under close supervision by the principal investigator.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were downloaded from the ODK aggregate as an XLS file and exported to SPSS V.27 for the statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics such as simple frequencies, mean and SD were used to describe the socio-demographic and economic characteristics of participants as well as HH-related factors, maternal healthcare and feeding practice-related factors.

A binary logistic regression model was used for the inferential analysis, and the level of statistical significance was set at a probability level of <0.05. The adjusted OR (AOR), along with a 95% CI, was used to assess the strength of association.

Results

Socio-demographic and economic characteristics

A total of 410 LMs having a child with age ≤24 months were interviewed, which gives a response rate of 96.92%. About 300 (73.2%) of the mothers were in the age group of 25–34, with a mean age of 28.21 (±4.725 of SD) years. About half of the participants, 212 (51.7%) were not able to read and write. Regarding the family size, 289 (69.7%) of the HH are family members ≥5 (online supplemental table 1).

Maternal healthcare and feeding practice-related characteristics

More than three-fourths, 335 (81.7%), of the participants had antenatal care (ANC) follow-up for the last pregnancy, and 253 (61.7%) had postnatal (PNC) follow-up. 249 (60.7%) respondents had home gardening. More than half, 215 (52.4%), of the participants had three meals per day. Of those total LMs, 255 (62.2%) received nutritional counselling to eat an additional (extra) meal during lactation (online supplemental table 2).

Nutritional knowledge of the mother

The knowledge assessment questions were adapted from validated sources, including the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) nutritional knowledge assessment guidelines and other peer-reviewed literatures relevant to the study context. Prior to data collection, the knowledge questions were pretested and the internal consistencies of the questions were evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha for confirming the reliability of the tool.15 16 21 26

The study finding showed that more than half, 236 (57.6%), of the participants had good knowledge, and 155 (37.8%) had poor nutritional knowledge. Out of the total participants, 239 (58.3) and 265 (64.6%) participants responded correctly to the knowledge questions; carbohydrates and fats are energy-giving foods, and a balanced diet is important to prevent infection (online supplemental table 3).

Household food security

Regarding the HFIAS, 244 (59.5%) of the participants were food secure, while 103 (25.1%), 40 (9.7%) and 23 (5.6%) were mildly, moderately and severely food insecure, respectively (figure 1).

Minimum DD score of LMs

The prevalence of MDD-W (at least taking five or more food groups) was 181 (44.1%) (95% CI 39.3% to 48.9%). The majority of the study participants had consumed starchy staple foods (86.34%), and the least consumed food group was flesh foods (meat, poultry and fish) (0.24%) (figure 2).

Factors associated with minimum DD

The odds of MDD-W among those mothers who had <5 family members are 1.9 times (AOR=1.92; 95% CI 1.09 to 3.397) higher compared with those HHs with ≥5 family members. Mothers who ate more than 3 times a day were 3.43 times (AOR=3.43, 95% CI 1.69 to 6.95) more likely to have MDD-W compared with mothers who had less than or equal to two meals per day.

Regarding home gardening, mothers who had home gardening were 3.56 times more likely to achieve their MDD-W compared with their counterparts (AOR=3.56; 95% CI 1.96 to 6.37). The odds of MDD-W were 2.73 times higher among mothers who had PNC follow-up than those who had no PNC follow-up (AOR=2.73, 95% CI 1.54 to 4.84). Mothers from food-secure HHs were 2.1 times more likely to experience MDD-W than food-insecure HHs (AOR=2.07; 95% CI 1.24 to 3.45) (online supplemental table 4).

Adequate VA-rich food consumption

Adequate consumption of VA-rich food was 101 (24.6%) (95% CI 20.5% to 28.9%). Among the food items, DGLVs (93.9%) and milk (70.5%) were the highest consumed plant and animal sources of VA within the last week before the survey (online supplemental table 5).

Factors associated with adequate VA-rich food consumption

The adequate VA consumption of LM was nearly four times (AOR=3.6; 95% CI 1.6 to 7.94) higher among mothers who completed secondary school and above compared with their counterparts. The odds of adequate VA consumption were 2.73 (AOR=2.73; 95% CI 1.03 to 7.28) times more likely among mothers who had ANC follow-up for the last baby compared with those mothers who did not have ANC follow-up. Regarding home gardening practice, those mothers who had home gardening were 2.21 (AOR=2.21; 95% CI 1.12 to 4.34) times higher compared with those mothers who had no home gardening (online supplemental table 6).

Discussion on DD

In this study, 44.1% of LMs met the MDD-W. In order to have optimal micronutrient adequacy, the 2016 FAO recommendation states that reproductive-age women should obtain the MDD-W (≥5 food groups) in 24 hours.23

The findings of the present study are consistent with studies conducted in Aksum town (43.6%), Ataye district (48.8%), North Shoa zone, Angecha district, Central Ethiopia region (47.8%) and Southern Ethiopia.27 28 The finding of this study is relatively lower than studies conducted in Asian countries, India and Uganda, which were 55.2%, 56.5% and 57.9%, respectively.29 30 This could be associated with inadequate availability and access to food items both at the HH and market level in Ethiopia.31 This could also be the cause for high burden of MNDs among women in Ethiopia as DD is the main predictor of micronutrient adequacy.1

The prevalence of the current study is relatively higher than studies conducted in Jimma Zone (32.8%), Gedeo Zone, Southern Ethiopia (24.5%), Finote Selam district (23.1%), Bangladesh (12.8%), Vietnam (22%) and Ethiopia (23.1%).32 33 The differences in the prevalences may be explained by the difference in study period, seasonal variation, and socio-demographic and sociocultural characteristics of the study participants. Moreover, the availability and accessibility of health service infrastructures in different settings might result in the inconsistency of results.34

The odds of MDD-W were 1.9 times higher among mothers who lived in HHs with <5 family members compared with mothers living in HHs with ≥5 family members. This result is comparable to the study done in Uganda.29 This could be explained by the fact that the ease of providing diversified food for small family members is less challenging compared with the HHs with large family members, and mothers who live in large families may prioritise other members of the family, mainly their husband’s and children’s nutrition over their own by sacrificing their food.

The findings of this study indicated odds of MDD-W among mothers who had home gardening were 3.56 times higher than their counterparts. This is comparable to the study conducted in the Ataye district, North Shoa zone, and Central Ethiopia.27 28 This might be due to the fact that mothers from HHs with home gardens would grow vegetables and fruits, and they benefit from gardens to diversify their daily food intake.35

Those mothers who ate more than 3 times a day were 3.43 times more likely to have MDD-W compared with mothers who had ≤2 meals per day. This is consistent with studies conducted in Nepal, Southern Gondar and Deber Tabor.36

Regarding PNC follow-up, mothers who had PNC follow-up were 2.7 times more likely to achieve the MDD-W compared with their counterparts; this finding is supported by a study conducted in Maharashtra, India. Women in the early phase who received health and nutrition counselling services through PNC follow-up were 2.37 times more likely to consume a diversified diet.37 The possible justification could be that PNC follow-up is a major service given for postpartum mothers, which mainly works to promote the mother and newborn’s health through follow-up and counselling, including their nutritional intake.

Mothers from food-secure HHs were 2.07 times more likely to have MDD-W than mothers from food-insecure HHs. This is supported by the study conducted in Bangladesh, Vietnam and Northern Ethiopia.32 According to the FAO, food security allows people to consume an acceptable amount and quality of food, which in turn helps the mother to attain a minimum score for DD.25

Discussion on adequate VA food consumption

This study’s findings showed that the consumption of adequate VA-rich foods was 24.7% that was comparable with a study conducted in India, which revealed that 20% of the study participants had adequate VA-rich food consumption.38 This study’s finding was lower than a study conducted among LMs in Gondar, Ethiopia, which found that 38.7% of LMs had adequate VA-rich food consumption based on the HKI tool assessment.16 This difference may reflect the difference in seasonal variation, study setting, which was facility-based, and as the study participants were urban mothers, their economic status was a major determinant for adequate consumption of VA-rich food.34

Furthermore, only 13% of low-income urban mothers in Bangladesh had achieved the recommended dietary allowance of VA; this finding is lower than the present study’s result, which revealed that 24.6% of LMs had adequate VA-rich food consumption. The low consumption of VA-rich food among Bangladeshi women may be attributed to the socio-demographic and economic characteristics, where food scarcity and limited income-generating opportunities exacerbate the nutritional vulnerability. Moreover, a rice-based diet with a low consumption of nutrient-dense foods, along with poor sanitation and overcrowding in Bangladesh, increases the risk of infection and hinders the absorption of VA.39

Adequate VA-rich food consumption was associated with educational status. Mothers with an educational level of secondary and above were 3.6 times more likely to consume adequate VA-rich foods compared with mothers who were unable to read and write. This was supported by a study done in Gondar, Ethiopia, which showed that there was a positive association between adequate VA-rich food consumption and the educational status of LM.16 This might be because mothers who attended secondary and above education are more likely to get more information about the source of VA-rich foods, as well as the consequences of VAD.

Mothers who had ANC follow-up in the last pregnancy were 2.73 times more likely to consume adequate VA-rich foods compared with those who had no ANC follow-up. This might be linked to the possibility that mothers who had appropriate access to healthcare during their antenatal period were more likely to receive adequate information and education about appropriate nutrition and DD.

Furthermore, mothers who had home gardens were 2.2 times more likely to achieve adequate consumption of VA-rich food compared with those who had no home gardening. This may be because mothers who had home gardens would produce vegetables and fruits. Thus, they are more likely to attain a variety of foods and food groups that are rich in VA. This may also relate to the fact that home gardening practice is one of the intervention methods to increase DD and VA-rich food consumption.35

Conclusions

Nearly half of the LMs achieved the MMD-W; family size, meal frequency, home gardening, PNC follow-up and food security were significantly associated with MDD-W among LMs. Almost one-fourth of LMs consume adequate VA-rich foods. Educational status, ANC follow-up and home gardening were factors significantly associated with adequate VA-rich food consumption among LMs in the Arba Minch surrounding district. Based on HKI threshold values, 75.4% of LMs had inadequate VA intake, which shows a public health problem in the community.
