Incidence and Determinants of Implantable Cardioverter‐Defibrillator Therapy With Loss of and Impaired Consciousness
Authors: Alwin B. P. Noordman, Michiel Rienstra, Yuri Blaauw, Bart A. Mulder, Hessel Groenveld, Alexander H. Maass
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionImplantable cardioverter‐defibrillators (ICDs) are important in the prevention of sudden cardiac death. However, some appropriate ICD therapies may be considered unnecessary, delivered when patients do not experience symptoms of hemodynamic instability such as loss of consciousness. Therefore, we aimed to determine the incidence of appropriate ICD therapy with loss of consciousness and impaired consciousness, as well as the psychological impact following any ICD therapy, in patients with an ICD by investigating patient‐reported symptoms.MethodsIn this prospective, single‐center study conducted at the University Medical Center Groningen (UMCG), 100 consecutive patients with an ICD for primary or secondary prevention indications who received ICD shock and/or ATP within the previous 6 months completed a questionnaire. The primary outcome was appropriate ICD therapy with loss of consciousness.ResultsThe patient population consisted of 100 patients, 92 (92.0%) of whom had appropriate therapy. The mean age was 62.7 ± 12.8 years and 21 (21.0%) were female. Loss of consciousness occurred in 40% of patients with appropriate ICD therapy and in 65% of patients with appropriate shocks. Determinants of appropriate ICD therapy with loss of consciousness included the shortest cycle length, ventricular fibrillation, the occurrence of shock, a secondary prevention indication for ICD, the presence of a CRT‐D and the fragmentation and width of the QRS complex. The psychological impact of ICD shocks was considerable, with patients without loss of consciousness during ICD shocks suffering significantly more from anxiety than those with loss of consciousness (9 (60.0%) versus 12 (42.9%), p = 0.041).ConclusionsA considerable number of patients do not experience appropriate ICD therapy with loss of consciousness and thus experience potentially unnecessary therapy while still being at risk of sustaining the consequent psychological impact associated with ICD shocks.

Introduction

Sudden cardiac death (SCD) is an important cause of death, being responsible for 15–20% of all deaths in Western countries [1]. It is most commonly caused by ventricular arrhythmias, including ventricular tachycardia (VT) or ventricular fibrillation (VF) [1]. The implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) can reduce the risk of SCD and thus improve survival in patients at risk of experiencing life‐threatening ventricular arrhythmias. ICDs can detect and treat such arrhythmias by delivering shocks or antitachycardia pacing (ATP) [2].

Previous studies found that more conservative ICD programming resulted in fewer appropriate ICD therapies, so that the prevented therapies may be considered unnecessary, while also reducing all‐cause mortality [3, 4, 5]. In fact, 35% of VTs have previously been shown to terminate spontaneously, so that active termination is not always required [6]. Unnecessary ICD therapies occur in the presence of otherwise spontaneously terminating ventricular arrhythmias and when patients do not experience hemodynamic instability [3, 7, 8]. Previous studies have shown that a substantial proportion of patients, varying from 30%–63%, do not experience symptoms as a result of the arrhythmia during ICD therapy, while loss of consciousness or near‐syncope occurred in 10%–32% of patients [9, 10]. This suggests that a considerable number of patients experience ICD therapy while being (still) hemodynamically stable.

However, there is a lack of contemporary studies regarding the occurrence of potentially unnecessary ICD therapies. This topic is of great importance, because potentially unnecessary shocks can be painful [11]. In fact, shocks may contribute to anxiety, depressive symptoms and posttraumatic stress [12, 13]. Unnecessary ATP too may be detrimental, because it may be proarrhythmic and thus accelerate the arrhythmia, potentially necessitating the delivery of a shock and converting a hemodynamically stable VT into VF [6]. Therefore, it is essential to determine the incidence of appropriate ICD therapy with loss of consciousness or impaired consciousness, in order to gain a better insight into the frequency of occurrence of unnecessary ICD therapies.

In the present study, we sought to determine the incidence of appropriate ICD therapy with loss of consciousness or impaired consciousness, as well as the psychological impact following any ICD therapy, in patients with an ICD by investigating patient‐reported symptoms.

Methods

Study Design

In this single‐center, prospective, observational questionnaire study conducted in the University Medical Center Groningen (UMCG), 100 consecutive patients with any type of ICD for primary or secondary prevention of SCD who received ICD shock and/or ATP within the previous 6 months were included (Figure 1). All eligible patients who were identified were approached for participation in the study. Inclusion of patients began on April 21, 2023 and ended on December 31, 2024. Patients were included if they met each of the following inclusion criteria: presence of an ICD regardless of the time it was implanted, occurrence of any ICD shock and/or ATP, age ≥ 18 years and proficiency in the Dutch language. Patients were excluded if their ICD shock and/or ATP took place more than 6 months prior to inclusion in order to minimize recall bias. The presence of a left ventricular assist device (LVAD), in which case ventricular arrhythmias are generally hemodynamically tolerated, was not an exclusion criterion. All included patients filled in a questionnaire regarding symptoms experienced during ICD therapy as well as the psychological impact following ICD therapy.

This study does not fall under the scope of the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO), as concluded by the Medical Ethics Review Board of the University Medical Center Groningen (METc UMCG) (METc 2023/163). This study was approved by the Central ethics Review Board non‐WMO studies of the UMCG (CTc UMCG). Written informed consent was obtained from all included patients.

Data Acquisition and Definitions

Patient characteristics were obtained for all patients from electronic medical records. Data was obtained on medical history, medication use, electrocardiography, laboratory analysis and echocardiography, generally prior to and as close to the ICD therapy as possible. Other acquired data include the type, number, appropriateness and success of ICD therapies, as well as the type and cycle lengths of the arrhythmias for which ICD therapy was delivered and the occurrence of loss of consciousness around the time of ICD therapy. Data on ICD therapies and the underlying arrhythmia was assessed by two independent observers (ABPN and AHM). Appropriate ICD therapy was defined as shock and/or ATP in the presence of VT or VF. A shock and/or ATP delivered in the absence of VT or VF was defined as inappropriate ICD therapy. Loss of consciousness was considered to be present if patients reported loss of consciousness on the questionnaire, if this was registered by history taking in the electronic medical records or in case of lack of experience of ICD shock when one or more shocks occurred while the patient was not asleep. Impaired consciousness was defined as the composite of loss of consciousness and near syncope. In addition to the aforementioned, this included patient‐reported near syncope either in the questionnaire or by history taking in electronic medical records, as well as reduced consciousness before, during or directly after ICD therapy.

Questionnaire

For this study, we developed a questionnaire, based among others on previously reported symptoms which may occur during ICD therapy [10], in order to investigate the occurrence of loss of consciousness and impaired consciousness around the time of ICD therapy, as well as the psychological impact following ICD therapy. The questionnaire consisted of 30 questions, divided in four separate sections: general questions, experience during, before and after ICD therapy. In the section with general questions, respondents were asked to state their understanding of and satisfaction with the ICD, among others. The ensuing questions concerned patients’ experience during ICD therapy, including patients’ experience of the shock and/or ATP itself, the length of time that has passed until the date of filling in the questionnaire, as well as the painfulness of ICD therapy and patients’ state of consciousness at the moment of occurrence of ICD therapy. The questions that followed concerned patients’ experience in the moments prior to the ICD therapy, such as state of consciousness as well as the symptoms experienced before the ICD intervened. The final questions concerned patients’ experience after the ICD therapy, including respondents’ state of consciousness, as well as the psychological impact of the ICD therapy.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the occurrence of appropriate ICD therapy with loss of consciousness. The secondary outcome was the presence of impaired consciousness.

ICD Programming

We use a standardized protocol which is based on current expert consensus [14]. Patients with sinus rhythm are programmed to 3 zones: monitor only 150–188 bpm, VT zone with morphology discrimination from 188–230 bpm with 2 burst ATP followed by shock therapy, 230 bpm and above: ATP during charging followed by shock therapy. For patients with complete heart block, morphology discrimination was turned off to prevent inadequate delay or withholding of ICD therapy. Patients with AF were programmed to 2 zones: monitor only 150–200 bpm, 200 bpm and higher: ATP during charging followed by shock therapy. In patients with episodes with AF and fast ventricular conduction in the monitor zone, therapy zone can be programmed higher (e.g. 214 bpm) by discretion of the attending cardiologist. In patients with a history of sustained VT, VT zone with morphology discrimination can be lower than 188 bpm to capture the clinical VT by discretion of the attending cardiologist. In general, probabilistic counters were set to a number of intervals to detect of 30 out of 40, while consecutive counters were generally also programmed with a number of intervals to detect of 30.

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented using numbers and percentages if dichotomous or categorical. If the data were continuous, they were expressed using mean and standard deviation if normally distributed and using median and interquartile range (IQR) if not normally distributed. The chi‐square or Fisher's exact test, Mann‐Whitney test and t‐test for independent groups were used where appropriate to compare groups with respect to the baseline characteristics and questionnaire responses, for instance based on the presence or absence of loss of consciousness. The incidences of outcomes were presented for patients with appropriate ICD therapy, as well as for patients with inappropriate therapy and patients with an LVAD as comparator groups. Univariable logistic regression analyses were performed for the baseline characteristics for both the primary and secondary outcomes and selected on the basis of an alpha of 0.25 for backward stepwise multivariable logistic regression analyses to find determinants of appropriate ICD therapy with loss of consciousness and impaired consciousness. All statistical analyses related to potentially unnecessary ICD therapies were performed in the patient population with appropriate ICD therapy (shock and/or ATP) without an LVAD. Sensitivity analyses were performed in patients with only appropriate ICD shock without an LVAD, as well as in the population with appropriate ICD therapy (shock and/or ATP) including patients with LVAD. To investigate the psychological impact according to the type and number of ICD therapy, patients with either appropriate or inappropriate therapy were analyzed. Relevant interactions were tested of first‐line interactions. No statistically significant interactions were found in the analyses.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS, version 28.0 (SPSS Institute, Chicago, IL, USA). Statistical significance was defined as a p‐value < 0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Of the 100 included patients, 90 (90.0%) patients had only appropriate therapy, 2 (2.0%) patients had both appropriate and inappropriate therapy, and 8 (8.0%) had only inappropriate therapy. 9 (9.0%) patients had an LVAD. The mean age was 62.7 ± 12.8 years and 21 (21.0%) were female. 70 (70.0%) patients had an ICD for a primary prevention indication, while 30 (30.0%) had an ICD for secondary prevention of SCD. 35 (35.0%) patients had a VVI‐ICD, 33 (33.0%) had a DDD‐ICD, with 27 (27.0%) patients having a CRT‐D. The median time elapsed since the first ICD implantation was 9.2 (IQR 3.7–13.4) years. 38 (38.0%) patients had a history of myocardial infarction. The mean left ventricular ejection fraction was 36% ± 14% (Table 1). A more comprehensive overview of patient characteristics can be found in Tables S1–S3.

Psychological Impact of ICD Therapy

For the total study population, 45 (45.0%) patients reported that they experienced more anxiety since the ICD therapy, with 69 (69.0%) patients at least sometimes having anxiety for future ICD therapies. Patients with appropriate shock and/or ATP with and without loss of consciousness did not differ significantly in terms of psychological symptoms (Table S13). However, when considering only patients with appropriate shock, anxiety occurred significantly more frequently in patients without loss of consciousness (9 (60.0%) versus 12 (42.9%), p = 0.041) (Figures 2 and 3, Table S14). Anxiety was reported significantly more often by patients with shock than those without shock (32 (55.2%) versus 13 (31.0%), p = 0.041), by patients with a shock ever since ICD implantation (40 (54.8%) versus 5 (18.5%), p = 0.003) and by patients who had 3 or more shocks as compared to patients who had 1 or 2 shocks (12 (85.7%) versus 20 (45.5%), p = 0.003) (Figure 4, Tables S15–S17).

Discussion

In this study, we found that 40% of patients with appropriate ICD therapy (shock and/or ATP) and 65% of patients with appropriate shock lose consciousness during ICD therapy. No patients with an LVAD experienced loss of consciousness during ICD therapy. Patients without loss of consciousness during ICD shocks suffered significantly more from anxiety than those with loss of consciousness. The shortest cycle length, VF, the occurrence of shock, a secondary prevention indication for ICD, the presence of a CRT‐D and QRS complex fragmentation and width were determinants of loss of consciousness.

Appropriate ICD Therapy With Loss of Consciousness

Of all patients with appropriate ICD therapy, 40% experienced loss of consciousness, which means that nearly two‐thirds of patients seemed to remain hemodynamically stable. For appropriate shock, 65% of the population experienced loss of consciousness. Our reported rates of loss of consciousness are higher than in previous studies, which found rates of 3%–15% from history taking during hospital visits [9, 10, 15], with another study finding a higher rate of 27% [16]. In addition to our definition of loss of consciousness, which included lack of experience of ICD shock when one or more shocks occurred, another important difference between these formerly conducted studies and our study is that the former were performed several decades ago whereas our study was conducted among a contemporary patient population. This may be important, because the incidence of ICD therapies has decreased with more conservative ICD programming strategies in recent decades [17], so that it might be speculated that a higher rate of loss of consciousness might be expected in the current time, although this has not been demonstrated [3, 4]. Another explanation may lie in the fact that the present study was conducted in an academic, tertiary hospital, with generally sicker patients who may have less reserve and who may thus more easily lose consciousness. Of interest, it has previously been demonstrated that loss of consciousness occurs more commonly with immediate shock delivery as opposed to arrhythmias initially treated with ATP, with ATP being a frequent cause of loss of consciousness through proarrhythmia [16, 18], findings which are consistent with our results. When considering both loss of consciousness and near syncope, previous studies report rates of 10%–32% [9, 10], with one study finding a higher rate of 40% among patients with appropriate shocks [19]. Our reported rate of impaired consciousness was higher, namely 59% among patients with appropriate therapy (shock + ATP) and 72% among patients with appropriate shock.

In our study, a considerable number of patients seemed to remain hemodynamically stable during ICD therapy, a finding with potentially important clinical implications, especially since shocks may be painful and even traumatic and ATPs may be proarrhythmic, converting a stable and potentially non‐sustained VT into a hemodynamically unstable arrhythmia. Therefore, potentially unnecessary shocks and ATPs should be prevented, so that therapies are only delivered when necessary and not when a ventricular arrhythmia is (still) hemodynamically well‐tolerated. Delaying the delivery of ICD therapy, if warranted, may achieve this. Patient confirmation could be exploited and works adequately in patients with a wearable cardioverter‐defibrillator [7]. A specific subgroup of patients for whom the prevention of unnecessary shocks is especially relevant concerns those with an LVAD, who generally tolerate ventricular arrhythmias well [20], as was also observed in our study. Despite the aforementioned, in the case of ATPs, potential outcomes of such a delay include spontaneous termination of the ventricular arrhythmia which is beneficial, but it may also accelerate, necessitating an ICD shock. Furthermore, further delays may result in loss of consciousness, which may occur in dangerous situations. In addition, if a VT remains untreated for too long, detrimental consequences may follow, including for instance the development of shock, myocardial ischemia, syncope and heart failure [21]. Therefore, it is important to note that although a further delay in the delivery of ICD therapy may be beneficial by preventing potentially unnecessary ICD therapy, it may not be beneficial in all cases.

In our study, nearly half of patients reported that they experienced more anxiety since the ICD therapy. Unlike hemodynamically unstable patients, those remaining hemodynamically stable experience shocks while conscious, which, one might speculate, may provide a potential explanation for the finding that patients without loss of consciousness suffered significantly more from anxiety than those with loss of consciousness during ICD shocks, which is consistent with existing literature [13]. It must be stated, however, that causality cannot be claimed. In this context, it is worth noting that patients surviving a sudden cardiac arrest generally experience anxiety [22], so that at least part of the patient‐reported anxiety observed may be explained by the sudden cardiac arrest rather than solely resulting from ICD shocks. Furthermore, patients with loss of consciousness may have experienced earlier shocks while conscious, which may have contributed to their anxiety.

Determinants of Loss of Consciousness

Some studies described no significant clinical determinants for hemodynamic instability except for ventricular arrhythmic rate [9, 10, 15, 16, 18], consistent with our finding of shortest cycle length as determinant of the outcomes. However, one study found a significant association between a low left ventricular ejection fraction and chronic atrial fibrillation on one hand and loss of consciousness on the other [16], although the investigators did not focus solely on patients who already experienced ICD therapy. In our study, left ventricular ejection fraction was a significant determinant in univariable analyses but not in the multivariable model. VF was significantly associated with loss of consciousness, which was expected because VF describes a chaotic rhythm resulting in uncoordinated and ineffective ventricular contractions. The occurrence of a shock was another determinant of loss of consciousness, which may be explained as follows: arrhythmias requiring termination by shock generally have persisted beyond ATP delivery or are faster and thus more severe, increasing the likelihood of losing consciousness. Patients with a secondary prevention ICD indication were more likely to experience loss of consciousness during ICD therapy than primary prevention ICD recipients. Since secondary prevention ICD recipients have already had hemodynamically not‐tolerated VT or VF, it is not surprising that arrhythmic recurrences are likely to result in loss of consciousness. Presence of a CRT‐D was associated with a lower risk of loss of consciousness, which may result from biventricular pacing, which may improve cardiac function in CRT‐responsive patients. This cardiac functional improvement may render patients less prone to hemodynamic instability. Finally, QRS complex fragmentation and width were significantly associated with loss of consciousness. A wide QRS complex indicates conduction abnormalities, which may signify both a relatively more diseased heart but also hypothetically a less well‐coordinated contraction pattern during ventricular arrhythmias, thereby predisposing patients to hemodynamic instability.

Limitations

This study contributes contemporary findings to the currently scant and old literature concerning the incidence of loss of consciousness and impaired consciousness during ICD therapy and therefore provides an estimate of the incidence of unnecessary ICD therapy. However, several limitations are worth mentioning. First, the questionnaire used in the present study has not been used before and has therefore not been validated. Second, the moments of ICD therapy and filling in the questionnaire were separated by up to 6 months, which means there is a risk of recall bias. Third, loss of consciousness was registered for patients who became unconscious during at least one episode of ICD therapy. In practice, however, patients with multiple therapies might be hemodynamically unstable during some while being hemodynamically stable during others, so that a considerably large share of patients will experience unnecessary ICD therapy at some point. Some patients were sleeping during ICD therapy, which sometimes precluded accurate determination of the state of consciousness. As for the psychological impact of ICD shocks, no conclusions can be drawn with regard to causality. Another limitation concerns the relatively small sample size. Furthermore, since only intracardiac electrograms were available for the arrhythmias, information regarding ventricular conduction disturbances during the arrhythmia, which impacts myocardial contractility, was lacking. Additionally, although shocks are a clearly defined entity for patients, this is not always the case for ATP, which may not be experienced by patients and thus contribute to recall bias. Finally, the generalizability of the presented findings to patients with an ICD in general is limited by the fact that the study was conducted in an academic, tertiary hospital, as well as by the fact that the study population may not necessarily represent a usual ICD population considering that ischemic heart disease was underrepresented as an etiology in our study.

Future Directions

We intended to provide an estimate of the incidence of hemodynamic instability during ICD therapy to gain insight into the incidence of necessary and unnecessary therapies. Further research is needed to corroborate our results, especially given the major lack of knowledge regarding this topic. Future studies should also investigate the merit of even more conservative ICD programming, which may especially benefit patients with an LVAD considering that all of them seemed to remain hemodynamically stable during ICD therapy, though no conclusions can be drawn regarding causality. For LVAD patients, the value of refraining from ICD implantation or deactivation of the shock function should be determined through further investigation. The use of a device capable of sounding an instantaneous alarm alerting the patient and/or emergency medical services during sustained ventricular arrhythmias might even be reasonable.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a considerable number of patients do not experience loss of consciousness during appropriate ICD therapy and to a lesser extent also during appropriate ICD shock, with at least a third of patients who receive ICD therapy experiencing potentially unnecessary therapy while remaining at risk of sustaining the psychological impact associated with ICD shocks.
