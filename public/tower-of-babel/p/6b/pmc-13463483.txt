Long-term changes in the genetic diversity of the endangered freshwater fish Microphysogobio koreensis (Gobioninae) in Korea
Authors: Kang-Rae Kim, Keun-Sik Kim, Hyun Yang, Ju-Duk Yoon, In-Chul Bang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicrophysogobio koreensis is an endangered freshwater fish endemic to the Nakdong and Seomjin River systems in Korea. Because its distribution is restricted and population decline has been reported, genetic monitoring is needed to evaluate temporal changes in diversity and to define conservation units. In this study, we developed novel microsatellite markers and assessed long-term changes in genetic diversity, effective population size, and population structure in M. koreensis. ResultsWe analyzed two populations from the Nakdong and Seomjin Rivers sampled in 2012 and 2021 using seven newly developed microsatellite loci, together with mitochondrial cytochrome b sequences. Across the four population samples, observed heterozygosity ranged from 0.783 to 0.832 and expected heterozygosity ranged from 0.822 to 0.827, indicating that overall heterozygosity was largely maintained over the 9-year interval. However, the number of alleles declined in both river systems in 2021 relative to 2012. Significant bottleneck signals were detected in 12ND, 12SJ, and 21SJ, and effective population size estimates ranged from 19 to 43, except for 12SJ where estimation was not possible. Pairwise genetic differentiation, Bayesian clustering, discriminant analysis of principal components, and haplotype network analysis consistently showed clear genetic separation between the Nakdong and Seomjin River populations. Year-specific STRUCTURE and DAPC analyses further confirmed that this differentiation was consistently maintained within both sampling years. Mitochondrial data also revealed strong differentiation between the two river systems.ConclusionsAlthough overall heterozygosity remained relatively stable over time, the loss of alleles and very small effective population sizes indicate ongoing genetic vulnerability in M. koreensis. The Nakdong and Seomjin River populations should be treated as separate management units, and both recent populations require urgent conservation attention. Continued long-term genetic monitoring will be important for guiding conservation management and population reinforcement strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12900-x.Keywords: Microsatellite, Bbottleneck, Genetic structure, Genetic diversity

Introduction

A sixth mass extinction is currently underway on Earth [1], emphasizing the urgent need to develop conservation strategies for endangered species [2, 3]. Without regular and consistent surveys, it becomes difficult to implement effective protection measures in a timely manner, which can ultimately determine the fate of fish populations [3, 4]. Conducting regular surveys (e.g., every five years) is necessary to prevent species extinction [3, 5].

Threatened species often exhibit low genetic diversity, which poses significant risks to their survival and adaptability [6]. This low genetic diversity arises from several factors, primarily population bottlenecks, inbreeding, and limited gene flow [7]. To conserve endangered species, population reinforcement through artificial propagation is used [8]. However, these methods have the risk of generating genetic contamination and potential loss of genetic diversity in natural populations [9, 10]. Therefore, genetic monitoring assesses changes in genetic variation and population structure over time and helps reduce extinction risk by detecting warning signs at an early stage [11, 12]. In general, threatened species often experience genetic impoverishment as a result of past demographic bottlenecks, and the resulting loss of genetic diversity can reduce effective population size [11]. Regular estimates of effective population size can provide insight into the sustainability of the population [13]. Long-term genetic monitoring also allows us to estimation of effective population size (Ne), one of the most important parameters in conservation biology [13, 14]. Ne is the observed rate of change in heterozygosity of the population, the size of the ideal population with identical allele frequencies [15]. A potential reduction in Ne could lead to inbreeding depression, loss of genetic diversity and evolutionary potential in the short or long term [16]. Monitoring a species’ Ne and genetic diversity enables conservation interventions before populations enter an extinction vortex [11].

To develop effective conservation strategies, it is important to identify genetic endemism within a species (genetic resources unique to a particular region) [17]. In particular, freshwater fish populations distributed across multiple regions are often isolated by mountains or the ocean [18]. For this reason, when gene flow is blocked and genetic exchange is prevented, genetic differentiation and uniqueness can occur among populations [19, 20]. Preserving these unique genetic traits contributes to maintaining diversity at the species level [21]. Therefore, it is important to identify populations with unique genotypes as management units (MUs) [22]. Maintaining the genetic integrity of each population by preventing admixture is therefore crucial. In particular, when formulating conservation strategies, reintroduction and transplant decisions are often made based on these MUs [22].

Microphysogobio koreensis is a Korean endemic species distributed only in the Seomjin River and Nakdong River watersheds on the Korean Peninsula [23]. It is a small species with a body length of 10 to 12 cm [23]. This species inhabits relatively fast-flowing sections of the middle and upper river reaches, typically over gravel and sandy substrates, and mainly feeds on attached algae [23]. M. koreensis is protected as an endangered fish by the Ministry of Environment as its population and distribution have recently been rapidly decreasing due to various water pollution and habitat destruction [24]. To date, studies on spawning characteristics have been report [25, 26], but no study has yet examined the genetic diversity and population structure of this species. Declines in distribution and habitat populations create bottlenecks and thus reduced genetic diversity [11, 27]. To date, no official records have been confirmed regarding the movement of M. koreensis populations between the Nakdong River and Seomjin River basins. In particular, recent artificial breeding efforts for the species in 2023 involved releasing broodstock collected from the Seomjin River into the same river basin, and did not constitute movement between basins [28]. Therefore, for the conservation of endangered species, it is necessary to develop molecular markers for population genetic analyses and to conduct genetic monitoring [12, 27]. Additionally, when establishing a conservation strategy, it is necessary to genetically identify MUs for artificial propagation [22].

Microsatellite markers are DNA with repetitive base sequences in a specific region and are abundantly distributed within the genome [29]. Microsatellite markers are particularly useful in conservation genetics of endangered species because they are highly polymorphic, allow sensitive assessment of population structure and genetic differentiation, and can be applied to infer recent bottlenecks, inbreeding, and effective population size [27, 30–33].

Similarly, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), particularly the mitochondrial Cytochrome b (Cyt b) region, is highly effective for studying gene flow across long-term barriers and uncovering fixed evolutionary patterns [30, 34]. mtDNA is maternally inherited, non-recombinant, and exhibits a relatively low mutation rate [35]. As a result, significant genetic changes are unlikely to occur within 10 years [35], allowing single-time analyses to provide reliable insights into the long-term genetic characteristics and population structure of a species.

In this study, we reported seven newly developed microsatellite markers of M. koreensis and analyzed the genetic diversity and structure of populations in the Seomjin and Nakdong River basin. In addition, mitochondrial Cyt b data, together with microsatellite markers, were used to assess genetic structure and to identify management units (MUs). Genetic information for the conservation of endangered species was obtained by assessing genetic diversity and effective population size through long-term genetic monitoring conducted in 2012 and 2021.

Materials and methods

Ethical and regulatory compliance

All procedures involving live vertebrate animals complied with institutional and national regulations in the Republic of Korea and adhered to the ARRIVE 2.0 reporting guidelines and the ARROW guidelines for wildlife research. Field collection permits were issued by the Ministry of Environment, Korea (Jeonbuk Regional Environment Office: 2011-11 and 2021-5; Daegu Regional Environment Office: 2011-11 and 2021-7; Nakdong River Drainage Basin Area Environment Center: 2011-11 and 2021-7). Animal use was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Institute of Ecology (NIE IACUC; approval no. NIEIACUC-2020-007). We complied with relevant national legislation and institutional policies and followed taxon-specific guidance for fishes. Measures consistent with the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) were implemented as detailed below.

We studied the endangered freshwater fish Microphysogobio koreensis in two river systems (Nakdong, Seomjin). Fish were sampled in 2012 and 2021 at sites shown in Fig. 1. The numbers of animals handled were: 2012 Nakdong (12ND): 29, 2012 Seomjin (12SJ): 27, 2021 Nakdong (21ND): 19, 2021 Seomjin (21SJ): 28. Sample sizes were chosen a priori to provide adequate precision for microsatellite diversity indices and contemporary effective population size (Ne) estimation using LD-based methods, which are sensitive to allele-frequency sampling error at small n. The realized n per site-year ( ≥ ~ 20 where feasible) reflects logistics and conservation constraints while meeting commonly used thresholds for multilocus diversity and LDNe analyses [36].

Sampling location of M. koreensis. Abbreviations for populations are given in Table S1. The number preceding the population indicates the sampling year

Fish were captured with casting nets or dip nets in lotic reaches over gravel substrates. Immediately post-capture, individuals were transferred to aerated, shaded containers filled with ambient river water. Water temperature matched the river at capture, and dissolved oxygen was maintained by battery aerators. Fish were anesthetized using MS-222 for only a short period of time (typically less than 10 min) and released at the time of capture after full recovery.

The sole intervention was a non-lethal fin-clip (pelvic fin; 2–3 mm) for DNA. Fin clipping was performed under brief anesthesia to minimize handling time and nociception.

Analgesia/anesthesia: Fish were anesthetized in buffered MS-222 (tricaine) at 100 mg/L until loss of equilibrium, then moved to a wetted cradle for sampling. No postoperative analgesics were administered because the procedure is minimally invasive in teleosts when conducted under brief anesthesia with rapid recovery. This justification aligns with fish research guidelines.

Consistent with ARROW, we: (i) obtained institutional animal care approval and cite the approval number(s) above; (ii) complied with national, international, and institutional animal-care guidelines (named above); (iii) complied with applicable animal-care legislation in the country of research; (iv) implemented the 3Rs by using a non-lethal biopsy, minimizing sample sizes compatible with statistical power, and refining capture/handling; and (v) followed taxon-specific guidelines for fishes, and provide citations in the manuscript.

Sampling and DNA extraction

Microphysogobio koreensis populations were sampled from the Nakdong and Seomjin Rivers in 2012 and 2021 using fishing nets; detailed information is given in Fig. 1 and Table S1. Ethical approval and field collection permits are described in Sect. 2.1. Pectoral fin tissues were preserved in 99% ethanol, and genomic DNA was extracted from all specimens using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Germantown, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Microsatellite marker development

DNA cutting and ligation

The extracted genomic DNA (Nakdong River, 1 µg) was digested with RsaI (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) for 10 s following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA fragments were treated with mung bean nuclease (New England Biolabs) for 30 min and blunted and dephosphorylated by calf intestinal phosphatase (New England Biolabs). DNA fragments of 200 to 800 bp were separated using electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels and extracted with the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA). Next, DNA fragments were ligated to adapters (SNX/SNX reverse linker).

Enrichment of microsatellite DNA libraries

To selectively enrich microsatellite regions, the DNA was hybridized with biotin-labeled probes (GT)10 and (CT)10 in the presence of 50 µL of hybridization solution. The mixture was heated to 95 ℃ for 15 min, incubated at 60 ℃ for 12 h, and captured using Streptavidin paramagnetic particles (Promega). Single-stranded DNA was eluted in low-TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 0.1 mM EDTA) at 95 ℃ for 15 min. PCR amplification was performed using Vent (-exo) polymerase (New England Biolabs) with a mixture containing 10 µL DNA, 4 µL of 10 µM SNX primer, 0.3 µL Vent polymerase, 4 µL dNTP mix, 5 µL of 10x Thermopol buffer, and 26.7 µL of sterilized water. The PCR conditions consisted of denaturation at 96 ℃ for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 96 ℃ for 45 s, 62 ℃ for 1 min, and 72 ℃ for 2 min, with a final elongation step at 72 ℃ for 5 min.

Cloning selection

Double-stranded DNA fragments were digested with NheI, the pUC18 vector was cut with XbaI. After dephosphorylation using calf intestinal phosphatase, 100 ng of DNA fragments were ligated to the vector in a reaction. Ligations were incubated at 16 ℃ for overnight. The ligated products were transformed into the XL1-Blue MRF strain (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA, USA) using cold shock and plated on LB agar with IPTG and X-Gal for blue/white screening. White colonies were screened by PCR using M13 forward/reverse primers, and the PCR products were confirmed using 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. Positive colonies were cultured and isolated with the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen), and sequenced by Macrogen (Seoul, Republic of Korea). Primer sequences for PCR were designed using PRIMER 3 software (ver. 0.4.0) [37].

Microsatellite genotyping and mtDNA sequencing

Primers were designed for 102 potential microsatellite regions identified from the sequenced genome and tested using PCR. PCR reactions included 10 ng genomic DNA, Ex-Taq polymerase (TaKaRa, Japan), Ex-Taq buffer, dNTP mixture, M13 tailed forward and reverse primers, and fluorescently labeled M13 primers [38] in a total volume of 20 µL. PCR conditions followed initial denaturation at 94 ℃ for 5 min, 30 cycles of denaturation (94 ℃, 30 s), annealing (56–58 ℃, 45 s), and extension (72 ℃, 45 s). The final extension was performed at 72 ℃ for 10 min, and the reactions were held at 4 ℃. The PCR products were mixed with GeneScan™ 500 ROX size standard (Applied Biosystems) and HiDi™ formamide, denatured at 95 ℃ for 2 min, and analyzed on an ABI 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems). Allele sizes were determined using the GeneMarker® software (ver. 2.6.7) [39]. From the 102 microsatellite candidate loci initially identified, loci were screened stepwise for their suitability in population analysis. Candidate markers were evaluated for amplification success, reproducibility, polymorphism, and peak quality during genotyping. Loci showing weak or inconsistent amplification, monomorphism, ambiguous allele calling, or excessive stutter patterns were excluded. The final seven loci were selected because they produced clear and stable genotypes, showed relatively high polymorphism and PIC values, and were suitable for reliable scoring across individuals. Additional information for the final marker set, including allele number, heterozygosity, PIC, and null allele assessment, is provided in Supplementary Table S2. The successfully developed microsatellite marker sequences have been deposited in GenBank under the following accession numbers: JX179157, JX179159, JX179160, and JX179164–JX179168.

The primers for Cyt b of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) were obtained from the study by Kim et al. [40] (MKCOB_F: AAACATAARATASGTCATAATTCTTGC, enkCOB_R: GTGGGAGTTARAATCTC), and PCR was performed using a MJ Research PTC-220 Peltier Thermal Cycler DNA. PCR was performed using the AccuPower® PCR Premix Kit (BIONEER Co., Daejeon, Korea). The reaction mixture consisted of 1 µL genomic DNA, forward and reverse primer (5.0 mM each), for a final volume of 20 µL. The PCR conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 ℃ for 30 s, annealing at 52 ℃ for 30 s, and extension at 72 ℃ for 30 s. A final extension was carried out at 72 ℃ for 10 min. Amplified Cyt b fragments were sequenced bidirectionally by Macrogen (Seoul, Republic of Korea) using an ABI 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems) in the specimen-based mtDNA dataset generated by Keun-Sik Kim in 2012. Raw forward and reverse chromatograms were visually inspected and edited in Geneious Prime (ver. 2026.0.2.), and consensus sequences were generated only when the sequence signals were reliable in both directions. Low-quality terminal regions were trimmed prior to alignment, and only unambiguous nucleotide sites were retained for haplotype identification. Only representative Cyt b haplotype sequences from this specimen-based dataset were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers JX179169-JX179175.

For mitochondrial DNA analysis, Cyt b sequences were obtained from individuals collected in 2012 from the Nakdong River and Seomjin River populations. Corresponding mtDNA sequence data were not included for the 2021 populations, because additional mitochondrial datasets could not be secured under the strict sampling restrictions applied to this endangered species. Therefore, the mtDNA dataset analyzed in this study was limited to the 2012 collections.

Microsatellite and mtDNA genetic diversity analyses

MICRO-CHECKER software (ver. 2.2.3) [41] was employed to detect potential scoring errors in microsatellite loci. Genetic diversity parameters, including the number of alleles (NA), expected heterozygosity (HE), and observed heterozygosity (HO), were calculated using the CERVUS software (ver. 3.0) [42]. Population inbreeding coefficients (FIS) and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) analyses were performed with GENEPOP (ver. 4.2) [43] and ARLEQUIN software (ver. 3.5) [44]. Population bottlenecks were analyzed using two approaches: the BOTTLENECK software (ver. 1.2.02) [45], which tests for heterozygosity excess under the infinite allele model (IAM) [46], and the two-phase model (TPM) and stepwise mutation model (SMM) [47]. For the TPM, a variance of 10% and 90% SMM was applied, with each model running 10,000 iterations. The significance of results was assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test [48]. The effective population size (Ne) was estimated using linkage disequilibrium analysis with the LDNe software (ver. 1.31) [36].

Haplotype and nucleotide diversity were calculated using DnaSP software (ver. 5.0) [49]. Phylogenetic relationships among mtDNA sequences were visualized using a median-joining network analysis performed with Network software (ver. 10.2.0.0) [50].

Population genetic structure and year-specific gene flow

Microsatellite and mtDNA data were analyzed for pairwise genetic differentiation (FST) among populations using ARLEQUIN software (ver. 3.05) [44]. The significance of pairwise FST values was assessed using permutation tests in ARLEQUIN. Because all pairwise comparisons were highly significant, correction for multiple testing did not alter the interpretation. The STRUCTURE software (ver. 2.3) [51] was utilized to estimate the minimum number of genetically homogeneous groups (K) based on microsatellite genotypes using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) Bayesian approach. K-values ranging from 1 to 10 were tested with 10 iterations per K. Each run included a burn-in period of 50,000 iterations followed by 100,000 iterations. The analysis applied an admixture model suitable for the sampled aquatic systems. The most likely value of K was selected following the methodology using STRUCTURE SELECTOR [52]. A discriminant analysis of principal components (DAPC) of the microsatellite data set was performed on the population using the R package ADEGENET (ver. 2.1.3) [53].

The year-specific STRUCTURE analyses were used as supplementary tests to determine whether the major genetic partition between the Nakdong and Seomjin River populations was consistently recovered within each sampling year (2012 and 2021). Bar plots for the year-specific analyses are presented in Fig. S3. In addition to the analysis including all four population samples, year-specific DAPC analyses were conducted separately for the 2012 samples (12ND vs. 12SJ) and the 2021 samples (21ND vs. 21SJ). Because the samples were collected nine years apart, combining all individuals in a single clustering analysis could confound spatial differentiation between river systems with temporal genetic change within river systems. Therefore, year-specific DAPC was used as a supplementary analysis to test whether the separation between the Nakdong and Seomjin River populations was consistently observed within each sampling year (Fig. S1-S2).

Recent migration rates were estimated using BA3 (ver. 3) based on multilocus microsatellite genotypes [54]. Because the samples were collected in two different years (2012 and 2021), BA3 analyses were conducted separately for each sampling year (12ND vs. 12SJ; 21ND vs. 21SJ) to avoid confounding temporal genetic change with contemporary gene flow among populations.

Results

Microsatellite and mitochondrial DNA genetic diversity

A total of 102 candidate fragments were screened for seven markers (Table S2). Non-amplified markers and markers without polymorphisms in each population were excluded. The seven selected primers (MKms205, MKms256, MKms363, MKms631, MKms633, MKms747, MKms781) showed high polymorphic information content (PIC) values in the initial screening dataset of 16 individuals from two populations (12SJ and 12ND) all of which showed values greater than 0.770 with a mean PIC of 0.839 (Table S2). The number of alleles (NA) ranged from eight to 19, and the polymorphism information content was > 0.5, indicating that these were appropriate markers. HWE analysis showed no significant deviations at any of the seven loci, and thus all were included in the population analysis. Genotyping with MICRO-CHECKER revealed no evidence of scoring errors or null alleles (Table S2).

Seven loci were analyzed for genetic diversity indices in the four populations (Table 1). NA ranged from 8.7 to 12.4, HO ranged from 0.783 to 0.832, and HE ranged from 0.822 to 0.827. When allelic richness (AR) was considered to account for differences in sample size, the temporal patterns differed between the two river systems. In the Nakdong River population, AR increased slightly from 10.6 in 2012 to 11.0 in 2021, whereas in the Seomjin River population it decreased from 9.2 to 8.0. Thus, unlike the simple reduction in NA observed in both populations, AR suggested a slight increase in genetic variation in ND but a clear decline in SJ over time. We found that the 21ND population deviated from HWE, whereas the other three populations followed HWE. Positive inbreeding coefficients (FIS) were observed in the 12ND, 21ND, and 12SJ populations, but these were not significant. The NA values by year for each population were 12ND = 12.4, 21ND = 11.1, 12SJ = 10.3, and 21SJ = 8.7, indicating that the number of alleles decreased in both populations in 2021 compared to 2012. While HO remained similar for 12ND (0.783) and 21ND (0.787), genetic diversity increased at 12SJ (0.783) and 21SJ (0.832).

Bottleneck analysis

The infinite mutation model (IAM) identified significant bottlenecks in the 12ND, 12SJ, and 21SJ populations (P < 0.05). Among the four populations, the effective population size ranged from 19 to 43, excluding the 12SJ population, where estimation was not possible. The population with the largest effective population size was 21ND, and the smallest at 19 in 21SJ (Table 2). The 95% confidence intervals (CI) were 13 to 30 and 20 to 395, respectively (Table 2).

Population structure and genetic differentiation analyses and gene flow

In the mtDNA dataset, the FST value for Cyt b was significant, indicating high genetic differentiation between the 12ND and 12SJ populations (FST = 0.983, P < 0.001; Table 3). In contrast, the FST values derived from the microsatellite data were all significant but lower: 0.046 for 12ND–21ND and 0.026 for 12SJ–21SJ, both pairs belonging to the same watershed and thus showing relatively low genetic differentiation. However, when comparing populations from different watersheds (12ND, 21ND vs. 12SJ, 21SJ), FST values ranged from 0.110 to 0.132.

Bayesian clustering analysis, using STRUCTURE, showed the highest ΔK value at K = 2 (Fig. 3). At K = 3, the first cluster included 12ND, the second 21ND, and the third 12SJ and 21SJ. The non-model-based DAPC analysis yielded similar results, also suggesting a division into two main populations, consistent with the STRUCTURE findings (Figs. 4 and 5). Year-specific STRUCTURE analyses also supported clear genetic differentiation between the Nakdong and Seomjin River populations within each sampling year (Fig. S3). In both the 2012 and 2021 datasets, the highest ΔK values were observed at K = 2, indicating that two clusters represented the strongest level of subdivision within each year. Year-specific DAPC analyses also supported genetic differentiation between the Nakdong and Seomjin River populations within each sampling year. In the 2012 dataset, individuals from 12ND and 12SJ were clearly separated along the first discriminant function (Fig. S1). A similar pattern was observed in the 2021 dataset, in which 21ND and 21SJ also formed distinct clusters with little overlap (Fig. S2). These supplementary results indicated that the genetic separation between the two river systems was not driven solely by temporal differences among samples, but was consistently maintained within both sampling years.

Recent migration rate estimates based on microsatellite data indicated that both river populations were characterized by very high within-population retention and low between-population gene flow in both sampling years (Fig. 6). In 2012, the self-assignment proportions were 0.9880 in 12SJ and 0.9877 in 12ND, whereas migration rates between populations were low and nearly symmetric (12SJ→12ND = 0.0120; 12ND→12SJ = 0.0123). In 2021, similarly high self-assignment proportions were observed in 21SJ (0.9883) and 21ND (0.9877), but migration was slightly asymmetric, with a higher estimated rate from 21SJ to 21ND (0.0167) than in the opposite direction (0.0114). These results indicate limited recent gene flow between the Seomjin and Nakdong River populations and support the maintenance of genetic distinctiveness between the two river systems.

Discussion

Genetic diversity and structure through population genetic monitoring

In this study, we evaluated the genetic diversity and population structure of two endemic and endangered populations of M. koreensis (Seomjin River and Nakdong River) at two time points separated by nine years, using a panel of seven highly polymorphic microsatellite loci (all PIC > 0.770, mean = 0.839). This approach aligns with the “few loci with many alleles” principle [55] and is supported by evidence that even a small number of hypervariable microsatellite markers can provide high discrimination power [56, 57]. For example [55], detected subtle genetic differentiation between Chinese and Japanese chub mackerel populations using only eight microsatellites, and [58] resolved six distinct Atlantic striped bass stocks with an eight‑locus panel. Our seven‑locus microsatellite set yielded robust FST estimates, accurate individual assignments, and reliable parentage analyses, while offering significant cost and time efficiencies [59]. Consequently, this high‑PIC marker panel represents an optimized strategy for conservation genetics under resource‑limited conditions. We further corroborated population structure by analyzing the mitochondrial cytochrome b (Cyt b) sequence variation in both river populations. Given that M. koreensis is restricted to these two river systems on the Korean Peninsula, our dual-marker approach provides critical insights into its genetic architecture, informing targeted conservation and management efforts for this imperiled species [60].

We used seven microsatellite markers for population genetic diversity analysis of M. koreensis, an endangered fish species. The average number of alleles observed in M. koreensis (NA = 8.7–12.4) was intermediate among related Gobioninae species, being lower than in M. rapidus (NA = 22.4), G. naktongensis (NA = 16), and P. tenuicorpa (NA = 27.9), but higher than or similar to that reported for P. nigra (NA = 4.4–8.1) [27, 30, 61, 62]. In general, endangered species tend to have low genetic diversity due to various factors such as genetic drift and inbreeding [63]. The average HO for genetic diversity at the species level was 0.796, which was similar to or higher than the values found for other threatened fish of the same subfamily (P. tenuicorpa, HO = 0.798; P. nigra, HO = 0.640; M. rapidus, HO = 0.757) [27, 30, 61, 62]. Additionally, it was higher than the values observed in M. rapidus and P. nigra and similar to P. tenuicorpa [27, 30, 61]. These observations suggest that the endangered status of M. koreensis did not have a significant impact on its genetic diversity. However, this species is still at risk of extinction due to weir construction and habitat destruction.

The inclusion of AR provided additional insight into temporal changes in genetic diversity by accounting for unequal sample sizes among populations. In the Nakdong River population, AR increased slightly from 10.6 in 2012 to 11.0 in 2021, suggesting that the decline in NA should be interpreted cautiously and may partly reflect sample-size differences. Although HO was maintained or slightly increased in 2021, the number of alleles decreased, indicating that heterozygosity and allelic diversity did not change in parallel. This pattern may reflect the retention of common variation despite the loss of rare alleles.

By contrast, the Seomjin River population showed a decrease in AR from 9.2 to 8.0, supporting the interpretation that allelic diversity declined over time in this population. The decrease in both NA and AR suggests that this reduction is less likely to be explained solely by sample-size differences. Although negative FIS values may indicate heterozygote excess, the causes of this pattern cannot be determined from the present data alone.

Overall, these results suggest that genetic diversity was broadly maintained at the level of heterozygosity, but that some low-frequency alleles may have been lost over time. Continued genetic monitoring is therefore needed to determine whether these temporal changes reflect stochastic sampling effects or ongoing erosion of genetic variation.

In conservation genetics, small population sizes accelerate extinction due to extinction factors [64]. At small population sizes, inbreeding depression is one of the important genetic factors that can affect extinction risk [16]. However, these Ne estimates should be interpreted with caution because they were derived from a relatively small number of microsatellite loci and modest sample sizes in some populations, both of which can reduce the precision and stability of LD-based effective population size estimates [36]. Therefore, the low Ne values reported here are best regarded as preliminary indicators of genetic vulnerability rather than exact demographic estimates. The effective population size was determined to be 19 to 43, which is less than the effective population size of 100 needed to maintain the population size in the short term, suggesting that it is vulnerable to extinction [16]. Compared to 12ND in 2012, the 21ND sampled in 2021 had similar but slightly increased effective population sizes. Effective population size may vary depending on the sample. This effective population size was approximately similar when comparing 2012 (Ne = 39) and 2021 (Ne = 43). Therefore, these results suggest that M. koreensis is at risk of short-term genetic vulnerability [16, 64]. Additionally, 21ND and 21SJ, which were recently sampled in 2021, have a very small effective population size, so there is a possibility that they will become more vulnerable to extinction if inbreeding continues. In the genetic differentiation analysis, population pairs sampled within the same watershed (12ND vs. 21ND and 12SJ vs. 21SJ) showed low genetic differentiation in the microsatellite data, supporting temporal continuity within each river system.

mtDNA genetic differentiation among water systems (Nakdong River vs. Seomjin River) showed a very high level of differentiation (FST = 0.983). Additionally, the haplotype network showed 24 genetic mutations between 12ND and 12SJ, clearly dividing the Nakdong River and Seomjin Riverwater system. Although the mtDNA Cyt b dataset showed a much higher FST value (0.983) than the microsatellite dataset, these results are not necessarily contradictory [10].

In the present study, the mtDNA result reflects the near-complete separation of haplotypes between the 2012 Nakdong and Seomjin River populations, whereas the microsatellite pairwise FST values between watersheds (0.110–0.132) still indicate clear and significant population differentiation. Such differences in magnitude are expected because mtDNA is maternally inherited, haploid, and nonrecombining, and therefore has a smaller effective population size and is more sensitive to genetic drift and lineage sorting than nuclear microsatellite markers [65]. Accordingly, the mtDNA data in this study appear to capture stronger historical divergence between watersheds, while the microsatellite results support the same major geographic subdivision at the nuclear level rather than suggesting an opposing pattern.

Bayesian clustering analysis showed the highest ΔK value at K = 2, indicating that this was the most strongly supported uppermost level of population subdivision in the microsatellite dataset [51]. Although additional clustering patterns were also observed at higher K values, K = 2 was considered the most biologically interpretable solution because it consistently reflected the primary division between the Nakdong River and Seomjin River systems. This interpretation was also concordant with the pairwise FST results, DAPC clustering, and mitochondrial haplotype differentiation, all of which supported a major separation between the two watersheds.

The year-specific STRUCTURE and DAPC analyses further strengthen the conclusion that the major genetic subdivision in M. koreensis reflects spatial divergence between the Nakdong and Seomjin River systems rather than temporal variation between the 2012 and 2021 samples. In both years, the two river populations were clearly separated, and STRUCTURE consistently recovered the strongest clustering signal at K = 2. Together with the significant pairwise FST values and the low recent migration rates estimated by BA3, these results indicate that watershed-level genetic structure has been maintained over time. Nevertheless, the STRUCTURE results should be interpreted with caution, given the limited number of microsatellite loci and the relatively small sample sizes in some populations. Therefore, K = 2 should be regarded as the most parsimonious and biologically meaningful clustering result in the present study, rather than the only possible interpretation of the data.

Migration estimations using BA3 data revealed that gene flow between the Nakdong River and Seomjin River populations remained consistently low in both 2012 and 2021, while the rate of gene retention within the populations was high. Microsatellite analysis confirmed a strong population structure, while STRUCTURE and DAPC analyses showed bifurcated clusters, and mitochondrial DNA haplotype network analysis revealed distinct genetic differences between the two river basins. Taken together, all this evidence suggests genetic structural differentiation between the Nakdong and Seomjin Rivers. Although slight asymmetry in migration direction was observed in 2021, the absolute migration rate remained negligible, suggesting that recent dispersion was insufficient to induce genetic homogenization between the two populations.

Since the two water systems are clearly divided, it was suggested that caution should be taken regarding genetic contamination between water systems. However, if M. koreensis in the Nakdong River basin, which has low genetic diversity (21ND, HO: 0.787; 21SJ, HO: 0.832), becomes locally extinct, restoration through reintroduction can be considered, but genetic diversity at the species level must be considered. It is judged to be evolutionarily more efficient to conserve M. koreensis in the Nakdong River water system (21ND). The reason is that the effective population size is larger than that of 21SJ. However, considering species-level diversity, the population in urgent need of conservation is thought to be the 21SJ population, which has the smallest effective population size. However, the mtDNA-based interpretation should be treated with caution because the Cyt b dataset was limited to the 2012 populations and did not include corresponding data from the 2021 collections. Accordingly, the mitochondrial results should be regarded as supplementary evidence for divergence between river systems, and future studies with complete mtDNA data across sampling years are needed for a full temporal evaluation.

According to the results of STRUCTURE and DAPC, it is divided into the Nakdong River and Seomjin River water system, and the existence of the two populations is judged to be advantageous for the continuation of the species compared to M. rapidus due to the high genetic diversity at the species level [30, 63, 66]. In the 2012 mtDNA dataset, the observed haplotype count was lower in 12ND than in 12SJ, and haplotype diversity was also lower in 12ND. However, because the Cyt b dataset was limited to the 2012 collections and was restricted in size, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously and not overgeneralized as definitive evidence of lower within-population mitochondrial diversity in the Nakdong River population. M. rapidus, an endangered species of the same genus, has three habitats but has been reported to have only one population [30]. In the case of M. rapidus, despite its high genetic diversity, its population declined so rapidly that it was unable to actively respond to environmental changes [30]. Therefore, like M. rapidus, the Nakdong River population of M. koreensis is also likely to experience a rapid population decline due to habitat destruction and climate change. Considering the genetic structure, diversity, and effective population size, the most urgent population is the 21SJ, and the second, considering the case of M. rapidus that exists in the same Nakdong River water system, is the 21ND population. As a result, both 21SJ and 21ND populations appear to be in urgent need of conservation. In the future, continuous genetic monitoring of genetic diversity, population structure, and effective population size will be required, together with reinforcement through captive breeding.

Conservation implications

Genetics is an essential component of conservation strategies. M. koreensis is an endemic species that only lives in the Seomjin River and Nakdong Rivers on the Korean Peninsula. As an endemic species, its extinction in these river systems would represent a global loss [23]. This study is the first to analyze the genetic diversity and structure of the M. koreensis population, and will be helpful in establishing conservation strategies.

Species management units (MUs) are usually established by grouping genetically similar populations to avoid genetic disturbance [66]. The populations of M. koreensis in the Seomjin River and Nakdong River water systems are clearly divided genetically. Therefore, genetic mixing should not occur between individual water populations, and reintroduction strategies can be developed assuming local extinction of one or the other. However, in the current situation, it is believed that conserving the populations of each water system will be more evolutionarily efficient and diversity. This is because the higher the genetic diversity, the more actively we can respond to environmental changes [11].

M. koreensis populations require protection because their genetic diversity is lower than that of M. rapidus and their effective population sizes are small in the 21ND and 21SJ populations. Additionally, considering genetic differences, it is necessary to select mothers that do not cause genetic contamination between each water system and perform artificial propagation to increase and maintain the population size [67]. Additionally, it is necessary to prevent habitat destruction of M. koreensis and establish conservation areas. Additionally, genetic monitoring of M. koreensis should be performed to assess genetic diversity over time. We believe that genetic studies on populations from the Nakdong River and Seomjin River water systems will help conserve the M. koreensis species in the future.

Over the past decade, genetic diversity in both the Nakdong and Seomjin Rivers has increased, although there has been a loss of rare alleles. Future management measures should focus on identifying and breeding individuals with rare alleles to boost genetic diversity. We believe that continued genetic studies of populations from both the Nakdong and Seomjin River systems will play a key role in the long-term conservation of M. koreensis.

Conclusions

We developed seven novel microsatellite markers for M. koreensis and used them, together with genetic monitoring, to assess the species’ genetic diversity and structure. Genetic diversity in the 21ND population was not reduced and persisted compared to the 12ND population. The 21SJ population had increased genetic diversity compared to the 12SJ population, but the number of alleles had decreased. 12ND, 12SJ, and 21SJ have experienced bottlenecks and have extremely low effective population sizes of less than 50, making it difficult to sustain the species in the short term. The genetic structure between ND and SJ was clearly divided, and the STRUCTURE results supported this with K = 2, FST values, DAPC, and haplotype network methods. Therefore, when establishing a conservation strategy, should be managed as separate units, and population reinforcement through artificial propagation was necessary.
