Life-threatening hemorrhage during removal of a Nuss bar embedded in the sternum: a case report
Authors: Ruixin Xu, Zhiqiang Zou, Mei Zhang, Ning Xin, Yu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlate erosion of the sternum leading to hemorrhage during NUSS bar removal procedure for pectus excavatum is a very rare complication, and the few cases reported worldwide are currently only briefly described in the statistical results.Case presentationA 22-year-old female patient underwent a surgical repair for pectus excavatum(Nuss procedure)2.5 years ago. During the removal of the bars in our center, we found that there was great resistance when removing the upper Nuss bar, and a rapid flow of bright red hemorrhage flowed from both incisions immediately after removal. A longitudinal sternotomy confirmed that the plate had eroded into the sternum and damaged the internal thoracic artery during removal.ConclusionWe should pay attention to the interpretation of preoperative images before the removal of orthopedic plates for pectus excavatum, and the correct preoperative preparation and intraoperative strategy can help to reduce the incidence of hemorrhage.Keywords: Sternal erosion, Internal thoracic artery, Pectus excavatum, Nuss bar removal, Intraoperative complications

Background

In 1998, Nuss et al. [1] reported a new minimally invasive surgical repair for pectus excavatum (Nuss procedure), which has gradually become the current method of choice for pectus excavatum correction due to its safety and favorable results. As the application of the Nuss procedure has become more widespread and intensive, the complications of this procedure, especially serious complications such as persistent arrhythmias, sternal infections, and cardiac or peripheral vascular injuries, have also been gradually recognized [2–7]. However, at the same time, there is often insufficient attention paid to the serious complications that may result from the removal of Nuss bars, and the few cases reported worldwide are now only briefly described in statistical results [8–10]. This case of hemorrhage due to sternal erosion and injury of the internal thoracic artery is the first report that have detailed imaging data and intraoperative images and it will provide a helpful reference for the management of difficult Nuss bar removal.

Case presentation

The patient was a 21-year-old female who had undergone a Nuss procedure in August 2022 in another hospital, and two Nuss bars were inserted behind the sternum. Four days after the operation, she underwent thoracoscopic exploration of the right thoracic cavity and clot removal due to right-sided pleural effusion. In January 2025, she came to our hospital for Nuss bar removal. After removing the fixation wires on both sides, we straighten the bars moderately and then loosen them, and the flipper was used to remove the bar along the right side of the chest wall obliquely downward in the direction of the plate curvature. The steel plate on the side of the foot was successfully removed by us. The head side of the plate was removed with significant resistance, and a rapid flow of bright red hemorrhage flowed from both incisions soon after removal. Immediately, the sternum was split longitudinally, and the heart was found to be undamaged. The longitudinal section of the sternum showed obvious indentation of the Nuss bar, and the left internal thoracic artery and its branches at the upper edge showed active bleeding, which was successfully controlled through suture ligation along the rib margin. Upon re-exploration, no active bleeding was observed from either thoracic incision. We proceeded with chest closure after placing a mediastinal drainage tube in the retrosternal space, supplemented by subcutaneous drainage tubes bilaterally. The patient’s postoperative recovery was uneventful. All three drainage tubes were removed sequentially by postoperative day 5, following which the patient was discharged in stable condition. A transient drop in blood pressure occurred during the procedure and blood loss was approximately 1200 ml.

Discussion and conclusions

Although complications associated with Nuss bar removal procedure are rare and mostly easy to manage, some serious complications such as cardiac or macrovascular injuries are often life-threatening when mishandled. Frank-Martin Haecker [10] reported a case of cardiac rupture leading to fatal hemorrhage during removal of an Nuss bar for pectus excavatum, which was urgently treated with a clamshell incision. Sarah Bouchard [11] reported 4 cases of severe cardiac injury during minimally invasive surgery for pectus excavatum, one of which occurred 6 months after surgery when the bar was removed due to chest pain.

In the case reported in this paper, the upper bar was locked and difficult to move after about 6 cm of plate removal (Fig. 1a), and a deep indentation of about 4 mm was seen after longitudinal splitting of the sternum (Fig. 1b). Severe sternal erosion can be seen by performing a two-dimensional (Fig. 2) and three-dimensional (Fig. 3) reconstruction of the preoperative spiral CT. It is assumed that the smooth part of the plate passed with fair resistance, but the jagged part of the caudal end injured the left internal thoracic artery during forceful pulling, resulting in active bleeding. Nina S. Cohen [12] and Notrica [13] reported a case of hemorrhage caused by injury to the retrosternal vein and artery during removal of orthopedic plates, which was successfully stopped by right and median sternotomy, respectively. Mustafa Yüksel [14] performed internal thoracic arteriography before and after the Nuss procedure for pectus excavatum in 34 patients from his center and showed that the blood flow was compromised in 15 patients (44%), of whom 12 (35%) had complete occlusion of at least one internal thoracic artery. Due to insufficient cognition and preparation for sternal erosion and bleeding from parasternal vessels before this surgery, what could have been observed and managed through a minimally invasive subxiphoid incision ultimately transformed into a life-threatening hemorrhage and a larger longitudinal sternotomy. Fortunately, the patients were ultimately safely extricated.

Identifying and preparing for those at high risk for bleeding during Nuss bar removal can help us minimize similar risks. Professor Mohamed R. Aly [15] of Mayo Medical Center analyzed the complications of 1555 patients undergoing Nuss bar removal for pectus excavatum and found that sternal erosion, plate displacement, and reconstruct of a previously failed sternal repair were independent risk factors for hemorrhage. Nuss [16] pointed out that the plate should be straightened as much as possible prior to plate removal to prevent the curved plate from creating a hooking force that could result in inadvertent injury to the chest during removal. De Campos JR [17] used silicone tubing to encase the serrations on the caudal side of the plate to reduce the risk of serrations pulling and tearing the chest wall and mediastinal tissues. Deol PK [18] suggested the use of transesophageal echocardiography to dynamically observe changes in the cardiac silhouette, pericardial effusion, and arrhythmias during the removal of the plate in order to avoid cardiac injury. For bleeding in the plate tunnel, Luzia Toselli [19] used hemostatic strips for hemostasis, which is similar to method of using gauze strips to compress against the esophageal bed in esophageal evacuation surgery. All of the above measures are useful in preventing injury to important tissue structures of the mediastinum. We have summarized our experience in the prevention and management of intraoperative hemorrhage in Table 1 for reference, taking into account the relevant experience of our center. We now highlighted two critical points for greater emphasis: First, considering the close proximity between the sternum and bar, reverse bending (rather than simple straightening) of the bar during removal can reduce friction against the posterior sternal wall. Second, most importantly, creating an additional subxiphoid endoscopic port to visualize the retrosternal space and surrounding structures provides the most reliable method for preventing such hemorrhagic complications.

Perioperative countermeasures to prevent and control bleeding

In conclusion, Nuss bar removal needs to be performed in a large, experienced cardiothoracic surgery center, with preoperative patient-doctor communication adequately explaining the serious risks that may occur during plate removal. Perfect preoperative and intraoperative preparation and close postoperative monitoring are important measures to minimize perioperative fatal bleeding.
