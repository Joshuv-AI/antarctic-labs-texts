Association of dietary total antioxidant capacity with KRAS gene mutation in metastatic colorectal cancer patients: a case-control study
Authors: Fahmideh Bagrezaei, Bahram Pourghassem Gargari, Sorayya Kheirouri, Reza Eghdam Zamiri, Sedigheh Yusefinejad, Roghayeh Molani-Gol, Mohammad Alizadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRecent studies have shown that nutritional factors may contribute to KRAS gene mutation. This study aims to explore the association between dietary total antioxidant capacity (DTAC) and KRAS mutant status in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC).MethodsThis case-control study was conducted on 90 patients with mCRC. Participants’ dietary intake was assessed using a validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) of 147 items. The ferric-reducing antioxidant potential (FRAP) method was used to determine DTAC. Multivariate analysis evaluated diet by DTAC categories, and Logistic regression examined the relationship between DTAC and KRAS gene mutation, adjusting for confounders.ResultsThe mean DTAC was 14.10 ± 6.10 and 15.95 ± 8.52 for case and controls, respectively, with no significant difference (p = 0.300). An inverse, but non-significant, association was observed between a higher score of DTAC and odds of KRAS mutation [(crude; OR = 0.640, 95%CI = 0.279–1.47, P = 0.293) (adjusted; OR: 0.309, 95% CI: 0.077–1.236, P = 0.097)]. The mean fat intake of participants among the case and control groups was significantly different in the higher scores category of DTAC [(93.91 ± 30.77) vs. (114 ± 42.15), P = 0.010].ConclusionThe findings suggest a non-significant relationship between DTAC and KRAS gene mutation. Future well-designed longitudinal observational studies with higher populations are warranted to investigate the comprehensive relationship between the DTAC and KRAS mutant gene.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12263-025-00782-9.Keywords: Dietary total antioxidant capacity, DTAC, KRAS gene, Metastases, Colorectal cancer

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a heterogeneous disease with multiple genetic alterations [1]. KRAS (Kirsten rat sarcoma virus) oncogene mutations are somatic mutations that arise spontaneously in tumor tissue during cancer development. These mutations occur in 30–50% of CRC cases and are linked to a poorer prognosis [2, 3]. However, it has been found that patients with CRC KRAS mutations have an inadequate response to anti-EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor) treatment [4]. Free radicals play a crucial role in cancer development by damaging DNA and causing mutations that increase cell proliferation, transforming normal cells into malignant ones [5].

Among environmental factors related to CRC, diet has a crucial role in pathogenesis, prevention, and management. In this context, studies have indicated that the interaction between molecular changes and environmental factors, particularly dietary components, can influence the risk of KRAS mutations [6, 7]. Dietary antioxidants affect cancer processes through various mechanisms. Polyphenolic compounds like resveratrol and curcumin inhibit KRAS downstream signaling pathways, including mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PI3K)/AKT (protein kinase B), by binding to specific sites on signaling proteins, thus blocking their activity and reducing cancer cell proliferation [8, 9]. Additionally, antioxidants can disrupt lipid metabolism in cancer cells by inhibiting fatty acid synthase (FASN), which is crucial for tumors with KRAS mutations that depend on enhanced lipid synthesis for growth and survival [10]. Furthermore, lutein, another powerful antioxidant, contributes to these protective effects by scavenging free radicals and reducing oxidative stress [11]. In vitro experimental studies demonstrated that phenolic compounds like quercetin, luteolin, and ursolic acid effectively inhibited the expression of KRAS-dependent proteins, thereby reducing tumor growth [12]. At the epigenetic level, dietary antioxidants can alter the DNA methylation pattern in the promoter region of genes involved in cell metabolism. For instance, EGCG from green tea reversed hypermethylation of tumor suppressor gene promoters and inhibited tumor growth in head and neck cancer cells [13]. Dietary antioxidants significantly influence KRAS mutations and tumor growth. These findings suggest that dietary interventions could be valuable in cancer prevention and therapy.

While KRAS mutations are recognized as key drivers in colorectal carcinogenesis, there is limited epidemiological evidence regarding how the cancer risk associated with these mutations is modulated by specific dietary components [6]. A prospective cohort study found a significant inverse relationship between higher intake of healthy plant-based foods and KRAS mutation incidence in CRC patients, with the strongest association observed in wild-type KRAS tumors [14]. In addition, studies also examined the relationship between dietary fats and Western and healthy food patterns with KRAS mutation in CRC patients. The investigators of these studies reported that a healthy diet protects against CRC from mutation [7, 15].

Dietary total antioxidant capacity (DTAC) refers to a diet’s ability to neutralize free radicals and mitigate oxidative stress. This approach to healthy eating focuses on assessing DTAC, as different antioxidants in the diet can work together additively or synergistically, making it an essential tool for evaluating dietary quality and its overall health impact [16]. Additionally, previous studies have indicated that DTAC is linked to a reduced risk of breast, lung, and colon cancer [17, 18]. However, the mechanisms of this relationship, especially in patients with KRAS mutations, are not yet fully understood. From a clinical perspective, investigating the relationship between DTAC in patients with mCRC KRAS mutations is of particular importance. Studies have shown that dietary regimens rich in antioxidants may be associated with improved responses to chemotherapy and increased overall survival. These effects are likely mediated through multiple mechanisms, including the reduction of oxidative stress, modulation of systemic inflammation, and increased sensitivity to chemotherapy drugs [19].

Although some studies examined the connection between nutritional factors and KRAS mutations in CRC patients by focusing on individual antioxidants, research on the relationship between the DTAC score and KRAS mutations is limited, and based on our best knowledge, no studies have been conducted to date on the association between DTAC score and KRAS gene mutation. Therefore, this study aimed to fill this gap by evaluating the association between the DTAC score and the odds of KRAS gene mutations.

Materials and methods

Participants

This case-control study used convenience sampling from October 2023 to May 2024 at the oncology department of Shahid Madani Hospital in Tabriz, Iran. The sample size was determined based on the frequency of KRAS gene mutations, resulting in the selection of 90 patients. These patients were divided into two equal groups of 45 based on their genetic characteristics: the case group with KRAS mutations and the control group with wild-type KRAS, indicating the absence of mutations [20]. The patient selection followed predetermined inclusion criteria in which the patients in the case group were adults (aged > 24 years) with an established diagnosis of metastatic CRC (via CT imaging modalities), confirmed KRAS gene mutation, and undergoing chemotherapy. The control group had to meet eligibility requirements similar to those of the case group, except for not having a positive KRAS gene mutation. Exclusion criteria involved individuals with active infections, pregnant women, those with other malignancies, and participants who did not respond to over 50% of the study questions. This study was conducted under the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the ethics committee at Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (ID: IR. TBZMED.REC.1402.187). All participants gave informed consent.

Assessment of KRAS gene mutations

Targeted somatic mutations in specific codons of the KRAS gene were qualitatively identified using the Easy - KRAS kit from Diatech Pharmacogenetics (Supplementary Table 2). Tissue samples were collected from patients through biopsy techniques during colonoscopies and surgeries. Genomic DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples using a Qiagen Rapid DNA Extraction Kit. The extracted DNA came from tumor micro-selected enrichment areas. DNA was mixed with KRAS-specific primers, fluorescent probes, and a buffer/enzyme solution, then analyzed using a Bio-Rad Real-Time PCR system. The cycling conditions included denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, repeated for 40 cycles. After PCR, fluorescent signals were assessed to identify KRAS mutations. The results were analyzed by calculating ΔCT values and comparing them to reference values to identify specific mutations.

Dietary intake assessment

Dietary information from study participants over the past year was collected through face-to-face interviews using a validated 147-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). Further details on the questionnaire’s design and validity are available in prior publications [21]. Household measurements were used to convert daily food intakes into grams per day, and total daily energy and nutrient intakes were estimated with Nutritionist IV software.

Evaluation of DTAC

The DTAC score was determined using the ferric reducing/antioxidant power (FRAP) method reported by the USDA, expressed in millimoles per 100 g of food (µmol/100 g). The DTAC score reflects the total antioxidant capacity (TAC) of all foods, calculated from the sum of the TAC of each food item based on the FFQ [22]. Confidence in calculating DTAC using the FRAP assay stems from its accuracy, reliability, and reproducibility. It offers a direct measurement of total antioxidant power with high sensitivity and consistent results, making it a valuable tool for assessing antioxidant status and dietary patterns, as well as investigating disease risk. Its standardized nature enhances its credibility in research [23, 24].

Assessment of other variables

Participants underwent face-to-face interviews and completed medical history questionnaires that collected data on age, gender, smoking status (non-smoker, former smoker, current smoker), chronic diseases, and medication use. A dietitian measured participants’ weights to the nearest 100 g while they wore light clothing and no shoes. Height was measured to the nearest 0.5 cm using a tape measure. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing a person’s weight in kilograms by the square of their height in meters.

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Subsequently, we determined the median cut-off for the DTAC and classified participants accordingly. We used the Multivariate analysis and Chi-square to examine differences in terms of general characteristics between categories of DTAC. Multivariate analysis was also applied to evaluate significant differences in dietary intakes across categories of DTAC. The association of the DTAC and KRAS mutation was examined using logistic regression. In the first model, we controlled for age. The second model included adjustments for gender, metastatic sites, diabetes mellitus, chemotherapy drugs, family history of cancer, and total energy intake. The third model also accounted for BMI. The first DTAC category was the reference for all analyses conducted using SPSS version 18.0, with p-values considered significant < 0.05.

Results

This study was conducted on 90 patients diagnosed with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), which included 45 individuals in the case group with KRAS mutations and 45 individuals in the control group with KRAS wild type. The dietary intake of participants across different categories of DTAC is shown in Table 2. Discussion

The present study’s findings demonstrated no significant association between the DTAC score and the odds of KRAS gene mutation, though a non-significant trend suggested that higher antioxidant intake might reduce mutation likelihood.

While previous studies demonstrated binary effects limited to the KRAS-wildtype subgroup, our findings for the first time suggest a potential gradual pattern across the entire study population. This difference may stem from the more comprehensive TAC assessment approach that evaluates the complete spectrum of antioxidant compounds. Although this finding did not reach statistical significance, it provides new insights into the dynamic interaction between dietary factors and tumor molecular mechanisms and highlights the importance of accurately reporting biological patterns even when they don’t reach statistical thresholds [7, 14]. Our findings, alongside other studies, emphasized the complexity of the relationships between nutrition and carcinogenesis. These results suggested that certain nutritional interventions might have been effective regardless of KRAS mutation status, while others demonstrated specific subgroup effects. To fully understand these relationships, it is necessary to simultaneously examine multiple nutritional indicators and their related biological pathways.

In patients with mCRC KRAS mutations, who typically show a lower response to targeted anti-EGFR therapies, increased DTAC may exert therapeutic effects through alternative mechanisms. Antioxidant compounds can enhance the sensitivity of these tumors to chemotherapy by reducing intracellular oxidative stress and inhibiting DNA repair systems [19, 25]. However, the effects of dietary antioxidants depend on dosage and the tumor’s molecular context, as very high doses of antioxidant supplements may have adverse effects [26]. Therefore, emphasizing whole food sources of antioxidants rather than high-risk supplements is a more reasonable approach.

Fruits and vegetables contain natural pigments that have potent antioxidant activity and have been shown to be inversely associated with CRC [27]. However, there is no consensus on the association between DTAC intake and cancer risk. Numerous studies have examined the association between DTAC and the risk of cancer. Some studies reported a negative association between DTAC and cancer risk [18, 28–30], while others showed no significant link [31–33]. However, the number of studies on dietary antioxidant intake and total dietary antioxidant capacity in cancer patients with mutations is limited.

Some explanations exist for the association of dietary antioxidant factors with cancer mutation. For example, a study by Milena Matuszczak et al. found that the zinc-to-copper ratio in the blood could indicate cancer risk in BRCA1 mutation carriers. A ratio above 6.38 was linked to a lower cancer risk [34]. Ze-Bo Jiang et al. studied the effects of luteolin and apigenin on lung cancer with KRAS mutations. They found that these flavonoids suppress cancer cell growth, induce apoptosis, enhance immune function, increase tumor T-cell infiltration, reduce PD-L1 expression, and inhibit STAT3 phosphorylation [35]. Research conducted by Didier et al. highlighted that oxidative stress plays a crucial role in cancer development. Antioxidant supplements, including vitamins A, C, and E, helped mitigate this stress. The study indicated that these vitamins lowered the risk of certain types of cancer by reducing DNA damage and preventing harmful cellular changes [26]. Another paper published by JP Jose Merlin et al. showed how dietary antioxidants may prevent cancer and inhibit tumors via p53-dependent mechanisms [36].

Based on the findings, patients with higher antioxidant scores consumed less fat than those with lower scores. This observation may suggest that dietary pattern contributes to reducing general cancer risk factors in some extends including oxidative stress. It is agreed that the consumption of dietary fats, especially saturated fats, is associated with increased oxidative stress and subsequent systemic inflammation, which may create a favorable environment for the growth and spread of cancer cells [37, 38]. Further, a negative association between fat and DTAC likely reflects multiple synergistic biological processes. First, excessive lipid availability in KRAS-mutated cancers promotes ROS generation through mitochondrial dysfunction and altered metabolic flux. Our finding that lower-fat consumers maintain higher antioxidant capacity suggested that this oxidative pathway may also operate in healthy populations at risk for carcinogenesis. Additionally, the emphasis on FASN inhibition by antioxidant compounds suggests that lower-fat diets may reduce dependence on de novo lipogenesis, thereby decreasing associated oxidative stress [10]. Studies have demonstrated that eating a diet high in healthy fats can help improve total antioxidant capacity and reduce oxidative stress in chronic diseases [39]. Healthy fats can help lessen the damage caused by free radicals and inflammation in chronic diseases by activating cellular signaling pathways and enhancing the expression of antioxidant genes [40]. Therefore, a diet rich in healthy fats may be an effective therapeutic strategy for managing these diseases [41]. Previous research demonstrated that reducing dietary fat intake decreased the incidence of cancers and enhanced treatment effectiveness in oncology cases [42]. The relationship between fat subtypes and KRAS mutations remains unresolved. Current evidence presents conflicting patterns regarding fat subtypes and KRAS mutations, with some studies reporting no association between total fat intake and KRAS-mutated tumors [27, 43, 44], Some other studies have identified specific associations that indicate a positive relationship between certain subtypes of fats, such as saturated fats, MUFAs, and PUFAs, and KRAS-mutated tumors [15, 45–47].

We examined mutations in the KRAS gene across various codons; however, due to the uneven distribution of these mutations and the limited sample size, we were unable to fully explore the relationship between these mutations and DTAC.

Overall, a better understanding of the relationship between DTAC and disease progression in patients with mCRC KRAS mutations could pave the way for the development of personalized nutritional strategies in this patient population. This approach may be particularly significant in situations where there are limited targeted treatment options available for these patients.

This study has several strengths. First, it examined the association of DTAC with KRAS mutations rather than individual dietary antioxidants. We used a valid FFQ and controlled for key confounders, enhancing the accuracy of our findings. The FRAP method evaluated DTAC, offering advantages over other assessment techniques. A limitation of this research was the small sample size, which may affect statistical power and the ability to detect differences among the subgroups. The study also only examined mutation status in stage 4, potentially overlooking significant differences in dietary choices/patterns affected by disease severity, medications and other stages. Moreover, dietary assessment via FFQ carries inherent constraints, including recall bias and imprecise portion estimation. Future research with a larger and more diverse sample size is essential to better understand the impact of KRAS mutations on DTAC and to facilitate more comprehensive statistical analyses.

Conclusion

The study found no significant relationship between DTAC score and KRAS gene mutation. However, patients with higher DTAC scores had lower fat intake. It would be necessary to conduct longitudinal observational studies with larger populations and high follow-up duration in the future to investigate the comprehensive association between the KRAS mutant gene and DTAC in more detail.
