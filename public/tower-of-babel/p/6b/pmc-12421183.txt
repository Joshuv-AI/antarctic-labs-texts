Public perceptions of digitalisation and patient safety: a cross-sectional survey in Germany
Authors: Olga Anastasia Amberger, Dorothea Lemke, Hardy Müller, Dagmar Lüttel, David Schwappach, Max Geraedts, Beate S Müller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore perceptions of digitalisation and patient safety from the view of the German general public and related sociodemographic factors.DesignCross-sectional survey.SettingA nationwide survey was undertaken in 2024, using data from the Techniker Krankenkasse (TK) Monitor of Patient Safety. The TK Monitor of Patient Safety is an annual survey of the population on the state of patient safety in medical care.Participants1000 German adults (18 years and older).Primary and secondary outcome measuresOrdinal logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate the associations among sociodemographic factors (age, gender, education and household income) and perceptions on digitalisation and patient safety.ResultsThe majority of respondents expected benefits from digital applications in healthcare. Over half of the respondents (58%) believed that artificial intelligence (AI) can help reduce complications and errors, while 49% of the respondents believed that the use of AI poses serious new risks for the healthcare sector. The results showed that sociodemographic variables are important factors influencing patient safety perceptions of digitalisation and AI. Female, older, less educated and/or lower-income individuals were less likely to perceive benefits from digital care applications and AI.ConclusionsIn our study, the German public appears to view digital technologies and AI as tools both for improving patient safety and as potential risk factors. Our findings also highlight the importance of analysing sociodemographic factors to identify specific disparities in how different groups are affected by digitalisation. Such analysis is essential for developing targeted strategies that mitigate current patient safety risks, ensuring that digital health solutions are equitable and safe across all demographic groups.

Introduction

The importance of digital transformation in the healthcare sector has grown significantly over the past few decades.1 Digitalisation in healthcare refers to the integration and application of digital technologies such as robotics, electronic health records, wearable health devices, telemedicine and artificial intelligence (AI). These tools have been introduced to improve efficiency and accuracy, reduce costs and enhance access.2 3 Moreover, this revolution has great potential to improve patient safety.4 Patient safety is a key aspect of healthcare quality and focuses on preventing harm that can be caused by medical treatment.5 Adverse events due to medical care represent a major source of morbidity and mortality worldwide, creating a high economic burden.6 7 A meta-analysis showed that electronic health records systems, when properly implemented, resulted in a lower number of medication errors and adverse drug effects.8 AI-powered tools improve diagnostic accuracy and safety, both inside and outside of the hospital, by providing solutions to predict harms.9 10 Based on epidemiological evidence and a scoping review, Bates et al expect AI to have the most significant impact in areas where existing approaches fall short and where the ability to integrate and analyse complex, unstructured data is crucial to making precise predictions.10 This is particularly relevant to adverse drug events, disease decompensation and diagnostic errors including missed and delayed diagnoses.10 In addition, digital platforms enable the secure provision of telehealth services, reducing unnecessary inperson visits11 and preventing hospital-acquired infections.12

On the one hand, digital tools can contribute to patient safety by enabling early detection of infectious outbreaks and more precise tracking of patient outcomes. On the other hand, they pose threats to sensitive information.13 14 The risks associated with poor digital technologies and their implementation are becoming increasingly apparent.1518 There is a growing body of studies demonstrating the effects of usability concerns, with the potential for severe patient harm. Moreover, technical issues and system failures could put patient safety at risk, causing people to hesitate in trusting digital tools. Unequal access, especially in underserved areas and among older adults, immigrants and persons living with a disability, may contribute to disparities in care and safety perceptions.19 20

In general, research on safety and digitalisation is growing, and the broader context of perceived safety in relation to healthcare digitalisation is receiving more attention.2124 The research group around Minartz et al21 23 24 focused on the context of emotional and psychological safety referring to the perceived level of danger compared with the perceived level of comfort when interacting with digital technologies. This concept relates to patients’ subjective feelings of safety and well-being.25 Minartz et al concluded that digital (health) literacy is a key factor in perceived safety.21 However, they did not consider the patient safety perspective, which is understood as the focus on preventing harm during healthcare delivery. Ekman et al performed key informant interviews in Sweden on technical, regulatory and patient safety aspects.19 Respondents highlighted both the potential benefits and challenges regarding patient safety and care quality. On the one hand, digital technologies were perceived as reliable and capable of enhancing care quality by providing more data to support clinical decision-making. On the other hand, respondents pointed out possible limitations of telemedicine, particularly for specific conditions and patients with more complex needs. It becomes apparent that a certain disappointment and disillusionment is spreading, especially among medical professionals. Wosny et al described frustrating experiences, raising concerns about negative impacts on clinicians’ well-being.26 Hansen et al observed a negative attitude among German physicians towards digitalisation, suggesting that this might be due to challenges related to shifting power structures.27 However, these studies did not include the public’s perspective.

Studies examining patient safety perceptions on digitalisation and their influencing factors are highly valuable for developing interventions and implementing strategies to improve perceived safety.21 Despite the importance of this research, there is a lack of focus on identifying sociodemographic factors that affect the public perception of digitalisation and patient safety. Identifying these factors is crucial to designing targeted strategies to tackle patient safety problems. Therefore, this study first aimed to provide data on patient safety perceptions on digitalisation from the view of the German general public, and, second, it examined the sociodemographic factors influencing these perceptions.

Methods

Study design

The main data source is the Techniker Krankenkasse (TK) Monitor of Patient Safety, an annual national survey designed to track trends in patient safety from the perspective of the German public. A comprehensive description of the study design is available elsewhere.28 In brief, the TK Monitor of Patient Safety gathers population-based data on perceptions, experiences and knowledge related to patient safety, using computer-assisted telephone interviews (CATIs) with a random sample of 1000 participants from the residential population. The survey is updated each year to address specific topics, and the first round was conducted from October to November 2019. Subsequent rounds took place in August 2020, June 2021, April–May 2022, June 2023 and April–May 2024. Selected findings from previous survey years are published elsewhere.2833 The current data are from the 2024 survey. The checklist for Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology was used as a reporting guideline (see online supplemental file 1).

Participants

Data were collected from a random sample of 1000 adults in the residential population of Germany, with participants aged 18 years and older (70 million adults in Germany in 2024).34 The study sample excluded non-German speakers, that is, individuals residing in the 5% of all German households whose members have limited German-language skills.35 The sample’s representativeness was maintained through Association of German Market and Social Research Institutes (ADM) samples36 and comparison with data from the German Federal Statistical Office.34 The ADM dual-frame sampling method, using a mix of landline and mobile phone numbers, covers nearly the entire German population.37 We used a stratified multilevel random sampling approach, with 70% landline and 30% mobile numbers. About 83% of German households have landlines, and 82.2% of the population use smartphones.38 Within each household, the respondent was chosen using the last-birthday method, where the participant is the person with the most recent birthday. The survey was anonymous, voluntary and participants received no financial incentive. Informed consent was obtained verbally from all participants. Data collection was conducted by the Society for Social Research and Statistical Analysis (orsa).39 In compliance with German data protection laws, response quotas were deleted immediately after interviews.

Questionnaire

The TK Monitor of Patient Safety questionnaire was developed with input from patient safety researchers, social scientists, physicians, health scientists and psychologists. It incorporated questions and response options based on established questionnaires,4043 previous own work42 43 and discussions among developers. The questionnaire was pretested and validated by survey methodology experts, with minor adjustments made to the questions. The survey began with an introduction explaining the survey’s purpose and included closed-ended questions in three sections (see online supplemental file 2). Section A assessed perceptions, experiences and subjective views on patient safety in healthcare, with response options on a Likert scale from ‘very likely’ to ‘unlikely’. Section B focused on perceptions of and knowledge about digitalisation, while Section C collected sociodemographic information. Sections A and C remained largely the same each year, while Section B varied annually. Data from Sections B and C are reported here.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were generated using R software.44 Data were weighted by gender, age, education level and population distribution between metropolitan and rural areas, using iterative proportional fitting. Ordinal logistic regression assessed the factors influencing respondents’ perceptions of digitalisation, with ORs and 95% CIs calculated. Model variables included sociodemographic characteristics from survey responses. These characteristics included age (18–39 years, 40–59 years and ≥60 years), gender, education level (up to secondary school, tertiary/university and unspecified) and monthly income (<€3000, ≥€3000 and unspecified). Monthly income was used as an indicator of economic status. While professional position and marital status/household structure were initially considered as covariates, 50% of respondents did not provide this information. Given the multiple comparisons, p values <0.01 were considered statistically significant. All tests were two-sided.

Patient and public involvement

This population-based survey was conducted in line with patient safety priorities. No patients were involved in drafting the research questions or in the study design. Results will be published and used in policy development.

Safety perceptions on digitalisation

The majority of respondents expected benefits from digital applications in healthcare (online supplemental file 3). Most respondents (84%) saw advantages in electronic health records, followed by electronic prescriptions (69%) and the use of robots in surgeries (64%). Only about half of the respondents (51%) expected benefits from digital health applications used directly by patients, such as those for managing chronic illnesses and digital care applications (50%), which help individuals in need of care and their families to better manage and organise care. Care robots that provide tasks such as delivering food or medication or calling emergency services were only seen as beneficial by 44% of respondents.

More than two-thirds of respondents viewed AI as a significant opportunity to improve healthcare (69%). A large majority expected AI to enhance the analysis of X-ray images (86%). Over 60% also anticipated AI will aid in early detection of disease deterioration, offer support in correct diagnosis and provide relief for staff in clinics and hospitals. Over half of the respondents believed that AI can help reduce complications and errors in healthcare (58%). However, nearly half of the respondents believed that the use of AI in healthcare brings serious new risks (49%), and one third expressed fear about its application in this field (33%). Many respondents (72%) also perceived AI as primarily relevant in research, rather than in direct patient care (56%). Only 36% of respondents believed that AI will help decrease the need for inperson doctor visits.

Determinants of safety perceptions on digitalisation

Online supplemental file 3 presents the results of the ordinal logistic regression models. Sociodemographic variables significantly influenced perceptions of safety regarding digitalisation and AI. Female respondents, compared with male respondents, showed a significantly lower tendency to view benefits from digital care applications. Respondents aged 60 years and older, compared with young adults (18–39 years of age), exhibited a lower tendency to perceive benefits of electronic health records, surgical robots, digital health applications and care robots. Education level and income, however, were not associated with perceptions of digitalisation benefits.

Regarding safety perceptions on AI, female respondents, compared with male respondents, showed a lower tendency to view AI as beneficial for X-ray evaluation, to view AI primarily as a topic of research; to help in healthcare improvement and/or in early detection of disease progression and in correct diagnosis. Female respondents, however, showed a lower tendency to be afraid of AI. They demonstrated, however, a greater tendency to perceive AI as creating new risks and reducing inperson visits. Respondents aged 60 years and older, compared with young adults, showed a higher tendency to perceive AI as relevant to patient care but a lower tendency to see it as helpful for reducing inperson visits. Respondents aged 60 years and older also showed a lower tendency to fear AI. Middle-aged adults (40–59 years), compared with young adults, demonstrated a greater tendency to perceive AI as a source of new risks. Respondents with higher education levels, compared with those with education up to secondary school, exhibited a greater tendency to perceive AI primarily as a topic of research, as taking a toll on healthcare improvement and as a means of achieving correct diagnosis. Finally, respondents with high incomes, compared with those with lower incomes, showed a lower tendency to perceive AI as effective in reducing inperson visits.

Discussion

This study showed that the majority of respondents perceive benefits in using digital applications in healthcare, with over half believing that AI can help reduce complications and errors. However, nearly half of respondents expressed concerns about new risks associated with AI in healthcare. The findings also highlighted that sociodemographic factors play a crucial role in shaping these perceptions. Women, older individuals and those with lower education levels and income were less likely to perceive benefits from digital care applications and AI.

The positive perceptions of digital applications and AI in healthcare can partly be explained by these specific tools’ prevalence and familiarity in Germany.4648 Many people have an idea what electronic health records are due to extensive media coverage and may even have already used an electronic prescription. In contrast, far fewer people are likely to have encountered care robots.49 Robots’ benefits may not be immediately apparent to the general population. Our findings closely align with results of another online survey of 1014 internet users, investigating the general attitudes towards digitalisation in 2020 in Germany50; the majority in that study noted that digitisation will be important for therapy and care, as well as for health maintenance in the future. Less than 5% of participants in another cross-sectional survey among German patients in early 2020 rated the use of AI as negative.47 In contrast to that study, our sample appeared to be more fearful, perceiving new and serious risks generated by AI. In 2022, the launch of ChatGPT, an advanced AI chatbot developed by OpenAI, created a sensation and sparked critical discussion among both the general public and medical professionals.51 Our survey findings align closely with international trends in public perception of AI in healthcare. A systematic review conducted in the USA revealed that while many Americans recognise the potential benefits of AI in improving healthcare delivery, they also express strong concerns, particularly regarding AI’s role in clinical decision-making and data privacy.52 Similarly, European studies indicate that privacy, data security and trust in digital health systems are consistent concerns influencing public attitudes.53 54 In Sweden, although the population generally shows a higher level of trust in healthcare compared with other European countries and the USA, studies focusing on elderly patients still highlight similar apprehensions.55 Moreover, research shows that perceptions of risk, emotional responses and trust in healthcare providers significantly shape individuals’ acceptance of and sense of safety regarding AI-driven healthcare systems.56 To further enhance acceptance and awareness of both the opportunities and risks of digital health applications and AI, it is particularly important for various stakeholders such as healthcare professionals, governmental or legal regulatory bodies, software developers and industrial providers to highlight specific examples of tangible benefits in patients’ daily lives.57 58 It is often unclear to patients and also to healthcare workers whether and how AI is applied.59 Addressing this knowledge gap requires a multilevel communication and education strategy. This may include the integration of patient-centred use cases in public health communication, and the promotion of transparency in the development, approval and implementation of AI applications.60 61 A noteworthy example is the Scan4Safety programme in the UK, which aims to enhance the traceability of medical devices through digital technologies, thus contributing to better patient safety outcomes.62 Moreover, educating the public on AI capabilities and practising greater transparency regarding its use in routine care could foster a balanced understanding of AI’s opportunities and risks.58 Such transparency could help the public more accurately assess the current role of AI in routine care beyond the research context.

Our findings align with the well-established understanding that users of digital technologies are typically younger, more educated and wealthier.20 48 50 However, our study also provides novel and partially unexpected results related to patient safety and digitalisation that merit further investigation. These include the findings that higher educated respondents showed a greater tendency to perceive AI primarily as a topic of research. As digital technologies advance, healthcare systems must balance these innovations with a commitment to safety. For further improving the public perception of patient safety when digital health tools are used, it is essential for healthcare systems to actively address the known problems and negative effects on patient safety and to support patients in all demographic groups in making the most of new digital technology.

Strengths and limitations of this study

Several factors should be considered when interpreting these results. First, although the questionnaire was developed through a review of scientific literature on patient safety, it has not been validated in a separate study. However, a rigorous development process was undertaken to ensure that the questionnaire’s content, structure and wording were suitable for respondents. Second, the cross-sectional design of our study limited our ability to determine causal relationships between sociodemographic factors and perceptions of patient safety related to digitalisation. Longitudinal studies with sufficient follow-up are needed to explore how sociodemographic factors influence perceptions over time. Third, due to compliance with data protection regulations, the contact data were deleted immediately after collection, so information on response rates and reasons for non-participation is unavailable, which may affect the generalisability of findings. Differences may exist between the views of participants and non-participants. The over-representation of economically inactive individuals living in rural areas could also introduce a potential bias. Their views may differ from those of the broader population, as individuals from rural and/or economically disadvantaged backgrounds often express greater reservations about digitalisation.6365 These concerns are commonly driven by limited access to technology and infrastructure, lower levels of digital literacy and a stronger preference for personal, face-to-face healthcare. However, the study sample closely aligns with the broader population in terms of age, gender and education level. The sample size was both large and statistically robust.

The main strength of our study is the large, representative sample randomly selected from the general population in Germany. For the first time, data were gathered on public perceptions related to digitalisation and patient safety across various stages of life. Additionally, the CATI technique minimises measurement errors and item non-response.

Conclusions

Digitalisation is an emerging field in improving patient safety in Germany. Our study is the first assessing public perceptions related to digitalisation and patient safety and the sociodemographic factors associated with such perceptions. The results show that digital applications in the healthcare sector are perceived among the German public as beneficial, particularly in the areas of electronic health records and electronic prescriptions. There is generally a positive outlook among the German population on the use of AI in healthcare. However, it is essential to take the legitimate fears and concerns people have seriously. Sociodemographic variables are important factors influencing public perception of digitalisation regarding patient safety. Moreover, our findings indicate that gender, age, education level and income of the different members of the population must be taken into account when addressing perceptions on AI, in terms of safety. Thus, identifying the degree of influence such sociodemographic variables have on the perception of safety in digitalisation is a first step to designing specific action strategies to address the current risks to patient safety.
