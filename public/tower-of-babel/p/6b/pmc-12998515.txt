Synergistic electronic-topological strategy enables spatiotemporal control of covalent adaptable networks
Authors: Hongfei Huang, Lijie Sun, Yalin Zhang, Luzhi Zhang, Yang Wang, Wei Sun, Zhengwei You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Covalent adaptable networks (CANs) hold considerable promise for combining the advantages of thermosets and thermoplastics. However, their use in high-speed melt spinning is restricted by insufficient dynamic bond reactivity at processing temperatures and the mismatch between network rearrangement kinetics and industrial requirements. Here, we establish a spatiotemporally regulated platform based on internally catalyzed oxime-urethane chemistry within a four-arm cross-linking topology. Neighboring urea groups provide internal catalysis that greatly accelerates oxime-urethane dissociation at 110°C, improving melt fluidity. During extrusion, the slight temperature drop rapidly drives bond recombination within the four-arm topology, while hydrogen bonds deliver immediate reinforcement to retain melt strength. This synergistic design enables continuous melt spinning at 100 meters per minute over a short 10-centimeter distance. The resulting fibers combine high mechanical performance (tensile strength: 261.7 megapascals; toughness: 630.1 megajoules per cubic meter) with excellent stretchability, self-healing, and recyclability. This molecular engineering approach overcomes the processing-performance tradeoff in CANs, offering a scalable pathway toward high-performance, sustainable polymers for industrial manufacturing.

INTRODUCTION

By 2025, more than a century had passed since Staudinger’s (1) groundbreaking macromolecular hypothesis fundamentally reshaped our understanding of polymers. Over this period, research has established clear correlations between molecular architecture and polymer properties, guiding innovations in their synthesis, processing, and performance. This foundation has enabled the development of thermoplastics, characterized by linear polymer chains and recyclability, and thermosets, distinguished by covalent cross-links that provide mechanical strength and dimensional stability but prevent recycling, thereby exacerbating plastic waste issues (2, 3). In recent decades, dynamic covalent chemistry has emerged as a promising pathway to address this challenge by constructing covalent adaptable networks (CANs) (4). CANs, characterized by dynamic covalent bonds, have attracted considerable attention in materials science owing to their exceptional self-healing properties, recyclability, and reprocessability (5, 6). These dynamic bonds can reversibly dissociate and reform, allowing the polymer network to rearrange its internal structure continually. However, despite these advantages, the industrial implementation of CAN-based materials, particularly for applications such as melt spinning, faces substantial practical limitations stemming primarily from unresolved scientific and technical challenges.

A key obstacle is the inherent mismatch between the kinetics of the dynamic covalent bond exchange and the rapid timescales required for high-throughput manufacturing (7). In industrial processes, particularly melt spinning, polymers must undergo a rapid transition from a low-viscosity melt state to a mechanically robust solid within a brief interval to achieve efficient manufacturing and good mechanical performance (8). However, dynamic covalent bonds often exhibit relatively high activation energies (Ea), which result in slow exchange kinetics at typical processing temperatures (4, 7). Attempts to enhance reaction rates by increasing processing temperatures often lead to undesirable side reactions, polymer degradation, and compromised material performance. The widely studied Diels-Alder (DA) dynamic bond is an illustrative example (9). Although DA-based CANs dissociate efficiently at elevated temperatures, enabling good melt fluidity, their recombination kinetics upon cooling are considerably slow. This sluggish recombination severely restricts rapid solidification and mechanical recovery immediately after processing, limiting the practical application of these CANs in fast-paced industrial spinning processes. Our previous work further highlights these issues: Polyurethane-based CANs containing dynamic dimethylglyoxime-urethane bonds (Fig. 1A) and nondynamic cross-linkers, such as glycerol or trimethylolpropane, required excessively high spinning temperatures (>135°C), exhibited low spinning speeds, and produced fibers with inadequate mechanical properties (10, 11). These challenges highlight the urgent need for molecular strategies to reconcile dynamic bond kinetics with the demands of industrial-scale processing.

At the fundamental level, this difficulty arises because the dynamic bond-exchange kinetics within polymer networks are markedly different from those observed in small-molecule systems (12). Polymer networks present inherently complex environments characterized by restricted molecular mobility, diverse local polarities, and intricate topologies, all of which substantially alter the reaction pathways and kinetics of dynamic covalent bonds (7). Consequently, the established structure-reactivity-property relationships derived from small molecules often fail to accurately predict or control the bond-exchange behavior within polymeric matrices. In addition, recent research indicates a strong correlation between the polymer network topology and its dynamic bond-exchange mechanism (associative or dissociative), both of which profoundly influence the macroscopic performance of the material and the network rearrangement rate (13–17). To address these challenges, internal catalysis, particularly through neighboring group participation, has emerged as a promising strategy (14–16). By strategically placing catalytic functional groups adjacent to dynamic bonds within the polymer structure, internal catalysis can effectively lower the activation barriers and accelerate bond exchange without the disadvantages of external catalysts, such as aging, leaching, or toxicity (18, 19). Our recent studies have demonstrated that internal catalysis can substantially improve room-temperature self-healing performance, validating its potential (20). However, the broad capability of internal catalysis in facilitating rapid and controlled polymer processing under industrial conditions remains largely unexplored.

Here, we propose spatiotemporal control based on an internally catalyzed dynamic chemistry platform to overcome this challenge by integrating multilevel dynamic bonding and internal catalysis into a topological molecular design (Fig. 1B). Specifically, we designed a polymer network that combined dynamic oxime-urethane bonds and hydrogen bonding within a four-arm cross-linking structure (Fig. 1C). In this system, the urea group and the oxime-urethane dynamic bond are covalently connected and separated by approximately five atoms, enabling the formation of a five-membered cyclic transition state during bond exchange. This characteristic proximity for neighboring group participation substantially lowers the activation energy of oxime-urethane dissociation (14–16). During processing at elevated temperatures, internal catalysis substantially accelerates oxime-urethane bond dissociation, generating short polymer chains and enhancing fluidity. Upon exiting the spinneret, where a slight temperature drop naturally occurs even without external cooling, the same internal catalytic mechanism facilitates the rapid recombination of the oxime-urethane bonds and reorganization of the four-arm cross-linked network, thereby rapidly restoring the mechanical strength and allowing efficient melt spinning. Simultaneously, hydrogen bonds between the four-arm cross-linking units provide immediate mechanical reinforcement, ensuring sufficient melt strength throughout the process. Collectively, this synergistic interplay between internal catalysis and hierarchical dynamic interactions effectively resolves the conventional tradeoffs among processing speed, mechanical performance, and structural adaptability inherent in CAN-based materials. Thus, our integrated molecular approach offers a robust and scalable solution for optimizing CANs for rapid industrial processing, potentially accelerating their commercial application and opening different avenues for high-performance and sustainable polymer manufacturing.

RESULTS

Molecular design of internally catalyzed CANs and their spatiotemporal control during melt spinning

We incorporated the four-arm reversible topological structure of diaminoglyoxime (AMG) into CANs as a cross-linking unit (Fig. 2A). The CANs were synthesized via the single-step polycondensation of poly(butylene glycol adipate) diol (PBGAD), isophorone diisocyanate (IPDI), AMG, and the ionic liquid 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([EMI][TFSI]) (see the Supplementary Materials for details). [EMI][TFSI] was introduced into the network to affect ionic conductivity. The CAN-k-m-n series is defined by the molar ratio of PBGAD to AMG (k-m) and mass ratio (n) of [EMI][TFSI] in CANs (Fig. 2A and fig. S1). Both cross-linked polyurethane (CPU, control group) and CAN-4-3-30/50 (experimental group) maintained the same PBGAD-to-AMG ratio (k:m = 4:3) but did not contain [EMI][TFSI]. Attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy was used to investigate the molecular interactions between [EMI][TFSI] and the polymer matrix. Compared with the spectrum of neat [EMI][TFSI], the C─H stretching vibrations of the imidazole ring in CANs shifted to 3159.5 and 3123.8 cm−1 (fig. S2A). These changes confirmed the presence of hydrogen bonds between [EMI][TFSI] and the polymer chains. In addition, no absorption was observed at 2264 cm−1 (corresponding to N═C═O skeleton stretching vibrations) (fig. S2B), indicating the complete consumption of IPDI (21). Furthermore, four characteristic bands of the [TFSI] anion—symmetric S─N─S and O═S═O stretching, as well as asymmetric CF3 and O═S═O stretching—appeared at 1051.6, 1133.4, 1179.7, and 1347.1 cm−1, respectively, and exhibited downshifts in the ATR-FTIR spectra (fig. S2C). This suggests that [EMI][TFSI] functions as a lubricant, enhancing chain mobility (22). To assess the thermal stability of the CANs, we performed thermogravimetric analysis because CAN characterization and processing involve multiple heating steps (fig. S3). The thermal-decomposition temperature of the CPU is 269°C. Notably, the introduction of [EMI][TFSI] increased the decomposition temperature, improving the thermal stability. Differential scanning calorimetry demonstrated a plasticizing effect, reflected by a reduced glass transition temperature (Tg) in the CANs compared with that in the CPU. Specifically, at 30 wt % [EMI][TFSI], the Tg of CPU decreased from −43.7 to −61.3°C (fig. S4), and it further decreased below −65°C at 50 wt % [EMI][TFSI]. Owing to the high compatibility between the polymer and [EMI][TFSI], the CANs also exhibited optical transparency. For instance, a 1-mm-thick film of CAN-4-3-50 showed >91.1% transmittance in the visible range (400 to 800 nm; fig. S5). Furthermore, [EMI][TFSI] incorporation conferred ionic conductivity to the CPU (22, 23), as reflected by the ionic conductivities of CAN-6-2-50, CAN-4-3-50, CAN-2-4-50, and CAN-4-3-30 at 25°C—2.7 × 10−2, 2.0 × 10−2, 1.8 × 10−2, and 0.3 × 10−2 S m−1, respectively (figs. S6 and S7). Uniaxial tensile tests were conducted to investigate the mechanical properties of the CPU and CANs (fig. S8). Increasing the AMG content in the CANs enhanced the mechanical performance. At a fixed concentration of 50 wt % [EMI][TFSI], the tensile strength and elongation at break were 23.6 ± 1.8 MPa and 879.9 ± 12.6% for CAN-6-2-50, 91.8 ± 6.0 MPa and 959.5 ± 73.0% for CAN-4-3-50, and 142.1 ± 23.3 MPa and 642.3 ± 57.9% for CAN-2-4-50, respectively (fig. S9). The results demonstrated that increasing the proportion of the cross-linker AMG substantially enhanced the mechanical strength of the material while maintaining a relatively high elongation at break without changing the ionic liquid content. When the PBGAD:AMG molar ratio was 4:3, increasing the [EMI][TFSI] content from 0 to 30 wt % and then to 50 wt % reduced the tensile strength because of the plasticizing effect of [EMI][TFSI]. The tensile strength and elongation at break were 230.1 ± 31.6 MPa and 784.9 ± 123.3% for CPU, 156.4 ± 19.8 MPa and 1010.0 ± 87.7% for CAN-4-3-30, and 91.8 ± 6.0 MPa and 959.5 ± 73.0% for CAN-4-3-50, respectively (figs. S8 and S9). Owing to the plasticizing effect of [EMI][TFSI], the CANs exhibited excellent elongation while retaining high mechanical strength.

Next, CANs were melt spun to validate the spatiotemporal control of this internally catalyzed dynamic chemistry during CAN processing. The spinning path from the heated barrel and spinneret was divided into areas I and II (Fig. 2B), and a no-quenching chamber was used for cooling. Above the processing temperature, the four-arm topological cross-links dissociated, releasing short polymer chains while weakening the hydrogen bonds. Consequently, the melt exhibited a lower viscosity. Upon exiting the spinneret, once the temperature dropped, hydrogen bonds and oxime-urethane bonds (catalyzed by adjacent urea moieties) rapidly reformed, restoring sufficient mechanical strength to withstand the high-speed drawing forces of melt spinning (Fig. 2C).

To probe neighboring group participation in the four-arm AMG cross-linker, we prepared a set of oxime-urethane model compounds. N-hydroxyacetamidine (b3) and phenethyl isocyanate (a1) afforded a1b3a1, which bears a urea unit adjacent to the oxime-urethane bond (figs. S10 to S12). Two reference compounds lacking neighboring urea groups—a1b1 and a1b2—were obtained by reacting phenethyl isocyanate with acetone oxime (b1) and diacetylmonoxime (b2) (figs. S13 to S18). The exchange kinetics were evaluated by mixing each oxime-urethane bond (a1b3a1 or a1b1) with the model nucleophile b2 (Fig. 2, D and E). At 0° to 14°C, real-time proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy revealed that the methyl signal of product a1b2 at 1.898 ppm appeared only when a neighboring urea group was present (figs. S19 and S20). Repeating the experiment at 100°C produced the same trend but at a notably accelerated rate: the 1.898-ppm peak intensified, whereas the b2 peak at 1.718 ppm diminished (figs. S21 and S22). Integration of these resonances gave the conversion χ = [a1b2]/([a1b2] + [b2]); after 330 min at 100°C, χ was consistently higher for the urea-containing system than that for the control (fig. S23). Treating the reaction as first-order transcarbamoylation [𝑘−1 = −ln([b2]T/[b2]0)/T, where T represents the reaction time] revealed that, after only 5 min at 100°C, the rate constant for the urea-containing system was approximately four times that of the reference (Fig. 2F) (24).

These data provide a quantitative picture of the dissociation-recombination process: Neighboring urea groups not only accelerate exchange at elevated temperatures but also render the oxime-urethane bonds dynamic under ambient conditions. These results provide unambiguous evidence for the neighboring group participation effect and demonstrate that internal catalysis substantially enhances the temperature responsiveness of dynamic covalent bonds.

Mechanism study of oxime-urethane catalyzed by neighboring urea bonds

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to clarify the reaction mechanism and energy landscape of the neighboring urea–catalyzed exchange of oxime-urethane bonds. Geometry optimization and frequency calculations were conducted at the M06-2X/6-31+ G(d,p) level, whereas single-point energy calculations were performed at the M06-2X/def2-TZVP level (details in the Supplementary Materials). The optimized molecular structures are presented in fig. S24, and the computed Gibbs free energy profile in Fig. 3A delineates the key transition states and intermediates in the reaction pathway.

Mechanism study of oxime-urethane exchange promoted by neighboring urea bonds.

(A) Computed Gibbs free energy profile for the internal catalysis mechanism of neighboring urea bond participation. (B) Calculated path intermediates and transition-state Gibbs free energy change statistics.

The process begins with the formation of Int1 from the reactant complex (RC)—an endergonic step (ΔG⧧ = +14.68 kcal mol−1)—indicating reduced stability. This intermediate undergoes structural rearrangement via transition state TS1 (ΔG⧧ = 17.71 kcal mol−1), resulting in Int2 (exergonic by 13.67 kcal mol−1), which is thermodynamically more favorable. This transformation is governed by a proton-coupled electron transfer mechanism, wherein a proton shifts from a hydroxyl group to a nearby O atom, accompanied by electron redistribution, leading to stabilization. Following this, Int2 progresses through the high-energy transition state TS2 (ΔG⧧ = 25.10 kcal mol−1) to form Int3a (exergonic by 37.91 kcal mol−1), where bond reorganization and electronic delocalization enhance stability. The transition from Int3a to Int3b (ΔG⧧ = 22.02 kcal mol−1) suggests that Int3b is a transient species with limited stability. The transformation from Int3a to Int3c (ΔG⧧ = 14.81 kcal mol−1) represents a stabilization phase, followed by transition through TS3 (ΔG⧧ = 20.09 kcal mol−1), leading to dissociation into Int6 + Int5 (exergonic by 18.67 kcal mol−1). In the final stage, Int6 undergoes structural rearrangement yielding Int4 (exergonic by 20.64 kcal mol−1), whereas Int5 remains unchanged, indicating completion of the reaction. Bond reorganization and conformational adjustments contribute to overall system stability.

The activation energies associated with each step of the intrinsically catalyzed oxime-urethane bond dissociation pathway were determined (Fig. 3B). This catalytic process stabilizes transition states through hydrogen bonding and electronic delocalization. By modulating the activation barriers and leveraging noncovalent interactions, this mechanism offers a rational strategy for designing thermally responsive CANs with tunable stability and enhanced processability.

Thermomechanics and spinnability evaluation of CANs

Studies of small-molecule model systems have highlighted the potential of reversible covalent bonds for tunable network architectures. Building on these findings, we investigated the polymer-level thermodynamics in CANs designed with an internally catalyzed four-arm cross-linking unit (AMG). At high temperatures, the dynamic bonds at the cross-linking sites exhibit high exchange rates, which facilitate topological rearrangements and enable rapid melt spinning at low temperatures.

Temperature-sweep rheology revealed that both storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (Gʺ) decreased with increasing temperature, indicating progressive dissociation of the cross-linked network (Fig. 4, A to C, and fig. S25). Notably, the G′ and Gʺ of CAN-6-2-50 remained relatively stable until 100°C and then dropped sharply, whereas formulations with higher AMG content exhibited more pronounced reductions in both moduli. This result reveals that the enhanced thermal responsiveness arises from the higher cross-linking density and more active dynamic bonds. However, when the AMG content is increased beyond a certain point, the rate of decrease for both G′ and Gʺ decreases again, owing to the dense hydrogen-bond network formed by the multifunctional AMG species. These hydrogen bonds function as physical cross-linking sites, restricting chain mobility and conferring additional rigidity at high temperatures.

The flow temperature (Tf) increased from 110° to 114° and 153°C with increasing AMG content (Fig. 4D), whereas increasing the [EMI][TFSI] content from 30 to 50 wt % had a comparatively modest effect on Tf. Similarly, the complex viscosity dropped with rising temperature (Fig. 4E); however, the rate of decrease diminished above 80°C. Even for CAN-4-3-50, which contained more cross-linkers than CAN-6-2-50, the complex viscosity decreased more sharply at high temperatures, reflecting the generation of short polymer chains that functioned as lubricants upon network dissociation. Altering the [EMI][TFSI] content had a weaker impact on the viscosity trends than changing the cross-linker content.

To further clarify these effects, we examined the change in modulus relative to the initial value, that is, ΔG′/G′0 and ΔGʺ/Gʺ0 (Fig. 4, F and G). CAN-6-2-50 exhibited a slower decline in both G′ and Gʺ compared with formulations with higher cross-linking density, consistent with the reduced overall dynamic exchange at lower AMG content. Nevertheless, all formulations exhibited a marked transition around 100°C, providing direct evidence that the melt strength increases notably below the spinning temperature. Comparison with a previously reported polyurethane-based CAN (DOU-IG-30) (10), which relies on nondynamic cross-linkers and lacks internal catalysis, indicated that CAN-4-3-30 exhibited an order of magnitude higher G′, Gʺ, and complex viscosity at room temperature, along with a considerably steeper decline upon heating (Fig. 4H). This structural design increased the tensile strength from 7.1 ± 0.4 MPa in DOU-IG-30 to 156.4 ± 19.8 MPa in CAN-4-3-30—a 22-fold improvement—while maintaining good spinnability.

Shear-thinning behavior in a frequency-sweep test at 120°C confirmed the spinnability of CAN-4-3-50 (Fig. 4I). Stress-relaxation studies revealed complete relaxation in all samples, indicating their malleability and the active dissociation of oxime-urethane bonds between 75° and 90°C (Fig. 4, J and K). The Maxwell model for viscoelastic fluids was used to determine the relaxation time (τ*), which was found to be 37% (G/G0 = 1/e = 37%) of the normalized relaxation modulus. Furthermore, the temperature-dependent behavior of the relaxation time can be described by the Arrhenius equation τ (T) = τ0 exp. (Ea/RT), where τ represents the characteristic relaxation time, τ0 represents the preexponential factor, and Ea represents the activation energy for stress relaxation (25). The Ea of stress relaxation was 110.7 ± 12.7 and 103.3 ± 1.7 kJ mol−1 for CAN-4-3-30 and CAN-4-3-50, respectively (Fig. 4L), indicating that the [EMI][TFSI] content exerted only a minor influence on the dynamic behavior at a fixed ratio of soft to hard segments. Frequency-sweep measurements confirmed that G′ consistently exceeded Gʺ under ambient conditions (fig. S26), reflecting strong elasticity. With higher AMG loading, both moduli increased, corroborating the role of the enhanced cross-linking and hydrogen-bonding density. These findings collectively demonstrate that internally catalyzed four-arm cross-linkers not only enhance the mechanical properties but also preserve high degrees of thermal responsiveness and spinnability in CANs.

Investigation of dynamic bond-exchange kinetics in polymer networks

At the fundamental level, the processing challenges encountered in CANs stem from discrepancies between the dynamic bond-exchange kinetics observed in polymer networks and those predicted by small-molecule studies. Owing to their inherently limited chain mobility, heterogeneous local polarities, and topologically complex structures, the reaction pathways and kinetics of dynamic covalent bonds in polymer matrices deviate markedly from those of their small-molecule analogs. Consequently, the structure-reactivity-property relationships derived from small-molecule systems often fail to accurately predict or control the bond-exchange behaviors in polymeric materials. To further validate our strategy, we characterized the dynamic bond-exchange kinetics within the polymer network to determine whether they align with previous findings.

Figure 5 (A and B) illustrates the reversible cross-linking process of CANs upon heating. Above a critical temperature, the four-arm oxime-urethane cross-linker preferentially dissociates at the cross-link junctions, generating short polymer chains with terminal N═C═O groups. Once the temperature decreases, the equilibrium shifts toward recombination, enabling rapid network recovery. Real-time variable-temperature FTIR spectroscopy of CAN-4-3-30 revealed progressive generation of N═C═O groups during heating (Fig. 5C). Conversely, when the temperature was rapidly reduced, the N═C═O signals sharply decreased and disappeared at room temperature (Fig. 5D). These results confirm that the internally catalyzed oxime-urethane bonds in the four-arm cross-linked network respond rapidly to temperature changes, consistent with the earlier small-molecule NMR data and polymer rheological measurements.

In addition to dynamic covalent bonds, we investigated hydrogen-bond network rearrangements in the four-arm cross-linked system. Figure 5 (E to G) shows the hydrogen-bond acceptor sites, particularly the C═O groups in the urethane, oxime-urethane, urea, and ester moieties. Temperature-variable FTIR (60° to 110°C) and the corresponding perturbation-correlation moving window (PCMW) analysis (Fig. 5, F and H) revealed characteristic peaks at 1752, 1725, 1703, and 1633 cm−1, assigned to the free ester carbonyl [ν(C═O)_free(ester)], hydrogen-bonded urethane carbonyl [ν(C═O)_H-bond(urethane)], hydrogen-bonded oxime-urethane carbonyl [ν(C═O)_H-bond(oxime-urethane)], and networked urea carbonyl [ν(C═O)_networked(urea)], respectively. Upon heating, the intensity of the 1752-cm−1 peak increased, suggesting exposure of additional free ester groups, whereas the 1725-, 1703-, and 1633-cm−1 peaks weakened, reflecting the dissociation of hydrogen-bonded domains. Two-dimensional correlation spectroscopy (2DCOS) following Noda’s rules (Fig. 5, I and J) indicated that the sequence of these thermally induced transitions was 1752 → 1725 → 1633 → 1703 cm−1, corresponding to ν(C═O)_free(ester) → ν(C═O)_H-bond(urethane) → ν(C═O)_networked(urea) → ν(C═O)_H-bond(oxime-urethane) (table S1). The notation “→” indicates the temporal order of spectral transitions, where the left-hand component changes earlier. In contrast, upon cooling (Fig. 5, G and K), these absorption bands exhibited the opposite order [1752 → 1633 → 1725 → 1703 cm−1, corresponding to ν(C═O)_free(ester) → ν(C═O)_networked(urea) → ν(C═O)_H-bond(urethane) → ν(C─O)_H-bond(oxime-urethane)]. In addition, the overall intensity of C═O signals increased, signaling the recombination of oxime-urethane bonds (table S2). These observations confirm that, as the temperature decreases, the urea bonds at the four-arm cross-linking sites rapidly form well-ordered hydrogen-bonded associations, thereby reinforcing the melt.

Figure 6A illustrates the various hydrogen-bonding motifs present in the network. To assess whether the N─H groups exhibited analogous thermal responses, we monitored hydrogen-bond donors in the 3600- to 3100-cm−1 region (Fig. 6, B and C). The overall N─H stretching intensity (3425 to 3200 cm−1) decreased upon heating, mainly because of partial oxime-urethane bond dissociation (Fig. 6B). As the temperature increased, the free N─H peak at 3428 cm−1 intensified (ν(N─H)_free), the signal at 3393 cm−1 [disordered hydrogen bonding, ν(N─H)_disordered] became more prominent, and the ordered hydrogen-bonded N─H peak at 3374 cm−1 [ν(N─H)_ordered]gradually weakened and shifted to 3379 cm−1. In addition, a broad peak below 3236 cm−1 [ascribed to topologically aggregated urea-urea domains, ν(N─H)_deep-ordered] diminished, indicating partial disruption of these deep-ordered hydrogen bonds (Fig. 6, B and D). Synchronous and asynchronous 2DCOS analyses (Fig. 6, E and F) indicated the following sequence of thermal events: 3428 → 3393 → 3236 → 3374 cm−1, corresponding to ν(N─H)_free → ν(N─H)_disordered → ν(N─H)_deep-ordered → ν(N─H)_ordered (table S3). This implies that the most strongly ordered hydrogen bonds dissociate last, ensuring network stability near room temperature. In contrast, upon cooling (Fig. 6, C and G), these N─H peaks shifted in the opposite direction, and their intensities increased, indicating reformation of oxime-urethane bonds that increased the overall N─H population. Following Noda’s rules, the synchronous and asynchronous spectra (Fig. 6, H and I) revealed that the order of transitions reversed to 3428 → 3236 → 3374 → 3393 cm−1, corresponding to ν(N─H)_free → ν(N─H)_deep-ordered → ν(N─H)_ordered → ν(N─H)_disordered (table S4). These findings again suggest that, as the temperature decreases, the urea bonds in the four-arm cross-linker rapidly establish robust hydrogen-bonded assemblies, improving the melt strength. This aligns well with the C═O results and confirms that the dynamic bond-exchange kinetics within the four-arm cross-linked polymer networks match the findings of both small-molecule NMR studies and polymer rheological tests. Consequently, the structure-reactivity-property relationships established using small-molecule models are translatable to the polymer matrix, validating our approach for tuning CANs through internal catalytic moieties and well-defined topological cross-linking.

Spinnability of CANs and self-healing, reprocessable behavior of CAN fibers

Figure 7A shows the melt spinning apparatus used for the CANs. On the basis of rheological data, admissible spinning windows were established for each formulation. CAN-2-4-50 was found to be intractable between 110° and 140°C, whereas CAN-4-3-50 and CAN-4-3-30 were spinnable from 110° to 125°C, and CAN-6-2-50 was spinnable from 110° to 120°C (Fig. 7B). Therefore, CAN-4-3-30 was selected for the present study. Photographs collected at progressively higher spinning speeds (Fig. 7C) indicated that the diameters decreased from 876 ± 154 μm at 2 m min−1 to 110 ± 34 μm at 100 m min−1 (Fig. 7E). The fibers were optically clear and retained smooth circular cross sections (fig. S27). Elemental mapping confirmed the homogeneous distributions of C, O, N, F, and S, indicating that [EMI][TFSI] remained molecularly dispersed without phase separation (fig. S28) (26). Bright blue fluorescence was emitted under 365-nm irradiation (fig. S29). Because of its low melt viscosity and rapid network reformation, CAN-4-3-30 was continuously spun and collected (Fig. 7D and movie S1). A diagram of the spinning process is shown in fig. S30. Tensile testing (Fig. 7F) indicated that melt drawing enhanced the strength and toughness relative to the CANs, which was ascribed to shear- and drafting-induced chain alignment. Among the three formulations examined, CAN-4-3-30 fibers exhibited the highest performance, with strength and toughness of 215 ± 11 MPa and 595 ± 76 MJ m−3 at 20 m min−1, respectively (fig. S31). When the winding speed was increased to 100 m min−1, these values increased to 262 MPa and 631 MJ m−3, respectively (Fig. 7G and fig. S32), consistent with greater molecular orientation. To reveal the network structure and orientation behavior of CAN-4-3-30, we characterized its films and fibers at different spinning speeds using polarized optical microscopy (POM) (fig. S33), x-ray diffraction (XRD) (fig. S34), and 2D wide-angle x-ray diffraction (2D-WAXD) (fig. S35). The POM images of the films showed negligible brightness changes under crossed polarizers at 0°, 45°, and 90°, indicating a random polymer chain orientation and an isotropic structure. For fibers spun at 20 m min−1, the brightness increased at 45° and decreased at 0° and 90°, suggesting partial chain alignment. As the spinning speed increased to 100 m min−1, the brightness contrast became more pronounced, and typical extinction patterns appeared, indicating that polymer chains were highly oriented along the fiber axis. The XRD patterns exhibited only broad diffuse peaks, without distinct diffraction peaks, indicating that the CAN-4-3-30 network was predominantly amorphous. 2D-WAXD diffraction rings were approximately circular and did not exhibit clear orientation patterns. This apparent discrepancy arises because POM detects optical anisotropy rather than crystalline order. During spinning, the polymer chains can partially align along the drawing direction, producing birefringence, as observed via POM. In contrast, WAXD probes the crystalline order; owing to the absence of long-range crystalline sequences, only isotropic diffuse rings were observed. Therefore, the CAN-4-3-30 fibers exhibited chain orientation without crystalline order, with POM reflecting macromolecular alignment and WAXD confirming the amorphous network structure.

The fibers, being chemically cross-linked yet dynamic, resisted common solvents (fig. S36), remained stable under ambient humidity (55% relative humidity, 15°C), and exhibited intrinsic hydrophobicity arising from the fluorinated [EMI][TFSI] (fig. S37). The transparency was maintained even at high tensile strains (Fig. 7H). Cyclic loading indicated excellent elasticity, with minimal hysteresis below 100% strain; beyond this threshold, increasing hysteresis indicated efficient energy dissipation that underpinned the high toughness (figs. S38 to S40) (27, 28). Rapid photothermal healing was enabled by the internally catalyzed four-arm cross-links. After the fibers were cut and realigned, they were irradiated with a 808-nm light for 10, 30, and 60 s. As the irradiation time increased, the fibers exhibited improved healing behavior. After 60 s of irradiation, the tensile strength and elongation at break recovered to ~70 and 80% of their original values, respectively (Fig. 7I and fig. S41). Macroscopically, the CAN-4-3-30 fibers healed within several seconds under near-infrared irradiation when cut and realigned. After the light source was removed and the sample cooled to room temperature, the healed fiber was stretched to four times its original length (fig. S42). Infrared thermal imaging revealed that, after a few seconds of exposure, the temperature in the irradiated area reached ~93°C (fig. S43). At the microscale, the incision disappeared within 9 s (fig. S44 and movie S2). Because of its high ionic conductivity, a CAN-4-3-30 fiber kept a light-emitting diode lit; the brightness decreased reversibly upon stretching, indicating potential for strain-sensing applications (fig. S45). To test the reprocessability of CAN-4-3-30 fibers, we collected them and spun again via melt extrusion (Fig. 7J). After three recycling cycles, the mechanical performance of the CAN-4-3-30 fibers was mostly retained compared with that of the original samples, demonstrating their excellent recyclability (Fig. 7K and fig. S46). In summary, record levels of strength, toughness, preparation rate, recyclability, and healability were achieved relative to those of previously reported gel fibers (Fig. 7, L to N, and fig. S47) (10, 29–39).

Stretchable textiles and electroluminescent devices based on CAN-4-3-30 fibers

The CAN-4-3-30 fiber combines ionic conductivity, bright blue photoluminescence, high tensile strength, optical transparency, large-strain flexibility, a mechanical modulus comparable to that of soft tissue, and the ability to accommodate body motion, making it well suited to wearable electronics (23, 40–42). Its melt processability enables various textile and device formats. A plain-weave fabric assembled entirely from CAN yarns retained a well-defined weave under a microscope (Fig. 8A) and emitted intense blue fluorescence under 365-nm excitation (Fig. 8B). When CAN-4-3-30 fibers were interlaced with conventional (nonconductive and nonstretchable) yarns, an electronic bracelet was obtained (Fig. 8C and fig. S48). The CAN fibers endowed the composite with elasticity (fig. S49), as indicated by minimal hysteresis in cyclic tests at 10 to 50% strain (Fig. 8D), and provided a stable, reproducible piezoresistive response over the same strain range (Fig. 8E), satisfying the key criteria for strain-sensing conductors (43, 44).

In addition to sensing, fibers serve as soft electrodes for flexible electroluminescent (EL) displays. Block-type devices were fabricated by screen printing the letters “DHU” with CAN fibers and then laminating a PDMS@ZnS:Cu EL layer between two CAN sheets (sandwich geometry; Fig. 8F). Under an alternating field, opposite charges accumulated on the two transparent CAN electrodes, exciting the ZnS:Cu phosphor precisely where it contacts the DHU fibers and producing bright, well-resolved blue characters (Fig. 8G). To highlight the fiber-level integration, we coated CAN-4-3-30 fibers with a waterborne polyurethane (WPU) and ZnS:Cu phosphor slurry (WPU@Zn:Cu) to obtain coaxial core-shell EL fibers (Fig. 8H). When an uncoated CAN fiber touches a coated one, a microscale sandwich stack is formed—the CAN core, ZnS:Cu-laden shell, and external CAN electrode—such that light is generated only at the contact points (Fig. 8I). Uniform brightness was retained along the length of the coated fiber (Fig. 8J), and stable emission was sustained under bending, contact, and torsion during weaving (Fig. 8K). The luminance increased from 0.2 ± 0.1 cd m2 at 0.7 V μm−1 to 209 ± 17 cd m2 at 4.0 V μm−1 (Fig. 8L). Electronic bracelet- and fiber-based EL modules demonstrated that CAN-4-3-30 fibers can be processed continuously into mechanically robust, stretchable, and emissive textiles, offering a scalable platform for next-generation wearable optoelectronics.

DISCUSSION

This study presents a spatiotemporal control strategy based on an internally catalyzed dynamic chemistry platform that fundamentally advances the processability of CANs. The strategic incorporation of neighboring urea groups as internal catalytic motifs enabled efficient oxime-urethane bond dissociation at industrially relevant temperatures (~110°C), notably improving melt fluidity without the need for external catalysts or elevated thermal input. Even with a modest temperature reduction postextrusion, the same internal catalysis mechanism facilitated rapid reformation of the dynamic network. This was further reinforced by reversible hydrogen bonding, which provided transient mechanical stability and ensured sufficient melt strength during high-speed processing. The cooperative interplay between dynamic covalent and supramolecular interactions enabled melt spinning at an unprecedented linear velocity of 100 m min−1 and a short solidification distance of 10 cm—far surpassing the capabilities of previously reported CAN systems. The resulting fibers exhibited a rare combination of high tensile strength (261.7 MPa), exceptional toughness (630.1 MJ m−3), excellent stretchability, and robust functional durability, including recyclability, self-healing, and solvent resistance.

Beyond these record-setting processing-performance metrics, our work offers a generalizable molecular design paradigm for reconciling the intrinsic tradeoff between dynamic bond kinetics and the demands of industrial polymer processing. The four-arm topological structure minimized chain entanglement, facilitating the transfer of small-molecule reaction kinetics to the polymer networks. Crucially, a combination of small-molecule reactivity studies, polymeric fluid dynamics, and in-network molecular dynamics analyses demonstrated that the structure-reactivity-property relationships derived from model compounds are translatable to the polymer matrix. This translatability underpins the rational design of dynamic networks with programmable behavior at multiple lengths and timescales.

Together, these findings suggest that spatiotemporally regulated CANs represent a viable platform for high throughput sustainable polymer manufacturing. By aligning dynamic molecular interactions with real-time processing demands, this study provides a path toward the industrial translation of dynamic covalent materials, bridging the gap between adaptive chemistry and functional commodity applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

The dimethyl sulfoxide–d6, diacetylmonoxime, acetone oxime, and phenethyl isocyanate were purchased from Adamas-beta. Tetrahydrofuran (THF), IPDI, and ditin butyl dilaurate (DBTDL) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. N-hydroxyacetamidine and AMG were obtained from Bide Pharmatech. PBGAD (Mw ∼ 1000) were supplied by Jining Baichuan Chemical. Co. Ltd. (Jining, China). It was vacuum dried for 120 min at 110°C before use. Dimethylolbutanoic acid (DMBA) was provided by Macklin. [EMI][TFSI] (≥99%) was provided by the Lanzhou Institute of Chemical Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Polytetrahydrofuranediol (PTMEG; Mn = 1000) was supplied by the Aladdin and vacuum dried for 120 min at 110°C before use. EL phosphor (ZnS:Cu) was supplied by Shanghai Keyan Phosphor Technology Co. Ltd. Triethylamine (TEA, 99%) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent. SYLGARD 184 was provided by Tianxia Trading (Tianjin) Co. Ltd. All the chemicals were used as received with no further purification process unless otherwise specified.

Synthesis of CANs and CPU

PBGAD was subjected to vacuum dehydration at 110°C for 2 hours. PBGAD (k millimoles) and AMG (m millimoles) were firstly dissolved in 7 ml of THF in a magnetic stirrer–equipped glass vessel at 60°C, where the k:m = 6:2, 4:3, and 2:4. IPDI (10 mmol) and DBTDL (0.2 wt %) were added, and the mixture was allowed to complete the reaction for 15 hours in a nitrogen atmosphere. Then, the required equivalent of [EMI][TFSI] (n wt %; n = 30 or 50) was added. The obtained mixture was subsequently transferred to a polytetra fluoroethylene mold and reacted at 60°C for 24 hours. The mold containing the reaction mixture was then transferred to a vacuum oven and kept for 24 hours at 60°C to produce CAN-k-m-n. CPU was prepared following the same procedure as CAN-4-3-30 and CAN-4-3-50 but without adding [EMI][TFSI]. Both CPU and CAN-4-3-30/50 maintain the same PBGAD-to-AMG ratio (k:m = 4:3).

Synthesis of water-based polyurethane

PTMEG was subjected to vacuum dehydration at 110°C for 2 hours. PTMEG, IPDI, and DBTDL were dissolved in THF in a magnetic stirrer–equipped glass vessel at 80°C for 2 hours. A mixture of DMBA and THF was then added at 60°C and continued to be stirred for 22 hours. The TEA was subsequently added, and the stirring continued for half an hour. The mixture was then cooled to room temperature, and the water was poured into the glass vessel containing the mixture and subjected to stirring at 2000 rpm for 120 min to acquire a transparent polyurethane solution. The remaining THF in the mixture was then removed by rotary evaporation, obtaining a water-based polyurethane (WPU) mixture having a solid content of ~31.2%.

Preparation of the luminescent layer and fiber

To prepare the mixture, the ZnS:Cu phosphor was dispersed in polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) 184 with a weight ratio of 3:1 using 30 min of magnetical stirring. The mixture was poured into a film and cured at 100°C for 2 hours to obtain a luminescent layer with a thickness of 300 μm. The ZnS:Cu phosphor was evenly distributed within WPU at a weight ratio of 3:1 through magnetic stirring for 30 min, resulting in a homogenous mixture. Next, the CAN-4-3-30 fiber was submerged in the mixture at room temperature for 5 min. Following this, the treated fibers were extracted from the mixture and vacuum dried at room temperature. This sequence was three times repeated to yield the luminescent fiber.

Computational details of DFT calculations

All DFT calculations were conducted using the Gaussian16 software package, Revision A.03 (45). Geometry optimization of all molecules was performed with the M06-2X functional (46) and the 6-31 + G(d,p) basis set. The following single-point calculations were performed with the 6-311 + G(d,p) basis set. The integral equation formalism polarizable continuum model (IEFPCM) (47) implemented in Gaussian was used in geometry optimizations, and the solvation model based on density (SMD) (48) was used in single-point calculations. Frequency calculations ensure that there is only one imaginary frequency for transition state species and none for the other species.
