Vaccination in individuals with Down syndrome: immune vulnerability, safety, efficacy and opportunities
Authors: Bernard Khor, Noemi A. Spinazzi, Lina R. Patel, Erika Chelales, Nicole Baumer, Peter Bulova, Brian Chicoine, Arturo Borzutzky, Hana M. El Sahly, Joaquin M. Espinosa, Melissa D. Gans, Andrew A. McCormick, Rebecca Partridge, Eric Rubenstein, Jonathan D. Santoro, Cate Speake, Juanita Valdes Camacho, Kishore Vellody
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although individuals with Down syndrome (DS) remain highly vulnerable to severe infections, vaccination remains underutilized. Here we review, specific to people with DS, the safety and efficacy of vaccination, drivers of susceptibility to infection, and existing and emerging opportunities to improve vaccine response. We find that vaccines are generally safe and immunogenic in individuals with DS, although continued research is essential to improve vaccine efficacy and health outcomes.

Introduction

Down syndrome (DS, trisomy 21) is the most common chromosomal abnormality, affecting approximately 1 in 800 live births1,2. Average life expectancy for individuals with DS (~ 60 years) remains shorter than individuals without DS, despite an estimated 5-fold increase over 60 years driven by improvements in infectious disease prevention, medical, surgical, and critical care3–5. Respiratory infections are major contributors to this increased morbidity and mortality, representing a leading cause of healthcare utilization6, hospitalization7, ICU admission, and death across the lifespan of individuals with DS8. Respiratory infections are the third highest cause of death in people with DS9, with mortality rates 10 times higher than individuals without DS6,9. Respiratory pathogens of particular concern include SARS-CoV-27,10–14, influenza9, measles15,16, pneumococcus, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). Common childhood respiratory illnesses such as bronchiolitis, asthma, and pneumonia are frequently complicated by respiratory failure, which is associated with exceedingly high morbidity and mortality in children with DS, especially when comorbidities are present17. In adults with DS, pneumonia remains a primary cause of morbidity and mortality6.

Vaccines are first-line tools that protect against severe disease from many respiratory infections. Despite their increased susceptibility to infection, vaccination uptake in people with DS is negatively impacted by caregiver concerns18, which are influenced by the paucity of DS-specific literature. Discussion

Factors contributing to altered response to infection and immunization in people with DS

Anatomic and physiologic risk factors that contribute to increased vulnerability for respiratory infections in DS include gastrointestinal dysmotility, dysphagia, and silent aspiration19,20; midface hypoplasia with eustachian tube dysfunction and sleep-disordered breathing; airway malacia, impaired airway clearance, and alveolar simplification21; congenital heart disease and pulmonary hypertension. Airway epithelium in individuals with DS shows abnormal responses to redox stress and hypoxia both at baseline and after RSV infection22,23. These dysregulated airway processes may impair pathogen clearance and increase susceptibility to infection.

Developmental and behavioral factors such as persistent mouthing behaviors24, decreased independence with hygiene-related activities of daily living25, and impaired understanding of infection transmission may all increase vulnerability to infectious diseases in individuals with DS.

In addition, individuals with DS exhibit complex immune dysregulation, leading to increased infection susceptibility and autoimmunity26. Key contributors to infection risk include deficits of the innate immune responses, adaptive immune response, and immunogenic pathways.

Innate immune responses represent initial defense. Dendritic cells in DS show impaired maturation, excessive pro-inflammatory cytokine production (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), and elevated co-stimulatory molecules (CD80/86)27–29. Monocytes and macrophages exhibit dysfunctional phagocytosis, impaired pathogen clearance, and pro-inflammatory bias, promoting broader immune dysregulation including “inflammaging” and adaptive immune disturbances in DS30,31. Mannan-binding lectin, which facilitates pathogen clearance, is decreased in DS, particularly in individuals with recurrent infections32.

Although B cells in people with DS still generate protective antibodies, quantitative and qualitative defects contribute to increased infectious susceptibility and dysfunctional humoral immunity, including lower IgG, IgM, and IgE levels27,28,33–36. Altered bone marrow output may reduce B cell diversity, and both children and adults with DS show decreased counts of class-switched memory B cells in blood and lung37–40, which are essential for long-term immunity. Thymic hypoplasia and reduced thymic output in DS are associated with lower numbers of naïve CD4⁺ T cells, which combined with an increased CD8/CD4 ratio, may impair the cellular repertoire available to respond to pathogens41,42. Memory T cells also exhibit phenotypic changes indicative of chronic activation and exhaustion41, further compromising anti-pathogen responses43. Studies showing dysregulated structure of germinal centers (where key B cell-T cell interactions occur post-vaccination) highlight important processes in lymphoid organs that are not directly seen in peripheral blood44.

DS is associated with chronic interferon (IFN) hyperactivity across multiple cell types2–4 due to overexpression of four IFN receptor genes on chromosome 21, leading to hyperactive JAK/STAT signaling3,4,6. While IFN signaling is essential for pathogen defense, excessive IFN activity can paradoxically impair normal infection responses, as seen in DS and in STAT1 gain-of-function hyperinterferonopathies linked to increased infection susceptibility45. DS also exhibits features of premature immune aging, inflammaging, and immunosenescence30, which likely amplify age-related vulnerability to infections. Other mechanistic drivers of interest include dysregulated signaling via miR-155 and miR-125b44.

Safety and immunogenicity of existing vaccines in people with DS

A growing body of literature supports the safety of routine immunizations in individuals with DS, including studies summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The Pfizer-BioNTech mRNA COVID-19 vaccine has been shown to cause only mild side effects, comparable to the general population46. Prospective trials of hepatitis A and SARS-CoV-2 vaccines in children and adults with DS demonstrated similar seroconversion rates and no increase in severe adverse events46–48. Live replicating vaccines, while contraindicated in severely immunocompromised patients, are not contraindicated for DS. Overall, these findings support the safety of vaccines in DS46–48, although we recognize that the small cohorts in many of these studies, heterogeneity in study design and variable outcome measures limits sensitivity, pointing to the value of larger studies. Continued monitoring remains critical to detect rare or unexpected adverse events, particularly with novel vaccine platforms and adjuvants.

Respiratory pathogens, including SARS-CoV-2, influenza, and pneumococcus, drive much of the increased morbidity and mortality in DS, often at young ages10,11,22,23,49,50. Novel mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccines elicit robust antibody and cellular responses in children and adults with DS, as shown in Supplementary Table 151–54. Influenza subunit vaccines induce significant antibody responses, with seroprotection rates comparable to controls in children (see Supplementary Table 1)54. Both conjugate and polysaccharide pneumococcal vaccines are immunogenic, with booster doses improving responses in some children (see Supplementary Table 1). Non-respiratory vaccines—mumps, measles, rubella, polio, tetanus, diphtheria, and hepatitis B—are also safe, with standard schedules recommended.

Discussions of vaccine benefits in DS should extend beyond severe morbidity and mortality. Even mild infections requiring outpatient care, emergency visits, or brief hospitalization can be traumatic for individuals with DS, affecting participation in healthcare, missed therapies, and school absences, which may hinder skill development and exacerbate behavioral challenges55–57. Illness also leads to missed workdays for caregivers, increasing stress and financial burden10,11.

Barriers to immunization in people with DS

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends all children with DS receive routine immunizations, including influenza and RSV vaccines, unless contraindicated40,58. However, only 58% of children with DS were up-to-date on the combined 7-vaccine series at 19 months18, compared with 71% of the general population58. Among caregivers, 18% identified as vaccine hesitant5, while 35% expressed safety concerns and 42% feared serious side effects18. Studies of families of individuals with DS59 and research in the broader intellectual and developmental disabilities community show higher hesitancy, particularly among lower-income and Black or Hispanic families60. Hesitancy is often driven by concerns about harm, including theories linking vaccines to autism, despite extensive evidence showing no causal link between vaccination and autism61–64. Since children with DS have a 3- to 8-fold higher risk of co-occurring autism, this population may be particularly vulnerable to vaccine hesitancy. These findings highlight the need for targeted policies and strategies to improve vaccine confidence and coverage in individuals with DS.

Opportunities to improve immunization response in people with DS

Many vaccines and vaccine types have been studied in individuals with DS. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, nearly every study shows immunogenicity in people with DS with most showing impaired antibody responses including lower peak responses, reduced durability, and/or gaps in epitope coverage (e.g., pneumococcus)53,65–67. The key drivers of this dysregulated antibody response remain to be clearly elucidated and may include defects in innate, B and/or T cell immunity. Emerging studies of antigen-specific T cells, largely in the context of SARS-CoV2 vaccination, find lower frequencies (and conflicting findings of impaired Th1) elicited post-vaccination51,52. One study found fewer antigen-specific IgG-secreting B cells elicited by influenza, but not pneumococcus, vaccine, suggesting differences may vary with vaccine type54. More integrated analyses of antigen-specific B and T cell responses are likely to be critical to advancing our understanding and highlighting novel druggable targets. Further, antibody titers incompletely account for vaccine-elicited protection68, highlighting persistent need for both mechanistic translational studies and epidemiologic studies to maximize clinical protection for people with DS. Despite these gaps in understanding, the uniform negative directionality of these differences, together with the infectious susceptibility of people with DS, highlight the likely need for strategies to optimize vaccine responses49,52,69. Near-term approaches may draw on interventions used in older adults without DS, who similarly show diminished responses70–75, and could be applied earlier in DS due to premature immune aging30. Future strategies that improve responses in DS may also benefit older adults more broadly such as boosters, high-dose vaccine, adjuvanted vaccine and other novel strategies are in development. These are summarized in Fig. 1.

Booster immunizations, including influenza and SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, are safe and effective, and people with DS should be prioritized for these (Fig. 1). The benefit of more frequent boosters for other vaccines in DS remains to be clarified.

High-dose influenza vaccines safely enhance immune responses and are standard for adults over 6570,76–79. Recent studies show they are also safe and immunogenic in younger adults79 and immunocompromised children and adults80–85, making them a promising near-term strategy to improve vaccine responses in people with DS, with potential applicability to other immunizations (Fig. 1).

Adjuvanted influenza vaccines, approved to improve responses in adults over 6586–89, may also enhance immunity in younger individuals with DS (Fig. 1), though safety and efficacy data are limited. Ongoing development of new adjuvants90 could benefit people with DS, but long-term safety monitoring is essential.

Other longer timeframe approaches are also under consideration and target specific pathways discussed above (Factors contributing to altered response to infection and immunization in people with DS) including hyperinterferon signaling (leading to excessive JAK/STAT signaling), inflammaging and immunosenescence (Fig. 1). JAK inhibitors, which can attenuate hyper-JAK/STAT signaling, are under investigation for autoimmunity in DS (NCT04246372, NCT05662228), and transient type I IFN inhibition may enhance vaccine responses91, suggesting value for people with DS31. However, JAK inhibitors can impair vaccination effectiveness in rheumatoid arthritis and, therefore, further investigation is needed into appropriate dosing92. Black box warnings for some JAK inhibitors limit trials solely for vaccine enhancement, but observational data may be informative. Inflammaging refers to the sterile inflammation that accompanies immune aging, which is advanced in people with DS. Metformin, which reverses aspects of inflammaging93,94, improves influenza vaccine responses in non-DS individuals with diabetes95,96, though its utility in DS is untested96. Rapalogs, which inhibit mTOR and exhibit anti-aging effects97,98, modestly enhance influenza vaccine responses99,100, but infection risk may limit use in DS and in other populations101,102. Immunosenescence accompanies immune aging and includes accumulation of senescent cells. Senolytics induce death in senescent cells and improve outcomes in murine respiratory infection models103,104 and may mitigate DS immunosenescence, though their effect on vaccine responses remains unclear.

Optimizing vaccination in people with DS requires a mechanistic understanding of their immune dysregulation, including studies of: (i) diverse vaccines, vaccine types, and adjuvants; (ii) immune responses beyond antibody titers, including affinity and antigen-specific cellular responses; (iii) individuals across the lifespan; (iv) the impact of co-occurring immune conditions and medications; (v) epidemiologic data on infection outcomes and vaccine protection; and (vi) murine models that recapitulate DS immunobiology.

In summary, people with DS have unique behavioral, anatomic, physiologic, and immunologic factors that increase infection susceptibility and influence vaccine responses. Routine vaccination is critical, and current evidence supports the safety and efficacy of standard vaccines. Ongoing research and targeted interventions are needed to ensure optimal, equitable vaccine protection for individuals with trisomy 21.
