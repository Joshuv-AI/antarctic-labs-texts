Histologic and Molecular Type Changes in Endometrial Cancer Recurrences in Comparison With Their Corresponding Primary Tumors
Authors: Esther Moreno-Moreno, Tamara Caniego-Casas, Irene Carretero-Barrio, Alfonso Cortés, Alfonso Muriel, José Antonio Domínguez-Rullán, Carmen Martín-Gromaz, Gema Moreno-Bueno, Xavier Matías-Guiu, José Palacios, Belén Pérez-Mies
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this study, molecular alterations in endometrial carcinoma (EC) recurrences were analyzed. We aimed to identify genes implicated in tumor progression and to evaluate whether histologic and molecular type shifting occurs in recurrences. Thus, we analyzed 50 samples corresponding to 24 primary ECs (15 low-grade endometrioid endometrial carcinomas [LG-EECs] and 9 high-grade endometrial carcinomas) and their corresponding 26 recurrences. These were studied by immunohistochemistry, next-generation sequencing, and MLH1 promoter methylation. We observed shared mutations in all primary tumors and their recurrences, indicating a clonal relationship between both lesions. Most morphologic and molecular changes associated with progression were found in LG-EEC. In this group, 6 patients (40%) presented additional mutations in the recurrence. These mutations more frequently affected genes of the PI3K/AKT/PTEN pathway, implicating this pathway not only in tumor initiation but also in progression. In addition, 2 patients (13%) in which the primary tumor belonged to the nonspecific molecular profile subtype, shifted to the mismatch repair deficient (MMRd) subtype after the acquisition of MLH1 promoter methylation in the recurrence lesions. In 3 patients (20%) with MMRd, there was a change from LG-EEC to G3-EEC. One TP53-mutated LG-EEC transformed into an undifferentiated carcinoma in a mediastinal lymph node metastasis after losing the expression of SMARCA2 while preserving SMARCA4 and SMARCB1. Morphologic and molecular changes in EC recurrences, especially dedifferentiation and the acquisition of MMRd, should be considered for a correct diagnosis and treatment. MMRd should be tested in metastatic lesions, if available, in patients with primary tumors reported to be of a molecular subtype different from MMRd.

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is the fourth most frequent cancer in women.1 The historical classification of EC had 2 categories (types I and II) and has undergone major changes to incorporate morphologic and, more recently, molecular features. The World Health Organization (WHO)2 includes several histologic types. These are grouped into 2 broad classes: (1) low-grade endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (LG-EEC): grades 1 and 2 LG-EEC and (2) high-grade endometrial carcinoma (HG-EC): grade 3 endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (G3-EEC), serous endometrial carcinoma (SEC), clear-cell endometrial carcinoma (CCEC), undifferentiated/dedifferentiated endometrial carcinoma (UEC/DEC), uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS), neuroendocrine endometrial carcinoma (NEEC), and the recently identified mesonephric-like endometrial carcinoma (MLEC), among others.2

It is well known that molecular alterations differ among histologic subtypes. Whereas TP53 mutations are observed in 90% of SEC and UCS, EEC frequently carry mutations in genes of the PI3K/AKT pathways, such as PTEN and PIK3CA, and the WNT/β-catenin pathway, such as CTNNB1. ARID1A mutations, which frequently occur in EEC, are also typical in CCEC. KRAS mutations are nearly always present in MLEC, although they are also relatively common in EEC.3–5 Other histotypes have a more heterogeneous molecular profile (see below).

“The Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network” proposed 4 molecular categories for EC, with prognostic value.4,6 Nowadays, these are incorporated in the WHO classification and in the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging system for treatment adjustment.7 These molecular types are: POLE mutated/ultramutated (POLEmut), microsatellite unstable/hypermutated, copy-number low, and copy-number high (with frequent mutations in TP53).4,6 Currently, a surrogate molecular classification is used in routine clinical practice, analyzing the immunohistochemical (IHC) expression of p53 and mismatch repair proteins and the presence of mutations in the exons of POLE. According to the results, EC is classified in one of the 4 surrogate categories: POLEmut, mismatch repair deficient (MMRd), p53-abnormal (p53abn), and nonspecific molecular profile (NSMP).4,8–10

POLEmut EC is characterized by mutations in the exonuclease domain of POLE and high mutation rates, with a very good prognosis, representing 7% to 10% of EC.4,8 From a histologic point of view, most cases have endometrioid histology, although tumors with an ambiguous morphology and high grade are relatively frequent. MMRd is most frequently secondary to promoter hypermethylation of MLH1 but can also be due to somatic or germline (Lynch syndrome) mutations of mismatch repair (MMR) genes. This category usually shows a high mutation rate and an intermediate prognosis4,8 and occurs in up to 30% of EEC. Moreover, this is a frequent alteration in tumors with morphologic intratumor heterogeneity, such as G3-EEC, dedifferentiated carcinomas, mixed EEC-CCEC, and NEEC. The NSMP group typically presents a low mutation rate, the prognosis is intermediate, and it is the most frequent category in LG-EEC,4,11 although most histologic types, except SEC and UCS, are represented in this molecular group. Lastly, the p53abn group is characterized by a low mutation rate and high copy-number alterations. TP53 mutations are observed in 90% of cases of SEC and other high-grade tumors, such as UCS, and this molecular type is considered the one with the worst prognosis.4

Despite our current knowledge of the molecular alterations of primary EC, genes and molecular patterns implicated in EC relapse are not well studied.12–15 In this sense, despite the relevance that the surrogate molecular classification has in the management of patients with EC, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have analyzed possible changes in the molecular subtype during progression. Thus, the aim of the present study was to analyze the molecular alterations found in a series of EC recurrences, from both low-grade and high-grade primary EC, not only to identify genes implicated in tumor progression but also to evaluate whether molecular type shifting is present in recurrences.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Case Selection

ECs with clinical relapses were selected from the Pathology Department in the Ramón y Cajal University Hospital (Madrid, Spain), in which both the primary tumor and the recurrence had available histologic material. Clinical data were obtained from the hospital’s clinical databases. The present study has received approval from the ethical committee of the Ramón y Cajal University Hospital (reference number 226/19).

Pathologic Analysis and IHC Study

Tumor histologic type and FIGO grade were evaluated by 2 expert pathologists in 3 to 6 whole histologic slides, depending on the case, following the WHO Fifth edition and FIGO criteria.2,7 We performed IHC studies on whole sections of primary tumors for surrogate molecular categorization. IHC results were analyzed following the 2023 “American Society of Clinical Oncology and the College of American Pathologists” criteria.16 Immunostaining was performed using Dako REAL EnVision Detection System and Ventana Medical Systems (DAKO). The antibodies used in the complete series were p53, MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6, ARID1-A, β-catenin, and Napsin A. HER2 was studied in all primary HG-EC and their recurrences following the Template for Reporting Results of Biomarker Testing of Specimens From Patients With Carcinoma of Gynecologic Origin (American Society of Clinical Oncology and the College of American Pathologists).16 We have selected Additional IHC analysis were performed of specific primary and/or recurrence tumors for a better categorization. Additional antibodies used included CKAE1/AE3, E-cadherin, SMARCA2, SMARCA4, INI-1, chromogranin, and INSM1 for the diagnosis of UEC/DEC carcinoma; and TTF1, Gata3 and CD10 for the diagnosis of MLEC. The list of antibodies, conditions, and evaluation criteria are shown in Supplemental Table 1 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/PAS/B934).

Next-generation Sequencing

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed in both primary and recurrence tumors (2 relapses in 2 cases). Regions of interest were marked on hematoxylin-eosin stains and subsequently punched. DNA extraction of paraffin tissue was performed using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (QIAGEN GmbH), following the instructions established by the manufacturer. The quantification of the extracted DNA was performed was fluorometric analysis using the QUBIT 3.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer).

A custom panel was used, designed with the SureDesign platform from Agilent Tech., including POLE. The panel included 61 genes frequently mutated in gynecologic cancers (AKT1, ARID1A, ARID1B, ARID5B, ATR, BCOR, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRAF, CASP8, CCNE1, CDH1, CDH4, CDH19, COL1A1, CSMD3, CTCF, CTNNB1, EGFR, ERBB2, ESR1, FBXW7, FGFR1, FGFR2, FOXA2, GRB7, GSDMB, MAP2K4, KRAS, MAP3K1, MLL3, MLH1, MKI67, MSH2, MSH6, MYC, NCOR1, NF1, NRAS, PGAP3, PIK3CA3, PIK3R1, PMS2, PNMT, POLE, PPP2R1A, PRPF18, PTEN, KMT2B, RB1, RPL22, SF3B1, SPOP, STARD3, TAF1, TBX3, TCAP, TP53, VGLL1, ZNF217, and ZNF703). Library preparation was carried out following an adaptation made in our laboratory, based on the Agilent SureSelectXT FFPE protocol, and the sequencing was performed using the Ion S5 Torrent (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Subsequently, bioinformatics analysis was carried out following a specific pipeline and using Novoalign V3 (2021)17 and VarScan.18 Variant annotation was performed using Ensembl VEP 88 version.19 Annotated variants were filtered using the functional information (discarding benign variants), allelic frequency (>5%), and selecting those variants that directly affect the coding of the protein (exonic and intronic that alter splicing). Lastly, visual analysis was performed by Navegador IGV.20

Two recurrences from case 17 were analyzed by an external genomic sequencing test (FoundationOne CDx assay [Foundation Medicine, Roche]).

Other Molecular Studies

We have carried out MLH1 methylation analysis by methylation-specific polymerase chain reaction in primary tumors and recurrences with loss of MLH1 expression. Specific primers for the methylated and unmethylated alleles are shown in Supplemental Table 2 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/PAS/B934). Polymerase chain reaction conditions and results analysis were performed according to Yoshioka et al.21

We performed fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) for the analysis of TP53 copy number (SPEC TP53/CEN 17 Dual Color Probe, ZytoVision) in case 6. FISH was also performed for the analysis of ERBB2 amplification in all HG-EC, both primary tumor and recurrence (FISH probe PathVysion, Abbott). ERBB2 was considered amplified when ERBB2/CEP17 ratio ≥ 2.0 or ERBB2/CEP17 ratio < 2.0 with average ERBB2 copies ≥ 6 per nucleus.

RESULTS

We analyzed 50 samples corresponding to 24 patients. In 22 patients, samples were studied from the primary tumor and one recurrence, while in 2 patients, we studied samples from the primary tumor and 2 recurrences. In case 17, the second relapse (also analyzed by our panel) and another subsequent relapse (lung) were referred to FoundationOne for analysis. Recurrences were located at the vagina (n = 7), lung/pleura (n = 6), extraregional lymph nodes (n = 5), urinary bladder (n = 2), and cervix, vulva, rectum, abdominal wall, soft tissue, and brain (n = 1, each).

Clinicopathological and IHC features of this series are presented in Table 1. Regarding the histologic type of primary tumors, we studied 15 LG-EEC, 5 SEC, 1 G3-EEC, 1 UCS, 1 NEEC, and 1 MLEC. All LG-EECs showed a homogeneous morphology in all slides studied. According to the surrogate molecular classification, 12 primary tumors were NSMP (10 LG-EEC, 1 G3-EEC, and 1 MLEC), 4 MMRd (4 LG-EEC), and 8 p53abn (5 SEC, 1 UCS, 1 NEEC, and 1 LG-EEC). Tumors with MMRd showed loss of MLH1 and PMS2 proteins with MSH6 preserved. MLH1 promoter hypermethylation was observed in 3 cases (1 case could not be evaluated, but no mutations were identified in MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6). All cases were negative for Napsin A. Regarding HER2, only one primary SEC (case 2) was positive (2+ by immunohistochemistry, FISH amplified). However, its recurrence was not amplified. We detected mutations in the recurrences that were not present in the primary tumor in 8 out of 24 patients (33%; 5 LG-EEC, 1 SEC, 1 UCS, and 1 MLEC). MMRd tumors more frequently showed additional mutations in their recurrences (3 out of 4, 75%) than NSMP (3 out of 12, 25%) and p53abn (2 out of 8, 25%). Additional mutations were in the genes PIK3CA (4 recurrences), CTNNB1 (2 recurrences) and ESR1, FBXW7, FGFR1, GRB7, PTEN, NF1 and TP53 (1 recurrence each).

In contrast, mutations in ARID1A and PIK3R1 were not detected in 4 recurrences; and PIK3CA, PTEN, and PPP2R1A in 1 recurrence, respectively.

In both primary tumors, we performed MMR protein IHC study in 2 additional slides, confirming the preserved expression of all MMR proteins in both cases.

DISCUSSION

A major determinant of prognosis in patients with EC is recurrences, so a better understanding of their mechanisms is needed for appropriate patient management. In the present series, we observed more morphologic and molecular changes associated with progression in LG-EEC than in HG-EC. In LG-EEC tumors, we observed shared mutations in all primary tumors and their recurrences, indicating a clonal relationship between both. Moreover, we observed additional mutations in the recurrence in 33% of tumors, a change to HG-EC in 26%, and a shift to a different molecular subtype in 13%.

As expected, the number of mutations per recurrence and the percentage of mutated recurrences were higher among MMRd tumors. However, in the complete group of LG-EEC, we did not observe a specific pattern of mutations in recurrences. Mutations were present in different genes in each tumor, a finding also reported by Gibson et al,13 in a series of primary EC and their paired peritoneal metastases. In our series, the most frequent mutations in recurrences were in PIK3CA (26%), which occurred in tumors with previous mutations in PIK3/AKT/PTEN pathway. Primary EECs harbor multiple cooccurring mutations in the PI3K pathway, including PTEN, PIK3CA, PIK3R1, and KRAS.22 Our observations suggested that the PIK3/AKT/PTEN pathway is not only implicated in LG-EEC initiation but also in progression.

Among other mutations observed only in recurrences, we detected a hot-spot ESR1 mutation in one tumor. Mutations in ESR1 are infrequent in EEC and are usually associated with hormonal treatment, although this patient did not receive it.

Some mutations were only present in the primary tumor and not in their corresponding recurrence. These mostly affected the ARID1A and PIK3R1 genes. This may be due to intratumor heterogeneity and subclonal mutations in ARID1A and PIK3R1, which have been previously reported.23–25

An interesting observation of our study, with clinical implications, was that 2 NSMP LG-EECs shifted to MMRd. In both cases, the recurrences lost the expression of MLH1 and PMS2 due to MLH1 promoter hypermethylation, indicating that during progression, tumors not only acquire new genetic alterations but also epigenetic changes. The exact frequency of MMRd acquisition during tumor progression is not well established. However, Ashley et al,26 after reanalyzing data published by Gibson et al,13 reported that 1 out of 19 EECs shifted from a POLE-related mutational signature in the primary tumor to an Microsatellite instability-related mutational signature in the metastasis. In addition, the authors reported a shift from aging to an Microsatellite instability-related signature in 2 of 3 copy-number high SEC analyzed. From a practical point of view, since MMRd in metastatic EC can be an indication for the use of immunotherapy, these results suggested that MMRd should be tested in metastatic lesions, if available, in patients with primary tumors reported to be of a molecular subtype different to MMRd. In addition, a careful analysis of the IHC study in the primary tumor should be performed to detect possible subclonal loss of MMR that could explain the molecular shift. In this regard, a subclonal loss of MMR proteins, more frequently MLH1 and PMS2, has been reported.27,28

Together with these molecular changes, we observed a change in histotype in 4 LG-EECs. Three of them evolved to a G3-EEC in the context of MMRd. In 2 cases, MMRd was present in the primary tumor and developed in the remaining case after the occurrence of MLH1 promoter methylation in the first recurrence. The frequency of MMRd increases in G3-EEC, which frequently shows low-grade areas, supporting the notion that genetic instability promotes morphologic heterogeneity.

The fourth case of shifting from LG-EEC to HG-EC corresponded to a LG-EEC. This case transformed into a UEC in a mediastinal lymph node metastasis, with a previous liver metastasis with low-grade morphology. We studied the primary LG-EEC and the UEC metastasis by NGS, but not the liver metastasis. This case is unusual for different reasons. First, it was p53abn, while most UEC/DEC develop from MMRd or NSMP, being infrequent among p53abn tumors.29 Interestingly, this was the only primary LG-EEC in our series classified as p53abn, confirming the potential poor prognosis of LG-EEC carrying TP53 mutations. Second, although infrequently, dedifferentiation can occur in metastasis without evidence of this change in the primary tumor, despite adequate sampling.30–32 This makes diagnosis difficult if, as in the present case, metastasis occurs at a distant site and years later after the primary. Awareness of the phenomenon of dedifferentiation of EC in a metastatic site is important to avoid misdiagnosis.31 Third, the lymph node metastases in our case had mutations in common with the primary tumor but, in contrast to the primary tumor, the metastases did not express epithelial nor neuroendocrine markers and lost SMARCA2 expression. However, the expression of other SWI-SNF proteins was preserved, such as ARIDA-1, SMARCA4 and SMARCB1, without detectable mutations in ARID1B. For this reason, if UEC/DEC is suspected in any lesion, a complete study of SWI-SNF proteins, including SMARCA2, should be performed. Although SMARCA2 is frequently lost as a secondary event after SMARCA4 loss,33 some cases, such as the present one, can lose only SMARCA2. This situation has been reported in a subset of small cell carcinoma of the ovary of the hypercalcaemic type with retention of nuclear immunoreactivity of SMARCA4 but with SMARCA4 alterations identified on molecular testing. All cases exhibited loss of SMARCA2 immunoreactivity.34

Compared with LG-EEC, a lower number of genetic alterations were found in high-grade tumors. This may be due to the low mutational frequency that characterizes p53abn and NSMP tumors, which were the only molecular groups represented in our HG-EC series. All SEC in our series belonged to the p53abn group. SEC frequently shows chromosomal alterations that are implicated in tumor initiation. Chromosomal alterations, which cannot be detected by our panel, may also be involved in tumor progression.14 Thus, Ashley et al26 reported a shift from an APOBEC-related mutational signature in the primary tumor to a homologous recombination-related signature in the peritoneal metastasis in 1 out of 3 SECs analyzed. In this tumor, changes mostly consisted of large-scale transitions.

Among the 9 HG-EC recurrences in our series, only 2 cases acquired a CTNNB1 mutation (1 UCS and 1 MLEC), and 1 SEC acquired a NF1 mutation. While CTNNB1 mutations are known to be involved in tumor initiation of LG-EEC, they are infrequent in other histologic types. Thus, they have only been reported in 1% of UCS35 and in 7% of MLEC.36

In summary, we observed mutations in the recurrences that were not present in the primary tumor in 33% of cases, and we detected more mutational changes in the progression of LG-EEC than in HG-EC. Although a specific pattern of mutations was not observed, mutations more frequently affected the PI3K/AKT/PTEN pathway. In addition to genetic changes, MLH1 promoter hypermethylation occurred in some LG-EEC, inducing a shift in the molecular subtype from NSMP to MMRd (13% LG-EEC). In a subset of LG-EEC, genetic and epigenetic changes induced a shift to an HG-EC, challenging the diagnosis of an endometrial origin. Morphologic and molecular changes in EC recurrences, especially dedifferentiation and the acquisition of MMRd, should be considered for a correct diagnosis and treatment.
