Disparities in availability of new cancer drugs worldwide: 1990-2022
Authors: Meng Li, DukHee Ka, Qiushi Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDespite progress in the development of new cancer drugs, concerns about equity of access remain. This study aimed to examine the availability and timeliness of availability of new cancer drugs around the globe over the past three decades and their associations with country characteristics.MethodsFrom a pharmaceutical intelligence database we identified new cancer drugs launched between 1990 and 2022. We calculated the number of new drugs launched in each country and the delay in launches. Using a multivariable linear regression and a Cox regression model with shared frailty, we examined the associations of the country’s Gross National Income (GNI) per capita, cancer incidence, number of physicians per population, and Gini index with the number of new cancer drug launches and launch delay in a country, respectively.ResultsA total of 568 cancer drugs were launched for the first time globally between 1990 and 2022. Among these, 35% had been launched in only one country by 2022, 22% in 2–5 countries, 15% in 6–10 countries, and 28% in more than 10 countries. The number of new cancer drugs launched in a country in this period ranged from 0 to 345. The average delays from the first global launch to the second, third, fourth, and fifth launch were 18.0 months, 24.3 months, 32.5 months, and 39.4 months, respectively. Our multivariate models showed that higher GNI per capita and cancer incidence in a country were associated with more launches and shorter delays.ConclusionThis research reveals significant disparities in the availability and timeliness of availability of new cancer drugs across countries. These disparities are likely to have contributed to the poor cancer outcomes observed in many countries.

Introduction

Over the last two decades, the advent of many breakthrough therapies has transformed the way we treat cancer. For example, the development of imatinib in chronic myelogenous leukaemia has changed the usually fatal disease into a manageable condition.1 Long-term data also show that the new chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy is likely curative for a subset of patients with B-cell lymphomas with minimal toxicities.2 Moreover, the development of targeted oral anticancer medicines offer patients the convenience of treatment at home, which is generally preferred by patients over intravenous administration of chemotherapy.3

While these and many other cancer therapies can significantly improve outcomes, they are not always available to every patient who needs them or only available after a significant delay, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). A recent study from Brazil found that only 60% of cancer drugs approved in the US between 2010 and 2019 eventually received marketing authorisation in Brazil, after a median delay of more than 500 days.4 For the 39 cancer drugs in the national price negotiations in China between 2015 and 2018, the median delay—the difference between the first global approval and the approval in China—was 44 months.5 In an analysis of the national medicines lists of 135 countries with a per-capita gross national income less than $25 000 in 2015,6 only 10% of countries included all 25 essential cancer medicines from the WHO Model Lists of Essential Medicines (EML). In a survey of oncologists from 87 countries, the proportion of respondents indicating universal availability of each top 20 high-priority medication was 9–54% in low-income and lower-middle-income countries, 13–90% in upper-middle-income countries, and 68–94% in high-income countries.7

Lack of availability and delayed availability of new cancer medicines can be a challenge even in some high-income countries (HICs). An analysis of 12 cancer drugs in 25 European countries between 2011 and 2018 found that marketing approval for cancer drugs came on average 242 days later in Europe than in the US.8 Among 46 oncology products approved by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) between 2018 and 2021, 45 of them were available in Germany by 2023, while less than 10 were available in Estonia, Latvia, Serbia, Slovakia, Lithuania, North Macedonia, Turkey, Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Malta.9

In countries where the latest technologies and innovations in cancer treatment are not available, patients are either treated with inferior therapies in their home country or have to travel abroad to receive state-of-the-art care.10 11 The high cost of travelling abroad to receive cancer care, which is usually not covered by health insurance, means that very few patients can afford it. Furthermore, even among patients who have the resources, research has found they often face transportation, language, and cultural barriers when seeking care.11

Existing studies on the differences in the availability of new cancer drugs across countries usually focused on one region (eg, Europe) and examined a small sample of drugs.71215 In this study, we analysed all commercially developed anticancer drugs in all countries from 1990 through to the end of 2022. We characterised their availability by region and income, and further investigated factors associated with lack of availability and delayed availability of new cancer drugs.

Methods

Data sources

We defined the availability of a cancer drug in a country as whether a drug had been launched in that country. We identified launches of new cancer drugs worldwide from Pharmaprojects,16 a commercial database that tracks global pharmaceutical Research & Development activities in more than 150 countries. The database collects information from company press releases, academic presentations, and other public sources, on drug name, disease name, origin (eg, chemical, biological, natural product), therapeutic classes, event history (eg, advancement in clinical trial phase, approval, launch), and so on. This database has been used in research for emerging drugs and oncology drug approvals in a variety of settings.1719

Study sample and variables

To identify cancer drugs, we selected all drugs with the disease name containing “cancer” and the therapeutic class containing “anticancer” in the database. In practice, a cancer drug could be launched multiple times in the same country for different indications and in multiple countries for the same or different indications. However, due to the substantial missingness in the information of the indications for each drug launch from the database, we focused on the first launch of each drug in a country, irrespective of its indication, in our analysis at the drug level. Each drug was identified by a unique drug name in the data without specifying the dosages. We extracted the date when the drug was first launched in a country, representing the time when a cancer drug first became available to the country’s population. Subsequent launches of the same drug in the same country (eg, for different indications) were excluded from the analysis. Since information on drug launches in Pharmaprojects relied primarily on drug companies’ voluntary reporting, the exact drug launch date was likely subject to missingness, especially when outside the major markets such as the US, Europe, Japan, and China. To supplement drug launch data, we used the date of regulatory approval, if recorded in the database, as a proxy for the launch date to indicate the date of drug availability, when the launch date was not available. We included cancer drugs that were first launched in any country between 1 January 1990 and 31 December 2022 in this study. We excluded the records stating drug launches in the “EU” as it is unlikely that a drug became available in all EU member states simultaneously. For each cancer drug that had been launched, its essential medicine status was determined based on the WHO’s EML.20

For country characteristics, we obtained population size,21 Gross National Income (GNI),22 Gini index,23 and the number of physicians per 1000 people24 for each country from the World Bank. The Gini index measures the inequality in the distribution of income or consumption among individuals or households within an economy, with a value of 0 representing perfect equality and 100 perfect inequality. In addition, to characterise the disease burden and need for cancer drugs we extracted crude (malignant) cancer incidence rates by country from the Global Cancer Observatory.25

Statistical analysis

Our primary analysis focused on the availability of new cancer drugs around the world. We first estimated the trend in annual number of cancer drugs that were launched for the first time in the world between 1990 and 2022. This measures the overall progress on the discovery and development of anticancer therapies. To understand the cross-country variations in the availability of new cancer drugs, we calculated the total number of new cancer drug launches in each country during the same period. We further examined the trends in the average number of cancer drugs launched each year by a country’s income level (high, upper middle, lower middle, and low), following the year-specific classification of 218 economies based on gross national income (GNI) per capita by the World Bank.26

To examine the association of the total cancer drug launches in a country within the latest 5 years (ie, 2018–2022) with country-level characteristics, we employed a linear regression model with independent variables including the country’s GNI, Gini index, cancer incidence, and number of physicians per 1000 people. Countries not in the Pharmaprojects database were assumed to have no drug launch or approval. We calculated cancer incidence by multiplying the crude cancer rate with the population size of the country. All country-level characteristics were measured in the baseline year 2018 except for cancer incidence, which was measured in 2020 due to data availability. We chose the most recent 5 years of data (ie, 2018–2022) to examine recent patterns in the variation in cancer drug availability across countries, which also constituted a substantial proportion of all cancer drug launches during 1990–2022 due to the significant acceleration of progress on cancer drug discovery and development in recent years. We repeated the analysis for drugs on the WHO’s EML.

Next, we examined delay in availability of new cancer drugs across countries. We defined the launch delay of a cancer drug as the time lag between its first launch in the world and a subsequent launch in a different country. To examine the associations between country characteristics and launch delay, we ran a Cox proportional hazards model with shared frailty27 within the same drug. A shared frailty model introduces random effects in the survival analysis model that can capture the potential correlation between launch delays in different countries of the same drug (ie, same cluster), providing more efficient estimators by properly accounting for the within-cluster correlations.28 The origin was defined as the first global launch of a drug, and the events were defined as subsequent launches of the same drug in other countries (or censored if no event was observed by 31 December 2022). Independent variables included a country’s GNI, Gini index, cancer incidence, number of physicians per 1000 people, and the year of the drug’s first global launch. Similarly, to examine the most recent trends and patterns in launch delay, we included drugs with their first global launch between 2018 and 2022 in the Cox models. We also repeated the analysis by restricting it to the drugs on the WHO’s EML. Data cleaning, processing, and descriptive analyses were performed using Python and regression analyses were conducted in Stata.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the study design and conduct, dissemination of results, and evaluation of this study.

Results

Drug launches

A total of 568 unique cancer drugs with the first global launch between 1990 and 2022 met our inclusion criteria (online supplemental figure S1). We included a total of 4184 drug launches or approvals for these cancer drugs in 111 countries in our analysis, among which the dates of 1115 drug launches (26.6%) were approximated by the drug’s regulatory approval information due to the lack of the exact drug launch information. Among these cancer drugs, 63% were chemical drugs, 31% were biological drugs, and 6% were natural products (table 1). More than half of new cancer drugs were launched in the past decade (35% during the latest 5 year period of 2018–2022 and 20% during 2013–2017), compared with 22% during 2003–2012 and 18% during 1993–2002. By 31 December 2022, 35% of the 568 drugs had been launched in only one country, 22% had been launched in 2–5 countries, and 43% had been launched in more than five countries.

There is a substantial geographic variation in the number of new cancer drug launches worldwide. Regions with the most launches during 1990–2022 were North America, Western Europe, East Asia, and Australia — the high-income regions of the world. In comparison, Africa, Southeast Asia, the Middle East and Central Asia, as well as Eastern Europe — the low- and middle-income regions of the world — had the least number of launches (figure 1). At the country level, the number of new cancer drugs launched between 1990 and 2022 ranged from 0 to 345 (online supplemental table S1). Countries with the highest number of launches during 1990–2022 were the United States (total=345), Japan (total=224), Canada (total=221), Australia (total=204), the United Kingdom (total=191), and China (total=169); these countries also had the highest number of launches as the first globally or within 12 months after the first launch. The difference in the number of cancer drugs launched between wealthy and poor nations also widened over time (figure 2, online supplemental table S2). The average number of new cancer drug launches per country each year increased from 0.5 per year in the early 1990s to 8.7 per year in 2022 in high-income countries, from 0.1 to 1.5 per year in upper-middle-income countries, while remaining minimal among lower-middle-income and low-income countries.

In the multivariable linear regression model using launch data during the most recent 5 years (2018–2022) (table 2), we found that a country’s GNI and cancer incidence were positively associated with the number of new cancer drug launches. A $1000 increase in GNI per capita was associated with 0.66 more new cancer drug launches (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.40 to 0.93, p<0.001); a 100 000 increase in annual cancer incidence was associated with 2.96 more cancer drug launches (95% CI: 2.35 to 3.57, p<0.001). Drug launch delays

In addition to significant variation in the availability of new cancer drugs across countries, there were also long delays in drug launches in many countries. Nearly half (45.2%) of the cancer drugs in our sample were first launched in the United States, followed by 10.6% in China, 10.2% in EU5 countries (the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Italy, and Spain), and 8.8% in Japan (table 1). The average delays between the first global launch and the second, third, fourth, and fifth launch were 18.0 (standard deviation (SD): 25.6) months, 24.3 (SD: 27.3) months, 32.5 (SD: 36.4) months, and 39.5 (SD: 41.9) months, respectively (online supplemental table S3). Over time, launch delays appeared to be decreasing: The average launch delays between the first global launch and the second, third, fourth, and fifth launches were 20.2 months, 25.6 months, 37.9 months, and 44.5 months in 1990–1999, respectively, which decreased to 15.9 months, 21.5 months, 29.0 months, and 36.6 months in 2010–2022, respectively (online supplemental figure S2).

The Cox model with shared frailty (table 3) estimated the adjusted HR of a $1000 increase in per capita GNI to be 1.05 (95% CI: 1.039 to 1.051, p<0.001) and the HR of a 100 000 increase in annual cancer incidence 1.10 (95% CI: 1.09 to 1.11, p<0.001), showing that higher GNI and higher cancer incidence were associated with an increased hazard rate for the time to launch, which means a shorter launch delay. For cancer drugs on the WHO’s EML, GNI and cancer incidence remained positively associated with shorter delays but with smaller HR estimates.

Discussion

We analysed commercially developed anticancer drugs globally over the past three decades and found that despite significant progress in the discovery and development of new cancer medicines, few were launched in lower-middle-income or low-income countries, and the gap between wealthy and poor nations has widened over time. Launch delays were also common, and it can take a few months to a few years for a cancer drug to be launched in other countries after its first global launch.

Consistent with economic theory, we found fewer launches and longer delays in markets with lower expected revenue, measured by lower GNI per capita (a proxy for ability and willingness to pay) and lower cancer incidence (a proxy for volume). Fewer launches and longer launch delays of anticancer medicines may have contributed to the disproportionally high cancer morbidity and mortality in LMICs. Previous research had shown that although overall incidence was lower in LMICs, mortality-to-incidence ratios were significantly higher in LMICs, especially among women.25 Without timely access to effective treatments, this disparity is expected to worsen as cancer incidence is projected to increase much faster in LMICs than in HICs, driven by demographic changes and increasing risk factors associated with a growing economy.25

Drug manufacturers’ decisions to not launch or to delay the launch of products in LMICs may be driven by the low ability to pay among patients in poor countries and the fear of price erosion in high-priced markets. Academics, industry, and global health policy-makers have long advocated using differential pricing —pricing drugs in poor nations systematically lower than in wealthy nations — to improve access to life-saving medicines around the world.29 The economic case for differential pricing is strong: when markets can be separated, pricing the product based on the consumer’s willingness or ability to pay is a profit-maximising strategy.30 Furthermore, differential pricing can increase consumer welfare for both low-priced and high-priced markets.30 However, the practical challenge of global differential pricing is demonstrated clearly by the increasing pressure from the public and political parties in high-priced nations to use external reference pricing and parallel trade to lower drug prices. For example, many American politicians, including presidential candidates, have repeatedly called for benchmarking drug prices in the US based on prices in other industrialised nations.31 32 Recently, the state of Florida was authorised by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to import cheaper prescription drugs from Canada.33 Such downward pressure on prices in high-priced markets may have resulted in manufacturers’ strategic behaviours, such as holding off the launch in lower-price markets until the price in high-priced markets was secured.

Lack of launch and delayed launch of new cancer drugs in LMICs and some HICs could also be due to demand-side factors.34 35 For example, countries may not have sufficient funds to cover new cancer drugs in their national formulary or to support infrastructure for administrating specialty cancer therapies. They may delay adoption of new drugs and technologies in an effort to control healthcare spending. Additionally, in many countries, the regulatory approval and the price and reimbursement process of new drugs can take years, due to inadequate human resources, financial constraints, and insufficient transparency and communication with industry stakeholders.36

To achieve appropriate and sustainable price differences across markets will require either that higher-priced markets forego trying to “import” low drug prices from low-priced markets through parallel trade and external referencing, or that such practices become less feasible.29 The most promising approach is for each nation to negotiate confidential rebates with manufacturers.29 While manufacturers sell to distributors at uniform prices, final transaction prices after the rebate and discount can differ across markets and are confidential, thus eliminating opportunities for parallel trade and external referencing.

In addition to “fair” pricing strategies that provide more incentives for pharmaceutical companies to launch drugs in LMICs,37 38 timely access to new cancer drugs in LMICs also requires strengthening their regulatory process. Compared with more mature markets, LMICs’ drug regulatory capabilities are much more limited. Many of these countries rely to some extent on assessments by major regulatory authorities in the world, such as the US FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for their own regulatory decisions. For example, Mexico requires only abbreviated marketing applications when products have already been approved by a stringent regulatory authority.39 Drug approval by one of the major authorities such as the FDA or the EMA can provide a valid basis for marketing a product in LMICs, which could reduce duplication of efforts, lower costs of approval, and shorten approval times. The WHO’s Prequalification of Medical Products can facilitate this process.40

There are several limitations of our study that are worth noting. First, we used drug launch date to measure the time of availability, and information on drug launch relied on voluntary reporting from the manufacturers or other public sources of information such as company reporting revenue of a drug in a country. As a result, there might be a considerable amount of missingness in data from small markets, which may partially explain the extremely low numbers of launches in lower-middle-income countries and low-income countries. To address this limitation, we used data on regulatory approval to supplement launch data when information on launch was not available. However, both launch and regulatory approval are proxies for availability. Second, prescription drug coverage in a country was not considered in this study. Insurance coverage can lower the costs of cancer drugs to patients thus increasing uptake and revenue, which could incentivise the pharmaceutical companies to minimise the delay in drug launches. Wealthier nations generally have a more advanced health insurance system and more generous prescription drug coverage. Therefore, we expect prescription drug coverage to be positively correlated with more launches and shorter launch delays. Third, information on drug prices is not available in this dataset. Drug price may play a role in strategic launch behaviours. A manufacturer would not launch a drug in a country if only very few patients could afford it. Therefore, there could be greater cross-country differences in drug launches for more expensive drugs. Lastly, due to limited drug class information in our data we were not able to determine whether a cancer drug at its launch is the first-in-class in a country. It may be argued that having one drug in a drug class available is more important than having a second one in the same class available. Future studies could examine differences in availability of first-in-class cancer drugs across countries.

Conclusion

Despite considerable progress in the discovery and development of new cancer medicines in recent decades, many of these medicines remained unavailable many years after their first global launch or were only available after long delays, especially in the lower-income parts of the world. This disparity underscores the need for policy solutions to provide more equitable access to cancer medicines globally.
