From anti-drug antibody incidence to clinical relevance: interpreting highly sensitive immunogenicity assays in biosimilars
Authors: Anita Krishnan, Somesh BP, Gaurav Mehta, Shilpa Ramaswamy, Soumen Chakraborty, Shyamapada Mandal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHighly sensitive anti-drug antibody (ADA) assays in biosimilar comparative trials can generate high apparent positivity clustered near decision thresholds without corresponding clinical impact. Using approved biosimilars of ustekinumab and denosumab, this work retrospectively examines alternate analytical strategies to improve the biological interpretability of ADA findings.MethodsClinical ADA datasets were re-analyzed using a variability-anchored minimum screening cut point (mSCP), magnitude-aware log S/N versus percent inhibition contour visualization, and subject-level classification into preexisting, treatment-boosted, and treatment-emergent categories. A subset of the data was also subjected to longitudinal change from baseline evaluation.ResultsConventional tiered analysis produced overall screening sample positivity of 48–82% across all studies and subject-level ADA incidence >80% in 10 of the 11 arms (range, 64–100%), without any corresponding impact on pharmacokinetics, safety, or efficacy. The mSCP-based approach reduced cut point–adjacent noise-consistent signals, enabling subject-level classification that is clinically more relevant. Longitudinal analysis further separated persistent from transient responses.ConclusionBiosimilar immunogenicity testing is a confirmatory exercise against an already-approved reference, not an investigative characterization. As regulatory frameworks move toward reduced reliance on comparative Phase 3 studies, identifying persistent ADA responses at Phase 1 becomes increasingly important; a fit-for-purpose, comparability-anchored analytical strategy warrants collective regulatory rethinking.Clinical trial registration: www.isrctn.com; identifier: ISRCTN1142400EudraCT Number: 2021-006668-25;ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05335356 ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05323708EudraCT number: 2021-006545-36, CT.gov number: NCT05345691

Introduction

Biosimilars are developed to be highly similar to an already-approved reference biologic, with no clinically meaningful differences. As with originator biologics, they are large, heterogeneous proteins produced in living systems and therefore require rigorous process control and extensive Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls/analytical characterization. In contrast to originator development, however, biosimilar development programs typically rely on a reduced clinical package centered on comparative pharmacokinetics (PK) and targeted safety and immunogenicity evaluations, rather than large clinical efficacy programs designed to establish benefit de novo [1–6]. Recent regulatory guidance from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency reflects a continued streamlining direction, with reduced reliance on comparative Phase 3 efficacy studies where analytical and Phase 1 clinical pharmacology data provide sufficient assurance of biosimilarity [7–11]. This approach reduces development time and cost, contributing to lower prices and improved patient access while maintaining stringent manufacturing and quality requirements [7,12].

Consistent with this paradigm, biosimilars are characterized through comprehensive, multi-attribute analytical and functional comparability to the reference product. While the primary amino acid sequence must be identical, orthogonal biochemical, biophysical, and functional methods establish similarity for higher-order structure, aggregates/fragments, and post-translational modifications (PTMs), supported by mechanism-relevant bioassays [13–15]. Process-related impurities such as host cell proteins and residual DNA, together with extractables/leachable from container closure systems, are also evaluated to demonstrate process consistency [16–18]. Together, these controls provide confidence in quality, safety, and comparability and reduce residual uncertainty arising from manufacturing differences [16–18].

Some uncertainty may nonetheless remain – e.g., in higher-order structural features below current analytical resolution, small differences in aggregates/fragments/PTMs within scientifically justified ranges [13–15], or trace-level “hitchhiking” host cell proteins that may differ compositionally between products and could contribute to immunogenicity risk, although high-resolution mass spectrometry-based approaches can now interrogate such differences even at very low levels [18]. Clinical studies, where conducted, therefore primarily help contextualize whether any remaining uncertainty translates into clinically meaningful immunogenicity [19]. When a biosimilar and its reference product are highly similar in sequence and product quality, with process-related impurities controlled, high anti-drug antibody (ADA) positivity observed in both arms of recent comparative studies warrants careful interpretation beyond incidence alone.

With accumulated biosimilar experience, it is increasingly evident that ADA incidence generated by highly sensitive contemporary assays can be disconnected from clinical outcomes [20–23]. High sample positivity encountered in two recently approved biosimilar programs, Bmab 1000 (denosumab biosimilar) and Bmab 1200 (ustekinumab biosimilar), created an interpretational challenge for comparative immunogenicity assessment across Phase 1 [24,25] and Phase 3 studies [26,27]. Although the conventional tiered testing strategy met the regulatory need for binary ADA-positive/ADA-negative classification and product-labeling disclosure, many ADA-positive samples were clustered close to screening and confirmatory decision thresholds, requiring extensive downstream testing and complementary titer analyses to distinguish likely treatment-emergent (TE) or treatment-boosted (TB) responses from low-magnitude, noise-adjacent reactivity.

The objective of this article is to examine the interpretational limitations of the current FDA-aligned tiered ADA testing paradigm when applied to highly sensitive, drug-tolerant biosimilar immunogenicity assays and to argue that immunogenicity testing in a biosimilar program is a confirmatory exercise against an already-approved reference product rather than an investigative characterization of a novel biologic [21,28,29]. Clinical ADA datasets from Bmab 1000 and Bmab 1200 were retrospectively re-processed using selected alternative strategies – reduced reliance on the confirmatory tier, variability-anchored screening thresholds, magnitude-aware versus percent inhibition visualization, subject-level preexisting (PE)/TB/TE classification, longitudinal change from baseline evaluation, and integration of biomarker-like evidence from PK, safety/efficacy, and orthogonal in vitro immunogenicity assays. Using real regulatory clinical datasets, this retrospective exercise evaluated the practical advantages, limitations, and implementation considerations of these approaches for distinguishing noise-adjacent ADA signals from biologically and clinically interpretable responses – while preserving biosimilar–reference comparability conclusions and aligning with the fit-for-purpose principle warranted for streamlined biosimilar development.

Methods

ADA results from Phase 1 and Phase 3 studies of approved biosimilars – Bmab 1000 (Evfraxy; denosumab biosimilar) and Bmab 1200 (Yesintek; ustekinumab biosimilar) developed by Biocon Biologics Limited – and their respective reference products (Prolia and Stelara) were reviewed. The clinical datasets were originally analyzed for regulatory submissions using the predefined, tiered cut point framework [28,30]; these are referenced here as the conventional approach and were not recalculated. The same datasets were retrospectively reanalyzed using an alternate approach (variability-anchored cut point) [31,32], together with magnitude-aware log(S/N) versus percent inhibition visualization, subject-level classification into PE, TB, and TE categories, and longitudinal change from baseline assessment to evaluate how cut point definition influences apparent ADA positivity and its biological interpretation.

Clinical studies and immunogenicity sampling schedule

Immunogenicity datasets were derived from previously conducted comparative Phase 1 PK studies in healthy volunteers and Phase 3 comparative efficacy studies in the intended patient populations. For Bmab 1200, the Phase 1 STELLAR-1 study (ISRCTN11424009) compared Bmab 1200 with US-licensed and EU-approved ustekinumab in healthy subjects [25], and the Phase 3 STELLAR-2 study (NCT05335356) compared Bmab 1200 with reference ustekinumab (US and EU) in patients with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis, including a single-switch period [27]. For Bmab 1000, the Phase 1 study compared Bmab 1000 with reference denosumab (US) in healthy adult volunteers [24], and the corresponding Phase 3 DEVOTE study compared Bmab 1000 with reference denosumab in post-menopausal women with osteoporosis, incorporating a single-switch design (NCT05345691) [26]. Full trial designs, dosing schedules, sampling schedules, and endpoints are described in the primary publications. Across both programs, ADA was measured in serum samples collected at predefined time points, and subjects were classified as ADA-positive if at least one post-dose sample tested positive per predefined assay criteria [24–27].

ADA assay methods (platforms, formats, and performance)

For Bmab 1000 and its US-licensed reference product, ADA testing was performed using a bridging electrochemiluminescence (ECL) immunoassay on the Meso Scale Discovery platform. For Bmab 1200 and its US-licensed and EU-approved reference products, ADA testing was conducted using an acid capture elution (ACE) format combined with ECL detection to improve detection in the presence of circulating drug, with clinical serum samples subjected to acid dissociation followed by affinity capture and elution prior to ECL detection. Across both programs, ADA assays were developed and validated in accordance with current regulatory guidance for immunogenicity testing [28], including evaluation of precision, selectivity, and drug tolerance. Clinical ADA testing followed a tiered strategy using predefined acceptance criteria. Full validation parameters, cut point determination, control performance, drug- and target-tolerance characteristics, and acceptance criteria are provided in the Supplementary Appendix (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

ADA data analysis – conventional approach

Clinical ADA testing followed the conventional industry-standard tiered strategy comprising screening, confirmatory, and, where applicable, titer assessments [28,30,33,34]. Screening cut points (SCPs) and confirmatory cut points (CCPs) were established during pre-study validation using baseline serum samples, after exclusion of preexisting reactivity/outliers as recommended [28,30,33,34], to control nominal false-positive rates (FPRs) of approximately 5% and 1%, respectively. Samples exceeding the SCP were subjected to confirmatory testing, and confirmed positives proceeded to titer determination using a titer cut point (TCP) based on a more stringent 0.1% FPR criterion [30]. For PK interpretation, serum concentrations over time were assessed across ADA titer categories defined by interquartile ranges: low (≤Q1), moderate (>Q1 to ≤Q3), and high (>Q3).

ADA data analysis – alternate approach

For the alternate analysis, the minimum screening cut point (mSCP) was recalculated using the framework described by Kubiak et al. [31]. In contrast to the conventional percentile-based SCP, this variability-anchored method avoided additional outlier removal and derived the screening threshold from assay precision. Assuming a log-normal response distribution, the mSCP was calculated as:mSCP=exp−1n1+1n2lnCV2+1Φ−1FPR

where n1 and n2 are the numbers of negative-control and test-sample replicates, respectively, CV is the inter-assay coefficient of variation, FPR is the false-positive rate, and Φ−1 is the inverse standard normal cumulative distribution. For all recalculations, n1 = 4, n2 = 2, FPR = 5%, and the maximum observed validation %CV for each program were used to provide a conservative variability-informed threshold. The recalculated mSCPs were applied to the same clinical ADA datasets and compared with the original conventional SCPs. Confirmatory interpretation continued to use the established conventional CCPs specific to each program (% inhibition); only the screening overlay was shifted to the mSCP-based S/N threshold. All screened samples were visualized using log (S/N) versus percent inhibition contour density plots, with conventional SCPs and mSCPs overlaid, to identify low-magnitude, cut point–adjacent screening responses and compare sample positivity under both approaches. Subject-level ADA classification into PE, TB, and TE categories was assessed under both approaches.

In addition, a subject-level longitudinal analysis was performed for the Bmab 1200 Phase 1 dataset using two readouts: absolute S/N over time and change from Day 1 baseline. Post-baseline S/N values were divided by each subject’s Day 1 value, and the probability of a spontaneous signal increase by a factor f was interpreted using p(f)=Φ[−ln(f)/√(2ln(CV2 +1))], by considering 1%FPR, as described by Kubiak et al. [32]. Responses exceeding the variability-derived threshold were reviewed against day-wise subject profiles to distinguish persistent TE or TB ADA patterns from preexisting, baseline-associated, or noise-adjacent reactivity.

In vitro immunogenicity assessments

Innate immune activation was assessed using the RAW-Blue NF-κB/AP-1 reporter assay (InvivoGen), quantifying secreted embryonic alkaline phosphatase in culture supernatants after incubation with Bmab 1000, Bmab 1200, or their respective reference products under predefined conditions. Lipopolysaccharide was used as the positive control at 100 ng/mL. Adaptive immune activation was assessed using a CFSE-based CD4+ T-cell proliferation assay in HLA-typed peripheral blood mononuclear cells from healthy donors, quantified by flow cytometry and summarized as a stimulation index (SI) relative to unstimulated controls; donor responses were classified as positive at SI ≥ 2.0 in line with published thresholds [35]. For the human CD4+ T-cell proliferation assay, OKT3 and keyhole limpet hemocyanin were used as positive controls at 100 ng/mL and 10 μg/mL, respectively.

Results and discussion

ADA assay performance and benchmarking

Regulatory guidance for immunogenicity bioanalysis has progressively emphasized high analytical sensitivity and adequate tolerance to circulating drug, with commonly cited targets of ≤100 ng/mL sensitivity and demonstrated drug tolerance at clinically relevant drug concentrations [28]. Reflecting this evolution, FDA guidance reduced the recommended ADA assay sensitivity target from 250 ng/mL to 100 ng/mL, encouraging development of more sensitive and drug-tolerant assays [28,34]. Relative to this benchmark, the assays used in the Bmab 1000 and Bmab 1200 programs operated in the sub-ng/mL to low-ng/mL range. The validated bridging ECL assay used in the Bmab 1000 program demonstrated screening and confirmatory sensitivities of 0.912 ng/mL and 0.564 ng/mL, respectively, and the validated ACE-ECL assay used in the Bmab 1200 program demonstrated screening and confirmatory sensitivities of 0.709 ng/mL and 0.839 ng/mL, respectively. Both assays also demonstrated high drug tolerance: the ACE-ECL format detected 100 ng/mL ADA in the presence of up to 240 µg/mL circulating drug across Bmab 1200 and its US- and EU-approved reference products, and the bridging ECL format detected 100 ng/mL ADA in the presence of up to 50 µg/mL circulating drug across Bmab 1000 and its approved reference product. Drug tolerance was evaluated during validation for both assays and is summarized in Tables 1 and 2 of the Supplementary Appendix.

Direct comparison of ADA positivity rates between contemporary biosimilar programs and historical originator programs is limited by sparse methodological detail in many originator-era publications, which often reflected enzyme immunoassay (EIA)/enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay-era methods with greater susceptibility to drug interference [36]. As an illustrative benchmark, an ustekinumab regulatory assessment reported an EIA sensitivity of 125 ng/mL [37], whereas recent high-sensitivity ECL-based ADA assays have reported sensitivities in the single-digit to sub-ng/mL range, including 0.4 ng/mL in an adalimumab platform [38,39]. The more relevant question in a comparative biosimilar trial is therefore not whether absolute ADA incidence matches historical originator labels, but whether the biosimilar and reference product show comparable ADA classification, response magnitude, longitudinal profile, and clinical context when assessed using the same modern assay. Positive control binding properties and reagent choice can materially influence reported assay sensitivity and drug tolerance and remain an active area of method development [40].

ADA data analysis – conventional approach

In line with regulatory guidance [28,36], immunogenicity in both programs was assessed using the conventional tiered strategy: samples above the SCP (5% FPR) were tested for confirmation (CCP; 1% FPR) and confirmed positives were assigned titers using a TCP (0.1% FPR). Subjects were classified as ADA-positive if at least one post-dose sample was confirmed positive, else they were classified as ADA-negative. This binary framework was applied consistently across the Phase 1 and Phase 3 studies of both programs.

To directly evaluate whether these high apparent ADA rates carried any clinical signal, an interquartile analysis of PK exposure across ADA titer groups was performed. For both Bmab 1200 and Bmab 1000 (Supplementary Figure S2A and S2B, respectively), the PK profiles were overlapping across the low, moderate, and high titer groups and across the biosimilar and reference arms, with no titer-dependent reduction in exposure, reinforcing that binary classification captured predominantly noise-adjacent reactivity rather than functionally relevant ADA.

In Phase 3 patient studies, the conventional analysis again produced high sample positivity (Bmab 1200: 59.4%, 69.6%, 49.6%; Bmab 1000: 53%, 48%, 58%; Table 1), with 50–70% and 43.6–57.9% of the positives progressing to titer assessment, respectively. The subject-level ADA incidence was 87.6–93.6% and 83.7–86.7%, respectively, yet Phase 3 comparative efficacy and safety endpoints supported biosimilarity for both programs [26,27]. Contour plots (Figure 2(A), Bmab 1200; Figure 2(B), Bmab 1000) reproduced the Phase 1 clustering pattern near program-specific SCP and CCP boundaries, again below the 100 ng/mL surrogate reference. Despite representing distinct disease settings – plaque psoriasis for Bmab 1200 and post-menopausal osteoporosis for Bmab 1000 – the cross-program consistency indicates that apparent ADA positivity was driven largely by statistical threshold behavior on a continuum of low-magnitude signals, rather than by biologically distinct positive responses. ADA data analysis – alternate approach

The interrogation of the conventional-approach results in Section 3.2 indicated that both Bmab 1200 and Bmab 1000 clinical datasets carried a large fraction of low-magnitude, cut point–adjacent screening signals with no accompanying impact on PK exposure. Kubiak et al. [31] argued that this pattern is a foreseeable consequence of the standard cut point derivation procedure: aggressive outlier removal from the validation dataset can strip biological variability, producing an SCP that approaches the mSCP – the analytical-noise floor derived from assay precision alone. When the resulting SCP is too close to the mSCP, the tiered strategy generates excess false-positive classifications that dilute any true relationship between ADA status and clinical outcome [31]. To test whether this phenomenon contributed to the observations in Section 3.2, two variability-anchored analyses were applied to the Phase 1 datasets and then extended to Phase 3: (i) reclassification of screening positives using a program-specific mSCP and (ii) longitudinal change from baseline evaluation for the higher-positivity Bmab 1200 Phase 1 dataset, as proposed by Kubiak et al. [32].

Phase 1 sample positivity: mSCP-based reclassification

Program-specific mSCPs were calculated using the maximum observed inter-assay %CV from validation, in line with the conservative convention recommended by Kubiak et al. [31] (Methods, Section 2.4; Table 2), yielding mSCPs of 1.3370 S/N (log 0.127) for Bmab 1200 and 1.4240 S/N (log 0.154) for Bmab 1000 – both notably higher than the conventional SCPs (1.24 S/N [log SCP 0.0918] and 1.04 S/N [log SCP 0.017], respectively), consistent with the observation by Kubiak et al. that conventional cut points can settle close to the analytical-noise floor [31]. Applying the mSCPs to the Phase 1 datasets substantially reduced sample positivity in every arm (Table 1 and Supplementary Figure S1A and S1B). For Bmab 1200, screening positivity fell from 65% to 29% (biosimilar), 82% to 51% (ustekinumab-EU), and 78% to 51% (ustekinumab-US). The mSCP-based reclassification was directly visualized on log (S/N) versus % inhibition contour density plots (Figure 1(C), Bmab 1200; Figure 1(D), Bmab 1000), with the conventional SCP and the mSCP overlaid and program-specific CCPs retained. Even before applying the mSCP, the spatial distribution of screening positives differed markedly between the two products. In Bmab 1200, positives were dispersed across a broader range of log(S/N) values, extending from just above the conventional SCP into higher signal territory. In Bmab 1000, positives were densely overlapping and concentrated in a narrow band immediately above the conventional SCP, close to the analytical-noise floor. Introducing the mSCP overlay reclassified almost the entire conventional positive population in Bmab 1000 as negative, whereas for Bmab 1200 a defined population of higher log(S/N) points persisted above the mSCP – providing a visual anchor for a biologically more interpretable subset of responses.

Two considerations are relevant when interpreting the between-program difference. First, the mSCP calculation is anchored to the maximum observed inter-assay %CV per Kubiak et al.’s convention [31]; however, the distribution of %CV across positive-control levels was product dependent (Table 2). For Bmab 1200, the %CV values were relatively similar across the negative control, low positive control, medium positive control, and high positive control (18%, 7.2%, 20.6%, 17.3%); therefore, the choice of anchoring control had limited impact on the derived mSCP. For Bmab 1000, the %CV increased from low to high controls (6.9%, 6.1%, 21.7%, 25.2%), with the maximum falling on HPC; anchoring the mSCP to this value produced the highest achievable threshold and the most aggressive reduction of screening positives. There is no established consensus on the most appropriate %CV to use for mSCP derivation, and Kubiak et al. themselves describe the max-%CV choice as conservative [31]; this remains an open methodological question, particularly for assays in which %CV escalates with positive-control concentration. Second, the pre-mSCP distributions themselves suggest a genuine product-related contribution – the broader, more dispersed positive distribution in Bmab 1200 versus the tight, overlapping, noise-adjacent distribution in Bmab 1000 is unlikely to be entirely explained by a %CV choice alone. Given that both Phase 1 studies were conducted in normal healthy volunteers (NHVs), disease- or indication-related differences are not applicable to the Phase 1 observations; the between-program difference likely reflects product- and assay-intrinsic factors, including differences in target biology (IL-12/23 for ustekinumab, RANKL for denosumab), the corresponding matrix behavior of drug–target–ADA interactions in healthy serum, and the different assay formats (ACE-ECL for Bmab 1200 versus bridging ECL for Bmab 1000), each with a different susceptibility to low-level matrix-related reactivity. NHV cohorts frequently include volunteers who have previously participated in unrelated therapeutic monoclonal antibody trials; even with the specified washout period, residual preexisting reactivity to shared monoclonal antibody structural motifs can persist and contribute to elevated apparent baseline positivity [22,41]. Taken together, methodological, product-intrinsic, and cohort-related factors plausibly contribute in combination to the observed between-program difference, which is best interpreted as multifactorial rather than attributable to any single cause.

Phase 1 subject positivity: mSCP-based classification and longitudinal analysis

The substantial reduction in sample positivity under the mSCP-based approach, in turn, made downstream subject-level interrogation more tractable, allowing ADA classification to be assessed on a manageable number of screening positives rather than a dataset dominated by cut point-adjacent noise. Subjects were categorized using the harmonized ADA terminology [34] into four mutually exclusive groups – negative, PE, TB, TE – under both the conventional and the mSCP-based classifications (Table 3). Under the conventional approach, every subject in every arm across both programs was classified as ADA-positive at some post-dose time point, with no true negatives identified: ustekinumab arms 76–81% TE (with 19–24% PE/TB) and denosumab arms 90–94% TE (with 6–10% PE/TB). This near-universal positivity, disconnected from the comparable PK, safety, and efficacy outcomes, is characteristic of the pattern predicted by Kubiak et al. [31] when the operational SCP sits at the analytical-noise floor. Under the mSCP-based classification, subject-level ADA distributions collapsed onto a smaller and biologically more coherent set of responders, consistent with recent examples in which magnitude-informed classification concentrates positivity on the clinically more interpretable subset (e.g., the integrated analysis of dostarlimab immunogenicity [42]). For Bmab 1000, 80% (biosimilar) and 87% (denosumab-US) of subjects were reclassified as negative, with TE responses reduced to 18% and 11%, respectively, and no residual PE or TB subjects. For Bmab 1200, negative subjects were identified in every arm (12–35%), PE reduced from 19–24% to 0–14%, and TE responses reduced to 52% (biosimilar), 77% (ustekinumab-EU), and 75% (ustekinumab-US). The pattern within each program was directionally similar across biosimilar and reference arms, preserving the comparative biosimilarity conclusion under the alternate framework. The Bmab 1200 Phase 1 dataset, which retained the higher residual positivity under the mSCP-based analysis, was further evaluated using the longitudinal change from baseline concept described by Kubiak et al. [32]. Rather than classifying a sample as positive or negative relative to a population-derived percentile threshold – an approach whose real-world limitations at ultra-low cut points have been recently reviewed [43] – this method evaluates whether an individual subject shows a post-baseline signal change that exceeds what could reasonably be attributed to assay variability from that subject’s own Day 1 baseline. Baseline correction was performed at a 1% FPR and post-baseline S/N values were expressed as fold-change from Day 1. Figure 4(A) displays absolute S/N over time for each Bmab 1200 subject and Figure 4(B) expresses the same responses as fold-change from Day 1 baseline; both are overlaid with the variability-derived threshold used to interpret longitudinal signal change. The longitudinal visualization separated three qualitatively different response patterns not distinguishable within the binary tiered framework: a subset with clear post-baseline rise persisting across multiple time points and remaining above the variability-derived threshold (consistent with TE, biologically interpretable ADA), a second subset with transient excursions returning to baseline at subsequent visits (more consistent with sporadic assay variability), and a third subset with no meaningful post-baseline signal change (remaining within the variability envelope throughout follow-up). This longitudinal, subject-anchored view provides a biologically grounded means of distinguishing sustained TE or TB responses from noise-adjacent or baseline-associated reactivity [32,43], complementing the mSCP-based reclassification without relying on a population-derived threshold applied uniformly across subjects. The alternate framework departs from the conventional reliance on titer as the semi-quantitative measure of ADA magnitude. Titer stratification in a highly sensitive assay setting is confounded by the same cut point behavior that drives sample-positivity inflation itself, as illustrated by the absence of a titer-dependent effect on PK exposure across biosimilar and reference arms in these programs (Section 3.2). In the alternate framework, magnitude is instead captured continuously through log(S/N), used both as the primary axis of the contour density plots and as the input to the longitudinal fold-change from baseline analysis. This provides a subject-anchored, quantitative readout of response magnitude that is directly interpretable in a comparability context [32,43] and, when combined with structured PE/TB/TE classification, functions as a biomarker-informed comparative immunogenicity readout without invoking the categorical brackets inherent in a titer-based approach. In the datasets analyzed here, titers were correspondingly low and the interquartile PK analysis (Section 3.2) demonstrated no titer-dependent effect on drug exposure, indicating that titer stratification did not carry an independent interpretive signal beyond the continuous magnitude and persistence readouts. This supports consideration of titer determination as a conditional analytical step in a biosimilar setting – to be triggered only when the primary comparative log(S/N) analysis identifies a differential signal between biosimilar and reference arms. Neutralizing antibody testing, analogously, can be positioned as a targeted mechanistic follow-up applied to the same flagged subset, addressing whether an observed differential has functional consequence. Such a conditional architecture requires pre-specification of the differential-signal trigger criterion in the clinical bioanalytical plan, ensuring the strategy is transparent and reproducible rather than post hoc.

The ability to isolate persistent ADA responses is particularly relevant to biosimilar programs, in which the immunogenicity readout is used to confirm the absence of a differential signal against an already-approved reference rather than to characterize a novel immunogenicity profile [21,29]. As biosimilar development frameworks increasingly move toward reduced reliance on comparative Phase 3 studies [8–11,44,45], the Phase 1 longitudinal readout emerges as the primary lens through which comparative immunogenicity between biosimilar and reference product would be meaningfully interpreted. For biosimilar products wherein a Phase 1 NHV study is not feasible due to the mechanism of action of the molecule that may potentially impact safety of subjects, a Phase 1 patient study triggers additional considerations. The persistent-response framework generalizes to a Phase 1 clinical pharmacology study in the intended patient population and is arguably of greater interpretive value in that setting. Patient cohorts typically carry higher baseline immunoreactivity than NHVs – driven by disease-related matrix effects, prior biologic exposure, and, in autoimmune indications, elevated background immune reactivity [22,41] that further amplifies noise-adjacent screening positives under the conventional tiered framework. Longitudinal, subject-anchored interpretation of sustained above-threshold signals, combined with structured PE/TB/TE classification, is therefore an even sharper discriminator of biologically interpretable ADA in patient Phase 3 datasets than in the conventional binary approach. Practical adaptations are limited: the change from baseline probability factor should be interpreted alongside a patient-population baseline distribution and PE subjects should be handled as a distinct interpretive category with their potential for TB persistence explicitly assessed. Crucially, extracting greater interpretive value from a well-designed Phase 1 patient study – rather than expanding its scope or duration – is the direction that best serves the streamlining objective of biosimilar development [8–11,44,45]. In this framing, Phase 1 clinical pharmacology in patients, combined with a persistent-response interpretive framework, is positioned as a scientifically defensible primary comparability readout for immunogenicity in future biosimilar submissions, without the elaboration inherent in investigative workflows designed for novel biologics.

Phase 3: application to patient studies

Extension of the mSCP-based analysis to the Phase 3 datasets reproduced the Phase 1 patterns in the intended patient populations. Sample positivity was substantially reduced across every arm (Table 1 and Supplementary Figure S1C and S1D). For Bmab 1200, screening positivity fell from 59.4% to 31% (biosimilar), 69.6% to 54% (ustekinumab-EU), and 49.6% to 45% (Bmab 1200–ustekinumab-EU switch arm). For Bmab 1000, screening positivity fell from 53% to 13% (biosimilar continued), 48% to 7% (denosumab continued), and 58% to 14% (denosumab-to-Bmab 1000 switch arm). The mSCP overlays on the log (S/N) versus % inhibition contour density plots for Phase 3 (Figure 2(C), Bmab 1200; Figure 2(D), Bmab 1000) again cleanly separated the noise-adjacent population from a smaller retained subset of higher log(S/N) responses. Consistent with Phase 1, the residual mSCP-defined positivity remained higher for Bmab 1200 than for Bmab 1000; in the patient populations, this pattern is compatible not only with the product- and assay-intrinsic factors discussed for Phase 1 but also with disease- and indication-related contributors [22] – the chronic autoimmune inflammatory setting for Bmab 1200 (moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis) versus the bone metabolic setting for Bmab 1000 (post-menopausal osteoporosis) – where preexisting immune reactivity and target-mediated matrix effects are recognized as more prominent in autoimmune indications.

Importantly, the switch arms provided a direct read on long-term and cross-product immunogenicity. In both programs, the switch arm did not display higher retained ADA positivity than the corresponding continued biosimilar or reference arm under either the conventional or the mSCP-based analysis; the Bmab 1200–ustekinumab-EU switch arm showed the lowest retained mSCP-based positivity of the three Phase 3 arms (45%), and the denosumab-to-Bmab 1000 switch arm (14%) was directly comparable to the continued biosimilar and continued reference arms (13% and 7%, respectively). Together with the consistent alignment of PK, safety, and efficacy outcomes across arms in the Phase 3 programs [24,26], these observations indicate no incremental long-term immunogenicity risk associated with a single switch between reference and biosimilar. The same persistent-response interpretive lens applied to the switch arms demonstrates that the mSCP + longitudinal framework generalizes naturally to the cross-product exposure scenario [7].

For contextual benchmarking, originator labeling and regulatory review documents report substantially lower subject-level ADA positivity where available (denosumab < 1%; ustekinumab < 10%) [46,47]. Despite similar high sample and subject-level positivity across biosimilar and reference arms in the present datasets under a supersensitive testing paradigm [20,39], the modern platforms and cut point strategies can yield markedly higher apparent incidence than historical originator programs, without necessarily implying new or differential clinical risk. Notably, the mSCP-based reclassification narrows this gap: retained positivity under the alternate approach is closer to the historical originator range, particularly for Bmab 1000, consistent with the interpretation that a substantial portion of the apparent gap reflects analytical detectability rather than a change in the underlying immunogenicity of these products.

In the context of the streamlining trajectory of biosimilar development, these Phase 3 datasets are best understood as a confirmatory anchor validating that the mSCP-based and persistent-response framework applied at Phase 1 preserves the biosimilar–reference comparability conclusion when Phase 3 data are subsequently available. In future biosimilar programs pursuing streamlined pathways without a comparative Phase 3 study [8–11,44,45], the Phase 1 interpretive framework demonstrated here would carry the immunogenicity comparability signal; the Phase 3 results presented in this section provide empirical support that this approach does not compromise the eventual comparability outcome. This positions the analytical strategy proposed here not as a replacement for Phase 3 immunogenicity data where such data exist but as a scientifically defensible framework for arriving at the same biosimilar–reference comparability conclusion in programs where Phase 3 comparative studies are no longer required.

Comparative in vitro immunogenicity assessment

The clustering of ADA signals near SCP/CCP in the conventional analysis (Section 3.2), together with the biologically more coherent PE/TB/TE distributions produced under the alternate framework (Section 3.3) and their consistent absence of impact on PK exposure, motivated orthogonal, mechanism-relevant testing to determine whether the products themselves elicited any measurable innate or adaptive immune activation (Supplementary Figure S3) [35]. In vitro immunogenicity (IVIC) assays are recognized as supportive tools in this context and, when applied comparatively to biosimilar and reference materials, can interrogate upstream immune pathways plausibly linked to ADA formation without being clinically validated surrogates for incidence [35,48]. Recent regulatory and scientific consensus further supports that comparative analytical assessment combined with a single-dose PK/immunogenicity study, together with selected IVIC evidence, can provide a robust package for concluding comparable immunogenicity of biosimilars [8–11,44,45].

Bmab 1200, Bmab 1000, and their respective reference products were evaluated in two complementary IVIC assays (Figure 5(A,B)): (i) the RAW-Blue NF-κB reporter assay to interrogate innate immune signaling and (ii) a human CD4+ T-cell proliferation assay in HLA-typed donor PBMCs to probe adaptive T-cell activation. For the CD4+ assay, donor positivity was defined by SI ≥ 2.0 (Methods). Positive controls confirmed expected assay responsiveness and dynamic range in every run. Across both assay platforms, responses for Bmab 1200, Bmab 1000, and their respective reference products remained at the background and were well separated from positive control signals, with no appreciable difference between biosimilar and reference (Figure 5(A,B)). In the comparative biosimilar context, the absence of differential innate or adaptive activation in vitro – together with comparable product quality attributes, consistent clinical PK, safety, and efficacy, and the predominance of cut point–adjacent low-magnitude clinical ADA signals reclassified as noise-consistent by the alternate framework – supports the interpretation that the apparent ADA incidence is largely assay- and threshold-driven rather than indicative of product-specific immune activation. Positioned this way, IVIC assays function as targeted orthogonal support for the comparability conclusion reached from the primary ADA analysis, consistent with their role as situational rather than routine components of a fit-for-purpose biosimilar immunogenicity strategy.

Conclusions

In highly sensitive ADA assays with sub- to low-ng/mL sensitivity and high drug tolerance, the interpretive question in a biosimilar comparative trial shifts from how many samples test positive to what does positivity mean in a comparability and clinical-relevance context. As shown across the two Phase 1 and Phase 3 programs analyzed here, apparent sample- and subject-level ADA incidence in this regime is dominated by cut-point behavior rather than by underlying differential immunogenicity. The biologically informative signal is not the binary tiered outcome, but the combination of response magnitude, longitudinal persistence, subject-level PE/TB/TE classification, and time-matched PK, safety, and efficacy readouts with orthogonal IVIC evidence positioned as situational, not routine, support.

The authors’ position, informed by this analysis, is that biosimilar immunogenicity assessment is fundamentally a confirmatory exercise designed to establish the absence of a differential immunogenicity signal against an already-approved reference product, not an investigative characterization of a novel biologic. Direct extrapolation of investigative ADA workflows (high-sensitivity assays coupled with aggressive percentile-based cut points, routine confirmatory and titer testing, and maximum serum concentration-level drug tolerance targets) into biosimilar programs generates analytical burden disproportionate to residual uncertainty, without commensurate interpretive gain. Recent cross-industry recommendations on right-sizing drug tolerance and the broader regulatory direction toward streamlined and convergent biosimilar development are consistent with a lighter, comparability-anchored analytical strategy. As regulatory frameworks increasingly move toward reduced reliance on comparative Phase 3 studies, Phase 1 clinical pharmacology combined with longitudinal persistent-response interpretation and structured PE/TB/TE classification becomes the primary comparability instrument for biosimilar immunogenicity – not an add-on to conventional tiered testing but a scientifically defensible substitute for it.

Several questions warrant collective resolution by the industry and regulators. First, should a comparability-anchored ADA analytical strategy for biosimilars be codified at guideline level – e.g., mSCP-based screening with magnitude-aware visualization as a default – or should analysis remain case-by-case with a defined menu of biosimilar-appropriate approaches? Second, is confirmatory testing incrementally informative when the screening signal distribution is continuous rather than bimodal, as observed here? Third, how should the longitudinal change from baseline framework be operationalized for regulatory acceptability, given that no consensus probability threshold has been established? Fourth, and central to the authors’ position, given that log(S/N) and change from baseline continuously capture the magnitude information that titer categorically summarizes, and given the demonstrated absence of a titer-dependent PK effect in these case studies, titer determination, and by extension neutralizing antibody testing, should be repositioned as conditional analytical steps in a biosimilar setting, triggered by pre-specified differential-signal criteria in the primary comparative analysis rather than as routine tiers applied to every clinical study. Each alternate approach has its own boundaries: the mSCP framework depends on the %CV anchor choice and assumes log-normal response distribution, longitudinal change from baseline analysis does not readily translate to binary label-required outcomes and retains an analyst-dependent component, contour visualization is descriptive rather than prescriptive, and IVIC assays remain directional tools without validated clinical surrogacy. No single approach replaces the tiered framework – their value lies in coherent, comparability-anchored integration rather than in isolated application.

Finally, both alternate approaches were applied here retrospectively to two already-approved biosimilar programs and preserved the biosimilar–reference comparability conclusion reached from the conventional tiered analysis. The Phase 3 datasets in these programs function as a confirmatory anchor demonstrating that the mSCP + persistent-response framework applied at Phase 1 would arrive at the same comparability outcome in submissions where Phase 3 data are no longer required. The unresolved question is whether this framework would be regulatorily acceptable prospectively as the primary immunogenicity readout, either as a standardized default or as a pre-specified case-by-case package. Our experience across biosimilar submissions in over 100 markets – where regulatory rigor and advocacy for streamlining vary widely, but where the affordability and access imperative is shared – reinforces that a comparability-anchored analytical strategy could deliver reduced downstream burden, shorter development cycles, and lower cost without loss of comparability confidence. Advancing this dialogue between developers, bioanalytical scientists, and regulators is, in our view, the most productive next step for the interpretation of highly sensitive immunogenicity data in biosimilar programs.
