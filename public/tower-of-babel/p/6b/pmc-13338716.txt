QutRNA2: robust tRNA modification discovery from Nanopore direct tRNA sequencing
Authors: Michael Piechotta, Wei Guo, Isabel S Naarmann-de Vries, Felix Kallenborn, Swastik Mishra, Francesca Tuorto, Bertil Schmidt, Christoph Dieterich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransfer RNAs (tRNAs) are essential for protein synthesis and are extensively modified to ensure their structure and function. Direct RNA sequencing with Oxford Nanopore Technologies enables positional modification analysis but is challenged by tRNAs’ short length, redundancy, and dense modifications. We present QutRNA2, a scalable workflow that includes GPU-accelerated local alignment, statistical filtering, pairwise error profile comparison, and customizable visualization. Achieving up to 25-fold speed gains over CPU methods, QutRNA2 identifies enzyme-dependent modifications in nuclear- and mitochondrial-encoded tRNAs, demonstrated in high-volume human and mouse samples. This open-source solution provides a comprehensive, multiplexing-compatible framework for tRNA analysis, addressing a key gap in current tools. QutRNA2 is released under Apache-2.0 license and is available at https://github.com/dieterich-lab/QutRNA2.

Introduction

Transfer RNAs (tRNAs) play a central role in protein translation by decoding messenger RNA codons and providing amino acids to the ribosomes. Chemical modifications heavily influence tRNA folding, stability, and codon–anticodon pairing [1]. More than 170 modifications have been identified, and this number is increasing as the development of new detection methods continues [2]. Each tRNA is typically modified at 5–15 positions (average 8) [3]. Most modifications are evolutionarily conserved [4], with increasing complexity in terms of the number of modifications and the chemical structure of the modifications from simpler organisms like Escherichia coli to more complex eukaryotes such as humans [2]. In particular, modifications at the wobble position (position 34) are key determinants of translational accuracy and overall proteostasis [5].

Recently, tRNAs have been sequenced using Oxford Nanopore Technologies direct RNA sequencing chemistry [6–8]. Here, modified residues alter current signal traces and lead to out-of-domain signals, which in turn result in basecalling errors that manifest as substitutions, insertions, or deletions of canonical RNA bases. The comparison of these error profiles has been used to identify RNA modifications across multiple RNA types in the past [9]. The unique properties of tRNAs, such as their short length, the presence of several identical copies in the genome, and the high frequency of modifications, pose a challenge to the mapping algorithm employed. Traditional approaches, such as seed-extend methods, often fail to achieve satisfactory accuracy.

We have recently presented an improved alignment protocol and modification detection workflow (coined QutRNA) that successfully identified tRNA queuosine modifications in tRNAs of Schizosaccharomyces pombe and E. coli [10].

Here, we present an improved and extended workflow (QutRNA2: https://github.com/dieterich-lab/QutRNA2), which masters a much higher transcriptome complexity, sequencing throughput, and the new RNA004 chemistry. The extensions to QutRNA are summarized in Fig. 1A. Briefly, QutRNA2 involves four principal components: (i) an optimal local GPU-accelerated ultra-fast read alignment step (Fig. 1B); (ii) rigorous statistical filtering of spurious alignments (Fig. 1C); (iii) postselection of aligned reads by formal requirements (e.g. adapter overlap, Fig. 1D); and (iv) a new score normalization approach by subsampling (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. S1). The reported scores are robust to technical fluctuations and serve to highlight candidate modification positions in the Sprinzl coordinate space [11] (see Fig. 1F). As a final step, our workflow produces diagnostic heatmap plots to pinpoint modification level changes on tRNAs (Fig. 1G).

JACUSA2 [12] remains at the heart of QutRNA2 for modification detection by pairwise comparisons. JACUSA2 detects RNA modifications by calculating and comparing error profiles derived from mismatches, insertions, and deletions in paired samples from control, knockdown, knockout, or in vitro transcribed (IVT) conditions for a wide range of modification types [13].

We demonstrate the performance of QutRNA2 on a comparison of queuosine-positive wild-type (Q+) versus negative QTRT1 knockout (Q−) HCT116 cells across all detected nuclear and mitochondrial tRNAs. Moreover, we extend this analysis to experiments in Mus musculus, where we study the impact of the absence of Qtrt1 protein (Q−) on tRNA modifications in murine embryonic stem cells (mESCs) and hippocampus (mHC). Finally, we demonstrate that QutRNA2 generalizes to other modifications such as 5-methoxycarbonylmethyl (mcm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{5}$\end{document}), pseudouridine (Y), and N\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{6}$\end{document}-isopentenyladenosine (i\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{6}$\end{document}A).

Materials and methods

Sample and library preparation

All details on the sample and Nanopore library preparation can be found in the Supplementary Methods. All newly sequenced libraries are listed in Supplementary Table S1. All reused S. pombe libraries are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

tRNA mapping and alignment

Raw reads from tRNA sequencing libraries were aligned using the GPU-accelerated tool gpu-tRNA-mapper. Optimal alignment coordinates were first determined on the GPU, after which alignments were extended using parasail v2.6.2 [14]. Results were written in SAM format [15]. The mapper was executed with –scoring 1,-1,-1,-1 –batchsize 100000. Computations were performed on a Slurm cluster using an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 GPU.

tRNA reference sequences

Mature Homo sapiens (GRCh38/hg38) tRNA sequences were downloaded from GtRNAdb [16]. Identical sequences were collapsed to a single representative. To support splinted ligation products, adapter sequences were appended:

>5'-splint adapter

CCTAAGAGCAAGAAGAAGCCTGGN

>3'-splint adapter

GGCTTCTTCTTGCTCTTAGGAAAAAAAAAA

Mus musculus (GRCm38/mm10) reference sequences were processed identically.

GPU-accelerated mapping

Alignment kernels were implemented in CUDA following concepts of CUDASW++4.0 [17]. Sequence pairs were processed by up to 32 threads using warp intrinsics. In contrast to protein-focused implementations, gpu-tRNA-mapper supports the nucleotide alphabet {A,C,G,T/U,N} and performs local and semiglobal alignment with affine gap penalties. Kernels compute optimal alignment scores and corresponding start and end coordinates using 32-bit precision.

The workflow comprises (i) GPU computation of local alignment scores; (ii) selection of best-matching references; (iii) recomputation to obtain alignment boundaries; (iv) CPU traceback using parasail; and (v) SAM output generation.

The software supports NVIDIA GPUs with compute capability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\ge}$\end{document}7.0 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\ge$\end{document}4 GB VRAM and has been validated on Volta, Turing, Ampere, Ada, Hopper, and Blackwell architectures.

Running time analysis

Runtime performance was evaluated using downsampled RNA004 sequencing data from the HCT116 cell line (wild type and QTRT1; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $10^6$\end{document} reads each). Identical scoring parameters were used for parasail and gpu-tRNA-mapper. Parasail runtime was unaffected by batch size or thread count. Because parasail does not report the highest-scoring alignment per read, a custom Python script was used to select optimal matches. GPU runtimes include PCIe data transfer overhead.

Post-alignment filtering

Optional filters were implemented to remove spurious alignments.

Random alignment filter

A random alignment model was generated by mapping reads to reversed reference sequences. Based on this null distribution and a user-defined precision threshold, an alignment score cutoff was computed and applied. Unless stated otherwise, precision was set to 99.9%. Score distributions and selected cutoffs are reported in the workflow summary.

Adapter overlap filter

Reads were retained only if they exhibited a user-defined overlap with both the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $5^{\prime }$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $3^{\prime }$\end{document} splint adapters. In this study, a minimum overlap of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $20\%$\end{document} was required for each adapter.

Removal of multimappers

Reads aligning to multiple reference sequences with identical maximal alignment scores were discarded.

JACUSA2 scoring

An extended version of JACUSA2 v2.1.16 [12] was used to identify positions with differential basecalling profiles between conditions using the mismatch (M) score. The modified software was run with the following parameters:

-Q 20 -f X:reads:nonref_ratio:insertion_ ratio:deletion_ratio \

-u DirMult:subsampleRuns=100 -m 1 -q 1 -D -i \

-P1 FR-SECONDSTRAND -P2 FR-SECONDSTRAND

Subsampling of scores

To mitigate coverage imbalances between libraries, JACUSA2 statistics were stabilized using subsampling (Supplementary Fig. S1). For each site, reads from the higher-coverage condition were subsampled to match the coverage of the other condition, and subsampled M scores were computed (100 iterations). Raw M scores were then normalized by subtracting the maximum subsampled score, thereby improving signal-to-noise characteristics.

Visualization of tRNA modifications

Calculated JACUSA2 scores were visualized as heatmaps, with rows representing tRNAs and columns corresponding to the exact Sprinzl coordinates. Where necessary, plots of subsampled M scores were restricted to show specific tRNAs or mark positions of special interest. All plots were generated with the parameters –remove_prefix=tRNA-, –hide_varm. The number of minimal reads per replicate was set to –min_reads=30.

Secondary structure alignment

Covariance models for eukaryotic tRNA (TRNAinf-euk.cm) were obtained from https://github.com/UCSC-LoweLab/tRAX/blob/master/ [18]. tRNAs of each species were split into nuclear and mitochondrial tRNAs, and the matching covariance model along with Infernal 1.1.5 [19] was used to calculate secondary structure predictions for nuclear tRNAs with the following parameters: –notrunc– nonbanded -g. The sequence to Sprinzl coordinate mapping was obtained from Suzuki [1]. The naming scheme from Sprinzl et al. [11] labeled the consensus secondary structure.

Results

QutRNA2 features and impact

We have expanded, added, and enhanced several features of the QutRNA workflow to meet new challenges in data volume and complexity as outlined below (see the “Introduction” section and Fig. 1A).

Rapid GPU-accelerated optimal local alignment

In our previous work [10], we showed that optimal local read alignments are superior to heuristic approaches, such as the classical seed-and-extend read mapping approach. The latter often misassigns reads to false database records. However, data processing time becomes prohibitively high if millions of reads need to be analyzed. Figure 1B compares the running time for mapping 1 million reads against a human reference tRNome using our previous approach and our newly implemented GPU-accelerated read mapper (see “Materials and methods” section). We achieve a median 25-fold speedup.

Automatic alignment score threshold determination

We estimate the score cutoff for a given specificity level by comparing the distribution of true versus random alignment scores (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S2A). If we set the alignment precision to 99.9%, we retain best alignments for between 67.6% and 77.8% of all reads in the input (Fig. 2A).

Post-alignment filtering steps

We enable some flexibility in downstream processing by offering a set of user-configurable filters (see Fig. 1D): ignore specific tRNAs from the reference, require a minimal adapter overlap on both ends, and remove multi-mapping reads. Figure 2B summarizes the effect of this series of filtering steps. For example, in the wild-type RNA004 sample, we start with a median read pool size of 77.8% of library input. Approximately 17% of mt-tRNA reads are removed with the first filter. We required an overlap of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $20\%$\end{document} for both adapters, which removes another 17% of reads. If we omit reads mapping to multiple reference sequences with the same best score, we decrease the pool size to 29.3% of the original pool size. Similar effects can be observed for the tRNA RNA002 library (see Supplementary Fig. S3).

Enhancing robustness through JACUSA2 score subsampling

We use JACUSA2 [12] to calculate differences in mismatches (M score) between two conditions. However, given the higher data variability, high M scores might already emerge from minor differences when read coverages vary substantially across conditions. To account for this observation, we introduced and implemented an efficient subsampling strategy (see Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. S1). Briefly, the M score is normalized by subsampling base calls from the condition with the highest coverage according to the coverage in the other condition (see Supplementary Fig. S1 for details). This normalization is performed at the site level, reducing overall scores as shown in Fig. 2C.

Analysis of queuosine tRNA modifications in the HCT116 cell line

We created a gene knockout of QTRT1 in HCT116 cells (Q−condition) (see Supplementary Material and Supplementary Fig. S14). Both wild-type (Q+) and knockout cell line (Q−) were sequenced with Nanopore sequencing chemistries RNA002 and RNA004, which is the latest iteration of direct RNA sequencing (see Supplementary Figs S2–S8 for results).

The QTRT1 gene encodes the catalytic subunit of queuine tRNA-ribosyltransferase (TGT), an enzyme complex responsible for the modification of tRNAs. TGT catalyzes queuosine modification of position 34 (wobble position) in cytoplasmic and mitochondrial tRNAs with GUN anticodon, namely, isoacceptors that carry one of the following amino acids: asparagine, aspartic acid, histidine, and tyrosine [22]. Depletion of queuine results in deregulated translation and consequently upregulation of the unfolded protein response and endoplasmic reticulum stress [23].

Analysis of nuclear tRNAs

We sequenced RNA from wild-type and QTRT1 knockout cell lines with RNA004 chemistry. Results for RNA002 may be found in the Supplementary Figs S3 and S6–S8. Pairwise QutRNA2 analyses were executed on wild-type versus QTRT1 samples. False alignments were removed with a specificity cutoff of 99.9% in a first step after mapping, keeping the best alignments as shown in Fig. 2A. Sample-specific alignment score distributions and thresholds are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2A.

We retained the highest percentage of reads with a median of 77.8% in the wild-type sample, followed by QTRT1 with 73.4%. For RNA002 chemistry, wild-type and QTRT1 samples are on par with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\approx 68$\end{document}% each. When comparing the chemistries, RNA004 shows a higher percentage of retained reads for wild type and QTRT1 than for the chemistry RNA002. Subsequently, we filtered all best alignments post hoc. Figure 2B shows the impact on read counts for this stepwise procedure. First, we remove mt-tRNAs with the “Ignore tRNAs” filter to focus on nuclear encoded tRNAs. Approximately 16% of sequenced reads in wild type and QTRT1 are mt-tRNAs and are presented elsewhere downstream. The second filter, “Adapter overlap,” retains reads that have at least 20% overlap with the 5′- and 3′-adapter. This ensures that all remaining reads cover the entire tRNA locus full length and removes 17% and 16% of reads for wild type and QTRT1, respectively.

The last filter step removes reads that map to multiple tRNAs with the same best score. Taken together, 29% of the reads remain for the wild-type samples and 27% of the reads for QTRT1 samples. Candidate positions for tRNA modification changes are then identified by calculating subsampled M scores (see Fig. 2D) for wild type versus QTRT1.

As a last step, we translate the reference coordinates from our database to widely accepted Sprinzl coordinates using a eukaryotic-specific covariance model. Normalized JACUSA2 M scores for each comparison are presented as heatmaps in Sprinzl coordinates. Known target positions are highlighted by red boxes. Figure 2D depicts the scores calculated for expected substrates of QTRT1, which are tRNAs with GU(N) anticodons, such as tRNA-Asp, -Asn, -His, and -Tyr [23]. The strongest signal was observed for tRNA-Asp-GTC around Sprinzl positions 30–35, which overlaps with the expected position 34.

Analysis of mt-tRNAs

We employed an mt-tRNA-specific workflow similar to the one presented in the previous section. This time, we omitted nuclear-encoded tRNAs and retained mt-tRNAs instead. The adapter overlap filter leads to a loss of 9%–10% of sequenced reads across all conditions. This accounts for almost 50% of the mt-tRNAs (see Supplementary Fig. S4B). This loss was lower with the RNA002 chemistry, where only 19% of mt-tRNAs are removed upon adapter overlap (see Supplementary Fig. S6B). We calculated normalized M scores for all mt-tRNAs with JACUSA2 and displayed them in heatmaps. Figure 2E shows the scores for the wild-type versus QTRT1 comparison. We observe two tRNAs with an elevated score at position 34. MT-TH shows the highest score and encodes for aspartate. MT-TY encodes for tyrosine and shows an appreciable yet lower score level at position 34.

When comparing the results to the previous chemistry, similar results are observed for wild-type versus QTRT1, where MT-TH at position 34 has the strongest signal (see Supplementary Fig. S8).

Analysis of nuclear tRNAs in murine ES cell and brain tissue

Finally, we investigated the effects of Qtrt1 knockout in mice, to demonstrate that QutRNA2 can be used to analyze the modification effects across diverse organisms. We use mESC and mHC tissue. At the time of sample preparation, RNA002 was the only available chemistry. We used the nuclear-tRNA workflow presented for the HCT116 cell line and analyzed wild-type versus Qtrt1 knockout in mouse samples mentioned earlier. See Supplementary Fig. S9A for the threshold calculation to filter random alignments. Approximately 72% of sequenced reads could be mapped, passing the random alignment filter when using the mESC (see Supplementary Fig. S9B and C for the impact of filters on read length and read counts). As previously outlined, we have identified a set of known Qtrt1 targets. Supplementary Fig. S10 shows the scores for mESC, and high scores can be observed at the known position 34 for tRNAs carrying asparagine, aspartate, and histidine. Traces of scores at Sprinzl positions 28–35 for tyrosine might indicate tRNA modification. We carried out the same analysis for mHC (see Supplementary Fig. S11A–C for details on filtering) and achieved similar score profiles (see Supplementary Fig. S12). Wild-type versus Qtrt1 in mESC and mHC share the modified tRNAs. Even the tyrosine-carrying tRNAs show slightly elevated scores at positions 30–32, much narrower when compared with mESC.

Discussion

We present QutRNA2, a software suite for direct tRNA sequencing on the Nanopore platform. To our knowledge, its design is unique: it implements rapid GPU-accelerated optimal alignment with modest hardware requirements (e.g. a laptop or desktop equipped with a consumer GPU), provides a statistically principled filter to reduce false alignments, and introduces a robust scoring scheme to identify candidate positions in complex tRNomes. Results are visualized as heatmaps in standard Sprinzl coordinates.

Q modifications in human and mouse samples

We have selected a well-known tRNA modifier, QTRT1, for a proof-of-principle experiment of QutRNA2. The TGT enzyme complex, composed of QTRT1 and QTRT2, specifically recognizes nuclear-encoded tRNAs with GUN anticodons: tRNA-Asp (anticodon GUC), tRNA-Asn (anticodon GUU), tRNA-His (anticodon GUG), and tRNA-Tyr (anticodon GUA). Figure 2D shows all tRNA candidates that fall into this class and meet minimal coverage requirements. We observe a dominant signal for tRNA-Asp (anticodon GUC) but only lower signals for any other tRNA isoacceptor (tRNA-Asn). The observed pattern can originate from different underlying factors: e.g. Zhang et al. [24] showed that modification rates vary substantially between tRNAs in human cell culture settings with tRNA-Asp being the fastest. Indeed, we observe the strongest signal for this tRNA. Importantly, all murine samples show clear signals for Asn, Asp, and Tyr, but not His isoacceptors (see Supplementary Figs S10 and S12).

For mitochondrial tRNAs, suspected targets of QTRT1 include mt-tRNAs for Asp, Asn, His, or Tyr. Intriguingly, we see a clear modification signal for mt-tRNA-His (MT-TH gene) and mt-tRNA-Tyr (MT-TY gene) in Fig. 2E. This would predict that Q34 modifications differ between cytoplasmic and mitochondrial tRNAs.

Other modifications in fission yeast

In the last results part, we showed additional data to prove that other modification types can be detected in pairwise comparisons with QutRNA2. Despite variations in the signal patterns, the strongest signal could always be attributed to the anticipated tRNA positions. This observation supports a more widespread utility of our QutRNA2 workflow.

Comparison with other methods

Comparison with existing methods was limited. At the time of writing, no peer-reviewed open-source software could process our dataset without modification.

Lucas et al. [6] provide the Nano-tRNAseq workflow, which is restricted to yeast. Using the supplied yeast wild-type and pus4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\Delta$\end{document} dataset prepared using the RNA002 kit, QutRNA2 achieved a higher AUC (0.913 versus 0.902; Supplementary Fig. S13).

A more recent version of this workflow appears to be commercially available from Immagina Biotech and was not evaluated further.

Shaw et al. [25] describe a method for detecting yeast tRNA modifications using RNA002 chemistry; however, it requires an IVT control to train an HMM-based error model and uses a yeast-specific coordinate system, precluding application to human or mouse data. Similarily, another recent method presented by Guo et al. [26] is optimized for prokaryotes and uses total RNA. The approach operates on Nanopore signals from matched pairs of input and control material and requires an IVT sample.

In summary, QutRNA2 identifies tRNA modifications using error profiles from matched pairs of direct tRNA sequencing. QutRNA2 uses tRNA-specific reads, such as those obtained via adapter sequences specific for tRNAs [6]. We have implemented optional adapter-specific filters in QutRNA2 to retain only reads where both adapters are present. As we previously showed in [10], local alignments are better suited for tRNA sequencing analyses than heuristic approaches. Here, we present the ultra-fast GPU-assisted read mapper, which outperforms previous approaches in alignment accuracy and runtime. Finally, QutRNA2 features transformation to Sprinzl coordinates if either covariance models or Sprinzl coordinate maps are available.

Conclusion

QutRNA2 is unique in its capabilities. First, we contribute an optimal local alignment approach that achieves a 25-fold speedup over our previous approach. This performance increase enables optimization and exploration of workflow parameters without sacrificing alignment accuracy. Second, alignments are selected based on their statistical significance and their compliance with formal requirements (e.g. full tRNA coverage). Third, the JACUSA2 component, which identifies candidate positions of differential RNA modification, was enhanced with a subsampling module. This normalization step increases the signal-to-noise ratio and reduces the number of reported candidate sites without sacrificing the detection performance of known modification sites. Moreover, QutRNA2 offers customizable plots to monitor and control the impact of filtering on read length and counts. Finally, we provide an automated mapping of reference sequence coordinates to Sprinzl positions if a matching covariance model is available for the studied organism. In our utility evaluation, we have applied QutRNA2 to data from different organisms, cells, and sequencing chemistries. We have demonstrated that QutRNA2 identifies known targets of QTRT1 in human HCT116 cells regardless of their genomic origin (nuclear or mitochondrial). We also show that QutRNA2 performed equally well on a different complex tRNA transcriptome from mouse samples, obtained from either cell culture or brain tissue. We concluded with a showcase on additional modification types in S. pombe. We acknowledge general limitations of read error profile based detection method on Nanopore data such as potential signal spread or absence of detectable signal. Potential future directions of research encompass the use of multiple covariance models for coordinate mapping (e.g. nuclear + mt-tRNA models or multiple species in the same sample), use of RNA oligos as ground truth data and tRNA-specific parameter optimization.

In summary, QutRNA2 is the only publicly available open source solution that works on high-volume and complex tRNome datasets at the time of writing.
