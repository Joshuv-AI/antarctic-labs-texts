Precision for all children: embedding equity into precision medicine for children
Authors: Victor Do, Justin Lam, Sanjay Mahant, Eyal Cohen, Indra Narang, Astrid Guttmann, Damien Noone
Published: 2026

Precision medicine promises to improve the health and well-being of individuals with rare diseases1 2 and describes an approach to disease prevention and treatment that accounts for individual genetic, environmental and lifestyle factors.3 In paediatrics, precision medicine has included the use of genomic technologies, biomarker-guided therapies, predictive analytics and individualised prevention and treatment strategies that account for children’s contexts.3 To date, many paediatric hospitals around the world have launched precision medicine initiatives, which have largely focused on advancing diagnostic and therapeutic options for patients through genomic discovery and targeted therapies.4 Although precision medicine offers important opportunities for improving diagnosis and treatment, many children globally continue to face barriers to accessing healthcare, medications, preventive services and other foundational supports necessary for health. Reimagining precision medicine through a health equity and public health lens offers an opportunity to address systemic inequities that undermine children’s health outcomes. As precision medicine evolves, equity must be intentionally integrated so that the concept of precision extends beyond genomics and rare diseases, ensuring that persistent disparities in paediatric care are not unintentionally widened.

We need to learn from the past as we embark on this new movement towards Precision Child Health (PCH) and avoid a perpetuation of inequities. There have been instances where giant leaps have been made, but the beneficiaries of these breakthroughs have been limited. For example, intersectionally marginalised families are less likely to engage in genetic testing, driven by fears of exploitation and misapplication of findings based on past healthcare system interactions. Genetic research also risks perpetuating existing racial biases, as studies often rely on data from predominantly white or European populations. This issue is compounded by the potential for clinical misapplications, similar to the flawed use of race as a biological construct, such as the practice of race-correction in estimated glomerular filtration rate calculations, which disadvantages black patients by unfairly delaying their eligibility for kidney transplants. As precision medicine advances, it is critical to question how these technologies, and indeed the ethos of the movement, can be scaled and leveraged to benefit all populations.

To advance precision medicine for children, paediatric institutions have established programmes and partnerships to share resources, data and expertise.5 These initiatives promise to transform paediatric care through innovative diagnostics and treatments tailored to children’s genetic profiles and the application of genomic technologies.6 7

Yet the benefits of precision medicine remain inequitably distributed.8 Structural inequities shape who can access genetic testing and emerging therapies, as well as whose data inform their design and development.9 Genetic research can also reinforce racial biases when studies draw predominantly on white or European populations. Families from structurally marginalised communities may hesitate to participate in precision medicine initiatives because of barriers such as cost and access, as well as justified mistrust rooted in historical and ongoing exploitation.10 11 In high-income countries with publicly funded healthcare systems, inequities in access to medications, diagnostics and specialised paediatric services also persist. At the same time, childhood represents a uniquely important period for intervention because inequities experienced early in life can shape developmental, educational and health trajectories across the lifespan.12 13 Unless equity is embedded in the foundation of precision medicine, these advances risk disproportionately benefiting those with existing privilege and amplifying long-standing disparities in child health outcomes.14 Without intentional safeguards, precision medicine also risks reproducing the ‘inverse care law’, whereby populations with the greatest health needs are least likely to benefit from healthcare innovations.15

Precision medicine must evolve beyond its current genomic focus to encompass the social, structural and environmental forces that determine health. This work must influence broad public health measures. Some precision medicine initiatives have begun to integrate demographic and contextual factors, such as postal code16 or environmental exposure data. This narrow framing neglects the broader social, economic and environmental conditions that impact the health of all children, but especially those from structurally marginalised populations.14 16

We propose a PCH Equity framework that expands the definition of precision through an equity lens. Unlike broader child health equity frameworks, which seek to improve health outcomes across paediatric populations generally, PCH Equity focuses specifically on ensuring equitable access to, participation in, benefit from and governance of precision health innovations. These innovations include genomic testing, advanced diagnostics, biomarker-guided therapies, predictive analytics, artificial intelligence and other emerging technologies increasingly shaping paediatric care. The framework therefore asks not only whether innovations improve outcomes, but also who is represented in the data used to develop them, who can access them, who benefits from them and who may be left behind.

Importantly, PCH Equity expands the focus of precision medicine beyond rare diseases or genomics. The framework can be applied wherever access to specialised expertise related to advanced diagnostics, novel therapeutics or healthcare innovation is unevenly distributed across paediatric conditions and subspecialties. In this way, the framework extends precision medicine beyond biological variation alone to include the social, structural and environmental factors that shape children’s healthcare experiences, developmental trajectories and long-term well-being. Equity-oriented precision medicine should complement, rather than replace, investments in public health, prevention and universal access to care. Clinical practice

The clinical practice domain focuses on ensuring equitable access to precision health innovations to ensure that all families benefit from them. Particular attention should be paid to barriers that influence access to genomic testing, advanced diagnostics, targeted therapies, paediatric subspecialty expertise and emerging digital health technologies. Achieving equitable precision care requires attention to equitable access not just to innovations, but also to language access and socioeconomic supports, cultural humility and structural competency, equitable digital health and AI integration, and workforce development. Together, these domains help ensure that advances in paediatric precision medicine are accessible, understandable and responsive to the needs of diverse children and families rather than disproportionately benefiting those already positioned to access healthcare innovation.

Research

The research domain focuses on broadening who contributes to the generation of precision health knowledge and whose experiences are reflected in resulting innovations. Under-representation of racialised, Indigenous, newcomer, rural and low-income populations within genomic databases, AI training datasets and paediatric clinical research can reduce diagnostic accuracy, limit generalisability and perpetuate inequities in care. PCH therefore requires both inclusive and representative research practices and robust health equity metrics, data governance and accountability mechanisms. These efforts are essential to ensuring that the data, algorithms, diagnostic tools and therapies that emerge from precision medicine are relevant, effective and equitable across paediatric populations.

Beyond hospital walls

Precision medicine does not operate in isolation from the social and structural conditions that shape children’s health. Environmental exposures, community contexts, healthcare infrastructure and funding decisions all influence who can access and benefit from innovation. This domain therefore highlights the importance of addressing structural barriers through community partnership, integrating environmental health and climate justice considerations and promoting equitable funding strategies that support implementation alongside discovery. Without attention to these broader systems, precision medicine risks improving technological capability while leaving the root causes of child health inequities unchanged. Sustained investments in community partnerships, public health infrastructure and equitable implementation are necessary to ensure that advances in PCH translate into meaningful improvements in outcomes for all children.

Funding

A central challenge for precision medicine lies in how resources are allocated. Investments in genomic technologies, advanced diagnostics, artificial intelligence and targeted therapies may improve outcomes for some children, but they also risk concentrating benefits among populations already positioned to access healthcare innovation. PCH Equity therefore requires attention not only to the development of new technologies, but also to the infrastructure needed for their equitable implementation. This includes investments in interpretation services, patient navigation, community engagement, digital access and supports that enable families to participate in and benefit from precision health initiatives. At the same time, precision medicine should complement rather than compete with investments in public health, prevention, primary care and social supports. Balancing innovation with equitable implementation is essential to ensuring that precision advances narrow, rather than widen, disparities in child health outcomes.

Future vision

A PCH equity framework has the potential to fundamentally shift population health trajectories for children and families who have historically been left behind. By aligning diagnostic innovation with social responsiveness, healthcare systems can better prevent avoidable complications, reduce long-term costs and promote healthier lives.

Engaging communities in research strengthens data validity while building long-term trust, making families more likely to seek care, participate in research and benefit from emerging therapies. By integrating precision tools with equity strategies, such as disaggregated metrics, provider training in structural competency and community-driven design, health systems can more accurately identify and intervene on the drivers of health inequity.

Moving towards an approach that meaningfully embeds equity into precision medicine represents a critical shift in paediatric healthcare and positions equity and precision as foundational principles. Importantly, PCH equity should complement, rather than replace, broader investments in public health, prevention and universal access to care. Our framework can support researchers, policymakers and healthcare providers in taking concrete steps toward a more equitable and effective healthcare system for every child.
