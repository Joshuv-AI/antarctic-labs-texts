Characteristics of Pain and Factors Associated with Fatigue among First-Diagnosed Solid Malignancy Patients: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Rajesh Kumar, Aarti Rana, Alka Pasi, Parveen Parveen, Priyanka Malhotra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Cancer-related pain and fatigue are prevalent and debilitating symptoms experienced by most cancer patients. Chronic fatigue in cancer patients leads to sleep disturbances and reduces performance status, especially during treatment. This study aims to explore characteristics of pain and factors associated with fatigue among patients with solid malignancies diagnosed for the 1st time.Materials and Methods:This cross-sectional, prospective survey purposively included 141 patients with solid malignancies presenting for the 1st time at a tertiary care hospital. Information was collected using a structured pre-tested socio-demographic and clinical profile, the McGill pain questionnaire and the multi-dimensional fatigue inventory-20. Relevant descriptive and inferential statistics were used to generate the results.Results:In this study, 141 patients (male 66, female 75, mean age 49.87 [standard deviation = 10.45]) were included. The most frequent pain descriptor terms were ‘pricking’ (n = 49, 34.8%), ‘sharp’ (n = 39, 27.7%), ‘shooting’ (n = 33, 23.4%), ‘stabbing’ (n = 29, 20.6%), ‘pulling’ (n = 27, 19.1%) and ‘suffocating’ (n = 25, 17.7%). Severe clinical insomnia was presented in 14.9% of patients. Findings reported that advanced age, informal education, distant metastasis, no pain medications, sleep disturbances and poor performance status were associated with higher fatigue in patients.Conclusion:Patients with cancer reported a multifaceted nature of pain. Cancer stage, tumour extension, performance status and sleep disturbances are among the factors associated with higher fatigue among patients. The use of evidence-based pain and fatigue management protocols, along with targeting sleep quality and performance status, may help improve the care and well-being of cancer patients.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer has been considered one of the leading health threats and arduous challenges for healthcare policymakers.[1] Cancer is associated with many adverse outcomes, either due to the course of the disease or treatment. Pain is one of the most encountered debilitating symptoms in the majority of patients.[2] It has been reported that 30% of patients report pain at the time of diagnosis, which may increase to 65–85% of cases as the disease advances[2] and is reported by 90% of in-hospital patients.[3] Pain can be attributed to many unpleasant outcomes, including fatigue, insomnia, psychological problems such as anxiety and depression and poor subjective well-being[1] and decreased cancer survival.[4] Despite revolutionary progress in the field of pain management over the past few decades, pain is still one of the most commonly reported medical symptoms among cancer patients.[5] Nevertheless, it has been reported that pain is the most neglected and ignored aspect of medical care in developing countries.

Meanwhile, fatigue is another equally frequently reported complaint, with a prevalence of nearly 80% in some cancer types.[6] Cancer-related fatigue is diverse, encompassing distressing, persistent physical and emotional weakness, tiredness and a lack of energy. In this type of fatigue, patients are always anergia without indulging in any work, which does not improve with sleep or rest.[7] There are many factors associated with chronic fatigue among cancer patients. It has been reported that impaired sleep leads to daytime fatigue and napping, exacerbating fatigue. Sleep disturbances were previously reported as a significant predictor of fatigue in cancer patients.[8,9] Conversely, anaemia and the physiological effects of fatigue may lead to daytime sedation and make night sleep difficult.[10] However, the nature of fatigue was found to be associated with many other attributes, such as duration of illness, severity, stage of cancer and existing comorbidities.[10] Fatigue and pain in cancer patients play a key role in adversely affecting general health, normal daily functioning and health-related quality of life.[11,12]

Given the above situation, it is crucial to understand the characteristics of pain and factors associated with fatigue among patients with solid malignancies. Understanding the nature of pain and fatigue-related factors will help clinicians to develop interventions to improve fatigue in cancer patients. Therefore, we undertook the study to explore the nature of pain and the factors associated with fatigue among first-diagnosed patients with solid malignancy in the study setting.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A cross-sectional, prospective survey design was used in the outpatient area of the radiation oncology department at a tertiary care hospital in Uttarakhand in March and April 2024. Using purposive consecutive sampling, 141 patients with solid malignancies who presented to the outpatient department for the 1st time were included in the study. We have maintained a recruitment log for the patients included in the study until the required sample size was reached. The study included patients aged 18–60 years with a 1st-time diagnosis of a solid malignancy and not on any cancer-related drugs. However, patients with haematological cancer and solid malignancies who have already been diagnosed and are under cancer-related treatment were excluded from this study. We have used a consent form and detailed informant participant sheets before initiating data collection. Questionnaires were administered after they consulted with a specialist in a separate room in the oncology department’s outpatient area during morning outpatient hours. All the scales, including the socio-demographic questionnaire, were read aloud to participants by the researcher and rated. However, participants were asked to discuss and clarify any doubts to ensure a clear understanding of the items before responding. Clinical data were retrieved from medical case records, and only clearly recorded and legible entries pertaining to the clinical profile were considered for analysis. It took an average of 20–25 min to complete all the questionnaires.

Socio-demographic and clinical profile sheet

A self-structured, pre-tested profile sheet was used to collect personal and disease-related information from patients. The sheet contains information on age, gender, educational status, pain medication and its types, tumour size, extent, metastasis, stage and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) scores for the patients’ current performance status.

McGill pain questionnaire (MPQ)

This self-reporting measure of pain measures the quality and intensity of subjective pain in the participants. It is a multidimensional tool that consists of 78 pain descriptors belonging to its 4 dimensions: Sensory (1–10 items, 42 pain descriptors), affective (11–15 items, 14 pain descriptors), evaluative (only item 16, 5 pain descriptors) and miscellaneous pain (17–20 items, 17 pain descriptors). Qualitative differences in pain may be reflected in the respondents’ word choice.[13] The scale is widely used in similar studies and has good psychometric properties.[14,15] We have used a Hindi version of the scale validated among rheumatoid patients with acceptable psychometric properties.[16]

Multi-dimensional fatigue inventory-20 (MFI-20)

The MFI-20 self-assessment questionnaire was used to measure fatigue in participants. This 20-item questionnaire uses a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Yes, that is true, to 5 = No, that is not true) to rate general fatigue (1, 5, 12 and 16), physical fatigue (2, 8, 14 and 20), reduced activity (3, 6,10 and 17), reduced motivation (4, 9, 15 and 18) and mental fatigue (7, 11, 13 and 19) participants experienced during the course of the disease. The scale has a balanced number of positive and negative items to counteract response tendencies. A higher cumulative score suggests higher fatigue.[17] Both English and Hindi versions of the scale were used in this study for the interest of the population.[18] The scale showed good construct validity and excellent reliability (α = 0.80).[17,19]

Insomnia severity index (ISI)

A brief self-reported questionnaire is used to assess sleep disturbances in patients. The scale consists of 7 items on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (no problem) to 4 (severe problems), for the past 2 weeks. The cumulative score was categorised into normal sleep (0–7), mild insomnia (8–14), moderate insomnia (15–21) and severe insomnia (22–28) and a cumulative score suggests higher sleep disturbances. The scale has excellent reliability (α = 0.74–0.91)[20] and strong construct validity[21] across clinical and community samples. A Hindi version of the scale was used in the present study.[22]

Ethical consideration

The research proposal was approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee (IEC) for approval (AIIMS/IEC/23/269). The researchers adhered to all the methods and procedures per the updated ethical guidelines and regulations for human participants in research while collecting and disseminating information.[23] An individual participant was asked to provide written informed consent to indicate their participation and willingness to participate in the study. Participants were assured that their confidentiality and privacy would be protected throughout the research process.

Sample size calculation

A proportion-based sample size was calculated based on the total patient load (n= 217) with solid malignancies attending the radiation oncology department. An absolute precision of 5% is fixed, and the standard deviation (SD) is set at 1.96 for a 95% confidence interval. The recommended sample size for the study was 139. However, a sample of 141 was taken in the current study.

Statistical analysis

Data were imported into Microsoft Excel and cleaned before analysis in IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences Statistics for Windows, version 23.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.).[24] Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, are used to depict participants’ personal and clinical information. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data distribution; because the data were nonnormal, the Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to compare groups across variables. All hypotheses are tested at p< 0.05 (two-sided).

RESULTS

Table 1 presents the patients’ personal and clinical characteristics. The mean age of the patients was 49.87 (SD = 10.45) years. 53.2% of patients were female, and 46.8% were male. More than three-fourths of the patients (78.0%) belonged to rural areas, while 22.0% were from urban sector. 38.3% of the participants had never attended formal schooling, while 33.3% had completed up to primary education, followed by 17.7% had studied up to 12th class; and the remaining 10.6% were graduates and above. The clinical profile reported that 9.9% of the patients were diagnosed with bone metastasis and were currently on pain medications (51.8%). Regarding types of pain medications, 36.9% of patients were on oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) pain medication, followed by 4.3% on intravenous NSAIDs along with oral NSAIDs and the remaining 10.6% were on opioid drugs (i.e. codeine and tramadol). Around half of the patients (48.9%) had local tumour spread, and 42.6% and 8.5% had regional and distant metastasis, respectively. 62.0% of patients had a tumour size <2 cm, while 38% had a tumour size >2 cm. 19.1% of the patients were in cancer stage 2, followed by 17.0, 9.9% and 7.8% in stage 1, 3 and 4, respectively. Further, 60.3% of the patients were diagnosed with gastrointestinal malignancies, followed by reproductive system (18.4%), respiratory (9.9%), neurological (5.7%), musculoskeletal (4.3%) and genitourinary (1.4%). According to ECOG criteria, an equal number (30%) of the patients reported mild restriction in activity, and 18.5% reported severe disability [Table 1].

Possible predictors of fatigue

Sociodemographic factors and fatigue

Patients with advanced age (>51 years) have significantly higher mental fatigue (p= 0.03) than the other age groups. There was a significant difference in the degree of activity reduction between male and female patients (p= 0.03). Female patients reported significantly greater restriction in performing activities compared to their counterparts (p= 0.03). Patients with higher age reported significantly higher mental fatigue (p= 0.03) than their counterparts. Patients who never attended formal schooling or were informally educated reported a higher burden of physical fatigue (p= 0.04) and reduced activity (p= 0.03) compared with those with formal education [Table 4].

Clinical factors and fatigue

General fatigue was significantly higher in patients with tumour size <2 cm (P= 0.02) and those who were completely disabled (p= 0.01) as per ECOG score criteria. Patients with distant metastasis reported significantly higher physical fatigue (p = 0.03), reduced motivation (p= 0.01) and mental fatigue (p= 0.02) than their counterparts. Patients reported bone metastasis (p= 0.02) and were completely disabled (p< 0.001), reporting reduced motivation. Disabled patients, as per the ECOG, reported a greater reduction in activity (p= 0.01) and higher mental fatigue (p= 0.03). Patients with stage 3 cancer significantly reported higher physical fatigue (p= 0.02), reduced activity (p= 0.02) and decreased motivation (p= 0.01) in comparison to their counterparts [Table 4].

Pain medications and fatigue

Patients who are on pain medication reported higher physical fatigue (p= 0.01) than patients who are not on pain medications. Further, patients on both oral and intravenous infusion pain medication reported higher physical fatigue (p= 0.01) and reduced activity (p= 0.03) compared to those on either oral or opioid medications [Table 4].

Sleep disturbances and fatigue

Patients with higher sleep disturbances, especially severe insomnia, reported a high level of general fatigue (p= 0.01), physical fatigue (p= 0.04), reduced activity (p< 0.001), decreased motivation (p= 0.01) and higher mental fatigue (p< 0.001) [Table 4].

Patients reported the complex nature of cancer pain alongside multidimensional fatigue, with poor sleep quality, decreased activity level and clinical factors had a direct influence on fatigue severity.

DISCUSSION

This cross-sectional survey was conducted in the outpatient area of the oncology department at a newly established tertiary care hospital in Uttarakhand. The study aims to explore the characteristics of pain experienced and factors associated with fatigue in patients with solid malignancies. The findings report that patients frequently employed the terms shooting, stabbing, sharp, lacerating, pulling and wrenching as sensory characteristics of the pain. Close similar descriptors for sensory pain are used in a previous study conducted on breast cancer patients, who reported that patients employed the terms stabbing, nagging, shooting and annoying most.[25] Another study used a short version of the MPQ and found that patients most frequently used the terms aching, tender and sharp to describe pain characteristics, findings similar to those of the current study.[26] It has been revealed in another previous study that pain following mastectomy with breast reconstruction is reported to be itching, burning, aching, tightness, annoying and exhausting.[27]

In our study, patients also reported pain as aching, wrenching, suffocating, tiring and blinding. A study on cancer patients in Rabat reported that throbbing, shooting, hot, burning, stabbing and aching are the most frequent sensory descriptors of pain used by the patients.[28] Tiring, exhausting, sickening, fearful and punishing are the most frequently used affective pain descriptors in another study,[28] which are consistent with the pain perception of our study patients. These similar characteristics of cancer-related pain are consistently reported in the previous literature.[29]

The assessment of the nature and characteristics of pain in cancer patients reported that aching, tiring-exhausting, sharp and dull are the most frequently used pain descriptors by the population of cancer patients,[30] which aligns with our study findings. The findings on pain characteristics in cancer patients are consistent with previous studies.[31-33] The use of different types of analgesics, including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and opioids, was also high among the study participants. This is consistent with the literature, which suggests the use of a variety of drugs, including NSAIDs and opioids use in the management of cancer pain.[33-37]

Fatigue is another commonly reported symptom in cancer patients. In our study, mental fatigue was the most frequently reported fatigue among cancer patients, followed by physical fatigue and reduced activity. However, the mental fatigue was reported to be higher in the previously published studies.[38,39] Some of the previously published reports stated higher general fatigue, followed by physical fatigue and mental fatigue.[40] Our study findings on general fatigue and reduced physical activity are consistent with those of the Ethiopian and Danish cancer cohorts.[40,41] Further, the findings for higher mental fatigue and reduced motivation among cancer patients are also presented in other previously published reports.[38,39,42,43] The reason for higher mental fatigue and reduced motivation in previously published studies could be due to timing, stage or type of cancer,[44] cultural perception of mental fatigue[45] and presence of psychological problems,[46] which may exaggerate the mental burden in patients. Patients also reported low motivation, which might have contributed to higher mental fatigue and reduced activity. The low motivation could be due to barriers and challenges in accessing health services, including financial and travel costs, disparities in essential medical resources between rural and urban areas, provider-related issues, specialised health facilities and other technology-related challenges at the current healthcare facility.[47,48]

Socio-demographic and clinical factors are associated with fatigue in our study. Women patients reported higher fatigue across all domains than men. The findings on fatigue among cancer patients were consistent across gender, with higher fatigue reported in female than male patients.[43,49-51] Another previously published study presented higher fatigue among women with cancer.[52,53] Authors speculate that women take care of household chores in the Indian family system and have to perform a lot of other social responsibilities, with relatively allow few break times for women. Further, patients with advanced age reported greater physical fatigue and reduced motivation in our study cohort. These findings are consistent with a few published reports of increased fatigue among older patients.[40] An explanation could be that older patients have reduced physical reserve, more comorbidities due to ageing and often have the responsibility of supporting their families and young children. Findings revealed that illiterate patients reported higher fatigue, consistent with the Ethiopian study.[41] However, it is difficult to draw conclusions about the impact of each socio-demographic factor on different types of fatigue in cancer patients, as these associations are rarely systematically studied.

Further, performance status, extent of disease and cancer stage were significantly associated with fatigue in one or more domains in this study cohort. These findings are well tested in the previously published studies that reduced performance status and a higher stage of cancer are significantly associated with higher fatigue[43,49,52]and decreased social functioning.[54] The association between performance status and fatigue in cancer patients has been well studied and is consistent with our findings.[43,55,56] The findings on the impact of the advanced stage of cancer have shown significantly higher fatigue,[49] restricted social activities and worse cognitive function,[41,54] consistent with our study findings.

An association between centrally acting analgesics and fatigue was anticipated, and a nonmonotonic association of pain medication with physical fatigue only was observed, consistent with a previous report.[39] Further, it has been reported that centrally acting drugs are associated with higher physical fatigue and restricted activity. This could be that the physician appropriately adjusted the doses to relieve pain and other symptoms without excessively sedating the patients, or be nature of fatigue in cancer patients is more likely due to other causes. However, fatigue is not universally improved across all domains, and the findings of cross-sectional studies should be interpreted cautiously when assessing the association between pain medication and fatigue.

Sleep is reported to be one of the common factors associated with fatigue among cancer patients. The impact and association of sleep have been well studied in relation to fatigue in cancer patients,[57-59] findings that are consistently reported in our study cohort.

Strengths and limitations

Several limitations of this work must be considered when using the findings. We presented results from a cross-sectional study because we have data on the study variables at a single point in time. Participants were enrolled from an individual setting; therefore, the findings may not be generalisable to other similar settings at the national and individual levels. Second, both the Hindi and English versions of MFI were used in the study. More than half of the participants were illiterate, so the researcher had to read every item aloud to complete the questionnaire, which may have led to measurement non-equivalence. The study included only one group and lacked a matched control group, making it unable to determine whether fatigue differs among cancer patients, healthy individuals or patients with other diseases. Further, patients with severe fatigue and pain might be underrepresented, which could lead our mean scores to underestimate the true burden of duo fatigue and pain in the study group. Including patients with different cancer types may lead to considerable variability within groups, especially in cancer burden for patients. This heterogeneity should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. At the time of sample recruitment, many of the patients were on different types of pain medications, which may influence actual pain and fatigue among the patients. Finally, with this small descriptive sample, it is not possible to firmly establish direction or relationships among fatigue and demographic and clinical characteristics.

Despite these limitations, this study has certain strong points. First, the strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, which limit the study to first-diagnosed cases of solid malignancies at the hospital, make it a one-of-a-kind work. The use of valid and reliable instruments for fatigue, pain and other variables increases the credibility of the findings in the study cohort.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the multifaceted nature of pain experienced by cancer patients. Further, patients reported higher levels of mental and physical fatigue. The extent of disease, cancer stage, performance status and sleep disturbances are commonly identified factors for higher fatigue in patients. Future research on larger patient groups would be beneficial for drawing precise, reliable conclusions to improve the management of cancer patients.
