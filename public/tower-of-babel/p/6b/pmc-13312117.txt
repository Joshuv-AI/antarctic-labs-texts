Multi-system thromboembolism: a case report of concurrent pulmonary, myocardial, and abdominal embolism
Authors: Mariah Mascara, Lovish Gupta, Ramzi Khalil, Saad Tabbara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultisystem thromboembolism is a rare but potentially fatal phenomenon that can develop in patients with underlying hypercoagulability and rapidly progress to multiorgan failure if not promptly identified. Concurrent arterial and venous embolic events should raise suspicion for paradoxical embolism, particularly in patients with a patent foramen ovale (PFO).Case summaryA 63-year-old male with a history of malignancy, renal transplant on tacrolimus, and limited mobility presented with dyspnoea and abdominal discomfort. During initial evaluation, he developed acute chest pain, with ECG demonstrating acute posterior myocardial infarction (MI) requiring emergent percutaneous coronary intervention. A CT angiogram subsequently revealed bilateral pulmonary emboli (PE) with right heart strain. After percutaneous management of both PE and MI, he developed worsening lactic acidosis and multiorgan failure. A repeat abdominal CT demonstrated acute mesenteric ischaemia with extensive pneumatosis intestinalis. Transthoracic echocardiography with bubble study revealed a PFO with right-to-left shunt, suggesting paradoxical embolism as the aetiology of simultaneous PE, MI, and mesenteric ischaemia.DiscussionThis case illustrates a presentation of possible PFO-mediated multisystem thrombosis across coronary, pulmonary, and mesenteric circulations. Persistent or unexplained lactic acidosis following treatment of known thrombotic events should prompt urgent abdominal imaging, particularly in intubated or non-communicative patients who cannot report pain out of proportion from physical examination. Early recognition of paradoxical embolism is essential in preventing delays in diagnosing occult mesenteric ischaemia, a complication with high mortality when detected late.

In critically ill patients with multiorgan ischaemic insults, multisystem thromboembolism should be considered as an underlying unifying aetiology.

Introduction

Case presentation

A 63-year-old male patient with a past medical history of stage 1B non-small cell lung cancer in remission on last appointment in 2021 and lost to follow-up since then, chronic kidney disease post renal transplant on tacrolimus, insulin-dependent type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hypertension, with ambulatory dysfunction at baseline presented to the emergency department with new-onset dyspnoea, abdominal discomfort, and bloating. He had no prior history of atrial fibrillation or known coronary artery disease. The patient was found to be hypoxic on arrival and was placed on 4 L of oxygen by nasal cannula. An initial workup included an electrocardiogram (ECG), troponins, a CT of the abdomen and pelvis (CT AP), a complete blood count, a complete metabolic panel, lactic acid (LA), chest x-ray, and a pro-BNP. ECG demonstrated normal sinus rhythm without ischaemic changes and elevated but flat troponins (120 and 121 ng/L, respectively). Initial CT AP did not show evidence of an acute intra-abdominal process.

While in the emergency department, the patient developed sudden-onset chest pain. A repeat ECG showed ST depression in anterolateral leads, concerning for posterior myocardial infarction (MI) (Figure 1). He was started on a heparin drip and nitroglycerine, with the decision to proceed with emergent cardiac catheterization. Prior to cardiac catheterization, he went into pulseless electrical activity (PEA) cardiac arrest, with return of spontaneous circulation within 4 min.

ECG with ST depression in V1–V3 and tall R waves (R/S > 1), a classic reciprocal pattern of posterior MI.

Following PEA arrest, the patient was taken urgently for a CTA chest (CT PE), which revealed bilateral pulmonary emboli (PE) with right heart strain (Figure 2). The patient then proceeded for emergent cardiac catheterization and drug-eluting stent placement of the totally occluded left circumflex artery (Figure 3). The patient remained clinically stable after PCI with a pulmonary artery pressure of 30/14 by right heart catheterization. Pulmonary mechanical thrombectomy was deferred until the next morning, with adequate extraction of clot. A lower extremity venous duplex ultrasound was not performed, as confirmation of pulmonary embolism had already been established, and management would not have changed acutely.

Axial CT PE showing a hypodense filling defects in the segmental branches of the left and right pulmonary arteries (red arrows), indicative of pulmonary emboli.

Coronary angiography demonstrating totally occluded left circumflex artery (red arrow).

Following the management of PE and MI, the patient was haemodynamically stable and remained in sinus rhythm without evidence of atrial fibrillation. However, he continued to have worsening lactic acidosis and began to clinically deteriorate with worsening renal and hepatic function. Physical examination was unremarkable; the patient did not display abdominal distension, and there was no evidence of wounds or necrosis of the extremities. Although mesenteric ischaemia was considered in the differential diagnosis given the hypercoagulable context, the initial non-revealing abdominal imaging and competing cardiopulmonary pathology lowered early clinical suspicion.

With the presence of both venous and arterial thrombi, broad differential diagnoses were considered, including heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. Both the PE and MI occurred prior to the patient initiating anticoagulation, making this diagnosis less probable. Of note, the patient did not have a history of atrial fibrillation, nor were there any documented episodes throughout the hospitalization.

A transthoracic echocardiogram with bubble study was performed, which revealed a new reduction in systolic ejection fraction to 40%–45% (previously 60%–65%) and shunting due to a patent foramen ovale (PFO) (see Figure 4). A transoesophageal echocardiogram was not performed due to the patient’s clinical instability and rapidly deteriorating state. No intracardiac thrombus was visualized on transthoracic imaging. A repeat CT AP was ultimately performed in the setting of worsening lactic acidosis and rapid clinical deterioration despite resolution of underlying cardiopulmonary pathology. Imaging demonstrated significant pneumatosis of the bowels due to underlying acute mesenteric ischaemia.

Transthoracic echocardiogram apical four-chamber with bubble study demonstrating shunting secondary to patent foramen ovale (PFO).

Due to the advanced disease and extensive bowel necrosis at the time of discovery, the patient was not a surgical candidate. The patient’s family transitioned the patient’s care to a comfort measures approach in view of multiorgan failure (Table 1).

Discussion

Multisystem thromboembolism is a rare but potentially fatal phenomenon that can develop in patients with a hypercoagulable state, which can lead to rapid clinical deterioration with multiorgan failure if not promptly detected. Here, a case of suspected paradoxical embolism due to PFO is described in a patient with a known underlying hypercoagulable state with a history of immobility.

PFO is observed in up to 20%–30% of the general population, and while it is typically asymptomatic, it is a known cause of paradoxical embolism.1 Embolic events may occur in the setting of a PFO when a thrombus from the peripheral venous system is shunted to the systemic circulation via the interatrial defect.2 The development of paradoxical embolism is rare, encompassing less 2% of all cases of systemic arterial emboli.3 However, even amongst paradoxical emboli, concomitant PE, MI, and mesenteric ischaemia are an extremely uncommon presentation. In the present case, the patient was at an increased risk of venous thromboembolism due to his history of ambulatory dysfunction. Following evaluation for atrial fibrillation, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, sepsis-induced coagulopathy, and disseminated intravascular coagulopathy, all of which were negative, a PFO was deemed the most likely pathologic entity that could explain the presence of both venous and arterial thromboembolic events occurring contemporaneously. While definitive confirmation with transoesophageal imaging was not feasible, the presence of right-to-left shunting on bubble study in the absence of alternative embolic sources supported this working diagnosis.

The timing of abdominal imaging in this case reflects a diagnostic challenge commonly encountered in critically ill patients. Notably, a CT abdomen and pelvis performed on presentation was non-revealing, which initially lowered suspicion for intra-abdominal pathology. Even though the report did not point towards mesenteric ischaemia, it is noted that CT of the abdomen without contrast carries a low diagnostic sensitivity and specificity for such a diagnosis. Initial clinical deterioration was attributed to the coexisting PE and MI, both of which were promptly managed. After intubation, the patient was unable to report pain out of proportion from examination, further delaying reconsideration of mesenteric ischaemia. It was only the persistent upward trend in lactic acid despite resolution of the cardiopulmonary events that prompted repeat abdominal imaging. In retrospect, earlier consideration of mesenteric ischaemia at the onset of refractory lactic acidosis may have facilitated earlier detection. Given that mesenteric ischaemia was the ultimate driver of multiorgan failure, this case illustrates the prognostic importance of maintaining a low threshold for abdominal re-evaluation in patients with unexplained metabolic acidosis following treatment of other thrombotic events.

Although mesenteric ischaemia was presumed to be embolic in origin based on its temporal association with concurrent pulmonary and coronary thromboses and the presence of a right-to-left shunt across the PFO, a dedicated CT angiogram of the mesenteric vasculature was not performed. Therefore, non-occlusive mesenteric ischaemia secondary to transient hypoperfusion during cardiac arrest or a low cardiac output state remains a plausible alternative, even though the cardiac arrest was very brief with prompt return of spontaneous circulation. While paradoxical embolism remains the most unifying explanation for the coexistence of venous and arterial thromboses in this patient, the precise aetiology of mesenteric injury cannot be definitively established. The patient did require pressor support during his hospitalization, but he was never profoundly hypotensive, rendering this alternative hypothesis less likely.

This case underscores the need for a high index of suspicion in clinically deteriorating patients with hypercoagulable states, particularly when communication is limited. Simultaneous acute PE, MI, and acute mesenteric ischaemia due to paradoxical embolism from a PFO are extremely rare and frequently underrecognized. Persistent lactic acidosis should be viewed as an early warning sign of occult ischaemia, even when initial imaging is unrevealing. Prompt reassessment and multidisciplinary approach may improve the chance of intervention before irreversible organ damage occurs.
