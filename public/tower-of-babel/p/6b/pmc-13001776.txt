Remimazolam in anesthesia and sedation: A narrative review
Authors: Yan Dong, Jun Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRemimazolam, an innovative sedative, provides a rapid onset and quick recovery by being rapidly hydrolyzed into pharmacologically inactive CNS7054 via ubiquitous nonspecific esterases. Its pharmacokinetic characteristics (high clearance rate, small steady-state volume of distribution, short terminal half-life, and no tissue accumulation) further lay the foundation for clinical application. Compared with traditional sedative drugs, the prominent innovations of this drug are reflected in three aspects: first, it has less impact on the respiratory and circulatory systems, with stable hemodynamics and a low incidence of discomfort at the administration site; second, its sedative effect can be specifically antagonized by flumazenil, allowing for more flexible safety regulation; third, it shows excellent safety and effectiveness in special populations such as elderly patients and those with comorbid cardiovascular diseases. Clinical evidence shows that remimazolam is more effective than similar drugs in scenarios such as gastrointestinal endoscopy, operating rooms, and ICUs. Its reliable performance in special populations further supports its value as a sedative that combines innovation with clinical practicality and holds broad application prospects. This review aims to systematically elaborate on the pharmacological properties and clinical application value of remimazolam, a new benzodiazepine derivative, and provide references for its standardized use in various diagnostic and therapeutic scenarios and special populations.Keywords: Remimazolam sedative, General anesthesia, Pharmacokinetics, Safety

Introduction

Currently, commonly used clinical sedative-hypnotics include propofol, etomidate, and midazolam, among others. As a widely used sedative, propofol is characterized by advantages such as a short half-life and rapid recovery, with adverse reactions including respiratory depression, hypotension, and injection pain.1 Etomidate is noted for its rapid onset of action and minimal impact on the respiratory and circulatory systems, but it tends to cause myoclonus upon injection and has a high incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting.2 While midazolam exhibits significant anxiolytic and anterograde amnesic effects, it also presents dose-dependent respiratory depression. As a hydrophilic parenterally administered benzodiazepine derivative, remimazolam has gained regulatory authorization for adult general anesthesia across numerous regions.3 Its pharmacological action centers on potentiating GABA receptor function, triggering processes such as neuronal membrane hyperpolarization and subsequent chloride ion flux.4 Crucially diverging from midazolam, the metabolic byproducts of remimazolam demonstrate insignificant binding affinity at target receptors, thus eliminating risks of prolonged sedative effects through bioaccumulation. Notable therapeutic attributes include swift induction/recovery kinetics, non-specific enzymatic（primarily carboxylesterases in blood and tissues— degradation pathways, and preserved cardiovascular homeostasis, collectively solidifying its status as a preferable agent for both interventional sedation protocols and comprehensive anesthetic management.

Pharmacokinetics

Remimazolam, chemically classified as C21H19BrN4O2 (Fig. 1A), with an average mass of 439.31 Da,5 exists in two salt forms: remimazolam besylate and remimazolam tosylate. It integrates multiple characteristics of its parent compounds, midazolam (pharmacodynamic properties) and remifentanil (pharmacokinetic properties). Compared with midazolam, it provides sedation with a faster onset and shorter duration. This unique pharmacokinetic profile is attributed to the incorporation of a carboxyl ester linkage into the midazolam molecule, rendering it a substrate for esterase metabolism and conferring exclusive elimination advantages.4 Remimazolam undergoes rapid enzymatic degradation to CNS7054 via systemic esterase activity (Fig. 1B), with hepatic carboxylesterase 1 A serving as the principal catalytic enzyme.6 This mechanism facilitates prompt therapeutic onset following intravenous administration (0.01–0.5 mg/kg), achieving peak plasma concentrations of 189–6960 ng/mL within 20–30 min. The resultant metabolites demonstrate drastically reduced γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABA-A) receptor engagement, exhibiting 300-fold lower binding affinity compared to the parent compound.7 Such pharmacological characteristics preclude cumulative sedation effects, ensure rapid drug action termination, and confer functional independence from hepatic/renal metabolic pathways.

Remimazolam exhibits pharmacokinetic advantages, characterized by a high clearance rate (1.15 ± 0.12 L/min, mean ± SD), a low steady-state volume of distribution (35.4 ± 4.2 L), and a short terminal half-life (70 ± 10 min).8 Remimazolam’s context-sensitive half-times result in rapid elimination. After an infusion lasting roughly 2 h, it can attain a steady-state within 7–8 min, and this seems unaffected by the duration of the infusion.9 Loss of consciousness occurs at 5 ± 1 min after intravenous administration, and full recovery of alertness is achieved at 19 ± 7 min after discontinuation of the infusion.8 In Chinese healthy adult subjects, following a single intravenous administration of remimazolam (calculated as remimazolam tosylate, dose range 0.01–0.45 mg/kg), the peak plasma concentration is reached at approximately 1 min post-administration, with a volume of distribution of 34.8 ± 9.4 L, a clearance rate of 70.3 ± 13.9 L/h, and a terminal half-life of 0.75 ± 0.15 h.10 When administered as a single intravenous dose of 0.01–0.30 mg/kg in healthy adult subjects, the metabolism of remimazolam conforms to first-order pharmacokinetic characteristics, with no significant correlation between clearance rate and body weight.10

Remimazolam's pharmacokinetic profile exhibits population-specific variability, with clinically significant alterations observed in geriatric cohorts and individuals exhibiting hepatic/renal dysfunction. Current research suggests that remimazolam does not require dosage adjustment when administered to patients with mild or moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh A and B), and in vitro studies confirm preserved metabolic competence in primary human hepatocyte cultures (no direct cytotoxic effects on cellular viability or enzymatic function).11 However, for patients with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C), a dosage reduction is recommended. The pharmacokinetics of remimazolam are essentially unaffected by renal insufficiency; therefore, no dosage adjustment is needed when administering it to patients with renal impairment.12

Assessment of sedative effect

Contemporary anesthetic monitoring protocols increasingly integrate processed electroencephalographic (EEG) biomarkers for quantifying hypnotic depth, particularly when administering intravenous agents characterized by heightened pharmacodynamic heterogeneity compared to volatile anesthetics.13 Electroencephalographic dynamics during remimazolam infusion manifest as biphasic spectral alterations: a primary surge in β-band oscillatory activity followed by progressive δ-wave predominance.14 Quantitative analysis reveals the β power ratio exhibits superior predictive validity for stratifying MOAA/S-defined sedation levels, whereas comparative analyses demonstrate Narcotrend-derived indices display diminished concordance with clinical sedation scales. This evidence-driven divergence establishes β-ratio monitoring as the electrophysiological biomarker of choice over Narcotrend quantification for optimizing sedation protocols with remimazolam. Both BIS and PSI can serve as reliable indicators for monitoring remimazolam-induced sedation. In a previous study by Liu et al.,15 only a weak correlation was observed between BIS reduction and sleep state transitions during remimazolam-induced anesthesia induction, which might be attributed to the interference of high electromyographic activity under remimazolam anesthesia with the accuracy of BIS. However, a subsequent study by Zhao et al.16 demonstrated a strong correlation between BIS and PSI in intraoperative monitoring of remimazolam’s sedative effects (P < 0.01, R = 0.796), and both methods could accurately reflect the depth of sedation. According to the correlation analysis with the MOAA/S scale and ROC curves, BIS is more accurate than PSI in intraoperative monitoring. Other indicators, including pupil diameter, can also serve as auxiliary indicators for sedation.16

Multimodal pharmacodynamic evaluation of remimazolam—incorporating quantitative EEG metrics (β frequency band power ratio, bispectral index/BIS, Narcotrend staging) alongside validated clinical scales (modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation, MOAA/S)—demonstrates distinctive kinetic properties: rapid achievement of target sedation levels with corresponding swift recovery trajectories. Procedural sedation and analgesia

Procedural sedation and analgesia (PSA) refers to a therapeutic approach that involves the use of sedative and/or analgesic drugs to effectively perform diagnostic or therapeutic procedures, while closely monitoring patients for potential adverse reactions. The ideal characteristics of hypnotic drugs used for procedural sedation include rapid onset and recovery, minimal residual sedation, ease of use, and few adverse reactions. Propofol, as a commonly used sedative, has the characteristics of rapid onset and short half-life, but it is associated with many adverse reactions, mainly including injection pain, hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, and propofol infusion syndrome. These adverse reactions have limited its clinical application. Compared with propofol, remimazolam has a smaller impact on hemodynamics8, 19 with a lower incidence of sedation-related adverse reactions such as injection pain and respiratory depression,20, 21, 22, 23 and a milder inflammatory response.24 For propofol-induced injection pain, pretreatment with remimazolam before infusion can significantly alleviate it, along with a reduction in propofol dosage, shortened recovery time of patients, and increased satisfaction.24

Remimazolam exhibits distinct pharmacodynamic advantages over midazolam, demonstrating faster onset and offset of sedative effects while causing fewer hemodynamic disturbances and less respiratory depression. In endoscopic procedures including both gastroscopy and colonoscopy, clinical comparisons reveal equivalent procedural completion rates and overall sedation quality between the two agents. However, remimazolam treatment significantly reduces the need for supplemental dosing and rescue medications during procedures.25 Furthermore, patients receiving remimazolam experience lower rates of hypotension, require reduced analgesic doses, achieve faster recovery of neurocognitive function, and meet discharge criteria earlier compared to those administered midazolam.26

Both dexmedetomidine and remimazolam are suitable for monitored anesthesia care (MAC) across various clinical scenarios,27 with remimazolam demonstrating several clinical advantages. During fiberoptic bronchoscopy, remimazolam provides comparable safety and efficacy to dexmedetomidine while offering superior clinical outcomes, including enhanced hemodynamic stability, reduced need for airway interventions, lower procedural interruption rates, decreased incidence of oxygen desaturation, and improved overall MAC quality, thereby increasing both procedural safety and patient compliance.28

In summary, procedural sedation and analgesia (PSA) have clear requirements for the ideal properties of hypnotic drugs. In comparison with commonly used sedative drugs, remimazolam shows significant advantages: compared with propofol, it has a smaller impact on hemodynamics and fewer sedation-related adverse reactions, and can also alleviate propofol-induced injection pain and reduce the dosage of propofol; compared with midazolam, its sedative effect starts and fades faster, has a milder impact on blood flow and respiration, can reduce additional dosing during procedures, and enables patients to recover and be discharged faster; compared with dexmedetomidine, it achieves better clinical outcomes in scenarios such as fiberoptic bronchoscopy, which can improve procedural safety and patient compliance.

Remimazolam for sedation in ICU

The critical condition of ICU patients, frequently complicated by hepatic or renal dysfunction, creates a strong clinical need for short-acting sedatives with organ-independent elimination pathways. Remimazolam’s unique pharmacological profile-featuring extrahepatic metabolism, minimal accumulation potential, and flumazenil reversibility-positions it as a theoretically optimal agent for prolonged ICU sedation. Clinical evidence demonstrates its efficacy across the full spectrum of sedation depth requirements (mild to profound),29 while maintaining predictable pharmacokinetics characterized by rapid onset and offset. Importantly, remimazolam preserves respiratory and hemodynamic stability in mechanically ventilated patients, facilitating earlier extubation (75 vs 114 h, P < 0.05)30 and reduced ventilator dependence compared to conventional sedatives.31

These properties may translate into improved clinical outcomes in intensive care settings, including reduced hospital stay duration and reduced complication rates.30 The drug’s metabolic characteristics appear particularly advantageous for patients with concurrent liver or kidney impairment, though further research is needed to fully establish its role in this vulnerable population.

Application of remimazolam in different groups of people

Patients with different ASA grades

Remimazolam is suitable for both procedural sedation and general anesthesia in patients across varying American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classifications. Studies indicate that its efficacy and safety profile remains consistent in procedural sedation (e.g., colonoscopy) across both low-risk (ASA I-II) and high-risk (ASA III-IV) populations.26

For ASA I-II patients undergoing general anesthesia, remimazolam demonstrates favorable safety during induction and maintenance phases.32 Compared to propofol, remimazolam is associated with a prolonged time to loss of consciousness and delayed postoperative extubation, while exhibiting a lower incidence of hypotension and decreased need for vasoactive medications.33

Further research supports remimazolam’s safety and efficacy in ASA class II or higher patients, confirming non-inferior anesthetic effects relative to propofol. Additionally, its use correlates with fewer vasopressor requirements and a lower rate of anesthesia-related adverse events.34, 35, 36

Elderly patients

Elderly patients face increased perioperative risks due to age-related physiological decline, including reduced vascular compliance, diminished cardiovascular reserve, and impaired compensatory mechanisms.37 Remimazolam demonstrates clinical advantages in this population by providing greater hemodynamic stability during anesthesia induction compared to conventional agents. Studies report reduced incidence of hypotension, bradycardia, and injection site pain when using remimazolam for general anesthesia in elderly patients.38

The drug maintains more stable mean arterial pressure and heart rate throughout induction while improving patient comfort levels.19 These properties position remimazolam as a potentially safer sedative option for geriatric anesthesia.39 Additionally, remimazolam appears to better preserve postoperative neuroendocrine rhythms, including melatonin and cortisol secretion patterns, with associated benefits for sleep quality and recovery. This pharmacological profile correlates with reduced incidence of postoperative delirium in elderly patients (11.7 % vs 26.7 %，P < 0.05).40

Pediatric patients

Emerging clinical evidence supports remimazolam as a viable sedative and general anesthetic for pediatric populations. Its advantageous pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic properties suggest clinical utility across multiple perioperative applications, including intraoperative neuromonitoring and difficult airway management scenarios.41 Comparative studies with propofol demonstrate comparable efficacy in achieving sedation targets, with similar postprocedural recovery times（21.91 ± 10.67 vs 22.53 ± 8.03 min）and PACU durations（23.42 ± 11.86 vs 24.36 ± 10.74 min）.42 Notably, while remimazolam exhibits a slower induction profile, it offers distinct safety advantages including reduced frequencies of injection pain, respiratory depression, and hemodynamic instability (hypotension and bradycardia). Additional benefits include mitigation of preoperative anxiety and decreased incidence of postoperative delirium in pediatric patients.43

A prospective randomized trial by Yang et al.44 specifically examined remimazolam’s effects on emergence delirium following adenotonsillectomy procedures. The intervention group demonstrated both reduced emergence delirium occurrence (12 % vs 44 %, relative risk 0.27, 95 % CI: 0.12 to 0.60; P < 0.001) and lower PAED scale scores compared to controls (7 vs 9, P < 0.001). However, no intergroup differences were observed in postoperative pain levels, PACU stay duration, or behavioral changes during recovery.

Patients undergoing cardiac surgery

Compared to conventional sedatives, remimazolam demonstrates superior hemodynamic stability, making it particularly beneficial for patients with cardiovascular compromise undergoing cardiac procedures. Clinical evidence indicates that remimazolam is effective for both induction and maintenance of anesthesia in cardiac surgery.45 Its use is associated with improved perioperative hemodynamic control, reduced incidence of complications, and enhanced patient outcomes.46

Dosage and use

The manufacturer-recommended dosing protocol for remimazolam specifies an initial induction rate of 12 mg/kg/h followed by a maintenance infusion of 1 mg/kg/h for general anesthesia, administered as a 1 mg/mL solution via infusion pump. Although high-flow pump administration is preferred, manual slow infusion over one minute may serve as an alternative, albeit with potential compromise in dosing precision.47 For procedural sedation in adults, remimazolam requires individualized titration, with Chen et al. developing a customized dosing algorithm incorporating a standard 5 mg IV induction dose over one minute, followed by optional 2.5 mg boluses at ≥ 2 min intervals if needed.48 ASA III/IV patients require dose reduction to 2.5–5 mg for induction and 1.25–2.5 mg for supplementation, with final dosing determined by the anesthesiologist’s clinical assessment. Critical safety protocols mandate administration by sedation-trained personnel with immediate access to emergency equipment, continuous vital sign monitoring throughout the procedure and recovery period, and special vigilance for potential complications including hypoxia, bradycardia and hypotension, as outlined in the prescribing information.

Adverse effects

Extensive clinical studies have established the adverse effect profile of remimazolam during sedation and anesthesia, which mirrors the known pharmacological effects of benzodiazepines. The most frequently reported adverse reactions include hemodynamic alterations (blood pressure and heart rate fluctuations), nausea, and vomiting, with less common occurrences of headache, drowsiness, and hypoxia.

Of particular clinical significance is the potential for severe allergic reactions to remimazolam. A case review of hypersensitivity reactions revealed possible cross-reactivity between remimazolam and midazolam.49 The reported case involved a patient with documented midazolam allergy but tolerance to bromazepam, suggesting a distinct cross-reactivity pattern that differs from conventional benzodiazepines. This finding emphasizes the need for further investigation and accumulated clinical experience to fully characterize remimazolam's safety profile, particularly regarding hypersensitivity reactions.

The antagonism of flumazenil

As a competitive antagonist at the GABA-benzodiazepine receptor complex, flumazenil effectively reverses the sedative-hypnotic effects of both benzodiazepine and non-benzodiazepine compounds including remimazolam. The standard reversal protocol recommends an initial flumazenil dose of 0.2 mg administered via slow intravenous injection over 15 s to minimize potential adverse effects such as hypertension, tachycardia, and anxiety.50 Should the desired level of consciousness not be attained within 60 s post-administration, additional 0.2 mg doses may be repeated at one-minute intervals, with a maximum cumulative dose of 1 mg within a single treatment session. Studies have shown that special caution should be exercised when administering flumazenil to certain high-risk patients. Such populations include patients with psychiatric disorders, those with a high incidence of delirium, and individuals with a history of seizures. The use of flumazenil in these patients may induce withdrawal symptoms and seizure activity,51 likely due to the complex interaction between the pharmacological effects of flumazenil and the neurophysiological changes caused by long-term benzodiazepine administration. This renders the aforementioned population particularly sensitive to the abrupt reversal of drug effects. To prevent such adverse outcomes, a comprehensive assessment of the patient’s medication history and the development of individualized administration strategies are essential. Additionally, growing attention has been paid to other flumazenil-related adverse reactions, such as gastrointestinal disturbances and neurological responses.

So，clinicians should be aware of flumazenil’s competitive antagonism mechanism and pharmacokinetic properties. As plasma concentrations decline following administration, remimazolam’s hypnotic effects may recur due to the drug’s continued presence. The onset of flumazenil’s antagonistic action typically occurs within 1–2 min post-injection, necessitating extended patient monitoring for possible re-sedation, respiratory depression, or other recurrent hypnotic effects throughout the recovery period.

Postoperative recovery

Clinical evidence suggests remimazolam may offer superior postoperative recovery characteristics compared to conventional sedatives. Its favorable recovery profile includes accelerated return of consciousness following procedures,52 reduced incidence of postoperative complications, and decreased requirement for cardiovascular support medications (vasoactive and positive inotropic agents). Additionally, remimazolam demonstrates clinical benefits in oxygenation maintenance with lower rates of hypoxemia, fewer overall adverse events,53 and enhanced quality of recovery metrics.54 Particularly noteworthy is its association with reduced occurrence of delayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR), suggesting potential neurological protective properties.55

Other issues

Physicochemical limitations

Several important physicochemical limitations affect the preparation and clinical use of remimazolam. The drug’s solubility decreases significantly at pH levels exceeding 4.0, resulting in notable incompatibilities with numerous commonly used perioperative medications.56 Sasaki57conducted a systematic study on the dissolution of remimazolam in Ringer’s solution. The results showed that precipitates are formed when remimazolam is dissolved in both lactate-based Ringer’s solution (pH 5.9–6.2) and acetate-based Ringer’s solution (pH 6.0–7.5), and the formation of precipitates is concentration-dependent. Therefore, when concurrent administration with Ringer’s solution is clinically necessary, mitigation strategies can be adopted, including reducing the concentration of remimazolam and increasing the infusion rate, so as to minimize the risk of precipitate formation.

Molecular mechanism

Emerging preclinical evidence suggests remimazolam may possess therapeutic benefits for ischemia-reperfusion injury, mediated through its anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective properties. Experimental studies by Shi et al. demonstrated remimazolam’s ability to mitigate hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury through selective inhibition of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway.58 Complementary research by Wan et al. revealed neuroprotective effects against oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) injury, similarly mediated via modulation of MAPK pathway activity.59

Gene metabolism

The metabolism of remimazolam relies on the hydrolytic action of nonspecific esterases, particularly carboxylesterase 1 (CES1).60 Therefore, genetic variations in CES1 represent a core target influencing individual differences in remimazolam metabolism. Studies have shown that certain gene mutations may lead to reduced CES1 enzymatic activity. Patients carrying CES1 genotypes with diminished function may exhibit decreased clearance of remimazolam and an increase in AUC (area under the curve), resulting in prolonged sedative effects or an elevated risk of adverse reactions. Hence, in the future, genetic testing could be utilized to optimize remimazolam dosage, especially for high-risk populations (such as surgical patients requiring precise sedation and those on multiple drug regimens), providing a basis for precision anesthesia.

Conclusions

This review has certain limitations. Firstly, the clinical studies on remimazolam involved in this review are mostly medium- and short-term observations, lacking data on the safety and efficacy of its long-term application. Secondly, while existing studies have clarified the basic dosage of remimazolam in the general population, no unified standard exists for individualized dosage adjustments in complex patients with multiple underlying diseases, which may increase the risk of under- or overdosing.

In view of the above limitations, future research directions can focus on the following aspects. First, conduct large-sample, multi-center clinical studies in special populations such as elderly patients, children, and patients with hepatic or renal insufficiency, so as to determine the optimal dosage, administration interval, and safety monitoring indicators of remimazolam, thereby providing a foundation for individualized clinical use. Second, design long-term follow-up studies to observe the cumulative adverse reactions of remimazolam in long-term sedative treatment (such as the impact on neurological function and the risk of drug dependence) and define the safety boundary of its long-term application.

In addition, systematic studies can be conducted on the interaction between remimazolam and commonly used clinical drugs to establish a risk assessment model for drug combination, providing a reference for rational clinical drug combination. Moreover, further in-depth studies can be carried out on the specific pathways of the anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects of remimazolam from the perspective of molecular mechanisms, so as to provide a theoretical basis for optimizing its clinical application and developing combined medication regimens.
