Primary hyperoxaluria type 1—current practice in the siRNA era: an ERA Genes & Kidney Working Group survey

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPrimary hyperoxaluria type 1 (PH1) is a rare inherited metabolic disorder leading to the formation of kidney stones, nephrocalcinosis, and kidney failure. Besides, PH1 poses the risk of developing systemic oxalosis, a life-threatening condition with oxalate deposits in multiple organ systems. The rarity of the disorder combined with recent major additions to therapeutic options based on small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapeutics make a formal assessment of current practice and implementation of treatment recommendations an important asset.MethodsAn international questionnaire survey was conducted among medical doctors involved in the treatment of patients with chronic kidney disease. The survey included 32 questions addressing demographics, diagnostics and therapeutics, and educational needs related to the care for PH1 patients.Results176 participants from 43 countries completed the survey, the majority of them were from Europe. The results indicate clear shortcomings in the availability of recommended diagnostics, especially with regards to plasma oxalate. Genetic testing strategies often do not include patients who may have PH1, e.g. when the underlying cause of kidney failure is unknown or in patients with nephrolithiasis or nephrocalcinosis. Treatment modalities are only partly harmonized and intensified dialysis is not fully implemented across centers. Strategies toward combination of conventional therapeutics such as hyperhydration and pyridoxine with new siRNA therapeutics depend on the treating physician’s expertise. The survey identifies clear needs regarding implementation of current treatment recommendations as well as important educational gaps.ConclusionThe advent of targeted treatment opportunities for PH1 comes with an increased need to provide guidance to the field. Filling the existing gaps will ensure that a growing number of patients get access to optimal care and novel life-changing therapies.

INTRODUCTION

The primary hyperoxalurias (PH 1–3) are rare autosomal recessive diseases of glyoxylate metabolism resulting in a hepatic overproduction of oxalate. Continuous hyperoxaluria in the primary range promotes the formation of recurrent and multiple kidney stones, nephrocalcinosis, urinary tract infections, and tissue inflammation, and bears a type-dependent risk of kidney failure (KF). PH type 1 disease (PH1) constitutes the most commonly diagnosed and most severe PH type worldwide. In PH1 patients with declining kidney function, there is a substantial risk of lethal systemic oxalate deposition [1–8].

Early diagnosis is key to optimize the treatment of PH patients, preserve kidney function, and avoid the need for a kidney replacement therapy, in particular in PH1 disease [9]. A first step in the diagnostic pathway is usually the determination of oxalate in the framework of urinary lithogenic profile testing. Genetic testing is the gold standard to confirm the diagnosis but is increasingly used as a first-line diagnostic in pediatric patients presenting with severe nephrocalcinosis and/or KF or adults with chronic kidney disease of unexplained cause (CKDx) [10].

The treatment of PH depends on PH type and kidney function and aims to reduce supersaturation and calcium oxalate crystal formation. For this purpose, hyperhydration to reduce oxalate concentration is recommended in all patients (with uncompromised urine output) [9].

KF in PH1 requires intensive dialysis therapy to minimize systemic oxalosis [9]. While orthotopic liver transplantation cures the congenital metabolic defect, restoration of kidney function is still the only effective measure to improve systemic oxalosis. Combined or sequential liver kidney transplantation is a major procedure and is associated with considerable morbidity and mortality [11, 12].

Supraphysiologic pyridoxine (vitamin B6) treatment was the first targeted treatment option, but is only effective in some AGXT missense variants [13]. In contrast, new targeted RNA interference (RNAi) therapeutics (lumasiran and nedosiran) now allow for repression of endogenous excess production of oxalate irrespective of the AGXT genotype in PH1 patients [14].

In Europe, currently only lumasiran has been EMA approved, while nedosiran is available via a compassionate use program [15, 16]. Although many questions regarding the full therapeutic potential of RNAi-based therapy remain unresolved (e.g. overall renal survival, safety, and outcome of isolated kidney transplantation), these medications have already fundamentally transformed the PH field, best illustrated by calls to include PH1 in genetic newborn screening programs [17] .

On the other hand, these therapies are very expensive, which limits their accessibility for many patients suffering from PH1 worldwide [18]. However, upon availability of the siRNA therapeutics, isolated kidney transplantation might increasingly become possible [19, 20]. Patients with PH1 require highly individualized management and recently updated treatment recommendations provide helpful guidance in this regard [9, 21].

The primary aim of the current survey was to assess the implementation of state-of-the-art diagnostic pathways and therapeutic strategies considering the changing landscape after approval of the siRNA therapeutics [9, 12]. This knowledge provides a good basis to identify topics which require more attention in guideline development and medical education. Importantly, the survey did not only include experts on PH1 but was extended to the wider field of general nephrologists and pediatricians since general awareness of this diagnosis is an important asset.

RESULTS

Characteristics of survey respondents

In total, 176 participants from 43 countries completed the survey (Fig. 1A and B). The majority of responses came from Europe (82%). Most participants were adult nephrologists (56%) or pediatricians/pediatric nephrologists (40%). Further replies came from five urologists, one endocrinologist, and one human geneticist. Although most adult nephrologists treated only adult PH1 patients (71%) and most pediatricians/pediatric nephrologist treated only children and adolescents (89%), 10% and 11%, respectively, treated both age groups (Fig. 1C). About 30% of the survey participants had no experience in treating PH1 patients. The majority of practitioners who treated PH1 patients had managed either <3 patients (∼ 32%) or 3–9 patients (29%) overall. A minority of participants indicated that they had broader expertise in treating these patients (6%: 10–15 patients; 3.4%: >15 patients) (Fig. 1C).

Standard diagnostics and hurdles

Genetic testing is a key investigation in the workup of patients with suspected PH as well as in patients with CKDx [9, 10].

Although the majority of survey participants would perform genetic testing (next-generation sequencing (NGS) gene panel, exome, or genome sequencing) in patients with CKD and clinical findings consistent with PH1, ∼31% responded that they would not initiate genetic testing in patients irrespective of age with CKDx/ KF of unexplained cause (Fig. 2A, subitem B). This also applied to the group of young adults (18–45 years of age) with CKDx, where 13% of the practitioners stated that they do not perform genetic testing in this population (Fig. 2A, subitem A). Besides, a significant proportion of the participants do not initiate genetic testing in patients with CKD/KF and a history of nephrolithiasis (23% of the practitioners; subitem D), nephrocalcinosis (12% of the practitioners; subitem C), or unexplained early graft failure (19% of the practitioners; subitem E) (Fig. 2A).

Furthermore almost 40% of participants do not perform genetic testing in young adults (<45 years of age) when a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus or hypertension is known (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Genetic testing was available for almost 90% of the participants usually as NGS-based panel diagnostics (77%) or targeted Sanger sequencing (14%) (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 2). Sixty-seven percent of the participants confirmed that all three (AGXT, GPHPR, and HOGA1) causative genes are available in their genetic testing (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Notably, routine genetic testing of siblings of PH1 patients is performed by 61% of the treating physicians, while 28% restricted genetic testing to symptomatic siblings (Fig. 2C). A separate analysis of adult and pediatric nephrologists revealed that adult nephrologists, in particular, tend to perform fewer routine testing, especially if siblings are not symptomatic (Supplementary Fig. 7). A comparison of replies from European and non-European countries revealed no significant differences in this question (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Difficulties in access to one of the key diagnostic layers (genetics, urine, and plasma oxalate measurement) were reported by 47% (Fig. 2D). Especially measurement of plasma oxalate was reported as a significant diagnostic gap (24%) by a considerable part of the respondents, followed by access to genetic testing (11%) (Fig. 2D). Moreover, 53% of the participants reported no restrictions here.

After referral, the waiting time for the genetic testing results was mostly 1–4 months (Fig. 2E; <1 month: 12.5%, 1–2 month: 29%, 3–4 month: 38%), while measurement of urinary oxalate (Uox) was reported to take <1 month in most of the centers (76%) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Treatment regimens

The questions regarding treatment strategies for PH1 patients were restricted to colleagues actually treating at least one PH1 patient (124 of the 176 survey participants).

Hyperhydration, alkaline citrate treatment, and pyridoxine were included in the treatment strategy of almost every PH1 patient. RNAi therapeutics are considered as first-line treatment strategy in around half of the patients, while use of stiripentol and probiotics was rarely reported (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 4). RNAi therapeutics were not available to 25% of the survey participants (Fig. 3B). Due to current EMA approval status, lumasiran was mostly prescribed by practitioners with access to RNAi therapeutics, while nedosiran was used by a minority (Fig. 3B).

Upon KF, over 71% of practitioners reported initiation of an intensified dialysis regime, i.e. a significant proportion of practitioners would go for a standard dialysis regimen, even in patients with PH1 (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. 8).

A combined kidney and liver transplantation was preferred by 46% of the participants of this survey, while a sequential approach was favored by 15%. Moreover, 18% voted for an isolated kidney transplantation as preferred choice (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 9). Regarding both the question of dialysis and the transplantation strategy, there were certain differences between adult and pediatric/pediatric nephrologists (Supplementary Figs 8 and 9).

In response to a question, 75% of participants stated that they had knowledge on the pyridoxine susceptibility of AGXT genotypes and take genotype information into account when treating PH1 patients (Fig. 3E). A pyridoxine dose of 5–10 mg/kg body weight was chosen by most physicians (Fig. 3F). Regarding the hyperhydration recommendations, daily volumes of 2–3 and 3–4 l/1.73 m2 were suggested in 44% and 48% of the answers, respectively (Fig. 3G).

Implementation of RNAi medication into the treatment schemes

In this survey, RNAi therapeutics were mostly prescribed after individualized decision making either by the treating physician (47%) or after consulting an external expert panel (18.5%).

Among respondents, 31.4% reported use of RNAi medication as a second-line treatment after insufficient treatment response to pyridoxine and other conventional measures, while 20% of practitioners stated treating all PH1 patients with siRNA therapies (Fig. 4A). After initiation of RNAi therapy, pyridoxine treatment was mostly continued (72%), whereas 21% only continue with pyridoxine if the response to the RNAi therapy alone is not sufficient (Fig. 4B). Here, more experienced (treatment of five or more PH1 patients) physicians were more likely to continue with the pyridoxine treatment, as well as pediatric/pediatric nephrologists in comparison to adult nephrologists (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. 10). Regarding the hyperhydration and alkaline citrate treatment strategy, the more experienced physicians tended to continue with the hyperhydration recommendations without changes but recommended to adjust the alkaline citrate supplementation more often (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. 5).

The majority of practitioners stated to monitor treatment response by a 24-h urine collection (43%) or plasma oxalate levels (20%: in all patients; 13%: in patients with renal failure only) (Supplementary Fig. 6). Spot urine analysis was used much less frequently than 24-h urine analysis.

Educational needs

Although PH1 is an inborn error of hepatic glyoxylate metabolism, the disease is mostly perceived as kidney stone disease in which calcium oxalate deposits contribute to nephrocalcinosis and progressive KF (Fig. 5A and B). Even though primary liver failure had been added as a feature not classically associated with PH1, 23% of the survey participants suspected liver failure to be a manifestation of PH1 (Fig. 5A). Other well-documented extrarenal manifestations of PH1 are an involvement of the bones (contributing to fractures), skin, bone marrow (anemia, pancytopenia), retina, blood vessels, or heart. Knowledge about clinical findings in patients with PH1 was—as expected—dependent on previous experience in the management of these patients (Fig. 5B). There was also a substantial difference between the experience in physicians for considering KF of unknown etiology as sign of potential PH1 as underlying cause of KF.

Overall, there was a broad interest in numerous aspects regarding screening, genetic testing, symptoms, disease manifestations, and treatment of PH1 patients, especially from physicians with less experience regarding this disease (Fig. 5C and D). Educational meetings, e-seminars/webcast, and medical journals are the preferred channels to learn more about diagnostics and treatment of PH1 patients (Fig. 5E).

DISCUSSION

Diagnostics and treatment of patients with PH1 remains a very challenging task requiring expert knowledge about the possible clinical presentation, genetic and nongenetic differential diagnoses, complications, the long-term clinical course, and treatment options of this orphan disease.

Our survey on the current practice and implementation of recommendations in the treatment of PH1 was completed by physicians from all over the world. The main share of responses was from Europe, which is likely due to the fact that European Renal Association (ERA), European Society of Paediatric Nephrology (ESPN), and OxalEurope are based in Europe. This is a limitation of our study, since countries outside of Europe are underrepresented and there are considerable differences in the access to diagnostics and therapeutics worldwide [18]. Furthermore, it is possible that there was a bias toward physicians interested in the topic as these may be more likely to answer the survey. Consequently, the results of our survey may not be readily applicable to the general community of adult and pediatric nephrology. However, the finding that a significant proportion of participants stated they had not treated PH1 to date, points to the fact that not only experts participated.

An important goal of this survey was to identify gaps between current standards and published guidance [9]. An important finding of this survey is, that only just over 60% of the participants would perform routine genetic testing in asymptomatic siblings of PH1 patients. This is concerning in a disease where family members have an “a priori” risk of at maximum 25% to be affected, especially since PH1 is known for its diverse intrafamilial expressivity [22, 23]. This lack in testing is a clear discrepancy in comparison to the recently published clinical practice recommendations issued by ERKNet and OxalEurope (consensus statement number 2: “We recommend offering genetic counselling to patients with PH and their families” [Grading A]) [9].

The diagnostic delay is a key threat to all PH1 patients, especially those patients with a rapid decline of renal function, e.g. children with infantile oxalosis or adults with oligosymptomatic course that show rapid progressive kidney dysfunction [24]. This may underscore a general educational need in assessment of family members at risk in genetic conditions.

A comprehensive, guideline based diagnostic approach is complicated by the fact that about half of the respondents stated that they have difficulty carrying out all recommended diagnostics [9]. In over a fifth of cases, there are significant hurdles to obtain plasma oxalate levels, that get more important when the kidney function declines (consensus statement number 13: “We recommend measuring plasma oxalate levels only in patients with CKD stage 4 or higher.” [Grading A]) [9]. Besides the fact that plasma oxalate measurement is only available in specialized laboratories, various pre-analytical challenges further complicate the availability of reliable and comparable plasma oxalate values [25].

Apart from cases with a known genetic kidney disease, nearly one-third of the respondents refrain from initiating genetic testing when CKD or KF remains unexplained (CKDx) [10], despite the possibility that PH1 or another hereditary kidney disease may underly such presentation. In the context of PH1, a relevant fraction of respondents did not consider the combination of KF and nephrolithiasis/nephrocalcinosis a reason for genetic testing. While this may be justified in a subset of cases, especially nephrocalcinosis or recurrent kidney stones should raise the question of a genetic cause like PH1 or other hereditary kidney stone diseases (e.g. cystinuria), in particular at young age or with a positive family history [26]. A missed diagnosis of PH1 has direct implications on the outcome of kidney transplantations and can lead to immediate loss of the new graft [27]. Importantly, the recent clinical practice recommendations for PH provide a strong recommendation toward genetic testing in patients with high clinical and/or biochemical suspicion of PH1 (consensus statement number 1: “We recommend genetic testing of each patient with high clinical and/or biochemical suspicion of PH.” [Grading A]) [9]. Genetic testing is also crucial to determine if PH1 is due to a pyridoxine sensitive variant [28]. Consequently, even if genetic testing may not be the first step, urinary oxalate assessment should definitely be included in all of such cases. Genetic counseling of the index patient and her/his family is recommended to provide information about PH1, to inform parents of the index patient about the risk of recurrence in future children, and to explain the importance of screening siblings [9]. Ultimately, in every case of CKD, an effort should be made to identify the underlying cause, as this directly influences the treatment strategy, can open up targeted treatment options, and is a decisive factor, particularly in the context of transplantation. Besides, the co-existence of CKD and diabetes mellitus type 2 or arterial hypertension often leads to diagnoses of convenience suppressing the need for further clarification. Especially in young patients with CKD, further testing including genetics appears warranted to clarify the cause of CKD. Since the clinical presentation and associated findings may also be atypical, it is also important to emphasize that comprehensive and broad genetic testing (e.g. exome or genome sequencing) should be pursued whenever possible [29, 30]. In this regard, implementation of the structured framework of the recently published consensus statement on CKDx would be an important asset [10].

To date, treatment of PH1 patients consists of supportive measures such as hyperhydration, alkaline citrate supplementation, and pyridoxine, and the vast majority of participants implement these general measures in line with the current recommendations (clinical practice consensus statements number 15, 16, 18, and 20) [9]. Depending on the treatment response to these basic measures or the presence of risk factors like an impaired kidney function or signs of systemic oxalosis, the addition of RNAi therapies is recommended, if they are available [9]. Interestingly, a significant proportion of participants (20%) report treatment of all PH1 patients with siRNA therapeutics, while the clinical practice recommendations foresee patient selection based on the response to pyridoxine and the urinary oxalate excretion (consensus statements number 42–45) [9]. However, considering these aspects appears to be reasonable in light of the costs associated with therapy and the still limited experience with side effects in the context of long-term (and potentially life-long) therapy.

If there is an indication for dialysis treatment, an intensified regime is recommended (consensus statement number 22: “In situations of no access or response to oxalate-lowering therapies, we recommend intensified hemodialysis, which is dose titrated to the clinical condition and plasma oxalate levels, and in accordance with the tolerance levels of the patient and family.” [Grading X: strong recommendation]). Ideal would be daily hemodialysis sessions with a high-flux filter and maximum blood flow, possibly even combined with peritoneal dialysis, although this can hardly be implemented in everyday life or tolerated by the patients—especially in the long term [9], a strategy, which is implemented by the majority of respondents. Nonetheless, almost 30% of the survey participants chose a standard dialysis regimen or did not specify their preferred dialysis mode. Further emphasis might be necessary here, that even with an intensified dialysis regimen, oxalate cannot be sufficiently eliminated and thus the risk of systemic oxalosis is increased [13]. This is even more true, if a standard dialysis regimen is used.

Regarding the new siRNA treatment choices, it is important to keep in mind that lumasiran was approved by the EMA and FDA in November 2020 for all age groups and without limitations regarding the kidney function. On the other hand, nedosiran is currently only approved by the FDA since September 2023 for patients 9 years of age or older with rather preserved kidney function. To date, nedosiran has no approval by the EMA and is currently available in Europe only in compassionate use programs. This explains the much larger proportion of lumasiran in this survey compared to nedosiran. A recent survey study analyzed the access to diagnostics and therapeutics for PH worldwide [18]. There were significant differences in the access to diagnostics like genetic testing and urine oxalate analysis between low- and high-income countries. There were also great differences in the therapeutic care of patients, especially in specialized and expensive medical procedures such as intensified dialysis regimens, transplants, or RNAi therapeutics [18]. While this previous work focused on the description of the significant differences in the care of PH patients, especially depending on the economic situation of the respective country, the current survey addresses the status quo regarding diagnostic and therapeutic measures in PH1 patients in the light of the current practice recommendations [9].

After siRNA therapy is initiated, continuing previously effective support measures appears reasonable, especially in those with apparent remaining hyperoxaluria under RNAi. This view is also shared by most of the practitioners who participated in our survey. In detail, however, depending on the degree of experience of the practitioner, some centers would indeed discontinue these measures. More experienced colleagues show a greater adherence to stick to the recommended basic treatment measures like hyperhydration and the pyridoxine treatment. On the other hand, the alkaline citrate supplementation, for which the evidence compared to the aforementioned treatments (hyperhydration and pyridoxine) is lower, is more likely to be changed by practitioners who treated at least five PH1 patients.

Before the era of siRNA therapeutics, solely a combined liver and kidney transplantation was a causal treatment option when KF is reached, as long as the patients are not very well respondents to pyridoxine, which could enable an isolated kidney transplantation (consensus statement number 29: “Isolated kidney transplantation should be considered in patients with PH1 and stage 5D CKD who are homozygous for pyridoxine-responsive mutations.” [Grading B]) [9]. Nevertheless, around 18% of the survey participants opted for isolated kidney transplantation in PH1 patients as preferred transplantation strategy. What further considerations are behind this choice was not asked in sufficient detail in the questionnaire, importantly it should be mentioned that it was not possible to enter further information in the questionnaire that might have influenced the decision for an isolated kidney transplantation (e.g. very good pyridoxine or RNAi therapy responsiveness).

However, it should be noted once again that isolated kidney transplantation in PH1 patients should only be considered in patients who do adequately and persistently respond to either pyridoxine or siRNA [31]. Future studies regarding the long-term treatment and outcome with RNAi therapeutics will further inform the transplantation strategies in the future and could be the basis for an increasing number of isolated kidney transplantations under RNAi therapeutics [20].

Regarding the potential systemic manifestations of PH1, the survey highlighted a clear need to broaden education. Misconception and knowledge gaps were identified: for example, 23% of the survey participants suspected liver failure to be a manifestation of PH1. This answer was selected especially by colleagues less experienced in treating PH1 patients. On the other hand, numerous extrarenal manifestations such as bone, skin, or heart involvement were not sufficiently known by practitioners.

Fortunately, there is great interest in further educational content regarding PH1. This is especially true for potential treatment and management recommendations. It should be noted that some of the topics and distribution channels described here as particularly interesting are already being offered by various professional associations, including the ERA G&K Working Group. Here, it should be mentioned that specific treatment decisions (e.g. initiation of siRNA therapies and transplantation strategy) for this very rare disease should be made at specialized expert centers. On the other hand, it is important to emphasize that the basics of the disease and diagnostics should be known in broad practice, if possible, to identify patients and their relatives who might suffer from PH1. The new therapeutic RNAi options play a major role here, improving general interest in very rare diseases through the availability of specific treatment options for the first time. Only patients with a clear diagnosis can benefit from targeted treatment options, and even with the advent of targeted treatment options supportive measures such as hydration or pyridoxine supplementation should not be forgotten.

METHODS

An international questionnaire study was conducted among healthcare providers involved in the care of PH1 patients between March 2024 and July 2024. Responses were gathered using Microsoft Forms and participants were invited by the ERA Working Group Genes & Kidney and OxalEurope through their respective mailing lists. Furthermore, the survey was promoted and distributed by the ESPN Working Group on Inherited Kidney Disease through their mailing lists. The questionnaire aimed at obtaining information on the implementation of both state-of-the-art diagnostic approaches and therapeutic strategies after approval of siRNA therapeutics and included 32 questions addressing demographics, diagnostics and therapeutics, and educational needs related to the care for PH1 patients (see Supplemental Material for the complete questionnaire).

Statistical analyses (chi-square test, a P-value <.05 was considered to be significant) were performed using GraphPad (GraphPad Software, Boston, USA).
