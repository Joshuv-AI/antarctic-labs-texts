Workplace Violence, Burnout, and the Mediating Role of Interprofessional Collaboration Among Nurses in Acute Care: A Scoping Review
Authors: Amrina Rosyadah Beta, Irman Somantri, Maria Komariah, Muhammad Afiif Aziz, Reni Afriana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWorkplace violence (WPV) and occupational burnout among frontline nurses represent systemic crises that fundamentally threaten multidisciplinary team dynamics and global patient safety. This scoping review aims to map and synthesize the empirical evidence regarding the triadic interaction among WPV, burnout, and interprofessional collaboration (IPC) dynamics among nurses in acute care settings.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology and the PRISMA-ScR reporting guidelines. A comprehensive search was performed across three electronic databases (PubMed, EBSCOhost, and ScienceDirect) without publication year restrictions. From an initial 467 records, 13 primary studies involving clinical nursing populations met the eligibility criteria and underwent a descriptive narrative synthesis.ResultsThe findings demonstrate that recurrent exposure to WPV encompassing both external aggression from patients/visitors and internal horizontal violence is directly associated with severe emotional exhaustion and increased turnover intentions. Crucially, IPC serves as the definitive mediator in shaping psychosocial outcomes. Dysfunctional medical hierarchies and professional dominance foster structural disempowerment, exacerbating the destructive cycle of trauma and a pervasive “blame culture”. Conversely, a supportive IPC climate, characterized by collegial teamwork and transparent communication during care transitions (e.g, handoffs), functions as a robust psychological shield that neutralizes the impacts of aggression and enhances mental resilience.ConclusionThis review highlights that IPC acts as a dualistic mechanism, functioning either as a catalyst for systemic stress or a vital buffer against burnout among nurses in acute care settings. To safeguard nurses and optimize patient outcomes, management must decisively pivot from individual-focused resilience training toward systemic organizational restructuring.

Introduction

Workplace violence (WPV) and the escalating burnout syndrome among nursing professionals have emerged as a critical global challenge, fundamentally threatening the sustainability of healthcare delivery systems. Statistical evidence indicates a distressing prevalence; nearly 50% of clinical nurses are estimated to encounter at least one episode of aggression annually.1 Persistent exposure to WPV is no longer viewed merely as an isolated occupational risk. Rather, it is a primary predictor for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and severe burnout that destabilizes entire clinical teams. This cumulative psychological trauma triggers a systemic ripple effect, leading to compromised interdisciplinary patient safety standards,2 professional alienation, and high turnover intentions across the healthcare workforce.3

To mitigate this pervasive psychosocial crisis, strengthening Interprofessional Collaboration (IPC) across medical, nursing, and allied health disciplines has been identified as a pivotal systemic strategy. Theoretically, effective IPC bridges professional gaps, facilitates adaptive role negotiation, and fosters mutual trust, serving as a psychological buffer.4 However, contemporary literature suggests that IPC governance in acute care settings often acts as a double-edged sword. While essential for patient safety, unstructured collaboration dominated by rigid medical hierarchies can paradoxically induce role ambiguity, restrict nursing autonomy, and exacerbate moral distress.5,6 Furthermore, dysfunctional IPC during critical care transitions, such as handoffs, can significantly intensify emotional exhaustion. Under burnout conditions, reduced staff tolerance levels may paradoxically increase the risk of reactive aggression from patients.7

Addressing these intricate multidisciplinary dynamics requires a shift from conventional managerial approaches. Strategies solely focused on building individual cognitive resilience such as merely training nurses’ sense of coherence are now deemed overly reductive and inadequate.8 Consequently, hospital executives and policymakers must urgently pivot toward comprehensive systemic interventions. These imperatives include structural IPC reforms to clarify interdisciplinary role boundaries, the implementation of safe and transparent violence reporting mechanisms, and the cultivation of a multidisciplinary safety culture rooted in mutual respect.9,10

Despite the vocalized need for systemic reform, the triadic interaction between workplace violence, emotional exhaustion, and IPC governance in actual clinical practice remains under-explored. Given that the existing empirical evidence is dispersed across diverse clinical contexts and utilizes highly heterogeneous methodological approaches, a scoping review is the most rigorous methodological approach to map the current landscape and identify these fundamental knowledge gaps. Therefore, this review aims to comprehensively map the empirical evidence regarding the intersection of these three variables. A specific emphasis is placed on understanding how interprofessional disparities can either exacerbate psychological trauma or act as a primary protective factor. The findings are intended to provide a robust conceptual foundation for transforming organizational culture, ultimately ensuring patient safety and fostering a resilient, psychologically safe care ecosystem for all healthcare disciplines.

Methods

Study Design

This study employs a scoping review design to comprehensively explore, map, and synthesize the empirical evidence regarding the intersection of workplace violence, burnout syndrome, and interprofessional collaboration dynamics within clinical nursing settings. This methodology was selected for its efficacy in capturing diverse types of scientific evidence and identifying knowledge gaps within complex, interdisciplinary, and multi-dimensional phenomena.

The conduct of this review strictly adheres to the fundamental methodological framework proposed Arksey & O’Malley (2005) and its subsequent refinements by Levac et al (2010). To maintain scientific rigor across all phases from the formulation of research questions to data extraction all procedures were aligned with the latest Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis.11 Furthermore, to ensure transparency, reproducibility, and standardized reporting quality, the protocol and findings are reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.12 Although the methodology was guided by a rigorous a priori internal protocol to mitigate bias during study selection, it was not prospectively registered in a public database; however, all detailed frameworks and search algorithms are fully documented within this manuscript and its supplementary file 1.

Eligibility Criteria

The selection of studies for this review was governed by strictly predefined (a priori) inclusion and exclusion criteria. Following the JBI methodological guidance for scoping reviews, the PCC (Population, Concept, and Context) framework was utilized to align the search boundaries with the core research questions.

Search Strategy

The literature search strategy was systematically executed adopting the three-step approach recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI). A comprehensive primary search was conducted across three major electronic databases: PubMed, EBSCOhost, and ScienceDirect. The search syntax was iteratively developed by combining standardized subject indexing terminology (eg, Medical Subject Headings [MeSH]) with free-text keywords. These keywords were meticulously mapped using the PCC framework to encompass the target population (clinical nurses), the core concepts (eg, workplace violence, burnout, emotional exhaustion), and the context of team dynamics (interprofessional collaboration). To achieve an optimal balance between retrieval precision and sensitivity, all terms were combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR) and tailored to the specific algorithms of each database, with the complete search strings detailed in Supplementary File 1.

To ensure accurate scientific interpretation, the inclusion was restricted to English-language articles. Crucially, to capture a comprehensive mapping of the theoretical and conceptual evolution of these psychosocial phenomena across healthcare settings, no publication year restrictions were applied. The search was periodically updated until April 2026 to incorporate the most contemporary evidence. Following the retrieval process, all identified records were exported to a reference management software system to facilitate rigorous deduplication prior to the formal screening phase.

Study Selection

The literature selection was executed through a rigorous two-stage screening process. Following the removal of duplicates using Mendeley reference management software (Mendeley Ltd., Elsevier), two reviewers [A.R.B] and [M.A.A] independently screened titles and abstracts to assess their eligibility against the predefined inclusion criteria. Records identified as potentially relevant during this initial phase were then subjected to a comprehensive full-text assessment by the same independent reviewers to determine their final inclusion in the review.

To ensure the reliability of the selection, any discrepancies in assessment between the two primary reviewers were resolved through collaborative discussion until a consensus was reached. In instances where consensus could not be achieved, a third reviewer [I.S] was consulted for final adjudication. The complete selection process, including the specific reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage, is documented transparently and presented in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Figure 1). Data Extraction

Data extraction was performed systematically using a standardized charting instrument adapted from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance for scoping reviews. This tool was designed to consistently capture fundamental information from each selected primary study. The extracted data included bibliographic characteristics (author names, publication year, and country of origin), study objectives, methodological designs, population demographics, and sample sizes. Furthermore, the instrument recorded the specific clinical settings investigated to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the workplace environment and context.

A more profound level of extraction focused on identifying the clinical measurement instruments utilized and drawing thematic findings related to the triadic interaction between workplace violence incidents, burnout indicators, and interprofessional collaboration dynamics. To minimize bias and ensure reliability, data entry into the instrument was conducted independently by the reviewers. The extraction results were subsequently cross-checked to detect any discrepancies and ensure the accuracy of the data retrieved from the original manuscripts. This rigorous approach ensured that all data presented in the summary tables were verified before proceeding to the narrative synthesis analysis.

Data Analysis and Synthesis

In accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodological guidance for scoping reviews, this study did not include a formal risk of bias assessment or a statistical meta-analysis. This decision was necessitated by the high degree of heterogeneity regarding primary study designs, clinical measurement instruments, and the diverse acute care settings represented in the included literature. Instead, the extracted data were analyzed through a descriptive narrative synthesis integrated with a thematic analysis framework to provide a comprehensive aggregation of findings and map the conceptual landscape of the available empirical evidence.

The thematic synthesis categorized key findings into three primary conceptual domains: the clinical manifestations and situational triggers of workplace violence, the escalation mechanisms of burnout among nursing staff, and the dual role of interprofessional collaboration as either a stress catalyst or a protective psychological mediator These mapping results were visualized in a structured data tabulation matrix and elaborated upon narratively to identify relational patterns among variables and highlight critical knowledge gaps. The identification of these gaps serves as a pivotal outcome for establishing an evidence-based foundation for future nursing management policies and developing prospective research agendas.

Results

Study Selection

The initial literature search across the three principal electronic databases yielded a total of 467 records, distributed as follows: PubMed (n=86), EBSCOhost (n=142), and ScienceDirect (n=239). Following deduplication via reference management software, 410 unique records remained and subsequently underwent title and abstract screening. At this stage, 326 records were excluded for failing to meet fundamental eligibility criteria, such as lacking a focus on clinical nursing populations, omitting the intersection of the three core variables, or being classified as review publications. Consequently, 84 potentially relevant manuscripts were advanced to the full-text assessment phase.

During the full-text review, 71 manuscripts were excluded based on strict conceptual and methodological considerations to maintain the rigor of the review. The majority of these exclusions were attributed to non-empirical manuscripts, expert opinions, editorials, brief reports, or grey literature (n=43). Additional manuscripts were excluded for failing to measure team collaboration dynamics (n=6), lacking occupational burnout indicators (n=4), or not focusing on the triadic interaction among the primary variables (n=4). Furthermore, articles were excluded for utilizing non-clinical populations (n=6), involving pre-licensure or academic student samples (n=4), being published in languages other than English (n=3), or representing standalone research protocols (n=1). Following this rigorous, step-wise selection process, a final total of 13 primary studies were deemed eligible for inclusion in the scoping review synthesis. The complete inclusion and exclusion process is visually detailed in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Figure 1).

Study Characteristics

The 13 manuscripts included in this review exhibit a broad geographical distribution, encompassing North America (n=5),13–17 Europe (n=4),18–21 Africa (n=2),22,23 and Asia (n=2).24,25 Methodologically, the majority of the studies employed quantitative approaches (n=8), predominantly utilizing cross-sectional survey designs to measure intervariable correlations. These quantitative findings are complemented by qualitative studies (n=4) employing phenomenological, ethnographic, and semi-structured interview approaches,20–23 as well as one mixed-methods study.25 Sample sizes across the quantitative studies varied significantly, ranging from participants within specific care units to large-scale cohorts involving 3132 staff members at a major academic medical center,13 thereby mapping the phenomenon across diverse organizational scales.

The clinical settings most frequently investigated were predominantly high-stress and high-risk environments, such as Emergency Departments (ED),21,25 Intensive Care Units (ICU),13,16 operating rooms,18 and psychiatric wards.14,22,23 Additionally, several studies encompassed other contexts, including intensive cardiac care units, surgical suites, and oncology wards. The target populations primarily focused on registered nurses, nurse anesthetists, and early-career nurses17,21 cohorts identified as particularly vulnerable to psychosocial impacts within the work environment. Manifestations of Workplace Violence

The literature consistently demonstrates that clinical nurses confront workplace violence originating from two distinct trajectories: external threats and internal aggression. External violence is predominantly perpetrated by patients, their families, or visitors, manifesting in diverse forms ranging from verbal threats to physical assaults. The risk of these incidents tends to escalate in high-intensity care environments, such as emergency departments21,25 and psychiatric wards.14,22 Precipitating factors for external violence are reported to be multifactorial, encompassing unmanaged stress and aggressive behavior from patients and visitors across various hospital settings,13 alongside specific contextual disputes, such as conflicts over visiting hour regulations.20

Beyond external threats, the empirical evidence highlights a high prevalence of internal aggression, frequently conceptualized as horizontal or lateral violence. This aggression is intrinsically linked to dysfunctional dynamics involving peers, other medical professionals (particularly physicians), and hospital administration. Its manifestations encompass a broad spectrum of destructive behaviors. These include incivility within critical and acute care settings,17,18 passive aggression manifested as social isolation among colleagues,23 structural bullying,15,19 and a pervasive blame culture perpetuated by rigid clinical hierarchies and interprofessional conflict.16,24

Impact on Occupational Burnout

The literature consistently confirms that recurrent exposure to workplace violence is directly associated with elevated emotional exhaustion and severe burnout among nursing professionals.14,22 This condition is multifactorial, with its escalation exacerbated when clinical workloads are excessive,13,19 when staff experience structural disempowerment in responding to aggression,15,20 and when there are conflicting clinical care priorities.25 More specifically, these high-risk work environments manifest in extreme clinical anxiety and psychological trauma,21 compassion fatigue,16 and a significant decline in feelings of competence and personal accomplishment.18,23

A profound downstream consequence of unmitigated burnout is the emergence of a nursing retention crisis. Empirical evidence asserts that burnout resulting from violence exposure strongly correlates with increased absenteeism22 and robustly drives turnover intention.20 These impacts are particularly pronounced among vulnerable cohorts, notably early-career clinical nurses who frequently lack adequate coping mechanisms.17 Nevertheless, experienced nurses remain at a highly elevated risk of severe burnout when operating within environments that fail to facilitate safe and supportive conflict resolution.24

Interprofessional Collaboration Dynamics

The synthesized literature indicates that teamwork dynamics play a dualistic role in clinical settings, functioning as both a stress catalyst and a protective mechanism. Empirical evidence consistently demonstrates that rigid medical hierarchies and professional dominance over nursing inhibit equitable collaborative decision-making.20,22,24 This dysfunctional hierarchical environment is often exacerbated by punitive management behaviors, toxic team dynamics resulting from a lack of interprofessional respect,18,23 and a prevalent blame culture between nurses and physicians.16 Consequently, authority imbalances heighten the risk of interprofessional communication failures, particularly in high-pressure clinical situations,15,25 which ultimately intensifies the cycle of workplace violence and staff burnout.

Conversely, the literature underscores that a positive interprofessional collaboration climate acts as a primary protective factor and a robust psychological mediator. Supportive teams and environments that prioritize collegial collaboration are proven to neutralize the destructive impacts of violence, enhance staff mental resilience, and mitigate occupational burnout.14,17,21 At the operational level, the effectiveness of interdisciplinary communication specifically the transparency of information exchange during care transitions or handoffs serves as a critical determinant in preventing miscommunications that could trigger patient aggression or medical errors.13,19

Discussion

This scoping review comprehensively maps the triadic interaction among workplace violence, occupational burnout, and interprofessional collaboration dynamics within clinical acute care settings. The synthesized evidence consistently indicates that exposure to violence, whether originating from external sources (patients and families) or internal dynamics (horizontal aggression), is intrinsically associated with escalated emotional exhaustion and severe burnout. The primary novelty of this review, as conceptualized in Figure 2, lies in the delineation of interprofessional collaboration (IPC) as possessing a dualistic role. Rather than merely serving as a supportive element, team collaboration acts as the definitive mediator that dictates psychosocial outcomes. While rigid clinical hierarchies exacerbate the cycle of psychological trauma, a collegial multidisciplinary climate functions as a robust protective shield that neutralizes the destructive impacts of aggression. An important distinction emerging from this synthesis is the difference between internal and external sources of workplace violence and occupational stress. External stressors primarily arise from patients, families, or visitors and include verbal threats, intimidation, physical aggression, and conflict during emotionally charged care encounters.13,20,22,25 These events are often episodic or incident-based, although their psychological impact can be acute and severe, particularly in high-pressure settings such as emergency departments, psychiatric wards, and intensive care units.21,22,25 In contrast, internal stressors originate within the organization and include horizontal violence, incivility, bullying, hierarchical dominance, blame culture, poor communication, role ambiguity, and weak interprofessional support.15,16,20,23 These internal stressors may be more persistent because they are embedded in daily work routines, professional power relations, and organizational culture.15,16,20,23

Although the heterogeneity of the included studies prevents a quantitative ranking of the relative importance of internal and external stressors, the synthesis suggests that they operate through different but interacting mechanisms. External violence may function as an acute trigger of psychological distress, whereas internal organizational dysfunction may create a chronic vulnerability that shapes nurses’ capacity to anticipate, manage, report, and recover from violent incidents.13,20,22,25 Therefore, disturbed internal functioning may generate a multiplication effect: when psychological safety, interprofessional collaboration, leadership support, and communication systems are weak, nurses have fewer collective resources to de-escalate external aggression and recover from its consequences.13,15,16,20 Conversely, a strong interprofessional collaboration climate may buffer the impact of external violence by enabling shared situational awareness, coordinated responses, peer support, and blame-free reporting.13,14,17,21 This indicates that preventing workplace violence and burnout requires not only controlling patient- or visitor-related aggression but also reforming the internal organizational conditions that amplify its effects.

Within the context of acute care, dysfunctional clinical hierarchies specifically the deeply entrenched dominance of the medical profession over nursing emerge as the principal driver for the proliferation of internal aggression and the reinforcement of a pervasive “blame culture”. These manifestations, ranging from systemic bullying and incivility to social isolation, cannot be adequately dismissed as mere interpersonal coping deficits; instead, they reflect a profound state of structural disempowerment.15,23,24 Aligning with Kanter’s (1993)26 Structural Disempowerment theory, when clinical nurses are systematically deprived of autonomy, equitable voice in patient care decisions, and access to supportive interprofessional resources, they experience profound moral distress. This structural powerlessness forces staff to channel their frustration laterally through destructive power dynamics,6,27 cementing a toxic blame culture that directly accelerates severe emotional exhaustion and occupational burnout. Ultimately, this unprofessional behavior not only dismantles interdisciplinary team functionality but also severely compromises patient safety across diverse global contexts.28,29 Therefore, to effectively mitigate burnout and violence, hospital governance must urgently transcend autocratic leadership models, proactively transitioning toward shared governance and cultivating a strictly blame-free reporting culture to break this systemic cycle.

The accumulation of psychological trauma resulting from chronic violence exposure fundamentally devastates nursing workforce retention. The empirical evidence demonstrates that structural disempowerment in responding to aggression strongly correlates with extreme emotional exhaustion, elevated absenteeism, and fortified turnover intentions, particularly among newly graduated nurses.17,20,22 These findings are corroborated by global meta-analyses confirming that exposure to violence more than doubles a nurse’s risk of experiencing burnout syndrome and Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD).30,31 The long-term depersonalization accompanying these conditions reflects a severe structural crisis, as highlighted by the International Council of Nurses,32 which asserts that the nursing exodus strongly correlates with toxic psychosocial work environments. Consequently, executive strategies must definitively pivot from approaches that primarily emphasize individual cognitive resilience training toward systemic hazard elimination policies. A requisite foundational step is the comprehensive institutionalization of a zero-tolerance policy against violence.33

Crucially, this review underscores that IPC must be understood as a “double-edged sword” during clinical operations, representing both a protective mechanism and a source of systemic vulnerability. A supportive teamwork climate has been proven to significantly neutralize the impacts of violence and mitigate burnout effects.14,21 However, when communication deteriorates due to high care pressures and hierarchical barriers especially during critical phases such as interdepartmental handoffs miscommunication can exponentially trigger patient aggression and exacerbate interprofessional conflicts.13,25 From the perspective of High-Reliability Organizations (HROs), standardizing communication is not merely an administrative task; it serves as the first line of defense in preventing systemic safety incidents.34 Furthermore, while professional boundaries are often perceived as restrictive, clarity in role expectations is essential to prevent team inefficiency.35 Thus, clinical leaders must mandate structured communication protocols, such as the multidisciplinary SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) method, to ensure information transparency and suppress the potential for aggression originating from care transition phases.

In line with the multidisciplinary scope of this journal, it is imperative to state that the findings and strategic recommendations delineated in this study are applicable to healthcare delivery provided by at least two disciplines. Specifically, the proposed structural reforms, shared governance models, and standardized communication protocols necessitate the active, symmetrical engagement of both the nursing and medical professions to successfully foster a psychologically safe, highly reliable, and collaborative care environment.

Implications for Healthcare Management and Multidisciplinary Policy

The transformation of clinical management must decisively pivot from obsolete paradigms that emphasize individual cognitive resilience training toward robust, systemic organizational restructuring. Relying primarily on clinical nurses to inherently “build resilience” against escalating workplace violence is fundamentally reductive and unsustainable. The findings of this review confirm that horizontal aggression and interprofessional collaboration failures are not merely interpersonal coping deficits, but tangible manifestations of pervasive structural disempowerment within clinical settings.20,24 Consequently, the primary strategic mandate for hospital executives and policymakers is the comprehensive institutionalization of a zero-tolerance policy against all forms of violence, critically underpinned by a strictly blame-free reporting culture to dismantle entrenched cycles of interprofessional subordination.16 Restructuring anonymous and secure reporting channels is a non-negotiable prerequisite to ensure that all healthcare staff feel legally and psychologically protected when confronting unprofessional behavior.

At the operational level, the institutionalization of structured communication protocols is urgently required as the first line of defense in maintaining staff psychological stability and ensuring uncompromised patient safety. Given that our synthesized evidence identifies communication failures during critical phases such as interdepartmental care transitions or handoffs as exponential triggers for patient aggression and interprofessional friction,13,19 clinical leadership is compelled to adopt High-Reliability Organization (HRO) principles. This paradigm shift must be operationalized through the multidisciplinary implementation of standardized instruments, specifically the SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) framework.34 This application not only enhances the precision of clinical handoffs but also mandates a more equitable communicative exchange across disciplines, effectively neutralizing the hierarchical dominance that frequently obstructs collaborative practice.

Furthermore, human resource management must directly address the workforce retention crisis through collective capacity building. Organizations should institutionalize cross-disciplinary mentorship programs explicitly tailored for early-career nurses, who are disproportionately vulnerable to violence-induced turnover.17,21 Simultaneously, frontline nurses must be actively integrated into shared governance models. Symmetrical engagement between nursing and medical staff in formulating occupational safety policies is vital for restoring professional autonomy and interdisciplinary trust. Ultimately, these targeted systemic interventions will cultivate a psychologically safe ecosystem, sustainably suppressing burnout and elevating the overall quality of collaborative care.

Knowledge Gaps and Future Research

This scoping review identifies significant methodological gaps. Currently, empirical evidence is predominantly characterized by cross-sectional designs, which inherently limit causal inferences regarding the chronic progression from workplace violence to severe burnout. Consequently, future interdisciplinary research must prioritize longitudinal designs and interventional studies, such as Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs), to rigorously evaluate the systemic effectiveness of collaborative instruments including multidisciplinary SBAR protocols and anonymous reporting systems in mitigating clinical violence.

Furthermore, a discernible geographical disparity exists, as the majority of studies focus on Western healthcare systems. To address this, there is a profound need for mixed-methods approaches rather than purely qualitative explorations in developing nations. Utilizing mixed-methods designs will allow researchers to comprehensively measure both the statistical prevalence of violence and explore the nuanced local cultural values that influence reporting behaviors and interprofessional collaboration efficacy. From an organizational perspective, existing research disproportionately emphasizes individual psychological impacts rather than systemic outcomes. There is an urgent need to integrate organizational science to evaluate the long-term, cross-disciplinary effectiveness of zero-tolerance policies and shared governance models on overall healthcare productivity and retention. By expanding the focus toward these systemic interventions and embracing cultural diversity, healthcare management can foster a proactive cultural transformation that ensures a psychologically safe environment across all disciplines.

Strengths and Limitations

While this scoping review strictly adheres to a rigorous methodological framework, several limitations must be acknowledged. In accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance for scoping reviews, a formal risk of bias assessment and statistical meta-analysis were not conducted due to the significant heterogeneity observed across the primary studies’ methodological designs, clinical settings, and measurement instruments. While the absence of a formal quality appraisal implies that clinical recommendations derived from these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution, it is crucial to reiterate that the fundamental objective of this study is the broad conceptual mapping of the literature, not the evaluation of intervention efficacy. Furthermore, because the measurement diversity of specific psychosocial outcomes across the primary studies explicitly required a narrative synthesis rather than a meta-analysis, this descriptive approach serves as the most robust methodological standard for navigating the current evidence landscape.

Furthermore, restricting the literature search to English-language publications ensures linguistic precision but may introduce publication bias by potentially overlooking critical empirical evidence published in local languages, particularly from non-Western regions. Additionally, the predominance of cross-sectional designs in the synthesized literature restricts the ability to establish definitive long-term causal relationships among workplace violence, occupational burnout, and interprofessional collaboration dynamics.

Despite these constraints, a primary strength of this study is its comprehensive, multidisciplinary mapping of the triadic interaction, moving beyond isolated variable analyses. By deliberately omitting publication year restrictions, this review successfully captured a holistic evolution of the conceptual landscape. Finally, the strict adherence to PRISMA-ScR reporting standards ensures the transparency and reproducibility of the selection process, providing a robust, evidence-based foundation for systemic organizational reforms in healthcare management.

Conclusion

Workplace violence and the resultant epidemic of occupational burnout represent critical systemic threats to global healthcare delivery, fundamentally transcending traditional disciplinary boundaries. This scoping review comprehensively illuminates the intricate dynamics of this crisis, revealing that interprofessional collaboration acts as the definitive mediator in shaping psychosocial outcomes across the healthcare workforce. While rigid clinical hierarchies and professional dominance perpetuate structural disempowerment and destructive trauma cycles, a cohesive, multidisciplinary team climate functions as a vital psychological shield.

This study underscores that mitigating burnout is no longer an individual coping challenge, but an urgent organizational necessity requiring a profound shift in leadership and governance. To safeguard frontline staff and ensure uncompromising patient safety, healthcare institutions must definitively transcend autocratic leadership models. The symmetrical implementation of shared governance, the strict enforcement of zero-tolerance policies against aggression, and the standardization of cross-disciplinary communication protocols are no longer optional initiatives they are urgent operational imperatives. By fostering a psychologically safe, highly reliable, and collaborative ecosystem, healthcare systems can effectively mitigate burnout, secure multidisciplinary staff retention, and ultimately elevate the global quality of patient care.
