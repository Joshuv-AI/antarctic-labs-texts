Temporally separated pollen data track environmental changes in Northwestern Yunnan, Southwest China
Authors: Yi-Feng Yao, Hui Zeng, Zi-Yu Wang, Alexandra H Wortley, Yu-Fei Wang, Stephen Blackmore
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurface pollen studies are extensively used to explore the relationships among pollen, vegetation, climate and human activities. However, whether observed differences in pollen assemblages at the same locality accurately reflect changes in vegetation, climate and human activities over time remains poorly investigated.ResultsHere, we present pollen assemblages from twenty-eight modern samples (soil and moss polsters) taken 17 years apart (2005–2022) at Wenhai and Haligu in northwestern Yunnan, Southwest China, to track environmental changes. We found that the changes in the relative abundance of Abies and evergreen Quercus pollen are potential indicators of climatic change, and an increase in Artemisia pollen and spores of terrestrial ferns such as Osmundaceae, together with a decrease in spores of epiphytic ferns such as Polypodiaceae, are strong signals of human activity in the area. In addition, a comparison of overall palynological richness suggested slightly greater changes at Wenhai than at Haligu.ConclusionOur study reveals the possible influence of climate change and human activities on vegetation, provides an easy-to-use field methodology to support rapid assessments of relatively short-term changes in biodiversity, and suggests a role for long-term comparative studies of surface soil samples in monitoring site-specific vegetation changes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07494-7.Keywords: Biodiversity, Climate change, Human activity, Palaeoecology, Pollen, Vegetation

Background

Pollen analysis is an effective tool for reconstructing past vegetation and climate [1–4]. Investigating modern pollen deposition processes and establishing pollen–vegetation–climate relationships provide the premise and basis for the objective reconstruction of vegetation and climate in the geological past, which is central to the field of palaeoecology. To date, many studies of this kind have been undertaken around the world [5–8].

Within China, surface pollen studies cover a large area of the country, with climate zones including tropical and subtropical regions [9–15], temperate regions [16, 17] and alpine regions [18–23]. The represented vegetation types range from forests [24] and grasslands [25–27] to deserts [28–31]. However, such studies in southern China remain much scarce, resulting in relatively insufficient data about the pollen-vegetation-climate relationships from this region [32].

In general, previous studies have concentrated mainly on the relationships among pollen, vegetation, climate and human activities by analysing surface samples. However, comparisons of surface pollen assemblages via samples collected at the same locality in different years have received little attention [14], particularly regarding whether or not variations in pollen assemblages can reflect changes in regional vegetation, climate and human activities. Research in this regard can support rapid assessments of relatively short-term changes in biodiversity in response to anthropogenic activities and climate change, including species presence and relative abundance, providing easy-to-use field methodology compared with monitoring methods such as vegetation plot surveys, which are time-consuming and require taxonomic expertise, and metabarcoding/environmental DNA (eDNA) approaches, which require high-tech equipment.

Here, we investigated 28 modern samples (including soil and moss polsters) (Additional file 1: Table S1) collected from Wenhai and Haligu at the southern end of the Jade Dragon Snow Mountain, northwestern Yunnan, China, in 2005 and 2022 (Figs. 1 and 2), compared the pollen assemblages of these samples, and attempted to track environmental changes. This study provides a test of pollen sampling as a potential alternative method for the rapid assessment of vegetation change, as well as baseline data for evaluating the influence of future climate change and land use on vegetation in southwestern China.

Results

At Wenhai, a total of 8305 pollen grains and spores were counted across seven samples in 2005, and 50 palynomorphs were identified, which were assigned to 28 families and eight genera of angiosperms, three genera of gymnosperms, nine families and one genus of ferns, and one genus of algae. The pollen assemblage (Fig. 3, Additional file 2: Fig. S1) is dominated by coniferous taxa (average relative abundance (RA): 87.08%), including Pinus (80.66%), Abies (4.96%) and Tsuga (1.46%). Broad-leaved taxa accounted for 7.45%, among which evergreen Quercus (1.90%) was the most abundant. Fern spores accounted for 4.56%, with Polypodiaceae (2.75%) and Loxogramaceae (0.55%). The RA of algae was 0.90%.

Diagram showing changes in the relative abundance of major palynomorphs from the samples taken at Wenhai and Haligu in 2005 and 2022

In 2022, a total of 3224 pollen grains and spores were recovered from six samples at Wenhai, and 36 taxa were identified, which were assigned to 18 families and 11 genera of angiosperms, three genera of gymnosperms, and four families of ferns. The pollen assemblage (Fig. 3, Additional file 2: Fig. S1) is still dominated by coniferous taxa (69.32%), but this represents a large decrease in relative abundance compared with that in the 2005 samples, mainly due to a notable decrease in Pinus (65.29%) and Abies (2.70%) pollen. Broad-leaved taxa accounted for 20.13%, which was significantly greater than that in 2005, characterized by increases in evergreen Quercus (5.49%), Artemisia (4.78%), Cyperaceae (1.77%) and Polygonaceae (1.83%) pollen. Fern spores accounted for 10.27%, which was also higher than that in 2005, and included distinct increases in Osmundaceae (5.43%), Hymenophyllaceae (2.39%) and Polypodiaceae (1.09%).

At Haligu, a total of 7832 pollen grains and spores were obtained from seven samples collected in 2005, and 33 palynomorphs were identified, which were assigned to 17 families and five genera of angiosperms, three genera of gymnosperms, seven families and one genus of ferns. The pollen assemblage (Fig. 3, Additional file 2: Fig. S1) is characterized by high abundance of coniferous taxa (90.12%), including Pinus (84.60%), Abies (4.69%), and Tsuga (0.83%). Broad-leaved taxa accounted for only 6.78%, dominated by Ericaceae (2.71%) and evergreen Quercus (1.85%). Fern spores accounted for 3.10%, with Athyriaceae (1.39%) and Polypodiaceae (0.56%).

In 2022, a total of 4243 pollen grains and spores were counted in eight samples from Haligu, and 37 taxa were identified, assigned to 20 families and eight genera of angiosperms, three genera of gymnosperms and six families of ferns. The pollen assemblage (Fig. 3, Additional file 2: Fig. S1) is strongly dominated by coniferous taxa (72.45%), as was the case for samples from 2005, with notable decreases in Pinus (70.12%) and Abies (1.23%) populations. Broad-leaved taxa accounted for 26.26%, with a distinct increase in evergreen Quercus (16.47%) pollen. Fern spores made up 1.20% of those sampled.

The number of pollen types in samples, i.e., palynological richness [33], is a simple and widely used proxy for vegetation diversity [34, 35]. In this study, rarefaction analysis (Additional file 3: Fig. S2) revealed that the palynological richness of the Wenhai samples in 2022 (average 21.56) was greater than that in 2005 (18.85) at a sample size of 511; in contrast, the richness of the Haligu samples in 2022 (15.39) was slightly greater than that in 2005 (12.80), with a sample size of 501 (Fig. 4). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed a statistically significant difference between the palynological richness of the Wenhai 2005 and Haligu 2005 samples (P = 0.02394, P < 0.05), as did the Wenhai 2022 and Haligu 2022 samples (P = 0.006107, P < 0.01) (Additional file 4: Table S2). The richness at Haligu was generally much lower than that at Wenhai, probably because more weedy species at Wenhai were more likely to have wind-dispersed pollen that collected from the samples.

Discussion

By comparing the surface pollen assemblages at Wenhai and Haligu in 2005 and 2022, four main features were recognized:

(1) The RAs of Pinus (Wenhai: 71.68% vs. 65.30%, Haligu: 77.21% vs. 69.93%) and Abies (Wenhai: from 5.29% to 2.70%, Haligu: 7.85% to 1.24%) pollen decreased in the samples from both sites (Fig. 5a and b), whereas evergreen Quercus pollen increased (Wenhai: 1.97% to 5.48%, Haligu: 2.08% to 16.57%) (Fig. 5c).

Changes in the relative abundance of six selected palynomorphs recovered from samples from Wenhai and Haligu in 2005 and 2022

(2) The pollen RAs of human-associated plants such as Artemisia, Cyperaceae, and Polygonaceae increased in the Wenhai samples, particularly for Artemisia (from 0.52% to 4.80%) (Fig. 5d).

(3) Among fern spores, Osmundaceae sharply increased in the Wenhai samples by 2022 (from zero to 5.47%) (Fig. 5e), whereas Polypodiaceae decreased distinctly (from 6.86% to 1.08%) (Fig. 5f).

(4) A comparison of overall palynological richness suggested slightly greater changes at Wenhai (from 18.85 to 21.56) than at Haligu (from 12.80 to 15.39).

The above differences may be explained by climate change, changing (increasing or decreasing) human activities, or both:

(1) Climate change: Data from the Lijiang Meteorological Station from 1951 to 2010 (altitude 2393 m) [22] show a significant upwards trend in the mean annual temperature (MAT) over the 60 years, with a warming rate of approximately 0.15℃/decade and a slight increasing trend in the mean annual precipitation (MAP) (Additional file 5: Figs. S3a, S3b). Data for the most recent decade (2013‒2022) from the Lijiang meteorological observation field (altitude 3266 m) [36], located on the southern slope of the Jade Dragon Snow Mountain, show that MAT has also increased, but the change in MAP was not significant (Additional file 5: Figs. S3c, S3d). Rising temperatures in the region have led to the melting and retreat of Glacier No. 1 (a temperate glacier) in the Baishui River Basin in the Jade Dragon Snow Mountain, especially since the mid-20th century, which has shown an accelerating retreat trend [37, 38].

The warming climate in Lijiang may have caused an upwards shift in the timberline in the Jade Dragon Snow Mountain, changes in habitats, vegetation patterns (Fig. 6), and plant phenology. Abies is one of the dominant components of subalpine/alpine coniferous forest vegetation in southwestern China and is represented mainly by Abies ernestii var. salouenensis, Abies forrestii, Abies georgei and Abies georgei var. smithii. These cold-tolerant firs are well adapted to grow in these southwestern mountainous areas, with MAT requirements of 2‒8℃ and MAP requirements of approximately 600 mm [39, 40], and are extremely sensitive to temperature changes [41]. As the temperature in Lijiang increases, the range of suitable habitat for Abies populations may decrease. The RA of Abies in the surface pollen assemblages of both Wenhai and Haligu in 2005 was greater than that in 2022 (Fig. 5b), which may be due to the shrinking suitable habitat for fir species due to climate warming.

Vegetation patterns in 2005 (a) and 2022 (b) at Wenhai inferred from the distinct changes in dominant palynomorphs

In contrast, Quercus, particularly the evergreen sclerophyllous species, displays considerable ecological adaptability and can grow in either dry or humid environments. This genus is usually widely distributed near the fog zone (with relatively high humidity, approximately 3100 m) in the Jade Dragon Snow Mountain. Some scrubby oak trees can extend in distribution to an upper limit of approximately 3800 m. In recent years, with the rise in temperature and slight increase in precipitation observed at Lijiang, conditions may have become more favourable for the growth of thermophilic oak plants (e.g., Quercus aquifoliides and Quercus guyavaefolia). This would explain why the RA of evergreen Quercus pollen in the assemblages of the two sites in 2005 was lower than that in 2022 (Fig. 5c).

(2) Human activity: Wenhai was once an important stop on the ancient ‘Tea- Horse Road’ –historically, it was the only passable route between the major settlements of Lijiang and Diqing. Currently, Wenhai is a gathering place for ethnic minorities such as the Naxi and Yi. In the past two decades, the region has undergone great changes (Fig. 2). For example, the original dirt road to Wenhai has been transformed into a cement one, making transportation more convenient and enabling tourism to develop rapidly. Our discussions with local people in 2005 and 2022 also indicated a change in the materials used for house- construction, from a timber structure to a brick and tile structure. Although timber is less frequently used for housebuilding practices in 2022 than in 2005, the local people of the Naxi and Yi ethnic minorities still make heavy use of pine timber for charcoal production and fencing. The vegetation around Wenhai is composed mostly of Pinus armandii forest (below 2700 m), Pinus yunnanensis forest (below 3100 m), Picea likiangensis forest (3100–3300 m), and Abies georgei forest (3100–3900 m) [42]. Assuming that building materials are usually obtained nearby, the slight decrease in the RA of Pinus in the Wenhai and Haligu surface pollen assemblages may be an indicator of this human activity or the superimposed result of both human impact and climate change.

The distribution of economically useful plant species is strongly influenced by human activities [43, 44]. Around human settlements, pastures, and croplands, human activities tend to lead to an increase in the pollen of ‘weed’ species [45]. For example, the RAs of pollen from herbaceous taxa such as Artemisia (Fig. 5d), Cyperaceae and Polygonaceae increased in this study, which may be indicators of increased agriculture, disturbance and construction of roads, and increased tourism around Wenhai. Particularly, Artemisia is a common plant in grazing land, and its population density increases with the intensified grazing pressure. Based on our personal observations, grazing activities are becoming increasingly frequent in Wenhai village, which has led to the occurrence of abundant Artemisia plants.

The change in the RA of fern spores is also a likely indicator of human activities. Two fern groups observed to have changed noticeably in our samples are used by humans: Osmundaceae are commonly consumed as vegetables as their curly and tender leaves are also rich in nutrients, whereas Polypodiaceae are often used in traditional Chinese medicine. In addition, Osmundaceae are perennial terrestrial ferns that usually grow in the forest understorey, whereas Polypodiaceae are chiefly epiphytes of the forest canopy. As the canopy and trunks of trees have a strong filtering effect [46], they reduce the scope for spore dispersal in the forest. However, when degraded by human activity, a more open forest with decreased canopy density is likely more conducive to the dispersal of Osmundaceae spores but less favourable to the attachment and growth of Polypodiaceae (Fig. 6). This may explain why, in the Wenhai samples, the RA of Osmundaceae spores in the assemblage significantly increased, whereas the RA of Polypodiaceae spores decreased (Fig. 5e and f).

Conclusion

In summary, the comparison of modern surface pollen assemblages taken at the same sites almost two decades apart reflects the possible impacts of both climate change and human activities on vegetation in the study area. We highlight changes in the RAs of Abies and evergreen Quercus pollen, which are potential indicators of climate change, and increases in Artemisia pollen and terrestrial fern spores such as Osmundaceae, together with a decrease in epiphytic fern spores such as Polypodiaceae, as signals of increasing human activity in the area.

This work indicates that long-term comparative studies of surface samples, which are simple to collect in the field, can provide useful methods for monitoring vegetation changes at a particular locality without the need for detailed in situ ecological vegetation surveys. We intend to make future comparisons at the same sites to explore this possibility further.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study sites, Wenhai (26°59′48″ N, 100°10′10″ E, altitude: 3089 m) and Haligu (27°00′12″ N, 100°10′50″ E, 3277 m), are situated at the southern end of the Jade Dragon Snow Mountain in Yulong County (Figs. 1 and 2), northwestern Yunnan, southwestern China, approximately 23 km from northwest Lijiang. Wenhai village is located on the side of an enclosed ice-scour lake, covering an area of c. 0.16 km2 and is recharged hydrologically by rainfall and glacial meltwater from the surrounding mountains [47]. It is home to several ethnic minorities such as Naxi and Yi. Currently, the population of the village is approximately 1000. Historically, the village was an important stop on the ancient ‘Tea-Horse Road’ before 1000 AD, which was a trading route between inland agricultural and remote nomadic regions [48].

Haligu is about 2 km awayfrom Wenhai village. Owing the exceptionally rich floristic diversity there and its conservation value, Haligu is protected within the Lijiang Alpine Botanic Garden, which was constructed in 2001. Haligu is also the site of the Forest Biodiversity Observation Field Station of the Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences, which was developed into a National Observation and Research Station in 2021. Significant developments have occurred at the site during the past two decades, including the construction of several greenhouses, a meteorological station, a laboratory, and a germplasm bank.

The two study sites are influenced mainly by the southwest monsoon from the Indian Ocean; therefore, summers are warm and humid, and winters are cool and dry. The rainy season is from May to October, and the dry season is from November to April. The MAT and MAP are 8.9℃ and 935.8 mm, respectively. The vegetation of the Jade Dragon Snow Mountain area shows a distinct vertical zonation. From 2400 m to 3000 m, the vegetation is dominated by semi-humid evergreen broad-leaved forest–pine forest. Between 3000 m and 3300 m, the vegetation is replaced by needle- and broad-leaved mixed forest and sclerophyllous evergreen broad-leaved forest. At altitudes of 3300–4200 m, cold-temperate needle-leaved forest flourishes. Alpine heath scrubs and meadows are distributed above 4200 m [49]. By pollen analysis, we have an understanding of how the vegetation around the Wenhai Lake catchment changed on longer time scales during the late Quaternary [47]. Oaks, pines, rhododendrons and diverse herbs dominate the modern vegetation of the area. With the increase in human activities in recent years, land has been reclaimed from the lake for farming and grazing, and logging leaves some patches bare of natural vegetation (Fig. 2) [47]. The cultivated land covers an area of approximately 400 acres around Wenhai village. The main crops grown locally are potatoes (Solanum tuberosum L.), barley (Hordeum vulgare var. coeleste L.), oats (Avena sativa L.), and Brassica rapa L. In addition, Chinese medicinal herbs such as Paris polyphylla Sm. are planted. Palynology has also provided insights into the development of agriculture in the locality.

Sampling and pollen analysis

In January 2005, seven samples were collected from each site (Wenhai and Haligu). Seventeen years later (July 2022), six and eight samples were obtained from these two localities, respectively (Fig. 1, Additional file 1: Table S1).

For pollen analysis, 20 g of each soil sample was processed via the heavy liquid flotation method [50] via a mixture of hydroiodic acid and potassium iodide with a specific gravity of 1.9. The moss samples were immersed in water for 12 h, filtered, centrifuged and subjected to heavy liquid flotation similar to that of the soil samples. At least 500 pollen grains and spores (terrestrial pollen >300) from each sample (excluding 2005–Wha-3, 2005–Wha-5, 2005–HLGM-1, 2005–HLGM-2) were counted under a Leica DM 2500 light microscope at ×400 magnification. Palynomorphs were identified by comparison with palynological monographs [51–53]. The spores and pollen grains were allocated to four categories: coniferous pollen, broad-leaved pollen (including trees and herbs), fern spores and algae spores. The RA of each pollen taxon was calculated as RA = N/Nt × 100 (N, number of each type in the sample; Nt, total sum of pollen grains and spores in the sample).

Determination of palynological diversity

To compare the palynological diversity of the samples from 2005 to 2022, we used palynological richness, the total number of palynomorphs found in each sample (including ferns and aquatics), to represent the diversity of each sample. In most cases, pollen and spores are identified only at the family or genus level, so each pollen type may represent more than one plant taxon, meaning that the actual plant species diversity in modern vegetation is greater than that estimated from pollen data. To avoid artefacts due to differing total pollen counts [54], calculations were limited to 511 and 501 grains or spores (the number found in the smallest sample) per sample from Wenhai and Haligu, respectively. Rarefaction analysis [55] was used to compare the palynological richness of the samples collected in 2005 and 2022. Individual rarefaction curves and boxplots were created in PAST 3.15 (PAleontological statistics) to show variation in richness.
