Associations between Changes in Exposure to Air Pollutants due to Relocation and the Incidence of 14 Major Disease Categories and All-Cause Mortality: A Natural Experiment Study
Authors: Ge Chen, Zhengmin (Min) Qian, Junguo Zhang, Xiaojie Wang, Zilong Zhang, Miao Cai, Lauren D. Arnold, Chad Abresch, Chuangshi Wang, Yiming Liu, Qi Fan, Hualiang Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Though observational studies have widely linked air pollution exposure to various chronic diseases, evidence comparing different exposures in the same people is limited. This study examined associations between changes in air pollution exposure due to relocation and the incidence and mortality of 14 major diseases.Methods:We included 50,522 participants enrolled in the UK Biobank from 2006 to 2010. Exposures to particulate matter with a diameter ≤2.5μm (PM2.5), particulate matter with a diameter ≤10μm (PM10), nitrogen oxides (NOx), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and sulfur dioxide (SO2) were estimated for each participant based on their residential address and relocation experience during the follow-up. Nine exposure groups were classified based on changes in long-term exposures due to residential mobility. Incidence and mortality of 14 major diseases were identified through linkages to hospital inpatient records and death registries. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incidence and mortality of the 14 diseases of interest.Results:During a median follow-up of 12.6 years, 29,869 participants were diagnosed with any disease of interest, and 3,144 died. Significantly increased risk of disease and all-cause mortality was observed among individuals who moved from a lower to higher air polluted area. Compared with constantly low exposure, moving from low to moderate PM2.5 exposure was associated with increased risk of all 14 diseases but not for all-cause mortality, with adjusted HRs (95% CIs) ranging from 1.18 (1.05, 1.33) to 1.48 (1.30, 1.69); moving from low to high PM2.5 areas increased risk of all 14 diseases: infections [1.37 (1.19, 1.58)], blood diseases [1.57 (1.34, 1.84)], endocrine diseases [1.77 (1.50, 2.09)], mental and behavioral disorders [1.93 (1.68, 2.21)], nervous system diseases [1.51 (1.32, 1.74)], ocular diseases [1.76 (1.56, 1.98)], ear disorders [1.58 (1.35, 1.86)], circulatory diseases [1.59 (1.42, 1.78)], respiratory diseases [1.51 (1.33, 1.72)], digestive diseases [1.74 (1.58, 1.92)], skin diseases [1.39 (1.22, 1.58)], musculoskeletal diseases [1.62 (1.45, 1.81)], genitourinary diseases [1.54 (1.36, 1.74)] and cancer [1.42 (1.24, 1.63)]. We observed similar associations for PM10 and SO2 with 14 diseases (but not with all-cause mortality); increases in NO2 and NOx were positively associated with 14 diseases and all-cause mortality.Conclusions:This study supports potential associations between ambient air pollution exposure and morbidity as well as mortality. Findings also emphasize the importance of maintaining consistently low levels of air pollution to protect the public’s health. https://doi.org/10.1289/EHP14367

Introduction

Ambient air pollution plays an important role in increasing morbidity and mortality worldwide.1,2 It was estimated that ∼6.7 million deaths were attributable to air pollution exposure in 2019.1 The adverse effects of air pollution on respiratory3,4 and cardiovascular systems2,5,6 are well established. Recent studies also observe the effects of air pollution on certain diseases of the digestive, skeletal, metabolic, and urinary systems, such as peptic ulcer bleeding,7 bone loss and bone fracture,8 diabetes9 and chronic kidney disease.10 However, some of this evidence is limited or inconclusive, warranting further research to better characterize the associations.

Although the concentrations of ambient air pollution have decreased in developed countries over the past decades, recent studies indicate that the detrimental health effects of air pollution persist even in regions with air pollution levels below the current World Health Organization Air Quality Guideline.11–13 This suggests that further reducing the level of air pollution could lead to additional benefits for morbidity and mortality. There is growing interest in estimating improvements in health-related indicators such as mortality and cardiovascular and respiratory systems due to actual reductions in air pollution.

Despite the well-documented adverse effects of air pollution and the benefits of improved air quality at the population level, fewer studies have investigated the impacts of improved air quality from individual-level changes such as residential moves to cleaner locations.14,15 For example, Pope et al.16 evaluated the changes in life expectancy associated with differential changes in estimated exposure to ambient fine particulate matter that occurred in the United States. The study primarily relied on ecological data, which would not reflect the individual experience accurately. While previous studies did examine the individual-level changes in ambient particulate matter pollution, they were criticized for relying mainly on hypothetical changes in exposure to infer the dose–response relationship rather than assessing actual changes in exposure.17,18 Further criticism was that they were observational studies in nature, and while they adjusted for confounders, inferring causality remains challenging due to potential residual confounding.15,19–22 For these reasons, alternative study designs that provide evidence to strengthen the argument for causality are needed.

One potential alternate study design23 is a natural experiment that allows for assessment of air pollution changes due to relocation. This approach is especially interesting, as it allows for the study of the effect of a sudden change in exposure. With some rare exceptions, drastic exposure changes often do not exist in daily life. The use of natural experiments to study the impact of environmental factors on health has gained interest in recent years,24 although research in this area remains limited. While individuals may choose to relocate based on various unmeasured social and economic factors,25 residential relocation studies allow for the examination of changes in exposures and outcomes within individuals over a specific period of time.26 This may help to address the issue of bias and control for time-invariant confounding.27–29 Therefore, natural experiment design has the potential to provide stronger evidence for causality. However, only a small number of studies have utilized residential relocation designs to investigate the impact of changes in air pollution on specific diseases; of these, the Canadian census cohort30 and the Taiwan cohort focused only on one aspect of air quality—fine particulate matter, i.e., particulate matter with a diameter ≤2.5μm (PM2.5).31,32 In general, these designs have not yet been widely utilized to strengthen the evidence for the causal associations between comprehensive improvements in air quality and beneficial health effects.

To address these research gaps, this study focused on individuals who moved within the United Kingdom and the changes they experienced in ambient air pollution exposure. Using this data, the association between changes in long-term residential exposure to ambient air pollutants and pollution-related disease and mortality was examined.

Methods

Study Design and Population

Data for this analysis was obtained from the UK Biobank study, details of which are described elsewhere.33–35 In brief, during 2006–2010, more than 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years were recruited across the UK from 22 centers. A baseline survey collected demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and residential history information using a touch screen questionnaire and face-to-face interviews. Follow-up information was obtained through linkages to a national dataset. UK Biobank was approved by the North West Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee (REC reference for UK Biobank 11/NW/0382). This study has been completed under UK Biobank application 69550.

Data on address change history are provided by the Administrative Data Liaison Service (England and Wales), the Information Services Division (Scotland), or participant self-report.36 The analysis included participants who relocated at least once during follow-up, where relocation was defined as movement to a new location for at least 1 year (n=51,130). Participants who withdrew from the study during the follow-up (n=141) and those who had missing data for ambient pollutants exposure (n=467) were excluded, leaving 50,522 participants for analysis. For the analyses of 14 major outcomes, individuals with the corresponding prevalent disease at baseline and those who were diagnosed with diseases prior to relocation were excluded. The number of people included in the analysis for each disease is shown in the flowchart (Figure S1).

Air Pollutant Exposures

The mean annual concentrations of PM2.5, particulate matter with a diameter ≤10μm (PM10), nitrogen oxides (NOx), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and sulfur dioxide (SO2) were obtained from the UK’s Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs.37,38 The air dispersion model (Pollution Climate Mapping) estimated high-resolution grid cell data of ambient air pollution (1km×1km) for each year between 2002 and 2021. Comparison between modeled and measured pollutants indicated a generally acceptable predictive performance, e.g., the R2 of 0.80 for PM2.5 and 0.85 for NO2.39 To determine each participant’s exposure level, address during the follow-up was obtained, and latitude and longitude were geocoded. Using a bilinear interpolation approach,40 annual average pollutant exposure concentrations were estimated for each participant. Subsequently, number of days each participant resided at their respective addresses throughout the calendar year was calculated. Finally, average concentrations of air pollutants were calculated by weighting the time spent at residential addresses for each participant. Average annual concentrations of pollutants were separately grouped in tertiles before and after residential relocation. Cut-offs were set according to the distribution of the air pollutants. Based on moving to areas with less pollution, similar pollution, or higher pollution, the population was divided into nine groups.

Health Outcomes

Participants were followed up from enrollment until either disease onset, death, loss to follow-up, or the end date of this analysis (31 October 2021 for Scotland and 30 September 2021 for England and Wales), whichever came first. For each disease, individuals who developed other diseases during the follow-up period were also included in the analysis. Incident cases of interest were identified through linkages to death registries, primary care records, and hospital inpatient records.33 To determine the effects of changes in air pollution over a wide spectrum of health outcomes, 14 major disease categories were identified and defined per the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10): infection (A01-B89), blood diseases (D50-D89), endocrine diseases (E00-E35), mental and behavioral disorders (F00-F99), nervous system diseases (G00-G99), ocular diseases (H00-H59), ear disorders (H60-H99), circulatory diseases (I00-I99), respiratory diseases (J00-J99), digestive diseases (K00-K93), skin diseases (L00-L99), musculoskeletal diseases (M00-M99), genitourinary diseases (N00-N99), and cancer (C00-C97). All-cause mortality for participants was identified by linking to the National Health Service (NHS) Information Center death registries in England and Wales and the NHS Central Register Scotland for participants from Scotland.

Covariates

Sociodemographic and behavioral covariates known to be associated with disease categories and/or air pollution41–45 were included: age, sex, race, setting (rural and urban), income, education (college education, any school degree, vocational qualification, and other), Townsend deprive index (TDI) (bottom tertile, middle tertile, and top tertile), body mass index (BMI), vegetable consumption (<2, 2–3, and ≥4 times/week), fruit consumption (<2, 2–3, and ≥4 times/week), red meat consumption (<2, 2–3, and ≥4 times/week), oily fish consumption (<1 and ≥1 times/week), nonoily fish consumption (<1 and ≥1 times/week), smoking status, heavy drinking, physical activity, and the use of medications (antihypertensive medication, antidiabetic medication, cholesterol-lowering medication). Race was self-reported as white, Asian, black, mixed, and other. Due to the small counts, Asian, black, mixed, and other were combined into the other races category. Smoking status was divided into never smoking, previous smoking, and current smoking. Heavy drinking was defined as ≥6 drinks/day for women and ≥8 drinks/day for men. Physical activity was assessed with the short-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and grouped into three categories: low [0 to <600 metabolic equivalent minutes (MET-min)/week], moderate (600 to <3,000 MET-min/week), and high (≥3,000 MET-min/week). Income was categorized as, <18,000£, 18,000 to 30,999£; 31,000 to 51,999£; 52,000 to 100,000£; and >100,000£. BMI was calculated per the standard formula and categorized as underweight, normal, overweight, and obese. Baseline dietary data about usual food intake were gathered using surveys (self-administered or interviewer-administered).

Statistical Analysis

The characteristics of different exposure groups were compared using Student’s t-test, analysis of variance (ANOVA), or chi-square test. Cox proportional hazards regressions were used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) separately for the associations of each ambient pollutant exposure change category with 14 major diseases and mortality. We used Schoenfeld residuals to test the proportional hazard assumption. All models were adjusted for age, sex, race, setting, income, education, TDI, BMI, vegetable consumption, fruit consumption, red meat consumption, oily fish consumption, nonoily fish consumption, smoking status, heavy drinking, physical activity, and the use of medication.

Sensitivity Analysis

Due to different individual characteristics, such as Townsend deprivation index, moving behavior may vary among participants. To simulate a hypothetical randomized experiment in which eligible individuals can be randomly assigned to different exposure categories, a propensity score approach was used, fitting a Cox model with inverse probability weights (IPW).46,47 The estimates by using the inverse probability weighted method have a causal interpretation.46,47 Mean absolute correlation (AC) was calculated to test covariate balance after weighting. Good covariate balance was considered when AC was lower than 0.1. Because the covariates had missing values, multiple imputations were performed to assess their impact. We conducted the analyses first by estimating each of the imputed data sets, and then combining the results using the Rubin rule.48 To examine potential introduction of selection bias due to the possibility of each air pollution and the outcome predicting moving, sensitivity analyses were conducted. Participants diagnosed with disease or death within the first 1 year after relocation and those who had multiple moves were excluded. Second, a Fine and Gray model was used to account for competing risks, treating all-cause mortality as the competing event. Third, participants who did not change home addresses during the follow-up were included in the analysis.

Subgroup Analysis

To identify potentially susceptible populations, analyses were stratified by age (<65 or ≥65 years) and sex (male or female). Likelihood ratio tests were calculated for interaction terms to determine whether subgroup results were statistically different.

All analyses were carried out using R Software (version 4.0.3). A two-tailed p-value of <0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of Participants

A total of 50,522 movers were included in this analysis, with a mean age of 54.7 years [standard deviation (SD): 8.1]. There were significant differences in multiple characteristics between the movers and nonmovers (Table S1). Most movers were male (53.9%), white (93.5%), living in an urban area (81.7%), nonsmokers (52.5%), and low to moderate drinkers (74.1%). While half of movers had a relatively high level of education (50.3%), slightly less than half were of moderate income (43.1%). Approximately one-third reported moderate physical activity (33.4%). Most participants did not use antihypertensive (80.3%), antidiabetic (96.3%), and cholesterol lowering (82.3%) medications (Table 1).

Table S2 shows sociodemographic characteristics according to changes in exposure to PM2.5 due to residential relocation. Among the study participants, 9,629 (19.1%) with a history of low PM2.5 exposure moved to another low PM2.5 area, 4,529 (9.0%) moved to a moderate PM2.5 area, and 2,681 (5.3%) moved to a high PM2.5 area. Among those living in moderate PM2.5 areas at the start of the study, 4,771 (9.4%) moved to a low PM2.5 area, 6,756 (13.4%) moved to another moderate PM2.5 area, and 5,311 (10.5%) moved to a high PM2.5 area. Lastly, 2,439 (4.8%) people initially with high PM2.5 exposure moved to a low PM2.5 area, 5,555 (11.0%) moved to an intermediate PM2.5 area, and 8,851 (17.5%) moved to a high PM2.5 area. Regardless of initial exposure levels, participants who moved to areas with higher PM2.5 levels were more likely to be younger, male, smokers, non-drinkers, have the lowest household income, highest TDI, and take antidiabetic medicine; these individuals also were less likely to be white, live in rural areas, and have a higher frequency of physical activity. Sociodemographic characteristics according to changes in exposure to PM10, NO2, NOx, and SO2 arising from residential mobility were also examined; more details are shown in Tables S3–S6.

Changes in Exposure to PM2.5 and Adverse Health Outcomes

During a median follow-up of 12.6 years, 29,869 participants were diagnosed with a disease of interest. More specifically, 4,156 developed infections; 3,219 developed blood diseases; 2,854 developed endocrine diseases; 4,040 developed mental and behavioral disorders; 4,077 developed nervous system diseases; 5,132 developed ocular diseases; 2,908 developed ear disorders; 6,055 developed circulatory diseases; 4,805 developed respiratory diseases; 8,154 developed digestive diseases; 4,588 developed skin diseases; 6,228 developed musculoskeletal diseases; 5,426 developed genitourinary diseases; 4,259 developed cancer; and 3,144 died.

Cox regression analyses found that all 14 diseases, but not all-cause mortality, were associated with changes in exposure to PM2.5. As shown in Figure 1, significantly increased risk was observed among individuals who moved from a lower to a higher PM2.5 polluted area (including low to moderate exposure group, low to high exposure group, and moderate to high exposure group). Compared with constantly low exposure, moving from low to moderate exposure to PM2.5 was associated with increased risk of all 14 diseases, with adjusted HRs (95% CIs) ranging from 1.18 (1.05, 1.33) to 1.48 (1.30, 1.69); similarly, participants who moved from low to high PM2.5 areas were 1.37–1.93 times more likely to suffer from 14 diseases; the estimated risk for moderate to high exposure movers ranged from 1.29 (1.15, 1.44) to 1.76 (1.57, 1.98). For subjects who moved within constant PM2.5 levels (including moderate to moderate exposure group and high to high exposure group), increased risks were found in 10 of 14 disease categories with the exception of infections, nervous system diseases, skin diseases, and cancer. It is worth noting that when individuals relocated from areas with higher PM2.5 levels to those with lower levels (including moderate to low exposure group, high to low exposure group, and high to moderate exposure group), we did not observe a significant decrease in risk in any of the three groups. Instead, higher risks of endocrine diseases (1.37; 95% CI: 1.18, 1.60), mental and behavioral disorders (1.27; 95% CI: 1.12, 1.45), nervous system diseases (1.14; 95% CI: 1.01, 1.29), and digestive diseases (1.15; 95% CI: 1.05, 1.26) were observed in high to moderate exposure group when compared with constantly low exposure.

Changes in Exposure to Other Air Pollutants and Adverse Health Outcomes

Figures 2–5 present associations between changes in exposure to PM10, NO2, NOx, and SO2 and adverse health outcomes. Similar to the findings for PM2.5, relocating from areas with lower pollution levels to those with higher levels was associated with an increased risk of all diseases. However, relocating from areas with higher pollution levels to those with lower levels did not decrease the risk of disease. It should also be noted that individuals who relocated from areas with lower NO2 or NOx levels to higher levels, or consistently resided in areas with moderate or high levels, were more likely to experience an increased risk of mortality (1.24–1.33 times higher) compared to those who consistently resided in areas with low exposure levels. However, regardless of changes in pollution levels, no significant associations with all-cause mortality were observed for exposure to PM10 or SO2.

Sensitivity Analyses

In sensitivity analyses, increased risk of adverse health outcomes associated with increases in PM2.5, PM10, NO2, NOx, and SO2 remained consistent. Specifically, the association was unaltered when applying the IPW approach (see Tables S7–S11). Covariate balance showed a mean AC of <0.1, indicating high quality in recovering randomized experiments. Figures S2–S6 also show that AC is smaller than 0.1. Furthermore, excluding participants with diagnosed disease or death within the first year after relocation yielded similar results as when the entire cohort was analyzed (Tables S12–S16). When multiple imputations were performed, increased risk of 14 types of diseases and all-cause mortality were associated with increases in PM2.5, PM10, NO2, NOx, and SO2 (Tables S17–S21). Moreover, the association remained consistent after applying the Fine and Gray competing risk model (Tables S22–S26), including nonmovers (Tables S27–S31), and excluding the participants with multiple moves (Tables S32–S36).

Subgroup Analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted by age and sex. The associations between changes in PM2.5 and infections (Pinteraction=0.003); changes in PM2.5, PM10, and SO2 and mental and behavioral disorders (Pinteraction=0.008, 0.002, and <0.001, respectively); and changes in PM2.5 and PM10 and ocular diseases (Pinteraction=0.004 and 0.034) were stronger among adults <65 years of age. Conversely, the associations between changes in PM10 and respiratory diseases (Pinteraction=0.006), as well as changes in PM10, NO2, and NOx and all-cause mortality (Pinteraction=0.018, 0.028 and 0.022, respectively) were stronger among adults ≥65 years of age (Tables S37–S41). Moreover, the associations between changes in air pollution and genitourinary diseases were strengthened in males (Pinteraction=0.014 for PM10 and 0.018 for NO2). No significant interactions were detected between exposure changes and other stratifying variables (all Pinteraction>0.05) (Tables S42–S46).

Discussion

Based on the natural experiment design, associations of changes in ambient air pollutants arising from residential mobility with 14 major diseases, including infections, blood diseases, endocrine diseases, mental and behavioral disorders, nervous system diseases, ocular diseases, ear disorders, circulatory diseases, respiratory diseases, digestive diseases, skin diseases, musculoskeletal diseases, genitourinary diseases, cancer, and all-cause mortality, were systematically assessed. In this study, we found increased incidence of disease and, in some cases mortality, in participants who moved from areas with lower to higher air pollution. For example, compared with individuals exposed to constant low PM2.5, those who moved from low to moderate PM2.5 areas and low to high PM2.5 areas had a higher risk for 14 diseases examined (but not increased risk for all-cause mortality), with fully adjusted HRs (95% CIs) ranging from 1.18 (1.05, 1.33) to 1.93 (1.68, 2.21). We observed similar stronger effects for PM10 with 14 diseases (except for all-cause mortality), for NO2 and NOx with all diseases and all-cause mortality, and for SO2 for 14 diseases. These associations were consistent in sensitivity analyses and persisted when the inverse probability weighting approach was applied. Lastly, we found that participants ≥65 years of age were more vulnerable to PM–respiratory diseases and PM–mortality risk than were those <65 years of age.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large population-based study using a natural experiment design to examine the associations of changes in PM2.5, PM10, NO2, NOx, and SO2 with incidence of various disease outcomes and all-cause mortality. While a few similar studies have documented positive associations, most focused on move-related behaviors rather than air pollution exposure. For instance, Braun et al.26 examined effects of moving related changes in walking behavior on cardiometabolic risk in a sample of 1,000 middle-aged adults in the US. They observed that adults who relocated to more walkable areas had lower blood pressure. While a recent literature review27 reported 15 studies which examined the impact of air pollution on the health among relocated residents, most of these studies had significant limitation on assessing changes in exposure due to design flaws, such as small sample sizes, limited statistical analysis, and lack of individual level exposure assessment. Although Awad et al.28 tried to reduce the residual confounding by using IPW and stratifying ZIP code or baseline exposures, the study failed to consider residual confounding related to move, such as meteorological conditions, population density, and economic development. These limitations might result in inaccurate estimations and hinder understanding on the true impact of air pollution on human health due to the move. Moreover, there are only few natural experiments which examined the health effects of NO2, NOx, and SO2. Two cohorts in Canada49 and Europe50 observed positive associations between NO2 and NO exposure and mortality, and one Korean study51 observed that SO2 and NO2 exposure are independent predictors of peripheral arterial disease. In this current analysis of UK Biobank cohort data, participants were classified into nine change groups according to residential PM2.5, PM10, NO2, NOx, and SO2 levels before and after the move. Increased risk of disease incidence and mortality were found in all rising exposure groups regardless of background exposure level or pollutant type. Inversely, reductions in air pollution were not associated with a lower incidence of disease. This may be attributed to the nonreversible nature of adverse health effects of exposure to the pollutants. As such, air pollution policies and interventions should be strengthened continuously.

Focused on a broad spectrum of diseases, the current study provides new insights into the health effects of air pollution by observing positive associations between air pollutants and multiple disease categories. For example, our results support the findings of previous studies which have suggested an association between exposure to air pollutants and gastrointestinal diseases.7,52–54 This evidence may have important implications in terms of protecting populations from air pollution. In addition, associations were observed between all five pollutants and genitourinary diseases. These results were inconsistent with those reported from a recent US veterans study10,55 and a Taiwanese study.56 Furthermore, positive associations were identified between air pollution exposure and mental and behavioral disorders, as well as musculoskeletal diseases, supporting findings of previous studies.57–59 Mental disorders and musculoskeletal diseases may play an important role in contributing to the global burden of disease.60,61 The observations in this study indicate that air pollution exposure may be an important risk factor, which could account for some of the unattributed disease burden.62

There are well-documented biological mechanisms of health effects of air pollution on cardiorespiratory diseases, including systemic inflammation, autonomic imbalance favoring sympathetic tone, oxidative stress, and activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis.2,63,64 For noncardiorespiratory diseases, multiple hypotheses have been proposed and supported by new clinical and experimental evidence. For example, epidemiological studies have reported associations between PM2.5 exposure and insulin homeostasis, an important factor in diabetes mellitus and other metabolic/endocrine diseases.34,65,66 Exposure to air pollutants has been hypothesized to play an important role in the pathophysiology of central nervous system diseases, including dopaminergic neuron damage, neuro-inflammation, and oxidative stress67,68; in turn, these pathways may lead to mental and behavioral disorders.57–59 Studies in mice have reported that transported pollutants can cause direct toxic effects on gastrointestinal epithelial cells and change/influence gut microbial composition.7,69,70 Lastly, air pollution-induced cardiovascular toxicity may play an important role in air pollution-related effects on the kidneys via macro- and microvascular disfunction,10,71 which in turn can impact hypertension. It can be hypothesized that air pollution is associated with increased pain (e.g., chronic back pain) through interaction with the prostaglandin pathways; while animal and human studies report excessive prostaglandins associated with short-term exposures to pollutants (e.g., PM2.5),72–75 definitive mechanisms remain elusive.

The natural experimental population-based design is one of the major strengths of this analysis. This design made it possible to investigate the association between changes in exposure to air pollutants and health outcomes. In addition, by examining the associations of various kinds of air pollution, including PM (PM2.5 and PM10), NO (NO2 and NOx), and SO2 exposure, with a broad spectrum of diseases, this study provides new evidence to support causal effects of air pollution on adverse health outcomes. Moreover, many previous studies used an assumption of a linear concentration–response relationship; the current study verified associations using data from the “real world” with a “relocation” scenario.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, causal inference methods such as propensity score inverse probability weights rely on an assumption of no unmeasured confounding. Although we derived a multitude of potential confounders, residual confounding may still exist due to unmeasured variables. Second, some important information is lacking, such as household membership, which lead to significant exposure uncertainty. Third, temporal changes in life events, such as changing occupation or income, may differ between movers and nonmovers, potentially confounding the associations between change in exposure and outcome. These potential changes were not measured in our study due to limitations in the information available in the UK Biobank database. Lastly, our main analyses were restricted to nonmovers, which could bring in selection biases. Nevertheless, stable results from the sensitivity analyses which included nonmovers in the study cohort indicate the robustness of our original findings.

Conclusions

The findings of this natural experimental study provide further evidence to support associations between changes in concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, NO2, NOx, and SO2 arising from residential mobility and the risk of 14 categories of diseases and all-cause mortality. This study affirms the importance of maintaining the constant low conditions of air pollution.
