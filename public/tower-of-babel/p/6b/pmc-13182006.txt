Medicine retailers in Goma, the “white coats of the pharmacy”: unity among diversity
Authors: Amandine Oleffe, Jean-Bosco Kahindo, Céline Mahieu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the Democratic Republic of Congo, medicine retailers play a central role in access to medicines and, more broadly, to primary healthcare, within a fragmented health system marked by weak regulation. In the city of Goma, they constitute the first port of call with the health system for more than half of the population. Despite their rapid expansion, little is known about their everyday practices, professional trajectories, and modes of organisation.MethodsThis qualitative study draws on 24 semi-structured interviews conducted with medicine retailers in Goma. An inductive analytical approach, informed by the sociology of professions, was used. Data were analysed using descriptive and process coding and organised using Evetts’ framework to examine medicine retailers as a professional group in the making.ResultsDespite substantial heterogeneity in profiles, training backgrounds, and trajectories, medicine retailers share a set of common characteristics that support their analysis as a professional group. These include partially shared pathways into the activity, overlapping ethical orientations, fluid and negotiated professional identities, a shared understanding of their role in improving access to healthcare, relational practices structured by users’ trust and perceived expertise, and similar experiences of economic pressure and weak institutional regulation. Rather than revealing a clear opposition between commercial imperatives and healthcare motives, the findings highlight how these logics are pragmatically articulated in everyday practice.ConclusionsBy offering a profession-centred analysis of medicine retailers in a fragile and underregulated urban context, this study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how access to medicines and care is effectively produced in plural health systems. Conceptualising medicine retailers as a professional group in the making helps move beyond binary distinctions between formality and informality and invites reflection on regulatory and policy approaches that engage with existing practices and local forms of legitimacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14462-w.Keywords: Medicine retailers, Pharmacies, Professional group, Qualitative study, Health systems, Goma, Democratic Republic of Congo, Sub-Saharan Africa

Introduction

In Low- and Middle-Income Countries, private medicine retailers have become a major source of healthcare for large segments of the population, providing privately delivered services that are predominantly financed through out-of-pocket payments [1, 2]. These actors play a central role in people’s health-seeking behaviours, often constituting the first port of call with the health system [1, 3]. Their importance is closely linked to the limited capacity of formal public and private health services to meet population needs and expectations, particularly in resource-constrained settings [1, 4]. Compared to formal health facilities, medicine retailers (MRs) are frequently perceived as more accessible in terms of geography, opening hours, affordability, and social proximity [5, 6]. At the same time, concerns are repeatedly raised regarding the quality of services provided and the medicines dispensed, often based on assumptions about profit-driven motivations and insufficient professional qualifications [3, 7, 8]. These concerns are also echoed in a broader body of literature documenting the circulation of falsified and substandard medicines in underregulated pharmaceutical markets, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, and the associated public health risks [9, 10].

MRs encompass a broad and heterogeneous set of actors, whose levels of formality and regulation vary widely both within and across countries [7, 11]. In this article, we use the generic term “medicine retailers” to refer to any retail actor involved in the sale of medicines, regardless of their official recognition. The distinction between formal and informal providers is often presented as a dichotomy, yet empirical research suggests that formality is better understood as a continuum shaped by context, regulatory arrangements, and historical trajectories [8, 12]. In many settings ─ especially where a quality approach is not integrated ─ assessments of formality rely primarily on two criteria: possession of formal qualifications and compliance with regulatory frameworks [1, 12]. However, both dimensions are deeply context-dependent and time-specific [8]. For instance, a MR with a nursing background operating under partial authorisation may be considered more “formal” than a hawker with no health training, even though both would fall under the category of informal providers according to conventional definitions. These blurred boundaries call for analytical approaches that move beyond binary classifications and attend to the diversity of practices, identities, and roles among MRs.

In the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), the health system is characterised by a high degree of pluralism and fragmentation, largely driven by long-standing governance challenges [13]. Within this context, private providers have proliferated, and MRs have become a major source of healthcare [14, 15]. Although several legislative instruments exist to regulate pharmaceutical activities ─ including a ministerial decree specifying the conditions for opening and operating pharmaceutical establishments, adopted in 2000 ─ their enforcement remains limited [16]. In urban settings such as Goma, this weak enforcement also shapes medicine supply chains, with most MRs relying on pharmaceutical warehouses as their main source of supply, alongside more informal procurement circuits. In Goma, the capital of North Kivu province, MRs have become the first port of call for more than half of the population (51%) [17]. The city hosts a wide range of medicine retail outlets, most commonly street-side pharmacies that, while largely informal, resemble formally recognised establishments; these are hereafter referred to as “pharmacies”1. Available data indicate that only 31% of pharmacies hold an official license, just 2% employ a full-time pharmacist, and one pharmacy serves approximately 1,314 inhabitants instead of the legally prescribed 10,000 [18–20]. Beyond these “pharmacies”, other types of MRs also sell medicines at retail to the population, including independent hawkers called “pitaphars”. In Goma, MRs display highly heterogeneous profiles, ranging from individuals without formal health training to nurses and, more rarely, medical doctors, operating in a context marked by a shortage of qualified pharmacists and rapid expansion of retail outlets [17, 19].

These dynamics are further shaped by the broader socio-political context of North Kivu. The province’s porous borders facilitate intense cross-border circulation of goods, including medicines, often outside formal control mechanisms [21, 22]. Decades of armed conflict have weakened the rule of law and regulatory capacity, while contributing to widespread poverty and economic insecurity [23, 24]. This context has also fostered the presence of numerous humanitarian actors involved in healthcare provision, sometimes generating unintended and destabilising effects on the pharmaceutical sector. Another consequence of this long-standing instability is the militarisation of the economy. This concept can be defined as “the significant implication of military players or men in arms in the economic activities of the specific environment” (author’s translation), with serious consequences for the local economy such as with regard to the sale of retail medicines and difficulties with regard to regulating the sector [22]. Together, these factors have contributed to a highly deregulated medicine retail landscape in which MRs operate at the intersection of commercial imperatives, healthcare needs, and fragile governance arrangements.

A substantial body of literature has examined MRs in Sub-Saharan Africa, with a strong focus on regulatory compliance, dispensing practices, and disease-specific interventions [25]. These studies have largely approached MRs through a functional or normative lens, emphasising what they do ─ or fail to do ─ rather than how they understand and organise their practice. Other studies have explored their economic roles, entrepreneurial capacities, or function as sources of social support within communities [26–28]. In the DRC, existing research has largely concentrated on specific practices ─ such as antibiotic dispensing or contraceptive provision ─ or on mapping pharmaceutical establishments [19, 20, 29, 30]. As a result, MRs are most often treated as a category of providers to be regulated, rather than as social actors embedded in local health systems.

A recent scoping review highlighted the predominance of quantitative, disease-oriented studies and underscored the limited use of qualitative approaches to examine MRs as social actors embedded in health systems [25]. Notably, the organisation of MRs as a professional group, and their own perspectives on their roles, identities, and daily practices, remain largely underexplored. This gap is particularly salient in fragile and conflict-affected settings such as Goma, where weak regulation, economic insecurity, and plural health markets shape both professional trajectories and claims to legitimacy. Addressing this gap therefore requires analytical approaches capable of capturing how MRs collectively organise their work, define professional boundaries, and negotiate legitimacy in everyday practice.

To address this analytical gap, this study is grounded in the sociology of professional groups, drawing on the interactionist tradition developed by Hughes and later systematised by Evetts [31]. Rather than treating MRs as a residual or informal category, this perspective allows us to conceptualise them as a heterogeneous professional group in the making. Applying a sociology of professions lens makes it possible to move beyond binary distinctions between formality and informality, and to examine professionalisation as a situated, relational, and contested process within an underregulated pharmaceutical environment.

This study aims to address this gap empirically by exploring the organisation of MRs as a professional group in the specific context of Goma. Adopting an inductive qualitative approach guided by the sociology of professions, we examine MRs’ daily practices, modes of organisation, and professional trajectories. This analysis also resonates with the anthropology of pharmaceuticals work, which has shown that drug vending practices often reflect broader socio-economic and political failures in healthcare provision and pharmaceutical regulation [32, 33]. While this literature has primarily focused on medicines and their circulation, our study complements it by examining MRs themselves as social and professional actors embedded in these dynamics.

Such investigation is key to better understanding these increasingly important actors, and to inform ongoing efforts to improve access to healthcare while addressing concerns related to the quality and safe use of medicines in underregulated health systems.

Methods

Entry point and sampling

Data collection was conducted in two phases. An initial exploratory phase included semi-structured interviews with key health system stakeholders and focus group discussions with community members. This phase aimed to document local therapeutic practices and retail medicine sources, and to develop a typology of MRs based primarily on their mode of operation and location. This typology informed the purposive sampling strategy used in the second phase, which constitutes the empirical basis of the present analysis.

Participants were selected using purposive sampling, guided by a typology of MRs developed during the exploratory phase of the study to ensure coverage of the main forms of medicine retailing in Goma.

In total, 36 semi-structured interviews were conducted. The present manuscript draws on a subset of 24 interviews selected for in-depth analysis based on analytically relevant criteria that emerged through iterative data collection and preliminary analysis. These 24 interviews constitute the primary empirical material analysed in this article. While the initial sampling (N = 36) aimed to capture a wide range of actors involved in medicine retailing, this paper specifically focuses on MRs whose practices reflected a tension between commercial imperatives and healthcare motives ─ as observed during the analysis ─, and for whom industrial pharmaceutical medicines constituted the predominant products sold. These criteria were identified as central to the study’s analytical objectives and to the examination of MRs as a professional group. Interviews that did not meet these criteria were excluded from the present analysis but contributed to the broader understanding of the medicine retail landscape in Goma. This analytical selection allowed for a coherent and focused examination of professional identities, practices, and claims to legitimacy within an underregulated pharmaceutical context.

Supplementary File 1 provides an overview of all categories of MRs initially included in the sampling (N = 36) and documents the inclusion and exclusion criteria applied in this analysis. It details how the final sample of 24 interviews was derived and clarifies the rationale for excluding other profiles.

Inclusion criteria were to be aged 18 and over and to be an active MR in the city of Goma.

Data collection

Data was collected between April and October 2022. The time duration of the interviews was between 31 and 97 min, with an average duration of 56 min. The choice of the location of the interviews was left to the respondents. Most of the time, interviews were held on their working site or in public spaces according to the specificities and preferences of each respondent. Most interviews were held in Swahili for a matter of fluency and freedom of expression; a few were held in French. No financial compensation was given for the interview.

A team of four people collected the data: the main researcher (female) and a group of 3 Congolese experienced researchers (one woman and two men). The Congolese researchers team benefitted from a 3-day training provided by the main researcher. This training covered different aspects, such as on the aims of the study, qualitative methods (collection and analysis), and ethical issues. During the training, an initial interview guide was discussed by the team and adapted to be in line with local and cultural specificities. The initial guide was developed in French by the main researcher and then translated into Swahili with the help of an official translator. The guide was then tested on a limited number of MRs and adapted based on the specificities of the main categories of the typology. An English version of the summarised interview guide can be found in Supplementary File 2. This guide included open-ended questions and was structured around seven main themes: their career history; their perception of their practice; the types of medicines sold and their sources of supply; their place in the health-seeking behaviours of their users and the services delivered; their interprofessional relationships with different stakeholders; their perceptions of their users; and their views on the state and future of their practice. The guide ended with the collection of some socio-demographic information. Interviews were then completed with observations on site and some pictures if permitted by the MR.

Data analysis

The interviews with MRs were recorded, transcribed in full in Swahili (or French), and then translated into French. The translation into French was done by the researcher who conducted the interview and was then crosschecked by another member of the research team. The data analysis was performed in French, but for this article, the citations were translated into English by the first author and then cross-checked with the original French statements by the last author to ensure their accuracy.

Data collection and analysis were conducted iteratively. Early phases of analysis informed the adaptation of interview guides and the reorientation of sampling. Data collection was concluded when sufficient thematic saturation was reached for the main analytical dimensions examined in this manuscript. Across the categories of MRs included, interviews no longer generated substantially new insights regarding professional trajectories, practices, relationships with users, and perceptions of their role within the health system. Given the diversity of profiles, saturation was sought for core themes rather than for every item of the interview guide.

Transcripts were coded using Atlas.ti through an inductive and iterative analytical process combining descriptive and process coding, informed by the sociology of professions as an interpretive framework to structure and deepen the analysis. Specifically, the framework proposed by Evetts was mobilised as an analytical lens rather than as a predefined coding scheme. It conceptualises professions through four interrelated dimensions: a shared working culture; shared ways of understanding users’ problems and the group’s social role; patterned relational orientations toward users; and modes of socialisation and collective organisation. These dimensions provided a flexible structure to organise and deepen the interpretation of the most salient codes emerging inductively from the data [31]. This flexible framework made it possible to analyse MRs as a heterogeneous professional group in the making, to articulate micro-level practices and interactions with meso-level dynamics of professional organisation, and to remain attentive to contextual constraints related to weak regulation.

Results

This section presents the findings of the study and examines MRs as a professional group operating within an underregulated pharmaceutical sector. The analysis highlights shared patterns across four analytical dimensions: working cultures and professional identities; perceived social roles in addressing healthcare needs; interactions with users; and modes of organisation and relationships with other health system actors.

A total of 24 respondents were included in our study, 58% of whom were female and 42% male. The average age was 39 years but ranged from 24 to 62 years. Regarding education, all except one had completed secondary education (96%); 4 had no health background at all (16.5%); 7 had followed a short training in pharmacy (29%), 6 of whom had just this background in health; 6 had an A2 level in health (25%)2; 8 had an A1 level in health (33%)3, and only one had a master’s degree in medicine (4%). A table with the main characteristics of the respondents is available in Supplementary File 3.

A partially shared working culture

In Goma, MRs share a set of practices, values, and representations that contribute to a common working culture, despite highly diverse professional backgrounds and trajectories. This culture is shaped by constrained employment opportunities, close ties to family and social networks, and everyday exposure to users’ health needs, resulting in both shared ethical principles and internal disagreements regarding the boundaries of legitimate practice.

Becoming a medicine retailer: a plan B

Entry into medicine retailing in Goma is rarely the result of a vocation or a first professional choice. For most respondents, it represents a pragmatic response to unemployment and limited job opportunities. Medicine retailing is frequently described as a “plan B”, particularly among respondents without a health background, who initially trained in other sectors and turned to this activity as a temporary solution while hoping to access employment in their original field. For some, it also constitutes a means to finance further education.

Respondents with a health background describe a different trajectory. For them, selling medicines is often framed as a continuation or reorientation of a professional project rooted in the health sector, shaped by the lack of employment opportunities in formal healthcare facilities. As one nurse explained:

Why did I have this idea of opening a pharmacy? Everything began with the end of my studies. I looked for a job everywhere and I couldn’t find anything. Because me, personally, I am a nurse (…) I told myself I can open a pharmacy. That would then help me and relieve me in order to get something that could help in life. (R07, close to healthcare facility, male, nurse A1)

Across profiles, the primary motivation remains the need to secure a livelihood and support one’s family. Even when medicine retailing is not perceived as an ideal occupation, most respondents reported finding meaning and a certain level of satisfaction in their work, although many expressed the desire to eventually practice the profession for which they were initially trained.

Entry into the sector is also strongly shaped by family and social networks. Medicine retailing is frequently organised as a family business, either through pooled financial investments or through the involvement of relatives who assist or replace the main retailer. As a result, users may encounter different sellers within the same outlet, with varying levels of expertise. When family ties are absent, entry into the profession often occurs through acquaintances already active in the sector, highlighting the role of close social circles in shaping access to medicine retailing.

Common values, despite disagreements regarding healthcare provision

Despite their diverse trajectories, respondents share a number of ethical principles that contribute to a partially shared working culture. A central principle repeatedly mentioned is the duty to refer users to healthcare facilities when cases exceed the retailer’s perceived competencies. Referral is framed as both a recognition of professional limits and a responsibility toward users’ health.

Several respondents also emphasised a broader sense of responsibility toward the population of Goma, describing their role as socially useful beyond its economic dimension. This was particularly pronounced among respondents with a health background, who tended to prioritise users’ health over financial considerations and used this distinction to differentiate between “good” and “bad” MRs:

Non-qualified medicine sellers think that opening a pharmacy is just to find money. But we must pay close attention because the Hippocratic oath forbids us putting money before taking care of people. (R04, along major roads, female, pharmacy assistant)

Some respondents reported providing medicines free of charge to users in life-threatening situations, despite the financial cost. This sense of responsibility is closely linked to an awareness of the risks associated with medicine retailing, both for users’ health and for retailers themselves, particularly in terms of potential legal consequences.

However, respondents expressed divergent views regarding the provision of healthcare services, a practice that is legally prohibited. Those in favour justified it by pointing to the limited financial resources of the population and barriers to accessing healthcare facilities, while also acknowledging its economic benefits. As one respondent noted:

We provide [care] clandestinely because most people depend on us (…) their socio-economic level only allows them to depend on pharmacies or some ‘white coats of the pharmacy’4whom they trust. (R03, along major roads, female, nurse A1)

Conversely, respondents opposed to providing care referred to legal constraints and a division of labour between MRs and healthcare facilities. Confidentiality emerged as another widely shared ethical value, seen as essential to building users’ trust and defining what constitutes a “good” MR.

“I am like a white coat”: professional identities beyond formal training

Most respondents experienced difficulty in clearly defining their professional identity. Within the same interview, they alternated between labels such as “white coat”, “pharmacist”, “medicine seller”, “expert”, or “businessman”, suggesting blurred boundaries and flexible uses of professional categories.

I think that I’m a good medicine seller and people trust me. I sell good medicines, and my clients have my phone number (…) Hence, they know that I’m a good medicine seller, I’m a white coat (…) I am only like a trader. (R17, pitaphar, male, nurse A2)

Professional self-identification is strongly influenced by initial training, following a gradation from no health background, to short pharmacy training, to formal health qualifications. Respondents without a health background tended to distance themselves from the health sector, while those with limited training described themselves as “more or less from the field”. Respondents with more extensive health education were more likely to identify as healthcare providers, while still distinguishing themselves from staff working in healthcare facilities due to their more limited scope of practice.

Importantly, many respondents emphasised that professional identity is not solely grounded in formal qualifications. Practical experience and skills acquired through everyday practice were frequently presented as compensating for limited or absent health training:

Most of those who are from the field are more theoretical, but us, we focus on practice. It’s when you sell medicines in the pharmacy that you face the true reality. It’s through practicing that you get to know the medicines. (R10, neighbourhood pharmacy, female, no health background)

Professional identity thus evolves over time, shaped by experience, skills acquisition, and interactions with users. Many respondents constructed their identity through a moral distinction between “good” retailers—those oriented toward users’ health—and “bad” retailers, perceived as purely profit-driven. While formal training was often invoked as a marker of legitimacy, relational qualities such as trust, welcoming attitudes, and perceived care for users were equally central.

Despite these blurred and contested self-identifications, most respondents believed that users perceived them as “white coats”, regardless of their qualifications. This external recognition was described as a key source of credibility and success:

The majority call me ‘the white coat of the pharmacy’. (R13, neighbourhood pharmacy, male, no health background)

MRs filling the gaps in accessibility to healthcare

In Goma, MRs consistently describe their activity as a response to the concrete shortcomings of the formal health system, positioning themselves as essential intermediaries between the population and healthcare services. According to most respondents, people face multiple barriers when seeking care within the formal health system, including financial constraints, logistical difficulties, and negative experiences with healthcare staff. In this context, healthcare facilities are perceived as poorly adapted to both the needs and the economic means of the population.

As a result, turning to MRs is widely seen as the most practical option. Their services are described as inexpensive, quick, and geographically close, making medicines more readily available, accessible, and affordable for the community. Beyond dispensing medicines, many MRs report offering advice, first aid, and what they describe as a “free diagnosis,” reinforcing their perceived usefulness in everyday health situations. From their perspective, this positions them as an indispensable component of the local health system.

Some respondents go further, suggesting that MRs have partially replaced healthcare facilities in routine health-seeking behaviours, with hospitals being consulted only as a last resort in severe or complex cases:

So, all this shows that the community doesn’t need hospitals, and most people go to hospitals only because they’ve tried everything and when nothing has worked. It’s at that moment that people decide to head for the hospital but in itself, the hospital isn’t helpful to the people of Goma. Instead, it’s the pharmacies that help the people of Goma in terms of their health. (R06, close to healthcare facilities, female, short training in pharmacy)

Another issue frequently mentioned by MRs is the widespread practice of self-medication among the population. In this context, they see their role as reframing self-medication practices by advising users on the type of medicines, dosage, and duration of treatment. While this reinforces their sense of social and health responsibility, respondents also acknowledge the limits of their role, particularly the lack of diagnostic precision due to the absence of testing. This tension illustrates how MRs perceive themselves as filling critical gaps in healthcare accessibility, while operating within significant structural constraints.

Interacting with users: a key component of MRs’ practice

Interactions with users constitute a central dimension of MRs’ everyday practice and play a key role in the construction of their professional role. Because MRs operate at the intersection of care and commerce, their relationships with users are shaped by continuous negotiation around expertise, trust, and responsibility. These interactions also extend over time and underpin processes of user loyalty, which are central to both MRs’ economic sustainability and their claims to professional legitimacy. Our analysis highlights three main factors that structure these interactions: users’ perceived expertise, their socio-economic situation, and the degree of social closeness between users and MRs.

Users’ expertise, socio-economic status, and social proximity as determinants of interaction

MRs describe their clientele as highly diverse, encompassing all socio-economic categories and health-seeking behaviours. Users may consult MRs as a first resort or after having visited other providers, and they may come with or without a medical prescription. While MRs located close to healthcare facilities more frequently serve users with prescriptions, most MRs report that the majority of their users arrive without any formal diagnosis.

Users’ perceived level of expertise strongly shapes the nature of interactions. “Non-expert” users generally expect advice and guidance from MRs, who may take on a quasi-consultative role by asking questions and recommending treatment within the perceived limits of their knowledge. For many MRs, these interactions are a source of professional gratification, as they reinforce a sense of usefulness and competence:

In another situation, a person can come without a medical prescription. When I ask them some questions, they explain their illness to me. Then, according to what I learned during the training in pharmacy, I give them the medicines [they need]. And, when I give them these medicines, they will regain their health, and all this because I clearly understood them. In that sense, I will have really helped them. (R05, close to healthcare facility, male, short training in pharmacy)

By contrast, “expert” users—those who practice self-medication and request specific medicines—tend to limit interaction and reduce the scope of the MRs’ role. These encounters can be perceived ambivalently. For less qualified MRs, such users may be reassuring because they do not challenge their knowledge. For MRs with a health background, however, they are often viewed as a threat to professional legitimacy, particularly when users test their knowledge or contest their advice. In some cases, users themselves become sources of information on medicines, especially for MRs without formal health training, blurring boundaries of expertise.

The socio-economic status of users constitutes a second major determinant of interaction. Users with limited financial resources frequently negotiate prices or request smaller quantities of medicines, sometimes leading MRs to sell incomplete treatments by unit. While this practice reduces financial profit, it is often framed as a necessary adaptation to users’ constraints:

Sometimes we understand [that people have limited financial means], and we give them the medicines in balance with their money (…) by dividing the blister-pack (…) even if this is not the [complete] cure. (R06, close to healthcare facilities, female, short training in pharmacy)

Conversely, interactions with wealthier users are described as easier and more profitable, as these users are less likely to bargain and tend to purchase larger quantities or more expensive medicines. MRs report adapting their recommendations to users’ perceived economic means, highlighting the entanglement of medical advice and commercial considerations.

Finally, social proximity plays a key role in shaping relationships. MRs distinguish between “known” users ─ neighbours or long-standing clients ─ and “unknown” or occasional users. Trust-based arrangements such as granting credit are largely reserved for known users, sometimes through informal agreements settled at the end of the month. While extending credit to unknown users entails financial risk, some MRs adopt this strategy deliberately to build loyalty or demonstrate solidarity:

If I give them a medicine and they recover, directly this person becomes my friend. Then, when he comes back at the pharmacy (…) even if he doesn’t have money, you’ll give them more medicines on credit. (R05, close to healthcare facility, male, short training in pharmacy)

User loyalty and retention: recovery, trust, and recognition

According to MRs, user loyalty is primarily built through recovery or symptom relief. Successful treatment generates trust in the MRs’ competence and reinforces their professional credibility. Several respondents emphasised that building trust may require refusing to sell medicines when they are unnecessary, distinguishing “treating an illness” from merely selling products:

When they know that you are not just a medicine seller, this motivates them. Sometimes, a person can arrive, and I tell them ‘No, you just need to drink a lot of water, and you will recover.’ (…) When the person notices that they have recovered, they immediately understand that you weren’t after their money but rather wanted their recovery. You have already won their trust, and they will come back in the near future to buy [medicines] without any problem. (R02, along major roads, female, nurse A1)

Recovery not only fosters user trust but also provides MRs with a sense of professional recognition and pride. Conversely, treatment failure can undermine confidence in the MRs’ expertise, negatively affecting user retention and income.

Beyond clinical outcomes, loyalty is also shaped by the quality of the interaction itself. A “warm welcome” is repeatedly cited as a defining characteristic of a good MR and includes attentiveness, empathy, availability, and financial flexibility. Practices such as reducing prices, adjusting quantities, or granting credit are central to maintaining long-term relationships. These relational skills contribute to MRs’ legitimacy within the community and encourage word-of-mouth recommendations, potentially expanding their clientele:

Ok, the second thing is the welcome, and when prescribing medicines (…), I do my very best to give them the explanations of the medicines they buy and how they must behave so that they don’t fall ill again (…) This why they come back, and they encourage others to come here to my pharmacy to buy medicines. (R03, along major roads, female, nurse A1)

Overall, interactions with users constitute a core component of MRs’ professional practice, shaping both their economic viability and their claims to legitimacy. Through everyday negotiations around expertise, care, and trust, MRs continuously redefine their role at the interface between health provision and commerce.

Organising practice and navigating constraints in an underregulated pharmaceutical sector

This section examines how MRs organise their practice and navigate the structural constraints of an underregulated pharmaceutical sector, by focusing on learning processes, economic and regulatory constraints, and their relationships with other health system actors. Together, these elements shed light on the conditions under which MRs operate and negotiate their position within a fragmented health system.

Learning and professional socialisation in the absence of formal regulation

Learning how to sell medicines is described by most respondents as a progressive and continuous process that primarily takes place through practice. Observation, trial and error, and everyday interactions with users are central to this learning process, which is widely perceived as never fully completed. This perception reflects a form of humility regarding expertise and an acknowledgement of the limits of individual knowledge. Learning is often facilitated through exchanges with more experienced colleagues, whether working in the same “pharmacy” or in neighbouring ones, as well as through informal interactions with healthcare providers. For respondents with a prior health background, previous education is seen as easing this process, although practical experience remains central.

In this context, “pharmacies” function as spaces of professional socialisation, where knowledge is acquired, refined, and shared over time. Several respondents described their workplace as a form of informal internship, allowing them to complement theoretical knowledge with practical skills or, for those without a health background, to progressively acquire the competences required for the activity. Listening carefully to users and learning from their reactions and outcomes is also perceived as an important source of experiential knowledge.

Some MRs choose to enrol in short training courses in pharmacy, usually lasting between three and six months, offered by private training organisations. These courses provide basic knowledge about medicines and ethical principles, such as referring serious cases to healthcare facilities, and are particularly valued by respondents without formal health training. For them, such training contributes to a sense of legitimacy and increased confidence in their practice:

Well, because before I used to sell medicines with a lot of doubts. After the training, I knew the names of the medicines. (…) Thanks to the training I successfully completed, I think I am now more experienced in the sale of medicines (…) So I felt really good when I found this course, and now I’m a pharmacist like any other, similar to those people who are able to prescribe medicines without any problem. (R18, pitaphar, female, short training in pharmacy)

However, respondents with more extensive health training expressed ambivalent views regarding these courses. While acknowledging that they are preferable to no training at all and may improve basic practices, they also criticised their limited content and low entry requirements, which they felt could undermine users’ trust and the credibility of the profession. These contrasting views highlight ongoing tensions between experiential learning, partial formalisation of knowledge, and claims to professional legitimacy within the group.

Economic pressures, taxation, and competition as structuring constraints

MRs in Goma operate in a highly constrained economic and regulatory environment, marked by two major challenges: an opaque and coercive taxation system, and intense competition within a largely unregulated pharmaceutical market.

First, most respondents reported facing multiple and non-transparent taxes levied by agents from various public institutions. These agents were described less as regulators than as revenue collectors, frequently seeking to identify minor infractions as a pretext for imposing fines or extracting informal payments. When unable or unwilling to pay, MRs risked having their premises temporarily closed, leading some to adopt avoidance strategies such as closing their “pharmacy” upon seeing state agents approaching. For many respondents, these practices were perceived as arbitrary and predatory rather than protective of public health.

Sometimes, we flee the field. We close [the pharmacy] when we see the state agents coming. (R02, along major roads, female, nurse A1)

In response, MRs described various coping strategies, ranging from calls for a simplified and unified taxation system to more pragmatic arrangements, including cultivating informal relationships with specific agents who could offer protection against repeated inspections. While these practices allowed some MRs to continue operating, they also reinforced a climate of insecurity and uncertainty.

Second, respondents emphasised the intensity of competition within the pharmaceutical sector. The proliferation of MRs, combined with the presence of retail-wholesale pharmacies and pharmaceutical warehouses selling directly to users, has reduced profit margins and intensified rivalry. Competition was further exacerbated by the existence of purely business-oriented MRs willing to dispense medicines without regard for users’ needs, placing pressure on others to lower prices or compromise professional standards. In the absence of standardised pricing and effective regulation, these practices were described as having a destabilising and “destructive” effect on the market.

To cope with this competitive environment, MRs highlighted the strategic importance of “pharmacy” location, population density, proximity to healthcare facilities, and evolving urban dynamics. Choosing where to operate was described as a constant balancing act between running costs and potential profit, shaped by changes in the local pharmaceutical landscape over time.

Together, these fiscal pressures and competitive dynamics structure the everyday realities of MRs in Goma. Rather than operating in a stable professional environment, MRs continuously adapt their practices to survive within a market characterised by regulatory ambiguity, economic precarity, and shifting power relations.

Fragmented and pragmatic relationships with other health system actors

Overall, MRs operate within a dense but weakly institutionalised relational environment. Their interactions with other health system stakeholders are marked by asymmetrical power relations, limited formal collaboration, and a predominance of informal and pragmatic arrangements. These relationships play a key role in shaping both their daily practices and their possibilities for professional recognition.

Between control, taxation, and mistrust: conflictual relationships with public authorities

Relationships between MRs and public institutions, including provincial health authorities, are predominantly described as conflictual. Rather than being perceived as sources of guidance, support, or professional regulation, these institutions are mainly associated with inspections, fines, and the levying of multiple and often non-transparent taxes. Respondents frequently framed these interactions as coercive and arbitrary, with control visits seen less as mechanisms to improve practice than as opportunities for extortion by individual agents.

Although some inspections formally target issues such as medicine quality, expiry dates, or professional qualifications, respondents largely interpreted them as financial transactions, in which compliance was negotiated through informal payments. As a result, these interactions contribute little to professional oversight or capacity building and instead reinforce mistrust toward public authorities. Several respondents reported adopting avoidance strategies, highlighting the extent to which regulation is experienced as a threat rather than as a framework for professional recognition.

Beyond these control-oriented encounters, most respondents regretted the absence of constructive engagement with health authorities, including a lack of information sharing, training opportunities, or dialogue on professional standards. This perceived institutional vacuum further weakens the legitimacy of public regulation in the eyes of MRs and limits the development of more collaborative forms of governance within the pharmaceutical sector.

If they pass by your pharmacy and you give them some money, they leave. But if you don’t (…), they can close your pharmacy. It is really chicaneries. These people have no importance. (R08, close to healthcare facilities, female, nurse and short training in pharmacy)

Competition, referral, and informal arrangements with healthcare facilities

Formal relationships between healthcare facilities and MRs are generally limited, as MRs are often perceived as competitors. When interactions do occur, they take largely informal and pragmatic forms. These include direct sales of medicines to healthcare facilities, purchases by individual healthcare providers for their patients, or referrals of patients to specific MRs based on personal arrangements.

Such relationships are shaped by mutual interests rather than institutionalised collaboration. In some cases, proximity to healthcare facilities facilitates informal exchanges of information or access to training opportunities. However, these interactions remain uneven and dependent on individual connections rather than formal frameworks.

Medicine supply chains: between formal procurement and informal arrangements

Relationships with medicine supply sources are primarily commercial. Pharmaceutical warehouses constitute the main declared source of supply and operate through client–supplier relationships, occasionally accompanied by marketing strategies or information-sharing regarding product availability. Credit arrangements are rare.

Alongside these formal channels, alternative and informal sources of supply exist, including procurement from other provinces or neighbouring countries, diversion of medicines from healthcare facilities ─ including from military settings ─, and transactions involving “pitaphars”, a mobile and highly informal category of MRs occupying a specific position within local medicine supply chains5.

Well, we [the pitaphars] have a relationship with the warehouses. (…) we have an agreement that we sign with them. When the medicines are getting close to their expiry date, they call us, and we arrive. They give us the mission to offload these medicines in pharmacies to which we usually supply medicines (…) also onto the health centres and among the population, we report back, and they give us our percentage. (R17, pitaphar, male, nurse A2)

While these circuits allow access to cheaper medicines and greater financial flexibility, they also contribute to price deregulation and raise concerns regarding medicine quality and storage conditions. Some MRs therefore refuse to engage with these channels, while others rely on them to remain competitive.

Rivalry, solidarity, and interdependence among MRs

Despite a highly competitive environment, relationships among MRs are characterised by a combination of rivalry and interdependence. Competition arises from the growing number of retailers, price undercutting, and the presence of actors prioritising profit over health considerations. At the same time, everyday collaboration is common, particularly through the exchange of medicines, referrals of users, and sharing of information when cases exceed individual expertise.

In some neighbourhoods, informal unions or solidarity networks have emerged, allowing MRs to coordinate responses to inspections, share risks, and support one another. By contrast, formal professional associations are perceived as ineffective, costly, or overly focused on commercial interests, resulting in low levels of membership and engagement among our respondents. These dynamics illustrate a fragmented form of collective organisation, oscillating between competition and locally embedded solidarity.

Discussion

MRs are widely documented as essential providers of medicines in many sub-Saharan African countries. However, the DRC remains comparatively under-researched in this regard, despite the centrality of medicine retailing in everyday health-seeking behaviours. Existing studies conducted in other sub-Saharan African settings have predominantly focused on the operational dimensions of MRs’ activities, particularly in terms of service provision [25]. By contrast, MRs’ self-perceptions regarding their motivations, professional identity, ethical orientations, and the meaning they attribute to their daily practices have received far less scholarly attention [25, 34, 35]. Addressing this gap is essential to better understand the social organisation of medicine retailing and the role these actors play in the routine functioning of health systems understood in a broad and inclusive sense.

Drawing on an analytical framework inspired by the sociology of professional groups, this study examined MRs in Goma as a professional group characterised by both internal heterogeneity and shared social features [31]. Despite highly diverse profiles, trajectories, and levels of qualification, our findings show that MRs display a set of common characteristics that justify analysing them as a professional group rather than as a mere aggregation of informal providers. These shared features include partially convergent pathways into the activity, overlapping ethical principles, blurred but meaningful professional identities, a common understanding of their role in improving access to healthcare, relational practices shaped by users’ expertise and trust, and similar experiences of economic constraints and weak institutional regulation.

This combination of diversity and unity echoes findings from other contexts, where MRs have been described as a heterogeneous group structured around shared practices and representations [26, 35]. Our results further confirm that MRs’ motivations cannot be reduced to profit maximisation alone. As observed elsewhere, financial imperatives coexist with a desire to be useful, to help others, and to gain social recognition within local communities [26, 35]. In Goma, this tension between commercial and care-oriented logics is particularly pronounced and contributes to the multiplicity and instability of professional self-definitions. Notably, many respondents alternated between different labels ─ such as “medicine seller,” “white coat,” “pharmacist,” or “businessperson” ─ within the same interview. This fluidity reflects the blurred boundaries between formality and informality, as well as the fragility of regulatory frameworks in a context marked by conflict, economic insecurity, and weak institutional oversight [8, 21].

References to symbols associated with biomedical professions ─ such as the Hippocratic oath or the white coat ─ can be understood as symbolic resources through which some MRs articulate ethical commitments and claim moral legitimacy, drawing on widely recognised medical norms despite their ambiguous professional status.

Beyond identity and motivation, our findings highlight the centrality of relational work in MRs’ professional practice. Interactions with users are not merely transactional but constitute a key site where professional legitimacy, trust, and recognition are produced and contested. Users’ perceived level of expertise strongly shapes these interactions, influencing the degree of advice provided, the scope of the MRs’ intervention, and the balance of power within the encounter. In some cases, users’ experiential knowledge ─ well documented in the literature on self-medication ─ becomes a resource mobilised within retail encounters, particularly for less formally trained MRs. Such situational inversions of expertise challenge conventional hierarchies of pharmaceutical knowledge and situate MRs’ practice within broader processes of medical empowerment and the circulation of medical knowledge beyond formal institutions [36].

These relational dynamics also operate as a form of regulation. Building user loyalty through successful recovery, honesty, and attentiveness emerged as a powerful mechanism shaping MRs’ behaviour. As suggested by Hughes et al., such “effective community regulation” contributes to professional legitimacy by rewarding practices perceived as responsible and sanctioning those associated with poor outcomes [35]. In Goma, this form of regulation coexists with the perceived risk of legal proceedings in cases of adverse outcomes, creating a double external pressure on MRs’ practices. Together, these mechanisms encourage MRs to balance commercial imperatives with concerns for users’ health, even in the absence of effective institutional oversight.

At a systemic level, our findings reinforce the idea that MRs occupy a strategic position within primary healthcare systems in low- and middle-income settings [35, 37–39]. In Goma, MRs perceive themselves as compensating for the financial and organisational shortcomings of the formal health system and, in some cases, as having effectively become a first line of care. While referral to healthcare facilities is commonly mentioned and framed as an ethical obligation, opinions diverge regarding the legitimacy of providing healthcare by MRs. This ambivalence illustrates the ongoing renegotiation of professional boundaries in a context where institutional roles are weakly enforced and practical needs prevail. However, unlike in some rural settings where MRs’ role in care provision is tacitly accepted by formal health authorities, relationships between MRs and the formal health system in Goma are largely conflictual [37]. MRs report that healthcare facilities tend to view them as competitors rather than partners. Public authorities are primarily perceived as sources of taxation and harassment rather than as supportive or regulatory actors concerned with patient safety. These findings resonate with previous studies documenting corruption, “chicaneries,” and the instrumentalisation of regulation in fragile governance contexts [3, 11, 21]. While professional associations have been identified elsewhere as important regulatory and formative actors for MRs, notably in Nigeria, our findings point to a markedly different situation in Goma [26]. Here, such associations are largely perceived as defending commercial interests rather than supporting professional development or ethical practice, which partly explains their weak uptake and limited influence among our respondents.

The organisation of medicine supply chains further illustrates the structural constraints under which MRs operate. While pharmaceutical warehouses constitute the main declared source of supply, informal and illicit circuits ─ including diversion from healthcare facilities and transactions involving pitaphars ─ play a significant role in ensuring affordability and financial flexibility. These arrangements, already documented in other settings [40, 41], take on a particular significance in Goma due to their scale and their entanglement with conflict-related dynamics, including the involvement of military structures. Such practices contribute to price deregulation and raise concerns about medicine quality, while simultaneously enabling MRs to remain competitive in a saturated market.

Taken together, these findings support calls to move beyond simplistic binaries opposing formal and informal providers, or profit-making and service-oriented actors. MRs in Goma function as intermediaries ─ between populations and the health system, between biomedical and experiential knowledge, and between care and commerce ─ thus fulfilling a “bridging role” previously identified in the literature [33]. At the same time, their practices actively contribute to the reconfiguration of healthcare delivery, shifting elements of first-line care towards medicine retailing and reshaping power relations within the local health system.

These findings have important implications for debates on the regulation of medicine retailing in underregulated settings. While regulatory enforcement is often presented as a necessary response to the risks associated with informal medicine provision, our results suggest that such an approach would be difficult to implement and potentially counterproductive in the context of Goma. Given the pervasive non-compliance with existing regulations, strict enforcement would likely result in the closure of most “pharmacies” and could exacerbate reliance on illicit markets. Instead, regulation emerges in practice as a fragmented and socially embedded process, shaped not only by public authorities but also by market dynamics, professional norms, and everyday interactions with users.

In this perspective, our findings echo work that conceptualises pharmaceutical regulation as a plural process involving multiple actors and mechanisms beyond formal institutions [36, 42]. By foregrounding MRs as a professional group, this study highlights how users themselves contribute to processes of selection, recognition, and legitimisation through trust, loyalty, and reputation. This form of community-based regulation, while informal, plays a structuring role in shaping MRs’ practices and claims to legitimacy in a context of weak institutional governance.

Overall, this study contributes to the literature by offering a nuanced, profession-centred analysis of MRs in an underregulated pharmaceutical environment. By foregrounding their identities, motivations, organisational dynamics, and relational practices, it provides a deeper understanding of how MRs navigate uncertainty and structural constraints while sustaining access to medicines and care. Taken together, these insights invite further reflection on how regulatory frameworks and public health interventions might engage more constructively with MRs ─ not as marginal or problematic actors, but as central components of locally functioning health systems.

Limitations of the study

This study is primarily based on self-reported data collected through interviews with MRs, which entails a risk of social desirability bias. Some participants may have emphasised idealised representations of their practices or professional ethics, for instance by underreporting profit-oriented motives or overreporting referral practices. This limitation was nevertheless inherent to a deliberate methodological choice aimed at centring the analysis on the perspectives of a group of healthcare providers largely overlooked in health policy debates in Goma.

Most interviews were conducted at participants’ workplaces, occasionally in the presence of interruptions or time constraints. While such conditions may have affected the fluidity of some exchanges, the collection of sensitive information suggests that they did not substantially limit participants’ willingness to share their experiences.

A number of MRs declined to participate, often citing lack of time. Based on field observations, this reluctance may partly reflect suspicions that the research team was affiliated with regulatory authorities, which could have contributed to the underrepresentation of certain profiles, particularly retailers without a formal health background. Nevertheless, the final sample includes a wide range of educational and professional trajectories, including participants with no health training or only short pharmacy training.

Finally, as with most qualitative research, the findings are not intended to be statistically generalisable. Rather, they provide an in-depth and contextualised understanding of MRs’ practices and professional dynamics in Goma. While the specificity of this fragile and conflict-affected setting must be acknowledged, parallels with existing literature suggest that several insights may be analytically relevant for similar urban contexts in sub-Saharan Africa.

Conclusion

This study examined MRs in Goma as a professional group operating within a highly underregulated pharmaceutical environment. By adopting a sociology of professions perspective and foregrounding MRs’ own accounts, it moves beyond compliance-based or purely functional approaches that have long dominated the literature. Instead, it highlights how MRs’ practices, identities, and claims to legitimacy are shaped through everyday interactions with users, other health actors, and regulatory authorities in a context marked by economic insecurity and weak governance.

Our findings show that, despite significant internal heterogeneity, MRs share a partially common working culture, shaped by constrained employment opportunities, moral commitments toward users, and experiential learning. Their professional identities are neither fixed nor uniform, but negotiated over time through practice, background, and recognition by users. Importantly, our results challenge simplistic oppositions between profit-driven behaviour and healthcare motives. Rather than reflecting a simple opposition between commercial and healthcare logics, MRs’ practices reveal a pragmatic articulation of livelihood strategies and moral commitments, negotiated in everyday interactions.

MRs also occupy a central position in local health-seeking behaviours by filling gaps in accessibility to medicines and, to some extent, primary care. Their relationships with users ─ structured by trust, recovery, and perceived expertise ─ play a crucial role in shaping both professional legitimacy and informal forms of regulation. At the same time, their interactions with public authorities and healthcare facilities remain largely conflictual, reflecting broader dynamics of mistrust, competition, and fragmented governance within the health system.

Taken together, these findings underscore the need to reconsider how MRs are conceptualised within health systems in fragile and conflict-affected urban settings. Rather than viewing them primarily as a regulatory problem or a residual informal sector, this study highlights their role as central, if ambivalent, actors in the everyday functioning of pharmaceutical and healthcare provision. Understanding MRs as a professional group in the making offers a more nuanced basis for future research and policy debates on access, quality, and regulation, and invites reflection on regulatory approaches that acknowledge existing practices, relational dynamics, and local forms of legitimacy.
