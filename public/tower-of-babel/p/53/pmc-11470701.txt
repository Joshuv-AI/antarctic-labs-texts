Why were so few randomized trials of public health and social measures conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic? The Norwegian experience
Authors: Atle Fretheim, Petter Elstrøm, Cathinka Halle Julin, Unni Gopinathan, Ingeborg Hess Elgersma, Runar Barstad Solberg, Arnfinn Helleve
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFew randomized trials of public health and social measures were carried out during the COVID-19 pandemic. We report on the major barriers we faced when we tried to run such trials.MethodsWe reviewed all randomized trials proposed and initiated by the Centre for Epidemic Interventions Research in Norway during the COVID-19 pandemic.ResultsOf the 18 proposed trials, 11 trials were not implemented. One of the key legal and ethical barriers to conducting the trials was the Norwegian Health Research Act, which demands informed consent from all participants who are exposed to an intervention. A lack of sufficient political support was also a challenge, as was unpredictability, due to shifting disease incidence and changing recommendations from the authorities.ConclusionStrengthening the evidence base for public health and social measures in pandemics will require political and public understanding and support and a legal framework that allows for the conduct of such trials.

Introduction

The effectiveness of most public health and social measures (PHSM) for infection control, e.g., school closures, face masks, and improved ventilation, is uncertain [1]. Despite the apparent need for increasing evidence to inform policies about PHSM during the COVID-19 pandemic, few randomized trials of such interventions have been conducted [2]. Many research efforts have been made to evaluate the effects of drugs and vaccines for COVID-19, resulting in several thousand randomized trials [3]. In contrast, a recently updated Cochrane systematic review identified only seven trials of physical interventions to reduce or prevent the spread of respiratory viruses conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic [4].

Usually, randomized trials of health care interventions require approval from an ethical board, and the trial data management must follow current legislation such as the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). In addition, PHSM often target whole groups of people, e.g., all students in schools or all citizens in communities, and therefore PHSM cluster trials may require implementation approval from other local or national governing institutions.

The Centre for Epidemic Interventions Research (CEIR)—a unit at the Norwegian Institute of Public Health dedicated to studying the effects of PHSM—proposed a series of PHSM trials during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, in most cases, the trials did not move beyond the drawing board. To provide insight into the barriers to carrying out such studies, we reviewed all trials initiated by the CEIR and identified the main reasons why they did not take place.

Methods

We included all proposed randomized trials of PHSM from March 2020 to February 2024 that were initiated by CEIR researchers, including trials that were proposed before CEIR was formally established (autumn 2021). For inclusion, a study protocol had to exist in some written draft form.

For each proposed trial, we reviewed the following material to identify barriers to trial implementation:Ethical review board assessments and email correspondence with ethical review board committee members and their secretaries.Email correspondence with ministry officials, municipal administrations, etc.Email correspondence with external and internal research partners.Media reports and publicly available statements from key stakeholders.

Results

Since the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, our research group has proposed 18 PHSM trials that have reached at least the draft study protocol stage. Five of the 18 proposed trials were successfully completed [5–9], and one is ongoing [10]. One of the 18 proposed trials halted after randomization due to difficulties with recruitment [11]. Of the 11 proposed trials that never materialized, four were stopped before ethical review. Three of these trials were halted due to factors outside the research group’s control, such as changes to national guidelines that rendered control groups unfeasible and the roll-out of the COVID-19 vaccine, which cast doubt on the trial’s value. Four trials did not receive ethical review board approval, mainly due to unfeasible informed consent requirements. All these were trials in primary schools that required consent from the legal guardians of all the pupils who could be exposed to the intervention under evaluation (independent of whether the pupils contributed data). Discussion

We reviewed all randomized trials proposed and initiated at the Centre for Epidemic Interventions Research (CEIR) during the first 4 years of the COVID-19 pandemic. Of the 18 proposed trials, five were completed, one was ongoing, and one halted after recruitment began. The remaining 11 trials were not implemented.

One of the key legal and ethical barriers to conducting trials was the Norwegian Health Research Act, which demands informed consent from all participants. The wording of the law implies that all projects that qualify as health research and entail comparing groups of people (e.g., schools or municipalities in cluster randomized trials) require informed consent to be exposed to the intervention from every individual within each group/cluster. In this regard, the Norwegian Health Research Act is more restrictive than, for example, the Ethical Guidelines from CIOMS (Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences), which state that “Waivers or modifications of informed consent may be necessary in some CRTs [cluster randomized trials] in which it is virtually impossible to obtain individual informed consent” [15].

Another key barrier to conducting the proposed trials was that the relevant authorities were either reluctant to carry out the trials for political or practical reasons. An illustrative example is the trial of school reopening early in the pandemic. The Norwegian government believed that the trial would not be accepted by the public and therefore chose to reject it. Similarly, when we suggested arranging mass gatherings (concerts) for young people who had been randomized to rapid test screening, the incidence of COVID-19 in Norway was relatively high, and the municipal authorities worried that allowing such a trial could worsen the situation. Naturally, decision makers have a range of concerns to consider, and in some circumstances, generating more evidence is not, and should not be, their top priority. However, the state authorities in Norway are bound by regulations to ensure a sound basis for decision-making prior to the implementation of any measures, including an assessment of the expected positive and negative effects [16]. Randomized trials are specifically mentioned in government guidelines as an approach for testing new measures prior to full implementation [16].

Other barriers to carrying out randomized trials during the pandemic were related to timing due to unpredictable shifts in the incidence of disease and continuously changing recommendations and regulations from the authorities. For instance, while we were planning a trial of close contact testing, this became a national recommendation, which made it practically impossible to run the trial. Another example is the Norwegian TRAiN study (initiated by a research group at the University of Oslo), where participants were randomized to be allowed or denied access to training centers [17]. This was one of the first trials of PHSM during the COVID-19 pandemic, but despite an impressively rapid process, by the time the trial was implemented, the incidence of COVID-19 had fallen dramatically, and only one of the more than 3000 participants tested positive for COVID-19.

Methodological and practical barriers hinder the performance of PHSM trials, e.g., the expected effect sizes of PHSM may be small, meaning that the number of participants needs to be high. Additionally, some interventions are simply not amenable to randomization and need to be evaluated in other ways. One variant of cluster trials is the “stepped wedge” design, which entails a stepwise implementation of the intervention over time, until all groups (clusters) have received the intervention [18]. The stepped wedge design can “reconcile the need for robust evaluations with political or logistical constraints” [18] and thus be an attractive option for impact evaluation of public health measures. However, there are disadvantages with this design as well, especially if carried out in infectious outbreaks where the incidence of cases can vary substantially over time and space [19]. Our proposed trial of face mask distribution in schools was indeed planned with a stepped wedge design as this was thought to be more acceptable and practical than a standard parallel randomized cluster trial (see Table 2).

There is an obvious relationship between disease incidence and some of the barriers to conducting trials, e.g., the lower the incidence is, the larger the required sample size, and the higher the incidence is, the lower the willingness of the authorities and the population to implement experimental studies, especially for diseases with high mortality or morbidity.

The trials that we succeeded in implementing had one of the following two characteristics: they did not need ethical approval, or the interventions were such that everyone who took part could be individually randomized and provide informed consent. We are aware of very few trials of PHSM during the COVID-19 pandemic that involved randomizing groups of people (cluster randomized trials). The ones we are aware of could not have been carried out in Norway since they entailed exposing individuals to interventions without their consent, e.g., a trial of distributing face masks in Bangladesh, and trials of installing air purifiers in nursing homes and schools in the UK [20–22]. The individual consent requirement seems to be less strictly applied in neighboring Sweden: We are aware of at least one planned cluster trial of an infection control intervention in nursing homes during the COVID-19 pandemic, that received Ethical Review Authority approval without requiring individual consent from all affected residents (personal communication: Ollie Östlund, Uppsala University).

To our knowledge, no attempts have been made at carrying out randomized trials of PHSM in Norway other than the mentioned TRAiN trial and those coming out of CEIR.

Context and limitations

Many of the proposed trials were stopped at an early stage, and it is likely that we would have met additional barriers if we had come further into the planning process. For example, even if the government and the regional ethics committee had approved a randomized trial of school reopening, the requirement for a large sample size would have still posed a significant practical barrier to conducting the trial, though not necessarily an insurmountable one.

We do not know whether the shortage of trials of PHSM globally is due to a lack of attempts at conducting such studies or—as was the case in Norway—that many attempts failed. We suspect the former, as we are not aware of many failed attempts at carrying out such trials in other countries.

Before the COVID-19 pandemic, the 2014–2016 Ebola outbreak that predominantly affected Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone was another epidemic that drew attention to the need for better research preparedness. Some of the lessons were similar to those seen with COVID-19, including the need for rapidly adaptable core protocols that are carefully deliberated before an outbreak; streamlined processes for obtaining ethical approval during health emergencies; inclusive engagement of stakeholders both affected by and important for the delivery of interventions; and greater international collaboration and coordination to produce reliable evidence on interventions used during health crises [23, 24].

Future steps

Specifically on PHSM, further steps have been taken to address the lack of effectiveness studies. The WHO initiative on measuring the effectiveness and impact of PHSM during health emergencies has proposed the preparation of study protocols for rapid implementation during the next crisis [25]. This could address many of the barriers we faced, especially if provisional political support and ethical approval could be secured. Similarly, the recently announced Pandemic EVIDENCE Collaboration, a partnership of academic institutions, plans to “develop ‘oven-ready’ research protocols to launch research projects rapidly during the early phases of pandemics” [26].

Discussions are ongoing in Norway about how to facilitate more randomized trials of policy interventions, and the informed consent requirement in the Health Research Act is currently under review. In this process, there are at least three competing principles for informed consent to consider: i) All health research that entails exposure to an intervention requires individual informed consent; ii) individual consent requirements may be waived in some cases, e.g., if the intervention is assessed as low-risk to the participants [27], or iii) consent is not needed if the governmental body authorizing the trial is mandated to implement the same intervention in a non-research setting, without consent [28]. The latter may overlap with the concept of community consent, or consent provided by a “gatekeeper”, i.e., “individuals or bodies (such as a school principal or municipal council) who may be called on to protect the interests of organisations or communities that are the setting for a cluster randomised trial.” [27] Importantly, this form of consent is generally not regarded is a morally defensible substitute for individual informed consent [15, 27, 29].

Conclusions

In Norway, several attempts at conducting randomized trials of PHSM during the COVID-19 pandemic have been stranded due to regulations that require informed consent from all participants and due to a lack of political support. Timing the trials was also a challenge due to unpredictability in terms of shifting disease incidence and continually changing recommendations from the authorities. Strengthening the evidence base for PHSM in pandemics will require political and public understanding and support for population-based randomized trials and a revision of the Health Research Act.
