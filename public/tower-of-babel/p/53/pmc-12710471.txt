graphB3—an interpretable graph learning approach for predicting blood–brain barrier permeability
Authors: Sumit Kumar, Shashank Yadav, Dhvani Sandip Vora, Prateek Paul, Jaspreet Kaur Dhanjal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlood–Brain Barrier (BBB) is a specialized vascular structure that protects the brain by controlling what can pass from the bloodstream into the central nervous system. BBB’s highly selective nature prevents neurotoxins and foreign substances, including drugs, from entering the brain. Understanding and predicting whether a drug can cross the BBB is hence crucial in brain-related diseases. Over the past two decades, deep learning in drug discovery has witnessed an upward trend to reduce the cost, labor, and time for finding potential drugs. Majority of recent deep learning-based studies use molecular-level physiochemical properties of known drugs, which are limited and may not be sufficient to capture patterns. Here, we propose a parameter efficient Graph Convolution-based model, graphB3, which uses detailed information about the atoms in a molecule to predict the BBB permeability of drug candidates, outperforming existing methods. In addition to high predictive performance, graphB3 also offers an explanation of which parts of the molecule are most important for crossing the BBB. graphB3 can be employed to discover new BBB-permeable compounds and potential drugs. A free and easy-to-use graphB3 web server is available at https://dhanjal-lab.iiitd.edu.in/graphb3.html, and a standalone tool available at https://github.com/dhanjal-lab/graphB3.

Introduction

The blood–brain barrier (BBB) plays a critical role in maintaining the homeostasis of the brain environment by strictly regulating the transport of molecules, restricting the passage of toxins, pathogens, and large molecules, shielding it from potential damage and maintaining optimal neuronal functions [1]. While the highly selective permeability of BBB serves as a defense mechanism for the brain, it also poses a significant challenge to drug delivery for treating central nervous system diseases. BBB inhibits the permeation of almost all tested macromolecules and 98% of small molecules [2].

The need for discovering strategies for drugs that can effectively cross the BBB arises from the growing demand to develop treatments for challenging neurological conditions. Several experimental methods have been developed to measure the BBB permeability of molecules [3]. BBB permeability for drug molecules is commonly calculated by the log-ratio (logBB) of the drug concentration in the brain to that in the blood at a steady state. Alternatively, the permeability can also be expressed as permeability-surface area product (PS, also quantified as logPS) [4]. While the experimental methods are valid tests providing valuable insights into the transport mechanisms and BBB’s integrity to assess the permeability, they are expensive, labor-intensive, and time-consuming with low throughput. Alternatively, the in-silico prediction of drugs’ BBB permeability has drawn increasing attention in an attempt to cut the cost and time required for in vivo experiments [3, 5]. In silico predictions are broadly divided into qualitative and quantitative methods. Qualitative methods aim to classify whether a molecule is BBB-permeable or not. Representations such as molecular fingerprints encoding structural features of molecules can be used to calculate drug similarity. However, similarity search methods have significant drawbacks, including activity cliffs, i.e. a leap in bioactivity upon a slight modification in a functional group. It also fails to produce any novel structures as the methods are inclined to identify compounds similar to known compounds [6]. There has been a noticeable trend in applying machine learning (ML) techniques for the BBB permeability prediction [7–9]. For quantitative methods, LogBB and LogPS have been predicted using several regression models, e.g. Linear Regression, GradientBoostingRegressor, Artificial Neural Network, etc. [9]. However, the major drawback of quantitative methods is that only a few compounds are available with known LogBB or LogPS because of the low throughput from experimental methods.

To overcome the challenge of sparse data, Meng et al. (2021) have created B3DB, a large benchmark dataset for BBB permeability of compounds compiled from 50 published resources [10]. The dataset has 7807 compounds, including 1024 compounds with known LogBB values. Using B3DB, Tang et al. developed Deep-B3, a deep learning-based ensemble model combining Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) models [11]. However, the multimodal framework is computationally expensive with over 27 million parameters. Furthermore, CNN and RNN are better suited for images and sequences respectively [12, 13]. As molecules can better be represented as graphs for the task of drug discovery, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) or Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) are shown to outperform several deep learning models, including ANN, CNN, and RNN. Kalakoti et al. proposed a GCN-based model that distinguishes interactions between active, inactive, and intermediate drug−target pairs [14]. Stokes et al. created a GCN-based model for antibiotic discovery with a hit rate of 51.5% upon experimental validation of the top compounds, proving how GNN-learned features outperform hand-crafted features used in primitive ML/deep learning models [15].

Here, we present a GCN-based graph-level classifier, named graphB3, to leverage the graph representation of molecules to predict BBB permeability. We have trained our model on the reviewed B3DB dataset, and explainable AI (XAI) has been used to provide the most relevant atomic-level features and molecular substructures contributing to the model’s predictions.

Results

Developing graphB3 to overcome challenges in existing methods

In this study, we have proposed a GCN-based model called graphB3, shown in Fig. 1, to predict the BBB permeability of the molecules and find the features and substructures significantly contributing towards BBB permeation. The GCN model in graphB3 has a total of 107,393 parameters.

The model architecture of graphB3. (a) Graph representation of a SMILES string by DeepChem where each graph-object has 2 components (b) atomic–level feature vectors, and (c) an adjacency matrix. (d) In graph convolution layers, atoms update feature vectors by aggregating neighboring atoms’ features through message-passing. (e) After the message-passing, (f) the final feature matrix for each graph/molecule is flattened by the global-add pooling method to get (g) the even feature vector of each graph. (h) The flattened feature vectors of all the graphs go through (i) a multilayer perceptron to get the predictions BBB-permeable or BBB-impermeable. GNNExplainer is employed to get the most relevant substructures contributing towards its prediction. For a molecule with the prediction (j), node features or edges are iteratively perturbed (k), (l), (m) and predictions are re-estimated and each node feature or edge is assigned a score called node mask and edge mask respectively. graphB3 highlights the most relevant substructure(s) of importance based on edge masks.

The classes being imbalanced (data distribution is shown in Table 1), the train, validation and test sets were split to follow the same distribution of both classes using stratified sampling, and the model performance was checked by the MCC score. Supplementary File 1 (Fig. S1) depicts the violin plot for the evaluation metrics on the test set with 10 different random seeds and 10 different split-states of training and validation sets ensuring robustness in the model’s evaluation. To address class imbalance, we applied a positive class weight in the binary cross-entropy with logits loss function, proportionate to the ratio of negative to positive samples in the training set, thereby emphasizing the minority (BBB-impermeable) class during optimization. We performed extensive hyperparameter tuning across key parameters, including the number of GCN layers, embedding size, pooling strategy, activation function, optimizer, learning rate, dropout, and weight decay, as summarized in Table 2. A learning rate scheduler was used to reduce the learning rate if the validation loss did not improve, using varying patience and decay factors. The model was trained for up to 1000 epochs, and the checkpoint with the highest MCC on the validation set was selected. The final optimized model achieved an average accuracy of 0.88, AUC-ROC of 0.94, MCC of 0.74, and F1 score of 0.91 on the held-out test set.

Evaluation of the model’s performance in comparison to Deep-B3

The performance of graphB3 was compared with the recent state-of-the-art method for BBB permeability called Deep-B3 [11]. Deep-B3 is a multimodal framework that combines the input data in several different forms, including images of molecules, SMILES sequences, and tabular format data e.g. molecular descriptors and fingerprints. While Deep-B3 is a powerful tool for predicting BBB permeability, it lacks the interpretability that graphB3 offers, as graphB3 can explain its predictions. Additionally, Deep-B3 requires ~27.7 M parameters, whereas graphB3 only uses 107,393—a 99.99% reduction in trainable parameters. The Average Stochastic Gradient Descent Weight-Dropped Long Short-Term Memory (AWD-LSTM) [16] employed in Deep-B3, like most LSTM models rely on pretrained word embeddings with fixed vocabularies, where the embeddings for words are learned during training. Words absent in the pretrained vocabulary are often replaced by unknown (<unk>) tokens, potentially leading to information loss. Although these models can be updated with new vocabulary, doing so for large models like Deep-B3 is computationally expensive. Consequently, Deep-B3 may struggle with newly discovered compounds that have SMILES notations not covered by the pretrained vocabulary. In contrast, graphB3 doesn’t require a pretrained vocabulary since it operates with fixed atomic-level features, which is highly advantageous given the vast chemical space, encompassing ~1060 possible drug-like molecules [17].

The Fig. 2 demonstrates that graphB3 outperforms Deep-B3 across multiple evaluation metrics. Specifically, the performance of the methods was compared on the Receiver Operating Characteristics (ROCs), Fig. 2a, the Precision-Recall, Fig. 2b and the F1-MCC, Fig. 2c, which would generally be better suited to evaluating on imbalanced datasets than the other metrics. Beyond the standard threshold value of 0.5 for binary classification, we further evaluated MCC and F1 scores across a range of threshold values, where graphB3 consistently outperformed Deep-B3. Additional evaluation metrics, including accuracy (ACC), balanced accuracy (BACC), MCC, F1, precision, and recall, are provided in Table 3, with results based on 10-fold cross-validation. Explainability through graphB3

While recent methods like Deep-B3 predict BBB permeability of molecules, their results often lack interpretability. Tang et al. used attention weights to report feature importance in Deep-B3, but the complex features derived from images, texts, descriptors, and fingerprints are difficult to interpret through human inspection. We used the GNNExplainer to interpret atom-level features and relevant substructures influencing BBB permeability. This method was selected for its superior accuracy over alternatives like GRAD and ATT [18]. We applied GNNExplainer to molecules with high-confidence predictions (P > .9 for BBB-permeable and P < .1 for BBB-impermeable) to obtain the node and edge importance masks. The outputs helped identify key features in an instance-specific manner, suitable for our heterogenous dataset. The explainer was run 10 times with different seeds and performance was evaluated using the Unfaithfulness metric [19], consistently achieving a score of 0. Additional implementation details are mentioned in Supplementary File 1 (Note 1, Table 2). Figure 3 compares the node feature importance of BBB-permeable and impermeable molecules in our filtered train and test set. Following Fig. 3, biases from features like Carbon, Formal Charge: 0, and the hybridization state of sp3 could be noticed in the plot as a majority of the atoms in organic molecules have these common features. However, aromaticity and contribution of halogens like chlorine and fluorine was quite clear among the BBB-permeable compounds. Additionally, a majority of the features showed higher importance scores for BBB-permeable compounds, which may have arisen from the bias following the class imbalance in our test set. Interestingly, features like atom degree 2, Hydrogen bond donor, Sulphur, and Oxygen were showing more importance in the case of BBB-impermeable molecules despite class imbalance which could be indicating the dominance of hydrophilic functional groups e.g. -OH, -COOH, -SO2, etc.

To further investigate BBB-permeable and BBB-impermeable molecules, we used edge masks to highlight a substructure over a molecule. Here, we did not keep any threshold criteria for filtering edge masks to avoid missing any substructures, rather we mapped the continuous scale of edge masks with a color gradient scheme, i.e. red for BBB-permeable and blue for BBB-impermeable compounds. Therefore, the darker the color the higher the importance of the edges or substructures.

Upon investigation, we observed that most substructures highlighted in BBB-permeable molecules were predominantly lipophilic, as illustrated in Fig. 4, which was in line with the inference drawn from the feature important analysis shown in Fig. 3. This observation aligns with the well-established fact that lipophilicity is a key factor in passive diffusion, the primary mechanism by which most small BBB-permeable molecules cross the BBB [20, 21]. In contrast, BBB-impermeable molecules predominantly featured hydrophilic groups as highlighted regions of edge importance, consistent with the atom-feature importance bar plot shown in Fig. 3. While hydrophilic groups dominated in BBB-impermeable compounds, some BBB-permeable compounds also displayed highlighted hydrophilic groups, suggesting the need for a more detailed analysis.

Detailed substructure analysis using the GNNExplainer module of graphB3

To further investigate the chemical differences between BBB-permeable and BBB-impermeable molecules, as highlighted by our explainer, we extracted substructures with high edge importance (edge mask > 0.5) in SMARTS format and analyzed them for the presence of known molecular fingerprints—Morgan fingerprints. Since GNNExplainer initializes the model with random weights, we repeated this process 10 times using different random seeds to ensure consistency in the extracted substructures. Across all iterations, the identified substructures remained largely consistent, with only slight variations in their counts (details provided in Supplementary File 3). This confirmed that the explainer model’s findings were not biased by its random initialization. The chemical names of the extracted molecular fingerprints were then identified by matching their SMARTS patterns using PubChem. We computed the abundance of each fingerprint both within and across the BBB-permeable and BBB-impermeable compound groups. A comprehensive substructure analysis, detailed in Supplementary File 3, revealed distinct molecular features that differentiate BBB-permeable compounds from those that are impermeable. Only the substructures where the frequency of occurrence across the two groups was different with a P < .01, as computed by Mann–Whitney U Test have been listed.

Discussion

We present a novel tool, called graphB3, for predicting BBB permeability using GCNs. GCN techniques have demonstrated superior performance compared to other deep learning approaches. Beyond high performance, graphB3 offers an XAI-based estimation of the most significant atomic-level features and the substructures which contribute to BBB permeation.

The BBB-permeable compound set was characterized by the presence of aromatic rings and long alkyl chains. These structural features enhance lipophilicity, a key determinant of passive diffusion across the BBB, while also providing steric protection that improves metabolic stability [20, 21]. Dense halogenation, particularly with trichloride, trifluoride, trifluoromethyl, and trichloromethyl groups, emerged as a significant factor contributing to BBB permeability [22–26]. These halogen atoms enhance lipophilicity and modulate electronic properties, such as dipole moments, facilitating the traversal of the lipophilic endothelial barrier. Additionally, chlorobenzene and fluorobenzene were enriched in the BBB-permeable dataset. On the other side, halogenated benzene groups, if present in BBB-impermeable compounds, were often conjugated with additional polar functional groups, such as carbonyl (=O) and hydroxyl (-OH), which likely hindered their permeability (Supplementary File 1 (Fig. S4)). Furthermore, the presence of carbamate and urea derivatives in the BBB-permeable set introduced hydrogen bonding potential while maintaining a balance between lipophilicity and polarity—an essential factor for effective BBB penetration. Some other substructures found to be promoting the permeability across BBB, and have been shown in literature to be a contributing have been listed in Supplementary File 3.

In contrast, the set of BBB-impermeable compounds was enriched with sulfur-based functionalities, including multi-thiol, sulfonium, and sulfamic acid moieties. These groups significantly increase hydrophilicity, thereby reducing the compound’s ability to traverse the lipophilic BBB. Thiols, in particular, are prone to oxidation and can form disulfide bonds, which may further restrict permeability. Similarly, nitrogen-based functionalities such as guanidines, amidines, and imine linkages are highly polar and basic, leading to strong ionic interactions that hinder passive diffusion. These properties make such compounds more likely to be retained in aqueous environments or subjected to active efflux by transporters like P-glycoprotein, which prevents their entry into the brain.

Both BBB-permeable and impermeable compound sets also shared some common functional groups, including amines, alcohols, and unsaturated carbon chains. However, their structural integration was markedly different. In the BBB-permeable set, these groups were incorporated into heavily substituted or poly-halogenated scaffolds, optimizing their lipophilic and steric properties for efficient BBB penetration. For example, secondary and tertiary amines in this set were often embedded within hydrophobic frameworks, reducing their polarity while retaining hydrogen bonding capability. Conversely, in the BBB-impermeable set, these groups were associated with sulfur or nitrogen-rich scaffolds in simpler, less sterically hindered arrangements, amplifying their hydrophilic nature and decreasing their likelihood of crossing the BBB.

This analysis underscores the critical role of substructure composition, electronic effects, and steric factors in modulating BBB permeability. The findings provide valuable insights into the design of compounds that optimize the delicate balance of lipophilicity, hydrogen bonding, and steric protection required for effective BBB penetration. In conclusion, a GCN-based model called graphB3 is developed to predict the BBB permeability of compounds with easy-to-interpret explanations drawn from a graph XAI method. With only 33 atomic-level features and a total of 107,393 parameters, graphB3 shows superior performance over the baseline methods for BBB permeability prediction on an independent test set. For rigorous evaluation, the model was tested with 10 different random seeds along with 10 different random train-validation split-states to avoid biases from initial weights and data splitting for training and validation. Additionally, using the high-performing model graphB3 we also analyzed the importance of each atomic-level feature for BBB-permeable and BBB-impermeable molecules. A web server for graphB3 (https://dhanjal-lab.iiitd.edu.in/graphb3.html) and a standalone tool (https://github.com/dhanjal-lab/graphB3) is also provided to help researchers find the BBB-permeable molecules and interpret possible substructures for BBB permeability. We believe that the potential of graphB3 can help us find treatment for CNS-related diseases and conditions hindered by the BBB.

Methods

Data collection

In this study, we used the B3DB dataset, currently the largest benchmark dataset for BBB-permeable (positive class) and BBB-impermeable (negative class) molecules curated from 50 peer-reviewed literature or publicly available datasets [10]. The dataset contains 7807 molecules (4956 BBB-permeable and 2851 BBB-impermeable molecules), including 1058 molecules with logBB values indicating the level of permeation. For our classification model, 7800 molecules were used, and seven compounds, including gases, namely, Oxygen, Xenon, Argon, Lithium, Neon, Krypton, and water, were removed as their Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) representation could not calculate the features. SMILES is a notation to represent a chemical compound by encoding its structure in ASCII strings [27]. In case both isomeric and canonical SMILES were available for a compound, priority was given to the isomeric SMILES as the chirality of a molecule is known to influence BBB permeability [3].

Before splitting the dataset into train, validation, and test sets, a BBB distribution plot (Supplementary File 1 (Fig. S5) was drawn based on their molecular weight using kernel density estimation (KDE). KDE is a non-parametric kernel estimation method commonly used for data smoothing problems where inferences about the population are made based on a finite data sample. Following the distribution curve (Supplementary File 1 (Fig. S5)), all molecules over the molecular weight of 1202 were found to be BBB-impermeable. To avoid bias toward high-weight molecules, the 30 largest molecules (all greater than 1202 Da) were excluded from the training set. The selected 7537 molecules were first split into a ratio of 80:20, i.e. 6029 and 1508 molecules for train-validation and test sets, respectively. The train-validation set was further split into the same ratio of train and validation sets. While training the model with 10 different random seeds, the split state for the train and validation sets was different for each seed to avoid biases from seeds as well as the split state. Hence, we merged the 10-fold cross-validation with different random seeds for rigorous evaluation. The data was split using stratified k-fold sampling to avoid data imbalance among the classes from the split. Finally, 263 molecules held-out earlier were added to the test set, allowing evaluation of generalization on structurally unusual compounds. Therefore, the final training, validation, and test sets had 4823, 1206, and 1771 molecules, respectively. However, we also trained the model without excluding these 263 molecules (i.e. using a standard train-validation-test split). As shown in the Supplementary File 2 (Sheet 2), this yielded no significant improvement, confirming that the exclusion does not introduce bias but rather strengthened the model.

Feature extraction

The SMILES representation of the molecules was used to compute atomic-level features or descriptors, treating each molecule as a graph for graph-level predictions in drug discovery. Unlike traditional molecular descriptors such as those from RDKit or Mordred, the features are derived at the atomic (node) level rather than the molecular level [29, 30]. Furthermore, incorporating edge and bond-level features into a GCN model can enhance our understanding of the relationships among atoms (nodes) within a molecule, enabling the exploration of complex patterns among molecules for ligand-based virtual screening. To achieve this, we used DeepChem featurizers to calculate the features of the molecules at the atomic/node level [31]. MolGraphConvFeaturizer was used to calculate the features. By default, MolGraphConvFeaturizer provides 30 atomic-level features, which include atom type, formal charge, hybridization, hydrogen bonding, aromaticity, degree, and the number of hydrogen atoms to name a few. All the default features are in a one-hot encoded format except for the formal charge. We removed all features that were not applicable to our dataset and contained only NULL values and converted formal charge to a one-hot encoded format. In addition to the default 30 atomic-level features, we also used the one-hot encoded representation of atomic chirality.

In the current version of DeepChem (2.7.2), the atom indices of a molecule in DeepChem featurizers do not match the atom indices of a molecule by RDKit. In our study, matching atom indices in both the software were essential as atom or bond importance scores generated by the XAI model needs molecule images by RDKit to visualize the predicted substructures. Supplementary File 1 (Table S1) shows the features considered for the model training. DeepChem featurizers also need SMILES as input to produce atomic-level and bond-level features. In this study, only the atomic (node) features were utilized. Hence, each batch object represents each graph or a molecule and the edge index contains information regarding the connections between nodes, indicating which nodes are linked to one another. This is required for a GCN model since the message passing is done among neighboring nodes. An alternative approach would involve utilizing an adjacency matrix to represent node connections. However, it is worth noting that the adjacency matrix would require more storage space compared to an edge index tensor.

The convolutional layers in GCN work as message-passing layers where each node gathers information from its neighbouring nodes. Hence, the size of the feature vector of each node increases as the vector passes through a convolutional layer. Batching is a common practice to optimize computation and ensure efficient memory usage in CNN for images and GCN for graph-level predictions. In contrast to CNN where images of different sizes can be fixed through padding or rescaling to fixed size matrices for parallel computation, Supplementary File 1 (Fig. S6) shows batching in GCN that involves adjacency matrices of each graph stacked diagonally one after another to present all the graphs in a batch as a single large graph while preserving the identity of each graph inside the batch. This method follows the neighborhood normalization, which may reduce the expressivity of graphs [33, 34]. Since our study involves a wide range of molecules, we chose GraphConv as the convolutional model as it helps GCN to be more expressive in distinguishing graph structures. To ensure a fair and comprehensive evaluation, we also benchmarked other multiple GNN architectures, including GCNConv, SAGEConv, GINConv, and GATConv. Among these, GraphConv consistently achieved the most stable and highest performance across all evaluation metrics in 10-fold cross-validation on the held-out test set, details are available in Supplementary File 2 (Sheet 3).

Model evaluation

The proposed model has been evaluated with several evaluation metrics commonly known for binary classification tasks, including ACC, BACC, F1 score, confusion matrix, Precision, Recall, AUC-ROC curve, AUC-PR curve, Sensitivity, Specificity, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). In contrast to single-threshold metrics, the MCC-F1 plot compares a classification model’s performance across varying thresholds providing a more robust evaluation, especially for imbalanced datasets [35, 36].

Key Points

A graph convolutional network (GCN)-based model, graphB3, is developed to predict blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability using molecular graph representations, achieving superior performance compared to state-of-the-art models, while significantly reducing computational complexity.
