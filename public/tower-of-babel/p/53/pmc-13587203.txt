Microbiome and metabolomics insights into pediatric urinary tract infection: a narrative review
Authors: Jung Won Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections in infants and a significant cause of antibiotic exposure. Recent advances in culture-independent sequencing and improved urine culture techniques have revealed that urine contains resident microbial communities, collectively known as the urinary microbiome (urobiome). Additionally, emerging evidence supports the existence of a gut-bladder axis, where intestinal dysbiosis may lead to uropathogen colonization, immune modulation, and UTI recurrence. This review summarizes current pediatric research connecting alterations in the urinary and gut microbiomes to UTI susceptibility, recurrence, and clinical outcomes, specifically in infants. We also address the unique methodological challenges in pediatric urobiome research and outline future directions for applying microbiome and metabolomics insights to clinical practice.

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are the most commonly diagnosed bacterial infections in pediatric populations, particularly prevalent during infancy and early childhood [1]. Traditionally, UTIs have been understood as ascending infections caused by fecal contamination, highlighting a close anatomical and functional relationship between the gut and urinary tract. However, in the past decade, the understanding of the microbiome has advanced significantly. What was once thought to be a sterile environment, urine, is now recognized as hosting a resident microbial community known as the urinary microbiota or urobiome [2-5].

Emerging evidence indicates that the gut microbiome plays a crucial role in systemic health by regulating immune function, inflammation, and susceptibility to organ-specific diseases [6,7]. Pediatric UTIs are increasingly linked to dysbiosis in both the gut and urinary microbiomes, including a decrease in protective commensal species such as Lactobacillus spp. [8]. Additionally, changes in the perineal microbiome may increase the likelihood of UTIs in vulnerable individuals, particularly young girls, by promoting colonization by uropathogens [9].

Despite the growing interest in microbiome research, studies specifically targeting pediatric populations remain limited [10]. Furthermore, new data suggest that complex inter-organ communication networks, such as the gut-bladder-brain axis and the gut-vagina-bladder axis, may also play a role in influencing UTI susceptibility [7,11]. Recent metabolomics-based studies have advanced this field by revealing changes in fecal metabolic profiles in children with UTIs, offering functional insights that extend beyond mere taxonomic shifts in microbial composition [12]. Metabolomics measures the downstream metabolic activities of both microbes and hosts, thereby connecting microbial structure to biological function. This review summarizes current knowledge about the pediatric urinary microbiome, its physiological importance, and its involvement in childhood UTIs.

The urinary microbiome in children

Urine has long been considered sterile; however, advancements in culture-independent techniques, such as 16S rRNA gene sequencing and expanded quantitative urine culture, have revealed the presence of functional bacterial communities in the urinary tract of healthy, asymptomatic individuals [2-5]. These resident microbial communities are collectively known as the urobiome. In healthy children, the urinary microbiota typically includes genera such as Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, Gardnerella, Corynebacterium, and Staphylococcus. However, it has a markedly lower biomass and diversity compared to the gut microbiome [13]. Among these taxa, Lactobacillus species are widely recognized as protective commensals in the genitourinary tract. They help maintain an acidic microenvironment and produce antimicrobial substances that inhibit the overgrowth of uropathogens [14]. Depletion of Lactobacillus in children with UTIs may therefore represent a state of local dysbiosis that directly increases susceptibility to infection. The urinary microbiome plays a critical role in maintaining homeostasis in the urinary tract. It contributes to the integrity of the urothelial barrier, provides resistance to colonization by uropathogens, and modulates local innate immune responses within the bladder [15,16]. Emerging evidence suggests that the urobiome may influence neurotransmission and bladder sensory signaling, thereby playing a role in the regulation of lower urinary tract function [15]. Disruption of the balanced microbial ecosystem, known as urinary microbiome dysbiosis, has been increasingly linked to various pathological conditions. Dysbiosis is associated with a higher risk of UTIs, especially recurrent infections, as well as lower urinary tract dysfunction, which includes bladder overactivity and impaired voiding [8,17]. Rather than being solely a result of pathogen invasion, UTIs are now increasingly understood as consequences of ecological imbalance within the urinary microbiome. This imbalance is often exacerbated by antibiotic exposure, host immune dysregulation, and underlying urinary tract abnormalities. The recognition of the urobiome has shifted the conceptual framework of urinary tract health and disease from a strictly pathogen-centered model to an ecosystem-based perspective. This shift opens new avenues for microbiome-based biomarkers and non-antibiotic preventive strategies.

Microbiome dysbiosis in pediatric UTI

Pediatric UTIs are increasingly recognized as disorders of microbial imbalance rather than solely infections caused by a single pathogen [8,17]. During an acute febrile UTI, urinary microbial diversity decreases, protective taxa such as Lactobacillus decline, and uropathogens, including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella, and Enterococcus, become predominant [8,17]. Several pediatric studies have established a direct link between microbial dysbiosis and UTI risk. For instance, Paalanne et al. [10] found that children with UTIs had a higher abundance of Enterobacter in their gut microbiome, while Peptostreptococcaceae were more prevalent in healthy controls, indicating that intestinal microbial composition may influence UTI susceptibility. Similarly, Kinneman et al. [18] reported a significant reduction in urinary microbial alpha diversity among children under 48 months with UTIs, revealing distinct clustering of urinary microbiome profiles compared to non-UTI controls. Microbiome alterations have also been noted in children with underlying urological abnormalities. Vitko et al. [19] demonstrated that children with vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) exhibited unique urinary commensal bacterial profiles and altered metabolomic signatures compared to those without VUR. This suggests that host anatomy and urinary tract pathology may shape the urinary microbial ecosystem and contribute to infection susceptibility [19]. Additionally, a review by Lee and Chung [20] highlighted the growing importance of urinary microbiome dysbiosis in the pathogenesis of pediatric UTIs, reinforcing the need for an ecosystem-based understanding of the disease.

Metabolomics: emerging evidence

By profiling small-molecule metabolites, metabolomics enables the functional interpretation of microbiome dysbiosis in pediatric UTIs. Alterations in energy, amino acid, and bile acid metabolism identified through metabolomic analyses mirror reduced microbial diversity and the loss of beneficial short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)–producing bacteria in the gut and urinary microbiome during infection.

SCFAs are crucial microbial metabolites generated by the fermentation of dietary fibers by gut microbiota and are vital for host-microbe interactions. The primary SCFAs–acetate, propionate, and butyrate–support intestinal barrier integrity and exert systemic anti-inflammatory effects by modulating immune signaling pathways (Table 1) [21,22]. In these interactions, microbiome metabolites, including SCFAs and inflammasomes, play significant roles. Inflammasomes, which are multiprotein complexes, can initiate inflammatory responses, triggering inflammation and pyroptosis, ultimately contributing to disease development. SCFAs are essential for immune cell migration, cytokine production, and maintaining cellular homeostasis [22]. Additionally, SCFAs may impact the central nervous system through the gut-brain axis, with emerging implications for the gut-bladder axis [21,22].

Disruption of SCFA-producing microbial communities has been linked to increased susceptibility to infections. Worby et al. [23] found, using metabolomics-based analysis, that SCFA-producing taxa, especially butyrate-producing bacteria, were significantly reduced in women with recurrent UTIs. In pediatric populations, Vitko et al. [19] investigated urinary metabolomic profiles in children with VUR, a condition associated with recurrent UTIs. The analysis revealed alterations in the bacteria-associated metabolic pathways such as glutamate degradation, methyl-citrate cycle, and bile acid metabolism. These findings suggest a link between metabolic changes and bacterial metabolism in pediatric UTIs.

Clinical implications and future directions

Recognizing the urinary microbiome presents new opportunities for enhancing the diagnosis and management of pediatric UTIs. Microbiome-based diagnostics may aid in differentiating between true infections and asymptomatic bacteriuria, especially in children with structural abnormalities [16]. Early research suggests that modulating the microbiome through probiotics, dietary changes, or targeted manipulation of microbial metabolites may serve as a promising preventive strategy [24,25].

One potential preventive strategy involves probiotic supplementation, particularly with Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species, which are known to maintain mucosal homeostasis through lactic acid and SCFA production. These organisms may suppress uropathogen colonization by lowering local pH, competing for epithelial adhesion sites, and modulating innate immune responses. In pediatric populations, probiotics offer a relatively safe and noninvasive adjunct to conventional therapies, with the potential to reduce antibiotic exposure and recurrence rates of UTIs [26]. Dietary interventions aimed at enriching microbiota-accessible carbohydrates, such as dietary fiber and resistant starches, represent another promising approach. These substrates promote the growth of SCFA-producing bacteria and enhance microbial metabolic outputs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate. Increased SCFA availability may strengthen intestinal barrier integrity and exert systemic anti-inflammatory effects, indirectly supporting urinary tract immune defense.

Dietary modulation is particularly attractive in children, as it can be incorporated into long-term preventive strategies with minimal risk [27]. Beyond altering microbial composition, targeted manipulation of microbial metabolites is emerging as a novel therapeutic concept. Strategies may include supplementation with SCFAs or SCFA precursors, modulation of bile acid metabolism, or interventions designed to enhance specific metabolic pathways associated with epithelial protection and immune regulation [28]. Such metabolite-focused approaches shift the therapeutic paradigm from “who is present” to “what functions are preserved or restored,” aligning closely with metabolomics-driven precision medicine. Future approaches in precision medicine that integrate microbiome, metabolomic, and host genomic data may allow for individualized risk stratification and treatment of UTIs [29]. However, large-scale, longitudinal studies in pediatric populations are necessary to establish normative urobiome profiles and clarify the causal relationships between dysbiosis, recurrent UTIs, and long-term renal outcomes.

Conclusions

Advances in microbiome science have transformed our understanding of pediatric UTIs, shifting the perspective from a simple pathogen-driven disease to one that involves complex interactions among urinary and gut microbial communities. The urinary microbiome plays a crucial role in maintaining bladder homeostasis and supporting host defense, while gut-derived bacteria and metabolites affect susceptibility to infections. A comprehensive characterization of these microbial ecosystems may pave the way for microbiome-based diagnostics and therapies that could enhance outcomes for children with UTIs.
