Quantitative Microbial Cell-Free DNA Sequencing From Plasma: A Potential Biomarker for the Diagnosis of Staphylococcal Infection of Cardiac Implantable Electronic Devices
Authors: Adolf W Karchmer, N Jenifer Kaufman, Sarah Y Park, Ronuk M Modi, Ahmed Abdul-Azim, Polly van den Berg, Jason D Matos, Daniel B Kramer, Peter J Zimetbaum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia in patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) is often associated with infective endocarditis (CIED-IE). The CIED-IE diagnosis is syndromic. Diagnostic uncertainty is common. Frequently, these patients are classified as having possible CIED-IE, resulting in guideline-noncompliant treatment and heterogeneous outcomes. Improved outcomes require accurate diagnoses. In these patients, we evaluated whether metagenomic sequencing of microbial cell-free DNA (mcfDNA) in serial plasma specimens could improve diagnostic precision.MethodsWe studied 16 patients with staphylococcal bacteremia who were classified with definite or possible CIED-IE and recommended for device removal, if there was a positive blood culture within 7 days and no concurrent deep infection. Plasma specimens obtained at consent, before extraction, and during 96 hours after extraction underwent metagenomic sequencing and quantification of staphylococcal mcfDNA.ResultsIn 10 of 11 patients with definite CIED-IE, mcfDNA persisted during antibiotic therapy for prolonged durations (median, 11 days [interquartile range {IQR}, 7.5–15 days]). In these cases, mcfDNA concentration in plasma obtained early after lead extraction increased significantly and thereafter decreased rapidly. In 5 cases of possible CIED-IE, mcfDNA was undetectable after 6 days (IQR, 5.5–7.5 days) of antibiotic therapy and remained undetectable after CIED extraction. These mcfDNA patterns differ significantly (P = .001), suggesting 2 distinct patient populations: 1 with definite CIED-IE and 1 without lead infection.ConclusionsIf confirmed, these mcfDNA patterns can serve as biomarkers, together with clinical features, to improve precision in diagnosing or rejecting S. aureus CIED-IE. Strategically timed mcfDNA testing before and after CIED extraction may aid in planning therapy.Keywords: cardiac implantable electronic devices, CIED infection, infective endocarditis, plasma microbial cell-free DNA

Cardiac implantable electronic device infective endocarditis (CIED-IE) is a therapeutic challenge and a major cause of morbidity and mortality. In a Danish population study, the 10-year cumulative risk of Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia ranged from 2.0% to 3.7% in de novo recipients of various CIEDs [1]. Among patients with a CIED who experience S. aureus bacteremia, CIED infections occurred in 21%–51% [2–6]. Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) also commonly infect CIEDs. Extracting infected CIEDs during antibiotic treatment, compared to device retention, has been associated with improved survival [7]. Treatment guidelines recommend removing all components of definitely infected CIEDs [8–12]. To reduce morbidity and mortality, this recommendation has been extended to patients with S. aureus or CoNS bacteremia and possible CIED infection, even if device infection is not anatomically confirmed [9, 11, 12].

In patients with a CIED and staphylococcal bacteremia, the decision to remove the device is often challenging due to uncertainty regarding device infection and concern for extraction-related complications. There is no gold standard for the clinical diagnosis of CIED-IE. Transesophageal echocardiography, the primary imaging technology used to anatomically confirm CIED lead infection, has less than optimal sensitivity and specificity [13, 14]. Although combining fluorine-18-fludeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography and echocardiography increases sensitivity (retaining specificity), detection of intracardiac infection remains suboptimal [9, 12, 15, 16]. Conflicting reports of treatment outcomes may result from diagnostic imprecision. For example, CIED patients with S. aureus bacteremia classified as possible CIED-IE by the European Heart Rhythm Association (EHRA) criteria did not experience a significant survival benefit from treatment that included device removal [5]. Studies have questioned the benefit of device explantation in patients with S. aureus bacteremia who are not classified as definite CIED-IE [2, 6, 17]. However, among CIED patients with S. aureus bacteremia without evident CIED infection, bacteremia in some relapsed after treatment without device removal, but empiric device removal was associated with a 1-year survival benefit in others [4]. Thus, improved diagnostic precision is needed to clarify the role of device removal in treating these patients.

The concentration and duration of S. aureus cell-free DNA detected in plasma or serum by metagenomic sequencing or highly sensitive polymerase chain reaction differs in patients with endocarditis compared to patients with uncomplicated S. aureus bacteremia [18–20]. We hypothesized that the kinetics of staphylococcal microbial cell-free DNA (mcfDNA) in plasma determined by metagenomic sequencing could inform the diagnosis and planning of therapy for CIED patients with staphylococcal bacteremia. To evaluate this hypothesis among patients classified as definite or possible staphylococcal CIED-IE, we studied mcfDNA concentrations in serial plasma specimens obtained during antibiotic treatment, including specimens immediately before and following device removal.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient Selection and Specimen Collection

We prospectively studied CIED patients with staphylococcal bacteremia admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center from November 2019 through December 2023 for treatment of CIED-IE. Treating physicians determined patient management. We screened all patients with a CIED who were hospitalized with staphylococcal bacteremia. Patients aged ≥18 years with suspected CIED-IE for whom CIED removal was recommended and who provided informed written consent were enrolled if a positive blood culture was obtained within 7 calendar days, regardless of duration of antibiotic therapy. Patients were eligible for enrollment if entry portals for bacteremia and concurrent staphylococcal infections were treated. We excluded patients with deep staphylococcal infection, including left-sided endocarditis, wherein total source control was not assured.

Blood specimens were collected from patients on consenting; every 24 to 48 hours on therapy prior to extraction; immediately before and following CIED extraction (the latter termed “on extraction”); and 1 hour, 2 hours, 12–24 hours, and 48–96 hours after extraction. Specimens were collected by venipuncture or, when necessary, from a central line, using a 10-mL K2 ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tube. Specimens were promptly centrifuged and frozen at −20°C. When specimen collections for a patient were complete, specimens were shipped overnight, frozen, to the Karius, Inc laboratory.

The study was approved by Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center Committee on Clinical Investigation (#FWA00003245) and was Helsinki Declaration compliant. Each patient or their legally authorized representative provided written informed consent.

Clinical Data

Clinical data were abstracted from patients’ electronic medical records; these included medical history and physical examination findings, evidence of portal of entry for bacteremia and sites of infection, dates and results of blood and other cultures, results of diagnostic imaging including echocardiography, operative findings, pathology reports, and details of antibiotic treatment. Our hospital clinical microbiology laboratory used standard techniques to speciate recovered bacteria and determined staphylococcal antimicrobial susceptibility with Vitek (bioMérieux) or Phoenix (Becton Dickinson) gram-positive panels. Microbiology data from referral hospitals were determined in their diagnostic laboratories. All patients were hospitalized through emergency departments where generally 2 blood culture sets were collected simultaneously and promptly followed by antibiotic therapy. Accordingly, positive blood cultures on admission were considered a single set, if not indicated otherwise, and reported as a calendar day of bacteremia.

Definitions

Duration of bacteremia was defined as calendar days of positive blood cultures until cultures were consistently negative (minimum of 48 hours). Duration of antibiotic therapy to a clinical event was calculated as calendar days of pathogen-appropriate, guideline-consistent endocarditis therapy, starting the day therapy was initiated. Using the EHRA 2019 criteria [9] and medical record data, we classified suspected CIED-IE during treatment and before mcfDNA data were known. Definite CIED-IE required 2 major criteria (bacteremia on at least 2 days plus direct anatomic or microbiologic evidence of lead infection). Possible CIED-IE required 1 major plus a minor criterion or 3 minor criteria (admission day bacteremia, predisposition, or temperature >38°C).

mcfDNA Metagenomic Sequencing: Karius Test

Blood specimens were shipped to the Karius, Inc laboratory (Redwood City, California), certified under the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments of 1988 and accredited by the College of American Pathologists, for detection, identification, and quantitation of mcfDNA in plasma as previously described [21, 22]. Sequencing data were analyzed using the Karius Test bioinformatic pipeline 3.15, designed to report detected microbes from among >1000 microbes as long as the minimum sequencing coverage metrics required by quality controls are met and to provide absolute plasma concentration of mcfDNA in DNA molecules per microliter (MPM) for each microbe detected. The test detects 7 antimicrobial resistance markers, including SCCmec, mecA, and mecC, and reports S. aureus methicillin susceptibility or resistance [23]. The inability to determine antimicrobial resistance (eg, because of insufficient DNA sequences or ambiguous linkages to a qualified pathogen) is reported as indeterminant. Our cases experienced only staphylococcal bacteremia; thus, we report staphylococcal mcfDNA. Sequencing mcfDNA is pathogen agnostic; other microbes, not considered clinically relevant, were occasionally identified along with staphylococci, particularly from specimens collected from indwelling central lines. These mcfDNA data were not analyzed for this study.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR [ie, 25th–75th percentiles]), expressed as days. To compare the kinetic patterns of mcfDNA between groups (categorical data), the Fisher exact test was used with statistical significance set at P < .05.

RESULTS

Study Population

Twenty-one patients with a CIED and staphylococcal bacteremia were enrolled. In 1 patient, extraction resulted in fracture of a lead with a fragment remaining in the coronary sinus. This patient relapsed after 6 weeks of vancomycin therapy and was studied again; thus, we enrolled 22 episodes (Figure 1). Three patients became unavailable for specimen collection and thus were not studied. Three patients with deep infections diagnosed after enrollment were excluded, per protocol, because the infections would confound assessing the relationship of mcfDNA to CIED infection. Of the remaining 16 cases, 3 were admitted directly to our hospital and 13 were transferred from other hospitals. Each patient received pathogen-specific recommended endocarditis antibiotic therapy beginning with their presentation for care.

Eleven patients were classified as definite CIED-IE (Table 1). Each had multiple days of positive blood cultures for S. aureus or Staphylococcus epidermidis and direct evidence of lead infection. The median total duration of bacteremia was 7 days (IQR, 4–14 days; range, 3–17 days).

In 10 of the 11 definite CIED-IE cases, plasma mcfDNA persisted during antibiotic treatment and beyond the period of bacteremia (Table 3). In these 10 cases, plasma mcfDNA was detected at consent after receiving antibiotic therapy for a median of 6 days (IQR, 4–11 days) and on CIED extraction after a median of 7 days (IQR, 4–13 days) of therapy. In the 11th case (D-017), mcfDNA was not detected at consent on day 13 of antibiotic therapy (6 days after the last positive blood culture), but S. aureus mcfDNA was detected later that day in the “on extraction” specimen. Including the period after device extraction, mcfDNA was detected during antibiotic therapy for a median of 11 days (IQR, 7.5–15 days) and for a median of 4 days (IQR, 1.5–6 days) of therapy after the last positive blood culture (Supplementary Table 1). With ongoing antibiotic treatment following device removal, plasma mcfDNA concentration rapidly decreased, becoming undetectable by day 4 in both S. epidermidis infections and in 3 S. aureus infections, including D-012 with surgical lead extraction and tricuspid valve replacement. High mcfDNA plasma concentrations persisted after device extraction in cases D-005 and D-019, reflecting residual tricuspid valve vegetations and continued postextraction bacteremia for 2 and 12 days, respectively.

In each case of definite CIED-IE, mcfDNA MPM in either the “on extraction” or 1-hour postextraction specimen increased significantly compared to the respective preextraction specimen MPM and was greater than each patient's maximum preextraction MPM (Table 3, Figure 2). These increases exceeded test variability versus the published coefficient of variation [21] (Supplementary Figure 1). In 9 of these cases, the maximum MPM occurred following lead extraction through the generator pocket. Increased MPMs occurred with documented manipulation of lead vegetations in 3 cases. In case D-019 the increase occurred with lead vegetation debridement by transcatheter aspiration and increased further with subsequent transvenous lead extraction. In cases D-011 and D-012, MPM increased with disruption of lead vegetations during CIED removal under direct vision at cardiac surgery.

In cases of possible S. aureus CIED-IE, mcfDNA was initially undetectable after a median of 6 days (IQR, 5.5–7.5 days) of antibiotic therapy prior to extraction (Table 3). It was not detected “on extraction” after a median of 7 days (IQR, 6.5–8.5 days) of antibiotic therapy, nor after a median of 6 days (IQR, 5.5–7.0 days) of therapy following the last positive blood culture (Supplementary Table 1).

The kinetics of staphylococcal plasma mcfDNA differ significantly between our cases of definite and possible CIED-IE, even with relatively similar periods of appropriate antibiotic treatment after documentation of bacteremia (Fisher exact test, P = .001). During antibiotic therapy of patients with definite CIED-IE, mcfDNA detection was prolonged and persisted beyond device extraction, and the concentration increased early after device extraction (Figure 2). In contrast, in our cases of possible CIED-IE, mcfDNA was undetectable after a brief period of antibiotic therapy and remained undetectable after CIED extraction.

DISCUSSION

Our study prospectively evaluated mcfDNA sequencing and quantitation in serial plasma specimens to clarify the likelihood of CIED-IE in patients with a CIED and staphylococcal bacteremia and thus inform therapy. In patients classified as definite CIED-IE, we demonstrated the prolonged detectability of plasma mcfDNA during antibiotic therapy, even after treatment rendered blood cultures negative. Moreover, the concentration of plasma mcfDNA early after device extraction increased to levels exceeding those immediately before extraction. In patients classified as possible CIED-IE, we found that plasma mcfDNA became uniformly undetectable by day 6 of antibiotic therapy and remained undetectable after device extraction. Sequencing of detected mcfDNA confirmed the Staphylococcus species isolated from prior blood cultures as well as the methicillin susceptibility status of the majority of S. aureus. The kinetics of plasma staphylococcal mcfDNA can possibly serve as a biomarker for diagnostic and therapy decisions in patients with staphylococcal bacteremia and potential CIED-IE.

Given the absence of confounding extracardiac deep S. aureus infection in our definite CIED-IE cases, the prolonged duration of S. aureus mcfDNA in spite of antibiotic therapy is consistent with the long duration of S. aureus mcfDNA in plasma or blood of patients with complicated S. aureus bacteremia, including infective endocarditis [18, 20, 24]. The observed rapid decline of mcfDNA after device removal is likewise similar to that following definitive source control in patients with complicated bacteremia [18, 20, 24]. These data provide indirect evidence of CIED-IE.

In definite CIED-IE cases, the increase of plasma mcfDNA concentrations early after transvenous CIED extraction suggests the release of mcfDNA as a result of disruption of lead infection. This inference is supported by similar increases in plasma mcfDNA concentrations when lead infection was disrupted with transcatheter vegetation debridement or lead explantation at cardiac surgery. These observations are further evidence that the pattern of plasma staphylococcal mcfDNA in patients classified as definite CIED-IE indicates lead infection.

The mcfDNA pattern in cases classified as possible CIED-IE differs from that in definite CIED-IE cases. In our possible CIED-IE cases, plasma mcfDNA was undetectable by day 6 of antibiotic therapy and remained undetectable after transvenous lead extraction. The significant difference in the kinetics of plasma mcfDNA in the 2 clinically classified groups suggests that they are different clinical entities. Among cases classified as definite CIED-IE, the release of mcfDNA with device extraction strongly supports CIED lead infection. In contrast, the mcfDNA pattern noted among our patients classified as possible CIED-IE implies that bacteremia was uncomplicated and had not infected the CIED leads.

The timing of specimen collection is important if using mcfDNA as a biomarker to enhance diagnostic precision. Definite CIED-IE case D-017 demonstrates that prolonged antibiotic therapy after bacteremia is quenched can render mcfDNA undetectable. This illustrates the importance of specimens early after antibiotic treatment for distinguishing mcfDNA clearance effected by prolonged treatment versus rapid clearance associated with uncomplicated bacteremia. Patients without CIED infection can have detectable mcfDNA very early in antibiotic therapy (case P-015), underscoring the importance of specimen timing when assessing for early mcfDNA clearance associated with treatment of uncomplicated bacteremia. Our data do not suggest that all patients classified as possible S. aureus CIED-IE are free from lead infection, but rather that strategically timed mcfDNA testing can confirm definite CIED-IE and in possible CIED-IE, can potentially differentiate between patients with and without lead infection.

If the mcfDNA kinetics we observed are confirmed, plasma mcfDNA can potentially serve as a biomarker to clarify diagnoses in CIED patients being treated for S. aureus bacteremia. Testing could be utilized selectively where clinical data do not provide diagnostic certainty. For example, Nakajima et al reported 178 patients hospitalized with S. aureus bacteremia without evident CIED infection [4]; however, patient follow-up suggested diagnostic imprecision. Empiric device removal was associated with decreased 1-year mortality and treatment without device removal was associated with bacteremia relapse, often with evidence of CIED-IE [4]. Our data suggest that as few as 1 to 3 strategically timed mcfDNA tests, in conjunction with standard clinical evaluation, might enhance diagnostic precision in such cases. Persistence versus early clearance of mcfDNA on day 6 of antibiotic therapy might help distinguish patients with or without CIED-IE and inform decisions regarding device extraction. If residual diagnostic uncertainty prompts empiric device extraction, assessing mcfDNA concentrations in early postextraction versus preextraction plasma could clarify the CIED-IE question and guide postextraction treatment. Among Nakajima and colleagues' patients, such clarifications might expand the candidates for extraction, spare unnecessary device extraction in patients with unlikely lead infection, or inform antibiotic treatment decisions after empiric extraction. In patients with evident pocket infection and staphylococcal bacteremia, absence of increased mcfDNA concentration on extraction might negate intracardiac infection, permitting shorter duration antibiotic therapy and hospitalization. Extraction and replacement of an infected device (eg, for a cardiac resynchronization device average cost is $67 586 [25]) and prolonged intravenous antibiotics are costly and potentially associated with adverse effects. Although mcfDNA testing costs exceed those of conventional diagnostic tests, appropriate care resulting from strategically timed testing in selected patients could be highly cost-effective and improve care.

Our study is limited by the small population from a single institution; thus, confirmation and refinement are required for generalization. Additionally, concurrent S. aureus extracardiac deep infection or left-sided endocarditis confound mcfDNA kinetics as a biomarker for CIED-IE. Although our data do not address mcfDNA in non-staphylococcal CIED infection, staphylococci are the dominant cause of CIED-IE. Alternatively, the similarity of our findings to reported plasma mcfDNA in patients with complicated versus uncomplicated S. aureus bacteremia and the uniformity of the mcfDNA patterns distinguishing CIED-IE from rejected lead infection suggest that mcfDNA as a biomarker can enhance diagnostic accuracy.

CONCLUSIONS

Using serial plasma specimens for mcfDNA sequencing and quantitation in CIED patients with staphylococcal bacteremia, we demonstrated that mcfDNA patterns can serve as biomarkers to improve diagnostic accuracy in patients with suspected CIED-IE. If our findings are confirmed, selective use of strategically timed mcfDNA testing, in conjunction with clinical features, can improve diagnostic precision for CIED-IE and facilitate treatment trials evaluating device removal and duration of antibiotic therapy.
