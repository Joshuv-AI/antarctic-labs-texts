In vitro experimental study of MC-PMMA containing vancomycin for the prevention of infection in open bone defects
Authors: Haitao Liu, Yu Bo, Pengcheng Gao, Zhizhong Li, Shaodong Qiu, Gangning Feng, Zongqiang Yang, He Zhang, Zhanhu Mi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, vancomycin, bone cement (PMMA) and mineralized collagen (MC) were mixed in order to obtain a new composite drug-carrying biomaterial, which has good results in both drug slow release, good biocompatibility, and good growth of osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and mesenchymal stem cells on the surface of the biomaterial, which provides a new therapeutic idea for the clinical treatment of bone defect infections. In this study, the drug retardation system of vancomycin and mineralized collagen composite bone cement-carrying biomaterials was prepared in proportion to the drug retardation system, and the experimental studies were carried out using electron microscope scanning, HPLC drug retardation analysis, in vitro antimicrobials, and co-cultivation of osteoclasts, osteoblasts, and mesenchymal stem cells. We found that the composite drug-carrying material of vancomycin, bone cement and mineralized collagen had good slow-release effect and antimicrobial properties, and the addition of vancomycin and bone cement to mineralized collagen material had even better drug-release efficiency than that of bone cement plus vancomycin alone. In vitro antimicrobial showed that the composite material has excellent antimicrobial effect against Staphylococcus aureus. Co-culture of osteoblasts, osteoclasts and mesenchymal stem cells with the material showed that the cells were morphologically complete on the surface of the composites with good growth status. Vancomycin, bone cement and mineralized collagen composite drug-carrying biomaterials have excellent slow-release effect and antimicrobial properties with good biocompatibility, which is a new therapeutic idea for the future clinical treatment of bone defect infections.

Introduction

In clinical work, the incidence of bone defects caused by trauma, limb deformity, tumors and infectious diseases is increasing, and large bone defects can cause great inconvenience to patients, seriously affect their postoperative recovery, and jeopardize their physical and mental health [1, 2]. Bone defects are highly susceptible to bacterial infections, and post-fracture infections have become one of the most common forms of bone infections, with Staphylococcus aureus being the most common causative organism, and the incidence of post-fracture internal fixation infections can range from 0.4% to 16.1%, with an average incidence rate of about 5% [3–5]. Bone defects with infections can cause severe limb deformity and dysfunction if not treated in time or improperly treated. Prevention and treatment of postoperative fracture infection is urgent.

At present, the treatment of bone defect infection is mainly based on repeated thorough debridement, negative pressure drainage and antibiotic treatment, but its treatment period is long, costly, high recurrence rate and may produce antibiotic resistance and organ toxicity damage, treatment difficulty, and extremely difficult to deal with [6]. At the present stage, Polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) bone cement with antibiotics is a simple and effective technique for repairing segmental bone defects, in which scaffolding material with antimicrobial properties is prepared by loading antibiotics and filled into the infected bone defect site [7, 8]. However, in some patients, the use of PMMA bone cement with antibiotics for the treatment of bone defects with infections did not achieve the desired therapeutic results, and sometimes the infection worsened. The possible reasons for this are the poor biosolubility of the system, the low antibiotic release rate, the short effective release time of antibiotics, and the non-biodegradable properties [9]. Among them, the surface size and porosity of the bone cement are considered to be the key factors affecting the release of antibiotics from PMMA [10]. Recent studies suggest that the integration of bone mineralized collagen into bone cement substantially enhances its elastic modulus [11–13]. Furthermore, over time, new bone tissue progressively infiltrates the porous matrix, forming as the bone mineralized collagen is absorbed. Consequently, the incorporation of bone mineralized collagen into bone cement not only preserves the inherent benefits of the cement but also addresses its limitations, such as non-degradability and non-absorbability, thereby augmenting its mechanical properties and biocompatibility.

To address the problems of low antibiotic release rate and short effective antibiotic release time of existing PMMA-loaded antibiotics, we proposed to use mineralized collodion raw material (MC) plus bone cement (PMMA) plus vancomycin (vancomycin). We propose to use mineralized collodion raw material (MC) plus bone cement (PMMA) plus vancomycin (vancomycin) to construct MC-PMMA-vancomycin novel composites to solve the problems of low antibiotic release rate and short antibiotic effective release time. Mineralized collagen raw material, on the other hand, is a bionic composite material with collagen and hydroxyapatite as the main elements, simulating the chemical composition and microstructure of natural bone matrix. Mineralized collagen has good osteoinductive and angiogenic potential, biocompatibility, biodegradability, controllable shape and size, long shelf life and low cost [14, 15]. Based on the respective advantages and disadvantages of traditional bone cement as well as mineralized collagen raw materials in clinical treatment, mineralized collagen raw materials can increase the porosity of the bone cement surface, improve the release efficiency and release time of vancomycin in the novel MC-PMMA-vancomycin composites, and provide new therapeutic ideas for the clinical treatment of bone defect infection.

All instruments were pre-sterilized and should be dry at the time of use and have the same temperature (23 °C ± 1 °C) as the overall environment of the operating room. Scanning electron microscopy analysis of mineralized gum material raw material-bone cement-vancomycin (mc-pmma-vancomycin) biomaterials

We performed scanning electron microscopy experiments on the samples using a scanning electron microscope (Japan Electronics (JEOL), JSM-IT700HR) and an ion sputtering apparatus (Japan Electronics (JEOL), Smart Coater). The samples were first washed with PBS to remove surface adherents and mucus, etc., and 3% glutaraldehyde was added for fixation, after which the samples were washed with ultrapure water for 3 times, 10 min each time, and then fixed with 1% osmium acid for 1 h-2 h, and then washed with ultrapure water for 3 times, 10 min each time. The samples were dehydrated by alcohol step by step, and the concentration of the dehydrating agent gradient was 30% → 50% → 70% → 90% → 100% (100% concentration was exchanged for 3 times), 15 min each time; the samples were removed from the sample and then washed by alcohol.), 15 min each time; the sample was put into the critical point dryer for drying and then glued to the sample stage with conductive adhesive, and put into the ion sputtering instrument for gold spraying. Finally, the use of Japan Electronics Corporation (JEOL) production of JSM-IT700HR scanning electron microscope for image acquisition of samples, each sample first in the low magnification to observe the whole sample, and then select the region to be observed to collect pictures.

Drug retardation test ofmineralized collodion raw material-bone cement-vancomycin (MC-PMMA-vancomycin) biomaterials

We used High Performance Liquid Chromatography\HPLC analysis system to perform drug slow release test on the samples. The chromatographic conditions of vancomycin were as follows: column: Hypersil ODS2 (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm); mobile phase: acetonitrile-50 mmol of potassium dihydrogen phosphate solution (pH = 3.2 adjusted by phosphoric acid) =10:90 (see Fig. 2A); flow rate: 1.0 mL-min-1; column temperature: 30 °C; detection wavelength: 236 nm; injection volume: 20 μL. Under this chromatographic condition, the retention time of vancomycin was in the range of 6–7 min. The linear regression of peak area (A) on mass concentration (C) gave the standard curve equation A = y = 16087*C + 2786.8, r = 1 (n = 6), which showed that vancomycin had a good linear relationship in the range of 1–100 μg-mL-1. Finally, the in vitro release of the samples was assayed by simulating the human physiological environment pH 7.4 phosphate buffer as the release medium. The samples were transferred into a conical flask containing 40 ml of release medium and shaken at 37 °C and 100 r/min. A 2 mL sample was taken at the set time point and an equal volume of release medium was added. The sample solution was filtered through a microporous membrane (0.45 μm), and the content was detected by HPLC, and the cumulative release of vancomycin was calculated and plotted as a release curve.

In vitro antimicrobial experiments of mineralized adhesive raw material-bone cement-vancomycin (MC-PMMA-vancomycin) biomaterials

We conducted in vitro antimicrobial experiments against Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa on mineralized gum raw material-bone cement-vancomycin (MC-PMMA-vancomycin) biomaterials. Firstly, 100 μL of glycerol bacteria were taken and coated into LB agar medium and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Single colonies were picked and inoculated into LB broth and incubated for 16 h at 37 °C with 150 rpm oscillation for bacterial culture. Next, the bacterial concentration was determined by using the McDonald’s turbidimetric method, and the concentration of the bacterial solution was adjusted to 1 × 108 CFU/mL using sterile liquid medium, and the bacterial solution was set aside.

Finally, 200 µL of 108 CFU/mL bacterial solution was aspirated on LB agar culture, and each plate was placed with a bottom-containing drug plaster block (the drug was melted with saline and coated to the bottom of the plaster block), and the effect of bacterial inhibition was observed at 37 °C to detect the circle of inhibition.

In vitro co-culture of MC-PMMA-vancomycin biomaterials with mouse osteoblast cell line MC3T3-E1, mouse RAW256.7 cells, and rabbit primary mesenchymal stem cells

We co-cultured mouse osteoblast cell line (MC3T3-E1), mouse RAW256.7 cells and rabbit primary MSCs in vitro with mineralized collagen raw material-cement-vancomycin (MC-PMMA-vancomycin) biomaterial. Firstly, after PBS washed the medium adequately, trypsin digested moderately and then each cell line, inoculated at a density of 50,000/cell in a 6-well culture chamber for cultivation; the culture medium was changed every 3 days, and after 5 days of cultivation, mineralized collagen raw material-cemented bone-vancomycin (MC-PMMA-vancomycin) biomaterials were added to the medium, and the materials were taken from the culture and put into the culture of the 6-well culture chamber after one week of continued cultivation. Scanning electron microscopy was performed after fixation in electron microscope fixative, and the remaining liquid in the culture medium was subjected to CCK-8 assay. Remove the cell supernatant, add the serum-containing medium containing CCK-8 reagent, and put it in 37 °C, 5% (v/v) CO2 incubator for 2–4 h, then take it out to measure the absorbance value, and repeat the measurement 3 times for each sample to take the average value.

Discussion

The treatment of infected bone defects has always been a thorny problem for orthopedic surgeons, and anti-infective tissue-engineered bone is currently the most promising graft material for repairing infected bone defects, in which one of the very key techniques is the use of local antibiotic release system [16]. PMMA bone cement as the most commonly used antibiotic carriers has been widely used in domestic and international clinical treatment. However, PMMA combined with antibiotics has the problems of low release rate, short effective release time and non-biodegradable characteristics [17]. There is an urgent need to develop scaffold materials with good slow-release effect, good biocompatibility, and high efficiency antibacterial materials that are not easy to develop bacterial resistance for the treatment of infected bone defects, and to realize simultaneous treatment of antibacterial and bone repair. How to improve the shortcomings of PMMA bone cement as an orthopedic material has become one of the hot areas of biomaterials research in recent years.

The release of antibiotics in bone cement is a diffusion process, and this process is affected by several factors. The surface size and porosity of bone cement are considered to be the key factors affecting the release of antibiotics from PMMA. Duey et al. conducted a comparative study on the release of Van and tobramycin from Simplex bone cement with different shapes and volumes, and the results showed that the amount of antibiotics released was not significantly related to the volume of the bone cement, but rather there was a significant linear positive correlation with its surface area [18]. This finding was also confirmed by Masri et al. [19]. This is because the elution of antibiotics occurs mainly in the outer layer of the bone cement, and therefore, the size of the surface area has an important influence on the efficiency of antibiotic release. Schurman et al. showed that the release of antibiotics occurs mainly in the superficial 100 µm layer, and only 19% of the antibiotics were released from the deeper 700 µm layer [20]. Therefore, the shape of the bone cement should be rationally designed to increase the porosity of its surface according to the specific clinical needs and surgical requirements, so as to improve the release efficiency of antibiotics.

Previous researchers have added specific bioactive substances (e.g., chitosan, bioactive ceramics, graphene, etc.) to PMMA to enhance osteogenesis and antimicrobial substances (e.g., metal ions and antimicrobial peptides) to enhance antimicrobial capacity [21]. Due to the complexity of the manufacturing process or biosafety issues of the materials in previous studies, there is still a large gap between them and clinical translation. Better material improvement solutions are still needed to solve the existing problems and meet the clinical needs. Mineralized collagen materials can meet these requirements. Mineralized collagen is an important protein in many animal tissues, such as skin, bone, ligament, tendon and cartilage. Therefore collagen materials play an important role in the solution of many tissue engineering problems such as repair and reconstruction of tissues, bone and skin [14, 22]. Xing Fei et al. used composite scaffolds prepared from raw materials including mineralized collagen materials and demonstrated that they can promote cell differentiation and thus osteogenesis, and also have good biodegradability as well as mechanical properties [23]. In recent years, mineralized collagen artificial bone repair materials have been used for filling and regenerative repair of various types of bone defects in surgery, and good clinical results have been achieved [24].

In this experiment, vancomycin, bone cement (PMMA) and mineralized collagen (MC) will be mixed in order to obtain a new composite drug-carrying biomaterial, using electron microscope scanning to analyze the surface of the new material found that in the samples with the addition of the mineralized collagen raw material, it can be observed that there is a clear void on the surface of the bone cement, and the surface of the material of the bone cement alone group (without the addition of the mineralized collagen raw material) did not see any void, which may be the This may be the reason for the better drug release efficiency of the new composites. In addition to this, we also conducted in vitro antibacterial experiments on the new composite material, which proved that the new material has excellent antibacterial performance against Staphylococcus aureus in vitro. Co-culture experiments with mouse osteoblast cell line (MC3T3-E1), mouse RAW256.7 cells and rabbit primary mesenchymal stem cells (BMSC) also demonstrated the excellent cytocompatibility of the new composites.

Limitations of the study: This experiment also has some limitations, we chose to adopt the commonly used materials that have received general acceptance, mineralized collagen plus bone cement combined with vancomycin-loaded drug for the experiment, and did not conduct experiments on other materials, missing some potential materials for the treatment of infection in bone defects, and our subsequent experiments can be tried on other materials. Also affected by the experimental time and funding, we regret that we did not carry out in vivo animal experiments to verify the experimental results, and we will follow up with in vivo animal experiments.

Conclusion

The composite drug-carrying biomaterial of vancomycin, bone cement and mineralized collagen has excellent slow-release effect and antimicrobial properties with good biocompatibility, which is a new therapeutic idea for the future clinical treatment of bone defect infection.
