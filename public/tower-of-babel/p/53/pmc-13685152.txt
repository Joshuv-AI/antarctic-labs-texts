Targeting neuronal FKBP5 attenuates mechanical allodynia by suppressing NF-κB-mediated neuroinflammation in an NTG-induced chronic migraine mouse model
Authors: Cheng Peng, Tianhua Fan, Gang Zhou, Min Zhao, Tianxiao Wang, Tingting Cao, Jingyuan Zheng, Zijuan Guo, Guining Liang, Zhenchang Zhang, Ji-Song Guan, Yonggang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic migraine (CM) is a debilitating neurological disorder characterized by persistent trigeminovascular sensitization and neuroinflammation. While FK506-binding protein 51 (FKBP5) modulates spinal neuroinflammation in chronic pain, its specific role in trigeminovascular nociceptive processing and CM pathogenesis remains largely unknown.MethodsWe used a nitroglycerin (NTG)-induced CM mouse model. Following RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) of total RNA isolated from trigeminal nucleus caudalis (TNC) tissue lysates, target expression was validated via reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), Western blotting, and immunofluorescence. To establish causality, we used a neuron-specific CRISPR-Cas9 editing system for Fkbp5 knockout within the TNC, alongside pharmacological interventions using the specific FKBP5 antagonist SAFit2 and the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) inhibitor PDTC. Migraine-like behaviors were evaluated using von Frey filaments, coupled with biochemical analyses of NF-κB cascade activation and trigeminovascular neuroinflammation.ResultsRNA-seq, RT-qPCR, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence consistently confirmed a marked upregulation of Fkbp5 mRNA and FKBP5 protein in the TNC of NTG-treated mice, predominantly localized to neurons. Concurrently, significant microglial and astrocytic activation was observed in the TNC. Both neuron-specific Fkbp5 knockout and pharmacological inhibition with SAFit2 significantly attenuated NTG-induced periorbital and hind paw mechanical allodynia. Furthermore, FKBP5 deficiency blunted the NTG-triggered phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα, effectively suppressing the downstream release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), as well as the upregulation of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and c-Fos. Crucially, targeted blockade of the NF-κB cascade with PDTC recapitulated the protective behavioral and biochemical effects of FKBP5 deficiency.ConclusionsWe demonstrate that neuronal FKBP5 is significantly associated with CM pathogenesis. FKBP5 sustains mechanical allodynia by activating the NF-κB-mediated neuroinflammatory cascade. Background

CM remains one of the most disabling neurological conditions globally, presenting a persistent challenge in clinical management [1]. While the landscape of preventive treatments has expanded with the introduction of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)-targeted therapies, a substantial proportion of patients still experience refractory symptoms or intolerable side effects from available medications. According to the European Headache Federation consensus statement, this persistent therapeutic ceiling leaves a large subset of patients suffering from resistant and refractory migraine [2]. This clinical reality underscores the urgent need to elucidate alternative molecular drivers of CM pathogenesis to develop novel, effective therapeutic strategies.

FK506-binding protein 51 (FKBP5), classically recognized as a stress-responsive co-chaperone and a key regulator of glucocorticoid receptor signaling [3–5], has recently emerged as a pivotal modulator of chronic pain [6, 7]. It is increasingly recognized that systemic stress biology and glucocorticoid dysfunction can significantly regulate and contribute to the development of migraine [8, 9]. The convergence of these stress-related alterations with local neuroinflammation and subsequent central sensitization forms a critical axis that facilitates migraine chronification [10]. In this context, preclinical evidence indicates that FKBP5 exacerbates nociceptive processing by hyperactivating the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby driving the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) within the spinal dorsal horn [11, 12]. Accordingly, targeted inhibition of FKBP5 has been shown to robustly suppress microglial and astrocytic activation and blunt neuroinflammation, ultimately alleviating chronic pain, highlighting its profound analgesic potential [6, 11, 13, 14].

The pathophysiology of CM is intimately linked to neuroinflammation and central sensitization within the trigeminal nucleus caudalis (TNC), the brainstem equivalent of the spinal dorsal horn [10]. Sustained activation of this pathway drives the local upregulation of algogenic neuropeptides, particularly CGRP, which perpetuates central nociceptive sensitization [15]. Given that spinal and craniofacial nociceptive pathways share core inflammatory mechanisms, the capacity of FKBP5 to modulate sensory neuroinflammation suggests it may also act as an important modulator in migraine pathogenesis. However, whether FKBP5 actively contributes to trigeminovascular sensitization remains largely unexplored.

To address this critical knowledge gap, the present study investigated the role of FKBP5 in central sensitization and CM pathogenesis. We hypothesized that FKBP5 functions as a significant upstream regulator of trigeminovascular neuroinflammation, thereby promoting sustained central sensitization. Using a nitroglycerin (NTG)-induced CM mouse model, we applied targeted genetic and pharmacological approaches to systematically evaluate the impact of inhibiting the FKBP5 signaling axis on mechanical allodynia and the downstream neuroinflammatory cascade. Ultimately, this work aims to clarify the molecular mechanisms associated with CM pathogenesis and evaluate the translational therapeutic potential of this stress-responsive modulator.

Methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks of age, weighing 25–30 g), sourced from the Animal Core Facility of ShanghaiTech University, were used for all in vivo experiments. The animal cohorts were maintained within a specific pathogen-free (SPF) vivarium. Standardized housing parameters included a 12 h light/dark schedule, regulated ambient temperature (22–26 °C) and humidity (40–60%), acoustic isolation (noise levels < 60 dB), and unrestricted access to standard laboratory chow and water.

Ethical clearance for all experimental procedures was granted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at ShanghaiTech University (Approval No. 20260202001). Furthermore, our methodologies strictly complied with both the NIH guidelines [16] and the ARRIVE reporting standards [17]. The group sizes were selected in accordance with previously validated and widely accepted protocols in the NTG model [15, 18]. A sample size of n = 8 per group was utilized for behavioral assays to accommodate inherent behavioral variability, whereas n = 4–6 per group was adopted for subsequent biochemical and molecular analyses. These chosen sample sizes provide sufficient statistical power to detect meaningful biological differences in their respective endpoints while strictly adhering to the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) principles of animal welfare to optimize animal usage [19].

To minimize experimental bias, the allocation of mice into specific testing groups was completely randomized. Additionally, a rigorous blinding protocol was enforced: the researchers executing the pharmacological interventions, conducting the behavioral assays, and performing the subsequent statistical analyses were entirely masked to the group assignments.

Establishment of the CM model

To mimic CM pathology, we adopted a well-characterized recurrent NTG administration paradigm [20]. A concentrated NTG stock (5.0 mg/mL, supplied in anhydrous ethanol; Baiyunshan Pharmaceutical, Guangzhou, China) was diluted with 0.9% sterile saline to a final working concentration of 1.0 mg/mL immediately before use. Mice in the experimental cohort were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with this NTG preparation at a dose of 10 mg/kg. These injections were delivered on alternate days over a span of 9 days, amounting to five doses in total [20]. Conversely, control animals were administered an identical volume of a rigorously matched vehicle, which comprised 0.9% saline containing a trace amount of ethanol equivalent to that in the NTG working solution. Throughout the manuscript, this vehicle-treated control cohort is simply designated as the “saline” group.

Behavioral tests

To mitigate potential stress-related experimental artifacts, animals underwent a 2-day habituation period in the designated testing environment preceding any baseline measurements. Although this acclimation was conducted under strictly controlled conditions, we acknowledge that a 2-day duration is shorter than the 3–7 days typically recommended for rodent behavioral studies, which may represent a potential source of stress-related variability and a methodological limitation of our study. To circumvent diurnal confounding variables, all behavioral assays were invariably executed between 09:00 and 15:00. NTG-induced mechanical allodynia was evaluated at baseline and exactly 2 h post-injection on the scheduled observation days. To maximize methodological objectivity, a single proficient investigator, who remained fully masked to the experimental groups, carried out all evaluations [21]. Prior to data collection during each session, mice were allowed a 30 min acclimation period within the specific behavioral apparatus [22].

Consistent with established protocols, periorbital and hind paw mechanical withdrawal thresholds were determined using von Frey filaments via the standard up-down method. For periorbital threshold measurements, mice were individually placed into customized opaque paper cups (4 oz) to restrict excessive locomotion while minimizing restraint stress. Filaments were applied perpendicularly to the periorbital region; a positive nocifensive response was defined as a forceful stroking of the face with the forepaws or an immediate, brisk head withdrawal [20]. For the assessment of hind paw sensitivity, mice were positioned on an elevated wire mesh floor. Once exploratory behavior completely ceased, filaments were applied to the mid-plantar surface of the hind paw. A positive withdrawal response was identified by rapid paw retraction, lifting, or licking of the stimulated paw [23].

Pharmacological interventions

To further validate the functional roles of FKBP5 and the NF-κB cascade in vivo, mice were subjected to specific pharmacological regimens. We utilized SAFit2, a selective FKBP5 inhibitor (HY-102080, MCE, NJ, USA), alongside PDTC, a well-known NF-κB blocker (HY-18738, MCE, NJ, USA). Both compounds were formulated in a common vehicle consisting of 10% DMSO, 40% PEG 300, 5% Tween 80, and 45% sterile saline.

Guided by prior literature, SAFit2 was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) at a dose of 10 mg/kg every 12 h [24], a regimen proven to achieve sufficient central nervous system penetration [14, 25]. On the observation days, baseline mechanical thresholds were measured prior to the NTG injection. The SAFit2 dose was then administered 1 h post-NTG, and behavioral testing was performed 1 h after the SAFit2 injection (i.e., exactly 2 h post-NTG). Additionally, PDTC was administered i.p. at 40 mg/kg once daily, in accordance with previously validated dosages for neuroinflammation suppression [26]. These pharmacological treatments were maintained over a 9-day period, parallel to the NTG modeling phase. Vehicle-treated control groups received identical volumes of the corresponding vehicle. To ensure maximum compound efficacy, all drug solutions were prepared immediately prior to injection.

Immunofluorescence staining

To prepare tissues for histological evaluation, animals were subjected to deep anesthesia via an intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg). This was immediately followed by transcardial perfusion, initiated with a flush of chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and concluded with the delivery of 20 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The whole brains were rapidly extracted and immersed in the identical PFA fixative, where they were maintained at 4 °C for an additional 48 h post-fixation period. Upon completion of fixation, 40–50 μm coronal slices precisely capturing the TNC area were generated in a cold PBS bath utilizing a Leica VT1000S vibratome (Wetzlar, Germany). These resulting free-floating sections were then collected for downstream immunofluorescent labeling. A standard washing protocol, consisting of three consecutive 10 min agitations in PBS, was uniformly applied between all subsequent staining steps.

To prevent non-specific binding and facilitate tissue permeabilization, the slices were first immersed in a PBS-based blocking solution containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.4% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. This was followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies, which were pre-diluted in the aforementioned blocking matrix. These included: CGRP (1:1000; Santa Cruz, #sc-57053), c-Fos (1:500; CST, #2250), FKBP5 (1:200; Proteintech, #67874-1-Ig), NeuN (1:500; CST, #12943), Iba1 (1:500; Novus, #NB100-1028), and GFAP (1:500; CST, #12389). Following the standard washing procedure, the samples were exposed to matched fluorophore-tagged secondary antibodies (all applied at a 1:500 dilution) for 2 h at room temperature. The secondary repertoire comprised: goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Yeasen, #33206ES60), goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 555 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A21422), goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 647 (Yeasen, #33213ES60), donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Abcam, #ab150105), and donkey anti-goat Alexa Fluor 647 (Abcam, #ab150131). Post-incubation washes preceded a 20 min nuclear counterstaining step utilizing DAPI (1:5000; Yeasen, #40728ES03). Finally, the prepared tissues were carefully mounted onto microscope slides and sealed under coverslips with an antifade fluorescence mounting medium. High-resolution images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Immunofluorescence imaging analysis

Anatomical boundaries of the TNC were delineated with reference to the classic atlas, The Mouse Brain in Stereotaxic Coordinates (Paxinos and Franklin, 4th Edition, 2012). To guarantee reliable quantitative comparisons among biological replicates, confocal microscopy settings, including laser intensity, pinhole aperture, and detector gain, were kept strictly constant for all specimens. The expression profiles of CGRP, FKBP5, Iba1, and GFAP were evaluated by calculating the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) across the specified TNC regions. To assess c-Fos activation, we performed a manual enumeration of positively stained nuclei in the TNC after applying a uniform background subtraction protocol. Additionally, the cellular distribution of FKBP5 was mapped by analyzing its spatial overlap with established cell-type indicators (NeuN for neurons, Iba1 for microglia, and GFAP for astrocytes). All image processing and quantification steps were executed using ImageJ (version 1.8.0; NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). To eliminate analytical bias, these evaluations were conducted by a researcher who remained fully masked to the experimental groups.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

All transcriptomic workflows, encompassing RNA isolation, library preparation, and high-throughput sequencing, were executed by BGI Genomics (Shenzhen, China). Specifically, total RNA was extracted from the isolated TNC specimens utilizing TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After stringent quality assessments to confirm RNA integrity, polyadenylated mRNA was selectively captured via oligo(dT)-conjugated magnetic beads. Subsequently, the sequencing libraries were generated and subjected to paired-end sequencing on the DNBSEQ technology platform.

The standard BGI bioinformatics workflow was adopted for primary data handling and comparative transcriptomics. To guarantee data fidelity, raw sequencing reads underwent quality trimming via SOAPnuke (v1.5.2). The resulting high-quality clean reads were subsequently mapped to the GRCm38 mouse reference assembly employing the HISAT2 algorithm (v2.0.4) [27]. For baseline expression profiling, transcript abundances were quantified and normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) utilizing RSEM (v1.3.1) [28]. In parallel, rigorous statistical evaluations for differential expression were conducted directly on the unnormalized raw count matrices via the DESeq2 R package (v1.34.0) [29]. The definitive threshold for identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) required an FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05, accompanied by an absolute log₂ fold change ≥ 1.

To systematically quantify the activation landscape of migraine-associated pathways, a novel ‘Migraine Score’ was formulated. Initially, we queried the DisGeNET database via the Enrichr platform to retrieve the top 100 genetic risk factors linked to migraine pathogenesis [30, 31]. To ensure cross-gene comparability and eliminate baseline magnitude discrepancies, the expression profiles of these core targets were subjected to z-score transformation. The final integrated index for each biological replicate was then calculated as the arithmetic mean of these standardized values. This metric was visualized using violin plots, followed by correlation analyses against targeted biochemical markers to elucidate group-level transcriptional shifts.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

RNA isolation from the microdissected TNC specimens was accomplished utilizing the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA, #15596018). The extracted total RNA templates were immediately converted into first-strand cDNA employing a commercial reverse transcription system (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China, #AT311-03). Subsequent quantitative PCR reactions were prepared using the 2× SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA, #B21202) and executed on the QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The thermal cycling protocol was initiated with a 60 s hot-start activation at 95 °C. This was succeeded by 40 continuous cycles, each comprising denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, primer annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and strand extension at 72 °C for 30 s. To rigorously validate target specificity and rule out primer-dimer artifacts, a standard melting curve analysis (95 °C for 15 s, followed by 60 °C for 60 s, and concluding at 95 °C for 15 s) was appended to the run. Fold changes in target mRNA transcripts were evaluated in reference to the stable housekeeping gene β-actin (Actb) based on the established analytical methodology [32]. The custom-synthesized oligonucleotide pairs utilized for amplification are detailed below:

Fkbp5 forward: 5′-CGGCGACAGGTCTTCTACTT-3′ and reverse: 5′-TCTTCACCCTGCTCAGTCAT-3′;

Actb forward: 5′-CATTGCTGACAGGATGCAGAAGG-3′ and reverse: 5′-TGCTGGAAGGTGGACAGTGAGG-3′.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from the TNC specimens by mechanical disruption in pre-cooled RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, #P0013B), pre-supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (APExBIO, Houston, TX, USA, #K1007 and #K1015). Following sonication and incubation on ice to ensure thorough cellular lysis, the lysates were clarified via centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was harvested, and the precise protein concentrations were quantified utilizing a commercial BCA assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, #P0010). Aliquots were subsequently combined with a 5× loading buffer (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China, #C508320) and subjected to thermal denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min. Equivalent protein masses were resolved across 10%–15% SDS-polyacrylamide gels before being electroblotted onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). To avert non-specific antibody interactions, a 2 h room temperature blocking step was employed. Notably, the blocking buffer was selected based on the specific target: 5% skim milk was applied for total protein evaluation, whereas 5% BSA was reserved for detecting phosphorylation states.

Following the blocking step, specific antigen probing was achieved by incubating the membranes overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, uniformly prepared at a 1:1,000 dilution unless otherwise noted: CGRP (HUABIO, #HA722991), c-Fos (CST, #2250), FKBP5 (CST, #12210), p-p65 (CST, #3033), p65 (CST, #8242), p-IκBα (CST, #2859), IκBα (CST, #4814), IL-1β (Enkilife, #APRab03408), IL-6 (Enkilife, #APRab03851), TNF-α (Enkilife, #APRab00662), and β-actin (1:10,000; Proteintech, #81115-1-RR). After three consecutive 10 min washes in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) to remove unbound fractions, species-matched secondary antibodies, either goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000; Sparkjade, #EF0002) or goat anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000; Sparkjade, #EF0001), were introduced for a 1 h room temperature incubation. Immunoreactive signals were subsequently visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and captured on an Amersham Imager 600 digital imaging system (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Densitometric quantification of the protein bands was executed utilizing ImageJ software (version 1.8.0; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), with the relative abundance of each target normalized against the corresponding β-actin signal. Original, uncropped images of all Western blots are available in the Supplementary Material.

Plasmid construction and lentivirus preparation

The CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout system was established utilizing the lentiCRISPR v2 vector [33]. Candidate single-guide RNA (sgRNA) sequences targeting the mouse Fkbp5 gene were designed using the CRISPOR online tool (http://crispor.tefor.net/) [34]. For empirical validation of knockout efficacy, a pair of putative sgRNAs was inserted into the aforementioned vector and subsequently introduced into Neuro-2a murine neuroblastoma cell cultures via transient transfection. Genomic indels and the resulting reduction in target protein levels were quantitatively assessed employing the T7 endonuclease I (T7E1) mismatch assay and standard immunoblotting techniques, respectively. The specific sgRNA demonstrating superior silencing capability (sequence: 5′-CGATCCCAATCGGAATGTCG-3′) was selected for downstream in vivo applications. Following in vitro confirmation, the verified construct was sent to OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China) for viral packaging. This process yielded high-titer lentiviral suspensions carrying an EGFP reporter cassette, which were ultimately utilized for the localized ablation of Fkbp5 within the TNC through stereotaxic microinjection.

Stereotaxic injection

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China, #R510-22-10; induced at 3% and maintained at 1.5%–2.0%) and secured in a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Science). To achieve flat-skull alignment, the dorsoventral coordinates of bregma and lambda were leveled. Following a small craniotomy, the TNC was targeted bilaterally using the following coordinates relative to bregma: anteroposterior (AP) − 7.8 mm, mediolateral (ML) ± 1.5 mm, and dorsoventral (DV) − 3.5 mm from the dural surface. To ensure sufficient viral transduction throughout the longitudinal extent of the TNC, a volume of 1,000 nL of the lentiviral suspension was microinjected per hemisphere at a constant, slow rate of 50 nL/min using a microsyringe pump. After the injection, the needle was maintained in situ for an additional 10 min to facilitate diffusion and prevent backflow along the needle tract. Mice were allowed to recover for 21 days to ensure sufficient viral expression and target gene knockout before subsequent procedures. Following the experimental period, the accuracy of the injection sites was histologically verified by visualizing EGFP expression in medullary sections using fluorescence microscopy.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative evaluations were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Prior to significance testing, dataset normality was verified via the Shapiro–Wilk test. For two-group comparisons, variables satisfying parametric assumptions were subjected to a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, with Welch’s correction incorporated when homoscedasticity was violated. In contrast, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-parametric metrics. When evaluating three or more independent cohorts, normally distributed values were analyzed via a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) coupled with Tukey’s post hoc procedure, whereas Welch’s ANOVA was substituted for data exhibiting unequal variances. Multi-group non-parametric data were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons. Longitudinal behavioral observations across different treatments were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA to delineate main effects and group-by-time interactions, followed by Tukey’s post hoc validation. Quantitative results are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and exact biological replicate numbers (n) are explicitly denoted in the accompanying figure legends. A probability threshold of p < 0.05 was established to define statistical significance.

Results

Persistent mechanical allodynia and trigeminovascular activation in the NTG-induced CM model

To establish a CM model, we administered NTG to C57BL/6J mice every other day over a 9-day period (Fig. 1A). We systematically evaluated mechanical thresholds using von Frey filaments in both the periorbital region and hind paws at baseline and 2 h post-injection. Compared to saline-treated controls, NTG-treated mice exhibited a progressive and significant decline in withdrawal thresholds across the testing days, reflecting the development of robust basal and post-treatment mechanical allodynia (Fig. 1B, C). To further corroborate the fidelity of our model, we assessed canonical markers of trigeminovascular activation. Immunofluorescence analysis and subsequent quantification revealed a marked elevation in CGRP immunoreactivity alongside a significantly higher number of c-Fos-positive cells within the TNC of the NTG cohort (Fig. 1D–F). In agreement with these histological data, Western blotting confirmed a substantial upregulation of CGRP and c-Fos protein expression (Fig. 1G, H). Collectively, these behavioral and biochemical profiles strongly validate the successful establishment of the NTG-induced CM model.

Upregulation of FKBP5 in the TNC of the NTG-induced CM model

To unbiasedly identify the molecular alterations underlying CM, we first performed RNA-seq on TNC tissue lysates from both groups. Heatmap and volcano plot analyses revealed a distinct differential expression profile, highlighting a marked and statistically significant upregulation of Fkbp5 transcripts in NTG-treated mice compared to saline controls (Fig. 2A, B). To validate this transcriptomic discovery, we subsequently conducted RT-qPCR analysis. Consistent with the RNA-seq data, the mRNA levels of Fkbp5 were significantly increased in the TNC of the NTG group (Fig. 2C). Western blotting corroborated these transcriptional changes, demonstrating a significant upregulation of FKBP5 protein in TNC lysates from NTG-treated mice (Fig. 2D). Collectively, these transcriptional and translational data provide compelling evidence that FKBP5 expression is significantly enhanced in the TNC of the established CM model, positioning it as a prime candidate for further mechanistic investigation.

Cellular localization of FKBP5 in the TNC

To further delineate the cellular distribution of FKBP5 within the TNC, double immunofluorescence labeling was performed. We evaluated the colocalization of FKBP5 with established lineage markers for neurons (NeuN), microglia (Iba1), and astrocytes (GFAP). Confocal imaging revealed that FKBP5 immunoreactivity was predominantly localized within neurons, whereas minimal to no colocalization was observed with either microglia or astrocytes (Fig. 3A–C). This neuron-specific localization demonstrates that FKBP5 is selectively expressed within TNC neurons rather than glial cells. Quantitative immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed a significant increase in FKBP5 immunoreactivity in the TNC of NTG-treated mice compared to saline controls (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, to assess the neuroinflammatory status of the TNC microenvironment, we evaluated the activation levels of resident glial cells. This analysis revealed a significant increase in the fluorescence intensities of both Iba1 (Fig. 3E) and GFAP (Fig. 3F) in the NTG group. Collectively, these findings establish that while the NTG-induced upregulation of FKBP5 specifically occurs within TNC neurons, it is accompanied by marked astrocytic and microglial activation in the surrounding network. Based on this neuron-specific expression pattern, we next sought to determine whether selectively targeting neuronal FKBP5 could rescue NTG-induced mechanical allodynia.

Activation of the downstream NF-κB pathway and release of inflammatory mediators

To elucidate the downstream mechanisms by which FKBP5 modulates mechanical allodynia, we first analyzed our RNA-seq dataset. Bioinformatics pathway enrichment analysis revealed a significant enrichment of the “TNF-alpha signaling via NF-κB” pathway in the NTG group (Fig. 4A). Consistent with this transcriptomic clue, Western blotting confirmed significant NF-κB cascade activation, evidenced by elevated p-p65/p65 and p-IκBα/IκBα ratios in the TNC of NTG-treated mice (Fig. 4B). Consequently, the protein expression of downstream pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, was significantly upregulated in the NTG group (Fig. 4C). Collectively, these stepwise findings demonstrate that the NF-κB signaling axis and the subsequent pro-inflammatory cytokine cascade are potently triggered, highlighting a marked neuroinflammatory response in the CM model.

Targeted knockout of neuronal Fkbp5 in the TNC attenuates NTG-induced mechanical allodynia and NF-κB-mediated neuroinflammation

To directly establish a causal relationship between neuronal FKBP5 and CM pathogenesis, we employed a neuron-specific CRISPR-Cas9 editing system driven by the hSyn promoter. The gene-editing efficacy of candidate sgRNAs was initially validated in vitro using Neuro-2a cells, where successful targeted genomic disruption and subsequent protein depletion were confirmed via the T7E1 mismatch cleavage assay and Western blotting, respectively (Fig. S1). For in vivo functional studies, the validated constructs were packaged into lentiviruses and stereotaxically microinjected into the mouse TNC (Fig. 5A). Following a 21-day period to allow for sufficient neuronal Fkbp5 knockout, histological examination confirmed precise anatomical targeting via EGFP fluorescence, and Western blotting verified the efficient in vivo depletion of FKBP5 protein expression (Fig. 5B).

Subsequently, mice underwent the chronic NTG induction protocol and were evaluated for migraine-like behaviors. We observed that while neuronal Fkbp5 knockout did not alter basal nociceptive thresholds, it significantly attenuated the development of both periorbital and hind paw mechanical allodynia in NTG-treated mice compared to the control vector (sgCON) group (Fig. 5C). This behavioral rescue demonstrates that neuronal FKBP5 is required for pathological sensitization, without interfering with normal sensory processing.

Prompted by this behavioral mitigation, we next investigated the underlying biochemical mechanisms, focusing on the downstream master regulatory pathways. Western blotting revealed that FKBP5 deficiency significantly blunted the NTG-induced phosphorylation of p65 (p-p65) and IκBα (p-IκBα) in the TNC (Fig. 5D). Consequently, this inhibition of NF-κB pathway activation suppressed the downstream release of classical pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, the genetic ablation of Fkbp5 attenuated the upregulation of canonical trigeminovascular activation markers, CGRP and c-Fos (Fig. 5F). Collectively, these genetic interventions provide compelling in vivo evidence that neuronal FKBP5 acts as a key upstream modulator, driving the NF-κB pathway and neuroinflammatory cascade to sustain CM.

Pharmacological inhibition of FKBP5 with SAFit2 alleviates NTG-induced mechanical allodynia and neuroinflammation

While our genetic knockout strategy provided a compelling proof-of-concept, we next sought to evaluate the translational therapeutic potential of targeting FKBP5. To this end, we utilized SAFit2, a selective pharmacological antagonist of FKBP5. To rigorously control for solvent effects, an equivalent vehicle (VEH) containing DMSO was administered. Mice were treated with SAFit2 (10 mg/kg, every 12 h) or VEH throughout the NTG modeling protocol (Fig. 6A). Consistent with our genetic findings, behavioral assessments revealed that systemic SAFit2 administration did not alter basal mechanical thresholds in saline-treated mice. In the NTG group, treatment with SAFit2 significantly attenuated the progressive decline in both periorbital and hind paw mechanical withdrawal thresholds compared to VEH-treated controls (Fig. 6B). This significant behavioral mitigation indicates that pharmacological blockade of FKBP5 effectively alleviates migraine-associated mechanical allodynia.

Biochemical analysis of TNC tissue revealed that SAFit2 administration significantly blunted the NTG-induced activation of the NF-κB pathway, evidenced by a marked reduction in the p-p65/p65 and p-IκBα/IκBα ratios (Fig. 6C). Consequently, this pharmacological blockade suppressed the concomitant downstream production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, specifically IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, SAFit2 successfully diminished the elevated expression of canonical trigeminovascular activation markers, CGRP and c-Fos (Fig. 6E). Collectively, these results provide compelling preclinical evidence that pharmacological inhibition of FKBP5 by SAFit2 recapitulates the protective effects of genetic knockout, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for resolving NF-κB-driven neuroinflammation in CM.

Pharmacological blockade of NF-κB suppresses NTG-induced mechanical allodynia and neuroinflammation

Having demonstrated that targeting FKBP5 significantly suppresses NF-κB activation, we next sought to establish the NF-κB signaling cascade as the requisite downstream effector of this pathological process. To this end, mice received daily systemic injections of PDTC (40 mg/kg), a selective pharmacological inhibitor of NF-κB, or an equal volume of vehicle (VEH) throughout the NTG modeling protocol (Fig. 7A). Behavioral assessments revealed that, consistent with our FKBP5 intervention strategies, PDTC administration alone had no impact on the basal mechanical thresholds of saline-treated mice. However, in the NTG group, daily PDTC treatment significantly attenuated the development of both periorbital and hind paw mechanical allodynia compared to VEH-treated controls (Fig. 7B). This finding indicates that NF-κB activation is required for the behavioral phenotypes of CM.

To biochemically link this behavioral rescue to the suppression of the downstream inflammatory cascade, we evaluated protein expression in the TNC. Discussion

In the present study, we provide compelling preclinical evidence identifying FKBP5 as a key modulator of CM pathogenesis. Integrated transcriptomic and biochemical analyses consistently revealed a marked upregulation of Fkbp5 mRNA and FKBP5 protein levels in the TNC of NTG-treated mice, with expression predominantly restricted to neurons. Notably, this neuron-specific upregulation was accompanied by robust microglial and astrocytic activation. To establish causality, we demonstrated that both neuron-specific genetic knockout via the CRISPR-Cas9 system and pharmacological antagonism with SAFit2 significantly attenuated NTG-induced mechanical allodynia. At the molecular level, this analgesic effect was achieved by suppressing the NF-κB signaling cascade, thereby blunting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the upregulation of CGRP and c-Fos. Collectively, our findings delineate a crucial role for neuronal FKBP5 in trigeminovascular nociceptive processing, highlighting it as a promising therapeutic target.

Historically, FKBP5 has been extensively studied as a co-chaperone in stress-related psychiatric disorders [4, 5]. More recently, emerging evidence has implicated FKBP5 in modulating spinal neuroinflammation during chronic neuropathic pain [6, 7]. However, its specific involvement in CM, particularly within the trigeminovascular system, has remained entirely unexplored. In our study, rather than relying on a candidate-driven approach, we performed RNA-seq for an unbiased screen of the TNC transcriptome. Although certain transcripts exhibited more pronounced statistical alterations, we prioritized Fkbp5 for downstream mechanistic investigation based on its profound biological relevance and therapeutic tractability. Functionally, FKBP5 serves as a well-established hub integrating systemic stress responses with neuroinflammatory cascades [14, 35], which closely aligns with the extensive evidence that stress is a primary trigger for migraine chronification [8, 9, 36]. Furthermore, unlike many top-ranked transcriptomic hits that lack specific pharmacological modulators, the availability of highly selective, brain-penetrant inhibitors like SAFit2 makes FKBP5 a highly druggable and translationally viable target [37]. Thus, the robust upregulation of Fkbp5 identified through this approach strongly supports its pathological relevance to CM.

Interestingly, while neuroinflammation is classically attributed to glial activation [38, 39], our immunofluorescence analysis revealed that FKBP5 is predominantly localized to TNC neurons. Despite this neuronal restriction, the local microenvironment simultaneously exhibited robust microglial and astrocytic activation. Given that genetic ablation of neuronal Fkbp5 profoundly diminished the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) and suppressed the upregulation of CGRP and c-Fos, we postulate a crucial neuron-driven neuroinflammatory cascade orchestrated by FKBP5.

From a temporal perspective, because systemic NTG modeling initiates with peripheral events such as meningeal neurogenic inflammation and vascular changes that subsequently activate primary trigeminal afferents [20, 40], the robust upregulation of FKBP5 in TNC neurons likely occurs secondary to these upstream peripheral inputs. However, we postulate that once induced, FKBP5 ceases to be a mere downstream consequence; instead, it functions as a pivotal central amplifier. By initiating the release of early algogenic mediators, most notably CGRP, upregulated FKBP5 drives a paracrine signaling cascade. Released CGRP binds to its canonical receptor complexes, which are abundantly expressed on adjacent microglia and astrocytes, triggering rapid glial activation [41–43]. This reciprocal neuron-glia crosstalk establishes a positive feedback loop: activated glia secrete a potent secondary wave of pro-inflammatory cytokines that feed back onto neuronal receptors. Consequently, this dynamic neuro-immune interaction induces a profound state of neuronal hyperexcitability [44], which chronically lowers the nociceptive firing threshold and drives the synaptic plasticity underlying central sensitization [45]. Thus, targeting neuronal FKBP5 does not merely suppress isolated cytokines but effectively attenuates this vicious neuroinflammatory cycle at a critical upstream node.

Mechanistically, the NF-κB cascade serves as the crucial downstream pathway mediating the pronociceptive effects of neuronal FKBP5. While our transcriptomic enrichment analysis identified several globally altered biological processes, we strategically focused on the NF-κB signaling pathway because it represents the canonical molecular bridge linking upstream FKBP5 activation directly to the downstream production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [11, 12, 46]. We observed that abrogating FKBP5 profoundly blunted NTG-triggered phosphorylation of IκBα and p65 in the TNC. To contextualize the functional significance of this suppression, we propose a plausible mechanistic framework wherein activated neuronal NF-κB drives the transcription of Calca [47], and the resulting release of CGRP may subsequently trigger the secondary secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α). This amplified neuroinflammatory cascade likely culminates in sustained neuronal activation, as evidenced by c-Fos upregulation, and subsequent mechanical allodynia [39]. While our findings support this model, further targeted studies are necessary to independently validate each sequential link in this causal chain. Crucially, pharmacological blockade with PDTC effectively recapitulated the analgesic and biochemical effects of FKBP5 deficiency, supporting NF-κB as a key downstream effector of FKBP5. Collectively, these results delineate a critical FKBP5–NF-κB signaling axis that underpins CM pathogenesis.

An intriguing phenotypic observation in our study is that localized ablation of Fkbp5 within the TNC effectively alleviated not only periorbital but also hind paw mechanical allodynia. Since somatic nociceptive inputs from the hind limb are primarily relayed through the spinal dorsal horn rather than the TNC, this extracephalic behavioral rescue strongly suggests that our TNC-specific intervention modulates broader central sensitization mechanisms. During CM pathogenesis, persistent neuroinflammation and intense nociceptive firing originating in the trigeminovascular epicenter can ascend to sensitize higher-order pain processing hubs, such as the thalamus, and disrupt descending modulatory networks [10, 44, 48]. This systemic amplification ultimately drives widespread cutaneous allodynia [49]. Therefore, we propose that suppressing the primary neuroinflammatory drive within the TNC via Fkbp5 ablation effectively blocks the downstream propagation and maintenance of widespread central sensitization.

From a clinical perspective, traditional prophylactic medications and modern CGRP-targeted therapies remain the mainstay for CM management. However, a substantial proportion of patients experience refractory responses to current CGRP blockade [2, 50]. Given that our data position FKBP5 as an upstream central amplifier that drives not only CGRP release but also a broader network of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α), targeting FKBP5 may offer a more comprehensive approach to dampening neuroinflammation, particularly for these refractory cohorts. Crucially, our in vivo validation using SAFit2 firmly supports the druggability of FKBP5, highlighting its strong potential as a therapeutic target [37]. However, significant translational hurdles remain. While SAFit2 specifically avoids the calcineurin-dependent immunosuppressive effects characteristic of pan-FKBP inhibitors like FK506 [37], its comprehensive long-term safety, toxicity, and pharmacokinetic profiles in humans are currently uncharacterized. Given these evidence gaps, parallel investigations into drug repurposing, such as screening clinically available medications for off-target FKBP5-antagonizing properties, may offer a more realistic and accelerated translational pathway for CM management.

Importantly, our data establish that neuronal FKBP5 selectively contributes to pathological sensitization rather than acting as a generic analgesic switch. Inherently functioning as a stress-responsive co-chaperone, FKBP5 appears largely dispensable for basal sensory processing [6, 7]. Consistent with this, neither genetic ablation nor pharmacological inhibition altered basal mechanical thresholds in saline-treated control mice, confirming that physiological nociception remains intact. Instead, FKBP5 blockade specifically normalized the NTG-induced pathological hyperactivation of the trigeminovascular system, hallmarked by the pronounced local release of CGRP [42, 47]. Therefore, targeting FKBP5 provides a highly precise therapeutic window by neutralizing pathological pain states without disrupting normal sensory transmission.

Several limitations in the present study warrant consideration. First, given the broad physiological roles of FKBP5 in regulating glucocorticoid signaling and systemic stress biology [3–5], the potential endocrine risks associated with its long-term systemic inhibition must be carefully evaluated. Chronic systemic blockade could inadvertently interfere with normal stress resilience and metabolic homeostasis. Exploring TNC-targeted delivery systems will be an important next step to mitigate these systemic hurdles. Second, our experimental design lacked an episodic migraine (EM) control cohort or a longitudinal framework to track the transition from EM to CM. Consequently, we cannot definitively conclude that our findings are exclusive to CM pathogenesis. Finally, this study was conducted primarily using male mice; given the female preponderance in clinical migraine, expanding future investigations to include female cohorts will be crucial.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings demonstrate that neuronal FKBP5 is significantly associated with trigeminovascular sensitization and neuroinflammation in an NTG-induced chronic migraine model. By modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway, FKBP5 promotes the local production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and CGRP, thereby sustaining mechanical allodynia. Consequently, targeting the FKBP5-NF-κB axis represents a promising therapeutic approach for mitigating migraine-associated central sensitization.
