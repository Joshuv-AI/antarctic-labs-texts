Sucking lollipop after awakening from sevoflurane anesthesia reduces the degree of emergence agitation in children undergoing ambulatory surgery: A prospective randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xiaofei Mo, Jiequn Zeng, Xiaoying Wu, Fa Huang, Kunling Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Emergence agitation (EA) is a common complication in pediatric anesthesia, especially in preschool children maintained by sevoflurane, with incidence ranging up to 80%. The purpose of the study was to determine whether sucking lollipop after awakening from sevoflurane anesthesia reduced the degree of EA in children undergoing ambulatory surgery.Methods:In this prospective study, 40 children aged 2 to 6 years scheduled for ambulatory surgery with sevoflurane were enrolled. They were randomly allocated to 1 of 2 groups after evaluating baseline EA levels using the pediatric anesthesia emergence delirium (PAED) scale immediately after awakening from general anesthesia: group L (sucking lollipop) or group C (control group, without sucking lollipop). The primary outcome was the overall PAED score after intervention. Pain score, parental satisfaction, the incidence of propofol rescue and negative postoperative behavioral changes (NPOBCs) were assessed.Results:The overall PAED score after intervention was significantly lower in Group L compared with Group C, with an estimated difference of −1.857 (95% CI, −2.884 to −0.831; P < .001) using generalized estimating equations. However, no significant intergroup differences were observed in the pain score, parental satisfaction, the incidence of propofol rescue and NPOBCs.Conclusions:Sucking lollipop after awakening from sevoflurane anesthesia reduced the degree EA in children undergoing ambulatory surgery.

1. Introduction

Sevoflurane has been suggested to be a popular choice for pediatric anesthesia undergoing ambulatory surgery due to its rapid uptake and elimination.[1] However, since its introduction, emergence agitation (EA) has been recognized as a problem that may occur during recovery from pediatric anesthesia with sevoflurane. Children with EA can increase the risks of self-injury, dressing disruption, surgical dehiscence or accidental removal of intravenous catheters, and increase extra nursing care.[2] Moreover, the negative psychological impact of EA can stretch beyond the perioperative period, including negative postoperative behaviors, such as temper tantrums, nightmares, separation anxiety and eating disorders.[3,4] Therefore, the prevention and treatment of EA remains significant challenges in pediatric anesthesia.

Various pharmacologic therapies have been investigated to reduce EA in children. However, these methods have their own challenges and limitations.[4–12] Several studies have explored non-pharmacologic techniques as novel alternative, such as visual preconditioning with an eye patch prior to unilateral cataract surgery,[13] parental presence or playing video games and watching cartoon before anesthetic induction,[14–17] and listening to the mother recorded voice during the emergence period,[18] but they are either time-consuming, increase workload, or require special equipment. Sweet taste have been suggested to produce pain-reducing, calming, and stress reducing effects in infant humans and rats,[19–26] and most children like to suck lollipop in their daily routine. Taken together, it is expected that sucking lollipop after awakening can produce distraction, calming, and can reduce stress and pain in children. Therefore, the goal of this study was to evaluate whether sucking lollipop after awakening from sevoflurane anesthesia would reduce the degree of EA in children undergoing ambulatory surgery.

2. Methods

This prospective, randomized trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Women and Children Medical Centre (ref: 2021-194A01) on October 18,2021. The protocol was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ref: ChiCTR2100052986) on November 7,2021 before enrollment. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or the legal guardians of the participants of each subject before the surgery.

2.1. Participants

Paediatric patients aged 2 to 6 years with ASA physical status I or II, who were scheduled for ambulatory ophthalmic or urological surgery under general anesthesia with sevoflurane, were enrolled. The exclusion criteria were as follows: allergies to study drugs, failure to communicate normally, parental refusal, neurological illness, developmental delay, bilateral ophthalmic surgery, moderate upper tract infection, diabetes, carbohydrate malabsorption syndrome, and other endocrine or genetic metabolic diseases (contraindication to the ingredients of lollipop).

2.2. Randomization and blinding

The enrolled subjects were randomly assigned (1:1) to the lollipop group (Group L) or the control group (Group C) according to the computer-generated randomization list. Patients in the Group L received sucking lollipop after awakening from sevoflurane anesthesia. The Group C received standard medical care without sucking lollipop. Allocation was concealed by sequentially numbered sealed opaque envelopes. Upon arrival at the postanaesthesia care unit (PACU), the envelope was opened. The observer in PACU was aware of group allocation, but anesthesia providers, the researcher who was responsible for collecting relevant data before anesthesia and after discharge, and surgeons were not.

2.3. Anaesthesia

According to the ASA guidelines for preoperative fasting,[27] we adopted intervals of 6, 4, and 2 hours of fasting for solids, breast milk and clear fluids, respectively. After patients arrived at the preoperative waiting room and intravenous access for anesthesia was obtained, a researcher blinded to the groups collected baseline data from the family. This included the child age; sex; parent level of education; type of surgery; the parent anxiety score measured with the Chinese version of State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), which evaluates trait (baseline) and state (situational) anxiety; and the child preoperative anxiety baseline score assessed with the Chinese version of modified Yale Preoperative Anxiety Scale (m-YPAS).

In the holding room, all patients received propofol 1 mg kg−1 i.v. in the parental surveillance, and the children were then transferred to the operating theater immediately after loss of consciousness. Once the patient was positioned on the operating table, standard monitoring (ECG, noninvasive arterial blood pressure, and SpO2) were applied. Anaesthesia was induced by inhalation of 8% sevoflurane (with oxygen 8 L min−1) via a face mask, and intravenously with penehyclidine hydrochloride 0.01 mg kg−1 and sufentanil 0.5 μg kg−1. The laryngeal mask airway (LMA, The Henan Tuoren Medical Devices Company Ltd, China) was inserted without neuromuscular blocking drugs after adequate jaw relaxation was attained. LMA size was determined according to the manufacturer guidelines: size 2 for patients 10 to 20 kg in body weight; size 2.5 for 20 to 30 kg and size 3 for 30 to 50 kg. If LMA insertion failed after 3 attempts, tracheal intubation was performed, then the subject was withdrawn from the study. Anaesthesia was maintained via inhalation of 2% to 4% sevoflurane and 50% oxygen with pressure-controlled ventilation mode to maintain PETCO2 (35–45 mm Hg). All patients received prophylactic antiemetics with dolasetrone 0.3 mg kg−1 and preventive postoperative analgesia with flurbiprofen axetil 1 mg kg−1 10 minutes before the end of the surgery. In addition, after LMA insertion and before operation, we performed dorsal penile nerve blocks with 0.25% ropivacaine 0.2 mL kg−1 for circumcision surgery. We did not perform nerve blocks for the other types of surgery. Nerve blocks were deemed inadequate if the patient heart rate increased >20% within 60 seconds of skin incision. Only subjects with an adequate nerve blocks were included in this study.

At the end of surgery, sevoflurane was discontinued and the fraction of inspired oxygen was increased to 100% with oxygen flow at 6 L min−1. The LMA was removed spontaneously with tide volume >5 mL kg−1 under anesthesia state, and then the patients were transported to the PACU.

2.4. Intervention and data collection

Upon arrival at the PACU, the patients were continuously monitored for ECG, respiratory rate, and SpO2. At our institution, parents were not allowed to stay in the PACU. A well-trained PACU nurse checked all measurements. The subjects were stimulated to wake up by light patting on the shoulder and verbal command every 5 minutes after the arrival at the PACU. If patients started crying or the first response to verbal command, it is defined as emergence (T0; baseline). After evaluating baseline (T0) EA levels using the pediatric anesthesia emergence delirium (PAED) scale (Supplementary Table S1, http://links.lww.com/MD/K406) defined by Sikich and Lerman in 2004,[28] patients in the lollipop group received standard medical care and started to suck lollipop (Yake Food Co., Ltd, Fujian, China), while patients in the control group received standard medical care without lollipop. According to the product description, a lollipop weighs approximately 10.5 g. Each 100 g contains 1695 kJ energy, 0 g protein, 1.7 g fat, 96.0 g carbohydrate and 70 mg sodium. PAED values were recorded at 4 times points: 5 minutes (T1), 10 minutes (T2), 20 minutes (T3) and 30 minutes (T4) after intervention. The PAED scale has been tested for validity and reliability and is currently the most frequently used instrument in pediatric studies of EA, which consists of 5 psychometric items describing emergence behavior, with a range of 0 to 20 with higher scores indicating greater agitation.[28] Patients with PAED score of ≥ 10 were diagnosed as having EA. Pain scores were also evaluated at T0-T4 using the face, legs, activity, cry, and consolability (FLACC; Supplementary Table S2, http://links.lww.com/MD/K407) scale ranging from 0 to 15.[29] A FLACC score of ≥ 4 meant that pain was present. After the interventions were given according to the groups, the PAED score were still ≥13, which was considered to need medication administration to reduce EA,[18] propofol 1 mg kg−1 was administration as a rescue medication and repeated if the agitation did not subside.

When subjects were fully awake, and had stable vital signs, patent airway without support, and oxygen saturation ≥95% breathing room air, they were transferred to the outpatient recovery room and the duration of the PACU stay was recorded as the PACU stay time. Then children stayed with their guardian until the children were discharged when the ambulatory surgery discharge scoring (Supplementary Table S3, http://links.lww.com/MD/K408) reached 9.[30] Immediately before discharge, the parent satisfaction was evaluated with a 5-point scale as follows: 1, very disappointed; 2, disappointed; 3, so-so; 4, satisfactory; 5, very pleased.

Negative postoperative behavioral changes (NPOBCs) were observed at 1, 7, and 30 days after discharge via parent phone surveys by a blinded researcher. The presence of NPOBCs was assessed with the Post Hospitalisation Behavior Questionnaire for Ambulatory Surgery (PHBQ-AS; Supplementary Table S4, http://links.lww.com/MD/K409), an 11-item parental report. To score the PHBQ-AS, items were averaged by summing the items for each respondent and then dividing by the total number of items. The PHBQ-AS score produces a continuous variable with higher values above 3 (the midpoint) indicating greater maladaptive behavioral changes, lower values below 3 indicating improvements in behavioral change, and values equal to 3 indicating no behavioral change.

2.5. Outcomes

2.5.1. Primary outcome.

The primary outcome was the EA score assessed with the PAED score at 5 minutes (T1), 10 minutes (T2), 20 minutes (T3) and 30 minutes (T4) after intervention in the PACU.

2.5.2. Secondary outcomes.

The secondary outcomes included the incidence of EA before intervention, which was defined as a PAED score ≥10; FLACC score; the extubation time; the emergence time (time from discontinuation of sevoflurane to emergence); PACU stay time; outpatient recovery room stay time; parental satisfaction, the adverse events such as postoperative nausea or vomiting (PONV), laryngospasm, hypotension (defined as a value of at least 15% less than the normal mean value for the child age), hypoxemia (defined as SpO2 < 90%), the presence of NPOBCs.

2.6. Sample size calculation

The sample size estimation was based on the primary outcome. No previous data were available for sample size estimation. Therefore, a pilot study including 20 children was carried out in this study and the detailed data are available in supplementary table S5, http://links.lww.com/MD/K410. However, 2 patients were excluded from sample size estimation because they was administrated with propofol to prevent severe agitation, so they still be sedated at T1 and T2 making it impossible to be evaluated with PAED. Then the remaining 18 were used to provide parameters required for sample size estimation. The data of the pilot study were not included in data analysis in the current study. The sample size was estimated by repeated-measures analysis of variance and was calculated based on PASS software Repeated Measures Analysis module (version 15.0 NCSS, LLC. Kaysville, Utah, USA). With alpha (α) at the 0.05 level, a sample size of 14 per group or a total sample size of 28 would provide 90% power (1-β) to detect a significant difference. To account for 20% dropouts, we planned to enroll 40 subjects.

2.7. Statistical analysis

A statistician who was not involved in the data collection conducted all statistical analyses with SPSS software (version 25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For the normality test, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was performed. Data were expressed as the mean [standard deviation (SD)], the median (inter-quartile range), or the number (percentage) as appropriate. The differences between groups were compared using the Student t test (normally distributed data) or Mann–Whitney U test (skewed data). Categorical and proportions were analyzed using the Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher exact test as appropriate.

The overall profile of PAED scores and FLACC scores were assessed between groups with generalized estimating equations (GEE). Missing data were treated as missing at random and imputation for missing data was not performed. The test was followed the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple pairwise comparisons (4 comparisons) if a significant intergroup difference was found. A Bonferroni-adjusted P value < .0125 (0.05/4) was considered statistically significant.

Patients were analyzed according to modified intention-to-treat, defined as analysis of patients who were randomly assigned and received at least a portion of the study intervention. A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical tests were 2-tailed.

One patient in each group received propofol sedation due to severe agitation in PACU, so they did not received intervention according to groups at T1 and T2, but received intervention when they were re-awakening at T3 and T4.

4. Discussion

Our study demonstrated that sucking lollipop after awakening from sevoflurane anesthesia lowers PAED score in preschool children undergoing ambulatory surgery.

Many pharmacological techniques have been validated to reduce EA. However, pharmacologic interventions may result in a variety of side effects.[4,18] Then, the non-pharmacologic treatments have become more appealing in the efforts to reduce EA. Byun and colleagues[18] demonstrated that listening to the mother recorded voice during the emergence period reduced EA score in pediatric patients after general anesthesia. Nevertheless, this method required recording the mother voice before the operation, which required a lot of manpower, and needed special headphones playback equipment. A randomized clinical trial showed that visual preconditioning with an eye patch prior to unilateral cataract surgery under sevoflurane anesthesia reduced EA in children, but prolonged emergence time and PACU stay time.[13] Additionally, prophylactic eye-patching may cause inconvenience, confusions and potential risks of injury for children. Parental presence in the PACU during anesthetic emergence seems to be effective in reducing separation anxiety,[17,31] but it is difficult to apply in daily clinical practice in overpopulated Asian countries. In addition, the presence of the parents in the PACU may also be a psychological burden for the medical staff. Our method, however, is easy to administer, inexpensive and readily available for the reduction of EA score in the children after general anesthesia.

In our study, there was no difference in PACU stay time between the 2 groups. This finding was consistent with previous studies by Byun S. et al,[18] in which Mother recorded voice on emergence did not shorten the PACU stay time. This can be due to the observation duration (30 minutes) we set, so all patients both meet the PACU discharge criteria and complete the evaluation of T4 before leaving PACU. This may mask the true PACU stay time.

Postoperative pain and separation anxiety have been shown to be risk factors for EA. In order to minimize postoperative pain, sufentanil and flurbiprofen axetil were administered for all the patients in our study. In addition, nerve block was also performed in patients undergoing circumcision surgery. Indeed, a rat study has suggested that calming, analgesia and stress reducing can be induced via sweet taste.[24] Furthermore, clinical studies have shown that oral sweet-tasting solutions produces pain-reducing effects in human infants undergoing painful medical procedures such as venipuncture, intramuscular injections, heel prick and vaccination.[19–26] The use of orally administered sweet tasting solutions for managing neonatal pain in the clinical setting is widespread and is recommended in both national and international guidelines.[21] The mechanisms of orally administered sweet tasting solutions producing calming and analgesic effects remains unclear. Some studies[20,24] suggested that the calming and pain-relieving effects of sucrose are thought to be mediated by endogenous opioid pathways activated by sweet taste. Sweet taste stimulation activates the pleasure center of the brain, including release of dopamine, is another explanation.[23] Meng and colleagues[32] showed that lollipops sucking provided effective postoperative pain relief in children. Therefore, we speculated the reason for sucking lollipop to reduce agitation might be that sweet taste produces calming and pain-reducing effects in children after postoperative recovery period. In addition, listening music,[33] transporting by a toy car,[34] playing video games and watching cartoon[15,17] have been used with variable success to decrease pain and preoperative anxiety due to the distraction. So, another possible reason is that children may be distracted and feel less worried when they are enjoying sucking lollipop and tend to disregard strange environment.

The objective of preoperative fasting is to minimize the risk of pulmonary aspiration of gastric contents posed by the combination of regurgitation and loss of protective airway reflexes by anesthetic agents. However, children have a higher metabolic rate and reduced glycogen stores, and surgical stress leads to high consumption, which may cause children experienced a strong sense of hunger, thirst and hypoglycemia. Although we did not monitor blood glucose in our study in order to avoid unnecessary medical procedures, we inferred that sucking lollipop may alleviate children hypoglycemia, hunger and thirst to reduce EA.

The incidence of NPOBCs on the first day in our trial (40.0% in lollipop group; 42.1% in control groups) was similar to that reported in previous studies (49%)[17] and (47%).[35] Further, in our study, the incidence of NPOBCs decreased by time. There was a reduction in the incidence of NPOBCs in the lollipop group compared with control group on postoperative day 7 and day 30, however, the difference was not statistically significant. It is possible that the current sample size might have been insufficient to detect the effects of the different interventions on NPOBCs.

Our results suggest that sucking lollipop can reduce the PAED score of children with non-severe agitation after sevoflurane anesthesia. Therefore, this method can reduce the side effects caused by medication administration, such as respiratory depression and longer recovery time. Moreover, it appears to be easy-to-use, highly accepted, cost-less, and complication-free, so it could be used widely in clinical practice.

Our study also has limitation. First, double-blinding was impossible in this study because sucking lollipop or not were visible to investigators, and thus, observer bias may have influenced assessments of PAED score. Second, we did not include 1 group receiving pharmacologic treatment as a positive control group. Third, lollipops cannot be used for children with severe agitation.

5. Conclusions

The degree and incidence of EA were decreased in pediatric patients who sucking lollipop after awakening from sevoflurane anesthesia during the emergence period.
