Application of a diagnostic strategy for patients with suspected pulmonary embolism in a middle-income country
Authors: Oswaldo Chazi-Inga, Edison Ortiz-Freire, María Augusta Pesantez, Gabriela Ledesma, Betzy Jaramillo, Aida Palaguachi, Jorge Castillo, Francisco Faican, Hernán Aguirre-Bermeo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe implementation of diagnostic strategies in patients with suspected pulmonary embolism (PE) aims to reduce lethality and optimize hospital resources. The objectives of the present study were to describe the use of computed tomographic pulmonary angiography (CTPA) in a hospital located in a middle-income country and apply a diagnostic strategy to optimize the use of CTPA in the management of patients with suspected PE, while also evaluating its impact on hospital costs.MethodsRetrospective observational study. All CTPA studies ordered in patients with suspected PE during the study period were reviewed. CTPA requests from hospitalized and emergency department patients for suspected PE were included. Patients treated in outpatient clinics, with hemodynamic instability, incomplete medical history, pregnant women, and those under 18 years old were excluded. The Student’s t-test for paired samples was used to compare CTPA-related costs.ResultsCTPA was performed on 103 patients with suspected PE and 85 patients met the inclusion criteria. The diagnosis of PE was confirmed by CTPA in 13% [n = 11] of patients. Out of the 85 patients included, the diagnostic strategy could not be applied in 14 of them due to the unavailability of D-dimer. We found that the use of the diagnostic strategy would reduce CTPA requests by at least 20% and could significantly decrease hospital costs ($26,746 in all included patients vs. $17,621 with diagnostic strategy, p = 0.01).ConclusionsAmong all patients suspected of PE, 13% were confirmed by CTPA. The use of the diagnostic strategy decreases the percentage of CTPA requests and, consequently, significantly reduce patient irradiation and hospital costs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03963-9.

Background

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is defined as the obstruction of pulmonary blood flow due to the migration of thrombi, most commonly originating in the deep veins of the lower extremities or pelvis, although they may also arise from the upper extremities. Its formation is associated with venous stasis, hypercoagulability, endothelial injury, or may occur idiopathically [1, 2]. Other types of emboli include air, fat, tumor, and amniotic fluid embolisms [2, 3].

Obstruction of pulmonary blood flow disrupts the ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) ratio, impairing gas exchange and increasing pulmonary vascular resistance. This may compromise right ventricular function and result in hemodynamic collapse [2].The overall reported mortality of pulmonary embolism (PE) was 14.1%, and in patients with hemodynamic instability, mortality ranges from 14% to 58% [4]. The most common consequences of the disease include persistent dyspnea and reduced physical capacity, encompassed under the term post-pulmonary embolism syndrome [5, 6].

Pulmonary embolism can be an underdiagnosed disease. Ruppert et al. [7], found that among 291 autopsies with postmortem diagnosis of PE, only 13% had been suspected by physicians. Conversely, over the past few years, there has been a consistent and significant increase in the utilization rate of computed tomographic and the associated radiation exposure [8]. Non-clinically justified use of CTPA has been reported in up to one-third of patients with suspected PE [9, 10].

In recent years, diagnostic strategies have been implemented to reduce the mortality rates associated with PE and optimize resource utilization [11]. These strategies involve the combination of clinical scales, laboratory tests, and imaging techniques [1, 2, 12]. Currently, CTPA is considered the gold standard for diagnosing PE [13]; however, the excessive utilization of diagnostic imaging not only exposes patients to unnecessary radiation but also carries significant economic implications [14]. These economic consequences can be particularly pronounced in developing countries [15].

Freund et al. described a diagnostic strategy for patients with suspected PE [2]. To our knowledge, there is currently no clinical study that describes the application of this diagnostic strategy. Therefore, the objectives of our study were to provide an overview of the utilization of CTPA in a hospital located in a middle-income country, implement the diagnostic strategy proposed by Freund et al. [2] to optimize the use of CTPA in the management of patients with suspected PE, and evaluate its impact on hospital costs.

Methods

A retrospective observational study was conducted at Vicente Corral Moscoso University Hospital in a middle-income country (Cuenca, Ecuador) from January 1 to December 31, 2019. The study received approval from the teaching and research department at our hospital (203-UDI-HVCM-2018).

Patients

All CTPA studies ordered in patients with suspected PE during the study period were reviewed.

The study included all CTPA studies performed in hospitalized or emergency department patients with suspected PE. CTPA studies of patients seen in outpatient consultation, those with hemodynamic instability, incomplete medical history, pregnancy, and age below 18 years were excluded.

Variables

The following variables were collected: age, gender, reason for admission and comorbidities. Procedures performed within the 4 weeks prior to patient admission were considered as surgical procedures.

The hospital medical staff requested CTPA based on their clinical judgment, clinical prediction scales and complementary exams. There is no hospital protocol for performing CTPA in patients with suspected PE.

Scan and interpretation protocol

All CTPA examinations were performed following a standardized institutional protocol (“PE protocol”). Scans were acquired using a 64-slice CT scanner (Siemens, Forchheim, Germany) with high-resolution contrast-enhanced pulmonary angiography. Images were obtained with a slice thickness of 0.6 mm and reconstructed using coronal and sagittal multiplanar reformations, as well as maximum intensity projection (MIP) reconstructions.

Image interpretation was carried out by one of two board-certified thoracic radiologists from the hospital’s radiology department, each with over 10 years of experience in pulmonary imaging. This standardized acquisition and interpretation protocol ensured diagnostic accuracy and minimized the likelihood of false-positive or false-negative results.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as mean and standard deviation or as numbers and percentages, as appropriate. The Student’s t-test for related samples was used to compare the CTPA values. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS statistical software program (v.25.0: IBM-SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA).

Eighty-five (83%) of them were included in the study (Fig. 1).Fifty-seven (67%) of the patients were female, and the mean age was 57 ± 22 years. The most common comorbidities were chronic cardiac disease in 34 patients (37%), endocrine disorders in 16 (17%), and chronic pulmonary disease in 11 (12%) (Table 2).

Discussion

The present study shows that among patients in whom the hospital medical staff suspected PE and requested a CTPA, 13% received a confirmed diagnosis. If the diagnostic strategy were implemented [2], there would be a reduction of at least 20% in CTPA requests, leading to a significant decrease in patient irradiation and hospital costs.

The overall prevalence of PE described is 20%, with higher rates in Europe (23%) and lower rates in North America (8%) [20]. Van der Hulle et al. [21] conducted a study on 3465 patients and found a 13% prevalence of PE among those suspected of the disease. Martinez et al. [22] reported a higher percentage in patients with SARS-CoV2 infection (26%). The present study was not conducted on patients with SARS-CoV2 infection, and the prevalence of PE among patients with suspected PE was similar to that previously reported [21].

The European Society of Cardiology guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute pulmonary embolism recommend that the diagnostic strategy must be based on clinical judgment or in validated scales [18]. The use of prediction scales could be superior to the so-called “clinical hunch”, as their application improves the performance of CTPA in cases of suspected PE [23].

As previously described, Freund et al. [2] proposed a diagnostic strategy with four options for selecting patients with suspected PE who should undergo CTPA. All options include the use of the PERC rule. Previous studies have evaluated the PERC rule and demonstrated that its use decreases the number of CTPA requests [24, 25]. The PROPER study showed that applying the PERC rule reduced the use of chest imaging by 10% in patients with suspected PE [24]. Kothare et al. [25] conducted a retrospective study on 529 patients who underwent CTPA for suspected PE. If the PERC rule had been applied to those patients, the number of CTPA requests would have been reduced by 6% without missing a diagnosis of PE. In our study, applying the PERC rule alone would have avoided CTPA in 7% of cases (n = 5), consistent with the findings of Kothare et al. [25]. However, combining the PERC rule with other clinical prediction scores and D-dimer testing increased the percentage of avoidable CTPAs to 21% (n = 15).

Two options of the diagnostic strategy propose the utilization of the Wells score (options A and B), while two options suggest the use of the revised Geneva score (options C and D) as clinical risk prediction scores. In a meta-analysis involving 3613 patients, the Wells score was compared to the revised Geneva score, demonstrating that the Wells score was more effective in distinguishing PE in patients suspected of the disease (Wells score AUC: 0.789 vs. revised Geneva score AUC: 0.693; p < 0.001) [26]. Similarly, Wong et al. [27] found that compared with the revised Geneva Score, the Wells Score could safely reduce the number of unnecessary scans.

Options B and D of the diagnostic strategy propose the utilization of the YEARS rule to establish the D-dimer threshold for determining the need for CTPA. However, the YEARS rule demonstrates lower specificity compared to the Wells score (used in Option A) in patients with suspected PE (14% vs. 34%, respectively) [28]. Additionally, the YEARS rule may fail to detect PE in some cases, as described by Eddy et al. [29]. In their study of 3,314 patients, 1.2% of those excluded based on the YEARS rule (negative criteria and D-dimer < 1000 ng/mL) were later confirmed to have PE. Similarly, in a study by de Wit et al., the accuracy of the YEARS rule was compared with age-adjusted D-dimer in 1,703 patients. The YEARS rule missed 1 out of every 14 PE cases, whereas the age-adjusted D-dimer strategy did not miss any [30].

The main finding of our study is that the implementation of a diagnostic strategy is essential to identify patients with suspected PE who should undergo CTPA. Its application has the potential to reduce the number of CTPA requests by at least 20%. However, based on our study results, the specific choice of diagnostic strategy does not appear to significantly influence the number of avoided CTPA requests. Taking into account the following considerations: (a) the selection of a clinical prediction rule may depend on local conditions, physician preference, and familiarity [31]; (b) the Wells score has shown superior diagnostic performance for PE compared to the Geneva score [26, 27]; (c) the limitations previously discussed concerning the YEARS rule [28, 29]; and (d) Option A is easier for physicians to apply as it involves fewer clinical prediction tools (PERC rule plus Wells score), we believe that Option A offers a more streamlined and practical approach for clinical implementation.

Computed tomographic pulmonary angiography has demonstrated a sensitivity of 83% and a specificity of 96% for diagnosing PE [32]. Wang et al. [33] reported a significant increase of 450% in the utilization of CTPA for suspected PE in recent years, leading to higher exposure to radiation and intravenous contrast. Furthermore, the use of CTPA has been associated with a potential increase in the risk of cancer by up to 0.009% [34]. Additionally, diagnostic imaging, including CTPA, has been identified as a major contributing factor to rising healthcare costs [35], particularly in developing countries [15]. Therefore, standardizing the appropriate indications for performing CTPA is strongly recommended. In the current study, the implementation of diagnostic strategy options A and B would have resulted in a cost reduction of $4720 and would significantly decrease patient exposure to radiation.

According to the diagnostic strategy, patients with a negative PERC rule or those classified as high-risk for PE do not require D-dimer testing. However, for other suspected cases, D-dimer testing is essential to ensure the effectiveness of the diagnostic approach, as its combination with clinical prediction rules enhances both sensitivity and specificity. In a retrospective study of 917 patients by Kharawala et al., combining low clinical probability (Wells score, YEARS rule, or Geneva score) with a D-dimer < 500 ng/mL (or age-adjusted) achieved a sensitivity of up to 83% and a negative predictive value greater than 95%, supporting its importance in safely ruling out PE [12].

Pulmonary embolism has a high mortality rate, particularly in the presence of hemodynamic instability [4]. In fact, a systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg has been identified as the main independent predictor of death [36]. Freund et al. described a 30-day mortality rate ranging from 5% in low-risk patients to 20% in high-risk cases [2]. Although the present study did not include follow-up of mortality among patients diagnosed with PE, evaluating this outcome is crucial and should be considered in future prospective studies.

The importance of the present study lies in demonstrating that the use of a structured diagnostic strategy can significantly reduce unnecessary CTPA requests, thereby decreasing patient radiation exposure while generating substantial economic and time savings for healthcare personnel. Although this study was conducted in a developing country, its findings provide a valuable foundation for future large-scale randomized studies to confirm these results across different healthcare systems.

This study has limitations inherent to its design: (1) As a retrospective study, there is a potential risk of selection and information bias. (2) Patients were excluded due to missing D-dimer results, which reduced the sample size and may have affected the actual estimation of the reduction in CTPA requests. (3) Owing to the retrospective design, data regarding mortality and other clinical outcomes were not available. (4) This was a single-center study conducted in a middle-income country; therefore, extrapolating these findings to other healthcare settings requires caution.

Conclusions

In the present study, among all patients with suspected pulmonary embolism, 13% were confirmed with the diagnosis through CTPA. The implementation of a diagnostic strategy combining clinical prediction scales and D-dimer testing significantly reduced the number of CTPA requests. Moreover, this approach decreases unnecessary patient radiation exposure, reduces hospital costs, and saves healthcare staff time, thereby allowing more efficient consideration of alternative etiologies for patients’ symptoms. These findings provide valuable data supporting the need for further research, ideally through large randomized studies conducted in diverse healthcare systems.
