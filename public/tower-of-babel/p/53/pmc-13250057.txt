Enhanced anaerobic degradation and modeling of raw and treated municipal solid waste
Authors: Yaser Saleem Siddiqui, Asif Ali Siddiqui, Sohail Ayub, Fazlollah Changani, Mohammad Hadi Dehghani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional landfills suffer from slow waste stabilization, resulting in delayed methane recovery and persistent environmental pollution. This study addresses this challenge by investigating the comparative biogas generation potential and stabilization kinetics of raw and treated municipal solid waste (MSW) under anaerobic degradation in lab scale sealed bioreactors over a 300-day period, while integrating predictive modeling of biogas and chemical oxygen demand (COD) dynamics as an innovative analytical approach. Key parameters, including pH, volatile fatty acids (VFAs), chemical oxygen demand (COD), ammonia-nitrogen (NH3-N), electrical conductivity (EC) and heavy metal concentrations, were monitored to evaluate system performance. Treated MSW (windrow compost) exhibited early methanogenesis within one week, achieving a methane concentration of about 66%, whereas raw waste required nearly five weeks due to higher VFA buildup and acidic conditions that inhibited microbial activity. Cumulative biogas production stabilized at 68 L for treated and 365 L for raw waste, with methane content reaching 70% in the latter. Treated waste showed faster pH stabilization (above 6.5), sharper COD reduction of about 98%, and enhanced microbial efficiency compared to raw waste. Elevated NH3-N and heavy metal levels (Zn, Cu, Ni) in raw MSW declined progressively with digestion. A novel predictive modeling approach was applied using the ORANGE data-mining to reliably forecast biogas generation and organic matter degradation trends. The strong agreement between predicted and observed values demonstrates the capability of data-driven tools to capture the complex behaviour of waste degradation processes. This integration of experimental bioreactor studies with predictive modeling represents a key innovation of the work, enabling improved forecasting of landfill performance. From an environmental perspective, such predictive capability supports better bioreactor management, faster waste stabilization, and more efficient biogas recovery, contributing to sustainable waste-to-energy systems and reduced long-term environmental risks associated with landfill operations.Keywords: Municipal solid waste, raw waste, treated waste, bioreactor landfills, biodegradation, leachateSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Bioreactor landfills represent a modern and sustainable approach to waste management, distinct from traditional landfills involving the controlled introduction and recirculation of leachate to enhance biodegradation1. This process aids bacteria in breaking down waste into smaller fragments and the addition of liquid in controlled quantities accelerates microbial activity, leading to quicker waste biodegradation and stabilization. Globally, the average daily waste generation per person is 0.74 kg, though this figure varies widely from 0.11 to 4.54 kg across different regions. India generates about 1,70,339 tonnes per day of MSW out of which 1,56,449 tonnes is collected, 91,511 tonnes is treated, and 41,455 tonnes are landfilled2. Notably, India’s Kanjurmarg bioreactor landfill is the country’s only example of this technology, underscoring the need for broader implementation3–7. Very few studies25 have been done on bioreactor landfills in India so this study comprising biodegradation and predictive modelling becomes unique and crucial as far as solid waste management is concerned. Bioreactor landfills stabilize waste much faster than conventional landfills, which can take years or even decades. Raw wastes contain high amounts of organics, create an acidic environment that hinders methane-forming microbes. Treating the waste before landfilling is crucial to overcome this issue. Post closure landfill problems pose threat to the surrounding soil causing soil and groundwater pollution as conventional landfills take years and decades to stabilise wastes8. As wastes are pre-treated, the polluting potential is reduced, and maximum yield in terms of gassing and bioenergy is attained9,10. Leachate treatment and disposal costs are minimised as well5,11. Biogas and leachate parameter predictive modeling using machine learning is in great demand. Machine learning–based predictive modeling bridges experimental data and process intelligence, enabling efficient, data-driven management of biogas recovery and leachate treatment in waste-to-energy and landfill systems12–14.

Materials and methods

Two bioreactors made of perspex glass of 150 mm diameter and 1100 mm height were used in this study as shown in Fig. 1. In each reactor, R1(raw) and R2 (treated), a 10 cm gravel layer (10–12 mm) was laid at the base for leachate filtration and drainage, followed by a perforated perspex glass membrane (150 mm dia, 5 mm perforations) to prevent clogging. Approximately 5 kg of raw and treated waste were filled in reactors R1(raw) and R2 (treated) in 10 cm successive layers, compacted to densities of 560 kg/m³ (raw) and 629 kg/m³ (treated), reaching heights of 50.5 cm and 45 cm, respectively. Treated waste of 35 mm size was used and raw waste was also shredded to almost similar size. A perforated perspex glass disc was placed on top of waste matrix in both reactors. A 5 cm thick gravel layer (10–12 mm) was placed in each reactor to ensure uniform leachate distribution. Both reactors were connected to peristaltic pumps (Miclins PP30EX) via 6 mm rubber tubes for leachate recirculation to be done @ 1 L/hr, operated 12 h daily over 300 days maintaining optimum temperature range of around 26–35 °C. There are various studies done by maintaining different recirculation rates so to make it simple, 1 L per hour was adopted considering the size of reactor and to assure that leachate filled in the reactor is completely recirculated in 12 h. Intermittent leachate recirculation of 12 h was adopted as it enhances microbial activity and waste stabilization by maintaining optimal moisture, reducing acid formation and clogging, improving biogas recovery and leachate quality, lowering energy demand, and minimizing long-term environmental risks. A 3-way valve in the upper recirculation loop allowed sample collection. About 20 L synthetic leachate (10 L for each reactor) with anaerobic sludge (10% v/v) was injected in each reactor via bottom port, and reactors were sealed to maintain anaerobic conditions with gas collection bags attached on top port for biogas collection15–17. Although lab scale setups do not completely mimic real situations but this setup proved to be very useful in studying the biodegradation behaviour by strictly maintaining the crucial parameters and variables effecting biodegradation speed and effectiveness.

Schematic diagram for laboratory scale bioreactor (two separate reactors for raw waste R1 and treated waste R2).

Biogas and COD prediction was performed using ORANGE data mining software version 3.37. ORANGE is an open-source platform for data visualization, machine learning, and data mining, offering a visual programming interface for exploratory data analysis, interactive visualization, and predictive modeling. The software is built on Python and integrates widely used open-source scientific computing libraries, with core components implemented using Python, Cython, and C/C++.

ORANGE operates through modular components, known as widgets, which support data preprocessing, visualization, model training, and performance evaluation. Train–test splitting was performed using the Data Sampler widget, in which the dataset was divided into training and testing subsets in a 70:30 ratio. Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²) as other metrics like Root mean square error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) do not always accurately represent the effectiveness of the model and cannot always be relied upon. For biogas and COD prediction, experimental datasets from both raw and treated waste reactors were used along with key operational variables, including pH, volatile fatty acids (VFA), and temperature. As relatively few studies have applied ORANGE data mining to landfill bioreactor systems, this study explores its applicability for the present experimental setup.

This approach yielded promising results, similar to those obtained in this study. Anaerobic sludge (10% v/v) was used as an inoculum in the synthetic leachate to accelerate biodegradation in anaerobic conditions. Analytical methods

pH was measured using a HACH HQ30d digital pH meter with specified probe. Temperature was recorded using a laboratory thermometer. To maintain temperatures within the range of 26 °C to 35 °C during cold weather, a 2000 W double rod heater was utilized22. Electrical Conductivity (EC) was measured by HACH digital EC meter. Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD) analysis followed closed reflux method number 5220 C by analysing blank samples along with leachate samples, as outlined in the “Standard Methods for Examination of Water and Wastewater.” Volatile Fatty Acids (VFA) were analysed using the NUCON 5700 Gas Chromatograph equipped with a Flame Ionization Detector (FID). Ammonia Nitrogen (NH3-N) levels were assessed using the HACH Direct ISE method 10,001, which involved employing the Intellical ISENH3181 ammonia ISE probe and ammonia ISA powder pillows mixed into the samples. Heavy metals were analysed by PerkinElmer PinAAcle 900 F Atomic Absorption Spectrometer. Biogas was collected daily in tedlar bags. The volume of biogas collected was measured by water-displacement method. Methane composition was found out with the help of an electrochemical sensor based portable biogas analyser.

Results and discussion

Biogas production, pH, COD and VFA

The results obtained highlight the vital role of pre-treatment of wastes in enhancing the anaerobic digestion performance of municipal solid waste (MSW). It shows that pre-treatment significantly reduces stabilization time frame, improves biogas generation although quantity is low, promotes better pH stability, accelerates organic matter breakdown and enhances leachate quality, leading to a more efficient and environmentally sustainable waste management process thereby reducing leachate treatment costs. Figure 2 illustrates the cumulative biogas production in Ltrs for both raw and treated wastes. Gas production for treated waste (windrow compost) began shortly after the reactor was started. It took about a week for biogas production to commence, likely due to a slight accumulation of volatile fatty acids (VFAs) from low organics and the time required for methanogenic bacteria to acclimatize5. Following this period, biogas production increased as methanogenic conditions developed, confirmed by a methane content of approximately 66%. A rapid reduction in VFAs to methane and carbon dioxide was observed for treated waste, consistent with biogas production, aligning with the findings of21,23. After about 145 days, the cumulative biogas production trend stabilized. In the case of the raw waste bioreactor, high organic content led to higher VFA production, increasing acidity and impeding biogas production for about 5 weeks5,6. Subsequently, a slow increase in biogas production was observed. Due to the high organic content, a significant amount of biogas was generated, with methane content reaching about 70%, compared to treated waste19,24–26. Cumulative biogas production continued to rise and stabilized after about 260 days. Over 300 days, the cumulative biogas production was found to be about 365 L for raw waste and 68 L for treated waste. Initial pH for raw waste was 5.56 and that for treated waste was 6.22. The treated wastes having very limited amounts of organics generate very low amounts of biogas over a small duration as compared to raw waste however the stabilization in treated wastes is achieved at a faster pace. In the case of raw waste, the pH initially remained highly acidic due to the large amounts of organics and volatile fatty acids (VFAs) accumulation. However, after about 28 days, the pH began to rise and exceeded 6.5, allowing methanogenesis to commence. For treated waste, the pH was slightly acidic at first but rose above 6.5 within a week, initiating methanogenesis. Post-methanogenesis, the pH stabilized between 6.5 and 8 for the remainder of the study. Figure 3 illustrates the pH trend. COD for raw waste peaked at approximately 30 mg/kg. This was followed by a sharp decline over 40–50 days after gassing began, and then a gradual decrease due to biodegradation and the reduction in VFAs, which facilitated methanogenesis. In contrast, treated waste (windrow compost) exhibited much lower COD values, with a maximum of about 5 mg/kg. A similar pattern was observed for VFAs in both treated and raw wastes, as shown in Fig.5 with raw waste values peaking to about 25 mg/kg and that of treated to about 3 mg/kg. The interrelation between pH, VFA, and COD is evident: a reduction in pH is due to higher VFA and COD levels, but as biodegradation progresses, VFA and COD levels drop, causing a rise in pH and enabling methanogenesis as microbes utilize the by-products of the acidogenic phase as substrates21,24,27. Fig. 4 and 5 represent the COD and VFA values during the study period. About 96% and 98% COD removal was observed in raw and treated waste leachate respectively. About 98% and 99% VFA removal was observed in raw and treated waste leachate respectively.

Ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) and electrical conductivity (EC)

Ammonia-Nitrogen (NH3-N) levels were notably higher in raw waste leachate samples compared to those of treated waste. This accumulation of NH3-N persisted throughout the 300-day study, as shown in Fig. 6. The lower NH3-N levels in treated waste leachate are due to the pre-treatment of municipal solid waste, which is converted into windrow compost through microbial degradation.NH3-N accumulation was observed in both waste leachates until the end of the study, attributed to the anaerobic conditions and direct leaching of ammonia into the leachate20,21,28. Electrical conductivity (EC) at the start of bioreactor operation was 11.88 mS/cm in raw waste leachate and 9.86 mS/cm in treated waste leachate. The electrical conductivity as shown in Fig. 7 in raw waste peaked around 18.2 mS/cm at ~ 60 days, then declined to 7.5 mS/cm by day 300. In contrast, treated waste began near 11.2 mS/cm and steadily dropped to 4.7 mS/cm, showing lower ion release throughout.

Heavy metals

Analysis of leachate samples was done for determining the concentrations of Zn, Cu, Ni, Pb, Cr and Cd after filtering the samples through Whatman Grade 1 qualitative filter paper23. Samples were preserved by adding nitric acid (HNO3) till pH went below 2 in order to prevent precipitation of metals and thereafter refrigerated at 4 °C29. Trend of heavy metals can be seen in Figs. 8, 9, 10 and 11. The heavy metal concentrations tended to decrease in both waste leachates after the onset of methanogenesis owing to the highly reducing environment established i.e. low redox potential. Lead and cadmium were found to be low in case of raw waste leachate with values much below 0.001 mg/kg and remained below 0.0005 mg/kg at the end of the study. Zinc in raw waste leachate was high initially with values above 0.014 mg/kg. Nickel, copper and Zinc in treated waste leachate were very low as compared to raw waste leachate. There was a sharp drop in zinc during the first week falling from a maximum value of 0.008 mg/kg and after that it was seen that values remained below 0.002 mg/kg after 50 days and after that eventually it went below 0.001 mg/kg at the end of the study. Raw wastes had higher values19,21,30,31.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the critical role of pre-treatment in enhancing the anaerobic digestion performance of municipal solid waste (MSW), focusing on biogas production, pH regulation, organic matter biodegradation, and leachate quality. Over a 300-day experimental period, raw MSW produced a higher cumulative biogas volume (365 L) compared to treated waste (68 L) due to its greater organic load. However, treated waste (windrow compost) achieved methanogenesis within one week, whereas raw waste required nearly five weeks, reflecting the inhibitory effects of volatile fatty acid (VFA) accumulation and initial acidity in the latter. The pH of treated waste stabilized rapidly above 6.5, enabling early methane formation, while raw waste exhibited delayed recovery due to acidic conditions. COD and VFA levels declined sharply following methanogenesis, confirming substrate utilization, with peak COD reaching ~ 30 mg/kg (raw) and ~ 5 mg/kg (treated). About 96% and 98% COD removal was observed in raw and treated waste leachate respectively. About 98% and 99% VFA removal was observed in raw and treated waste leachate respectively. Electrical conductivity and NH3–N levels were consistently lower in treated waste, indicating reduced ionic strength and nitrogenous pollution. Heavy metals (Zn, Cu, Ni) also decreased substantially after methanogenesis, with lower overall concentrations in treated waste.

Predictive modeling using ORANGE 3.37.0 yielded strong correlations between observed and predicted values (R² = 0.962/0.893 for biogas; 0.989/0.859 for COD in raw/treated systems), validating the potential of machine learning for process forecasting.

The findings underscore that while raw waste has higher biogas potential, it stabilizes slowly and poses greater environmental risks. Pre-treatment accelerates methanogenesis, reduces pollutant loads, and enhances environmental safety, reduces leachate treatment and disposal costs underscoring its importance for sustainable MSW management. However, this lab-scale study using synthetic leachate presents limitations in replicating field-scale heterogeneity, moisture gradients, and microbial diversity. The lab scale system cannot replicate actual site conditions. Future research should focus on pilot-scale validation, life cycle assessment (LCA) of pre-treated systems, microbial consortium characterization, micropollutant analysis and integration of biokinetic and machine learning models for improved long-term forecasting. The key takeaway of this study is that effective pre-treatment is not merely preparatory but it is transformative, enabling faster stabilization, cleaner leachate, and more predictable biogas recovery, thereby advancing the shift towards data-driven, sustainable landfill bioreactor systems.
