Traumatic stress in times of war: a shared responsibility for health professionals
Authors: Hala Kerbage, Eva Alisic, Marit Sijbrandij, Iryna Frankova, Diane Purper-Ouakil, Eric Bui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Armed conflicts continue to inflict profound human suffering, dismantling health systems, displacing populations, and leaving lasting psychological scars. Although war is rooted in political and geopolitical dynamics, its consequences manifest daily in the clinical and ethical dilemmas faced by health professionals.Objective: This editorial calls for a greater ethical and professional commitment from mental health professionals – particularly those in safer contexts – to address the psychological, systemic, and societal impacts of war.Method: Drawing from current evidence in traumatic stress, humanitarian law, and global health, we examine the multilayered consequences of armed conflict, including PTSD, depression, grief, and intergenerational trauma. We reflect on the responsibilities of trauma specialists in times of political violence and mass displacement.Results: The psychological toll of war is amplified by the collapse of support systems, obstruction of care, and direct targeting of healthcare workers. Mental health professionals possess unique expertise not only in treatment, but also in advocacy, education, and testimony. However, trauma care often remains confined to clinical settings, disconnected from policy and public discourse. This editorial emphasises that silence – particularly from those in positions of relative safety – is not neutral, but complicit.Conclusions: There is a professional and ethical imperative for trauma specialists to move beyond the treatment room. This includes amplifying the voices of affected communities, informing policy with trauma-informed evidence, and confronting the systemic injustices that perpetuate violence. Upholding the core values of care, dignity, and justice requires a broader, more engaged response: not only to the aftermath of trauma, but to the structures that produce and sustain it.

Armed conflicts around the world have resulted in devastating human suffering, with profound implications for physical and mental health. These wars have killed or injured hundreds of thousands, displaced millions, and dismantled healthcare infrastructures as seen in Ukraine, Gaza, Sudan, Syria, and the Democratic Republic of Congo, among others (Frankova et al., 2024; Missoni & Sen, 2025; United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs, 2025; World Health Organization, 2024). While geopolitical in origin, the consequences of war manifest starkly in the clinical and psychosocial realities we, as health professionals, encounter daily. As specialists in traumatic stress, we must ask: what is our role – not only in treating the wounds of war, but in responding ethically and collectively to its root causes and enduring consequences?

The psychological toll of war is both immediate and long-lasting (Carpiniello, 2023; Geneva Declaration Secretariat, 2008; Zasiekina et al., 2025). Survivors face elevated rates of PTSD, depression, anxiety, and grief. These conditions are often compounded by the breakdown of support systems and the retraumatization triggered by ongoing violence and displacement (Zasiekina et al., 2025). Children, in particular, are at heightened risk, not only for intergenerational trauma transmission but also for direct psychological and health consequences, including emotional and behavioural difficulties, cognitive and developmental impairments, and long-term physical health vulnerabilities (Dimitry, 2012; Ingabire et al., 2023; Tol et al., 2013; Zasiekina et al., 2025). As practitioners and researchers dedicated to trauma care, we understand that silence in the face of such suffering is not neutrality – it is complicity.

Healthcare systems and workers are increasingly under direct attack in conflict zones, violating international humanitarian law and undermining access to essential care (Chalfie et al., 2024). This weaponization of health systems amplifies trauma and disrupts continuity of care for vulnerable populations. While some professional associations have condemned these violations, institutional responses remain inconsistent. Such silence may reflect not only political pressures but also structural vulnerabilities: threats of funding withdrawal, institutional sanctions, or risks to professional livelihoods can create powerful disincentives to speak out. In times of conflict, healthcare systems and universities may be destabilised not only by direct attacks but also by financial pressures and job insecurity, which weigh heavily on trauma professionals and may inhibit their ability to break the silence. The historic notion of medical neutrality – designed to protect care providers across conflict lines – must be reexamined when the very act of caregiving is targeted.

Critically, this is not a call for those working under threat to assume additional burdens. Rather, it is a call to those of us in relative safety in academic, clinical, or policy-making positions – whether defined as protection from direct violence, financial security, or the safety of ideas and freedom of thought and expression – to assume a proactive role in amplifying the voices of those affected and advocating for health-based responses to war. The ethical imperative to ‘do no harm’ extends beyond individual clinical encounters: it demands that we oppose systemic harm as well.

The field of psychotraumatology has a unique contribution to make in this regard. Our research documents the cascading consequences of traumatic exposure: not only increased psychiatric morbidity but disruptions in attachment, education, and community cohesion (Ingabire et al., 2023). Armed with this evidence, we must contribute to public and policy discussions that challenge the normalisation of war and promote investment in trauma-informed systems of care. Traumatic stress specialists can also offer critical expertise in legal forums, transitional justice processes, and post-conflict reconstruction by testifying to the psychological toll of violence. Simultaneously, we must invest in local capacity-building and support frontline providers through training, supervision, and international collaboration.

Finally, public engagement remains essential. Health professionals must challenge dehumanising narratives that justify violence and marginalise victims. Through education, media engagement, and teaching, we can shift the discourse toward compassion, accountability, and prevention. Our values – care, dignity, and justice – must guide us beyond the clinic and into the broader societal conversation about violence, health, and human rights. The very foundation of our field demands that we listen to suffering, speak truth to its causes, and act in solidarity with those affected. In times of war, our professional integrity is measured not only by how we treat trauma – but by how we stand against its perpetuation.
