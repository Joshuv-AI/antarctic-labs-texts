Structuring of lipid nanoparticle mRNA formulations at acidic and neutral pH: X-ray scattering and molecular dynamics studies
Authors: Amali G Guruge, Yujia He, Andrew J Clulow, Colin W Pouton, David K Chalmers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractmRNA lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) effectively deliver mRNA into cells. However, the structure of these particles remains poorly understood. LNP formulations are manufactured by initially forming nanoparticles at pH 4, to incorporate the RNA, then adjusting the pH 7.4 to trap the RNA within. This work investigates the structural rearrangements that occur during this process. We used the Martini 2 coarse-grained force field to model LNPs with varying water content and placement of PEGylated lipids. New parameters were developed for the lipids Dlin-MC3-DMA, its ionized form and the PEGylated lipid DMG-PEG 2000. At pH 4, the simulations suggest that bilayers are formed at the aqueous interface of the LNPs, with a largely amorphous core, whereas at pH 7.4, the bulk of the LNP is amorphous. Based on our models, we estimate that LNPs contain less than 1% w/w water, with the RNA cargo residing at the interface between water clusters and lipids. RNA entrapment is enhanced when water clusters form inside LNPs. Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) measurements of LNPs at the two modeled pH values are consistent with the MD simulations. This study provides insight into water cluster formation, lipid arrangement, and RNA distribution inside LNPs, advancing our understanding of mRNA-LNP formulations.Keywords: lipid nanoparticles, LNPs, molecular dynamics, mRNA, formulation, small angle X-ray scattering

Introduction

During the development of vaccines against the SARS-CoV2 virus that was undertaken to address the COVID pandemic, several technology platforms were evaluated including inactivated virus, protein subunits with adjuvants, adenovirus vectors, mRNA, and DNA.1 Of these, the mRNA platform proved the most successful approach due to several advantageous characteristics; short development cycle, capacity to stimulate a strong humoral immune response, simple production process leading to rapid industrialization, and flexibility to respond to variants of the virus.2 At this time, the strategy used for formulation of mRNA vaccines, such as the vaccines for COVID-19 by Moderna and BioNTech/Pfizer, is to trap mRNA in lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) which, when delivered by injection, are taken up by cells where the encoded antigen protein is produced, leading to induction of an adaptive immune response.3,4,5 mRNA delivery using LNPs has proven successful for the following reasons. First, mRNA is prone to enzymatic degradation by RNAses present in vivo6 and encapsulation of mRNA in the LNPs provides a carrier capable of delivering mRNA to the cell interior without degradation.7 Second, mRNA molecules have high molecular weight8 (105-106 Da) and are polyanionic, which restricts permeation of mRNA across cell membranes.9 Encapsulation of mRNA in LNPs facilitates the effective uptake of mRNA into the endocytic compartments of the cell interior.8,10 Third, the mRNA encapsulation efficiency by LNPs is remarkably high, which is a critical factor enabling ease of manufacture and effective mRNA delivery.11 Although mRNA technology has been proven effective, broader application would benefit from improved understanding of the internal structure of LNPs, particularly during the manufacturing process. Information gaps include understanding how the mRNA interacts with the LNP lipids, and how these interactions are affected by local changes in the microenvironment such as exposure to physiological fluids, changes in pH, or cellular uptake through the endocytic pathway. In this study, we work toward understanding the structure and interactions within mRNA formulations using computational modeling and experimental methods.

mRNA vaccine LNPs are formulated as a mixture of mRNA, a cationic lipid and three helper lipids,5 a polyethylene glycol lipid (PEGylated lipid), phospholipid and cholesterol. In the first FDA-approved siRNA product, Onpattro,12 the formulation consists of ionizable lipid, cholesterol, phospholipid, and PEGylated lipid in a molar ratio of 50: 38.5: 10: 1.5. The ratio of nitrogen atoms, provided by the ionizable lipid, to phosphate groups present in the polynucleotide in LNP products is typically in the range 5–6.7,13 The process of mRNA encapsulation is carried out at low pH in the presence of ethanol.14 In the first step, an ethanol solution containing ionizable lipid, cholesterol, phospholipid, and PEGylated lipid is mixed rapidly with an aqueous pH 4 buffer solution containing the RNA, usually in a volume ratio of 1:3. The particulate suspension from the first step then undergoes static dialysis or diafiltration to remove ethanol and exchange the buffer salts to produce finished LNPs in pH 7.4 buffer. At physiological pH, the deprotonated (electrically neutral) form of the ionizable lipid results in LNPs with approximately neutral surface charge, which minimizes toxicity after in vivo administration.14,15 During the buffer exchange process, the proportion of protonated ionizable lipid changes from nearly 99% (i.e., positively charged) to predominantly unprotonated. This induces particle fusion and significant structural reorganization within the LNP. This deprotonation of the ionizable lipid is thought to be essential for RNA entrapment, stability and the resulting biological activity of LNPs.16

The multicomponent nature of complex colloids, such as LNPs is difficult to study using experimental methods like small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) or NMR spectroscopy. Accordingly, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have become an important technique for exploring these complex colloidal systems at the atomic level.17,18,19,20,21,22 MD simulates the movements of atoms and molecules by solving Newton’s laws of motion, allowing for dynamic evolution of a system to be captured in detail with respect to the time.23 The coarse-grained (CG) Martini model, which models groups of atoms as a single “bead”, enables modeling of larger systems with faster equilibration,24 making it ideal for studying larger systems like LNPs. Precise structural determination of LNPs is important for many aspects of their preparation, delivery, stability, and therapeutic efficacy. Over the past few years there has been active interest in developing MD models of LNPs,25,26,27,28,29,30,31,32 but important questions remain regarding the structural and dynamic properties of LNPs. Specifically: (1) what is the arrangement of lipids at the core of LNPs and how this structuring is affected by changes in pH?25,29,33 (2) How are the formulated nucleic acids (i.e., RNAs or DNAs) are localized within the LNPs and does the localization changes with pH? And, (3) how is water distributed within the LNPs?29,34 Some proposed models suggest that water is likely to be found inside the LNPs, but how much water is incorporated and how the water affects internal structure is poorly understood.25,35,36,37 With these questions in mind, we aimed to use CG MD simulations to build LNP models at acidic pH (modeling the structure of LNPs during the process of formulation) and at physiological pH to investigate the internal molecular arrangement of lipids, and RNA at acidic and physiological pHs and to quantify and localize water molecules retained within the LNPs.

In this work we have developed CG MD models of LNP formulations to investigate the structure of LNP formulations for mRNA. The majority of the simulations were started from “pre-assembled” LNPs with varying water content inside the LNP and having different distributions of PEGylated lipids (either on the surface or randomly distributed within the LNP). Simulations were performed at both pH 4 and 7.4 to investigate the effect of pH on LNP structure. Additionally, we simulated systems starting from completely random molecular orientations under both pH conditions. Using the models, we evaluate water entrapment and its distribution within the LNP, as well as the entrapment and distribution of RNA. Finally, the models are correlated with SAXS studies carried out at the Australian Synchrotron.

Results

Molecular dynamics simulations of LNPs at physiological and acidic pH

The structural attributes and physical properties of LNPs are likely to significantly affect cellular internalization and the intracellular fate of nucleic acid payloads. We used MD simulations to model the internal molecular structuring of LNPs at acidic and physiological pH including the distribution of RNA and water molecules. We simulated a total of 12 model systems (Table 1). The structures of the lipids used in the MD and X-ray scattering studies are shown in Figure S1. We used previously reported Martini 2 force field parameters for the cholesterol38 (CHOL) and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC)39 components. We carefully derived parameters for CG models of Dlin-MC3-DMA (MC3), the ionized form of DLin-MC3-DMA (IMC3) and DMG-PEG 2000 (DMG-PEG) as described in the Supplemental Material. Each modeled system consisted of 20% w/w of formulation containing MC3, IMC3, CHOL, DSPC, DMG-PEG, with the remaining 80% w/w being water. RNAs were added to each system based on a molar ratio of nitrogen atoms from the ionizable lipid to the phosphate groups in the polynucleotide, maintaining a 5:1 ratio. Given that the ionizable lipid is predominately protonated at acidic pH, we modeled LNPs with 100% IMC3 at pH 4, and with 10% IMC3 at pH 7.4 (the ionized fraction calculated using Henderson-Hasselbalch equation at pH 7.4 is 10%). To investigate the overall organization of the LNPs, we initiated simulations from a variety of pre-built starting structures (Figure 1). We investigated varying water content inside the LNP (0%, 1%, and 10% w/w) and we further varied the location of DMG-PEG molecules by either distributing them randomly throughout the LNP interior or placing them exclusively in the outer layer of the LNP. In addition to the pre-built LNPs, we also commenced simulations from randomly distributed molecules at both pH conditions.

Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) is a useful indicator of the equilibration of colloidal systems, with a steady value indicating an equilibrated system.20,21 Accordingly, we calculated the SASA of the lipidic components (i.e., MC3, IMC3, CHOL, DSPC, and DMG-PEG) over the course of each simulation (Figure S5). The SASA of pre-built structures and those with randomly distributed molecules remained constant for the last ∼1 μs, except for the system at pH 4 (Sim 6), where the SASA continued to decrease gradually. We extended this particular simulation to 2 μs and observed no significant changes in SASA during the final 0.5 μs, suggesting that system had adequately equilibrated. Lipidic organization of LNPs at physiological and acidic pH

We first visually examined the LNPs derived from MD simulations under two different pH conditions. A key result of the simulations is that we observe a difference in the structuring of the lipids at pH 4 and pH 7.4. Figure 3 shows a zoomed-in view of Sim 3 (pH 4) and Sim 9 (pH 7.4), where both simulations started with an LNP containing 1% w/w water and DMG-PEG localized on the surface. At pH 4, the simulation resulted in the formation of a bilayer on the LNP surface, while the core remained an amorphous oily region, lacking regular molecular arrangement. The surface bilayer primarily consisted of CHOL, DSPC, and IMC3 molecules, with positively charged IMC3 head groups and the charged beads of DSPC interacting with water. At pH 4, systems that formed larger water clusters inside the LNPs, such as Sim 4 and Sim 5, showed more distinct bilayer formation around these clusters (Figures S11 and S12 under pH 4). At pH 7.4, a monolayer-like structure of CHOL, DSPC with a small amount IMC3 was observed (Figures S8, S9 and S10). However, this arrangement was disrupted by MC3, leading to a predominately amorphous structure. In systems with higher water content, a bilayer arrangement at the LNP surface, involving CHOL, IMC3, and DSPC was observed; however, this arrangement was disrupted by MC3 (Figure S11 andS12).

We investigated the distribution of the DMG-PEG helper lipid, running simulations of LNPs with the DMG-PEG distributed throughout the starting LNP or localized to the surface. In simulations where the DMG-PEG was randomly distributed (Sims 1, 2, 4), about 30% of the molecules migrated to the surface with polyethylene glycol chains contacting water and hydrophobic tails embedded in the bilayer at the LNP surface. DMG-PEG trapped inside the LNP, polyethylene glycol chains clustered around encapsulated water when water content was relatively high (Sims 2, 4) or near individual water beads when water was limited (Sim 1). Similar behavior was observed at pH 7.4. In starting configurations with DMG-PEG restricted to the surface (Sims 3, 5), nearly all molecules remained at the LNP crust, fully covering the surface and interacting with water. In Sim 5, two DMG-PEG molecules moved into the aqueous phase, indicating that 1.5 mole% DMG-PEG represents an excess that cannot be accommodated at the lipid-water interface (Figure S6). At pH 7.4, DMG-PEG distributed similarly, with ∼35% migrating to the LNP surface from random positions, slightly higher than at pH 4.

Two simulations (Sims 6, 12) were started from random distributions of water and lipids. Due to the nature of the periodic boundary conditions, these simulations formed self-assembled structures that spanned the simulation box (Figure 4). The lipids in the pH 4 system (Sim 6) formed an extended bilayer arrangement whereas the pH 7.4 system (Sim 12) formed a single, large aggregate. In this aggregate, the lipids organized into a monolayer on the surface, composed of CHOL and DSPC. However, this monolayer was not clearly visible in the presence of MC3. The interior lipids displayed an amorphous arrangement similar to that observed with the pre-built LNPs at pH 7.4. At both pH values, the DMG-PEG molecules were distributed across the surface of each structure, although at pH 7.4 some DMG-PEG molecules became entrapped within the structure due to the presence of small water clusters within the structure that made favorable interactions with the polyethylene glycol chains.

Water content and distribution within LNPs

The literature suggests that water becomes trapped inside the LNPs.25,40 Thus, we investigated the water content and its distribution within our pre-built LNP structures. Table 2 shows the water content of each LNP and the amount of water that was expelled from the particles during the MD equilibration process. Figure 5 shows the water distribution within simulated LNPs. We observe that LNPs with initial embedded water beads (with each bead representing four water molecules) expelled much of the initial water. In contrast, the systems without any initial water content absorbed a small amount of the surrounding water. LNPs at pH 4 entrapped slightly more water than those simulated at pH 7.4, though this difference was not significant.

The distribution of water in simulations from prebuilt structures is shown in Figure 5. Simulations 1 and 7, which started with 0% internal water, contain a small amount of water (mostly as isolated beads) near the LNP surface. Simulations 2, 3, 8, and 9 formed separated water clusters arranged in a mesh-like pattern within the oily interior region of the LNPs. When the DMG-PEGs were positioned on the surface (Sims 2, 8), the resulting water clusters were slightly smaller compared those formed when DMG-PEGs were randomly distributed within the LNPs (Sims 3, 9). Increasing the water content to 10% (Sims 4, 5, 10, and 11) resulted in the formation of very large, separate water clusters within the oily region of the LNPs. At pH 7.4, these clusters were larger and more spherical. At pH 4, the water clusters arranged in a hexagonally packed array, resembling an inverted hexagonal phase. When the DMG-PEG molecules were exclusively located on the surface of the LNP, the water clusters were smaller than those formed when they were distributed throughout the LNP. At pH 4, water clusters were predominantly surrounded by the polyethylene glycol chains of the DMG-PEG (observed only in Sim 2 and Sim 5) and the head groups of the IMC3 and DSPC molecules. At pH 7.4, water clusters were predominantly surrounded by polyethylene glycol chains (observed only in Sim 9 and Sim 11), the head groups of the IMC3 and the polar head groups of DSPC molecules.

RNA distribution within LNPs

Following the simulations, the RNA molecules were primarily located inside the LNPs often found at the interface between water clusters and lipids. Some RNA molecules migrated to the surface, as illustrated in Figure S13. Notably, none were observed inside the water-clusters formed within in the LNP structure. However, when the simulation started from randomly distributed molecules at pH 7.4, some RNAs were found in water. IMC3 and DSPC molecules were located near the RNA molecules suggesting interactions with charged groups in these lipid molecules. However, not all IMC3 or DSPC molecules interacted with RNAs. Radial distribution functions (RDFs) between the negatively charged phosphates of RNA backbone and the head groups of IMC3 and DSPC, (Figure S14) confirm this observation. The number of RNAs located on the outer surface and inside the LNPs is reported in Table S2. The encapsulation percentage (the number of RNAs that fully trapped inside the LNPs relative to the total RNAs present in the system) ranged from 67% to 70% for systems simulated at pH 4 and 62%–77% at pH 7.4. For the prebuilt structures, the number of RNAs encapsulated within LNPs was higher than the number of found on the surface, regardless of the pH condition or the starting configuration. When started from a random molecular distribution at pH 4 (sim 6), all RNAs were found on the surface, in contact with water, and no RNA molecules were trapped inside the LNP (note that the simulation formed a larger surface with a bilayer arrangement). At pH 7.4 (Sim 12), while many RNAs remained on the surface, a few were trapped inside the structure in contact with two small water clusters formed within the LNP morphology. Additionally, some RNAs left the lipidic surface and were found freely suspended in the aqueous medium. This behavior was not observed in at pH 4.

SAXS experiments

SAXS was used to investigate the structure of LNPs formed after dialysis and buffer exchange. Three formulations containing different charged lipids were made: (A) containing the permanently charged lipid, DOTAP, (B) containing the ionizable lipid DODAP, a structural analogue of DOTAP, and (C) containing DLin-MC3-DMA (used in the MD simulations described previously). The pH 4 particles were prepared using dialysis to remove the ethanol content, which was 25% v/v ethanol immediately after mixing. Figure 6 shows scattering profiles (intensity versus q) for the three formulations. The profiles in panel B and C show features that are common for LNPs containing ionizable lipids. A diffraction peak that is evident at high q in the pH 4 samples is lost when the pH is raised to 7.4. The peak present at pH 4 is broader than would be present in multilamellar liposomes but has its maximum at a q value that is consistent with the distance between lipid bilayers (5–6 nm), suggesting that a repeating bilayer-like structure exists at pH 4 albeit comparatively diffuse, suggesting it is less uniform than a liposome suspension. The loss of this peak at physiological pH can be explained by deprotonation of the ionizable lipid causing a structural reorganization into a substantially amorphous structure, presumably within the bulk core of the LNP. The structural change observed for the ionizable lipids was not observed for LNPs made using the permanently cationic lipid, DOTAP (Figure 6A), where the peak at q values corresponding to bilayer distance are maintained at pH 7.4. Our SAXS data is consistent with published data.36

Discussion

The structure of LNPs is critical to their role in delivering therapeutic RNA to targeted sites. Consequently, several studies have investigated LNP morphology25,29,34,37,41 and proposed a range of possible LNP structures including multilamellar vesicles and LNPs with nanostructured cores.40 In our study, we have used SAXS experiments and MD simulations to investigate the arrangement of lipids within LNPs under acidic (pH 4) and physiological conditions (pH 7.4). A key focus was to examine water entrapment and the distribution of RNA within the nanoparticles to better understand the role that water might play in maintaining RNA entrapment and the environment in which RNA is held. MD simulations were performed using coarse grain Martini models, starting from “preformed”, approximate LNPs. Lipid ionization states were selected to model the LNP state at pHs 4 and 7.4 (100% versus 10% ionization of MC3 at pH 4 and 7.4), representing the initial and final stages in the manufacturing process. The water content in the preformed LNPs was varied, starting with 0%, 1%, and 10% w/w water entrapment, representing a range from no water to high water content inside the LNPs. This variation was explored due to the lack of experimental data in the literature regarding the amount of water entrapped within LNPs. DMG-PEG molecules were positioned either at the lipid-bulk water interface or randomly distributed within the LNPs to study their migration and stability within the structure. Short RNA molecules were incorporated into the LNPs.

The MD observations in the current study align well with the findings of the 2012 study by Leung et al.25 Similarities between the two studies include the lipid arrangement inside the LNPs, the presence of PEGylated lipid coating, the presence of irregular water clusters containing siRNAs separated by a bilayer membrane, and the formation of structures resembling inverted hexagonal phase at high water content. Although Leung et al. used Dlin-KC2-DMA and PEG-c-DMA as the ionizable and PEGylated lipids respectively, their observations are consistent with or MD results at pH 4. Leung et al.’s model was also employed in another study using the Martini forcefield,33 which similarly reported the formation of water cavities inside the LNP separated by bilayers, the arrangement of polar head groups of ionizable lipids around the water cavities, the absence of RNAs inside water cavities, locating RNAs at internal or external lipid-water interfaces, and the surrounding of RNAs by ionizable lipids, all of which are consistent with our findings. During bilayer formation at pH 4, the ionized formed of MC3s align well with the DSPC, facilitating a well-ordered bilayered arrangement (see the rectangular area in Figure S15 (a)). However, at pH 7.4, MC3s do not exhibit the same alignment (see the rectangle area in Figure S15B), hindering the formation of a well-ordered monolayer/bilayer. The bead representing the tertiary amine group in MC3 tends to move away from the charged N bead of DSPC, indicating that MC3 can reside away from DSPC under these conditions (not all beads move away from charged N bead of DSPC). Ramezanpour et al. demonstrated that the neutral form of DLin-KC2-DMA segregates from POPC, while the charged form mixes with POPC in the lipid bilayer,42 a phenomenon similar to our observations, which may explain the promotion of bilayer arrangement at pH 4. Beyond MD studies, the oily core we observed in our simulations, characterized by CHOL and excess IMC3s, has been corroborated by Kulkarni et al. through cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) and X-ray diffraction experiments.37 Trollmann et al. also reported that LNPs at physiological pH exhibit an oil-like core surrounded by a lipid monolayer composed of DSPC, ionizable lipids (ALC-0315), PEGylated lipids (ALC-0159), and cholesterol.29 In our models, the monolayer contained DSPC, cholesterol, and a small amount of IMC3 with PEGylated layer forming the outermost layer of LNP. However, this monolayer was disrupted by the nonionized form of the ionizable lipid. Interestingly, Dehghani-Ghahnaviyeh et al. demonstrated that an increase in the nonionized proportion of ionizable lipid (e.g., systems with 40% and 50% ionizable lipid, containing equal parts charged and neutral form of the ionizable lipid) in a lipid bilayer, induces a transition from a membrane-like morphology to a disordered aggregate state in the presence of cholesterol.43 This explains the formation of amorphous LNPs observed in our MD simulations at pH 7.4, where our LNPs contained 45% ionizable lipids, with 10% in the ionized form and 90% in the neutral form.

Consistent observations with the literature and SAXS data from our study suggest that our models are reliable for predicting the atomic-level information about LNP systems and gain insights into the roles of molecular components in overall structure formation. The computational microscope provided by MD revealed that CHOL molecules contribute to bilayer/monolayer formation in LNPs. Excess CHOL is homogeneously distributed in the LNP core, contributing to the formation of an amorphous region. This indicates that CHOL likely partitions into lamellar and inverted hexagonal phases (when LNP entrapped high water content) at acidic pH.44 At pH 7.4, however, CHOL partitions into lipid monolayer and amorphous phases. Similarly, the phospholipid, DSPC contributes to bilayer or monolayer formation and is heterogeneously distributed within the core, with higher density in regions containing water or water compartments. While some studies45,46 have suggested that DSPC is primarily localized on the surface of mRNA containing LNPs, our results indicate otherwise. A recent study stated the possibility of DSPC being present within the interior of the LNP, where it may rearrange to form water-containing micelles.47 This observation aligns with our MD results. The literature also suggests that PEGylated lipids are primarily restricted to the LNP surface, forming an oil-water interface where ethylene oxide chains interact with water while the hydrophobic tails are buried inside lipidic region of the LNP. This arrangement is believed to prevent LNP aggregation.48 However, in our simulations, we observed that some PEGylated lipids are trapped inside the LNP, interacting with entrapped water at both pH conditions, in addition to being found at the LNP surface. While one could argue that longer MD simulations might eventually move these entrapped PEGylated lipids to the surface of LNP. However, simulations started with randomly distributed molecules yielded self-assembled structures where PEGylated lipids were located at the surface of internal water aggregates. This suggests a thermodynamically favorable arrangement, though solid experimental evidence supporting this observation remains lacking, as most studies indicate otherwise.48,49 Ionizable lipids, MC3s were mainly distributed homogeneously in LNP at pH 7.4. The ionized form of these lipids, IMC3, contributed to bilayer formation at pH 4 and monolayer formation at pH 7.4. Excess IMC3s at pH 4 were homogeneously distributed within the LNP. A similar behavior was observed by Park et al. in models using Dlin-KC2-DMA and DLin-MC3-DMA.50

Water entrapment within LNPs is challenging to determine experimentally, but MD simulations provide insight into water distribution within the LNP. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study investigating water entrapment in LNPs. Our models indicate that LNPs inherently retain small amounts of water. For example, even LNPs simulated without any initial water, eventually entrapped a small amount of water under both pH conditions. For LNPs loaded with 1% and 10% w/w water initially, the retained water at the equilibrium averaged ∼0.7% and ∼5% (of the total water content) respectively. These results suggest that water is not entirely expelled from the LNPs, and equilibrated water content is likely to be less than 1% most likely between 0.02 and 0.5%. The appearance with water clusters as an inverted hexagonal phase at the LNP core is consistent with prior MD25 and experimental studies, where mixtures composed of cationic lipids and anionic phospholipids enhance such phase formation in the presence of helper lipids such as cholesterol.51 Leung et al., reported that water trapped inside the LNP corresponds to approximately 6 water molecules per lipid.25 In our models, LNPs with high initial water content, the LNP contained 8 water molecules per lipid (Sim 4) and 5 water molecules per lipid (Sim 5) at acidic pH. At pH 7.4, the corresponding values were 6 water molecules per lipid (Sim 10) and 5 water molecules per lipid (Sim 11). These findings align closely with Leung et al.’s data. Simulations from randomly distributed molecules (Sim 6, 12) revealed water cluster formation inside the LNP facilitated RNA entrapment. This observation suggests that models incorporating water compartments inside the structure are rational and realistic representation of LNP morphology.

Understanding RNA entrapment is critical for optimizing LNP formulations. Our microscopical models show RNA encapsulation efficiency of ∼60%–80%, comparable to experimental efficiencies of ∼90%, despite using a much smaller RNA sequence (14 nucleotides) than the Pfizer BioNTech (BNT162b2) vaccine (4284 nucleotides, ∼1388 kDa52). Similar encapsulation efficiencies (∼50%) were observed in a study by Buyens et al. with short interfering RNA (siRNA).53 RNA localization within LNPs, such as on water cluster surfaces25 or the outer surface of LNP morphology, has been reported previously33,53 and aligns with our findings. RNA encapsulation typically occurs at acidic pH, where positively charged ionizable lipids interact electrostatically with negatively charged RNAs.16 Our MD data revealed interactions between RNAs and both IMC3s and DSPC, consistent with previous investigations reported in the literature.7,25,26,29 However, unlike Trollmann et al.29 who reported weaker interactions between RNAs and DSPCs compared to RNAs and ionizable lipids, our study observed strong interactions in both cases. This discrepancy is likely due to the higher concentration of RNAs and DSPC in our models, which increased the interactions and resulted in a high intensity peak for RNAs-DSPC interactions. Although most ionizable lipids are deprotonated at pH 7.4, reducing their affinity for RNAs, our simulations showed that RNAs still adhere to the LNP surface due to DPSC and small amount of IMC3 present in the systems. Figure S14 clearly shows a higher intensity in the first peaks of both the RNA-IMC3 and RNA-DSPC RDFs (Figure S14, Sim7, Sim8, and Sim9), indicating a greater RNA density around the positively charged beads of DSPC and MC3 at pH 7.4 compared to the same interactions at pH 4. The migration of RNA to the LNP surface during simulation of preformed LNPs may explain why Ribogreen assays typically report 85%–90% encapsulation. Surface exposed RNA, though associated with the particle, remains accessible for Ribogreen binding, leading to the conclusion that not all RNA is entrapped. At pH 7.4, it is interesting to note that if RNA is trapped in the core of the preformed LNPs during manufacture at pH 4, it will remain trapped kinetically at pH 7.4 (Sim 7–11), but when the simulations were started from random distributions, the more favorable dispersion of RNA in the aqueous phase was dominant. These findings support the Martini-RNA model for studying RNA interactions with biomolecules, though further refinement of this model may be needed.44 It should be noted that although our studies aimed to improve our understanding of the molecular interactions that occur during formation of LNPs, we did not include ethanol in the current simulations. Ethanol may have an important effect on the kinetics of entrapment of RNA during the initial formation of LNPs.

The SAXS data correlates with MD results in that bilayer-like features were observed in the core of simulated MC3 and particles at pH 4 but not at pH 7.4. The SAXS profile of MC3 and DODAP particles at pH 4 included a broad peak at q value close to 0.1 Å−1, indicating structures with repeating distance consistent with the thickness of lipid bilayers, though SAXS profiles lacked the sharp peaks that would be expected for multilamellar liposomes. This suggests bilayer-like structures at pH 4 but with more irregular morphology. At pH 7.4, the peak at q value close to 0.1 Å−1 was absent in MC3 and DODAP particles, suggesting the formation of LNPs with amorphous structure in the core after buffer exchange to physiological pH. In contrast, the SAXS profiles of LNPs produced with the quaternary cationic lipid, DOTAP, suggested that the bilayer-like structures were present at pH 4 and pH 7.4.

Conclusion

In this work, we aimed to investigate the structure of LNP mRNA formulations through MD simulations and SAXS experiments. Coarse grain MD simulations using the Martini 2 force field were used to explore the distribution of water and RNA inside LNPs and the internal molecular arrangement of lipids. LNP systems were modeled at pH 4 and 7.4. At pH 4, the simulations indicate the formation of bilayers at the aqueous interface of the LNPs, with a largely amorphous core, whereas, at pH 7.4, the morphology is entirely amorphous. Based on these models, we estimate that LNPs contain less than 1% w/w water, with the RNAs residing at the interface between water (i.e., water clusters inside the LNP or bulk water) and lipids. We find that RNA entrapment is enhanced when water clusters form inside LNPs. SAXS experiments were also conducted on LNPs at the two pH values using the same lipids. Conducting SAXS experiments alongside with MD simulations facilitates the validation of computational models and the SAXS measurements were consistent with the MD simulation results. This work provides, for the first time, an understanding of LNP morphology at acidic and physiological pH, with a focus on RNA and water distribution. We believe that these insights into LNP morphology, including the arrangement of all lipids in the formulation, as well as RNA and water, will aid in designing more efficient LNP formulations for mRNA delivery. Additionally, we believe the developed Martini CG model can be effectively employed in future investigations aimed at further advancing LNP formulation development.

Methods

Molecular dynamics simulations

The Martini topologies for DSPC, and cholesterol were obtained from the http://cgmartini.nl/website. For the RNA molecule, the structure with PDB ID 6YYT54 was extracted from the Protein DataBank. Among the four RNA segments in the structure, chain U, consisting of 14 nucleic bases was selected for this study. Using this edited structure, Martini parameter file for the RNA molecule was generated using the martinize-nucleotide.py44 script. For MC3, IMC3, and PEG2000, topologies were developed using Martini 2 following the conventional parameterization procedure provided on the Martini website. Detailed information on the parameterization of these molecules is available in the supplementary information (SI).

The LNP formulations were modeled considering DLin-MC3-DMA (MC3), cholesterol, DSPC, and DMG-PEG at a molar ration of 50: 38.5: 10: 1.5. The chemical structures of these components are shown in Figure S1. Number of RNAs in a system was determined by considering the ratio of DLin-MC3-DMA (N present in the molecule) to RNA base (P present in the base) as 5:1. Our systems contained 20% w/w lipid-based formulation in 80% w/w water. The water content inside the LNPs was changed as 0%, 1%, and 10% from total water content as shown in the Figure 1. At acidic and physiological pH, systems were constructed using two approaches.

(1)Randomly placing LNP molecules (i.e., lipids, RNAs, and any entrapped water molecules, see Figure 1) in a 25 nm radius of a sphere (to mimic the LNP). The remaining water molecules in each system were distributed in the simulation box avoiding the spherical volume.

Randomly placing LNP molecules (i.e., lipids, RNAs, and any entrapped water molecules, see Figure 1) in a 25 nm radius of a sphere (to mimic the LNP). The remaining water molecules in each system were distributed in the simulation box avoiding the spherical volume.

(2)Randomly distributing molecules throughout the simulation box.

Randomly distributing molecules throughout the simulation box.

The ionizable lipid DLin-MC3-DMA has a pKa value of 6.44.55 Therefore, to model the acidic conditions (pH 4), 100% of the lipid was modeled as ionized. At pH 7.5 we modeled 10% as being ionized (at pH 7.4, the protonated fraction was calculated using Henderson-Hasselbalch equation to be 11%). The location of DMG-PEG lipid within the LNP was considered as inside and on the surface as illustrated in the Figure 1. To neutralize the total charge of each system in Table 1, Na+ ions and Cl− ions were added within the simulation cubic box of 70 nm (which shrank to a ∼50 nm on reaching equilibrium).

Simulations used the Martini 2 force field24 and GROMACS-2018.856,57. All simulations utilized a 20 fs time step in NPT ensemble. After energy minimization, production simulations were run at 310 K using the velocity rescale algorithm58 with a coupling constant of 1.0 ps. The pressure was controlled isotopically using Parrinello-Rahman algorithm59 with a coupling constant of 12.0 ps at a reference pressure of 1 bar and a compressibility of 3 × 10−4 bar−1. Electrostatics were treated with the reaction-field algorithm60 with a cutoff of 1.2 nm and a relative dielectric constant of 15, while van der Waals forces were computed with the same cutoff. Each MD simulation was conducted out for 1.5 μs, except for Sim 6, which was run for 2 μs.

The final frames of the MD simulations were visually inspected using VMD61 and the images in the current study were generated using the same software. Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) and radial distribution functions were calculated using gmx_sasa and gmx_rdf tools in GROMACS. For RDF calculations, the last 10 ns of the simulation was used.

To extract RNAs inside and on the surface of LNPs, PyMol62 software was used. To extract water beads inside the LNPs, we selected the water beads within the appropriate radius from the center of mass of the each LNP. For this purpose, we used PyMol with the python script center_of_mass.py. However, if selected water beads from the above process included water beads on the surface of LNP, we removed those water beads manually and extracted water beads resides only in the inside of LNP.

SAXS

LNPs produced for SAXS studies were prepared with capped (Cap 1) modified mRNA (produced with N1-methyl-pseudoUTP) encoding a membrane-anchored receptor-binding domain of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, as described previously.63 Briefly, an aqueous solution of mRNA at pH 4 was mixed with a solution of the four lipids in ethanol, using a microfluidics mixing device (NxGen Ignite Nanoassemblr) supplied by precision Nanosystems. The suspension of nanoparticles was adjusted to pH 7.4 using a 1:3 dilution in Tris buffer, followed by dialysis against 25 mM Tris buffer to remove the ethanol. The LNP suspension was adjusted with sucrose solution to produce the cryoprotected, isotonic final form of the product. The product was sterile filtered (0.22 μm) prior to being aliquoted into sterile vials for storage at −80°C. Particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) were determined by dynamic light scattering, a standard method for submicron dispersions, using a Zetasizer (Malvern Instruments). Typically, encapsulation efficiency was 85%–95%, particle size (Z-average) of the 1.5% PEGylated lipid formulation was 80–100 nm with PDI <0.2.

SAXS measurements were acquired on the SAXS/WAXS beamline64 at the Australian Synchrotron, part of ANSTO. Experiments were performed at the ambient temperature of the experimental hutch, which is typically around 27°C. Samples and associated buffers for background subtraction (100 μL each) were loaded into 96-well PCR plates for sampling. An autosampler aspirated one sample or buffer background at a time into a 1.5 mm diameter capillary held in the X-ray beam (photon energy = 11.5 keV, wavelength λ = 1.078 Å) before returning the sample to the well from which it was drawn. Prior to each sample measurement the appropriate buffer background was measured for background subtraction and the capillary was automatically washed with Hellmanex detergent (2 vol% aqueous solution) and water before blowing dry with a stream of nitrogen after each sample measurement. Multiple 1 s acquisitions were collected as the samples were drawn into and expelled from the capillary. Images of the scattering pattern in 2D were recorded on a Pilatus 2M detector (Dectris, Switzerland) and reduced to plots of scattered X-ray intensity [I(q)] versus the magnitude of the scattering vector q [=(4π/λ)sinθ, where 2θ is the scattering angle] using software developed at the Australian Synchrotron, Scatterbrain. The sample-to-detector distance was set to ∼2790 mm and this afforded an observable q-range of 0.005–0.497 Å−1. Data were placed on an absolute scale (cm−1) using scattering from water in the capillary as the standard before the scattering profile for each buffer background was subtracted from that for the corresponding sample.
