Medical student’s perceptions of modern educational technologies in anatomy at VinUniversity, Vietnam: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Quang Thanh Nguyen, Thuy Minh Ha, Duy Mai Hoang, Kien Hieu Tran, Dung Phuong Pham, Ngoc Luong Khanh Nguyen, Linh Cu Le
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundModern digital tools are increasingly used in anatomy education, yet evidence from low- and middle-income settings on how students perceive the usefulness of these resources remains limited. Guided by the Technology Acceptance Model, this study explored medical students’ perceptions of the usefulness of high investment, technology enhanced tools versus standard anatomy teaching tools and examined how socioeconomic and educational backgrounds relate to these perceptions.MethodsA cross-sectional mixed methods survey was conducted among three cohorts of medical students who had completed a two-semester anatomy course at VinUniversity, Hanoi, Vietnam. A self-designed questionnaire asked students to rate the perceived usefulness of four high investment tools (3D anatomy application, plastinated models, virtual dissection table, advanced plastic models) and four standard tools (atlases and textbooks, Canvas platform, laboratory printouts, online resources) on a 5-point Likert scale. Demographic and academic variables included cohort, gender, hometown, high school type, grade point average, family income, and intended surgical versus non-surgical career. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests with p < 0.05. Open ended responses were analyzed through qualitative descriptive thematic analysis.ResultsOut of 144 invited students, 131 responded (91% response rate). The analysis revealed that high investment educational tools, particularly the 3D Anatomy App (4.39 ± 0.84) and plastinated models (3.91 ± 1.03), received high satisfaction ratings. Urban students and those from prestigious high schools rated these tools significantly higher (p = 0.045). The virtual dissection table had mixed reviews (2.92 ± 1.15), with better reception in later cohorts (p = 0.058). Family income influenced perceptions, with higher income groups rating the 3D Anatomy App (p = 0.006) and the virtual dissection table (p = 0.039) more favorably. Qualitative feedback highlighted the need for interactive tools, practical resources, clinical integration, better labeling, and language support.ConclusionsMedical students perceived selected modern anatomy tools, particularly the 3D anatomy application and plastinated models, as especially useful in supporting their learning, but these perceptions varied systematically by socioeconomic background and career intentions. The findings support targeted investment in high value tools, coupled with faculty development, better labeling and localization of resources, and more explicit clinical integration, in order to promote both educational quality and equity. Longitudinal and multi-institutional studies, together with cost effectiveness analyses, are needed to determine how these implementation strategies influence long term learning outcomes and guide future resource allocation in similar settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09064-3.

Background

Anatomy is a fundamental preclinical subject in medical education, forming the basis for future medical practice. Traditionally, it has been taught through lectures with slideshow presentations and auditory explanations, supplemented by dissections, clinical case studies, and self-directed learning using two-dimensional images and multimedia resources [1]. However, this traditional approach is inadequate for preparing students for the rapid expansion of medical knowledge anticipated in the 21st century [1].

Modern anatomy education increasingly incorporates a blend of modern or technology enhanced and traditional tools, each with distinct strengths and limitations. Modern resources such as 3D applications, high-end plastic models, plastinated cadavers and virtual dissection tables offer high-resolution visualization, interactivity, and the ability to simulate anatomical structures in three dimensions and lower students’ cognitive load [2]. These tools can address limitations in cadaver availability, allow repeated self-paced practice, and integrate with clinical case-based learning [3]. However, they often require substantial initial investment, ongoing subscription fees, and faculty training for effective use. In contrast, traditional resources remain widely used due to their affordability, accessibility, and familiarity. While these methods lack the interactive capabilities of newer technologies, they provide consistent reference material and are less dependent on institutional infrastructure [4]. Hybrid approaches that combine both categories have shown promise, allowing students to benefit from the depth of traditional learning and the engagement of modern digital tools [5]. Previous studies suggest that integrating multiple modalities can enhance spatial understanding, retention, and clinical application of anatomical knowledge, but the optimal balance depends on local resources, faculty expertise, and student learning preferences [5].

Additionally, there is a considerable disparity in resources for medical education between developed and developing countries, especially in anatomy teaching. Many developed countries have greater access to advanced technologies, well-equipped laboratories, and abundant cadaveric specimens, which are essential for thorough anatomy education. In contrast, low- to middle-income countries (LMICs) face severe shortages in these critical resources, impacting the quality of medical training [6, 7]. The socioeconomic background of anatomy students significantly impacts their access to and effective utilization of modern teaching tools [8, 9]. The reliance on paid subscriptions for high-quality commercial educational resources, including question banks and review videos, can also exacerbate existing inequalities, disproportionately affecting students from disadvantaged backgrounds [8, 9]. Indeed, cost is a primary factor influencing students’ choices when selecting computer-assisted learning resources in anatomy [10]. Addressing these socioeconomic disparities is therefore crucial to ensure equitable access and optimal learning outcomes in anatomy education.

Medical education in Vietnam and anatomy education at VinUniversity

Several studies have highlighted the need for healthcare system strengthening in LMICs to meet current and future demands, with medical education reform as a key component given persistent challenge in developing and distributing the physician workforce [11, 12]. In recent years, Vietnam has pursued medical education reform to better respond to societal needs, supported by broad policymaker consensus on modernization [11].

Medical training in Vietnam typically spans six years, with two to three preclinical years followed by three to four years of clinical training and hospital rotations [12]. Anatomy is usually taught in Year 1 or 2 using lectures, plastic models, and cadaveric dissection, but cadaver access remains severely limited. For example, Hanoi Medical University reportedly has only two to four cadavers annually for approximately 800 anatomy students [13], reinforcing the need for modernization and investment in anatomy teaching resources.

Founded in 2020, VinUniversity offers a six-year medical doctor program; as a private, not-for-profit institution emphasizing educational quality, intake is approximately 50 to 60 students per year, with anatomy taught in the second year [13]. To align more closely with international standards, VinUniversity has formed a strategic alliance with the University of Pennsylvania (UPenn), Philadelphia, United States. The medical curriculum has been co-designed with partners from UPenn to ensure that graduates acquire the knowledge, skills, and attitudes needed for effective practice and lifelong learning in both domestic and international settings, and this collaboration covers strategic planning, program structures, curriculum and lecture design, as well as assessment across undergraduate and postgraduate programs, including the Anatomy course [14].

Following a comprehensive review of preclinical science education at UPenn, VinUniversity invested in an advanced gross anatomy laboratory and a suite of modern teaching tools (Fig. 1). Medical students in Vietnam were, for the first time, given access to sophisticated infrastructure that includes two plastinated whole body models and several plastinated body parts from 1Von Hagens Gubener Plastinate GmbH (Fig. 2), more than 300 advanced plastic anatomical model sets from 3B Scientific, SOMSO, and Denoyer Geppert Science (Fig. 3), a virtual dissection table from Razvitiye LLC to replace conventional cadaveric dissection (Fig. 4), and subscriptions to the Complete Anatomy three dimensional simulation application by 3D4Medical from Elsevier (Fig. 5). This is complemented by the robust use of Canvas as a learning management system, which centralizes lecture notes, readings, multimedia resources, and lab schedules, ensuring that students can readily access course materials and manage their study time efficiently.

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) offers a structured framework for understanding students’ uptake of educational technologies by linking perceived usefulness to attitudes and intention to use [15, 16]. Using TAM, this study addresses an evidence gap by examining Vietnamese medical students’ perceptions of the usefulness of advanced anatomy resources. We applied this framework to compare students’ evaluations of specific modern tools, including the 3D anatomy simulation app, plastinated models, advanced plastic models, and the virtual dissection table, alongside standard resources such as textbooks, materials on the university’s Canvas platform, laboratory printout materials, and open access resources. We also examined whether evaluations varied by students’ socioeconomic and educational backgrounds, given the potential for differential access conditions and prior exposure to shape how these tools support anatomy learning in a LMIC’s setting.2

METHOD

Research design

We conducted a cross-sectional survey study with an embedded qualitative component to address the following research questions from the students’ perspective: (1) How do medical students perceive the usefulness and implementation fit of high investment, technology enhanced anatomy learning tools compared with standard resources in an LMIC setting? (2) Do these perceptions differ by cohort and learner background characteristics, including socioeconomic and educational background?

Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) was used as a structured lens to organize both instrument development and interpretation of findings. Specifically, questionnaire items were designed to capture students’ judgments of each tool in terms of perceived usefulness (the extent to which the tool was experienced as supporting anatomy learning and clinically oriented understanding) and perceived ease of use (the extent to which students experienced the tool as accessible, learnable, and feasible within time and workflow constraints). In analyses, TAM informed how we interpreted differences between tools, particularly when high perceived usefulness was accompanied by lower perceived ease of use, suggesting implementation barriers rather than lack of educational value.

The quantitative component included demographic items (e.g., age, gender, permanent residence, family income, high school background, and academic performance) and questions assessing students’ perceptions of anatomy learning resources. Each tool was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not useful at all, 5 = extremely useful) to measure its perceived usefulness in achieving learning outcomes. Permanent residence was self-reported and then dichotomized for analysis. We classified responses as urban if the residence was in a municipal or provincial city, and as rural if it was in a district or commune. Family income was self-reported in Vietnamese Dong (VND) by selecting one of the predefined survey brackets. For reader interpretability, we subsequently expressed these same brackets as approximate U.S. dollar (USD) equivalents using the average exchange rate during the data collection period. These income brackets reflect the questionnaire’s response categories and were used for descriptive reporting and subgroup analyses in this study; they were not based on, and should not be interpreted as, any official Vietnamese Government classification of low, middle, or high income. At the time of study design, we were not able to identify an official national classification scheme that mapped family income into categories directly comparable to the survey brackets used in this study.

The qualitative component consisted of open-ended questions that invited students to describe perceived strengths and limitations of the current resources, barriers to access or use, and practical suggestions for improving implementation. These responses were analyzed as perceptions and contextual feedback to inform resource optimization and equitable implementation and were not interpreted as evidence of learning effectiveness or superiority of any method, which would require experimental or quasi-experimental evaluation. This design enabled the survey to capture both comparable quantitative ratings and complementary narrative data that clarified how students experienced modern and traditional tools in routine learning.

To support the validity of inferences drawn from the questionnaire, we collected several forms of validity evidence. First, we sought evidence of content validity through structured expert review. The panel comprised three anatomy educators (4 to more than 20 years of teaching experience and at least one year of experience in using the modern anatomy tools; teaching knowledge coverage across multiple organ systems within the undergraduate anatomy curriculum; academic background: MD with postgraduate training in anatomy) and three medical education experts (at least 3 years of experience in survey design and medical educational evaluation). The panel reviewed each item for alignment with the study aims and TAM constructs, clarity and interpretability, and adequacy of domain coverage across the included learning resources. Panel members also verified that the descriptions of each tool reflected actual local implementation and that the items were appropriate for students with direct exposure to these resources. Second, we examined response process validity through pilot testing. Ten randomly selected students completed the survey and provided structured feedback on comprehension, interpretation of response options, survey length, and feasibility of completion. We revised wording to reduce ambiguity, adjusted item ordering to improve flow, and refined response options where needed based on student feedback. Finally, we assessed internal consistency reliability for multi-item scales intended to measure the same construct using Cronbach’s alpha.

For analytical purposes, the resources were grouped into two categories: modern tools, characterized by higher initial investment costs and perceived novelty, and standard tools, representing more conventional, widely used resources. This classification reflects both financial considerations and the perceptions of students and faculty. Although the Canvas platform requires a high annual subscription fee, it is used across multiple disciplines and thus was categorized as a standard tool. Additionally, since some other private institutions also use this platform, it is not considered modern by our students. All educational tools were mandatory components of the anatomy curriculum. Students had two academic semesters of exposure to each tool before participating in the survey.

Study participants

All 144 medical students enrolled in the Anatomy course at the College of Health Sciences, VinUniversity, were recruited for this study. Since the medical program at VinUniversity was established in 2020 and Anatomy is offered in the second year, three cohorts qualified for the survey: Cohort 1 (class of 2020–2026), Cohort 2 (class of 2021–2027), and Cohort 3 (class of 2022–2028).

Data collection

Data collection was conducted in June 2024 using a specially designed questionnaire developed specifically for this study (see Supplementary Material: Appendix 1 – Survey Questionnaire). The questionnaire was administered via an electronic survey on Microsoft Forms3 and the link was disseminated electronically by a research assistant to all three cohorts of students. For the qualitative component, two researchers (both with formal training and applied experience in qualitative methods and clinical education evaluation) analyzed open ended responses using a qualitative descriptive approach. Responses were independently coded with descriptive labels, grouped into subcategories, and organized into themes. The researchers then compared coding, discussed discrepancies, and refined the coding framework through consensus to support consistency and transparency. Where disagreements could not be resolved through discussion, a third researcher reviewed the excerpts and provided adjudication. Throughout analysis, the team held analytic meetings to challenge interpretations, document decisions, and ensure that the final themes remained aligned with the research question.

Statistical analysis

Data from the electronic survey was exported into an Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA) and subsequently analyzed using R version 4.4.04. Descriptive statistics were presented as numbers and percentages for categorical variables, and as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables with normal distribution. For continuous variables with non-normal distribution, results were expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test [17]. If the assumption of normality was not met, or when sample sizes were small and distributions were skewed, non-parametric tests were applied. Specifically: Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare non-normally distributed continuous variables between two independent groups; Wilcoxon Rank Sum test was used for related or paired comparisons; and Kruskal–Wallis test was used for non-parametric comparisons across more than two groups [17]. For normally distributed continuous variables, Student’s t-test or ANOVA was applied [17]. For categorical variables, comparisons were conducted using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate [17]. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Responses to the open-ended questions were analyzed using a qualitative descriptive approach.

RESULT

Out of 144 eligible students, 131 responded, yielding a 91% response rate. Cohort response rates were: Cohort 1: 90% (43/48), Cohort 2: 89% (40/45), and Cohort 3: 94% (48/51). Their age is ranging from 19 to 24 years (Table 1).

Quantitative data

Overall, modern educational tools received higher mean ratings than standard resources, with the 3D Anatomy App achieving the highest score (4.39 ± 0.84) and the virtual dissection table the lowest (2.92 ± 1.15) (Table 3). Ratings varied by demographic and academic factors: urban students and those from higher-income families rated modern tools significantly higher (p = 0.045 and p = 0.006, respectively) (Tables 2 and 4). Plastinated models were particularly valued by Cohort 2 (p = 0.006) (Table 3). Students pursuing surgical careers rated both modern tools and certain standard resources, such as atlases (p = 0.044) and Canvas (p = 0.039), higher than non-surgical peers (Table 5). Qualitative feedback (Table 6) reinforced these trends, highlighting a desire for more interactive visual resources, increased access to real cadavers, better labeling of models, integration with clinical practice, language support, and, for some, extended access to the 3D Anatomy App.

Discussion

This study shows that students did not view modern anatomy learning resources as uniformly beneficial. Instead, they differentiated clearly among specific tools: the 3D Anatomy App was rated highest, the virtual dissection table lowest, and standard resources remained highly valued, particularly online resources (Table 3). Interpreted through the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), these findings are educationally meaningful because TAM posits that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use shape learners’ acceptance of a technology and their intention to engage with it [15]. The marked variation in usefulness ratings suggests that students were making pragmatic judgments about which resources warranted their time and effort within a demanding curriculum. At the same time, the low ratings for certain high investment tools indicate that technological promise alone is insufficient when implementation fit is weak. Limited guidance, inconsistent integration into teaching sessions, and a steep learning curve can reduce perceived ease of use and thereby lower perceived usefulness, even for sophisticated platforms [15].

Against this broader pattern, the 3D Anatomy App illustrates how a tool can achieve high perceived usefulness when its features align closely with students’ learning needs and study habits. Prior work indicates that 3D visualization and manipulation can strengthen understanding of spatial relationships, facilitate self-directed learning, and enable repeated practice that supports retention and comprehension [16]. The qualitative responses reinforce this interpretation by emphasizing the value of interactive visual learning experiences and by explicitly requesting extended access to the app beyond the anatomy course (Table 6). These comments suggest that students valued not only the app’s features but also its portability across learning contexts, including preparation, revision, and consolidation after formal teaching. This aligns with evidence that interactive and visual resources can enhance spatial understanding and retention in anatomy learning [18].

Plastinated specimens and advanced plastic model sets were also rated highly, but the combined quantitative and qualitative findings suggest different mechanisms of value. Plastinated models were perceived as helpful for learning anatomy, consistent with literature describing plastination as a feasible and durable approach that can support anatomy education, particularly where cadaver availability is constrained [19]. Multiple studies report that students find plastinated specimens beneficial for anatomical understanding and examination preparation, including improved retention of anatomical detail [20–23]. However, the qualitative findings also clarify that high perceived usefulness does not eliminate perceived limitations. Students appreciated the realism of plastinated models yet described reduced interactivity in some learning situations and expressed a desire for more opportunities to learn from real cadavers (Table 6). This combination suggests that the educational value of plastinated resources may be maximized when they are used within active learning tasks and clinically oriented prompts, rather than primarily demonstrative sessions. Advanced plastic models were similarly rated positively, consistent with evidence that model-based learning can support performance in gross anatomy [24]. Students’ qualitative comments about labeling and guidance highlight that usability features, such as clear labels and structured guides, can determine whether models support independent learning or require continuous instructor mediation.

The virtual dissection table produced the clearest disconnect between theoretical promise and student perceived usefulness. Virtual dissection can enhance anatomy learning, especially by supporting comprehension of spatial relationships and enabling integration with imaging and case-based teaching [25–27]. The low rating observed in this study therefore likely reflects implementation conditions rather than an inherent limitation of virtual dissection as a modality. Virtual platforms often require deliberate curricular integration, structured tasks, and sufficient faculty familiarity to translate technical capability into meaningful learning. When a tool is not consistently embedded into laboratory activities, linked to assessed objectives, or supported by clear guidance, students may experience it as time consuming or peripheral to learning priorities. This interpretation is consistent with broader evidence that teaching resources support learning most effectively when they are aligned to course objectives and embedded in instructional design that connects foundational knowledge to clinical reasoning [28]. The qualitative themes emphasize stronger integration with clinical practice and the need for more interactive teaching approaches (Table 6) further support the conclusion that implementation fit is a key determinant of perceived usefulness.

A central contribution of this study is the evidence that socioeconomic background is associated with students’ evaluations of certain modern tools. Urban students rated modern tools significantly higher than rural students (Table 3), and family income was associated with differences in ratings for the 3D Anatomy App and the virtual dissection table (Table 4). These patterns are educationally important in an LMIC setting because socioeconomic position can shape prior exposure to learning technologies, access to personal devices, and confidence in navigating complex digital platforms. Within a TAM interpretation, such differences plausibly influence perceived ease of use and thereby perceived usefulness and intention to use, even when institutional resources are formally available to all students [15]. The qualitative findings further indicate that equitable implementation of high investment tools requires attention to onboarding, scaffolded learning activities, and support structures that enable students from diverse backgrounds to benefit similarly.

Cohort differences similarly suggest that perceived usefulness reflects an interaction between the tool and its implementation environment. Cohort 3 rated modern tools highest overall and plastinated models were rated particularly highly by Cohort 2 (Table 3). Such variability is consistent with the notion that as instructors gain familiarity with a resource and refine how it is used in teaching sessions, students may experience greater learning value. The qualitative emphasis on interactive learning design and clinical integration supports the interpretation that students’ evaluations capture the educational experience produced by the tool plus the surrounding teaching approach, rather than the tool in isolation [28].

Standard resources warrant explicit attention because the findings show that high perceived usefulness is not restricted to high investment technologies. Online resources received high ratings (Table 3), underscoring the central role of accessible, flexible materials in students’ day-to-day study routines. Qualitative requests for embedding videos and 3D elements into teaching sessions indicate that perceived usefulness can be strengthened not only through procurement of new technologies but also through improving how standard resources are curated and integrated into learning activities.

Career intention differences were modest but informative. Students intending surgical careers rated modern tools slightly higher overall and rated certain standard resources, including atlases and Canvas, higher than peers intending non-surgical careers (Table 5). These differences likely reflect perceived future relevance and anticipated application of anatomy knowledge. At the same time, the overall high value assigned to foundational resources suggests that anatomy learning remains broadly important across career directions. Making clinical applications explicit across diverse specialties may help ensure that anatomy teaching remains relevant for all learners, including those pursuing anatomy intensive non-surgical fields.

Broader implications and future research

Previous mixed methods work at our institution surveyed medical and nursing students together with anatomy faculty and reported high overall satisfaction with modern educational resources and instructional approaches, while traditional lectures were rated lowest, particularly by medical students [29]. That study also highlighted faculty concerns regarding time constraints, technical infrastructure, and the lack of structured faculty development, and it called for systematic training and institutional support [29]. The present study is consistent with these observations in showing strong student enthusiasm for selected modern tools, particularly the 3D Anatomy App and plastinated models, but it adds a more granular, student-centered perspective by demonstrating that perceived usefulness varies across learner backgrounds. Specifically, urban residence, higher family income bracket, and intended surgical career were associated with higher ratings for certain tools, suggesting that equity and access considerations are not peripheral but central to how high investment resources function in practice within LMIC settings.

Evidence from the same curriculum context further supports the importance of how tools are implemented, not only which tools are purchased. A prior study showed that when these resources are embedded within coordinated co teaching between anatomists and surgeons, students report very high satisfaction and perceive a stronger link between foundational anatomy and real clinical practice [30]. This aligns with the pattern observed in our qualitative findings, where students valued resources most when they supported interactive learning and clinically meaningful integration, rather than when they were encountered as isolated or inconsistently used technologies.

This study supports an approach that combines a carefully selected set of high value modern tools with strong standard resources, rather than assuming that broader technological expansion will automatically improve learning experiences. In LMIC settings where procurement and maintenance costs are substantial, collaboration across institutions may also be a practical strategy to broaden access. For example, plastinated specimens and advanced plastic models can be shared through structured regional initiatives, such as short intensive anatomy programs, to extend the benefits of high quality resources beyond a single campus.

To realize the full educational potential of these tools, faculty development remains a critical enabling condition. Training that supports faculty in aligning resources with learning objectives, integrating tools into lectures and practical sessions, and using case discussions to connect anatomy with clinical reasoning may help translate technical capability into experienced usefulness for students. Such training can be delivered through online modules or blended workshops, which can be feasible even when local expertise is limited. International collaborations, including partnerships such as VinUniversity and UPenn, may also support implementation by offering curricular design guidance and helping institutions prioritize investments that deliver the greatest educational value in resource constrained environments.

Future research should assess the long-term impact of modern educational tools on student learning outcomes and clinical performance. Longitudinal studies could clarify the durability of benefits and identify any unintended consequences of heavy reliance on technologies. Expanding research to multiple medical schools in countries with similar resource constraints would support comparisons across different institutional and cultural contexts and improve the generalizability of findings. Another important direction is cost benefit analysis to guide investment decisions and ensure that limited funds are used to achieve the greatest educational gain. Finally, identifying and evaluating best practices for faculty training that support sustained and effective integration of modern tools into anatomy curricula remains a key priority.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the sample size is relatively small due to the recent establishment of VinUniversity. Consequently, the limited number of cohorts that have taken the Anatomy course may affect the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, as a cross-sectional study, Cohorts 1–3 have taken the Anatomy course at different time periods with different instructors and teaching methods. Instructors with greater familiarity and self-efficacy using these tools may have integrated them more effectively, potentially enhancing student perceptions of their usefulness. Conversely, less experienced instructors may have conveyed them less effectively, dampening their perceived value. This variability was not controlled by our design and may account for differences across cohorts. In addition, potential recall bias may affect cohort comparisons because students’ ratings could be influenced by the recency of exposure, subsequent clinical or preclinical learning experiences, and changing perceptions of what was most useful over time. Therefore, cohort based differences should be interpreted cautiously as reflecting both tool perceptions and time dependent factors. Lastly, measuring the achievement of learning outcomes by the integration of modern educational tools is not yet presented in the current study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive evaluation of modern educational tools in anatomy teaching for medical students at VinUniversity. The findings indicate that tools such as the 3D Anatomy app and plastinated models could significantly enhance educational outcomes, receiving high ratings from students. However, disparities in ratings based on cohort, hometown, and income highlight the need for tailored implementation strategies. Additionally, the study underscores the potential for resource sharing and collaborative programs to mitigate costs and improve accessibility to high-quality educational tools. The insights gained from this research offer valuable guidance for medical institutions in similar contexts, emphasizing the importance of strategic investments and faculty training to optimize the use of advanced technologies in medical education.
