Evaluation of ozonoid olive oil as an indirect pulp capping agent in permanent molars: A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Hemal Bajaj, Rashmi Nair, Labdhi Maloo, Varnan Chakinala, Sarita Dasani, Smriti Verma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Context:Preserving pulp vitality is essential for maintaining tooth integrity, making indirect pulp capping (IPC) a preferred treatment for deep carious lesions. Traditional materials such as calcium hydroxide and mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) promote dentin repair but have limitations.Aims:To evaluate the clinical success of ozonated olive oil as an IPC agent compared to MTA and Dycal in the treatment of deep carious lesions in permanent molars.Subjects and Methods:A randomized clinical trial was conducted on 60 patients aged 19–40 years, divided into three groups receiving IPC with ozonated olive oil, MTA, or Dycal. Clinical and radiographic evaluations were performed at 3 and 6 months to assess treatment success.Results:After 6 months, MTA had the best effectiveness rate (94%), followed by ozonated olive oil (89%) and Dycal (75%). Ozonated olive oil has shown considerable microbial reduction and dentin remineralization.Conclusions:Ozonated olive oil proved to be an effective IPC agent, exhibiting strong antimicrobial properties and promoting dentin remineralization.Clinical Significance:Ozonoid olive oil can be considered a potential indirect pulp capping agent in permanent teeth due to its antimicrobial properties and ability to reduce bacterial load in dentinal caries.

INTRODUCTION

Dental caries remains the most prevalent oral health condition globally, affecting individuals across all age groups. This multifactorial disease results from the complex interplay of four key elements: The tooth structure, saliva, diet, and microbiota.[1]

Preservation of pulp vitality is of paramount importance, as the dental pulp plays a critical role in the formation of secondary dentin, peritubular dentin (sclerosis), and reparative dentin in response to biologic or pathologic stimuli.[2] In cases of deep carious lesions approaching the pulp, indirect pulp capping (IPC) is considered the treatment of choice for maintaining pulp vitality without direct pulp exposure.

The management of deep carious lesions remains debated, especially regarding how much dentin to remove. The current evidence supports minimally invasive approaches, as complete excavation increases the risk of pulp exposure and treatment failure. Residual microbes in demineralized dentin may trigger a mild inflammatory response that promotes pulpal healing and regeneration.[3] Therefore, the primary objective in the management of deep caries is to control inflammation while preserving pulp vitality through conservative treatment modalities.

IPC is founded on the principle that the pulp-dentin complex possesses innate regenerative potential, enabling it to form tertiary dentin as a reparative response. The primary goal of IPC is to arrest the carious process by stimulating dentinal sclerosis and promoting reparative dentinogenesis, while maintaining the vitality of the pulp tissue.[4]

Over the years, various materials have been used for IPC in teeth with deep carious lesions. Calcium hydroxide has long been regarded as the gold standard due to its ability to stimulate tertiary dentin formation and create a biological seal over the pulp.[5] However, calcium hydroxide presents several limitations, including poor adhesion to dentin, gradual dissolution over time, cytotoxicity in vitro, and inconsistent induction of odontoblastic differentiation. These drawbacks often lead to the formation of reparative dentin with tunnel defects, which can potentially serve as pathways for microbial ingress, thereby jeopardizing pulp health.[6]

To address these limitations, several alternative materials have been introduced. Among these, mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) has gained widespread acceptance because of its excellent biocompatibility, antibacterial properties, and capacity to promote rapid dentin bridge formation, improving clinical outcomes in vital pulp therapy procedures.[7] However, MTA presents certain limitations, including a prolonged setting time, challenging handling characteristics, risk of tooth discoloration, and relatively high cost.[8]

Recently, ozone therapy has garnered interest as a novel approach for managing deep carious lesions. Ozone is a powerful oxidizing agent with broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties, capable of disrupting microbial cell membranes and walls, thereby inactivating bacteria, fungi, and viruses.[9] It can be applied to oral tissues in three primary forms: Ozonated water, ozonated oil, and ozone gas.[1011]

Although ozone gas has shown therapeutic benefits in clinical studies,[12] its use often requires specialized equipment, limiting routine application. Ozonating olive oil produces ozonated (or ozonoid) olive oil, which preserves ozone’s antimicrobial and healing properties while allowing easier, equipment-free clinical use. This formulation is stable, easy to store, and can be directly applied to the treatment site. Its biocompatibility and effectiveness make it a practical alternative for conservative procedures, especially in minimally invasive caries management.

This study aims to explore the potential of ozonated olive oil in maintaining pulp vitality and promoting dentin bridge formation, thereby contributing to the advancement of minimally invasive, biologically driven strategies in conservative dentistry and endodontics.

The null hypothesis of the present study stated that there would be no significant difference among the materials used for IPC.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

The randomized clinical trial was designed with three experimental groups. This clinical trial was conducted from November 2023 to December 2024. The clinical trial was assessed and validated by the institutional ethical board (EC/NEW/INST/2020/1034) and was registered in the Clinical Trial Registry – India (CTRI/2024/01/061819). The selected patients were adults aged between 19 and 40 years.

Sample size was calculated using G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universitat Dusseldorf, Dusseldorf, Germany) with 80% power, 95% confidence, and 0.05 alpha, requiring 54 participants (18 per group) for ANOVA. To account for a 10% dropout rate, 60 patients (20 per group) were enrolled. All the participants were informed about the procedure, its benefits, risks, and alternatives, and provided written informed consent prior to treatment.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were initially selected using convenience sampling based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Included were patients aged 19–40 years with deep carious molars, normal or reversible pulpitis, and radiographic lesions involving ≥two-thirds of dentin with a Periapical index score of 1. Exclusion criteria comprised systemic illness, pulp exposure, spontaneous pain, sinus tracts, periodontal problems, mobility, cracks, resorption, or periapical pathology.

Randomization was conducted by the investigator’s colleague using a chit lottery method immediately before treatment.

Caries excavation and pulp capping procedure

For the investigation, sixty patients with deep carious lesions were chosen. Pulp vitality was assessed using Endo-Frost cold spray (Roeko; Coltène/Whaledent Inc., Langenau, Germany) applied to the affected tooth and adjacent clinically healthy control teeth. The affected tooth elicited the same response as control teeth – an immediate, sharp pain that subsided quickly upon removal of the stimulus – indicating vital pulp tissue. The electric pulp test was also performed; the affected tooth showed the same response compared to the adjacent and contralateral tooth. To assess the state of the hard tissues and periodontium, a preoperative radiograph was taken. Anesthesia was achieved by administering 2% lidocaine hydrochloride with epinephrine 1:80,000 buccally by infiltration for maxillary teeth and inferior alveolar nerve block for mandibular teeth (Lignospan, Septodont, France). A rubber dam (Hygienic; Coltene/Whaledent, USA) was used to accomplish isolation. Caries removal was initially performed by a sterile BR 31 ball round bur (Mani Inc., Japan) mounted on an airotor, followed by a spoon excavator. Excavation was continued using either the bur or hand instrument until resistance was felt. Teeth were excluded from the trial if pulp exposure resulted in bleeding. After caries excavation, a simple randomization technique was employed to assign each tooth randomly to one of the three experimental groups.

All samples received interim glass ionomer cement (GIC) restoration (Shofu, Japan). After 6 weeks, patients were recalled, the GIC was reduced to a base, and a nanohybrid composite resin (Beautifil II, Shofu, Japan) was placed using the oblique incremental technique. Cavities were etched with 37% phosphoric acid for 15 s, rinsed, and air-dried. A bonding agent (Adper Single Bond 2, 3M ESPE) was applied and light-cured for 20 s. Composite increments ≤2 mm was placed and cured for 40 s each. Occlusion was checked and adjusted, followed by finishing and polishing with the Super-Snap kit (Shofu Dental, Japan) [Figure 1].

The patients were recalled after 3 months and 6 months. Clinical and radiographic tests were repeated at every visit, and the readings were recorded in the data entry sheet. The clinical and radiographic outcomes were assessed for all patients by a single experienced endodontist. The teeth were examined for tenderness on percussion and palpation, and the results were recorded as 0 if absent and 1 if present.

Teeth that exhibited an exaggerated and lingering response or no response were considered failures during the cold and electrical pulp vitality test. Radiographic evaluation of success was based on the absence of furcal radiolucency, signs of root resorption, and/or periapical index score exceeding 2.

Statistical analysis

All the patients’ data were collected, tabulated, and subjected to descriptive statistics using the SPSS version 26 for Windows (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with a Chi-square test. Pearson correlation was used to obtain a statistical significance value kept at 0.05.

DISCUSSION

Preserving pulp vitality and its normal function is the goal of conservative treatment. By encouraging reparative dentin formation and resolving inflammation, only a healthy and functional pulp can repair itself. The conservative strategy of carious molar pulp capped with more recent materials was the main focus of our trial. Bleeding from the exposed pulp for more than 5 min and up to 10 min is used as a threshold for reversible versus irreversible pulpitis classification.[13]

In the present study, the null hypothesis was rejected as statistically significant differences were observed in the clinical success rates of ozonated olive oil, MTA, and Dycal when used as IPC agents in permanent molars.

Clinical evaluation and pulp vitality testing at 3 months showed two failures in the Dycal group, with one failure each in the Ozonoid olive oil and MTA groups. By a 6-month follow-up, the study assessed the effectiveness of Ozonoid olive oil, MTA, and Dycal in IPC of permanent molars. Since Dycal has been the gold standard material for pulp capping techniques for almost a century, it was included in the trial. There are several drawbacks, such as poor binding to dentin, gradual disintegration, dissolution, and tunnel defects in reparative dentin.[14]

MTA offers consistent outcomes; physicians are switching from Dycal to MTA as pulp capping materials.[1516] Aeinehchi et al. discovered that MTA has a higher incidence of dentinogenic process induction than Dycal. According to a few authors, MTA produces more reparative dentin and at a higher frequency.[1718] Some drawbacks of MTA include tooth discoloration, a longer setting time, and difficulties handling.[19]

The present study aimed to evaluate and compare the clinical efficacy of MTA, Dycal (calcium hydroxide), and Ozonated olive oil as IPC agents in permanent molars with deep carious lesions. The overall success rates observed at the 6-month follow-up were highest for MTA (94%), followed closely by ozonated olive oil (89%) and comparatively lower for Dycal (75%).

MTA demonstrated the highest clinical success, consistent with previous studies highlighting its superior biological and physicochemical properties. It promotes pulpal healing by stimulating the release of calcium ions and the formation of a dense dentin bridge, while also providing an effective seal against microbial infiltration. Its excellent biocompatibility and long-term stability likely contributed to the favorable outcomes observed in this study.

Ozonated olive oil exhibited a high success rate, underscoring its potential as an effective IPC agent. Radzimierska-Kaźmierczak et al. demonstrated that ozonated olive oil has enhanced antimicrobial properties, supporting its use in dental applications.[20]

Saini suggested that ozone therapy has emerged as a promising modality in conservative dentistry owing to its potent antimicrobial properties and ability to promote dentin remineralization.[9] Ozone (O3), upon decomposition, releases nascent oxygen (O), which is a highly reactive oxidizing species that effectively destroys bacteria, fungi, and viruses. When incorporated into olive oil through ozonation, the formulation becomes easier to handle, ensures controlled and sustained ozone release, and eliminates the need for sophisticated equipment. Kalaskar et al. reported that Ozonoid olive oil proved to be a viable IPC agent in primary molars and may also aid in bacterial reduction in dentinal caries, provided proper clinical protocols are followed.[21]

These characteristics enhance its clinical utility, particularly in minimally invasive approaches that aim to preserve pulp vitality. Ozonated olive oil acts as an efficient vehicle by boosting alkalinity and antimicrobial properties. Its potential to markedly lower microbial counts and promotes remineralization may have played a role in its observed clinical effectiveness in the current study.[22]

In contrast, the Dycal group demonstrated the lowest success rate. Although calcium hydroxide has long been used for IPC due to its antibacterial properties and capacity to induce hard tissue formation, it is associated with drawbacks such as poor sealing ability, material solubility, and the potential formation of porous dentin bridges, which may predispose to microleakage and eventual treatment failure.[23]

An interesting finding was that, although lesion site had no statistically significant impact on outcomes, all failures occurred in teeth with proximal lesions. These areas pose clinical challenges due to limited access, affecting isolation, caries removal, and material placement – factors likely contributing to the higher failure rate.

The electric pulp test and cold test were used during the follow-up to assess the vitality of the treated teeth.[24] These methods provided reliable confirmation of pulpal health and were instrumental in evaluating the success of the IPC procedures.

Overall, the results of the present study suggest that MTA remains the most predictable material for IPC. However, ozonated olive oil demonstrated promising clinical efficacy and could serve as a viable alternative, particularly in a noninvasive treatment approach.

The limitation of the study was a small sample size and a follow-up duration of only 6 months. Further longitudinal studies with larger populations and extended observation periods are warranted to validate these findings and explore the long-term effectiveness of ozonated olive oil in IPC. Future investigations incorporating histological evaluation could also enhance the understanding of the biological response of pulp tissue to these materials.[25]

CONCLUSIONS

Based on the comprehensive evaluation of clinical and microbiological data, the two-visit indirect therapy demonstrated the successful outcomes by significantly reducing the number of microorganisms and arresting decay. Ozonated olive oil has proven to be an effective IPC agent in permanent teeth, as well as a potent antimicrobial agent against microorganisms in deep dentinal caries.
