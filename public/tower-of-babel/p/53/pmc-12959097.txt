Ileal Diverticulitis Leading to Small Bowel Obstruction: A Rare Case of Ileal diverticular Disease
Authors: Immanuel R. Tullimalli, Vishnu S.R. Palyam, Srinath R. Allala, Nabil Badro, Sumanth K. Bandaru
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
While small bowel diverticulosis, particularly, ileal diverticulitis is rare and often asymptomatic, it can lead to chronic vague symptoms or acute complications. Acute presentations may mimic appendicitis, small bowel obstruction, or colonic diverticulitis but can also appear with atypical symptoms, making diagnosis challenging. In many cases, definitive diagnosis relies on imaging such as abdominal CT, which can identify features like extraluminal air or mass lesions, though surgical exploration may be required in complex cases.Small bowel diverticula are structural outpouchings of the intestinal wall, typically located on the mesenteric border, and are more common in men with a male-to-female ratio of 2:1. They are predominantly found in the proximal jejunum (75 %), followed by the distal jejunum (20 %) and ileum (5 %). Complications such as obstruction, perforation, and localized peritonitis may occur, necessitating prompt surgical intervention.Management of uncomplicated cases typically involves bowel rest, intravenous fluids, and antibiotics. For complicated cases, resection of the affected bowel segment with primary anastomosis is the treatment of choice. Early recognition and timely surgical intervention are crucial to preventing severe morbidity and mortality associated with this rare condition.

Introduction

I leal diverticulitis is a rare condition involving the small intestine, with most diverticula occurring in the jejunum and only a small percentage found in the ileum. Diverticula are rare in the small bowel, with a reported rate of 0.06 %–1.3 % on autopsy, predominantly involving the jejunum.1,9 Of jejunoileal diverticula, 80 % occur in the jejunum, 15 % in the ileum, and 5 % in both the jejunum and ileum. Diverticulosis of the terminal ileum is extremely rare, but the actual prevalence is unknown.2 Unlike colonic diverticula, small bowel diverticula are less common, smaller in size, and often asymptomatic. When symptoms do occur, they may range from vague abdominal discomfort to acute complications such as obstruction, perforation, or localized peritonitis. Due to its rarity and nonspecific presentation, diagnosing ileal diverticulitis can be challenging and often requires advanced imaging or surgical exploration. Management depends on the severity of the presentation, with conservative approaches for uncomplicated cases and surgical intervention for more severe complications. Our case report presents a rare case of ileal diverticulitis leading to small bowel obstruction in an elderly patient, emphasizing the diagnostic difficulties and the importance of early surgical intervention.

Case presentation

A 75-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a 2-day history of worsening abdominal pain. Initially, the pain was localized to the right lower quadrant, the pain later progressed to the left lower quadrant and was accompanied by vomiting. His past medical history was notable for Alzheimer’s disease and prior surgical repair of bilateral inguinal hernia repairs.

On physical examination, his abdomen was distended and diffusely tender to palpation. His vital signs were within normal limits. All laboratory studies were within normal limits, except for an elevated WBC count (15.2 k/μl) with reference range (4–10.8 k/μl). A computed tomography scan of the abdomen with intravenous contrast revealed a high-grade small bowel obstruction, with a transition point identified in the left lower quadrant Fig. 1.

The patient was initially managed conservatively with bowel rest and nasogastric tube placement. No clinical improvement was noted over the next 48 h, As his symptoms continued to worsen patient was taken to the operating room and underwent an exploratory laparotomy.

Intraoperatively, a complete small bowel obstruction was identified at the level of the mid-small bowel where the mid-small bowel was attached to the area of the cecum and terminal ileum, along with purulent material and exudate near the cecum and terminal ileum. The exudates were removed, and the bowel obstruction was released. Diverticulitis of the terminal ileum was discovered, necessitating resection of part of the terminal ileum, cecum, and the right colon. An end-to-end anastomosis was performed between the ileum and the hepatic flexure of the transverse colon. Pathology showed a perforated terminal ileum with evidence of inflammation & abscess formation. Also noted to have serositis and fibrinous adhesions.

The patient was treated with ceftriaxone and metronidazole, Cultures taken from abdominal abscess during surgery later grew Escherichia coli and Klebsiella oxytoca, both of which were sensitive to ceftriaxone. Postoperatively the patient was started on peripheral parenteral nutrition, was gradually started on a liquid diet & was transitioned to a solid diet. He was eventually discharged from the hospital to follow up with surgery as an outpatient.

Discussion

Diverticula are rare in the small bowel, with a reported rate of 0.06 %–1.3 % on autopsy predominantly involving jejunum.1,9 Of jejunoileal diverticula, 80 % of diverticula occur in the jejunum, 15 % in the ileum, and 5 % in both the jejunum and ileum. Diverticulosis of the terminal ileum is extremely rare but actual prevalence is unknown.2

Diverticula of the small intestine are structural outpouchings of the mucosa, submucosa, and serosa through weak areas in the intestinal wall, specifically where the vasa recta blood vessels penetrate. These weak points allow for herniation, resulting in thin-walled diverticula that are typically located on the mesenteric border of the bowel. Unlike colonic diverticula, those in the small intestine are smaller, less frequent in the distal regions, and often asymptomatic. Most cases are discovered incidentally through imaging or surgical procedures. However, when symptoms occur, they can range from chronic, nonspecific abdominal complaints to acute complications, including obstruction and perforation.3,4

Small bowel diverticulosis is observed more commonly in men, with a male-to-female ratio of 2:1. The condition predominantly involves the proximal jejunum in 75 % of cases, followed by the distal jejunum (20 %) and the ileum (5 %).5 Despite its frequency, only about 29 % of patients exhibit noticeable symptoms, making diagnosis both challenging and frequently delayed.6

The etiology of ileal diverticulitis includes congenital anomalies like Meckel’s diverticulum and acquired factors such as increased intraluminal pressure and weakened bowel walls. Diets low in fiber and high in processed foods can disrupt intestinal motility, increase intraluminal pressure, and promote the formation of diverticula.7 Irritation caused by trauma, surgical procedures, or foreign body ingestion can trigger inflammation in predisposed individuals. Additionally, conditions such as Crohn’s disease, bacterial stasis within diverticula, and disorders like small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) or neuromuscular dysfunctions (e.g., scleroderma or amyloidosis) increase the risk of diverticulitis by fostering inflammation and infection.8

Abdominal computed tomography (CT) is the diagnostic tool of choice for identifying small bowel diverticulosis and its complications. While CT may not reveal all diverticula, it can detect characteristic features such as extraluminal air bubbles and mass lesions, which are indicative of perforation or other complications.9 Acute presentations, such as ileal diverticulitis, may mimic appendicitis with right lower quadrant pain and can lead to localized peritonitis due to a contained perforation.10

Management of uncomplicated cases typically involves conservative treatment, including bowel rest, intravenous fluids, and antibiotics to control infection and inflammation. For complicated cases involving significant bleeding, obstruction, or perforation, surgical intervention is required. Surgical management generally involves resection of the affected bowel segment and reconnection of the remaining portions.3 Severe complications include intra-abdominal abscesses, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and complete bowel obstruction. Diffuse or localized perforation constitutes a medical emergency, necessitating prompt surgical intervention to prevent severe morbidity or mortality.6,10

Conclusion

This case highlights an unusual presentation of ileal diverticulitis leading to small bowel obstruction in an elderly patient, confirmed through exploratory laparotomy and treated with resection and anastomosis. It underscores the importance of considering ileal diverticulitis in the differential diagnosis of acute abdomen and highlights the critical role of early surgical intervention in preventing severe complications.
