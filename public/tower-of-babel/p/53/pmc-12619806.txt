Introduction

Sickle cell disease is a congenital hemoglobin disorder.

It is characterized by a structural abnormality in the beta chain of globin, leading to the production of abnormal hemoglobin (Hb S) [1].

It is the most common hemoglobinopathy, primarily found among individuals of African descent.

Sickle cell disease is highly prevalent, with more than 150 million people affected worldwide according to Beauvais [2].

In 1972, according to Cabannes et al. [3], it affected 12% of the Ivorian population. Today, although overshadowed by vaccination priorities or efforts to combat major endemic diseases (AIDS, Tuberculosis.) and even the coronavirus pandemic, sickle cell disease is expanding due to population growth, making it a true public health concern.

It is a complex disease both historically and medically, at the collective and individual levels, with its potential severity linked to anemic, infectious, and ischemic complications [4–15].

Until the end of the 20th century, it caused nearly constant and rapid infant mortality. Screening and the implementation of simple, standardized measures have significantly altered the natural history of this genetic disease by increasing patients’ life expectancy. As a result, the prevalence of the disease has risen, and severe conditions threatening life expectancy have emerged [16–22].

This study aimed to contribute to the understanding of morbidity and mortality among sickle cell patients within the Ivorian healthcare system, with the goal of developing efficient prevention and treatment policies to improve the quality of life of individuals affected by sickle cell disease.

The limiting factor in the present study could be the closure period of the Yopougon University Hospital, during which the Clinical Hematology department was relocated to another site (Cocody University Hospital), which resulted in a disruption in the follow-up of some patients who were lost to follow-up and thus led to the loss of certain data on mortality.

Materials and methods

Study setting

Bicentric, conducted in the Clinical Hematology Department of the University Hospital (CHU) of Cocody and the Internal Medicine Department of the CHU of Bouaké.

Study type

Retrospective, descriptive, and analytical study.

Study period

From January 2011 to January 2021.

Study population

Regularly followed sickle cell patients.

Inclusion criteria

Patients of all ages and genders, followed up either in outpatient clinics or during hospitalization, who received care in one of our sickle cell disease management services, had a biologically confirmed diagnosis of sickle cell disease recorded in their medical file, and gave their informed consent to participate in the study.

Data collection

Data were collected from hospitalization registers, medical records, and a questionnaire.

The studied parameters included epidemiological, clinical, biological, prognostic, therapeutic, and disease progression data.

Data analysis

Data were collected and analyzed using Excel (2010) and Epi Info (Version 7.2.2.6) software.

Results

There was a predominance of females, with a sex ratio (M/F) of 0.7 and an average age of 18 years.

More than half of the patients were students, accounting for 50.8% (160 patients), with 23% having their follow-up centers located far away.

Only 14% had health insurance, and 76.5% did not have an updated vaccination status.

Clinically, 17.8% presented with anemic syndrome, and 12.4% had vaso-occlusive crises.

Nearly half of the patients were hospitalized, with a rate of 49.5%.

Of those hospitalized, 54% received blood transfusions.

The patient profile was dominated by the SSFA2 phenotype in 38.41%, followed by the SC phenotype in 27.62% Table 1.

Almost all patients in this study received combination therapy.

Vasodilators (Tanakan) and folic acid were the main medications used in the management of patients, in 79.8% and 68.9% of cases, respectively. In 7.1% of cases, treatment was based on hydroxyurea Figure 1.

The majority of patients were well-followed, accounting for 72.3%.

26% of the patients developed complications, representing 82 individuals Table 2.

The main complications observed, in order of frequency, were: anemic complications (45.1%), infectious complications (30.5%), and ischemic complications (24.4%) Table 3.

There were 48 deaths, representing a mortality rate of 15.2% Table 4.

Discussion

Sickle cell disease is a congenital condition that affects the lives of patients. The global age distribution shows a predominance of young subjects with an average age of 18 years, with extremes ranging from 2 to 69 years. These data are consistent with those in the literature and may also reflect the young population that manifests most of the revealing symptoms of the condition, particularly in the context of the predominantly young demographic situation of the country [20].

We noted a predominance of female subjects with a sex ratio of 0.7. This trend could be explained by the fact that the recent data from the general census of the population and housing in Côte d’Ivoire (RGPHA) conducted in 2019 recorded a predominance of the female gender. Additionally, since sickle cell disease is not an autosomal disease linked to the X sex chromosome, the difference in the results obtained cannot simply be explained by a recruitment bias. However, this predominance of the female gender has been reported by several other authors [21–25].

Students predominated, followed by the unemployed and those working in the informal sector. These results are similar to those obtained by Chetcha [25], who in his study showed that 61.7% of patients were students, followed by the unemployed and those who were not in school. This concerned population could be the result of awareness campaigns conducted among them, who would be much more receptive to the message conveyed.

Clinical examinations primarily revealed an anemic syndrome and signs of vaso-occlusive crisis, as well as an infectious syndrome, a tumoral syndrome, and other signs. Latoundji S et al. [26] found that the main clinical manifestations were the anemic syndrome (40.6%) and hyperalgesia crises (34.5%), followed by other signs. This predominance of the anemic syndrome could be explained by the fact that in sickle cell disease, there is often a chronic anemic syndrome on which acute anemia can develop, leading to complications. Nearly half of the subjects were hospitalized (49.5%), and almost all of them had a maximum of 3 hospitalizations, accounting for 84.6%0.54.5% of hospitalized subjects received a blood product transfusion, representing 27% of all subjects.

Indeed, the implementation of a care strategy in the clinical hematology department has generally allowed for the control and monitoring of our sickle cell patients. As a result, they were most often aware of their treatment. It was only in the presence of persistent crises that they were prompted to consult. If it was a simple crisis, they were offered outpatient parenteral treatment for about 3 days. It was only in the case of crises with underlying evolving complications that they were most often hospitalized. This led to a reduction in the number of crises and prolonged hospitalizations, with an average of 1 to 3 days, equating to 82.7%.

Furthermore, only about a quarter (1/4) of them were up to date with their recommended vaccinations. This resulted in an increased risk of developing complications from the disease. Kamara in his work had shown a similar prevalence of 22.6% for major anemic sickle cell forms. The most common sickle cell phenotype was SSFA2, which was diagnosed in 38.4% of cases, followed by subjects with the SC phenotype (27.6%) and other forms. By comparing our results to those in the literature, we find that they align with those of many other authors.

On the other hand, Homawoo K et al. [27] in a retrospective study of 38 observations of aseptic osteonecroses found 56.6% for double heterozygosity SC and 26.3% for SSFA2 homozygotes. This difference in prevalence could be explained by the fact that their study focused exclusively on osteonecrosis, keeping in mind that osteonecrosis remains a common complication for double heterozygotes SC. Almost all of our subjects had maintenance treatment (99%) mainly consisting of vasodilators, followed by folic acid and NSAIDs. Hydroxyurea was used in 7.1% of cases. Indeed, our study on the morbidity and mortality of sickle cell disease involved patients who were regularly monitored, which probably influenced their quality of life by improving the prognosis of many subjects [28–31].

However, despite this follow-up, some did not adhere to the recommendations such as regular medication intake, systematic consultations, and carrying out assessments, as reported by Walter [32], where low-income groups with a detrimental role of the socio-economic environment were most subjected to the evolving risks of this pathology, which altered the quality of follow-up in 1/3 of the cases. Therefore, 82 patients presented at least one complication in a population of 315 subjects, resulting in an incidence of 26%.

The three main groups of evolving complications of sickle cell disease have been identified. Anemic complications were the most common at 45.1%, followed by infectious complications at 30.5%, and ischemic complications at 24.4%. Our results are consistent with those of Timite et al. [22], who in their study on the prevalence of complications of severe sickle cell disease found that anemic complications predominated, followed by infectious complications and finally ischemic complications. The anemic complications that ranked highest were dominated by acute hemolytic crisis (36.58%).

Then, infectious complications are dominated by severe malaria (17.07%), noting that indeed, in black Africa, sickle cell disease occurs in an area of high prevalence known as Lehmann’s sickle belt: the same area as malaria endemicity. And malaria, like sickle cell disease, affects red blood cells, leading to hemolytic anemia.

Furthermore, sickle cell children are the most transfused subjects and therefore, the most at risk of contamination regarding blood units that have been insufficiently treated for infectious risk, particularly viral [33].

Finally, there were the ischemic complications for which the present study recorded 9 cases of femoral head necrosis (10.97%), which was the predominant complication, followed by acute chest syndromes (5 cases), sickle cell retinopathy (3 cases), priapism (1 case), stroke (1 case), and respiratory distress (1 case). These data are consistent with those of several authors who reported higher frequencies concerning femoral head necrosis [34].

Our results are consistent with the electrophoretic profile of the subjects recorded during our study, which overall saw a predominance of subjects from the SSFA2 group (anemic form) followed by the SC group (non-anemic form with a strong tropism for the occurrence of infectious and ischemic complications).

The sickle cell mortality rate was 15.2%. Latoundji S et al. in their study on morbidity and mortality related to sickle cell disease in Benin found a specific rate of 10.6% [26]. On the other hand, according to Kremp O et al., the crude mortality rate related to sickle cell disease between 1979 and 2004 in France was 59.2% for major forms of sickle cell disease [18]. Indeed, the mortality related to sickle cell disease has been studied by several authors, but comparing the different published series remains difficult due to the fact that the conditions of the studies were not identical from one to another [34–37].

In this study, the cause of death was dominated by severe acute anemia (45.84%), followed by infectious causes and then ischemic causes. In 6.25% of cases, the cause was undetermined. By comparing our results with the data from the literature, we find that they are consistent with those of many other authors [18; 25].

For other authors, the causes of death varied depending on the studied population, with infectious diseases being the main cause of infant mortality in Africa, whether in sickle cell patients or in the general population. It has been noted that in a large part of Africa, acute fever is synonymous with malaria and antimalarial therapy is often administered without prior diagnosis. However, even though malaria is indeed a common cause of death among children, pneumococcus is also a major pathogen in Africa. Among adults, the causes were dominated by acute anemia, followed by infections, acute chest syndromes, and others. Acute anemia is often fatal due to the difficulties in accessing transfusions, such as low availability, high costs for certain populations, and frequent infectious complications or allo-immunization, as reported in Ranque’s work on the state of play and levers of sickle cell prognosis in Africa [38]. The OAP, cardiac arrest, pancytopenia, and other causes constituted 6.3% of the remaining causes of death among the patients included in our study.

Conclusion

Sickle cell disease is a serious condition whose progression is marked by severe, often fatal complications, especially in childhood. Its prevalence in our country remains significant.

In this study, this pathological entity affects individuals of all ages, without distinction of race or gender, in a homogeneous manner. The clinical and paraclinical symptoms observed are varied, specific, and severe, responsible for acute complications (acute hemolytic crisis, severe malaria, acute chest syndrome, stroke, etc.), which are often fatal, and other chronic complications that frequently jeopardize functional outcomes, such as femoral head necrosis.

The incidence of the observed complications is highly concerning, with a rate of 26%, dominated by anemic complications (45.1%). We also recorded a high mortality rate of 15.2%, with the main causes of death being the same as those reported in the literature, making sickle cell disease a significant public health issue that requires urgent and special attention.

To combat sickle cell morbidity and reduce the death rate, it is crucial to implement systematic screening policies as well as proper and early medical monitoring of cases. This would ensure a better quality of life and a good prognosis for individuals with sickle cell disease.
