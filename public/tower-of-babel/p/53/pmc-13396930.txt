Revisiting intensity normalization in brain [18F]FDG PET: What is reliable in autoimmune encephalitis?
Authors: Fiene Marie Kuijper, Antoine Rogeau, Hélène Rostand, Alberto Picca, Cristina Birzu, Vincent Navarro, Louis Cousyn, Vincent Davy, Dimitri Psimaras, Aurélie Kas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeData-driven intensity normalization has emerged as an alternative to proportional scaling (PS) and reference-region methods in brain [18F]FDG PET. However, no consensus exists for autoimmune encephalitis (AE), whose variable metabolic patterns and lack of a reliable disease-free reference region complicate normalization. We compared three methods; PS, iterative PS (iPS), and pons-based reference-region (RR) normalization, in patients with definite AE (n = 29, 42 ± 20 years) and healthy controls (HC; n = 53, 46 ± 15 y/o) examined with brain [18F]FDG PET at diagnosis.MethodsThree sets of normalized [18F]FDG PET images (2 MBq/kg, Biograph mCT Flow PET/CT system, Siemens Healthcare) were generated using: (1) PS with an eroded AAL grey-matter mask; (2) iPS, applying PS then excluding voxels deviating from controls (SPM12, F-contrast p < 0.01 uncorrected) to create a subject-specific mask; and (3) RR, normalizing to mean pons uptake. Effects were assessed through voxel-based AE vs. HC comparisons in SPM12. Two limbic AE cases were analyzed longitudinally.ResultsPS and iPS revealed basal ganglia and mesiotemporal hypermetabolism in AE vs. HC that RR failed to detect (p < 0.05 FWE corrected). All methods identified cortical hypometabolism, slightly more extensive with RR. PS and iPS yielded broadly similar results. Follow-up showed that iPS and PS better captured initial abnormalities than RR.ConclusionPS and iterative PS may provide more robust normalization than RR in AE. As interest in neuroinflammatory disorders grows, standardized normalization protocols are needed to ensure consistency across studies.

Background

[18F]FDG positron emission tomography (PET) is widely used to assess cerebral glucose metabolism in conditions such as neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, brain lymphomas, and epilepsy (For the EANM-EAN Task Force for the Prescription of FDG-PET for Dementing Neurodegenerative Disorders et al., 2018; Bordonne et al., 2021). As metabolic activity is influenced by physiological and technical factors (including age, sex, injected dose, and acquisition settings) intensity normalization is required to reduce inter-subject variability and allow meaningful group comparisons (Guedj et al., 2022; Arbizu et al., 2025).

Common approaches include proportional scaling (PS) – which scales voxel intensities by either the whole brain parenchyma (including white and grey matter) (Borghammer., 2012) or only grey matter (Lange et al., 2016; López-González et al., 2020) – and reference region (RR) normalization – which uses a region presumed to be unaffected, typically the pons or cerebellum in neurodegenerative disorders (Yakushev et al., 2009; Lucignani et al., 1993; Friston et al., 1994; Nugent et al., 2020; Verger et al., 2021). PS can fail in the presence of widespread cortical hypometabolism as often observed in advanced dementia, leading to an artificial overestimation of spared regions. RR normalization, in turn, depends on the assumption that the reference region is truly unaffected in the disease under study, and may also be sensitive to partial-volume effects, noise and co-registration errors. Both methods assume that global or regional variations are unrelated to the effects of interest. When this assumption is violated, normalization may produce spurious group differences.

To overcome these limitations, data-driven intensity normalization alternatives have been proposed. Among them, iterative proportional scaling (iPS) typically removes hypometabolic clusters iteratively in [18F]FDG PET images before applying PS (Lange et al., 2016; López-González et al., 2020; Andersson, 1997). Although promising in simulated settings, these methods have not yet been validated in conditions characterized by mixed hyper- and hypometabolic patterns, such as autoimmune encephalitis (AE).

AE is an emerging indication for [18F]FDG PET (Bordonne et al., 2021; Morbelli et al., 2016), yet its metabolic heterogeneity, varying across antibody subtypes and involving cortical and subcortical regions, often with coexisting hypermetabolism and hypometabolism (Probasco et al., 2018; Navarro et al., 2016), raises methodological challenges. Inappropriate normalization may lead to systematic biases, yet no consensus currently exists on the optimal strategy for AE (Guedj et al., 2022; Arbizu et al., 2025; Morbelli et al., 2017).

This study aims to compare three commonly used intensity normalization methods – PS, iPS, pons RR – in a voxel-based analysis of 29 patients with definite seropositive AE compared with 53 healthy controls (HC).

Methods

Population

We included 29 patients with definite seropositive AE according to Graus criteria (Graus et al., 2016) (age 42 ± 20 y/o, 45% female). At diagnosis, the most common symptoms were cognitive impairment (76%), seizures (52%), psychiatric symptoms (45%), confusion (41%), and abnormal movements (52%). Morphological magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and electroencephalography (EEG) showed abnormalities in 69% and 66% of cases, respectively. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed pleocytosis (38%), oligoclonal bands (59%), and intra-thecal synthesis (66%), with a mean protein level of 0.34 ± 0.16 g/L. Antibodies were found against N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor (NMDAr, n = 11), leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1 (LGI1, n = 8), glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD, n = 4), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, n = 2), and contactin-associated protein-like 2 (CASPR2), Ma2, dopamine receptor D2 (DR2), kelch-like protein 11 (KLHL11, each n = 1). [18F]FDG PET was acquired at a median of 25 days after treatment initiation (Q1 – Q3 = 3.2–45.5).

[18F]FDG PET

Patients underwent brain [18F]FDG PET/CT (Biograph mCT Flow, Siemens Healthcare) at diagnosis in the Department of Nuclear Medicine, Pitié-Salpêtrière Hospital (AP-HP, Paris). Imaging was performed for 10 min, starting 30 min post-injection of 2 MBq/kg [18F] FDG. Images were reconstructed using an iterative algorithm (OSEM3D, 8 iterations, 21 subsets, Gaussian filter 3 mm), and corrected for decay, attenuation, and scatter, but not for point spread function. A normative dataset of 53 healthy controls (HC, age 46 ± 15 y/o, 55% female) (Rozenblum et al., 2025), was acquired using the same scanner and acquisition protocol.

Use of our institutional [18F]FDG PET/CT database for research was approved by the French data protection authority (CNIL, No. 2111722). In accordance with French regulations, all patients were informed of the potential use of their clinical data for research and could opt out. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Image post processing

The same processing pipeline was applied to PET data for AE and HC. Reconstructed data (Bq/mL) were converted to standardized uptake values (SUV) using body weight and injected dose. PET spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space with the Old normalize tool using the default PET template in Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12) (Friston et al., 1994) resulting in 2 × 2 × 2 mm3 normalized volumes.

Spatially normalized SUV PET images were scaled using three intensity normalization procedures: (1) For PS, each voxel value was divided by the mean uptake within an explicit grey matter mask generated by concatenating eroded Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) (Tzourio-Mazoyer et al., 2002) regions (neocortex, cerebellum, basal ganglia, mesencephalon, pons). (2) For iPS, scans were first PS normalized. A SPM comparison with HC was then performed to capture hypo- and hypermetabolic voxels using a bidirectional F-contrast. Clusters identified at puncorrected < 0.01 and k = 0 were removed from the grey matter mask to create a subject-specific reference mask for final normalization (López-González et al., 2020). (3) For RR, each voxel was divided by the mean uptake in the pons, defined using the Wake Forest University (WFU) PickAtlas (Maldjian et al., 2003; Maldjian et al., 2004).

The PET images were then smoothed with a FWHM 8 mm Gaussian kernel. The full processing pipeline is available as a unified workflow in a publicly accessible repository (https://github.com/rogeau/fdg-pet-intensitynorm-ae).

Statistical analysis

The impact of PS, iPS and RR was assessed using voxel-wise group comparisons (AE vs HC) using SPM12 with age and sex set as nuisance regressors. For each method, the corresponding sets of normalized images were entered into two-sample t-tests to evaluate their effect on group-level differences. SPM (T) maps were thresholded at p < 0.05 and corrected for family-wise error (FWE) for group level analyses (k = 50).

The impact of intensity scaling methods was further assessed using single-subject voxel-wise analyses versus HC in two cases of definite AE, one NMDAr-positive and one LGI1-positive, who underwent baseline and post-treatment [18F]FDG PET/CT and presented a good clinical response after treatment. Baseline metabolic abnormalities obtained with PS, iPS and RR were analyzed and their clinical relevance assessed against follow-up findings used as a reference. SPM (T) maps were thresholded at p < 0.001 uncorrected for individual analyses (k = 50).

Results

Across all three normalization methods, AE patients showed a similar pattern of cortical hypometabolism in the bilateral prefrontal cortex, precuneus, and posterior cingulate gyrus (p < 0.05 corr., Fig. 1), with slightly more extensive decrease at the lower threshold, particularly in the associative occipital cortex and when using RR (p < 0.001 uncorr., Supplementary Fig. 1). With both PS and iPS, significant hypermetabolism was observed at p < 0.05 (corr.) in the MTL (mesiotemporal lobe), lateral temporal cortex, putamen, insula, and cerebellum (Fig. 1). At p < 0.001 (uncorr.), this pattern further extended to the perirolandic cortex, midbrain, and pons (Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, RR normalization revealed mild hypermetabolism confined to the left MTL only at the lower threshold (p < 0.001 uncorr., Supplementary Fig. 1). SPM summary statistics are shown in supplementary table 1.

At baseline, the NMDA-positive AE patient presented marked occipital hypometabolism extending to the superior parietal cortex that was similar with PS, iPS and RR, along with increased metabolism in the fronto-parieto-temporal cortices, cerebellum, putamen, midbrain and MTL, more widespread with PS and iPS than RR (Fig. 2, p < 0.001 uncor.). The LGI1-positive AE subject presented moderate frontal and parietal hypometabolism comparable with PS, iPS and RR, as well as increased metabolism in the striatum, insula, MTL, and pons, that were detected with PS and iPS but not RR (Fig. 3, p < 0.001 uncor.). Discussion

Although several guidelines exist to harmonize [18F]FDG PET processing pipelines (For the EANM-EAN Task Force for the Prescription of FDG-PET for Dementing Neurodegenerative Disorders et al., 2018; Arbizu et al., 2025), no consensus has been reached on the optimal intensity-normalization method for AE, a condition marked by highly heterogeneous metabolic patterns varying across antibody subtypes, making it difficult to identify a reliable unaffected reference region (Guedj et al., 2022; Morbelli et al., 2016; Morbelli et al., 2017). In this study, we compared the impact of three normalization strategies –PS, iPS, and pons RR– in a well-characterized AE cohort. PS and iPS revealed prominent hypermetabolism in the basal ganglia and MTL, which RR failed to detect. All three methods identified cortical hypometabolism, slightly more extensive with RR. PS and iPS yielded similar results overall.

The most reproductible [18F]FDG PET patterns reported in AE to date have emerged from studies using PS (Leypoldt et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2020; Rey et al., 2012; Novy et al., 2016). In contrast, studies using RR (employing pons, thalamus or frontal cortex) have yielded more heterogeneous findings (Probasco et al., 2017; Moreno-Ajona et al., 2020; Sadaghiani et al., 2023). While it remains impossible to define a “ground truth” [18F]FDG PET metabolic signature in AE, converging evidence supports the presence of hypermetabolism in AE in relation with a strong inflammatory state. The disappearance of hypermetabolism after treatment, consistent with the full clinical recovery and normalization of the EEG in our two illustrative patients, also supports that the PS- and iPS-detected hypermetabolism reflects genuine disease activity rather than a normalization artifact.

[18F]DPA-714 PET studies further support PS and iPS findings, showing increased activated microglial-related uptake in LGI1-positive AE with hippocampal, caudate, and frontal uptake correlating with worse cognitive outcome (Wang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). Follow-up PET after treatment showed a reduced uptake of [18F]DPA-714 associated with a relief of symptoms (Zhang et al., 2024). Similarly, [18F]fluorodeprenyl-D₂ PET, reflecting MAO-B upregulation in reactive glia, suggested astrogliosis in the MTL in LGI1-positive patients (Gernert et al., 2025). Although limited, histopathological data also support this interpretation. In NMDAr-positive AE, neuroinflammatory markers have been reported in the basal ganglia, hippocampus, and amygdala (Zrzavy et al., 2021). In a larger autopsy series (n = 43), perivascular inflammation and microglial activation were found in the limbic system, basal ganglia, cortex, and, less frequently, the diencephalon, brainstem, and cerebellum (Kerner et al., 2024), making pons and cerebellum unsuitable for RR normalization.

Unlike Lopez-Gonzalez and al7, iPS did not outperform PS in our study. This may possibly be due to the heterogenous metabolic profile in AE, combining hypo- and hypermetabolism which may have a cancelling effect on global uptake due to averaging, and differing from simulated data in which a predefined degree of hypometabolism is imposed. Also, in contrast to this study (López-González et al., 2020), we used an F-contrast to exclude both hypo- and hypermetabolism voxels when building individual masks, rather than a T-contrast focused solely on hypometabolism. This may partly explain the similarities observed iPS and PS findings.

While our findings offer new insights into normalization challenges in AE, several limitations must be noted. First, the retrospective design may limit generalizability; larger prospective cohorts will be needed to confirm these results. Second, our analysis focused only on PS, RR, and iPS. Future work should evaluate additional data-driven normalization methods to determine whether they offer advantages in autoimmune and neuroinflammatory disorders. Finally, our cohort is heterogenous, with varying antibody subtypes and treatment durations before PET FDG imaging. Although treatment regimens may influence metabolic patterns, it is unlikely to account for the differential performance observed between PS/iPS and RR, since all normalization methods were applied to the exact same dataset. Larger prospective cohorts will be needed to assess the relationship between metabolic changes, treatment duration, and antibody subtype. A multicenter prospective study is currently ongoing to address this question (NCT06079294).

Conclusion

Intensity normalization strongly influences [18F]FDG PET interpretation. In heterogeneous conditions such as AE, reference-region methods may lack the sensitivity to uncover hypermetabolic areas that are an important reflection of the ongoing neuroinflammatory processes. Proportional scaling and its iterative variant provide more reliable normalization approaches to uncover those contrasted metabolic changes. As interest in neuroinflammatory disorders grows, standardized normalization protocols are needed to ensure consistency across studies.
