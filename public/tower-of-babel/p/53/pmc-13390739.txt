Engineering A-site vacancies and Mo substitution in La2CuO4 for high-performance bifunctional water-splitting electrocatalysis
Authors: Islam Saad, Rafat M. Amin, Amel Haouas, S. I. El-Dek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing electrocatalysts that concurrently offer high activity, long-term durability, and low cost remains a major challenge in sustainable energy conversion technologies. In particular, bifunctional catalysts capable of efficiently catalyzing both the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) and hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) are highly desirable for practical water-splitting systems. Herein, La1.9Cu0.9Mo0.1O4−δ (d-LCMO-Ar) (“d” refers to deficiency) is synthesized via a citric-acid-assisted method followed by argon annealing, using a Ruddlesden–Popper parent phase as a structural platform to engineer enhanced bifunctional performance. The optimized catalyst achieves current densities of 10 and 100 mA cm−2 at overpotentials of 258 and 500 mV for the OER and −117 and −323 mV for the HER, respectively, in 1 M KOH. Moreover, d-LCMO-Ar maintains a stable current density of 50 mA cm−2 over 50 h, demonstrating excellent durability, and exhibits a large electrochemical surface area (183.7 cm2), low Tafel slopes of 67 mV dec−1 (OER) and 157 mV dec−1 (HER), as well as high turnover frequencies of 6.51 s−1 (OER) and 13 s−1 (HER). Collectively, these attributes highlight d-LCMO-Ar as a promising and cost-effective alternative to noble-metal based catalysts for alkaline water-splitting applications.

Introduction

Many efforts have focused on developing sustainable alternative energy sources to address the rapid depletion of fossil fuels and environmental concerns. Commercial alkaline electrolyzers, while practical for large scale, high purity hydrogen production, still suffer from inefficiencies.1 Several electrochemical devices, such as water electrolyzers, rechargeable metal air batteries, and electrochemical synthesizers, hold promise for clean energy conversion and fuel transformation. However, significant overpotentials arise mainly from the sluggish kinetics of the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) at the cathode and the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) at the anode.2 To drastically lower the overpotential, extremely active OER and HER electrocatalysts must be developed. Over the last 10 years, a lot of work has gone into finding novel OER and HER bifunctional catalysts with the necessary activity, durability, and selectivity. Numerous catalytic materials, such as carbon compounds, perovskites, spinel metal oxides, and layered double hydroxides, have been explored for their remarkable catalytic performances in alkaline electrolytes.3,4 Iridium/ruthenium oxides (IrO2/RuO2) and platinum (Pt) metal are now the state of the art electrocatalysts for the OER and HER, respectively; however, their limited availability, high cost, and poor stability have prevented their widespread use. Consequently, it is desired to create OER/HER catalysts with high activity and stability in alkaline solutions that are both affordable and plentiful in the earth.5,6 A continuous challenge is to optimize the architectures of the most active surface catalytic sites to serve as synthetic targets. Accurate mechanistic research that may provide a deeper comprehension of OER and HER catalysis and open up new avenues for the creation of better catalysts has been hampered by the same problem. Thus, how to locate and build well-defined catalytic centers for effective water-splitting catalysis and how to clearly define the atomistic mechanism and kinetics of the running reaction and competing reactions at this center are the two unresolved issues that are essential to heterogeneous OER/HER catalysis.7

La2CuO4, which has the general formula Lan+1CunO3n+1, belongs to the family of layered Ruddlesden–Popper (RP) phases. The perovskite type La2CuO4 and rock salt type layers alternate along the crystallographic axis to form the layered structure of La2CuO4 (also known as the K2NiF4 type structure). The capacity to accept overstoichiometric oxygen in the interstitial places in the rock salt type layers is a distinctive property of the RP structure.6 RP has been reported for widespread applications in the energy and environment fields. Their functions as bifunctional electrocatalysts have drawn more attention recently.8,9 However, their instability during the electrochemical process continues to be a significant obstacle. An efficient method of increasing catalytic efficiency is to build hybrids with interfacial contact between the constituents. Due to the presence of charge transfer and lattice tension between the various compounds, the interface may, on the one hand, function as new catalytic actives with the optimum electronic structure.10 Conversely, the hybrid systems have the capacity to provide certain synergistic effects that allow for the stabilization of catalytic sites and the achievement of a certain level of electrocatalytic activity and stability.11 La3+ has an ionic radius that is well-suited for the A-site of the Ruddlesden–Popper La2CuO4. The ionic radius of La allows it to stabilize the K2NiF4-type structures. Rare earth metal oxides, in particular, demonstrate outstanding catalytic performance by boosting material conductivity, increasing surface area, and providing improved pathways for ion and molecule diffusion. In addition to improving the ionic conductivity, carrier transportability, and catalytic performance.12,13 In order to preserve the compound's electroneutrality, the B element may be forced into a higher oxidation state or the creation of oxygen vacancies by replacing the A and/or B cations with a lower valence cation.12 These produced oxygen vacancies increase the lattice oxygen's mobility, which in turn raises the catalysts' oxidation activity.13 Since their simple metal oxides are very active for the complete oxidation reaction, Cu, Co, Fe, and Ni are often employed as B-site cations when La is in the A site. The equilibrium excess oxygen content fluctuation of B-site doped LCO. LCO based oxides may exhibit significant oxygen excess composition, with the quantity of excess oxygen being highly dependent on dopant species and might result from an imbalance in the charge.14

Several reports the A-site defects, which generate negative charges to the perovskite crystal and improve the catalytic activity by raising the concentration of oxygen vacancies and holes. Others reported that an A-site deficit causes the material's oxygen nonstoichiometry and hole concentration to rise. Improved conductivity and catalytic activity were the outcomes of introducing an A-site deficit.15 In catalysts, additional surface oxygen vacancies and Cu species were created by adjusting the quantity of A-site La ions in the perovskite structure (La1.9CuO4 catalyst), surpassing those of other transition metal based catalysts previously documented in the literature.16 Furthermore, it is technologically significant and urgently needed to develop a catalyst with high OER and HER electrolyte and low cost in practical applications.17 Although there has been considerable progress in this direction, there are still numerous obstacles to overcome before low-cost, highly effective catalysts for alkaline water splitting can be developed.2,16 This work aims to provide an innovative, high-stability electrocatalyst to be used in the water-splitting application by tuning the crystal structure and inducing defects using synergetic effects collected between A-site defects, doping, and reducing conditions such as Ar annealing medium.

Materials and methods

La(NO3)3·6H2O (Alfa Aesar, 99.9%), Cu(NO3)2·3H2O (Sigma-Aldrich, 99%), and (NH4)2MoO4 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.98%) were used as raw materials. Ethanol (99.5%, Alfa Aesar) and citric acid, anhydrous (Thermo Scientific, 99.5%), were utilized as solvent and combustion agent. Commercial epoxy resin adhesive, Ag wires, and Ni foam sheets of thickness 0.5 mm were purchased from SRNewTech Company, China.

Sample preparation

La2CuO4 catalysts were prepared by the citric acid combustion technique. Stoichiometric quantities of La(NO3)3·6H2O, Cu(NO3)2·3H2O, and (NH4)2MoO4 were dissolved in 0.1 M citric acid and stirred magnetically at 150 °C for 5 hours until gel formation. The grinded powder was calcined for 3 h at 800 °C, 5 °C min−1, as heating and cooling conditions were applied during the experiment. The final powders were grinded by pestle and mortar as well as sieved (using a fine mesh of 250 microns) for a homogenous distribution of the powders.

The same previous steps were applied for La1.9CuO4 (d-LCO), La1.9Cu0.9Mo0.1O4-Air (d-LCMO-Air), and La1.9Cu0.9Mo0.1O4-Ar (d-LCMO-Ar). “Air” and “Ar” refer to the annealing atmosphere.

Sample characterization

Using PANalytical Empyrean and under 40 kV accelerating voltage, 35 mA current, CuKα source, and λ = 0.15406 nm, the crystal structures of the prepared powders were detected. Using Quanta FEG 250 field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) the microstructures and morphologies were determined, bulk elemental quantification and compositional mapping were performed via an integrated Energy Dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectrometer operated at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), manufactured by PerkinElmer, was utilized for the surface functional group identification. The electrode preparation conditions: a mixture of 400 µl of high grade ethanol and 3% Nafion (by volume) was prepared, and 10 mg of the targeted electrocatalysts were added to the previously mentioned mixture. To confirm the optimum distribution of particles, they were ultrasonicated and magnetically stirred for 30 min on each device. 1 cm2 of 0.5 mm thickness of Ni foam electrode (NF) was welded with Ag wire and isolated by epoxy as a working electrode. The working electrode was immersed in the mixture (Nafion, electrocatalyst, and ethanol) for 20 s. The loaded electrode was dried at 60 °C for 15 min before the electrochemical tests. Electrochemical measurements: the potentiostat (Metrohom) and three electrode setup working electrode (NF), counter electrode (Pt), and reference electrode (calomel) were used to conduct all the tests for both OER and HER. Using 100 ml of 1 M KOH as a measurement medium for each measurement. Chronopotentiometry, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) were among the electrochemical testing methods. The LSV for OER and HER were carried out with a scan rate of 10 mV s−1 and a fixed applied range of voltage. The long term stability of chronopotentiometry was examined for 50 h, yielding 50 mA cm−2 for the optimized sample. The EIS was performed with a 10 mV amplitude and a 5–100 000 Hz frequency range. Electrochemical results

OER & HER performance

Using the Ni foam (NF) substrate as an electrode, the OER and HER of the different prepared catalysts were tested. Using 100 ml of 1 M KOH solution, a three electrode cell system, and 10 mV s−1, all the data were measured. The various tests showed excellent catalytic activity. The prepared d-LCMO-Ar catalytic activity in OER and HER is much enhanced compared to the other prepared samples due to the material manipulation appearing in the internal induced defects arising from the A-site defects, doping, and the Ar combustion medium, as shown in Fig. 4(A). For our optimized sample, the required overpotential for 10 mA cm−2 and 100 mA cm−2 for OER are 258 mV and 500 mV, and for HER are −117 mV and −323 mV, respectively. Despite the overall samples' overpotential (η) increasing after 10 mA cm−2 due to NF use and the preparation method, they still give excellent overpotential values at the high current density comparable with the state of the art for OER and HER. The main hidden novelty is clearly abbreviated in the high current growth with a sharp slope after 1.58 V (η = 350 mV). 200 mA, 300 mA, and 400 mA can easily be obtained before 1.9 V for the d-LCMO-Ar sample. Since no plateau is seen in Fig. 4(A), indicating a straight proportional connection, it is expected that increasing the potential would increase the current density.35 It suggests that more potential is provided, and the current will grow linearly. This implies that the electrochemical process being studied does not exhibit a clear saturation point within the investigated potential range. The enhanced HER electrochemical activity in the d-LCMO-Air and d-LCMO-Ar samples is attributed to the doping process, as evidenced by their significantly higher current densities compared to the other samples (LCO and d-LCO), which exhibited very low current densities below 10 mA cm−2. Determination of TOF from the integrated OER/HER polarization curve

One of the highly important parameters for the intrinsic activity is the turnover frequency (TOF), which can be calculated based on eqn (9). Several factors will be estimated to finally calculate it.

Determination of surface concentration from CV

The electrochemically active surface sites were estimated from the integrated area of the Ni3+/Ni2+ redox peaks in the cyclic voltammograms.49 The calculated charges for the different samples were 0.53, 2.55, 3.86, and 9.74 mA s, respectively. Based on these values, the total number of electrons transferred was determined using Faraday's law, assuming one electron redox process for the Ni3+/Ni2+ couple. Under this assumption, the number of electrochemically active surface atoms is considered equivalent to the number of transferred electrons. Discussion

The crystallite volume of LCO was 118.6 nm, more than double, and sharply inclined to lower the half and continued decreasing even in the tested samples under electrochemical tests for the samples d-LCO, d-LCMO-Air, and d-LCMO-Ar. The microstrain in LCO (1.5 × 10−3) was positive and increased to be about double (in sample d-LCO), then kept negative and increased in the negative with doped Mo samples, whether in the air or Ar; refer to Fig. 2 and Table 1. It meant that great changes occurred with small changes, such as tuning the structure with 10% of A-sited defects or doping with small amounts under different media. Any of these changes representing a driving force could assist the charge transfer via vacancies to the catalytic site beneficially. The a/c value was quite the same value even when the changes occurred in the fourth digit, except for the sample d-LCMO-Air that confirms a non-pseudo lattice due to the various effects in the prepared compounds and under the high temperature effect for 3 h. It is believed that the diffusion occurred between the contributing particles, whether La, Cu, or Mo, due to an A-site defect or doping, resulting in lattice distortion and particle surface defect creation, and then catalytic active sites increase. These slight changes in the d-LCMO-Ar caused the greatest effective electrocatalytic area. Porous structures formed in the d-LCMO-Air and d-LCMO-Ar with higher homogeneities in the pore size distribution. The influence on electrochemical properties seemed too much of an increment, as mesopores enhance electrolyte access to active sites.

The eqn (10)–(13) discuss the adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM), which is the closest explanation to the occurring reaction over the perovskite oxide, especially in alkaline medium for OER. Starting with the adsorption of OH− ions of water molecules on the perovskite active sites, causing the formation of several intermediates such as peroxide formation, hydroxide deprotonation, then peroxide deprotonation, and hydroxide reformation. These intermediates, such as OH*, OOH*, and O*, are all common, and electrons are described in the referred equations.56 The X in the following refers to the catalytic site.10X + OH− → X–OH* + e−11X–OH* + OH− → X–O* + H2O + e−12X–O* + OH− → X–OOH* + e−13X–OOH* + OH− → X + O2 + H2O + e−

Conclusions

In summary, the optimized sample d-LCMO-Ar showed excellent activity at low onset potential towards OER and HER coupled with the superior stability of 50 h at 50 mA cm−2 current density. Also, 10 mA cm−2 is achieved at 258 mV and −117 mV overpotentials. By checking the ECSA, Nyquist, and Tafel slope measurements, the same trend of data was found. The highest ECSA was 183 cm2, with the lowest Tafel slope value and the smallest semicircle of Nyquist, i.e., the largest double layer capacitance. The optimized sample is strongly recommended in OER and HER scenarios. It's a chance for both OER and HER electrocatalysts and a significant advancement in the research and development of inexpensive, naturally occurring catalysts for general water-splitting. Overall, d-LCMO-Ar showed a synergistic effect resulting from the lattice manipulation by controlling defects, which was revealed in the current density, stability, charge transfer, and adsorption/desorption of the oxygen gas molecules on the working electrode. The greatest output of this work is the importance of the minor ratio of defective sites, and doping by 0.1 of both of them plays a main role in enhancing the current density and reducing the overpotential of the LCO as a pristine sample. This strongly reflects the promising importance of material engineering for inspiring the researchers' work toward a permanent solution for the electrocatalysis process.
