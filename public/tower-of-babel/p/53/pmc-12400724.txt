Biomechanical comparison of patellar cementing techniques in total knee arthroplasty: a biomechanical study
Authors: Alper Emre Dikici, Gokay Eken, Teoman Atici
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to biomechanically compare three different cementing techniques for patellar component fixation in total knee arthroplasty. Materials and methodsSynthetic patellar bone model (Selbones© Research Laboratory, Kayseri, Turkey) was implanted with an all-polyethylene universal dome patellar component (Trauson® universal dome, Stryker, Changzhou, China) using bone cement (Surgical Simplex® P, Stryker, Limerick, Ireland). The test specimens were divided into three groups according to the cementing technique. Group 1 was named as “partial cementing”, Group 2 as “full cementing” and Group 3 as “modification” technique. Modification technique was performed with a 50 cc injector. After fixation of the materials, the strength of fixation was tested biomechanically by applying force with isolated scissors produced using a tensile compression testing machine (UVE, Bursa-Turkey). Curves and peak stress pressures were statistically analyzed to determine which cementing technique provided the best fixation strength.ResultsThe mean peak stress pressure of a total of 30 test specimens was 1305.86 (± 24.72) N (Newton) and these values were determined as 1013.00 (± 24.72), 1334.60 (± 37.17) and 1570.00 (± 26.56) N for group 1, group 2 and group 3, respectively (p < 0.001). A statistically significant difference was found in the multiple comparisons of all groups with each other and fixation of group 3 was found to be better than group 1 and 2 (p < 0.001). ConclusionThe modified cementing technique demonstrated superior biomechanical fixation strength compared to conventional methods in this in vitro study. This low-cost and easily applicable technique may help reduce patellar component-related complications and warrants further evaluation in clinical settings.

Introduction

Patellofemoral joint issues in total knee arthroplasty (TKA), such as patellar component insufficiency, account for about half of the cases that need revision following TKA [1]. Despite the development of implant technology, application features, and established standards, many different causes of failure in the patellar component, which is detected at rates of up to 10%, have been reported [2]. Factors such as male gender, obesity, significant difference in lower limb length, osteoporosis, inflammatory arthritis, patientrelated factors, design of the patellar component, design of the femoral component, type of polyethylene, metal content, sterilization method, compatibility of components, material and design of the implant, as well as differences in surgical technique and application such as trauma and alignment issues, may also contribute to the development of this problem [3]. Various methods are recommended to prevent patellar component insufficiency, which is one of the many patellofemoral joint problems that can arise [4].

Many factors influence the results of the cementing method in orthopedic procedures. These factors include preparing the bone for the prosthesis, mixing the bone cement, and applying the cement. There are various variables involved in this process, such as the viscosity of the bone cement, the patient’s body mass index, the design of the implant, the surface finish, the compatibility of materials, the use of a tourniquet, the time of cement application, the location of application, and the speed of mixing the cement. Despite these numerous variables, there is no definitive best practice for the cementing technique [5]. Studies conducted with both commercial companies producing bone cement and/or implants and orthopedic surgeons performing intensive TKA surgery have reported that there is no agreement on the best cementing technique. These differences in practice could potentially lead to problems [6].

There are very few publications and surgeons do not have a consensus on the parameters that need to be considered in the technique of cementing the patellar component, such as bone preparation, the method of applying cement to the bone and component, and the amount and duration of pressure applied with the clamp [7]. This study aimed to biomechanically compare various techniques for cementing the patellar component for fixation. Our main hypothesis is that making modifications using low-cost, practical, and readily available materials during the preparation and application of bone cement, produced using conventional methods, can enhance the biomechanical strength of patellar component implantation.

Materials and methods

A synthetic model of the patellar bone (Selbones® Research Laboratory, Kayseri, Turkey) was utilized in this biomechanical study. The Trauson® (Stryker, Changzhou, China) brand all-polyethylene universal dome patellar component was utilized as the patellar component of the knee prosthesis, and Surgical Simplex® P (Stryker, Limerick, Ireland) bone cement was utilized for fixation. The bone model is created in line with the properties of 3rd generation human tissue and has the actual load capacity that human bone can endure for biomechanical testing. The product is completely suitable for the anatomical structure. The patellar component has a symmetrical, round dome design with three pegs and is entirely constructed from ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE). The bone cement utilized provides greater penetration and superior fatigue resistance compared to high and low viscosity cements due to its medium viscosity feature.

Application of the patellar component: Cement components were kept in a dark place, below 25 °C and away from light, in accordance with the company’s recommendations. The surgical procedure was performed in a room where the temperature was 18 °C and the humidity was 45% to ensure operating room conditions. The patella joint surface was prepared with component-specific surgical instruments (Trauson® Patella Set, Stryker,

Limerick, Ireland) in accordance with the surgery protocol recommended by the company (Triathlon® Knee System Surgical Protocol Stryker, Limerick, Ireland). The remaining bone thickness after the incision was ensured to be 13–15 mm. In the measurements made in all groups, the size of the appropriate patella component was determined as size 5 (Fig. 1). For Group 1 and Group 2, the previously recommended mixing technique was applied for the bone cement mixing stage [9]. According to this; During the mixing and waiting phase, following mixing with the mixing rod at 3 strokes per second for 1 min, the remaining air bubbles in the cement were waited for up to 3 min to come to the surface and the cement to reach a non-sticky dough consistency (application phase). After the bone cement reached the consistency of non-sticky dough (application phase), the patellar components were implanted into the bone with the cement technique determined according to the grouping. Cement application began immediately after this 3-minute interval. In Group 3, the cement was loaded into the 50 cc injector within 30 s of reaching the application phase and delivered under pressure within the following 60 s. The time interval from mixing to final application did not exceed 5 min for any group.

In Group 1, with the method defined by Stulberg et al. and called partial cementing, cement was placed only in the holes in the patellar bone and on the bone surface by hand (hand packing) [8]. Cement was placed by applying pressure with the fingers. In Group 2, called full cementation, bone cement was applied to the holes in the patellar bone and the entire back surface of the patellar component using the “hand packing” technique (Fig. 2). Cement was placed by applying pressure with fingers. In Group 3, cement was applied with the injector technique (Abdul-Jabar et al., 2018a). By pushing with a piston, cement was applied under pressure to the holes prepared on the patellar bone surface and to the entire bone surface. Then, cement was applied under pressure into the grooves of the patellar component and around the peg (Fig. 3).

In all groups, implantation was performed and the component was fixed by applying 42 pounds of pressure with a clamp for 15 min (Fig. 4). After implantation, 48 h were waited for the bone cement to freeze completely. In our study, shear forces, which have been shown to play a primary role in the development of patellar component failure in TKA and have been used in many different biomechanical studies, were preferred as the force to be applied to the patellar component [10].

Biomechanical test

To perform the biomechanical shear failure test, the test procedures described in the literature were applied [11]. For this purpose, the implanted bones were processed 48 h after implantation so that they could be fixed to the machine that would apply biomechanical shear force. For this process; A 2-inch diameter polyvinyl chloride (PVC) pipe was cut into 2.5-inch long pieces, and cellophane tape was applied to one end of each piece to create a watertight seal. Next, PVC pipe measuring 1 inch in diameter was cut to an appropriate length to create a base for the patellar structure (to be inserted into the larger pipe). The length of the base was measured with the patellar bone immersed in 2.5-inch PVC pipe with the polyethylene component exposed and parallel to the top of the pipe. Sealant was placed to prevent the patellar structure from swinging on the base. The pipe was then filled with Tiechem G500, a structural adhesive. The resulting sample was left to dry for 48 h before testing.

After allowing the test sample to dry for 48 h, biomechanical testing was performed to evaluate the fixation strength of the implantation. To apply the isolated shear force, a metal “C”-shaped mold and device (UVE® tensile compression testing machine, Bursa-Turkey) was used, which completely fits the upper part of the patellar component. Mounting to the test machine was provided with connection bracket and screws. The axial displacement of the testing machine was set at 5 mm per minute. Then, shear force was applied directly to the patellar implant (Fig. 5). The load-displacement curve was recorded until the bone-cement interface failed. Curves and peak stress pressures were analyzed to determine which cementing technique provides the best fixation strength.

Statistical analysis

G*Power 3.1.9.7 program was used to determine the sample size. When the effect size of the pressure difference between the groups in the study was determined as 0.60, it was found that a total of 30 bones, 10 in each group, should be included in the study at 80% power and 0.05 significance level. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to examine whether the data showed normal distribution. For normally distributed data, one-way analysis of variance was applied for comparisons of more than two groups. If significance was found, the Bonferroni test, one of the multiple comparison tests, was used. The significance level was determined as p < 0.05. Statistical analysis of the data was performed in the statistical package program IBM SPSS 28.0 (IBM Corp. Released 2021 IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.).

Results

The peak stress pressures recorded for each sample are presented in Table 1. The mean peak stress pressure values for Group 1 (partial cementing), Group 2 (full cementing), and Group 3 (modified cementing) were 1013.0 ± 24.7 N, 1334.6 ± 37.1 N, and 1570.0 ± 26.5 N, respectively. These findings indicate that the fixation strength was lowest in Group 1 and highest in Group 3. The individual measurements for each sample within the three groups demonstrate consistent increases in peak stress across the techniques, with Group 3 showing the most uniform and elevated values.

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed a statistically significant difference in mean peak stress pressures among the three groups (F(2,27) = 869.169, p < 0.001). The assumption of homogeneity of variances was confirmed (p > 0.05), validating the use of ANOVA.

Post-hoc comparisons using the Bonferroni method (Table 2) showed that all pairwise differences between the groups were statistically significant (p < 0.001). Specifically, the mean difference between Group 1 and Group 2 was − 321.60 N (95% CI: − 355.83 to − 287.36), between Group 1 and Group 3 was − 557.00 N (95% CI: − 591.23 to − 522.76), and between Group 2 and Group 3 was − 235.40 N (95% CI: − 269.63 to − 201.16). These results confirm that the modified cementing technique in Group 3 provides significantly greater shear fixation strength compared to both conventional techniques.

Discussion

In this study, the effects of different cementing techniques on the biomechanical durability of patellar component fixation under shear force in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) were evaluated. The results showed that the peak stress pressures at which component failure occurred were significantly higher in the full cementing technique compared to the partial cementing technique. Furthermore, the modified cementing technique demonstrated significantly greater fixation strength than both the full and partial cementing methods.

The modified cementing technique offers several clinical advantages. It is simple to apply, cost-effective, and does not require specialized equipment beyond a standard 50 cc injector. These features make it highly accessible for routine intraoperative use. Moreover, pressure-assisted cement delivery may help standardize cement application, potentially reducing variability among different operators. However, it requires familiarity with the injector’s handling characteristics, which could represent a minor learning curve for some surgeons.

The all-polyethylene, three-peg patellar component is widely regarded as the gold standard in TKA. Given the small articular surface area of the patella and its exposure to biomechanical forces up to 20 times body weight during activity [12], the fixation strength of the implant is a critical determinant of clinical success. Accordingly, the cementing technique used for patellar fixation must be applied with precision and adhere to well-defined principles. However, there is a lack of consensus in the literature regarding key technical parameters—such as bone preparation, cement application method, and the amount and duration of clamping pressure—that affect patellar component fixation [7, 10]. Although various cementing techniques have been described, including hand packing, use of vacuum mixers, cement guns, injectors, pulsatile lavage, and subchondral pressurization, no single method has been supported by strong evidence as superior.

There are many factors that need to be considered and investigated in the patellar cementing technique. Wagner et al. conducted a cadaveric study to investigate how cement curing time and clamping pressure affect the shear strength of patellar component fixation in TKA [11]. Their findings demonstrated that allowing the cement to cure for 8 min prior to implantation significantly enhanced fixation strength compared to a 2-minute curing time. Additionally, applying moderate pressure (42 pounds) during fixation was biomechanically superior to higher pressure (62 pounds), which was associated with weaker fixation. These results emphasize the importance of optimizing both the timing of cement application and the amount of pressure applied to the patellar component. In alignment with these findings, our study also highlights that not only the cementing technique but also the delivery method and application parameters can substantially influence the stability of patellar component fixation.

Stulberg et al. reported a case of bilateral patellar component failure caused by fatigue fractures of the fixation pegs in a highly cross-linked all-polyethylene patellar design. To better understand this failure mechanism, they developed a novel biomechanical test protocol that simulated cyclic compression and shear loading, allowing assessment of the effects of peg orientation and cementing technique on patellar component fatigue strength. Their findings revealed that while peg orientation had minimal impact, insufficient cement application—particularly the absence of cement in the backside patellar groove—substantially reduced shear fatigue resistance. Notably, when the groove was fully cemented, the shear fatigue strength exceeded the estimated in vivo loading forces. These results underscore the critical role of cement distribution, especially around the peg-groove interface, in maintaining long-term patellar fixation and preventing mechanical failure—findings that are in concordance with the outcomes of our modified cementing technique, which emphasized complete pressurized cement delivery to both the bone and component surfaces [8].

The amount of load on the patellofemoral joint; It is known that it increases up to 20 times when carrying loads, going up and down stairs, squatting and jumping [13, 14]. It can be predicted that these activities, which are indispensable movements of daily life, will continue to increase after TKA application. Although studies have reported that many different factors such as trauma, surgical technical error, and malalignment play a role in patellar component failure after TKA, factors such as load bearing, repetitive climbing up and down stairs, squatting movement, and high body weight (BW) have been shown to be effective in the development of this problem [15, 16]. The cause of failure in all-polyethylene patellar components appears to be shear forces, especially along the patellofemoral joint.

High shear forces cause implant failure at the interface between the patellar bone and the patellar component, which is the weakest part of the structure [16]. In our study, biomechanical shear forces were preferred to create patellar component failure.

Synthetic Bone Models are artificial composite bones designed to simulate bone architecture as well as the physical properties of bone. Real bone has a unique internal architecture and viscoelastic properties. On the other hand, the synthetic bone model offers different advantages. It is consistent in size, strength and density and can be modeled in almost any shape. It does not require special storage techniques or ethics committee approval, and its cost is low. Comparability and reproducibility of results and ease of test standardization are important factors. In studies comparing similar operation techniques or implants, the use of synthetic bone is more recommended [17]. Although cadaver models are the gold standard simulators, they have significant drawbacks such as risk of disease transmission, high cost, cultural limitations, deterioration over time, storage requirements, limited availability of suppliers, shipping regulations, sample-to-sample variability, potential toxicity, and long preparation time, all of which impair their usability. are the influencing factors. Synthetic bone models have been validated with a high level of evidence in the orthopedic literature as one of the options used to simulate surgery [18]. Schwarze et al. compared 3 different femoral component cementing techniques on 9 synthetic femoral bone models and used computerized tomography to evaluate cement penetration [19]. It has been determined that cementing provides deeper cement penetration when done with a pressure applicator. Vanlommel et al. compared 5 different cementing techniques on 25 synthetic tibia bone models and found that the optimal cement penetration amount (3–5 mm) was exceeded in the group where the cement gun was used [20]. They also showed that applying cement only to the tibial component side instead of both surfaces caused insufficient cement penetration. No study has been found in the literature that evaluates the biomechanical strength of the patellar component cementing technique using a synthetic patellar bone model. It was concluded that this bone model could be used in future studies.

This study has several limitations. First, as an in vitro biomechanical investigation, it does not replicate the complex biological environment and dynamic loading conditions present in vivo. Factors such as bone quality variability, bleeding, soft tissue interaction, and physiological joint movement were not represented in the synthetic model. Second, only shear force was applied to assess fixation strength, whereas the patellofemoral joint is subject to multi-directional forces during daily activities. Shear force was selected as the focus of our biomechanical testing because numerous studies have identified it as the predominant mechanical factor contributing to patellar component failure in total knee arthroplasty. For example, Beck et al. [10] and Wagner et al. [11] demonstrated that shear stresses at the cement-implant interface are primarily responsible for loosening or failure of patellar components. These forces occur frequently during activities involving knee flexion and load transfer through the patellofemoral joint. While we acknowledge that compressive and torsional forces also act on the patella, our experimental setup was designed specifically to isolate and analyze shear force due to its recognized clinical relevance and reproducibility in in vitro models. Third, the cementing procedure was standardized and performed under idealized laboratory conditions, which may not fully reflect the variability encountered in surgical practice. Additionally, the absence of long-term cyclic loading prevents conclusions regarding fatigue failure over time. Despite these limitations, the synthetic bone model provided reproducible conditions for comparing cementing techniques, offering valuable insights for future clinical translation.

Conclusion

The modified cementing technique yielded significantly higher fixation strength than both partial and full cementing methods in this biomechanical model. Its favorable mechanical performance, combined with its practical applicability and low cost, suggests that it may represent a viable alternative to conventional techniques. These findings warrant validation through further in vivo and clinical investigations.
