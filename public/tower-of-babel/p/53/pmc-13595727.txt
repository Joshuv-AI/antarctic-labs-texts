Accuracy of teeth positioning in bonded three-dimensionally printed complete dentures: an in vitro study
Authors: Tan Zhi Hui Janice, Chia Ai Ping Vanessa, Chow Kye Sern Bryan, Yu Na
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWhile the mechanical and material properties of 3D printed denture components have been extensively studied, the accuracy implications of the assembly process, specifically the bonding of separately printed teeth onto the denture base, remain insufficiently investigated. Positional distortion introduced at this stage could potentially result in downstream occlusal discrepancies. This study therefore investigated the accuracy of different 3D printing materials, methods (monoblock versus assembled) and assembly configurations (complete arch versus segmented) in the fabrication of 3D printed dentures.MethodsFour groups of dentures (M, T, F, S; n = 15) were fabricated. M and T were printed monoblock units using model resin and tooth-coloured resin, respectively. F and S involved separate printing of denture bases and teeth, followed by bonding. Teeth were fabricated as a full arch in F and in three segments in S. All specimens were produced under standardised conditions. Digitized samples compared to a reference model using a surface-matching software. Tooth positional deviations were assessed as a complete arch and in sextants. Data was analysed using two-way analysis of variance and post-hoc tests (α = 0.05).ResultsFor complete arch analysis, Groups M and T demonstrated significantly lower deviations than Groups F and S (p = 0.001), with no significant differences within monoblock or assembled groups (p > 0.05). Sextant analysis showed consistent findings across all regions, with monoblock groups demonstrating superior accuracy.ConclusionMonoblock 3D-printed dentures exhibited greater positional accuracy than assembled dentures. Although deviations were within clinically acceptable thresholds, further clinical studies are required to determine their impact on occlusion and patient outcomes.

Introduction

The conventional workflow for fabricating complete dentures is a time-consuming and labour-intensive process that is highly technique-sensitive. This multi-step process is prone to errors at various stages, including investing, boil-out, packing, curing, and post-processing. Additionally, material shrinkage and expansion contribute to misfit, resulting in a cumulative negative impact on denture accuracy [1, 2] .

Recent advancements in digital workflows and fabrication techniques such as milling and additive manufacturing (3D Printing), have shown promise in addressing some of the limitations associated with conventional denture fabrication [3]. Milled dentures have demonstrated superior mechanical strength and fit accuracy [4, 5] however, their production is limited by high costs, long fabrication times, and material waste, which limit scalability. In contrast, 3D printed dentures offer advantages including reduced production time, cost-effectiveness, and the ability to fabricate complex geometries such as undercut regions [6]. Nevertheless, the accuracy of 3D printed dentures has been reported to be inferior to milled dentures [7, 8] and comparable to conventionally fabricated dentures [9–11]. The accuracy of 3D printed dentures is influenced by numerous factors, including light intensity, layer thickness, print orientation, software parameters, support structure design and post-processing protocols [12]. In addition, inherent material properties such as polymerization shrinkage and anisotropic behaviour combined with the cumulative effects of layer-by-layer fabrication may introduce distortion and dimensional inaccuracies. These deviations can propagate throughout the build process, potentially resulting in clinically significant discrepancies in the final prosthesis. Despite these limitations, the potential benefits of 3D printing in denture fabrication are substantial [13], and further research is needed to optimize printing parameters and establish best practices for achieving clinically acceptable outcomes. Beyond overall denture base accuracy, a critical yet underexplored aspect is the positional fidelity of the denture teeth relative to the denture base. Deviations in tooth position can result in occlusal disharmony, necessitating extensive chairside adjustment, altering the occlusal vertical dimension, and potentially compromising masticatory efficiency and patient comfort.

Most commercially available 3D printers are limited to processing a single material at a time, necessitating separate fabrication of denture bases and teeth, followed by manual assembly using light-cured bonding procedures [14–16]. This manual process introduces an additional potential source of positional error. Factors such as the amount of relief between the tooth intaglio surface and the denture base socket, coupling depth and angulation connector design, build orientation, and segmentation strategy may all influence the accuracy of tooth positioning and consequently, occlusal trueness [7, 16, 17].

This study investigates the accuracy of denture fabrication using different 3D printing materials, fabrication methods (monoblock versus assembled) and assembly configurations (full-arch splinted versus segmented denture teeth). It is hypothesed that: (1) monoblock printed dentures will demonstrate greater tooth positional accuracy than assembled dentures, and (2) among assembled dentures, full-arch splinted teeth will demonstrate greater accuracy than segmented teeth.

Materials and methods

This in vitro study utilized a fully anonymized construction file of a maxillary complete denture in standard tessellation language (STL) format generated using a design software (3Shape Dental System, Copenhagen, Denmark) as the digital reference model. Four experimental groups (M, T, F, S) were established based on the method of fabrication (Fig. 1). In Group M, both the denture base and teeth were printed as a single unit (Dental Model Resin, Star3D Material Development Pte Ltd). In Group T, the denture was similarly printed as a single piece, but with a tooth-coloured resin (Temporary Crown Resin-VITA Shade A2, Star3D Material Development Pte Ltd). In Group F, the denture base and the complete arch of teeth were printed separately. In Group S, the denture base and teeth were also printed separately, with the denture teeth printed in three segments (Right posterior teeth, Anterior teeth and Left posterior teeth). For Groups F and S, denture bases were printed with a pink denture base resin (Denture Base Light, Star3D Material Development Pte Ltd) while the denture teeth were fabricated using a tooth-coloured resin (Temporary Crown Resin-VITA Shade A2, Star3D Material Development Pte Ltd) (Fig. 2). A total of 15 dentures per group were fabricated using the 3D printer (MINI III Printer, Star3D Material Development Company (Singapore) Pte Ltd). All specimens were fabricated in a single production batch and printed under a controlled and identical environmental conditions (temperature 23 ± 1 °C; relative humidity 50 ± 5%) and processed by a single operator in the manufacturing company following their recommended settings to ensure procedural consistency and to minimize inter-batch and operator-dependent variability.

Printing parameters were standardized across all groups in accordance with manufacturer-recommended settings (Layer thickness: 50 μm; Light source wavelength: 405 nm; Exposure time er layer: 2.5 s). All specimens were printed at a 45° build orientation relative.

to the platform. Support structures placed at the cameo surfaces of the denture base and on the occlusal surfaces of the denture when printed separately. This orientation was selected as it has been shown to provide an optimal balance between dimensional accuracy, surface quality, reduction of layer stair-stepping effects on critical surfaces and support distribution in denture fabrication [18].

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software for Windows version 3.1.9.4 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on a one-way analysis of variance design with four groups, a large effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.40) was assumed, consistent with previous reported differences in denture fabrication accuracy [6]. With a significance level of α = 0.05 and a desired statistical power of 95% statistical (1- β = 0.95), the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 15 specimens per group.

After the completion of the printing process, all specimens underwent a standardized post-processing protocol. Specimens were rinsed twice in a 96% ethanol solution in an ultrasonic bath for 5 min per cycle to remove uncured resin. This was followed by post-curing using a light-curing unit (MINOR, Star3D Material Development Company (Singapore) Pte Ltd) for 30 min at a wavelength of 405 nm, as per manufacturer recommendations.

The support structures were then removed manually using rotary instruments by a single operator. In Groups F and S, the denture teeth were assembled onto the corresponding denture base using a standardized bonding protocol. A thin layer of uncured liquid resin was applied to the intaglio surface of the denture teeth prior to seating. The teeth were seated into their corresponding sockets under controlled finger pressure applied by a single operator. Complete seating was verified visually by ensuring intimate adaptation between the denture teeth and socket surfaces with no visible gaps. Excess resin was removed followed by an additional light-curing cycle of 20 min at 405 nm to ensure complete polymerization of the bonding interface.

The printed dentures were digitized using a laboratory desktop scanner (E3 Red, 3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark). The STL files were imported to the surface matching software (Geomagic Control X 2024.2.0 [Essentials], 3D systems, USA) to evaluate the accuracy of the teeth positions in the dentures in all the groups in comparison to the reference digital model. The alignment and superimposition of the denture bases of samples to the reference denture were performed in 2 stages: (1) an initial alignment using volume-based registration of the denture bases and (2) a refined alignment using the best fit algorithm to measure the maximum fit of the denture base. Alignment was restricted to the denture base to isolate deviations in the denture tooth position. The deviations of tooth positions were evaluated as a complete arch as well as in sextants (Right posterior, Anterior and Left posterior) (Fig. 3). The study protocol was as shown in Fig. 4. Three-dimensional deviation mapping was generated with a tolerance threshold of ± 0.01 mm and a critical threshold of ± 0.1 mm (Fig. 5). Accuracy was assessed in terms of trueness, defined as the deviation of each specimen from the reference model, expressed as root mean square (RMS) values and precision, assessed as the variability within each group and expressed as the standard deviation of RMS values.

To assess the repeatability of the scanning and alignment protocol, a specimen was randomly selected and scanned ten times under identical conditions. The resulting datasets were independently aligned to the reference model using the same two-step alignment protocol. The deviation between repeated measurements was quantified using RMS values. The mean difference between repeated scans was 0.003 mm ± 0.002, indicating high repeatability of the scanning and alignment procedure.

Statistical analysis was performed with IBM SPSS Statistics Version, (IBM SPSS, Inc., Armon, NY, USA) Version 31.0.0.0 (117) for MacOs. Normality was evaluated using Shapiro-Wilk test and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. All data satisfied the assumptions required for parametric testing. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine whether there was a difference between the 4 groups (M, T, F, S) for the complete arch comparison. Tukey comparison was used as a post hoc test in case of significance. The significance level was set at p ≤ 0.05. For the analysis of the denture teeth in sextants, two-way ANOVA and post-hoc Tukey tests were performed. The independent variables were GROUP (M, T, F, S) and LOCATION (Anterior, Right posterior and Left posterior). The dependent variable was root mean square (RMS) values. In addition to p-values, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to estimate the precision of group differences. Effect sizes (η²) were also reported to quantify the magnitude of differences between groups.

Discussion

The results of this study supported the initial hypothesis, with monoblock dentures (Group M, T) demonstrating significantly higher accuracy than assembled dentures (Group F, S).This finding is consistent with previous studies [17, 19]. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the observed differences reflect statistical significance, and their direct impact on clinical outcomes was not evaluated in this study. As such, the translation of these differences into meaningful clinical benefit remains uncertain.

Within the monoblock groups, no significant difference in accuracy was observed between the different types of resin used, suggesting that material selection may be based on other factors such as cost, aesthetic colour matching, and clinical indication. However, this interpretation is limited by the narrow range of materials evaluated, and resin-specific dimensional stability over time was not assessed. The inclusion of monoblock groups enabled evaluation of the inherent accuracy of the printing process independent of assembly-related variables. This distinction is relevant as emerging workflows utilizing monoblock dentures with subsequent surface characterization are gaining traction. While such approaches may reduce procedural steps, their clinical effectiveness requires further validation.

Contrary to the initial hypothesis, segmented assembly (Group S) demonstrated slightly better accuracy than splinted one-piece assembly (Group F) [16, 20], although the difference was not statistically significant. Several factors may contribute to this observation. Varying socket geometry between anterior and posterior teeth may influence seating accuracy and smaller segmented units may be less susceptible to cumulative distortion effects. Polymerization shrinkage, a known source of dimensional inaccuracy in additively manufactured resins, may be more pronounced in larger, splinted units due to increased material volume. Previous studies on multi-unit 3D printed restorations have reported increasing distortion with restoration span, particularly beyond five to six units, suggesting that size-dependent inaccuracies may play a role [21]. Shrinkage was found to be more concentrated on occlusal surfaces, which are known to be more error-prone, and larger restorations were consistently less accurate regardless of printer type [22]. In the context of this study, the larger volume of a full arch splinted unit may therefore render it more susceptible to cumulative shrinkage-related distortion compared to smaller segmented units, though these explanations remain hypothetical and require direct experimental validation.

The occlusal deviation analysis revealed predominantly positive discrepancies, indicating a tendency towards an increased occlusal vertical dimension (OVD). This may be influenced by factors such as inadequate relief within the tooth sockets, inaccuracies in support structure removal or incomplete seating during assembly. The removal of support structures represents an operator-dependent variable that may introduce localized surface irregularities and affect seating accuracy. In addition, scanner accuracy limitations must be considered, as deviations captured in digital superimposition analyses may partially reflect measurement error rather than true manufacturing discrepancies.

The optimal glue space for digital denture design remains uncertain, with varying recommendations in the literature, ranging from 0.1 mm to 0.25 mm. Yamamoto et al. [15] recommended 0.10 to 0.25 mm for the maxillary first molar, 0.25 mm for the maxillary first premolar, 0.15 and 0.25 mm for the maxillary canine, and 0.15 to 0.25 mm for the maxillary central incisor whereas Neena et al. [23] reported 0.1 mm to be the most accurate for individual tooth assembly. In this study, a uniform offset of 0.1 mm was used. However, the use of a single global parameter may not account for anatomical variation across tooth types. Inadequate or excessive spacing may influence seating, bonding thickness and final occlusal outcomes. Further investigation is necessary to determine optimal site-specific parameters, particularly for different assembly strategies such as full arch, segmented and individual placement.

The mean RMS values in the monoblock groups M and T (0.142 mm and 0.138 mm respectively) were similar to those reported in other studies [6, 16, 19, 24] whereas the RMS values in the assembled groups F and S demonstrated higher deviations (0.290 mm and 0.246 mm respectively). Although all these values fall below previously reported thresholds of 0.5 mm at the occlusal surface [25]. These values may not be directly comparable due to different fabrication methods, material and assembly protocol. While oral soft tissue resilience may compensate for minor discrepancies, small occlusal deviations can have amplified effects [26]. Mahler et al. reported that a distortion of 0.25 mm can lead to a 1 mm increase in occlusal vertical dimension (OVD) per arch, potentially resulting in up to 2 mm of OVD alteration when both arches are considered, which can have notable clinical implications [2].

The relatively large standard deviations observed in the assembled groups F and S may carry important implications for clinical reproducibility. This variability means that while some assembled dentures may achieve accuracy approaching that of monobloc units, others within the same fabrication batch may deviate substantially. This inconsistency is particularly problematic in routine clinical practice, where predictable and reproducible outcomes are essential for efficient workflow planning and patient management. The sources of this variability are likely multifactorial, encompassing differences in support structure removal, bonding resin volume, seating pressure, and stabilisation during polymerisation which were performed manually in this study. The lack of standardisation across these steps means that operator technique inevitably introduces case-to-case variation. This underscores the need for more tightly controlled assembly protocols to reduce variability and improve the reliability of assembled digital dentures in clinical settings.

A further upstream source of variability that may have contributed to the occlusal discrepancies observed in this study, relates to the printing orientation and support placement strategy adopted during fabrication of the denture teeth themselves. There is a lack of published data specifically examining ideal printing parameters for denture teeth, as the majority of existing literature has focused on denture base print orientation rather than the teeth themselves. In the absence of evidence-based consensus, clinicians and technicians are largely guided by manufacturer technical specifications. A review of currently available guidance from three prominent and widely adopted digital denture systems reveals not only variation between platforms, but in some cases fundamentally opposing philosophies regarding which surface of the denture teeth should bear the support structures during printing.

The Dentsply Sirona Lucitone Digital Print Illustrated Technique Guide for Asiga printers recommends orienting the full arch so that the basal ridge-lap surface faces the build platform — at 25 degrees for a full arch, 30 degrees for posterior segments, and 45 degrees for anterior segments. The occlusal surface is therefore left free of support structures in this workflow [27]. In direct contrast, the Formlabs digital denture guide recommends positioning the tooth arch at a 0 to 25 degree angle to the build platform and states explicitly that “the occlusal surface of the teeth can face towards or away from the build platform, depending on preference in finishing the part,” with supports generated on whichever surface faces the platform [28]. The Rapidshape Print Studio workflow guide recommends that the tooth arch be printed with the occlusal and incisal surfaces facing downward toward the build platform as the primary orientation, with supports placed on the occlusal and incisal surfaces by design [29].

There are trade-offs for each workflow, when supports are placed on the basal mating surface, their removal introduces variability at the seating interface. Over-removal of support material risks over-seating of the tooth within the socket, effectively reducing the functional glue space and potentially causing the tooth to sit lower than designed. Conversely, under-removal prevents full seating, raising the tooth above its intended position and directly increasing the occlusal vertical dimension. The resulting surface irregularity also makes it difficult to achieve a uniform adhesive layer across the socket floor, introducing further positional unpredictability at the bonding stage. When supports are instead placed on the occlusal surface, the basal mating area is preserved in its printed state and the flat occlusal surface of the printed segment provides a convenient and even platform for applying seating pressure during the curing process. However, support remnants on the occlusal surface, even after careful removal, may contribute directly to positive occlusal deviations and elevated RMS values. Residual surface irregularities following support removal from the occlusal surface are particularly consequential as they affect the very surface against which occlusal accuracy is measured and against which the opposing arch will occlude.

In this study, supports were placed on the occlusal surfaces of the separately printed denture teeth, consistent with the approach of preserving the basal mating interface. It is therefore plausible that residual surface irregularities following occlusal support removal contributed to the predominantly positive occlusal deviations observed in the assembled groups. The relative contribution of this factor compared to other assembly variables such as glue space design, bonding resin volume, and seating pressure cannot be determined from the current data. The choice of support placement strategy represents a meaningful and currently unstandardised variable in the digital denture assembly workflow. Comparative studies directly evaluating the effect of support placement location on the occlusal accuracy of assembled digital dentures are currently lacking, and their development would represent a meaningful and clinically relevant contribution to the optimisation of digital denture workflows.

From a clinical perspective, inaccuracies in denture fabrication may increase the burden of chairside adjustments, including occlusal refinement and potential remount procedures. In conventional denture fabrication, processing errors are routinely managed through a laboratory remount and selective spot grinding prior to the clinical appointment, allowing occlusal discrepancies to be identified and corrected before the prosthesis reaches the patient. Digital denture workflows, however, present a particular challenge in this regard. Unlike conventional workflows where physical casts and an articulator are available throughout the fabrication process, cast-less digital workflows — whether milled or printed — require additional steps to enable laboratory remounting to the physical articulator. García-Zea and Revilla-León described a technique utilising two magnet-retained, printed remounting cast systems to enable laboratory remounting of digitally fabricated complete dentures within a cast-less workflow, noting that this approach can minimise occlusal adjustments at the delivery appointment [30]. However, the authors acknowledged that studies are needed to assess the efficiency and accuracy of such remounting procedures compared with conventional methods. In the context of assembled 3D-printed dentures specifically, discrepancies arising from the assembly process — including those attributable to support removal variability and bonding technique — may be particularly difficult to anticipate and correct, as they are introduced after the digital design stage and are therefore not captured in the virtual articulation. Variability introduced during manual steps such as support removal and bonding may further affect reproducibility in routine clinical practice, potentially resulting in additional clinical visits, increased adjustment time, and compromise of occlusal morphology and aesthetics. The development of validated remounting protocols specifically adapted to the assembled digital denture workflow, and their evaluation against conventional remounting standards, represents an important and currently unmet clinical need.

This study has several limitations. As an in-vitro investigation, it does not account for intraoral factors such as saliva, mucosal resilience, functional loading and patient specific variability. The use of a single printer and a single software platform limits generalizability as accuracy may vary across different technologies and workflows. Only a limited number of materials were evaluated and their long-term dimensional stability was not assessed. Additionally, manual removal of support structures may introduce variability in surface integrity and tooth positioning and represents a potential source of operator-dependent error. Furthermore, there is currently no published consensus on ideal printing parameters specifically for denture teeth, including print orientation, support placement location, and post-processing conditions. Available manufacturer guidelines differ in their recommendations, and the printing protocol adopted in this study reflects one of several plausible approaches rather than an established evidence-based standard. Findings may therefore differ under alternative printing configurations, and this represents a significant gap in the current literature. The bonding protocol was also not standardized in terms of resin volume, seating pressure or stabilized during polymerization, introducing potential operator-dependent variability. Future research should include clinical validation studies to assess the real-world applicability of these findings. Further work is also needed to optimize assembly parameters, including glue-space design and bonding protocols to improve reproducibility and better isolate the effect of the assembly method.

Conclusion

Within the limits of this in vitro study, printed monoblock dentures demonstrated higher accuracy than assembled dentures under the tested conditions. Nevertheless, the interpretation of “clinical acceptability” should be approached with caution, as laboratory findings may not fully reflect clinical performance.

While not establishing definitive clinical superiority, these results point to the potential importance of optimizing assembly workflows to enhance denture accuracy. In particular, the optimisation of assembly parameters, including glue space design, bonding protocol standardisation, and printing orientation with respect to support placement strategy, represents a critical and currently unresolved area that warrants dedicated investigation. The absence of published consensus on ideal printing parameters for denture teeth, combined with conflicting guidance across manufacturer platforms, highlights a meaningful gap that future research should address. The development of validated, standardised protocols for denture teeth fabrication and assembly, analogous to the established remount procedures in conventional denture workflows, would be a meaningful step towards improving the reliability and clinical predictability of digitally assembled complete dentures. Further clinical investigations are needed to determine the relevance of these findings in patient care settings. and future studies incorporating functional loading, multi-material and multi-printer comparisons, and long-term dimensional stability assessments are needed before broader clinical recommendations can be made.
