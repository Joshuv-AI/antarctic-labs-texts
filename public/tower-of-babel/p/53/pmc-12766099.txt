A Roadmap to Improved Quality Outcomes in Neonates Exposed to Illegally Manufactured Fentanyl
Authors: Cristiane G Lin, Christine Wade, Becky Micetic, Pooja Rangan, Natalie Wade, Vrinda Arora
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe incidence of neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) has dramatically increased, and illegally manufactured fentanyl may be a significant contributor. The optimal treatment approach for an infant experiencing severe withdrawal from fentanyl is unknown. Our aim was to decrease the mean length of stay (LOS) by 20% over 19 months for infants with NOWS by implementing a transitional phase before establishing our modified Eat, Sleep, Console (ESC) model of care.Study designA multidisciplinary team used quality improvement methodology to improve treatment of NOWS in 2 phases, each adapted to fentanyl's complex pharmacology. Beginning May 2023, a hybrid Finnegan/ESC protocol was implemented to address hospital barriers for a year before initiating full ESC management. Data were collected on infants >35 weeks of gestational age admitted to a level III neonatal intensive care unit. Primary metrics included LOS and total days/doses of morphine. Rates of apnea and naloxone were used as balancing measures. The LOS was plotted on a statistical process control chart and the data were summarized with descriptive statistics.ResultsOutcomes were analyzed for 217 infants. The aim was exceeded with a mean LOS reduction of 29% (hybrid) and 49% (ESC). On average, decreased morphine days (22.5, 11.2, 6.9, P < .001) and doses (162, 63, 14, P < .001) also were observed. No significant apnea occurred, nor was naloxone used.ConclusionsThis quality improvement project provides a successful fentanyl-adapted ESC model that may be adopted to decrease postnatal morphine usage and LOS.Keywords: newborn, morphine, length of stay, intensive care units, neonatal, opioid

In the current US opioid epidemic, overdose from illegally manufactured fentanyl (IMF) remains a predominant cause of death. Nationally, education and abatement efforts are ongoing.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 This increase in ultrapotent opioids like IMF has led to use during pregnancy, subsequently increasing neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS) or specifically, neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS).1,6,7 Consequently, the incidence of NAS in our state from 2009 to 2022 increased nearly 6-fold up, to 11.1/1000 infants hospitalized.8 In response, the state's Department of Health developed a NAS Action Plan providing resources for pregnant persons with opioid use disorder (OUD) as well as leading our hospital system to prioritize implementation of the Eat, Sleep, Console (ESC) model of care.9,10

Fentanyl, and its analogs, are synthetic, high-binding full agonists of the mu opioid receptor. They are highly lipophilic and quickly cross the blood-brain barrier, resulting in rapid onset of action. Moreover, they are ultrapotent (100 to 10 000-fold more than morphine), have high abuse potential, and more severe withdrawal symptoms.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Because of the severity of fentanyl withdrawal, modifications to adult medication protocols for OUD were necessary. To achieve improvement in symptoms, the doses of methadone or buprenorphine required rapid escalation and higher maintenance doses.12,13,19, 20, 21, 22 Similar to adults, neonates exposed to in utero fentanyl display severe withdrawal symptoms. Traditional treatment regimens may not successfully capture these infants’ NOWS symptoms, and per recent literature review, there are no published fentanyl-adapted protocols.18,23

Historically, the Modified Finnegan Neonatal Abstinence Scoring System (MFNASS) relied on scheduled opioid administration, with slow dose escalation until capture, followed by gradual weaning, which often prolonged medication exposure and hospitalization.24, 25, 26, 27, 28 The American Academy of Pediatrics now accepts the function-based ESC method, first described by Grossman et al,9 as an acceptable strategy.29 It emphasizes nonpharmacologic measures, encourages family engagement to lessen withdrawal symptoms, and uses pharmacologic therapy only as needed. This method can decrease postnatal opioid exposure, length of stay (LOS), and health care costs; however, its implementation is not without hurdles.9,30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37 Institutional lack of support for education and culture change has been a barrier to ESC adoption. Providers’ personal biases and concern for undertreatment, as well as lack of buy-in, may result in overall resistance to change. Additionally, the ability of the family to be present at the bedside varies.

In accordance with statewide directives, our neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) planned to adopt ESC principles. Increasing prenatal IMF exposure and inadequate medication treatment algorithms prompted modifications to standard MFNASS and ESC protocols to address the challenges of severe fentanyl withdrawal. A hybrid model was formulated to provide real-time education and address the hesitation associated with the ESC transition. We believe our unique design tackled the distinct needs of the fentanyl-exposed infant whereas the staged approach allowed time to increase staff buy-in. The aim of this quality improvement (QI) project was to reduce LOS by 20% (from 25 to 20 days) over 19 months in infants exposed to opioids.

Methods

This initiative was conducted at an urban teaching hospital with more than 7000 deliveries/year and a 74-bed level III NICU affiliated with the local college of medicine in the southwestern US. Single-patient rooms provide adjustable lighting, space for innovative bassinets, swings, and most importantly allow parents to room-in. Parents or designated support persons are welcomed at the bedside 24/7. There is ample room for mothers to visit in a wheelchair or transfer into a recliner as soon as medically able postdelivery. Available facilities include showers, laundry, and a kitchen. Speech and occupational therapists participate in care and provide parental support and education. Lactation support is available to assist a parent in providing breastmilk if they are stable on OUD treatment or opioids prescribed by a health care provider. Hospital-based volunteers are available intermittently to comfort infants. Pediatric services beyond the newborn nursery or NICU are not available at our facility.

Interventions

A multidisciplinary QI team composed of neonatologists, newborn hospitalists, nurse practitioners, nursing leadership, therapists, pharmacists, and clinical educators was assembled. We identified key drivers influencing LOS and formulated interventions, as shown in Figure 1. A primary concern was the early and severe NOWS symptoms seen in infants exposed to fentanyl. Because standard MFNASS and ESC protocols seemed inadequate, the team advocated for modifications resulting in earlier treatment and symptom capture.18,38 We created a hybrid model allowing for transition to an adapted ESC model, optimizing workflow and encouraging family participation.

Design of Hybrid and ESC Algorithms

Traditional MFNASS and ESC protocols were modified (Figure 2). Infants were admitted to the NICU once the revised criteria for needing morphine were met. The hybrid algorithm design included initial management with MFNASS and scheduled/pro re nata (PRN) morphine to assist in capture. As infants' symptoms improved, both the scheduled and PRN doses were weaned simultaneously, and once at 0.05 mg/kg/dose, scheduled morphine and MFNASS assessments were discontinued. Only ESC assessments and PRN morphine were used in the final stage of the hybrid protocol.

Hybrid and ESC models of care design. Process change graphic delineates the designs of the hybrid and ESC models of care. N/A, not applicable.

When we transitioned to the ESC model, nursing staff recorded their assessments on the ESC Assessment Tool for clinical decision-making, which was an existing option in our electronic medical record.33 To encompass withdrawal related hyperthermia, we included an additional temperature screening to determine the need for PRN dosing.

Full participation of a physician, nurse, and caregivers in each bedside huddle as present in the traditional model was not always feasible in our busy unit. After education, nursing staff became competent to determine the need for dosing with a 2 nurse mini-team huddle, allowing prompt treatment of symptoms. If agreement was not reached, the provider was contacted for further discussion. The infant to nurse staffing ratio was modified to 2:1 and if symptoms were not captured in the first 48 hours, nursing assignments were re-evaluated and optimized as staffing allowed.

In both algorithms, fentanyl's unique pharmacology was addressed with novel morphine adjustments including early initiation, rapid escalation, a greater maximum dose, and increased frequency of PRN dosing. The morphine given to treat NOWS does not exceed the routine doses that are safely administered in the NICU. Morphine administration can only occur in the NICU at our facility, where infants are on continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring and signs of opioid overdose can be quickly identified. Both models were instituted for all infants with NOWS, regardless of family presence.

Our protocols promote timely treatment of NOWS symptoms by placing nonpharmacologic measures at the forefront with an emphasis on parental involvement. Although not protocolized, early institution of hypercaloric and nasogastric tube (NGT) feeds was recommended when poor feeding or significant weight loss (>10%) was noted. Safe discharge criteria were devised, and discharge preparation began when ≤3 PRN morphine doses were received in a 24-hour period (Figure 3).39 The establishment of care with the campus Toxicology Clinic team begins in the NICU. After leaving the hospital, individuals with OUD and their infants are seen within 1 week and as needed thereafter. The clinic continues to provide caregiver support and education as well as monitoring for weight gain and possible sequalae of NOWS. Appropriate steps were taken by Case Management to coordinate the Safety Plan with the Department of Child Safety to ensure a safe discharge.

During the planning period, the hybrid model was designed, and staff received individualized ESC education through dissemination of comprehensive toolkits and in-services, which included the use of person-first inclusive language. In addition, superusers were trained and one-on-one sessions with the NICU clinical educator were provided as needed. To establish proof of concept, some infants were managed with the hybrid approach and were not included in the analysis. The hybrid protocol was initially rolled out on May 15, 2023, for infants exposed to fentanyl only (hybrid phase I) (Figure 4). After a successful cycle, hybrid phase II began on November 1, 2023, including all infants exposed to opioids. When regular audits and feedback from the team demonstrated readiness to transition, we implemented ESC hospital-wide on May 1, 2024 (Figure 5).

Study of the Interventions

Comparisons were made between outcomes from the 3 identified time periods of our project: preintervention, hybrid, and ESC. A statistical process control (SPC) chart was used to compare our LOS with the goal (Figure 6). Data were audited quarterly within the QI team providing opportunities for re-education. Successful results were shared fostering staff acceptance. Because there were not any other changes in practice directed at decreasing LOS that occurred during this interval, we believe that the interventions of this QI project drove the observed improvements.

Measures

Deidentified data were gathered from the electronic medical records of all infants born ≥35 weeks of gestational age with prenatal opioid exposure. The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials diagram details enrollment (Figure 7). Demographic information was collected with parental race and ethnicity as self-reported on the state certificate of live birth worksheet to adequately describe the social groups served by our hospital. Measures chosen to analyze our initiative included fentanyl exposure, morphine use (days and doses), adjuvant therapy, LOS, and NGT presence. The percentage of days with family presence at the bedside was calculated. The outcomes studied were based on previously published measures for quality improvement of NOWS care.40,41 Balancing measures of apnea and/or need for naloxone were used as safety parameters to indicate potential morphine overuse. Data were reviewed by the authors for completeness, and erroneous values were corrected before analysis.

Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials diagram for patient selection.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data for all study variables. Continuous variables were reported as means and SDs and were compared using the t test, the Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis test, or one-way ANOVA test as appropriate. A simple linear regression was performed when necessary. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and were compared using the Fisher exact test or χ2 test. Mean LOS was the variable analyzed using an SPC chart. For each time period, the SD was calculated, and control limits were based on 2 SDs from the mean. Statistical analyses were completed using Stata, version 18.0, and P values < .05 were considered statistically significant. We used the SQUIRE checklist when writing our report.42

Ethical Considerations

The hospital's Research Determination Committee deemed this project not as research.

Results

Data were gathered from a total of 281 patients, with 217 evaluated for all outcomes (140 preintervention, 53 hybrid, 24 ESC). Sixty-four were transferred to a community subacute facility for continued NOWS treatment and were included only in demographic analysis (Table I). The majority of self-reported maternal race was White (74-83%) and ethnicity was Hispanic (45-50%). There were greater percentages of American Indian (10%-15%) in both the hybrid and ESC groups. Paternal race and ethnicities were not present on the certificate of live birth worksheets for about half of the infants. Comparisons were made between the 3 phases with hybrid and ESC considered the post-intervention groups. Birth weight was similar among the 3 cohorts; however, the hybrid group had a slightly younger gestational age (P = .04). As expected, fentanyl accounted for the majority (69%-80%) of in utero opioid exposure among all groups. Sixty-seven percent of infants were exposed to a combination of both opioid and nonopioid drug use though the specific types were not collected.

In analyzing all infants exposed to opioids, there was meaningful improvement in several quality measures including mean LOS, total dose and duration of morphine treatment, and family presence (Table II). Mean LOS decreased in both postintervention groups (25.5, 18, 13, P < .001). When compared with the preintervention group, the mean LOS decreased by 29% (hybrid) and 49% (ESC), further visualized in an SPC chart (Figure 6). To account for the smaller gestational age in the hybrid group, a simple linear regression was performed and did not show a relationship between gestational age and LOS (R2 = .0114, F [1, 215] = 2.49, P = .12). Fewer morphine days (22.5, 11.2, 6.9, P < .001) and doses (162, 63, 14, P < .001) achieved statistical significance. With introduction of the hybrid and ESC protocols, a 50% and 69% reduction in morphine days and 61% and 91% in morphine doses was observed. Family presence increased in the postintervention groups (58%, 73%, 68%, P = .01). One infant from each of the pre- and hybrid groups had a single apneic event, and use of naloxone was never required (P = .6, P = 1). No significant differences were seen in adjuvant use, days of NGT feeds, or NGT at discharge among all groups (P = .3, P = .28, P = .59).

In infants exposed to fentanyl, the mean LOS decreased by 33% and 45% in both post-intervention groups (27.8, 18.7, 15.3, P < .001). Likewise, there was a decline in morphine days (24.8, 11.3, 7.5, P < .001) and doses (183, 63, 18, P < .001). This translated to a 54% and 70% reduction in morphine days and 66% and 89% in morphine doses in postintervention groups. Those managed with the hybrid protocol reached peak morphine dose (capture) significantly sooner than the preintervention group (3.4, 2.1, P = .02) (Table II).

Discussion

This QI initiative surpassed the goal of decreasing LOS by 20% in 19 months in a population largely exposed to IMF. Implementation of the hybrid protocol led to improvements in quality metrics, which were further increased with our ESC model. The key parts of our initiative are modifying standard MFNASS and ESC protocols to account for ultrapotent opioid pharmacology and mitigating family and hospital barriers to ESC implementation. We believe this publication addresses a knowledge gap in the literature by describing 2 unique fentanyl-adapted medication protocols that can improve care for infants experiencing severe NOWS symptoms.

When comparing our hybrid and modified ESC medication protocols with other published literature, there was greater maximum dosing, and sooner initiation/escalation of morphine.28,43,44 These changes address the early onset and severity of withdrawal symptoms that can occur in infants exposed to fentanyl, as described by Gold et al.18 In addition, our protocols were designed to not revert to scheduled morphine at any point.45,46 As expected, when comparing infants exposed to fentanyl, symptoms were captured more than 1 day sooner (peak dose) in those managed with the hybrid protocol vs the preintervention group. Although these modifications were designed to initially target infants exposed to fentanyl, our results show that all infants exposed to opioids benefited.

The total morphine days and doses decreased by 69% and 91%, respectively, with the transition to ESC with PRN morphine, and although our number of morphine days are consistent with other initiatives, we administered fewer morphine doses.9,47, 48, 49 There were 2 infants with apneic episodes that were not considered related to morphine administration, and no instances of overmedication requiring naloxone occurred. Surprisingly, there was no difference in duration of NGT or adjuvant use, although there might be insufficient power to detect these effects.

Infants with NOWS may be discharged prematurely or lack sufficient support at home, increasing the risk of failure to thrive.37,50,51 In preparation for a safe discharge, we implemented increased caregiver education and thorough assessments to ensure the infant would tolerate a home environment (Figure 3). We hoped to achieve the same success in LOS reduction as seen in other quality projects, but our comprehensive discharge criteria may have resulted in a slightly greater LOS.9,45

Challenges to the implementation of ESC stem from both family and hospital factors. To ensure equitable care, our hybrid and ESC protocols were instituted for all infants with NOWS, irrespective of race, ethnicity, or family presence. The goal was to promote maternal-infant bonding through rooming-in, provide a welcoming environment, and decrease stigmatization. As is well known, family presence at the bedside plays an integral part in the success of ESC. Our partnership with the state programs created by the NAS action plan provided services to families including transportation to the hospital, possibly influencing the increased family presence seen in both postintervention groups (58%, 73%, 68%, P = .01).10

Staff resistance to change and busy NICU environments are known obstacles encountered with the significant practice shift to ESC. Implementing miniteam huddles and streamlining morphine administration were time saving in the busy NICU. Similar to other facilities, we were successful in implementing a phased approach.36,48,52, 53, 54 The hybrid model played a crucial role in mitigating these obstacles by using MFNASS until weaning. Our modifications facilitated a smooth transition to ESC for the treatment of infants who are exposed to fentanyl.

Limitations

After ESC was implemented hospital-wide, we noted a decrease in NICU admissions for NOWS, ultimately decreasing the size of the ESC cohort. The infants successfully managed with ESC beginning at birth in postpartum or the newborn nursery without the need for morphine may not have required a NICU admission and were not included in our dataset. We were unable to gather postdischarge information, including any readmissions. Our project could have been strengthened with the evaluation of inter-rater reliability for ESC assessments as well as the collection of the individual types of maternal drug use. All authors reviewed the data for completeness; however, there may be some inaccuracies present in the manual chart review.

The design of our quality initiative design may have limited the generalizability of the project. Our NICU allows for rooming-in, so modifications may be needed to implement our care models in centers without private rooms. Although our facility is limited to morphine administration within the NICU, other hospitals may have the ability to pharmacologically treat infants in the newborn nursery, postpartum, or pediatric settings. In addition, our resources such as the Toxicology Clinic and state programs may not be available elsewhere.

Future Steps

We hope to maintain our forward progress through the encouragement of staff while holding leadership accountable to maintain high-quality care. Length of stay from infants with NOWS will be incorporated on the hospital's quality dashboard and internal audits including morphine use and LOS will continue to provide a real-time indicator of performance. Training reinforcement will be held during quarterly nursing education sessions. Furthermore, our center is leading efforts to collaborate and adopt the ESC model of care across other facilities in our health care system.

Conclusions

This QI project provides a framework for implementing ESC principles with emphasis on the infant exposed to fentanyl. Modifications to the administration of morphine allowed for prompt capture of severe NOWS symptoms and resulted in decreased postnatal morphine use and LOS. Our phased hybrid approach provides time for staff education and acceptance before transitioning to ESC which may be feasible to implement across various hospital settings.
