Design of novel Z-scheme g-C3N4/TiO2/CuCo2O4 heterojunctions for efficient visible light-driven photocatalyic degradation of rhodamine B
Authors: Aws M Hamza, Hassan A Alshamsi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne of the most important environmental challenges that needs to be resolved is the industrial discharge of synthetic dyes. Graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4), Titanium dioxide (TiO2) and flower-like copper oxide (CuO)/copper cobaltite (CuCo2O4) nanocomposites were synthesized in order to synthesis an effective visible light driven photocatalyst that could degrade Rhodamin B (Rh.B) dye under simulated solar light irradiation. The SEM and TEM results verifies that the flower-like CuO/CuCo2O4 (CCO) structure and g-C3N4/TiO2 (g-CN/TO) generated a smart hybrid structure with superior g-CN distribution. According to the photocatalytic studies, g- C3N4/TiO2/CuO/CuCo2O4 (g-CN/TO/CCO) shows good photodegradation of Rh.B dye (99.9%) in minmal times (1 h) in CCO: g-CN/TO (2:1) ratio by Z-Scheme mechanism. The enhanced visible light absorption and effective electron-hole pair separation provided by the synergistic dispersion of CuO/CuCo2O4 and g-C3N4 can be attributed to the improved photocatalytic performances. These novel insights into g-CN/TO/CCO based photocatalysts are useful for treating industrial effluent.Keywords: Graphitic carbon nitride, Flower-like copper cobaltite, Photodegradation, Z-scheme mechanism, Rhodamine BSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Pollution remediation

Introduction

As freshwater supplies run out, reusing and recovering wastewater is becoming more and more important. Only 1% of the water on Earth’s surface is drinkable, even though 71% of the Earth’s surface is water. This is mostly because of several factors, such as more people living in cities and more factories being built1. The rapid growth of modern industry has created a huge threat to the health of people, animals, and the environment. This is because it has created many new organic pollutants that are hard for water bodies to degrade2. It is interesting to note that the textile industry, which releases more than half of the world’s organic pollutant wastewater, also causes the most color wastewater3. Acute effects of Rh.B exposure have been described as a stuffy nose, coughing up a burning feeling in the throat, headaches, feeling sick, and tears in the eyes all the time4,5. It is harmful to both humans and animals’ reproductive systems and growth, according to science. Due to its high stability and non-biodegradability, it is often employed as a standard model dye in studies of photocatalytic degradation6. Activated sludge process, flotation7, chemical oxidation8,9, and membrane filtering10 are few of the methods that have been used to remove RhB residues from water. Recently, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have been used a lot in a certain photocatalytic method to break down RhB in water. During photocatalysis process, the particulate semiconductors activated by ultraviolet, sunlight or artificial light generate an electron/hole (e−/h+) pairs that can be used to remove or oxidize pollutants from wastewaters11. Numerous researchers have attempted to degrade RhB using various semiconductor photocatalysts, including TiO212, SnO213, WO314, In2O315, NiCo2O416, Ni-Zn spinel ferrite17, g-C3N418,19, reduced graphene oxide25, and so on. While these semiconductors have been studied, TiO2 has received the most attention of its high surface area, inert, inexpensive, non-toxic, chemical and biological stability, and excellent photocatalytic activity20.

As a well-known n-type semiconductor, TiO2 is intensively used in a in a wide range of environmental, medicinal and energy applications. TiO2 is useful in wastewater remediation for the photocatalytic mineralization of organic pollutants21. With a wide band gap of 3.2 eV, poor visible-light absorption, poor reaction with pollutants, a high rate of photogenerated electron-hole pair recombination, and poor performance in solar-light-using applications, this material is not optimal for photocatalysis22. A good way to solve these problems and make photocatalysis work better is to combine the photocatalyst with other semiconductors. In recent years, graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4), a metal-free n-type semiconductor polymer, has attracted a lot of attention for its potential applications in photocatalysis23. g-C3N4 is superior to other photocatalysts due to its layered structure, high specific surface area, electron-rich surface, and appropriate band gap energy of 2.7 eV24. It is made up of tri-s-triazine units and reacts well to visible light. Its band-gap is medium (up to 460 nm)25. It’s very popular because it’s cheap, easy to make, chemically stable, has great mechanical and electrical properties, improves electrochemical performance, and suitable for the treatment of water contaminations. In addition, photogenerated electrons have a strong reduction potential since their ECB is only around − 1.1 V (compared to NHE)26. With visible light and g-C3N4, researchers found that hydrogen from water could be broken down and organic dyes could be broken down25. Composite materials made of g-C3N4 are now used a lot in photocatalysis. They are also some of the best photocatalytic materials because their main parts are not expensive.

In order to improve photocatalytic performance, many modification studies have been carried out. Accelerating the dissociation of light-excited e–/h+ couples and expanding the response range have both been proposed as means of improving the performance of photocatalytic semiconductor oxide catalysts. However, it is difficult to find a semiconductor photocatalyst that satisfies both requirements at the same time. Researchers are very interested in heterojunction complexes made up of two semiconductor photocatalysts because of this. A heterojunction is a junction formed by the proximity of two or more semiconductors27. These problems could be fixed by making heterojunction structures by mixing TiO2 with the right semiconductor materials. When making a heterojunction photocatalyst material, the electron affinity is more important than its structure for better photocatalytic activity28. It will be more suited to a couple of p and n-type semiconductor materials, such as CuCo2O4 (p-type) or TiO2 and g-C3N4 (n-type), to improve the photocatalytic capabilities. Due to its ability to be magnetized, high catalytic activity, and high chemical stability, spinel (general formula AB2 × 4) has seen widespread usage in a variety of sectors over the last decade, including energy storage and environmental treatments29. A lot of interest has been shown in cobalt-based spinel oxides because they show promise for activity, are cheap, are stable, and are safe for the environment30. Some researchers are interested in copper cobaltite (CuCo2O4), a bimetallic spinel oxide. It has great potential for usage in energy, photoelectricity, and catalysis, and it is a cold semiconductor with a low band gap (1.6–1.8 eV). Adding cobalt also makes the p-type properties better by increasing electron affinity31. In addition, copper oxide (CuO) have band gap around 1.47 eV32.

Cu2+ (3d9) and Co3+ (3d6) have partially filled 3d-orbitals in the cubic spinel CTCu. They can easily accept electrons from TiO2 and g-C3N4 and successfully create electron-hole pairs, which makes them last longer and is useful for photocatalysis33. The high conductivity of CTCu improves the rate of electron transfer and charge separation. This is because cation-to-cation electron transfer requires a low activation energy34. According to the literature, various composites based on TiO2 and g-C3N4 have been prepared and used for the photocatalytic removal of RhB. For example, Sharmaa et al. (2017) said that the composite with a 2:1 weight ratio of g-C3N4 and TiO2 has the highest photocatalytic activity, which is 10 times higher than pure TiO235.

Zhao et al. (2018) found that the TiO2/MgZnAl layered double hydroxide (Mg: Zn: Al = 1:3:1) destroyed 93.73% of RhB in 9.0 h36,37. A study by Zhang et al.38 used simulated solar light to test photocatalysis. They found that TiO2/g-C3N4 worked better than pure g-C3N4 and TiO2 nanosheets at breaking down Rh.B. Le et al.39 said that RhB was completely removed when C, N-TiO2 was present at a dose of 1.8 g/L for 3 h. Barzegar et al.40 find out that continuous flow loop photoreactors made of parabolic trough collectors can break down RhB by about 93.07%, when they are exposed to sunlight. According to Yang et al.41, the best photocatalytic degradation performance of 95% was reached when the mass ratio of TiO2 and g-C3N4 was 1:3 3. Efficient photocatalytic degradation of Rh.B dye was reached in the presence of CuCo2O4 nanoflower as reported by Priya et al.42. Researchers said that 31 nm CuCo2O4 nanoflowers destroyed 85% of RhB under sun light illumination.

According to the results of the photocatalytic activity of TiO2 and g-C3N4 (n-type semiconductors) as well as CTCu (p-type semiconductor), we will achieve an improved Rh.B photocatalysis based on Z-scheme photocatalyst43. The effects of photocatalyst dose, concentration of Rh.B, CCO amount, and pH have been studied on Rh.B degradation.

Materials and methods

Materials and devices

Materials acquired from Merck and Sigma Aldrich firms included urea, ethanol, cupric acetate, cobalt acetate, sodium acetate, melamine, Rhodamine B, and titanium tetraisopropoxide. Distinct solutions were prepared using bidistilled water. Rh.B was dissolved in water to prepare the stock solution of Rh.B with a concentration of 100 mg/L. The stock solution was used to prepare Rh.B a series of Rh.B dye solutions at lower concentrations. The synthesised nanomaterials were characterised using FT-IR model PerkinElmer Spectrum, FE-SEM model (FE-SEM) model TESCAN MIRA3, and XRD model X’-Pert Pro Powder model diffractometer set to Cu-K radiation. A Hitachi F-4500 spectrophotometer was utilised to acquire photoluminescence (PL) spectra, with a 150 W xenon lamp serving as the excitation source. Using a UV model UV-Vis device, the absorption spectra of Rh.B were examined. For visible light and photocurrent experiments, a 350 W mercury-xenon lamp with an intensity of 380.8 µw/cm2 at a wavelength less than 380 nm was utilised.

Synthesis of nanomaterials

Synthesis of g-C3N4

In typical, 4 g of melamine were put in a crucible and put in the muffle furnace to create g-C3N4. After 3 h, the temperature was increased to 550 °C and maintained there for another 3 h. The furnace was then cooled, and the yellow powder of g-C3N4 was obtained44.

Synthesis of TiO2

A 1:5 ratio was used to combine 5 mL of titanium tetraisopropoxide with ethanol. After adding the aforementioned solution dropwise while using sonication, it was vigorously stirred and left overnight. The entire solution was moved the following day to a stainless steel autoclave lined with Teflon, sealed, and kept at 140 °C for 24 h45,46.

Synthesis of g-C3N4/TiO2

Generally, g-C3N4 and TiO2 were synthesized and then mixed in a mass ratio of 1:1 into 100 mL of ethanol, followed by a 6-hour sonication. 6 h of further stirring were followed by an 80 °C drying. Light yellow color of the g-C3N4/TiO2 hybrid composite was obtained47.

Synthesis of CuCo2O4

Typically, 160 mL of cationic precursor solution containing 0.2 M cupric acetate and 0.4 M cobalt acetate was stirred with a magnetic stirrer to dissolve 10 millimoles urea and 4 mmol ammonium fluoride. The hydrothermal treatment was done in an electric oven for 12 h at 120 °C in a clear pink solution that had been transferred to a 200 mL Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave containing FSSM substrate. Finally, DI water and ethyl alcohol were used to wash the product. Moreover, the resulted powder were calcined in an air furnace for two hours at 450 °C after being dried overnight in an electric oven set to 60 °C44.

Synthesis of g-C3N4/TiO2/CuCo2O4

For preparation of the g-C3N4/TiO2/CuCo2O4 composite, as synthesized g-C3N4/TiO2 was dispersed in the solution of CuCo2O4 and followed by the method of pure CuCo2O4. For investigation of CuCo2O4 effect in photocatalytic performance, we have used half (X: 0.5) and X:1.5 of the initial concentration (X:1) of precursors that was used for the synthesis of g-C3N4/TiO2/CuCo2O4 (X:1) composite.

Photoctalytic study

Rhodamine B was subjected to photocatalytic degradation in a batch setup. In order to achieve this, 0.05 g of synthesized nanomaterial was added to 1000 mL of Rhodamine B solution (5 ppm) that had been prepared. For 30 min, the mixture was mixed in the dark. Subsequently, samples were taken at specific intervals while stirring the solution using a mercury-xenon lamp with a wavelength of less than 420 nm and an intensity of 350 W irradiance at a distance of 50 cm from the solution. Using a centrifuge, the catalyst was extracted, and the dye absorbance was recorded at a wavelength of 553 nm to examine the photodegradation efficiency. Results and discussion

Characterization

X-ray diffraction analysis was used to examine the crystallite structures of the as-synthesized g-CN, CCO, g-CN/TO, and g-CN/TO/CCO composites (Fig. 1a). In the XRD pattern of g-CN, two peaks can be found at 13.9° and 28.1°. These correspond to the (100) and (002) planes of the normal inter-layer structural packing and the interplanar stacking peaks of the aromatic systems of g-CN, respectively, based on JCPDS Card No. 87-152644. Cubic spinel CuCo2O4 phase was identified as the source of all diffraction peaks in the XRD pattern of CCO and g-CN/TO/CCO composites (Fig. 1a), which are in agreement with the values shown on standard JCPDS card no. 001-115551,52. The resulting CCO composites displayed phases of coexistence for both CuO and CuCo2O4. The cubic spinel structure of CuCo2O4 crystal contain (220), (311), (222), (400), (511), and (440) plane reflections are indicated by the diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 31.2, 36.7, 44.5, 59.2, and 64.8. The diffraction peaks of monoclinic CuO in the XRD pattern of CCO, where Bragg’s reflections of (110), (11 −1), (111), (− 112), (202), (020), (202), (11 − 3), (31 −1), (113), (311), (004), and (222) were seen at 32.54, 35.58, 38.79, 46.26, 48.76, 53.45, 58.34, 61.53, 66.32, 68.14, 72.39, 75.11, and 82.98, respectively, which is in good agreement with the CuO ICDD reference card No. 01-080-191744,53. The presence of carbon nitride is clearly visible in the XRD pattern of g-CN/TO, and the development of amorphous TO is indicated by the presence of a broad peak.

XRD patterns (a) and FT-IR spectra (b) of as synthesized materials.

The corresponding functional groups of g-CN, g-CN/TO, and g-CN/TO/CCO composites are investigated using their FT-IR spectra (Fig. 1b). In the FT-IR spectra of g-CN, the characteristic absorption peaks correspond to the vibrations of tri-s-triazine units (810.09/cm), heptazine heterocycles (1631, 1413, and 1244/cm), N-H or O-H stretching vibrations (3162–3510/cm), and C–N stretching vibrations (1200–1700/cm). When considering hybrid composites such as g-CN/TO, it is possible to observe the entire set of unique peaks of g-CN51. The absorption band at 3454.51/cm in this spectrum corresponds to the stretching vibration of O–H, while the moisture-containing water is associated with the bending vibration of O–H at 1631.78/cm. The Ti-O bond vibrates, resulting in the peak at 580/cm. The peak indicates that TO be present on the g-CN surface. The stretching vibrations of Cu-O and Co-O in the tetrahedral and octahedral complexes, respectively, are responsible for the formation of two bands at 565 and 670/cm in the g-CN/TO/CCO composites44. This results further indicate that the final composites efficiently bind all of the functional moieties of g-CN, TO, and CCO.

In this study, the effect of CCO ratio in the g-CN/TO/CCO composites were investigated. The results show that by increasing the amount of raw materials for the formation of CCO, the amount of CuCo2O4 in composite is increases in comparison of CuO (Fig. 2). It will be followed the effect of this component’s ratio in photocatalytic properties of synthesized nanocomposites.

XRD patterns of g-CN/TO/CCO (X) composites with different ratio of CCO.

The morphology of g-CN and g-CN/TO is lumpy and has non-uniform structures. The structure of synthesized CCO like a flower and has a uniform morphology. In the resulting three-component g-CN/TO/CCO composite structure, the presence of all three materials is quite clear. By examining the effect of the ratio of CCO on the morphology of the g-CN/TO/CCO composites, it can be concluded that at X:1.5, the amount of CCO material is more than TO and g-CN and the flower-like structure is dominant morphology. The composite with ratio of X:0.5 clearly shows that the amount of CCO is lower than that of g-CN/TO and the presence of all three substances is quite clear (Fig. 3).

SEM images of as synthesized materials.

According to the SEM images of all ratios of CCO, the best structure in terms of morphology is related to CCO, X:1, which shows that uniform structures have been obtained and the presence of all three components is evident. The TEM images related to this structure also prove the presence of all three components and prove the morphology related to the g-CN/TO/CCO composite structure (Fig. 4).

Photocatalytic degradation of Rh.B dye

Rhodamine B photocatalytic degradation was studied in an aqueous solution in the presence of 0.05 g/L of synthesized nanomaterials for 5 ppm concentration of dye. Prior to that, Rh.B was subjected to 60 min of light alone, as well as exposure to artificial nanomaterials in the dark. The findings demonstrated that in 60 min, light alone could only break down 4% of Rh.B. As Fig. 7 displays, the greatest degradation of Rh.B in the absence of light and in the presence of synthesized nanomaterials was 5%, suggesting that Rh.B cannot be degraded by light or synthetic materials alone. Rh.B was then subjected to light and synthesized nanomaterials. Figure 7 displays the photocatalytic degradation and adsorption efficiencies of Rh.B spectra in the presence of g-CN, g-CN/TO, and g-CN/TO/CCO. In all g-CN/TO/CCO composite studies where X is not expressed, its base value is chosen due to better morphology. As can be observed, Rh.B is degraded as the maximum absorption at a wavelength of 553 nm diminishes with increasing illumination time. This dye degraded at rates of 50%, 60%, 75%, and 99.4% in the presence of g-CN, TO, g-CN/TO, and g-CN/TO/CCO in 60 min, respectively (Fig. 7a–d).

Photocatalytic degrading of Rh.B (a–d), and the kinetic studies of Rh.B degradation (e and f) by as synthesized nanomaterials.

The results shown in Fig. 7e, f illustrate that in addition to the percentage of degradation in the g-CN/TO/CCO composite being higher than the other material, the rate of degradation is also significantly higher. This can be due to the increased contact surface of the materials in the g-CN/TO/CCO composite. As the SEM analysis show, in the morphology of the composite, where the masses of g-CN and TO be placed next to the flower-like structures of CCO, the electron transfer can be done at a higher rate. Flower-like structures have needle points, which are suitable places for accumulation and transfer of electrons between compounds. The rate constant related to the catalytic degradation process in g-CN, TO, g-CN/TO and g-CN/TO/CCO is equal to 0.001, 0.015, 0.023, and 0.085, respectively.

Effective parameters in photocatalytic degradation of Rh.B

Effect of CCO amount and pH in photocatalitic performance

In order to find the most optimal composite in terms of percentage composition of g-CN, TO, and CCO materials, different amounts of CCO were used in the g-CN/TO/CCO composite structure. As Fig. 8 shows, the results exhibited that in addition to changing the morphology of the material, the photocatalytic property also changes by increasing the amount of CCO. A better morphology was obtained in the basic structure with X: 1. But according to photocatalytic degradation experiments, it can be seen that with X: 1.5, the photocatalytic property is improved, but the difference with the basic state is not significant. Therefore, the basic structure can be our optimum structure in terms of component composition (Fig. 9a). One of the critical parameters in the photocatalytic degradation process is pH50. Therefore, to understand the performance of catalyst in photodegradation of Rh.B and the effect of this parameter, the photocatalytic degradation of dyes at different pHs was investigated. The results show that the catalyst has performed weaker in acidic conditions than the alkaline conditions. However, the alkaline conditions do not change significantly compared to the normal conditions of the solution, which is neutral and has a pH of 7. Therefore, the catalyst has a more optimal performance in neutral conditions and can be used in the process of photocatalytic degradation of Rh.B without changing the pH (Fig. 9b).

The effect of initial Rh.B concentration on the photocatalytic degradation efficiency of Rh.B.

Initial concentration of Rh.B

In this section, the photocatalytic degradation of different concentrations of Rh.B in the presence of g-CN/TO/CCO catalyst was investigated. Different concentrations of Rh.B including 5, 10, 15, and 20 ppm were studied in the presence of 0.05 g of catalyst as shown in the Fig. 9. As it is evident, with the increase in Rh.B concentration, the efficiency of photocatalytic degradation should be reduced, and this is due to the reduction of the contact surface of all the dye molecules with the catalyst and, naturally, the reduction of electron transfers and the reduction of chemical interactions subject to dye degradation reactions. The characteristics of photocatalytic degradation have been completely accomplished in 90 min at the concentrations of 5 and 10 ppm. In concentrations higher than 10 ppm, these values were lower, but with the power of the photocatalyst, it was so strong that it was able to destroy 81% of the Rh.B at a concentration of 20 ppm. The rate of degradation also decreased with the increase of dye concentration so that the rate constant in concentrations of 5, 10, 15, 20 ppm, is equal to 0.018, 0.028, 0.048, and 0.076, respectively.

The results show that more than 95% of the Rh.B has been degraded in 0.15 and 0.2 g within 60 min (Fig. 10). Effect of scavengars and reusibility in photocatalitic performance

The process of photocatalytic degradation of most pollutants can be easily performed in the presence of radicals and oxidizing species45,59,60. For this purpose, by using Isopropanol, EDTA, and Ascorbic acid as scavengers, which are used to trap h+, OH•, •O–2 species, respectively, it is possible to predict the photocatalytic degradation mechanism of Rh.B in the presence of the g-CN/TO/CCO catalyst. As the Fig. 11a shows, the lowest amount of photocatalytic degradation occurred in the presence of Ascorbic acid, in other words, •O− 2 species is the most important effective factor and driver of the photocatalytic degradation mechanism of the Rh.B dye. Hydroxyl radicals also play an important role in the photocatalytic degradation of Rh.B. The performance of the catalyst was checked in successive cycles (Fig. 11b). The results showed that the catalyst is still able to degrade Rh.B during five consecutive cycles. In addition, it can be said that the catalyst is almost stable up to three cycles and does not have a significant drop in performance.

Photocatalytic degradation of Rh.B in the percense of differnet scavengers (a), and five consecutive cycles (b).

The stability of the recycled photocatalyst was studied using XRD and FTIR techniques, as shown in Fig. 12. The results showed that the XRD pattern and FT-IR spectrum of remained unchanged with no appearance of any new crystalline orientation and changes in the chemical structure after five consecutive cycles (Fig. 12). As it is known, the powder samples have a lower peak intensity than the bulk samples (due to the influence of the absorption factor). In addition, with increasing grain size; The intensity of the peak will increase. It is necessary to explain, with the reduction of the grain size, the grain boundary, which is the amorphous part of the material, increases, and as a result, the background scattering in the diffraction pattern will increase and the intensity of the peaks will decrease. It is possible that the decrease in the intensity of the peaks in the samples collected after the photocatalytic degradation of the dye is due to the increase in the grain boundaries, which causes the decrease in the intensity of the peaks45.

Photodegradation mechanism

Based on the results of this research, a possible mechanism for the degradation of Rh.B by g-CN/TO/CCO composite is proposed and it is shown schematically in Fig. 13. As shown in Fig. 6, the indirect band gap energies of CCO, g-CN, and TO are 2.6, 2.9, and 3.14 eV, respectively. The valence band and conduction band energies (related to Mulliken electronegativity) of g-CN are 1.57 eV and − 1.15 eV, respectively, and for TO this level of energy has been calculated 2.91 and − 0.29 eV. According to the studies that have already been done for CuO/CuCo2O4 structure56,57, the values of the lowest valance and highest conductive energy level reported 1.7 and − 1.3 eV, respectively. In the case of CCO structure, which consists of copper cobaltite, it is assumed as a one-component structure in this schematic, the valence band is considered to be the lowest level and the conduction band is considered to be the highest level of ref57. Assuming this electronic structure, the resulting gap energy is consistent with the Eg of the synthesized CCO (Fig. 5). According to earlier reports61,62, a Z-scheme system is therefore likely to emerge in this photocatalyst. According to Fig. 13, the photogenerated electrons in the CB of TO are moving to the VB of g-CN at the contact interface while the holes in TO continue to accumulate in the CB of g-CN and the VB of TO remains empty. The photogenerated electrons in CB of g-CN transfer to VB of CCO and recombined with holes in VB of CCO. As a result, it was possible to efficiently segregate the photogenerated electrons and holes without compromising the redox capacity and the produced radical species react to Rh.B dye.

Possible mechanism of photodegradation of Rh.B with g-CN/TO/CCO composite.

Conclusions

In this work, by using the hydrothermal technique, a g-CN/TO/CCO heterostructures were synthesized and then used as a direct Z-scheme photocatalyst to remove Rh.B oxidatively in the presence of simulated solar light. Because of the improved charge separation efficiency at the g-CN/TO/CCO interface, this photocatalyst demonstrated higher photocatalytic activity for Rh.B elimination when compared to individual g-CN and g-CN/TO. Measurements from trapping tests verified that the photocatalytic process produced both •O2− and •OH as the main active species. The optimized conditions for photocatalytic degradation in the presence of g-CN/TO/CCO catalyst are neutral pH, ambient temperature and concentration of 10 ppm of the Rh.B dye, as well as the basic amount of CCO in the catalyst. This work offers a practical method for designing g-CN/TO/CCO heterostructures for excellent photocatalysis.
