Saliva and Anterior Nasal Samples for Detecting Respiratory Viruses in Children
Authors: Anu Haaramo, Anu Jääskeläinen, Anne Pitkäranta, Mikael Kuitunen, Enni Sanmark, Johanna Nokso‐Koivisto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFor detecting respiratory viruses, a nasopharyngeal swab sample for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays has been the benchmark. Less discomfort related to sampling is requested especially for children, and thus other sample types have been suggested. We examined anterior nasal samples and saliva samples of 59 pediatric patients with respiratory symptoms and compared the results to nasopharyngeal swab samples. The collection of saliva and anterior nasal samples was tolerated better than nasopharyngeal samples in children. Anterior nasal samples were more accurate than saliva samples in detecting respiratory viruses by a multiplex respiratory panel compared to a reference test (nasopharyngeal swab sample by fourplex PCR), and it seems to be a feasible sample type for detecting respiratory viruses with PCR tests in children with respiratory symptoms.

Introduction

The eruption of the COVID‐19 pandemic showed the importance of simple and fast diagnostic methods for viral infections. The main means for laboratory diagnosis of viral respiratory infections in health care is detecting a virus or its parts by using a nasopharyngeal aspirate or swab (NPS). However, nasopharyngeal sample collection is laborious and uncomfortable for the patient.

Respiratory viruses are known to replicate in the nasopharynx [1, 2, 3], and with SARS‐CoV‐2, the biggest quantities have also been measured in samples from the nasopharynx [4]. Thus, the benchmark in COVID‐19 diagnostics has been the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay for detecting SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA in NPS samples [5, 6]. However, due to testing inconvenience, other testing methods are needed. Saliva and anterior nasal samples are easy to collect and reproduce with no complications associated with sampling [5]. For detecting SARS‐CoV‐2, tests from saliva seem to be as sensitive and specific as tests from NPS [7, 8]. Although, in a large study with adult patients saliva samples did not reach the SARS‐CoV‐2 detection rate of the NPS sample but had the lowest discomfort rate [9]. In the same study oropharyngeal swab samples were better in detecting SARS‐CoV‐2 compared to NPS [9]. Due to the ease of collection, tests taken from anterior parts of the nasal cavity became popular with in‐home antigen testing during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Thus, the current literature gives conflicting results of the usability of saliva or anterior nasal samples in the diagnosis of COVID‐19 and other viral respiratory infections [1, 4, 10, 11, 12, 13].

Aiming viral testing wisely enables appropriate yet cost‐effective treatment and preventive actions. Therefore, studies investigating the association between virus etiology and clinical disease presentation of common respiratory infections such as RSV, Influenza A and B, and COVID‐19 are needed. The aims of this study are to compare nasopharyngeal, anterior nasal, and saliva samples in respiratory microbial diagnostics in children with acute respiratory infection, investigate the tolerability and feasibility of different sampling techniques in children, and analyze the role of respiratory pathogens associated with patients' symptoms.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Samples

Children aged 0–15 years were recruited to this study at the emergency department of New Children's Hospital, Helsinki, from March to April 2023, and from November 2023 to February 2024. Patients visited the emergency department due to symptoms of respiratory infection and were recruited during their visit if they were tested as part of normal clinical practice for respiratory infections with a nasopharyngeal fourplex PCR test for SARS‐CoV‐2, influenza A, influenza B, and respiratory syncytial viruses (RSV). Per hospital policy, this nasopharyngeal testing was limited to children with difficult symptoms and the need for hospital care and/or to infants with respiratory symptoms of unknown etiology. The NPS sample was taken by a nurse at the emergency department with a nylon‐flocked dry swab through one nostril from the nasopharynx. The nurses did not get extra training for NPS sample collection due to this study. Children with significant comorbidities that could affect the sample collection evaluation were excluded from the study. After informed consent, anterior nasal samples and saliva samples were taken from the recruited patients. An emergency room nurse or research nurse collected anterior nasal samples by rotating a nylon‐flocked dry swab (Copan Diagnostics) in both nostrils. Saliva samples were collected by an emergency room nurse, research nurse or the child's guardian. Saliva samples were collected from the mucosa of the mouth with a wrapped polyester swab (Copan Diagnostics) by rotating the swab on the mucosa of the cheek. For older children, the child spat the sample into a container (50 mL screw‐cap plastic tube). All swabs were taken in 3 mL UTM Universal Transport Medium (Copan Diagnostics). The nurses were trained and instructed on sampling verbally and were also given pictorial instructions for sampling. If a guardian collected a saliva sample, the nurse instructed the guardian and observed the sampling. Additional NPS were not taken. All the samples were taken during the same visit to the emergency ward. Saliva samples and anterior nasal samples were immediately stored either at +4°C for a maximum of 3 days, after which they were stored at −70°C, or directly at −70°C where the samples were kept until virus analyses were done a maximum of 8 months after the sample collection.

The guardians completed forms about background information and the presence and timespan of the respiratory symptoms: fever, cough, breathing difficulties, sore throat, runny nose, headache, muscle pain, diarrhea, nausea/vomiting, rash. The guardians and patients 4 years old and above were asked to evaluate the tolerance of different types of testing on a scale from 1 (pleasant) to 5 (unpleasant). The nurse taking the samples evaluated the ease of sampling on the same scale.

Virus Analyses

As part of the routine care, the NPS samples were analyzed for SARS‐CoV‐2, influenza A, influenza B, and RSV with the Xpert Xpress CoV‐2/Flu/RSV plus test (Cepheid), referred to later as Xpert fourplex, after which the samples were stored at −20°C. Later these NPS samples as well as anterior nasal and saliva samples were analyzed with the BioFire Respiratory Panel 2.1 plus (RP2.1 plus) test (BioMerieux), referred to later as BioFire respiratory panel. This is a cartridge‐based multiplex PCR test that detects adenoviruses, coronaviruses SARS‐CoV‐2, NL63, HKU1, 229E, OC43 and MERS‐CoV, rhino/enteroviruses, influenza A virus, influenza B virus, parainfluenza 1–4 viruses, RSV, human metapneumoviruses, Bordetella pertussis, Bordetella parapertussis, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae .

Ethics

The Helsinki and Uusimaa Hospital District Ethics Committee approved the study protocol (HUS/15711/2022), and institutional research approval was granted by Helsinki University Hospital, Helsinki, Finland (HUS/53/2023). Written, informed consent was obtained from all caregivers. Participating in the study was voluntary and did not affect the treatment of the patient.

Statistical Analyses

The associations between symptoms and viral findings were examined using chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test. The differences in the length of symptoms between virus positive and negative groups were compared using Mann–Whitney U test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 29 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Background Information

A total of 59 patients were recruited to the study. Forms about symptoms and background information filled by the guardians were received from 56 (95%) patients. Of those, 35 (63%) were male, and the median age was 8 months (range 7 days–15.77 years). In all, 47 (84%) of the patients did not have any medical condition; two patients had asthma, two had atopy, one had epilepsy, and four patients had other medical conditions. Five (8.9%) patients were of school age and attended school. Twelve (21%) attended day care, and others were cared for at home. A total of 19 (34%) of the patients were the only child in the household, 27 (48%) had one sibling, and 10 (18%) had two or more siblings living in the same household. Four (7.1%) of the caregivers reported smoking in the household, all outdoors.

Virology Results

The NPS of 59 patients was analyzed with Xpert fourplex; of these, 18 (31%) were positive for SARS‐CoV‐2 alone, 7 (12%) were positive for RSV alone, and 1 (1.7%) was positive for both SARS‐CoV‐2 and RSV. Three (5.1%) were positive for influenza A virus, and none were positive for influenza B virus. Thirty (51%) were negative for all four pathogens.

SARS‐CoV‐2 Detection

BioFire respiratory panel testing of NPS samples: 18 out of 19 SARS‐CoV‐2 positives from the reference test (Xpert fourplex) were positive. The one that remained negative by BioFire respiratory panel had a high Ct (cycle threshold) value of 40.8 for SARS‐CoV‐2 by Xpert fourplex. One sample was positive by BioFire respiratory panel but negative by Xpert fourplex.

BioFire respiratory panel testing of anterior nasal samples: 18 out of 19 SARS‐CoV‐2 positives (Xpert fourplex) were positive. The anterior nasal sample of the patient, whose NPS had a high Ct for SARS‐CoV‐2 by Xpert fourplex and was SARS‐CoV‐2 negative by BioFire respiratory panel, was SARS‐CoV‐2 positive. No SARS‐CoV‐2 positive anterior nasal samples were detected from patients whose NPS was SARS‐CoV‐2 negative.

BioFire respiratory panel testing of saliva samples: 14 out of 19 SARS‐CoV‐2 positives (Xpert fourplex) were positive. No SARS‐CoV‐2 positive saliva samples were detected from patients whose NPS was SARS‐CoV‐2 negative.

RSV Detection

BioFire respiratory panel testing of NPS samples: 8 out of 8 RSV positives by reference test (Xpert fourplex for NPS) were positive. One NPS was RSV positive by BioFire respiratory panel although negative by Xpert fourplex.

For anterior nasal and saliva samples, the RSV results were concordant with the reference test.

Discussion

Cost‐effective, simple, and reliable tests for viral respiratory infections are needed now and in the future. Testing is essential to estimate the natural course of infections, select the appropriate treatment methods for the patient, and prevent infections. Easy sample collection with less discomfort is especially needed for children. In our study, the collection of saliva and anterior nasal samples was tolerated better than NPS according to both children and guardians. The anterior nasal sample was taken from both nostrils after the collection of the NPS through one nostril and this might have increased the discomfort of nasal sample collection. Patients' assessment of tolerability was higher than that of guardians or sample takers for both saliva and anterior nasal samples. This is understandable considering patients were children with respiratory symptoms in a hospital setting. Sample collection from the mucosa of the mouth or from the anterior part of the nose with a swab was convenient, and thus less time‐ and resource‐consuming than NPS sampling.

NPS samples have been seen to be effective in SARS‐CoV‐2 and influenza testing since the viruses are present in the nasopharynx throughout the infection, and the biggest viral load is seen in the nasopharynx [1, 4]. To et al. did not find a significant difference in the detection rate of respiratory viruses between saliva and NPS samples in adults [8], although this might not be the case in children. In our study, anterior nasal and saliva samples yielded promising results in detecting all RSV, influenza A, SARS‐CoV‐2, and rhino/entero viruses with the BioFire respiratory panel. In detecting RSV, all eight patients with Xpert fourplex PCR test positive NPS samples also had anterior nasal samples and saliva samples positive for RSV with the BioFire respiratory panel. One NPS sample that was RSV negative with Xpert fourplex was positive with the BioFire respiratory panel. In detecting SARS‐CoV‐2, one out of 19 positives was missed using anterior nasal samples and five with saliva samples.

The manufacturer has validated BioFire Respiratory Panel 2.1 plus for NPS samples only. However, as anterior nasal and saliva samples are easier to collect, it is highly desirable for these sample types to be applicable for children with respiratory symptoms in need of hospitalization.

Anterior nasal samples seem appropriate for clinical use in children to detect respiratory viruses and are more accurate than saliva, especially in detecting SARS‐CoV‐2 and rhino/entero viruses. All positive samples could not be found with saliva samples. The viral load in saliva is smaller than in the nasopharynx, and this difference is already seen during the first days after presumed infection [4]; this might partially explain the false negatives. Saliva samples in this study were taken by a polyester swab with a wrapped tip from the mucosa of the mouth in all except three patients who were old enough to spit the sample into a container. In infants and small children it is not possible to gain a sample by spitting, and thus the amount of the sample remains smaller. More samples with larger quantities of the virus could provide more accurate results. Swabs with more surface area on the tip may increase viral adhesion and lead to better results [4].

The study population contained only three influenza A positives. All three positives were found with anterior nasal and saliva samples. However, the sample size was too small for drawing any conclusions, and studies with larger populations are required.

Respiratory viruses cause overlapping clinical presentations, making it difficult for physicians to distinguish the causative agents without laboratory tests. In our study, the only statistically significant symptom with a positive correlation to a viral finding was fever in association with SARS‐CoV‐2. In addition, the duration of cough, fever and breathing difficulty were connected to RSV. In children, viral coinfections are common [14, 15]. In our study, 10 out of 59 patients had more than one virus detected concurrently. Clinical presentation did not differ significantly between those with multiple viruses and just one virus or those with negative results in testing. Our clinical hypothesis that the etiology of respiratory symptoms in children is often not determinable by clinical presentation was supported.

Small number of samples tested is a limitation in this study. Hospital policy to limit the nasopharyngeal testing for patients with more severe symptoms and need for hospitalization limited the number of recruits. The sample collection was divided as planned into two periods according to the regional infectious season and the incidence of respiratory viruses. The first collection period started as soon as the permits were confirmed.

Conclusions

Saliva samples and anterior nasal samples give promising results as a more comfortable and less demanding—yet accurate—sample type for detecting viral infections in children with respiratory symptoms by multiplex PCR. Saliva and anterior nasal samples are preferred over nasopharyngeal samples among patients and caregivers due to less discomfort and anxiety. Anterior nasal samples were more accurate than saliva samples in detecting respiratory viruses. Additional studies with larger populations are needed to confirm these results.
