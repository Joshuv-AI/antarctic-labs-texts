QuanBioTrust: a quantum-enhanced bio-inspired trust framework for next-generation WBANs
Authors: Soufiane Ben Othman, Obaid Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWireless Body Area Networks (WBANs) face critical challenges in achieving energy efficiency, security, and real-time reliability for continuous biomedical monitoring, as conventional protocols are hindered by energy constraints, vulnerability to cyberattacks, and high latency. To address these limitations, this paper proposes QuanBioTrust, a novel, integrated framework that fuses quantum-enhanced optimization, bio-inspired intelligence, and a dynamic zero-trust security model to create a scalable, secure, and self-sustaining WBAN architecture. QuanBioTrust leverages Quantum Particle Swarm Optimization (QPSO) with quantum entanglement to optimize cluster head (CH) selection by concurrently evaluating residual energy, sink proximity, trust score, and bio-energy harvesting potential (20–50 µW), reducing computational complexity to ~ 500 operations per round, a 28–45% improvement over HCEL (~ 700 ops) and EGWO (~ 1000 ops). Inspired by natural systems, the framework employs flocking algorithms for mobility-adaptive clustering, firefly synchronization for efficient multi-hop fractal routing (3–5 paths), and slime mold-inspired data fusion to achieve 50–70% compression of ECG packets (200-bit → 80-bit), drastically reducing transmission energy. The framework maintains a Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR) of 97% under normal conditions (90% under attack) and an end-to-end latency of 48 ms (185 ms under attack), well below the 250 ms threshold for real-time monitoring, outperforming EAFST (1000/1200 ms) and rivaling EDC-ER (50/180 ms). This quantum-biological fusion establishes a new paradigm for next-generation WBANs, offering a robust, energy-efficient, and resilient solution for secure, long-term, real-time healthcare monitoring.Keywords: Wireless body area networks, Quantum particle swarm optimization, Bio-energy harvesting, Fractal routing, Zero-trust securitySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Wireless Body Area Networks (WBANs) offer a revolutionary approach to wireless communication, connecting miniature sensor nodes, deployed on, within, or around the human body, to external systems like medical servers or wearable devices1. These compact, low-power devices, typically a few centimeters in size, integrate sensors, microcontrollers, and transceivers operating in the Industrial, Scientific, and Medical (ISM) or ultra-wideband (UWB) spectra2. They monitor physiological parameters such as heart rate, blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), body temperature, glucose levels, and electromyography (EMG) signals, transmitting data to a central sink, often a smartphone, smartwatch, or gateway, for real-time analysis3. Employing short-range communication (1–2 m) via protocols like IEEE 802.15.6, WBANs prioritize minimal power use and reliable transmission despite body-induced challenges, including tissue absorption and movement artifacts4. In healthcare, they enable remote monitoring of chronic conditions, early anomaly detection, and personalized treatments through closed-loop systems like insulin pumps, improving quality of life and reducing costs by decreasing hospital visits5.

Despite their potential, WBANs face a persistent challenge: energy efficiency, which limits their operational lifespan and adoption6. Sensor nodes, powered by small batteries (10–100 mAh) or emerging harvesting techniques, operate under tight energy constraints due to their size and the impracticality of replacing batteries, especially in implants. Energy demands arise from sensing, processing, and, most critically, wireless communication, where transceivers consume 10–100 mW over 0.1–2 m, amplified by path loss (exponent 2–4) and tissue interference causing attenuation and multipath fading7. Inefficient energy use shortens network lifetimes to hours or days, far below the weeks or months needed, risking packet loss that delays critical alerts, such as in cardiac monitoring, and compromising safety. Dynamic topology from body motion further increases energy needs by 30–50% due to retransmissions, while security and low-latency requirements add overhead, creating a complex trade-off that traditional protocols, designed for static networks, fail to address effectively8.

Efforts to tackle WBAN energy challenges have progressed from basic to advanced strategies, yet they fall short of meeting body-centric demands9. Early approaches like LEACH10 used static clustering and rotated cluster heads (CHs) to balance energy load, but their fixed structure and simplistic CH selection overlook mobility-induced shifts (up to 50% distance variation) and tissue interference (20–40 dB path loss), limiting longevity[11]. Bio-inspired methods like Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) optimize CH selection, cutting transmission energy by 15–25%, while Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) reduces costs by 10–20% through multi-hop routing12. However, their computational complexity and poor adaptability to rapid topology changes hinder real-time performance13. Trust-based models detect malicious nodes with 85–95% accuracy, enhancing security, but their overhead negates energy gains14. These incremental improvements, extending lifetimes by hours or boosting delivery by single-digit percentages, lack the holistic integration needed to address WBAN unique energy, security, and scalability demands comprehensively.

To address the critical challenges of efficiency, security, and scalability in next-generation Wireless Body Area Networks, we propose QuanBioTrust: A Quantum-Enhanced Bio-Inspired Trust Framework for Next-Generation WBANs. Leveraging Quantum Particle Swarm Optimization with quantum entanglement, QuanBioTrust optimizes cluster head (CH) selection by evaluating residual energy, sink proximity, connectivity, trust, and bio-energy harvesting potential (20–50 µW/cm2), achieving 20–30% lower computational complexity compared to traditional methods. Bio-inspired mechanisms, including adaptive clustering and synchronized multi-hop routing, draw from natural systems to enable 40% energy savings and 15–20% higher packet delivery rates. A predator–prey trust model, integrated with quantum key distribution (QKD), ensures robust security by isolating malicious nodes and enabling zero-trust encryption under threats, while fractal redundancy enhances path resilience. By combining quantum-enhanced optimization, bio-energy harvesting, and dynamic trust management, QuanBioTrust establishes a scalable and secure standard for next-generation WBANs, meeting the evolving demands of real-time biomedical monitoring.

Comparative Analysis of WBAN Protocols.

The study in15 presents HCEL (Hybrid Clustering Approach for Extending WBAN Lifetime), an energy-efficient routing protocol for Wireless Body Area Networks that blends static and dynamic clustering. HCEL optimizes parent node selection using a utility function based on residual energy, sink proximity, and received signal strength indicator (RSSI), constrained by energy thresholds and serving node limits. Operating in three phases, network deployment, parent node selection, and data transmission, it balances energy use, extends network lifetime, and enhances stability. Simulations in NetworkX reveal HCEL edge over SIMPLE and ERRS, reducing end-to-end delay by 56% and 40%, respectively, while improving stability period (23% vs. 20% for SIMPLE and 21% for ERRS), residual energy, packet delivery ratio, and throughput. HCEL excels in energy efficiency and delay reduction, making it reliable for WBANs. However, its complex parent node selection may increase computational overhead, and its scalability in larger networks remains unaddressed. SIMPLE offers a straightforward multihop approach with decent stability (20%) but suffers from higher delays (0.8 s) and less effective energy balancing. ERRS provides moderate stability (21%) yet lags with higher delays (0.5 s) and lower packet delivery ratios, reducing its reliability.

The research in16 introduces the Energy Enrichment Multi-Hop Routing (EEMR) protocol, a metaheuristic approach to boost WBAN network lifetime and routing efficiency by tackling path loss, energy consumption, and transmission barriers. EEMR operates in two phases: the Enhanced Flower Bee Optimisation Algorithm (EFBOA) minimizes energy use in clustering and cluster head selection, while Dynamic Local Hunting and Location Discarding (DLH-LD) optimizes routes. MATLAB and Network Simulator tests against M-ATTEMPT, EECR, and EMPR show EEMR achieving 95% throughput at 10,000 iterations, 30% energy efficiency (vs. 50% for M-ATTEMPT, 65% for EECR, 80% for EMPR), 20% path loss (vs. 45%, 55%, 60%), and 90% network lifetime (vs. 60%, 45%, 15%). EEMR high throughput and energy efficiency suit healthcare applications like heart rate monitoring, though EFBOA and DLH-LD complexity may raise overhead, and scalability is underexplored. M-ATTEMPT maintains stable 30% throughput but falters with 45% path loss and 50% energy use. EECR offers 75% throughput yet struggles with 55% path loss and 65% energy consumption. EMPR simplicity is overshadowed by 60% path loss, 80% energy use, and a mere 15% lifetime.

The paper in17 proposes an Enhanced Gray Wolf Optimization (EGWO) method for WBANs in healthcare, optimizing cluster head selection, energy efficiency, and trustworthiness. It features network initialization, comprehensive trust (CT) calculations (direct, indirect, energy-based), and distance-driven energy models, with EGWO balancing trust and efficiency via a fitness function. MATLAB R2022b simulations outperform RRP, MOMHR, AODV using Graphs, and Traditional AODV, yielding 18,430 kbps throughput at 100 rounds (vs. 5000, 2400, 14,400, 1500 kbps), 48.14-s computation time (vs. 100, 3500, 400 secs), higher residual energy, and improved stability (70 live nodes at 1000 rounds vs. 30 dead). An adaptive penalty coefficient (theta) adjusts to abnormal behavior, enhancing trust management. EGWO excels in throughput and efficiency, ideal for healthcare monitoring, but its complexity may limit scalability, and real-world validation is absent. RRP offers moderate throughput (5000 kbps) but higher computation time (100 secs). MOMHR simplicity yields low throughput (2400 kbps). AODV using Graphs achieves 14,400 kbps but with moderate efficiency, while Traditional AODV lags with 1500 kbps and 3500-s computation.

The study in18 presents a clustering control scheme for WBANs using fuzzy logic and Nash equilibrium to enhance scalability, stability, and resource management. It optimizes cluster head (CH) selection, relay and channel choices, and secure transmission via lightweight Diffie–Hellman key exchange and reinforcement learning, outperforming TAFLR, EQRSRL, and SEBA. It achieves 3200 kbps throughput at 1800 s (vs. 2800, 2600, 2900 kbps), 93% packet delivery ratio (vs. 85%, 80%, 88%), 0.43 J energy use at 350 nodes (vs. 0.5, 0.48, 0.45 J), 95% cluster efficiency, and 0.31-s delay. This model shines in throughput, delivery, and security, fitting medical needs, though integrating fuzzy logic and Nash equilibrium may raise overhead, and extreme mobility is undiscussed. TAFLR offers 2,800 kbps throughput and 85% delivery but higher energy use (0.5 J). EQRSRL balances metrics but lags with 2600 kbps and 80% delivery. SEBA 2900 kbps and 88% delivery are offset by scalability challenges.

The authors in 19 propose an energy-efficient WBAN routing protocol using the Levy Flight and Frilled Lizard Optimization (LF2ZO) algorithm, Dynamic Self-executing Active Cross-propagated (DSAC) method for clustering, and Momentum Sparrow Search (MSS) for cluster head (CH) selection, targeting reliability and longevity in IoT healthcare. Two-hop neighbor clustering optimizes transmission distances, while LF2ZO prioritizes high-energy nodes. NS-2 simulations surpass PMQoSR, WMCP, HBCM, and HBCMHBO, with 97% PDR (vs. 86%, 89%, 88%, 90%), 6.2 J energy use at 500 nodes (vs. 7.4, 7.2, 7.0, 6.8 J), 15-s cluster building time (vs. 75, 65, 30, 25 secs), 80-s cluster lifetime (vs. 30, 35, 60, 65 secs), and 4,000-byte overhead (vs. 20,000, 18,000, 10,000, 8000 bytes). It excels in PDR and efficiency, ideal for healthcare, though LF2ZO and MSS complexity may strain computation, and scalability analysis is limited. PMQoSR 86% PDR is offset by 7.4 J energy and 75-s build time. WMCP 89% PDR comes with high overhead (18,000 bytes). HBCM and HBCMHBO offer moderate gains but higher energy and overhead.

The work in20 introduces the Energy Aware Forwarder Selection Technique (EAFST) for WBANs, enhancing stability and lifetime by arranging sensors in concentric rings around a coordinator. High-priority nodes transmit directly, while medium- and low-priority nodes use forwarders chosen by residual energy, queue size, and path loss, with roles rotated to save energy. Simulations outperform DFST, SIMPLE, and PCRP, with 56% better stability (0 dead nodes at 2000 rounds vs. 2, 4, 6), 51% higher throughput (6000 kbps at 14,000 rounds vs. 4000, 3000, 2000 kbps), 68% longer lifetime (12,000 rounds vs. 7000, 6000, 5000), and 38% less energy use (10 J vs. 20, 30, 40 J). EAFST also reduces path loss (50 dB vs. 100, 150, 200 dB) and delay (0.001 s vs. 0.003, 0.004, 0.005 s). It shines in stability and efficiency for healthcare, though multi-hop delays and lack of mobility focus are drawbacks. DFST, SIMPLE, and PCRP offer moderate performance but higher energy use and lower stability.

The Efficient Data Compression for Energy Reduction (EDC-ER) method in21 for WBANs combines Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Run Length Encoding (RLE) to achieve 98% energy savings in biosensors tracking blood pressure, respiration, oxygen (SPO2), and heart rate. It operates in four steps: training an LSTM network on time-series data, generating accurate predictions, computing prediction errors to reduce data size, and applying RLE to compress errors, outperforming Huffman (97%), Arithmetic (80–85%), and LZW (80–85%). EDC-ER excels in predicting repetitive signals like BPDias and respiration with low errors, though it falters with variable oxygen data, showing larger test errors. Its strengths, low complexity, high compression, and efficiency for repetitive data, suit WBAN sensors, but its weakness in handling non-repetitive signals suggests a need for refinement.

Related work on Wireless Body Area Network protocols, spanning references15–21, lays bare a series of critical gaps that threaten their real-world healthcare viability. Mobility remains a blind spot, with protocols like EAFST20 offering no countermeasures for node shifts due to postural changes, think arm swings or torso twists, leaving communication hanging by a thread. Computational complexity and scalability falter as HCEL15 wrestles with intricate parent node selection, EEMR16 juggles the elaborate EFBOA and DLH-LD algorithms, LF2ZO19 layers on DSAC and MSS, and fuzzy logic with Nash equilibrium18 piles up overhead, all while dodging serious scalability scrutiny for sprawling networks. Variable data prediction stumbles in EDC-ER21, where a fixation on repetitive patterns, fine for steady heartbeats, trips over erratic signals like oxygen (SPO2), exposing weak accuracy under pressure. Trust and security languish in most protocols15,16,19–21, save for EGWO17 with its thorough trust calculations and18 with its nimble Diffie–Hellman key exchange, leaving sensitive patient data dangling vulnerably. Energy harvesting is a glaring omission across the board, squandering the potential to stretch lifetimes beyond EEMR 90%16 or EAFST 12,000 rounds20. Real-world testing is nowhere to be found, with all protocols clinging to simulations (e.g., MATLAB, NS-2, NetworkX) as EGWO17 exemplifies, sidestepping practical proof. Multi-hop designs in EAFST20 (0.001 s vs. 0.0005 s direct) and HCEL15 drag down speed with propagation delays, a liability for urgent healthcare needs. QuanBioTrust steps into these breaches with quantum-inspired flair: quantum annealing flexes routing to match mobility dance; quantum neural networks (QNNs) tackle variable data twists, outpacing EDC-ER; quantum key distribution (QKD) fortify security and trust beyond17,18, piezoelectric and thermoelectric harvesting powers longevity, and entanglement-inspired routing cuts delays to a whisper, shows promise as a robust framework for secure WBANs healthcare monitoring.

System model

In this section, we outline the foundational system of the QuanBioTrust protocol by presenting the network architecture and subsequently formalizing the energy optimization problem that underpins its design.

Network model

The QuanBioTrust network model is designed as a heterogeneous Wireless Body Area Network comprising a set of 10–50 miniature sensor nodes strategically deployed on or within the human body, complemented by a single central sink node for data aggregation and external communication, as illustrated in Fig. 1 of the manuscript. Each sensor node is a compact, low-power device (dimensions ~ 1–2 cm2, weight < 5 g) equipped with advanced sensing, processing, and wireless transmission capabilities tailored for monitoring physiological parameters such as electrocardiogram (ECG) signals (360 Hz, 11-bit resolution), blood glucose levels, body temperature, or oxygen saturation (SpO2). These nodes operate under stringent energy constraints, with initial battery capacities of 0.05–0.15 J (equivalent to ~ 10–100 mAh at 1.5 V) and power budgets of 100–500 µW, necessitating ultra-low-power microcontrollers (e.g., ARM Cortex-M0, ~ 10 mW) and transceivers compliant with the IEEE 802.15.6 standard (2.4 GHz ISM band or ultra-wideband (UWB), 3–10 MHz bandwidth)[22]. Communication occurs over short distances (0.1–2 m), contending with significant body-induced challenges, including tissue attenuation (20–40 dB due to muscle, fat, and skin layers), path loss (exponent n = 2–4), and mobility-driven topology changes (± 50% inter-node distance variation due to walking, breathing, or posture shifts). To supplement battery power, each node integrates bio-energy harvesting mechanisms, such as piezoelectric generators (e.g., PVDF films, ~ 20 µW/cm2 from limb motion) and thermoelectric generators (e.g., Bi₂Te₃-based, ~ 30 µW/cm2 from body heat at ~ 310 K), yielding 20–50 µW depending on node placement (e.g., wrist, torso) and user activity level, offsetting ~ 30–40% of operational energy costs (e.g., 3–4 × 106 nJ over 10,000 rounds)23.

The network architecture.

The sink node, typically a more capable device like a smartphone or wearable hub (e.g., positioned at the waist), serves as the central data collection and processing hub with a higher energy capacity (0.5–2 J, equivalent to ~ 100–500 mAh) and enhanced computational resources (e.g., ARM Cortex-A series, > 1 GHz). It interfaces with external systems, such as medical servers or cloud platforms, via Wi-Fi or cellular networks, ensuring secure data transmission for real-time healthcare applications (e.g., arrhythmia detection, insulin dosing). The sink coordinates the WBAN by receiving aggregated data from dynamically selected cluster heads (CHs), which are sensor nodes chosen via Quantum Particle Swarm Optimization based on residual energy, proximity to the sink (0.5–2 m), connectivity degree (3–5 neighbors), trust score (0.5–0.8), and harvesting potential. CHs perform intra-cluster data fusion using slime mold-inspired algorithms (50–70% compression, reducing 200-bit ECG packets to 80–100 bits, ~ 10 nJ/bit) and relay data to the sink through multi-hop paths using firefly-synchronized fractal routing (3–5 paths, ~ 10 nJ/bit), ensuring resilience against link failures due to body mobility. The network topology is highly dynamic, with nodes forming adaptive clusters (90% coverage within 0.5 m of a CH) that reconfigure every 100–500 rounds to accommodate topology changes caused by body movements (e.g., ± 50% distance variation during walking at 1–2 m/s). Intra-cluster communication uses single-hop transmissions (0.1–0.5 m, 100 nJ/bit), while inter-cluster routing to the sink employs multi-hop paths (up to 3 hops, 100 pJ/bit/m2), optimized by QPSO to minimize energy-intensive transmissions and maintain low latency (< 50 ms normal, < 200 ms under attack) and high PDR (97% normal, 90% under attack).

QuanBioTrust

QuanBioTrust is an advanced, quantum-enhanced, bio-inspired trust framework designed to address the critical challenges of energy efficiency, security, and adaptability in next-generation Wireless Body Area Networks through a seven-step, event-driven architecture that seamlessly integrates quantum computing principles, biological intelligence, and zero-trust security. The process begins with network initialization, where each sensor node is provisioned with an initial energy reserve (0.05–0.15 J), bio-energy harvesting capability (20–50 µW from body heat and motion), and a trust score of 0.9, establishing a dynamic foundation for role assignment. This is followed by quantum-enhanced clustering using Quantum Particle Swarm Optimization (QPSO) with entanglement [24], where nodes are modeled as qubits in superposition to evaluate residual energy, proximity to the sink, connectivity, trust, and harvesting potential, enabling energy-efficient and mobility-adaptive cluster head selection. A bio-inspired predator–prey trust model is then employed to continuously monitor node behavior, identifying and isolating malicious “predator” nodes based on packet delivery anomalies and dynamic trust thresholds, ensuring a secure network backbone with minimal overhead. Within clusters, a slime mold-inspired fusion algorithm reduces data redundancy, followed by quantum compression that leverages multi-qubit encoding to shrink ECG packets by 50–70%, with the compressed data entangled with the sink’s quantum state for secure transmission. Simulation setup

The performance evaluation of QuanBioTrust is conducted on a simulated 50-node Wireless Body Area Network (WBAN) using NS-3 for network dynamics and Qiskit for quantum components [27], with nodes deployed across a 0.5 m2 torso area and initialized with 0.1 J of energy (capped at 0.2 J), trust scores of 0.9, and bio-energy harvesting capabilities of 20–50 µW derived from realistic piezoelectric and thermoelectric sources, with 50% variability to reflect activity levels; Result and discussion

This section presents a comprehensive performance evaluation of QuanBioTrust, analyzing key metrics including computational complexity, network lifetime, energy consumption, packet delivery ratio (PDR), security resilience, and latency. To ensure a fair and rigorous comparison, all baseline protocols, HCEL15, EEMR16, EGWO17, Fuzzy Logic/Nash Equilibrium Clustering18, LF2ZO with DSAC/MSS19, EAFST20, and EDC-ER21, were re-implemented and re-simulated within the same experimental framework as QuanBioTrust, using NS-3 for network dynamics and Qiskit for quantum component emulation. All simulations were conducted under identical conditions: a 50-node WBAN processing real ECG data from the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database, with 200-bit packets generated at 10 Hz, initial node energy of 0.1 J (0.2 J capacity), bio-energy harvesting (20–50 µW), and a mobility model featuring ± 50% variation in node-sink distance (0.1–0.8 m). The IEEE 802.15.6 standard was used for communication, with a 2.4 GHz carrier frequency, 0.5 m transmission range, and tissue attenuation modeled at 20–40 dB. All protocols were subjected to the same set of attack scenarios, including eavesdropping, data tampering, node compromise, Denial-of-Service (DoS), and Sybil attacks.

Scalability analysis

As wireless body area networks evolve toward multi-patient monitoring and comprehensive physiological sensing, the scalability of routing protocols becomes a critical determinant of their real-world viability, and while QuanBioTrust demonstrates exceptional performance in a 50-node configuration, achieving a computational complexity of , ~ 500 operations/round, 97% PDR, and 48 ms latency, scaling to larger networks (100–200 nodes) introduces significant challenges in routing overhead, clustering complexity, and end-to-end latency; in a 100-node network under identical simulation conditions (MIT-BIH ECG data, 20–50 µW bio-energy harvesting, IEEE 802.15.6), the current implementation sees the Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR) drop from 97 to 91% and average latency rise to 280 ms under normal conditions, exceeding the 250 ms threshold for real-time ECG monitoring, with further degradation under attack (PDR: 87%, latency > 350 ms), primarily due to two non-linearly scaling components: fractal routing, which generates 3–5 redundant paths per node, leading to over 500 total paths in a 100-node network and causing increased routing table size, control overhead, channel contention, and packet collisions in the 2.4 GHz ISM band, and Quantum Particle Swarm Optimization (QPSO), whose complexity, though superior to quadratic methods, results in ~ 600 operations/round for 100 nodes, with more frequent re-clustering triggered by higher energy variance, thereby amplifying processing delay; in comparison, state-of-the-art protocols exhibit varying scalability: HCEL15 and EAFST20, which employ hierarchical clustering, maintain better stability with moderate latency increases (60 ms → 120 ms) and PDR drops (94% → 90%) due to their constrained cluster scope, while EDC-ER21, with its complexity (~ 300 ops), scales best in computational efficiency but lacks dynamic security, rendering it vulnerable to Sybil and node compromise attacks in large-scale deployments; EGWO17 and Fuzzy/Nash18 face severe scalability bottlenecks, with computational loads exceeding 1000 operations/round and latencies surpassing 500 ms, leading to premature node failure and unreliable data delivery; to address these limitations, QuanBioTrust is designed with a forward-looking scalability roadmap that includes a hierarchical two-tier architecture featuring Super-Cluster Heads (SCHs) managing 10–20 clusters of 10–50 nodes each, reducing the effective QPSO complexity from O(m·log m) to (k = number of SCHs, ~ 10–20), drastically lowering computational load and re-clustering frequency; fractal routing is optimized by capping path multiplicity at 3–4 per node, and reinforcement learning (RL) is integrated to dynamically select high-trust, low-latency routes based on real-time energy, mobility, and security metrics, ensuring efficient and adaptive forwarding; this projected enhancement is expected to maintain PDR > 95% and latency < 100 ms even in 200-node networks, outperforming both classical hierarchical protocols and purely bio-inspired methods, thereby positioning QuanBioTrust not only as a high-performance solution for single-patient monitoring but as a foundational, scalable, and secure framework for future large-scale, real-time biomedical network applications. the framework achieves a network lifetime of 12,800 ± 250 rounds (~ 21.3 days), with the first node failure occurring at 11,500 ± 200 rounds, significantly exceeding HCEL (~ 8000 rounds), EGWO (~ 7500 rounds), Fuzzy/Nash (~ 7,000 rounds), LF2ZO (~ 8500 rounds), and EAFST (~ 9,000 rounds), and rivaling EDC-ER (~ 11,000 rounds), thanks to bio-energy harvesting (20–50 µW, Step 6) and dynamic re-clustering at 20% energy variance (Step 7); under attack, lifetime decreases to 10,500 ± 300 rounds due to zero-trust encryption overhead, but this cost is offset by harvesting replenishing ~ 30% of energy (3 × 106 nJ at 30 µW); total energy consumption is 9.8 × 106 nJ (sensing: 2.5 × 106 nJ, processing: 1.8 × 106 nJ, transmission: 3.8 × 106 nJ, security: 1.7 × 106 nJ), representing ~ 40% savings over HCEL (12.5 × 106 nJ), EEMR (12.3 × 106 nJ), EGWO (13.1 × 106 nJ), Fuzzy/Nash (12.5 × 106 nJ), LF2ZO (12.5 × 106 nJ), and EAFST (12.5 × 106 nJ), achieved through quantum-inspired compression (50–70%, Step 4, reducing 200-bit packets to 80 bits) and fractal routing (3–5 paths, Step 5), though it trails EDC-ER (8.5 × 106 nJ) by 13% due to its security overhead, a trade-off justified by superior adaptability to variable ECG signals (e.g., arrhythmia spikes, detected and isolated in 2–4 rounds); the framework maintains a Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR) of 97% ± 1% under normal conditions and 90% ± 2% under attack, dropping to 85% ± 2% under extreme attacks (> 50% compromise), with an average end-to-end latency of 48 ± 5 ms normally and 185 ± 15 ms under attack, well below the 250 ms threshold for real-time monitoring, outperforming EAFST (1000/1200 ms) and rivaling EDC-ER (50/180 ms); however, limitations exist: performance assumes consistent bio-energy harvesting (~ 30 µW), and in sedentary states (< 10 µW), lifetime may drop to ~ 10,000 rounds; under high interference (> 60 dB) or extreme mobility, PDR may fall below 90% and latency exceed 250 ms; scalability beyond 50 nodes increases fractal routing complexity and QPSO load (up to 600 ops/round for 100 nodes), where hierarchical designs like HCEL and EAFST maintain better stability.

QuanBioTrust, while demonstrating significant advancements in security, energy efficiency, and reliability for WBANs, has several important limitations that stem from its simulation-based evaluation and the inherent trade-offs of its innovative design. The current framework is entirely validated through simulation using NS-3 and Qiskit, meaning its quantum-inspired components (QPSO, entanglement-based encryption) are classically emulated and do not reflect the decoherence, gate fidelity, and hardware constraints of real quantum processors, which remain a significant barrier to immediate deployment. The framework multi-step, bio-inspired architecture, while effective, introduces higher computational complexity (~ 500 operations/round) compared to simpler protocols, which may challenge ultra-low-power microcontrollers in resource-constrained sensor nodes. Scalability is currently limited to around 50 nodes; extending to larger networks increases fractal routing overhead and QPSO optimization time, potentially leading to latency exceeding 250 ms and reduced PDR, necessitating future hierarchical extensions. Performance is also contingent on consistent bio-energy harvesting (20–50 µW), and its effectiveness may diminish for sedentary users where harvesting rates drop below 10 µW, reducing network lifetime. The mobility and channel models, though based on IEEE 802.15.6, are simplified and may not fully capture the dynamic interference and link instability of real-world environments. Finally, the security model, while robust against known attacks, assumes a static threat profile and may be vulnerable to novel, adaptive attacks not present in the current simulation, and the zero-trust mode increased energy cost (20–25 nJ/bit) relies on harvesting to remain sustainable. These limitations highlight the need for future work in hardware prototyping, scalability enhancements, and real-world validation.

In future work, we will transition from classical emulation to real-world quantum-enabling technologies by developing cost-effective quantum-capable sensor nodes (~ $100–$150/node) using Photonic Integrated Circuits (PICs) with CMOS-compatible waveguides and single-photon detectors for lightweight Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) and quantum-enhanced processing (~ 20–25 nJ/bit, < 50 mW), spin-based qubits (e.g., nitrogen-vacancy centers, quantum dots) offering ultra-low power (< 10 µW), room-temperature coherence (> 1 ms at 310 K), and minimal footprint (< 1 cm2), and hybrid classical-quantum System-on-Chip (SoC) designs integrating ARM Cortex-M microcontrollers with photonic or spin modules fabricated via high-volume processes (e.g., TSMC 65 nm), while validating quantum components through IBM Quantum Cloud to test entanglement generation and multi-qubit encoding on real processors under noise; to overcome scalability limitations, where fractal routing (> 5 paths) and QPSO complexity increase latency beyond 250 ms for networks > 50 nodes, we will develop a hierarchical two-tier architecture introducing Super-Cluster Heads (SCHs) managing 10–20 clusters of 10–50 nodes, reducing complexity to ( (k = number of SCHs, ~ 10–20), capping fractal paths to 3–4 per node via reinforcement learning (RL) to prioritize high-trust, low-latency routes, and implementing a distributed QPSO algorithm that parallelizes cluster head selection across SCHs (5–10 ops/SCH, 5 nJ/bit) using quantum-inspired gossip protocols to maintain < 100 ms latency and > 97% PDR in 100–200 node networks; to enhance robustness against real-world variability, particularly the dependence on bio-energy harvesting (20–50 µW), we will conduct a sensitivity analysis under reduced harvesting (5–20 µW) to model sedentary users, assessing impacts on PDR, latency, lifetime, and energy consumption (e.g., rise to 11 × 106 nJ), and mitigate this with micro-supercapacitors (1–10 mF, 0.2 J capacity, η = 0.8–0.9) for energy buffering and hybrid thermal-kinetic harvesters (piezoelectric + thermoelectric) to stabilize output at ~ 30 µW across activity levels, while refining the predator–prey trust model for interference robustness by dynamically adjusting thresholds to maintain PDR > 90% under high noise (> 60 dB); finally, to bridge the simulation-to-reality gap, we will prototype a 10–20 node testbed using open-source tools and COTS hardware (nRF52840), deployed under realistic conditions (± 50% mobility, 0.5–2 m range, 60 dB interference, 30 µW harvesting), and conduct hospital pilot studies to validate performance, targeting > 95% PDR, < 50 ms latency, 95% threat reduction, and 12,000-round lifetime, demonstrating QuanBioTrust superiority over classical protocols like HCEL and EDC-ER in real-world biomedical monitoring, ensuring it evolves from a simulation-validated framework into a practical, scalable, and deployable solution for secure, long-term, real-time healthcare.

Conclusion and future works

QuanBioTrust, a novel Wireless Body Area Network framework, integrates quantum-inspired optimization, bio-energy harvesting, fractal routing, and zero-trust security to deliver superior performance in a 50-node simulation using the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database over 10,000 rounds. It outperforms existing protocols, HCEL, EEMR, EGWO, Fuzzy/Nash, LF2ZO, EAFST, and EDC-ER, across key metrics: computational complexity of O(m·log m) (~ 500 operations/round, 15 nJ/bit) surpasses HCEL (O(m2), ~ 700 ops) and EGWO (O(m·n), ~ 1000 ops); network lifetime of 12,800 rounds (~ 3 weeks) exceeds HCEL (~ 8000) and EDC-ER (~ 11,000) with bio-energy harvesting (20–50 µW); energy consumption of 9.8 × 106 nJ (~ 40% savings) outpaces HCEL (12.5 × 106 nJ); packet delivery ratio (PDR) of 97% (normal) and 90% (attack) surpasses EAFST (94%/85%); security resilience (95.3%, 99.9% eavesdropping protection) exceeds EGWO (70%) and EDC-ER (24%); and latency of 48 ms (normal) and 185 ms (attack) ensures real-time performance (< 250 ms) compared to EAFST (1000/1200 ms) (p < 0.01 vs. EAFST). Validated by statistical significance (p < 0.01) and consistency (standard deviation < 5%), QuanBioTrust sets a new benchmark for scalable, secure biomedical data transmission. However, its performance assumes ideal conditions, such as consistent bio-energy harvesting (20–50 µW) and stable node behavior and may vary in less constrained or erratic environments (e.g., high interference > 60 dB or irregular mobility), potentially reducing PDR to < 90% or increasing latency beyond 250 ms. Challenges include high quantum hardware costs (> $1000/node), vulnerability to extreme attacks (85% PDR at > 50% node compromise), and harvesting variability in sedentary states (< 10 µW). Future work will enhance applicability by developing cost-effective quantum nodes using photonic circuits (~ $100–$200/node), improving resilience to extreme attacks with dynamic trust thresholds (adjusting to 20–80% compromise) to maintain PDR > 90%, stabilizing harvesting with hybrid thermal-kinetic sources for consistent 30 µW output, scaling beyond 50 nodes with hierarchical routing for < 100 ms latency at 100–200 nodes, validating performance under real-world interference and extended ranges, and integrating machine learning for predictive trust and compression to reduce energy to < 8 × 106 nJ and latency to < 40 ms. These advancements aim to solidify QuanBioTrust as a practical, next-generation WBAN solution for real-time biomedical monitoring.
