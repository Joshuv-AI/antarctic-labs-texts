Quality and Timeliness of Pharmacovigilance Reporting in Malawi: A Cross-Sectional Analysis of Individual Case Safety Reports
Authors: Francis Kachidza Chiumia, Frider T Chimimba, Evans Chimayi Chirambo, Ian Peter Manja, Theophilus Rolland Chadza, Wilfred Kantchilala, Peter Mchenga, Happy Magwaza Nyirongo, Wyson Mbewe Mkanda, Felix Khuluza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse events related to use of medicinal products are among the significant causes of morbidity and mortality. Quality of adverse event reports is essential for safety risk management. We therefore analyzed trends, timeliness and completeness of individual case safety reports (ICSRs) at a regional pharmacovigilance (PV) centre in Malawi.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study involving data extraction from ICSR reports that were submitted from 2017 to 2024. The data included patient demographics, adverse events, suspected products and reporter details. ICSR completeness was assessed using the vigiGrade® tool.ResultsThe PV centre received a total of 1,057 ICSRs during the targeted period. These included 940 (88.9%) ADR and 117 (11.1%) AEFI cases. Most of the ADRs were serious (56.1%, n=527) while most of the AEFIs were not serious (58.1%, n=68), p<0.001. Highest reporting rates were recorded in 2022. Most of the ADR cases were related to Isoniazid (16.6%, n=156), followed by tenofovir/lamivudine/dolutegravir (14.5%, n=136). For the AEFIs, most cases were related to pneumococcal conjugate (22.2%, n=26), followed by pentavalent vaccine (21.4%, n=25). The median vigiGrade score for the ADRs was 0.7 (95% CI: 0.7–0.9; IQR: 0.57–1). This was significantly lower (P<0.001) as compared to the AEFIs with a median score of 1 (95% CI: 1–1; IQR: 0.7 −1). There was no significant difference in transmission time between ADR and AEFI reports, p=0.106. The median duration for transmission of ADR reports was 19.5 days (95% CI: 17–21 days; IQR: 6–56 days), while the median time AEFI report transmission was 8 days (95% CI: 3–30.9 days; IQR: 1–156 days).ConclusionThe study highlights poor quality and delayed reporting of adverse events. This may hinder safety signal detection, proper regulatory decision making and compromise patient safety. Strengthening digital reporting mechanisms would enhance quality reporting and streamline timely data flow.Keywords: adverse drug reactions, adverse events following immunization, pharmacovigilance, vigiGrade tool

Background

Adverse events related to the use of medicinal products pose a significant health threat.1 About 16.5% of hospital admissions are attributed to adverse drug reactions and 18.4%–23.6% of hospitalized patients experience at least one adverse event.2,3 Despite vaccines and vaccination being the most successful public health intervention in reducing morbidity and mortality, concerns about their safety remain one of the contributing factors to vaccine hesitancy.4,5 For instance, the Cutter incidence of 1955 remains as one of the most notable vaccine tragedies in history, where more than 220,000 people were infected with live polio and 10 died due to a manufacturing error and thereby significantly disrupting the vaccination campaigns.6 Recently during the COVID-19 pandemic, reports of perceived and actual hematological adverse events following immunization (AEFIs) such as thrombosis and thrombocytopenia syndrome (TTS) also negatively affected the vaccine uptake worldwide.7,8

The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) receives about 2 million adverse event reports annually, of which over 50% of the events cause serious patient outcomes.9 This significantly presses on the health care delivery and budget as over $30 billion is spent on the management of these adverse events.10,11 Most studies done in Africa have reported ADR prevalences of ≥20%, while the prevalences for the AEFIs range from 13% to 34%.4,12–15 This is higher than most of the high-income countries (HICs) and may be attributed to among other factors: high disease burden including HIV/AIDS, poor healthcare systems and high burden of substandard medicines.16–18

A strong pharmacovigilance (PV) system is essential to ensure that these adverse events are timely detected, systematically assessed and appropriate measures are taken to prevent the occurrence of the adverse events.19 The World Health Organization (WHO) established the program for international drug monitoring (PIDM) in 1968 aimed at enhancing the effectiveness of safety data utilization across the globe. PIDM member states establish national PV centres which collect safety data from healthcare workers, pharmaceutical companies, patients and the general public. This safety data is shared for global use through a centralized WHO database, the VigiBase® which is based at the WHO collaborating centre in Uppsala, Sweden (referred to as the Uppsala Monitoring Centre).

Correct interpretation of pharmacovigilance data is critical for safety signal validation, assessments and the subsequent clinical and regulatory decision making.20 The signal management process therefore relies on the quality of the data that is submitted to the pharmacovigilance centre.21 The European Medicines Agency and FDA guidelines on good pharmacovigilance practices emphasizes on the need for timely submission of individual case safety reports (ICSRs) and maintaining completeness and accuracy of the data.22,23

Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) generally struggle to generate and use local safety data to inform policy, regulation and clinical practice. This is due to several challenges including limited knowledge and skills for good pharmacovigilance practices among healthcare professionals, low adverse event reporting rates, poor quality of reports, insufficient resources and lack of regional harmonization.24 The pharmacovigilance system in Malawi is relatively still in its infancy, with very few or no dedicated pharmacovigilance staff at all levels.25

At least 50% of healthcare providers in Malawi have very limited pharmacovigilance knowledge.26 In addition, the pharmacovigilance system is still not fully integrated and institutionalized in public health programs and most of health care facilities.24 Due to the limited awareness and accessibility of modern electronic tools, spontaneous reporting of safety cases in Malawi mostly relies on paper-based tools.26 Digital platforms such as medsafe360 which applies unstructured supplementary service data system are available for reporting, but their usage remains minimal. This raises concerns about the efficiency, quality and timeliness of transferring data from health facilities to the PV centre. We therefore analysed the trends and completeness of ADR and AEFI cases submitted to a regional PV centre based at a medical university in Malawi.

Methodology

Study Design and Setting

This was a cross-sectional study conducted at the regional PV centre in Blantyre, Malawi. The regional PV centre is based at the Kamuzu University of Health Sciences (KUHeS) and was previously the national PV centre for Malawi. Whilst KUHeS provides technical and operational support for running the centre, the Pharmacy and Medicines Regulatory Authority (PMRA) is responsible for the regulatory oversight of the centre. In Malawi, initial work to establish the basic activities for a national PV centre at KUHeS was started in 2015. However, these efforts were significantly enhanced in 2016 through partnership with GlaxoSmithKline (GSK) and Program for Appropriate Technology in Health (PATH).27 By 2017, the PV centre had made significant improvements in the rate of ADR reporting and other PV activities, enabling the country to meet the requirements of attaining full membership of the WHO program for international drug monitoring (PIDM); an achievement that was officially recognized in 2019.28 As of 2021, the national PV centre was moved to the PMRA central offices in Lilongwe and the KUHeS centre continued to operate, but as a regional PV centre for the southern Malawi. In this study, we included all reports submitted and retained at the regional centre from 2017 to 2024. Duplicate reports were removed from the study.

Data Collection

Data was collected from the local data repository at the centre. The source of the data was from ICSRs which were submitted using the PMRA standardized adverse event reporting forms. Authors IM and TC extracted data from the physical reporting forms using a data abstraction form. This included available patient and reporter information, description of the adverse event, suspected and concomitant medicines and reporting information such as dates for reporting and receiving the report at the PV centre. Details which were missing on the form were recorded as such for further analysis. The authors further assessed the completeness of the ICSRs using the WHO vigiGrade® manually and provided a completeness score for each ICSR. Differences in terms of assessment were resolved through discussion between the assessors. Where necessary, a third author was involved to reach a consensus. The vigiGrade assessment tool provides multi-dimensional criteria developed by the WHO Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC). It is a systematic and objective measure of completeness of clinically relevant information that are available on the reporting forms. This includes the age of patient, sex, indication for suspected medicines, administered doses, patient outcomes and health facility location.29 The maximum completeness score for an ICSR is 1, and the scores decrease systematically for each piece of information that is not provided on the reporting form. A report with a vigiGrade score ≥0.8 is considered as well documented.30

Data Analysis

All data were entered in Microsoft Excel and analyzed using STATA 17.0 for both descriptive and inferential statistics. Categorical variables including missing information were quantified in terms of percentages. Associations between the categorical variables were assessed using chi square test. Continuous variables were firstly tested for normality using both histogram plots and the skewness and kurtosis test. Variables such as vigiGrade scores and transmission time were not normally distributed and therefore presented in terms of medians (with 95% confidence intervals (CI)) and interquartile ranges (IQR). Differences among the non-normally distributed variables were tested using the Mann Whitney and Kruskal Wallis tests if there were only two groups and more than two groups, respectively. In all scenarios, a p-value ≤0.05 was considered as significant. All data visualizations were done in R using the ggplot2 package.

Ethical Approval and Considerations

Research ethics approval was granted by the institutional review board of the Kamuzu University of Health Sciences (College of Medicine Research and Ethics Committee) under study number P.12/24-1306 in February 2025. The study was approved in line with the principles of the Helsinki declaration. Access to data complied with relevant data protection regulations and confidentiality was maintained throughout the study.

Results

Demographics and Reporter Characteristics

The regional PV centre received a total of 1,057 ICSRs during the targeted period (2017–2024). These included 940 (88.9%) suspected ADRs and 117 (11.1%) AEFI cases. At least 18.6% (n=197) of the adverse events occurred among children and 59.4% (n=628) occurred among females. Most of the AEFIs occurred in children, ≤5 years (87.2%, n=108) while most of the ADRs were reported among adult patients, above twenty years (82.3%, n=820), p< 0.001. By sex, 61.1% (n=574) of ADRs occurred among females and 37.9% (n=356) among males, p= 0.024 (Table 1). Overall, most of the ADRs were reported by student pharmacists (37.9%, n=357) followed by clinicians (16.5%, n=155) and nurses (10.7%, n=100). For the AEFIs, majority of the cases were reported by HSAs (49.6%, n= 58) followed by clinicians (8.6%, n=10) and nurses (3.4%, n=4). Of all the cases, 53.5% (n=565) were serious, 40.1% (n=424) were not serious and 6.4% (n=68) were not classified. Majority of the reported ADR cases were serious (56.1%) while majority of the reported AEFI cases were not serious (58.1%), p<0.001. On case management, the most common action taken was withdrawing the suspected agent (61.4%, n=529). At the time of reporting, at least 37.7% of the subjects with ADRs had recovered or were recovering while about 60% of the subjects with AEFIs had recovered or were recovering, p<0.001.

ADRs and AEFI Reporting Patterns

There was an unstable pattern of safety case reporting over the period under review. Lowest reporting rates were recorded in 2017, with only 36 ICSRs and later in 2019 and 2021 with 52 and 18 ICSRs, respectively. Across the years, ADR reports were higher than AEFIs except in 2021 where the regional PV centre received 15 AEFIs against 3 ADRs (Figure 1).

Reporting trends for ADRs and AEFIs from 2017 to 2024.

Most of the ADR cases were related to Isoniazid (16.6%, n=156), followed by fixed combination of tenofovir/lamivudine/dolutegravir (TDF/3TC/DTG) (14.5%, n=136) and cotrimoxazole (8.6%, n=81). ADRs related to Isoniazid were mostly dermatological cases such as skin rashes (21.7%, n= 50) followed by numbness of extremities (7.4%, n=17). For TDF/3TC/DTG regimen, the most prominent ADRs were skin rashes (7.4%%, n= 16) and erectile dysfunction (7.4%, n=16). Almost all the reported ADRs related to cotrimoxazole were itching and skin rashes. As for the AEFI cases, most of them were related to pneumococcal conjugate (22.2%, n=26), followed by pentavalent (21.4%, n= 25) and oral polio (9.4%, n=11) vaccines. Common events related to pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) were abscesses (45.9%, n= 17) and fever (29.7%, n =11). Similarly, abscesses and fever were the top AEFIs related to pentavalent vaccine, with an occurrence in 51.3% (n=20) and 25.6% (n=10) of the cases respectively. Completeness of the Safety Reports

All valid cases included in the analysis contained at least some information for patient and reporter identification, generic name of suspected medicine and description of the adverse event. Important missing information in some reports included date of onset (33.3%, n=352), reporter’s profession (19%, n=201), action taken by the healthcare provider as part of case management, route of administration (16.8%, n=178), outcome of event (15.4%, n= 163), administered dose (14.9%, n= 157), patient’s sex (1.9%, n= 20) and age (1.2%, n=13) (Figure 2).

Showing missing information on individual case safety reports. ROA = Route of Administration.

Overall, the median vigiGrade completeness score for the reports was 0.9 (95% CI: 0.7–0.9; IQR: 0.63–1). As shown in Figure 3A, the median vigiGrade score for the ADRs was 0.7 (95% CI: 0.7–0.9; IQR: 0.57–1). This was significantly lower (P<0.001) as compared to the AEFIs with a median vigiGrade score of 1 (95% CI: 1–1; IQR: 0.7−1). There was no significant difference in the completeness of the reporting form between serious and non-serious cases, P=0.344. The median vigiGrade score for serious cases was 0.9 (95% CI: 0.7–0.9; IQR: 0.63–1), while the median vigiGrade score for non-serious cases was 0.8 (95% CI: 0.57–1; IQR: 0.57–1) (Figure 3B). There were significant differences in the levels of completeness among the cases reported by various health cadres. The median vigiGrade score was 0.8 (95% CI: 0.7–1; IQR: 0.5–1) for pharmacists or pharmacy technicians; 0.7 (95% CI: 0.7–0.9; IQR: 0.5–1) for nurses; 0.7 (95% CI: 0.7–0.7; IQR: 0.5−1) for clinicians or medical doctors; 1 (95% CI: 0.77–1; IQR: 0.7–1) for Health Surveillance Assistants (HSAs); and 1(95% CI: 1–1; IQR: 0.7–1) for pharmacy students (Figure 3C).

Completeness of individual case safety reports based on the WHO vigiGrade Scores categorized by type of ICSR (A), seriousness of event (B) and reporter cadre (C).

Transmission Time for ICSRs

The median transmission time from the healthcare facilities to the PV centre was 19 days (95% CI: 16–21 days; IQR: 5–58 days). There was no significant difference between the transmission times of reports from the health facilities to the PV centre. The median transmission duration for ADRs was 19.5 days (95% CI: 17–21 days; IQR: 6–56) while for the AEFI reports was 8 days (95% CI: 3–30.9 days; IQR: 1–156 days). Seriousness of the adverse event did not also affect the urgency for the ICSR submission to the PV centre. For serious reports, the median duration for submission was 20 days (95% CI: 18–23 days; IQR: 6−67.5 days) while for non-serious reports, the median duration for transmission was 14 days (95% CI: 9–19 days; IQR: 3–41 days) (Figure 4).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the trends of ADR and AEFI reports submitted to a regional PV centre in southern Malawi from 2017 to 2024. The centre was operating as the national PV centre from the time PV was established in Malawi until 2021, when the national centre was moved to the PMRA head offices in central Malawi. Thus, the reports received at the centre from 2017 to 2021 reflect the national reporting rates during that period. From 2021, ICSRs in Malawi were submitted either directly to the national PV centre or through the regional PV centre. This mostly depends on the convenience to the reporter. We also applied the WHO vigiGrade completeness scores to assess the level of completeness of the ICSRs. The vigiGrade is a comprehensive tool that allows the objective evaluation of data completeness based on available information on the ICSRs. The vigiGrade score reflects the amount, dimension and clinical relevance of the data. A vigiGrade score of ≥0.8 (out of a total of 1) is considered well documented.

Overall, we found significantly low reporting rates for adverse events in Malawi. In the presence of a fully functional and effective PV system, the WHO requires that at least 200 reports should be submitted per 1 million population.31 With a population of over 9.5 million people,32,33 the southern region of Malawi is therefore required to submit at least 1,900 reports every year. With the reported average of 142 ICSRs per year, the ICSR reporting rate is about 14.9 per million population, which is almost 14-fold below the WHO recommendations. This is a common challenge in LMICs countries where majority of the countries fail to meet the WHO requirement on the reporting of adverse events. For instance in 2019, the reporting rate was 174 per million population in Serbia,34 99 per million in Turkey,35 35 per million in Kenya, 6.7 per million in Ethiopia, and 4.1 per million in Tanzania.36 In comparison with HICs in Europe, the reporting rates in 2019 were 466.7, 448.6 and 111.5 per million population for Denmark, Netherlands and the United Kingdom, respectively.37 As of 2015, the FDA adverse events reporting system (FAERS) data indicated a reporting rate of about 6,100 per million inhabitants in the USA.9

Unlike in most of the HICs where patients contribute to the reporting of almost half of the national ICSRs,37 most of the reports in Malawi come from health care professionals.38 This may be due to a critical lack of awareness among the public. The unstable trend of adverse events reporting reported in this study may be explained by the levels of knowledge and/or motivation among the health care professionals. In our previous survey, we noted a drastic increase in the reporting rate for adverse events after conducting basic pharmacovigilance trainings among the health care workers.26 These trainings primarily targeted anti-retroviral treatment (ART) healthcare providers. Consequently, it is noted that the most common ADRs reported were related to the first-line ART regimen in Malawi (TDF/3TC/DTG) and anti-tuberculosis (TB) medicines, as TB is one of the most common opportunistic infections among people living with HIV/AIDS. Similar results were however also found in Zimbabwe where ART regimens and anti-TB medicines were among the top medicines related to ICSRs.39 On the other hand, most of the adverse events in HICs are related to non-communicable disease and life style medicines such as antidiabetics and weigh loss medications.

The drastic increase in the ICSR reporting rates in 2018 and 2022 may be attributable to the in-service PV trainings that were conducted by the KUHeS and PMRA over the years.26 Between 2019 and 2022, there was an increasing trend for AEFI reports while ADRs reporting rate significantly decreased. This is may be due to the active surveillance program for AEFIs that was initiated during the COVID-19 pandemic by the Ministry of Health and other agencies and partners.40–42 Similar strategies were also applied in most countries across the globe and led to the dramatic increase in the AEFI reports related to COVID-19 and other vaccines as well.43–45

In terms of ICSR documentation and transmission of reports, our findings are similar to most of the other LMICs. Zimbabwe was the only LMIC reporting well documented ADR reports, with a median vigiGrade score of 0.9.39 On the other hand, a study in Sierra Leone showed that about 50% of ADR reports were poorly documented and took longer than 30 days to be transmitted from the health facility to the PV centre. Further delays of up to 180 days were also incurred to commission the reports into the WHO vigibase.46 Another similar study in South Africa found that over 80% of the ADR reports were poorly documented with a mean vigiGrade score of 0.45.30 It is important to note that Malawi and Sierra Leone are at WHO maturity level 1, while Zimbabwe and South Africa are at maturity level3.47

Our study was different from the others as we also included AEFI reports in our analysis. The findings showed that AEFI reports were well documented as compared to the ADR reports. This may be due to the intensive trainings and supervisions for HSAs, who are responsible for administration of vaccines in communities by the Extended Program for Immunization.40 Similarly, the high vigiGrade scores for reports submitted by pharmacy students may be attributable to the intensive pre-service pharmacovigilance training which is included in the revised curriculum for pharmacy training in Malawi.26

Limitations

The study faces limitations due to the study design and administrative changes. Our retrospective reliance on paper-based reports and possible under-reporting risks missing out some data. This could be either through reporting bias of poor transcription of data during extraction. Further, the change of the PV centre from a national to regional centre in 2021 may have affected the flow of data.

Conclusion

This study highlights poor quality and delayed reporting of adverse events. This may hinder safety signal detection, proper regulatory decision making and compromise patient safety. This underscores the need to strengthen the reporting mechanisms in the country. To achieve this, we recommend maximizing the use of available digital or mobile-based systems such as the medsafe360 to reduce delays, providing regular training and feedback to healthcare workers, enhancing collaboration between health facilities and the pharmacovigilance centre to streamline data flow and establishing accountability and monitoring mechanisms to ensure timely submission of reports.
