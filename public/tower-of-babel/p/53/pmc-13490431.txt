Effectiveness of Hybrid Surgery Using Polyetheretherketone Rods for Preventing Proximal Junctional Kyphosis and Failure After Adult Spinal Deformity Surgery: Biomechanical Evaluation
Authors: Chao Li, Yao Zhao, Xiangning Meng, Longtao Qi, Beiyu Xu, Chunde Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK) and proximal junctional failure (PJF) are common complications of adult spinal deformity (ASD) surgery. Hybrid fixation techniques have emerged as a potential solution to mitigate this risk. However, the biomechanical benefits of hybrid rod fixation in long-segment spinal surgery have not been fully explored or validated. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of hybrid fixation using both polyetheretherketone (PEEK) rods and titanium rods on the increased incidence of PJK and PJF after long-segment ASD surgery using finite element analysis. A three-dimensional finite element model of the adult spine (T6-S1) was developed to simulate the biomechanical effects of different fixation techniques. An intact spine (model A) and two internal fixation configurations were modeled: model B incorporated titanium rods from T10 to S1 and model C incorporated PEEK rods from T10 to T11 with titanium rods from T11 to S1. Then, the resulting stresses on the intervertebral discs, facet joints, pedicle screws, and fixation rods were analyzed. Under six physiological motion states, intervertebral mobility and maximum von Mises stress on the adjacent segment (T9-T10) intervertebral disc, endplate, and facet joint was greater in models B and C than model A. Furthermore, intervertebral mobility and maximum von Mises stress on the adjacent segment intervertebral disc and facet joint were greater in model B than model C. Under all physiological motion conditions except for extension, the maximum von Mises stress on the adjacent segment endplate was greater in model B than model C. Hybrid fixation using PEEK and titanium rods shows promise for reducing the risk of PJK and PJF after ASD surgery. By decreasing intervertebral mobility and concentration of stress on the adjacent segment, hybrid fixation could improve long-term surgical outcomes and reduce the need for revision surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50653-5.

Introduction

Adult spinal deformity (ASD) surgery frequently requires long-segment spinal fixation (thoracic and lumbar); however, long-segment fixation carries a significant risk of proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK)1–6. PJK is characterized by abnormal kyphotic angulation at the proximal junction between the instrumented and non-instrumented spinal segments and can progress to structural failure. This is known as proximal junctional failure (PJF), which may require revision surgery. The high stiffness of titanium rods, which are traditionally used in spinal fixation, is believed to contribute to concentration of stress at the proximal junction. This concentration increases the risk of junctional complications7.

Hybrid fixation techniques utilizing polyetheretherketone (PEEK) rods in combination with titanium rods have emerged as a potential solution to mitigate this risk6–8. PEEK rods are less stiff than titanium rods and exhibit greater elasticity, which may help reduce the concentration of stress at the proximal junction and prevent PJK and PJF9–13. However, the biomechanical benefits of hybrid rod fixation in long-segment spinal surgery have not been fully explored or validated. This finite element analysis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of using PEEK and titanium rods in reducing the incidence of PJK and PJF after long-segment ASD surgery by simulating different fixation strategies to assess stress distribution and mechanical behavior at the proximal junction. The results will provide insight into the effects of hybrid fixation on surgical outcomes and complications in patients undergoing ASD.

Materials and methods

Three-dimensional Computed Tomography (CT) reconstruction of a thoracolumbar spine model

First, CT DICOM data of a normal spine was obtained by scanning a healthy volunteer with no thoracolumbar disease or history of spine trauma or surgery (slice thickness, 0.625 mm). Data extraction and processing were conducted using Mimics 21.0 software (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) and an STL model of the spine from T6 to S1 was reconstructed (Fig. 1). The STL file was then imported into Geomagic Studio (https://geomagic-studio.software.informer.com) for processing (repair, noise reduction, and surface smoothing) to produce a three-dimensional solid geometric model of the intact T6-S1 segments (model A). Intervertebral discs, endplates, and facet joints were reverse-engineered based on the model (Fig. 2). To investigate the biomechanical effect of different internal fixation systems on the onset of PJK and PJF, two internal fixation models were also developed: model B incorporated titanium rods from T10 to S1 and model C incorporated PEEK rods from T10 to T11 with titanium rods from T11 to S1 (Fig. 3).

Finite element meshing and material parameter settings

The STL files of the three models were imported into Hypermesh 14.0 software (Altair, Troy, MI, USA) for meshing and BDF files were exported. These files were then imported into Patran 2019 software (MSC Software, Newport Beach, CA, USA) for finite element preprocessing, where finite element mesh nodes were used to model each ligament in the thoracolumbar spine, including the anterior longitudinal ligament, posterior longitudinal ligament, ligamentum flavum, capsular ligament, interspinous ligament, supraspinous ligament, and intertransverse ligament. The ligaments were treated as spring elements with equivalent nonlinear elastic properties subjected to tension only and not compression14. Finite element mesh attributes, material parameters, load applications, and boundary condition constraints were defined. The models were then transferred to Nastran 2019 software (MSC Software) for analysis and results review. Finite element mesh models are illustrated in Fig. 4.

Finite element mesh models. (A-D) Anterior, posterior, and lateral views. (E & F) Perspective views. (G & H) Localized enlargement.

For each of the models, the vertebral bodies, facet joints, intervertebral discs, superior and inferior endplates, and rod-screw systems were meshed using solid elements. The screw model diagram and the detailed parameters of the rod-screw systems are shown in the supplementary materials. Intervertebral discs (nucleus pulposus and annulus fibrosus) were meshed with Hex8 hexahedral elements, while other structures were meshed with Tet4 tetrahedral elements. All ligaments were represented with 1D two-node nonlinear spring elements. The mesh node and element counts are provided in Table 1. Bone, cartilage, endplates, intervertebral discs, and rod-screw systems were defined as isotropic, homogeneous, continuous linear elastic materials based on data from previous studies14–20. During theoretical modeling, bonded contact with shared nodes was implemented at the interfaces between the pedicle screw-rod fixation systems and between the fixation systems and the vertebral bones. This configuration ensures complete locking engagement between screws and rods, preventing relative loosening during motion and thus minimizing interference with the primary biomechanical analysis. The annulus fibrosus of the intervertebral disc contains 5 layers of annular fiber units, and these are nonlinear units that are only subjected to tension and not compression. The nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral disc was assigned hyperelastic properties. Ligaments were modeled as nonlinear spring elements. Specific parameters are shown in Tables 2 and 314,15,18,20–24.

Loads and boundary conditions

Boundary conditions were applied to the finite element model as follows: (1) A fixed constraint was applied to the sacroiliac joint surfaces on both sides of the sacrum, restricting all six degrees of freedom to simulate a stationary sacrum. (2) A uniform vertical load of 150 N was applied to the upper surface of the T6 vertebral body, simulating the axial load generated by body weight above the thoracolumbar spine in an upright position22,25. Additionally, an external moment of 7.5 N m was applied to simulate various thoracolumbar biomechanical states under flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation23.

Biomechanical finite element analysis of thoracolumbar internal fixation

Von Mises stress was used to analyze structural stress, serving as a criterion for plastic yield. The von Mises stress model is simple to express and provides conservative results, making it widely applicable in fields such as engineering mechanics and biomechanics. Data analyses

Prior to formal computation, mesh convergence analysis was performed for all finite element models, meeting the convergence criterion for finite element simulations (Supplementary Materials). Biomechanical finite element simulation was conducted on the three models and the von Mises equivalent stress distribution patterns (stress contour plots in MPa) were obtained for intervertebral discs, endplates, rod-screw systems, bone-screw interfaces, and facet joints under six motion states: flexion, extension, left and right lateral bending, and left and right rotation.

Results

Model validation

To verify the appropriateness and accuracy of the modeling methods, simulation settings, boundary conditions, and model simplifications, model A (normal thoracolumbar spine) was first validated. The aim was to assess the rationality of the model’s settings and boundary conditions. According to previous literature, the same load and boundary conditions were applied to the superior surface of T6, while the inferior surface of T12 was fixed to simulate physiological motion, thereby validating the thoracic spine model26,27. In addition, this study primarily focuses on the T9-T10 segment; therefore, a separate validation of the T9-T10 segment was performed in accordance with the methods reported in the literature28. A vertical load of 200 N and a moment of 7.5 N·m were applied to the superior surface of the T12 vertebra, while the inferior surface of the L2 vertebra was subjected to a fixed constraint29. Additionally, the same load and boundary conditions (400 N vertical pressure and moment of 10 N·m) were applied to the superior surface of the L1 vertebra16,20,23,30–32. Biomechanical simulation analyses of the thoracolumbar spine were performed under flexion, extension, lateral bending, and rotation and compared with those of a previous in vitro study20,23,30–32. As shown in Fig. 5, by comparing the relative range of motion under different movement conditions, it was demonstrated that the thoracolumbar spine model is accurate and appropriate.

Compared with model A, the mean maximum stress across the six states of motion was 207.8% greater for model B and 123.8% greater for model C. Discussion

The current study provides valuable biomechanical insights into the use of hybrid fixation systems that combine PEEK rods with traditional titanium ones. Such systems should mitigate the risks of PJK and PJF after ASD surgery. Both complications can require revision surgery and adversely affect patient outcomes33–35. By evaluating stress distribution across various spinal structures, our study contributes to a deeper understanding of how hybrid fixation strategies can influence mechanical behavior at the proximal junction and adjacent segments.

Biomechanical efficacy of hybrid fixation

Our finite element analysis demonstrated that the hybrid model showed a notable decrease in von Mises stress on the T9-T10 intervertebral disc, endplate, and facet joints, align with the conclusions of Kang et al.36. Their biomechanical study on spinal fixation systems demonstrated that PEEK rods, due to their lower modulus of elasticity (3500 MPa vs. 110000 MPa for titanium, as in our Table 2), reduced stress concentration at adjacent segments more effectively than traditional titanium rods. This consistency supports that our observation of mitigated stress at the proximal junction is not isolated but corroborates existing evidence on the mechanical benefits of PEEK materials.

The reduced stress on the adjacent segment in the hybrid model is of critical importance, as excessive stress can accelerate degenerative changes in the intervertebral discs and facet joints, both of which are key factors contributing to PJK and PJF. This biomechanical benefit aligns with previous studies that have highlighted the potential of semi-rigid fixation systems to preserve adjacent segment function and reduce postoperative complications4,7,36,37. Given that PEEK rods exhibit more flexibility and have a lower modulus of elasticity than titanium, they may act as a biomechanical buffer, modulating the abrupt transition in stiffness between the fused and non-fused segments38. This result is particularly relevant in the context of long-segment fixation, where the rigidity of titanium rods can exacerbate stress accumulation at the proximal junction, leading to mechanical failure over time7.

Impact on intervertebral mobility and spinal stability

Maintaining physiological motion patterns is critical for a healthy spine39. Excessive intervertebral mobility can lead to accelerated degeneration in adjacent segment structures, especially the intervertebral discs and facet joints, which is a key contributor to postoperative complications40,41. The hybrid fixation system’s ability to reduce mobility in adjacent segments may help prevent these degenerative processes from occurring. In contrast, the traditional titanium rod fixation model exhibited greater mobility at the non-fused segments, resulting in an uneven mechanical load distribution and heightened stress concentration. In vivo, this would increase the risk of adjacent segment degeneration and instability7,42.

The ability to control excessive intervertebral mobility is particularly important for preventing PJK and PJF over the long term43,44. Excessive adjacent segment mobility is frequently associated with increased wear on the intervertebral discs, facet joint degeneration, and endplate deterioration, all of which may ultimately lead to surgical failure and require revision surgery43,44. Our finding that hybrid fixation controlled excessive intervertebral mobility is in line with Viswanathan et al.37. Their study on proximal junctional semi-rigid fixation noted that semi-rigid constructs (similar to our hybrid PEEK-titanium design) balanced spinal stability and mobility better than rigid titanium systems, thereby reducing the risk of adjacent segment degeneration—a key precursor to PJK/PJF. This comparison reinforces that our hybrid fixation’s ability to modulate mobility is consistent with established biomechanical principles of semi-rigid spinal fixation. Additionally, Turbucz et al. conducted a finite element study on preventing PJK via semirigid rostral fixation, and their result that semirigid constructs reduced proximal junction stress by ~ 30–40% relative to rigid fixation is comparable to our data (e.g., model C showed a ~ 41.4% reduction in mean disc stress vs. model B across six motion states). This quantitative alignment further validates the effectiveness of our hybrid fixation strategy in mitigating PJK/PJF risks43. Therefore, the hybrid fixation model’s advantage in maintaining more natural motion dynamics suggests that it should reduce the incidence of postoperative degenerative conditions related to increased mobility. In addition, its unique design, which appropriately modulates the mobility of non-fused segments, not only mitigates the risk of postoperative complications, but also improves long-term spinal stability and functional recovery.

Clinical implications and future directions

The results of this study carry targeted and substantial clinical implications for long-segment thoracolumbar fixation in ASD surgery. The biomechanical advantages of our customized hybrid fixation model (Model C: PEEK rods applied at T10-T11 and titanium rods used from T11 to S1)—namely the significant reduction in von Mises stress and intervertebral mobility at the T9-T10 proximal junctional adjacent segment—directly target the core mechanical causes of PJK and PJF (stress concentration and excessive motion at the proximal junction). This confirms that the hybrid fixation strategy is a precisely optimized solution for ASD patients requiring long-segment fixation extending to the sacrum (T10-S1), especially those with poor bone quality or high risk of proximal junctional complications. However, while our finite element analysis provides robust biomechanical evidence, in vivo clinical validation is still needed before this strategy is widely applied in clinical practice.

Future clinical trials should focus on prospectively enrolling ASD patients undergoing long-segment fixation with this PEEK-titanium hybrid rod system to observe long-term outcomes, especially the incidence of PJK, PJF and adjacent segment degeneration. In addition, the potential of this hybrid system to reduce revision surgery rates caused by proximal junctional failure deserves further exploration, because revision surgery is accompanied by higher surgical risk, patient suffering and medical costs. With clinical verification of its effect in reducing proximal junctional complications, this segment-specific hybrid fixation technique is expected to become a routine method for ASD patients at high risk of PJK and PJF, so as to improve the long-term safety and prognosis of surgery.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The finite element model used, while highly detailed, cannot fully replicate the complexities of variations in individual patient anatomy, bone quality, or biological responses to the implants. Nor does it account for the impact of factors such as muscle forces, ligament properties, or patient-specific spinal curvatures, which may affect the real-world performance of the fixation systems. Additionally, the results of finite element analysis are based on idealized loading conditions and may not reflect the full range of dynamic loads experienced by patients during daily activities. The sacral slope was derived from supine CT data rather than standing radiographs, which may affect sagittal alignment and load distribution. Future investigations incorporating standing imaging and subject-specific sagittal alignment may further improve the physiological relevance of the model. Despite these limitations, the study provides critical insights into the biomechanical advantages of hybrid fixation using PEEK and titanium rods, offering a solid foundation for future clinical research.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling biomechanical evidence in favor of using hybrid fixation with PEEK and titanium rods for treating ASD. By reducing concentration of stress at the adjacent segments, the hybrid system offers a promising solution for preventing PJK and PJF. While further clinical validation is needed, the results of our analysis provide evidence for the ability of hybrid fixation systems to improve long-term surgical outcomes, reduce postoperative complications, and enhance patient quality of life.
