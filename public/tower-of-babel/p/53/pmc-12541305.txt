Apparent learning biases emerge from optimal inference: Insights from master equation analysis
Authors: Prakhar Godara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceUnderstanding human decision-making in uncertain environments is crucial for fields ranging from cognitive science to artificial intelligence. Recent studies suggest that human behavior in two-armed bandit tasks is shaped by positivity and confirmation biases, implying suboptimal information integration. However, we show that these biases also emerge in agents performing optimal Bayesian inference. This challenges existing interpretations, suggesting that previously reported biases may stem from model-based artifacts rather than true psychological effects. Additionally, we demonstrate the power of the master equation framework in analyzing the stochastic learning dynamics of RL algorithms. Finally, our study suggests novel experiments to disentangle genuine cognitive biases from optimal learning mechanisms in humans.

Studying human decision-making in idealized experimental settings has been a cornerstone of both behavioral economics (1–5) and cognitive science (6–10). One particularly useful paradigm for such investigations is the N-armed Bernoulli bandit [called two armed Bernoulli bandit (TABB) when N=2] task (11), where participants must balance exploration and exploitation to maximize rewards. Each action (or arm) offers binary outcomes (zero or one) with unknown probabilities, and the subject’s goal is to maximize cumulative rewards over T trials.

This task captures the explore–exploit dilemma faced by all decision-makers operating in uncertain environments. Its simplicity has made it a widely studied paradigm in behavioral studies, where researchers explore not only optimal decision strategies but also how real human behavior deviates from this ideal. Recently, the TABB task has also been used to study how people make inferences from stochastic feedback, and research has revealed the presence of cognitive biases in human learning-most notably, positivity and confirmation biases. These biases have repeatedly been observed in adult humans (12–17), adolescents (18), rodents (19), and macaques (20) to name a few. These findings are based on fitting a reinforcement learning framework, Q-learning, to subject behavior data.

Q-learning, which has been linked to dopaminergic activity in the brain (21), describes how agents update their expectations of reward from chosen and unchosen options based on the difference between expected and received outcomes. The model can be expressed as[1]Qv,t+1=Qv,t+α+v·(rv,t−Qv,t),if (rv,t−Qv,t)>0,α−v·(rv,t−Qv,t),if (rv,t−Qv,t)<0.

where v∈{c,u} denotes either the chosen arm (c) or the unchosen arm (u), and rv,t is the observed reward at time t (corresponding to the v arm). Here, we have written the Q-learning in a general manner, accounting for tasks with (called “Experiment-2” in ref. 12) and without (“Experiment-1”) counterfactual information.* In particular for tasks without counterfactual information, we have α±u=0.

The parameters α+v (for positive prediction errors) and α−v (for negative prediction errors) are learning rates, dictating how quickly the agent adjusts its expectations based on the outcome. However, Q-learning is not the only framework used to model human behavior. Another influential approach is Bayesian inference, in which agents update their beliefs in a statistically optimal manner based on the likelihood of new evidence. Because Bayesian models are often considered the benchmark of rational inference (22), they have become central to many cognitive science models of decision-making (23, 24). While Bayesian inference operates differently from Q-learning, one might naturally expect that if we fit a Q-learning model to data generated by a Bayesian agent, the inferred learning rates would be unbiased—after all, a rational learner should not exhibit systematic distortions. In other words, if our methodology for detecting biases is sound, it should classify Bayesian inference as an unbiased process. Yet, as we show in Fig. 1A, this is not the case.

When fitting the Q-learning model to behavior generated by a Bayes-optimal agent (described in Section 1), we still observe the same biases—positivity and confirmation†-commonly found in human data. This result raises an important question: Are these biases inherent to human cognition, or do they emerge as an artifact of the Q-learning framework used to model behavior?‡ In this paper, we demonstrate that the latter is true. Briefly, this is because, when compared to an unbiased Q-learner, both Bayesian and confirmation biased Q-learner demonstrate substantially lower action switching probabilities. This, in turn, confounds model fitting.

The paper is organized as follows. We begin by defining Bayesian behavior in TABB tasks with counterfactual information. We stick to this task as both confirmation and positivity bias are observed in this setup. We then proceed to reformulate Bayesian inference in terms of Q-learning to allow comparison between the two algorithms. We show that while Bayesian inference leads to unbiased learning, the learning rates decrease over time, which can lead to the appearance of biases when fitting constant learning rate models. Next, we present the master equation approach (26) to study the stochastic dynamics of these learning systems and provide an explanation for the emergent biases. Our analysis reveals a general insight: Temporal variations in learning rates, when unaccounted for, may masquerade as confirmation bias. Finally, we compare the predictive power of Bayesian models versus Q-learning models in fitting human behavioral data from ref. 12 and propose experiments that could help disentangle true cognitive biases from temporally varying learning rates.

Bayesian Inference Models

Mapping Bayesian Inference to Q-Learning.

With the definition of Bayesian agent and the Q-learning agent in place, we now proceed with comparing the two. In order for Bayesian inference and Q-learning to be comparable, we need to be able to treat the two formulations in an equal setting, i.e. draw a correspondence between Q-learning (Eq. 1) and Bayesian inference (Eq. 2).

We notice that the Q values act as a prediction for the expected reward of the two arms (albeit with a geometrically decaying kernel). Therefore, it seems appropriate to equate them to the estimates of immediate expected rewards pi of the two arms. This result is of fundamental importance. We have established that Bayesian inference can be precisely mapped onto a Q-learning formulation with no inherent bias (i.e., α+v=α−v).** Given this, the findings from the model-fitting procedure in Fig. 1A present a significant conceptual challenge. The behavioral data are generated from an unbiased algorithm—Bayesian inference with optimal decision-making—yet, when fitting the Q-learning model (Eq. 1) to these data, the inferred learning rates exhibit asymmetry, suggestive of bias.

This discrepancy suggests that the failure to account for the temporal variation in the learning rate leads to the systematic misestimation of learning rates, introducing spurious asymmetries. In the following section, we provide a formal analysis of this phenomenon.

Explaining the Observation

The above results suggest the existence of a more general confound—one between temporally varying learning rates and confirmation bias. Therefore, in the following, we pursue the question: when behavior is generated by a learning process with a time-varying effective rate, under what conditions does fitting a misspecified constant-rate model (Eq. 1) produce apparent confirmation and positivity biases? To keep the exposition concrete, we will sometimes illustrate with the Bayesian agent, whose effective learning rate decays (cf. Eq. 6); however, the analysis below treats a general class of temporal profiles of the learning rate.

We begin by taking note of the fitting procedure itself (further details in SI Appendix). Recall that for a TABB task, the behavior trajectory can be completely described by a binary sequence where at each time, the agent either switches (1) or repeats (0) their previous action. Therefore, the likelihood of observing a behavior trajectory is completely described by the sequence of action switching probabilities (defined formally in the following) for each observed transition. This suggests that if two algorithms demonstrate similar action-switching dynamics, they are likely to be confused by the maximum likelihood fitting procedure. Further, it has been argued previously (12) that a major behavioral signature of confirmation bias is reduced action switching probability in comparison to unbiased learning.

Due to these reasons, we will focus specifically on the dynamics of action switching probabilities implied by confirmation-biased learning algorithms and algorithms with unbiased but temporally varying learning rates, and understand how the latter can also lead to low action switching probabilities (Fig. 1B) much like the former. More specifically, we will show that, starting from constant and unbiased learning rates, there are at least two distinct ways of reducing the action switching rates—either by introducing a (positivity/confirmation) bias and keeping the learning rates constant or by maintaining unbiasedness in the learning rates but decreasing them monotonically with time.†† Therefore, we begin by first exploring the effects of biases on action switching rates, then followed by temporally varying learning rates. Finally, as the action switching rates also depend on the reward statistics of the environment, we consider symmetric environments i.e. (p1,p2)=(p,p), so as to evaluate the exclusive effects of confirmation bias and temporally varying learning rates on the action switching probabilities.

The Master Equation Formulation.

Given the established relationship between Bayesian inference and Q-learning, we can write down a general learning algorithm as follows[7]Qv,t+1=Qv,t+α+,tv·(rv,t−Qv,t),if (rv,t−Qv,t)>0,α−,tv·(rv,t−Qv,t),if (rv,t−Qv,t)<0.

This equation is the same as Eq. 1, except that the learning rates can now be an arbitrary function of time. Eq. 7 is a stochastic difference equation (SDE) governing the time evolution of Q=(Q1,Q2). Combining the above equation with the action selection policy π(a=1|Q)=11+e−β(Q1−Q2), we get a general behavioral algorithm for the TABB task. We will consider two special cases of this algorithm - temporally constant but potentially biased learning rates (α±,tv=α±v) and unbiased but temporally varying learning rates (α±,tv=αt. As a special case, we will also look at Bayesian inference (αt=1t+3). Again, for both these classes of algorithms we assume a soft-max action selection policy.

Obtaining relevant quantities of interest would require us to perform multiple simulation runs (for averaging) of Eq. 7. This becomes particularly problematic if we are to explore a high dimensional parameter space (five dimensional system from Eq. 1). Alternatively, we could directly consider the time evolution of the probability distribution Pt(Q)‡‡ over the Q-values, via a master equation (26). This allows us to consider the time evolution of a deterministic system, and also allows further analytical treatment. We may write the master equation as[8]Pt+1(Q)=∫Pt(Q′)T(Q′→Q; {α±,tv})dQ′,

where T denotes the transition kernel of the stochastic process. In our case, T(Q′→Q; {α±,tv}) is given by[9]T(Q′→Q; {α±,tv})=∑iπi(Q′)T−i(Q−i,Q−i′; α±,tu)Ti(Qi,Qi′; α±,tc),

where πi is the probability of taking action i, T−i≡p1(Q−i−Q−i′−α+,tu(1−Q−i′))+(1−p)1(Q−i−Q−i′+α−,tuQ−i′) and Ti≡p1(Qi−Qi′−α+,tc(1−Qi′))+(1−p)1(Qi−Qi′+α−,tcQi′). Here, we have employed the notation −i to refer to not the i’th arm, and 1 is the indicator function. Ti represents the transition kernel for the i’th arm and the product T−iTi reflects the assumption of independent rewards for both the arms. The specific forms of the transitions reflect the update rules given by Eq. 7. We have also considered the reward probabilities (p1,p2)=(p,p) as aforementioned.

While Eq. 8 is a deterministic equation, the involved integral cannot be performed in closed form, and therefore the Master equation is not generally solvable analytically. However, the time evolution of Pt(Q) contains a lot more information than we might be interested in, and therefore, we might not have to actually solve the entire equation. Indeed, if we can conjure up some coarse quantity f(Q) of interest, we could use the Master equation to instead only consider the time evolution of the average of f and avoid the complexities of solving Eq. 8. The time evolution of the ensemble mean of f can be written as[10]⟨f⟩t=∫f(Q)Pt(Q)dQ=∫f(μQ+(Q−μQ))Pt(Q)dQ.

Even so, the time evolution of ⟨f⟩t involves moments of Pt which in turn are given by even higher-order moments, preventing a closed form expression of the evolution law. We therefore make use of a moment truncation approximation (29) to approximate the mean of a nonlinear function f in terms of finite moments of Q. If f is a smooth function, we can Taylor expand it around the mean of Q, μQ=(⟨Q1⟩,⟨Q2⟩), up to second order, which gives,[11]f(μQ+(Q−μQ))≈f(μQ)+∇f(μQ)·(Q−μQ)+12!(Q−μQ)THf(Q−μQ)+O((Q−μQ)3),

where Hf is the Hessian matrix of f. Finally, performing the integral over this approximation of f then allows us to write[12]⟨f⟩t≈f(μQ)+12Hf,ij⟨(Qi−μi)(Qj−μj)⟩t,

where we have used the Einstein summation convention. Eq. 12 allows us to approximate the mean of a nonlinear transformation f in terms of mean and covariance matrices of Q. This crucial step then allows us to write down the evolution laws of the moments of Q and consequently of any observable ⟨f⟩t!

This strategy hinges on the choice of f. For our purposes, as has been argued above, the coarse quantity that we are interested in is the 1-step action switching probability. In order to obtain that, we first write the action switching probability under a specific transition Q′→Q, which we represent by K(Q′→Q; {α±,tv}) and is given by K(Q′→Q; {α±,tv})=∑iπi(Q′)(1−πi(Q))T−i(Q−i,Q−i′; α±,tu)Ti(Qi,Qi′; α±,tc). The total probability of action switching is then obtained by marginalizing over the future states Q and is given by[13]K(Q′; {α±,tv})=∑iπi(Q′)∫(1−πi(Q))T−i(Q−i,Q−i′; α±,tu)Ti(Qi,Qi′; α±,tc)dQ.

The ensemble-average total probability of action switching at time t is then given by[14]⟨K⟩t=∫K(Q; {α±,tv})Pt(Q)dQ.

Writing down the time-evolution equation for ⟨K⟩t can be done by making use of Eq. 12, thereby allowing us to compare the two behavioral algorithms. However, this involves the Hessian of K which makes the algebra cumbersome and hence not particularly insightful. Instead we begin by taking a closer look at the functional form of K from Eq. 13 and observe its characteristic dependence on algebraically much simpler quantities - the squared difference of the value estimates δ=(Q1−Q2)2 and the learning rates α±,tv.

In particular, we see that for smaller values of the learning rate αt we see lower action switching probability. From the above relationships of K with δ and α±,tv, we can infer the time-evolution of action-switching probabilities from the readily obtainable time evolution of δ and the learning rates. This is what we pursue below.

Action Switching Under Time-Varying Learning Rates.

Eq. 15 are greatly simplified for the case of unbiased learning rates, as α±,tv=αt. Making the appropriate substitutions, we obtain the following set of evolution laws. [16a]⟨Q1⟩t+1=(1−αt)⟨Q1⟩t+pαt,[16b]⟨Q1Q2⟩t+1=(1−αt)2⟨Q1Q2⟩t+2pαt(1−αt)⟨Q1⟩t+p2αt2,[16c]⟨Q12⟩t+1=(1−αt)2⟨Q12⟩t+2pαt(1−αt)⟨Q1⟩t+pαt2. These evolution laws imply that Δt evolves according to [17]Δt+1=(1−αt)2Δt⏟epistemic relaxation+p(1−p)αt2⏞noise.

Eq. 17 is particularly insightful as one can identify two distinct components to the time evolution of Δt: a relaxation term and a second noise-driven term. We take note that the drift term has a coefficient (1−αt)2≤1, implying that the relaxation always tries to drive Δt to zero. This makes sense, as we are dealing with symmetric environments and an unbiased learning algorithm, therefore Δ∞=0. The noise-driven term is proportional to the variance of the environment p(1−p) which drives Δt to larger values (proportional to αt2). This allows us to guess how Bayesian inference sustains low ⟨K⟩t for the time-scales observed in experiments—in the small time regime because we start from Δ0=0, the relaxation term is small compared to the noise term. Therefore, random fluctuations of the environment drive the Q values apart, decreasing the action switching rates. But at slightly larger times, the noise term becomes insignificant as it is quadratic in αt and the relaxation term is linear in αt. This implies that Δt starts reducing, albeit at a very small rate, because for small αt, (1−αt)2 is close to unity. In totality, Bayes inference maintains low action switching rates, by first driving the Δt to higher values by environmental fluctuations, and then by decreasing the learning rates such that the Q values become “sluggish,” thereby sustaining the low action switching rates.

Eq. 17 provides us insight into a more general class of learning algorithms that have temporally varying learning rates. First, we notice that for a stationary αt=α, Δ relaxes to the steady state with a time-scale τ=1α(2−α) and the steady state is given by Δ∗=p(1−p)α(2−α). As α∈[0,1], Δ∗ is increasing in α. Additionally, K is monotonically increasing in α (Fig. 2A). This suggests that in order to obtain low ⟨K⟩ by modulating αt alone, one first starts with a relatively large value for α and keep the value large for times comparable to τ.## This will lead the system to have large Δt. Once the Δ equilibrates, decrease αt rapidly, thereby leading to small ⟨K⟩t. Such a temporal protocol can sustain low action switching probabilities for relatively long times as τ is large for very small α and therefore Δ will decrease over a slow time-scale. For the purpose of demonstration, we chooseαt=α1,t<τcα2,t≥τc,

and observe the impact on ⟨K⟩t in Fig. 4. We are able to notice that ⟨K⟩t experiences a sudden drop at τc=25 and then slowly approaches (a higher) equilibrium corresponding to α2. In doing so, for an intermediate time the action switching rates are lower than the case with α2=α1. The minimum ⟨K⟩t depends on the exact value of α1. More notably achieving the same ⟨K⟩min is possible for smaller values of τc as α1 increases, thereby confirming our intuitions arising from Eq. 16.

With these arguments, we are able to conclude that there are at least two distinct mechanisms via which one might reduce the action switching rates—by introducing a (confirmation) bias in the learning rates, or by introducing a temporal variation such that in the early time regime one maintains a relatively high learning rate and once the Δ (roughly) equilibrates one drives the learning rate to small values. As Bayesian inference leads to relatively low action switching probabilities, it is best fit by a confirmation biased algorithm in the space of constant learning rate algorithms. This explains the confounding observation presented in Fig. 1A. In SI Appendix, we provide further evidence for this explanation: First, we show that a “mean-frozen” likelihood built solely from the models’ ensemble average switching curves closely tracks the full observed likelihood gap and selects the same winner in ≈ 80% of episodes; second, a more “causal” (like) intervention in a high-performing RNN agent showing that adding an action-switching bonus to the training objective monotonically reduces—and eventually eliminates—the inferred confirmation bias. Taken together, these results isolate lower average switching as a major driver of the apparent biases presented in Fig. 1A.

Consequences on Human Behavior

We have so far explored that both confirmation bias and Bayesian inference can lead to similar behavioral signatures. However, this confound would not deserve much attention if these models do not constitute as plausible hypotheses for human behavior. Therefore, we take note of how well these models explain human behavior in stationary TABB tasks with counterfactual information. After that, we will briefly discuss the question of distinguishing biased from unbiased temporally varying learning rates.

Model Comparison.

We proceed by comparing model fits to data from ref. 12. When performing fits to data we consider, a Bayesian model (Eq. 3, referred to as “Bayes”), unbiased Q-learning model (Eq. 7 with α±,t=α, referred to as “Const.”) and the full Q-learning model (Eq. 1, referred to as “Full”) and also a “confirmation model agent” (Eq. 7 with α+c=α−u and α−c=α+u, referred to as “Conf.”). We keep the fitting procedure to be similar to that of ref. 12—i.e., we minimize the negative log-likelihood of the models for each participant’s data to find the best fit parameters. Additionally, we report the proportion of best describing models in the subject population in Fig. 5B. We say that a subject is best described by a model i if it has the lowest BIC of all the other models. As can be seen from Fig. 5B, most subjects are best described by the Bayesian model, and quite expectedly by the “full” model if we ignore issues of model complexity. As aforementioned, we have followed suit with (12) in so far as penalizing model complexity is concerned, but we acknowledge that penalizing complexity is not straightforward and therefore we do not take the above results to mean—“humans are Bayesian agents.” Indeed, further statistical tests suggest characteristic deviations from the Bayesian agent’s behavior (more details in SI Appendix). Although it is unclear whether these deviations are attributable to the learning process or action-selection alone.

Nonetheless, given these results it at least seems plausible that human learning rates may vary with time. In particular, behavior may be better described via hybrid models which have a combination of bias and temporally decreasing learning rates. Therefore, further investigation would be needed to disentangle temporally varying unbiased learning rates (for instance Bayesian inference) and biased learning rates from human behavior in TABB tasks.

Potential Empirical Investigations.

The central mechanism considered here is a temporal variation in learning rates. To quantify its extent in humans, it is useful to go beyond aggregate switching statistics and obtain more direct constraints on the learner’s internal value estimates (i.e. Q-values). We outline a simple probe that complements standard choice-probability analyses by reading out the value the learner assigns to a reference arm.

At the end of a block of the TABB task, introduce a new arm whose reward probability is p3. After a brief familiarization with this new arm, present binary choices between the new arm and a preselected reference arm from the block. Because the competing models (for example, Bayesian inference versus confirmation-biased Q-learning) imply different value estimates for the reference arm (Fig. 5C), they make distinct predictions about how often the new arm will be chosen at the same reward setting. In Fig. 5D we present the supposed probability of choosing the new arm (π3) as a function of its reward rate p3 for both the Bayesian and Q-learning models. By collecting choices at one or more carefully chosen reward levels and comparing the observed frequencies to each model’s predicted probabilities, the experiment yields direct evidence regarding the agent’s Q-values, providing crucial information which would help to distinguish between the hypotheses. An extended discussion on distinguishing Bayesian inference and confirmation bias is included in SI Appendix.

Discussion

This paper presents a rigorous theoretical comparison of Q-learning and Bayesian inference (in specific, and temporally varying learning rates in general) in a TABB task with counterfactual feedback. Our analysis exposes a subtle but consequential artifact: When behavior is generated by a Bayes-optimal learner, fitting to it a Q-learning model still recovers positivity- and confirmation-bias signatures that are generally taken as evidence of human irrationality. The mirage stems from certain time-varying (yet unbiased) learning-rate profiles—Bayesian updating included—that naturally suppress action-switching; to match this low action-switching, a Q-learner can only resort to asymmetric updates, thereby feigning bias. This insight both critiques and clarifies recent TABB findings (30, 32), where confirmation-biased agents outperformed unbiased Q-learners under the assumption of constant learning rates, and it sets a sharper benchmark for interpreting human data going forward.

While doing so, we have also demonstrated the usefulness of Master equation type formulations to study RL algorithms. Particularly, the appeal of our analytical technique is that it allows us to get algebraic relationships between crucial observables without the need for running costly simulations. An analysis similar to our steady state analysis, has been done already (30) without using the master equations. However their approach only works for epsilon-greedy algorithm with temporally constant learning rates, while our methodology is applicable to more general RL algorithms. In addition, the presented approach also allows us to also study the transient dynamics (Which is crucial to understand how Bayes inference leads to low action switching.) of these learning systems, which cannot be obtained from a steady state analysis as has been done previously.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that making claims about confirmation bias by fitting a Q-learning model as in Eq. 1 to behavior can be problematic, especially if we cannot rule out the possibility of temporally varying learning rates. As this methodology has been employed by a lot of studies in cognitive science literature, we believe that our analysis will allow cognitive scientists to make more nuanced and robust claims about confirmation bias when inferred from decision-making tasks.
