Distinct roles of the JADE and BRPF scaffolding subunits of the acetyltransferase HBO1 complex
Authors: Nitika Gaurav, Lena K Ebert, Catherine Lachance, Karthik Selvam, Ruo-Wen Chen, Weihua Qin, Vasilisa Pozharskaia, Congwu Chi, Valérie Côté, Khan L Cox, Harrison A Fuchs, Catherine A Musselman, Heinrich Leonhardt, Núria Morral, Kunhua Song, Michael G Poirier, Jacques Côté, Bernhard Schermer, Tatiana G Kutateladze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe human acetyltransferase complex HBO1 is implicated in cancer and developmental diseases. Here, we report the chromatin association mechanisms for scaffolding subunits of the complex, the JADE and BRPF paralogs. Structural and biochemical studies reveal a distinct paralog-specific engagement of the PZP domains of JADE1/2/3 and BRPF1 with the nucleosome. We show that only JADE1’s PZP domain possesses a strong intrinsic DNA binding activity and binds to an almost entire histone H3 tail, forming a tight complex with the nucleosome. In contrast, the BRPF1 PZP domain’s association with the nucleosome is a thousand-fold weaker due to its inability to engage with the large portion of H3 tail and a weak binding to DNA. These results suggest that the JADE1 PZP domain can protect the H3 tail from being modified, but the BRPF1 PZP domain cannot, which could contribute to functional differences observed in the HBO1 complexes. We demonstrate non-redundant functions of the paralogs throughout embryonic development and embryonic stem cell pluripotency, during cardiac cell differentiation, and in differentiated liver cells. Our findings provide insights into the diverse mechanisms of action of the HBO1 complex and help to understand how the scaffolding subunits mediate its different cellular functions.Subject terms: Histone post-translational modifications, Epigenetics, Acetyltransferases, NMR spectroscopy, X-ray crystallography

Introduction

The HBO1 (histone acetyltransferase binding to ORC1) complex is involved in the regulation of gene transcription, DNA replication and repair, chromosome remodeling, and centromere function1–3. HBO1 is a member of the MYST (MOZ, YBF2/SAS3, SAS2, TIP60) family of essential human histone acetyltransferases, and its aberrant activities are associated with developmental diseases and cancer4–8. Genomic studies show that this complex associates with the transcription start sites and coding regions of active genes and it acetylates a large portion of the human genome3. The canonical HBO1 complex contains four core subunits, the catalytic HBO1 (also known as KAT7), ING4/5 (inhibitor of growth 4 or 5), MEAF6 (MYST/Esa1-associated factor 6), and the scaffolding subunit JADE (gene for apoptosis and differentiation) that functions as a platform for the complex assembly (Fig. 1a). The JADE subunit can be replaced with another scaffolding protein, BRPF (bromodomain plant homeodomain finger)9–11.

Three paralogs JADE1/2/3 are encoded by individual genes in the human genome. They share a high degree of sequence similarity and structural organization but diverge in functions, and of them, JADE1 is the most studied. JADE1 was originally identified as a binding partner of the von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) tumor suppressor, which is mutated in VHL syndrome, renal cell carcinoma, and hemangioblastoma, and plays critical roles in a broad spectrum of cellular pathways, including epigenetic, transcriptional, and cell cycle regulation, and WNT signaling8,12–15. Much less is known about biological roles of JADE2 and JADE3, however, recent studies indicate that these paralogs are not redundant to JADE1. An E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of JADE2 was shown to mediate LSD1 degradation during neurogenesis16. JADE2 is essential for hippocampal synaptic plasticity and cognitive functions17 and forms fusions with NUP98 in pediatric acute myeloid leukemia18. JADE3 increases cancer stem cell-like activity and tumorigenicity in colon cancer19 but displays anti-oncogenic properties in gliomas20. JADE3 (together with HBO1) has a role in the maintenance of AML leukemia stem cells4 and restores homeostasis of the intestinal epithelium after damage-induced repair in a HBO1- and H3K14 acetylation-dependent manner21. JADE3, but not other paralogs, is also necessary and sufficient to restrict influenza A virus infection22.

Much like the JADE paralogs, the BRPF paralogs (BRPF1/2/3) contain all regions necessary for the recruitment of other HBO1 subunits as well as the PZP (PHD-zinc-knuckle-PHD) domain, and two additional modules, a bromodomain and a PWWP domain, are present in the C-terminal part of the BRPF proteins. Either JADE-HBO1 or BRPF-HBO1 complexes acetylate lysine residues in isolated histone proteins, including H3 and H4, but these complexes display different selectivities in the context of chromatin or when the nucleosome is a substrate. The JADE1-HBO1 complex preferentially mediates acetylation of histone H41,9,23–26, whereas the BRPF1/2/3-HBO1 complexes mediate H3 acetylation9,27,28. A number of in vivo studies have reported that lysine residues in histone H3 are the major substrates of the HBO1 complex, though it’s unclear which scaffolding protein such a complex harbors4,7,29,30, and overall, how the scaffolding subunits direct the HBO1 complex diverse functions remains undetermined.

Here, we report the structural and molecular mechanisms of the JADE paralogs’ association with chromatin, which differ from those observed for the BRPF paralogs and therefore can drive distinct functions of the HBO1 complex. JADE1 is the only subunit of the complex whose JADE1PZP possesses a strong intrinsic DNA-binding activity and binds to a large portion of histone H3 tail, which results in a tight interaction with the nucleosome. Our results suggest that JADE1PZP but not BRPF1PZP can protect the H3 tail from being modified, which may help to explain differences in substrate selectivities of the HBO1 complexes. We demonstrate non-redundant functions of the paralogs throughout embryonic development and ESC pluripotency, during cardiac cell differentiation, and in differentiated liver cells. Our findings shed light onto the diverse scaffolding subunit-mediated mechanisms of action of the HBO1 complex.

Results and discussion

JADEs are ESC pluripotency phase-specific

We have previously shown that either JADE paralog, when overexpressed, associates with other HBO1 complex subunits, HBO1, ING5, and MEAF624, however studies of different paralogs-containing HBO1 complexes within the same cell type or throughout a biological process have not been performed. Following our chromatome maps development strategy31, we generated sets of mouse PSCs and measured levels of chromatin associated HBO1, JADE1/2/3, BRPF1/3, ING4/5, and MEAF6 during three functional phases of pluripotency, naïve in mESCs (mouse embryonic stem cells), formative in EpiLCs (epiblast-like cells), and primed in EpiSCs (epiblast stem cells) (Fig. 1b). The most notable and dynamic changes during pluripotency were observed for the JADE paralogs. JADE1 was enriched in the naïve phase characterized by transcriptionally permissive and lacking repressive epigenetic marks chromatin, but its level decreased in the formative phase and further decreased in the primed phase. Conversely, JADE2 level peaked in the primed phase, and JADE3 level peaked in the formative phase. While both BRPF3 and BRPF1 were enriched in the naïve phase, the enrichment of BRPF3 was notably more pronounced. HBO1 and MEAF6 remained largely unchanged in all phases, whereas levels of ING4 slightly increased and levels of ING5 slightly decreased from naïve to primed phases. These results indicated that within the HBO1 complex, JADE paralogues are pluripotency phase-specific and show phase-specific enrichment patterns during early epiblast development or the pre- to post-implantation epiblast transition.

JADE1PZP but not JADE2PZP or JADE3PZP binds tightly to the nucleosome

The central PZP domain of JADE1 (JADE1PZP) has been shown to interact with histone H3 and DNA, and this interaction is essential for the recruitment or stabilization of the HBO1 complex at chromatin and H4 acetylation (Fig. 2a)2,24. In support, the acetyltransferase activity of HBO1 in complex with various length JADE1 on the nucleosome was observed primarily when the JADE1 construct contained the PZP domain (aa 1–509 of JADE1) (Fig. 2b). Little to no catalytic activity of the HBO1 complexes containing JADE1 without PZP (aa 1–113 and 1–198 of JADE1) was detected, even though all JADE1 constructs had the HBO1 binding site. Furthermore, mass photometry (MP) measurements showed that JADE1PZP of ~20 kDa forms a complex with the nucleosome core particle (NCP) reconstituted with a 147 bp 601 DNA (NCP147) (Fig. 2c). Surprisingly, neither JADE2PZP nor JADE3PZP showed binding to the nucleosome in MP assays at the concentrations tested (Fig. 2d, e). The inability of JADE2PZP and JADE3PZP to appreciably bind unmodified NCP, i.e., the nucleosome lacking posttranslational modifications, was corroborated by fluorescence anisotropy (FA). For FA assays, we reconstituted NCPs using a 207 bp DNA (NCP207), in which 30 bp linker DNA was added to both ends of 147 bp 601 DNA, and internally labeled with fluorescein 27 bp in from the 5’ end. FA measurements confirmed that JADE1PZP forms a tight complex with NCP207 (S1/2 of 0.12 μM), but binding of JADE2PZP and JADE3PZP was almost negligible (Fig. 2f, g).

H3 binding function is conserved in JADEsPZP

The robust binding of JADE1PZP to the nucleosome is due to multivalent contacts with an almost entire tail of histone H3 and linker DNA24 (Fig. 3a). To explain the different behavior of JADEs, we examined whether the interactions with histone and DNA are conserved in JADE2PZP and JADE3PZP. Substantial chemical shift perturbations (CSPs) in 1H,15N HSQC (heteronuclear single quantum coherence) spectra of 15N-labeled JADE2PZP and JADE3PZP were observed upon titration with H31–12 peptide (residues 1–12 of H3) or H31–31 peptide (residues 1–31 of H3) (Fig. 3b–e). CSPs were in the slow to intermediate exchange regime on the NMR chemical shift timescale, indicating formation of the tight JADE2PZP-H3 and JADE3PZP-H3 complexes. In support of NMR titration data, binding affinities of JADE1PZP, JADE2PZP, and JADE3PZP to H31–12 and H31–31 peptides were found to be in the same range of 1–2 µM, as measured by tryptophan fluorescence (Fig. 3f–h). Analysis of the fluorescence-derived binding curves for the JADEsPZP-H31–31 interactions required a two-site binding model, suggesting a cooperative engagement24,32,33 with both H31–7 and H320–28 regions of H31–31 (Fig. 3a). Binding to the H31–7 region required the primary amino group of Ala1 of the H3 tail to be unprotected, as JADE2PZP did not recognize H3A1ac1–12 peptide, however binding to the H320–28 region was unaffected when Ala1 was N-terminally blocked by acetylation (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Structural basis for the recognition of H3 by JADE3PZP

To gain insight into the molecular mechanism for the interaction of JADE2PZP and JADE3PZP with histone H3, we crystallized fusion constructs consisting of H3 (aa 1–12) linked to JADE2PZP (aa 197–372 of JADE2) or to JADE3PZP (aa 198–370 of JADE3) through a short linker. Although we were unable to obtain diffracting crystals of H3-JADE2PZP, crystals of the H3-JADE3PZP complex diffracted well, allowing for the structure determination (Fig. 3i, j and Supplementary Table 1). The structure showed that the H3-JADE3PZP complex is stabilized through an extensive set of intermolecular hydrogen bonds (Fig. 3j). The NH3+ group of A1 of histone H3 forms hydrogen bonds with the backbone carbonyl groups of P238 and G240 of JADE3PZP. The backbone amide and carbonyl groups of R2 are restrained through hydrogen bonds with the carboxyl group of D221 and backbone amide of F219, respectively, and another hydrogen bond is formed between the guanidino moiety of R2 and the side chain carbonyl of N224. The hydroxyl group of T3 is engaged with the side chain carboxyl of D216 of the protein. The ammonium moiety of K4 forms hydrogen bonds with the backbone carbonyls of V201 and P209, and the backbone amide of K4 is restrained through a hydrogen bond with the backbone carbonyl group of M217. The structure of the H3-JADE3PZP complex superimposes with the structure of the H3-JADE1PZP complex24 with an rmsd of ~1 Å, indicating that although the pattern of intermolecular hydrogen bonds in two complexes is not identical, overall, the histone H3 binding pockets are similar (Supplementary Fig. 2). Collectively, the described above crystallographic, NMR and fluorescence data revealed that binding of JADEsPZP to histone H3 tail is conserved and therefore unlikely causes the observed differences in chromatin binding and functions.

Intrinsic DNA-binding activity is not conserved

We next tested DNA-binding activity of JADEsPZP in electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA). DNA147 was incubated with increasing amounts of JADEsPZP, and the reaction mixtures were resolved on a native polyacrylamide gel (Fig. 4a–c). A gradual increase in the amounts of added JADE1PZP led to a shift of the DNA147 band, indicating binding (Fig. 4a). In contrast, neither JADE2PZP nor JADE3PZP were able to bind DNA147 (Fig. 4b, c). The electrostatic surface potential of JADE1PZP shows two highly positively charged patches located on the side that is opposite to the H3-binding site of the domain (Fig. 4d). One patch is formed by R272, K276 and K302 of JADE1PZP (patch 1), and another is formed by K331, R334 and K367 (patch 2), and both patches are involved in binding to DNA, though patch 2 plays a major role in the interaction24 (Supplementary Fig. 3). The electrostatic surface potential of JADE3PZP shows that while patch 1 is intact, one positively charged residue in patch 2 is replaced with a hydrophobic residue, I331 (Fig. 4f). Patch 2 of JADE2PZP lacks all three positively charged residues and instead contains P327, V330, and E363 (Fig. 4e). The importance of patch 2 for binding to DNA was confirmed by mutating K331, R334, and K367 of JADE1PZP to alanine and testing this mutant in an MP assay. As shown in Fig. 4g, the K331A/R334A/K367A mutant lost its ability to interact with NCP147. Conversely, I334R mutant of JADE3PZP gained DNA-binding activity, judging by the shift of DNA147 in EMSA and an increased molecular mass of NCP147 in MP assay (Fig. 4j, k). Furthermore, restoring the JADE1PZP’ patch 2 in JADE2PZP by mutating all three P327, V330, and E363 residues to K/R/K also led to the gain in the DNA-binding activity of JADE2PZP (Fig. 4h, i). These results demonstrated that among JADEs, only JADE1PZP has the strong intrinsic DNA-binding function, whereas JADE2PZP and JADE3PZP do not appreciably bind DNA. The lack of the DNA-binding activity could contribute to functional differences.

JADEsPZP but not BRPF1PZP protects H3 tail

In contrast to JADE1-HBO1 complex that shows selectivity toward acetylation of H4 in chromatin or NCP23,24,26,34,35, BRPF1/2/3 containing HBO1 complex acetylates H39,27,28. Furthermore, the intact PZP domain of JADE1 is required for H4 acetylation but is dispensable for H3 acetylation25. These findings prompted us to investigate the mechanistic basis that would explain the differences (Fig. 5). FA measurements yielded a 17 μM affinity of JADE2PZP to NCP207 and 131 μM affinity of BRPF1PZP to NCP207, which is ~150-fold and ~1000-fold, respectively, weaker compared to the affinity of JADE1PZP to NCP207 (Fig. 5a, b). Such a tight interaction of JADE1PZP with an almost entire histone H3 tail and DNA should protect this tail from being posttranslationally modified, including acetylated by the associated HBO1 subunit (Fig. 5d, left model). In other words, when bound by JADE1PZP, the H3 tail is unavailable for docking of the catalytic domain of the HBO1 subunit, however H4 tail is nearby and unprotected, therefore can be a substrate for acetylation (Fig. 5c). Although JADE2PZP and JADE3PZP do not appreciably bind DNA, their ability to interact with the entire H3 tail could still shield H3 to some extent (Fig. 5d, middle model). However, BRPF1PZP lacks both positively charged patches found in JADE1PZP and has a weak DNA-binding activity, mediated by a K383/K390/R392 patch10,11 (Fig. 5e). Furthermore, BRPF1PZP interacts with a short N-terminal region of the H3 tail (aa 1–3 of H3) and therefore cannot protect the rest of the H3 tail (aa ~5–40 of H3) from being acetylated by the BRPF-HBO1 complex (Fig. 5d, right model).

JADEsPZP mediate paralog-specific gene expression

To assess the importance of JADE1PZP, JADE2PZP, and JADE3PZP in transcriptional regulation, we generated constructs lacking the PZP domains (JADE1ΔPZP, JADE2ΔPZP, and JADE3ΔPZP) from full length V5-tagged WT JADE1, JADE2, and JADE3. We expressed these constructs in HEK293T cells and performed unbiased transcriptomic mRNA-seq analysis of all six samples, as well as analysis of control cells (Fig. 6). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed a narrow clustering of replicates and a clear separation of the gene expression profiles for the mutant and WT JADEs expressing cells (Supplementary Fig. 4). The deletion of PZP led to perturbation in expression of genes that were generally unique to each JADE, indicating that the paralogs can regulate distinct sets of genes, and PZP has a role in this regulation (Fig. 6a–c). We note that the genes differentially expressed (DEGs) in the mutant cell lines showed a reciprocal opposing change in the WT cell lines (Fig. 6d–f). Comparison of the three JADE paralogs showed minimal overlap in DEGs, with only 71 shared genes being identified for WT JADEs vs. control groups and 81 shared genes being identified for the PZP deletion mutants vs. control groups (Fig. 6g). The presence of >50% of uniquely regulated genes in all three WT JADEs vs control groups reinforced the notion that JADEs’ transcription-mediating functions are not redundant. Interestingly, the deletion of the PZP domain caused a marked decrease in DEGs for JADE1, from 51 to 36%, suggesting that the PZP domain plays a crucial role particularly in JADE1 functions (Fig. 6g).

JADEs are involved in paralog-specific processes

GO analysis revealed that the genes differentially expressed in the mutant vs WT cell lines are associated with several fundamental processes and these processes are again JADE paralog-specific (Fig. 7a). Interestingly, in addition to the anticipated changes in transcription and development, metabolic processes and the cardiac system were also perturbed. To gain insight into the role of the JADE paralogues in these processes, we measured expression of the HBO1 complex subunits during cardiac differentiation and in liver tissues (Fig. 7b, c). We differentiated two healthy control human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) lines into cardiac lineage cells using a mono-layer based cardiac differentiation protocol36. This led to mesoderm commitment on differentiation day 1, emerging of cardiac progenitor cells on differentiation day 5, and development of cardiac lineage cells, such as cardiomyocytes, on differentiation day 7. RNA-seq analysis of iPSC-differentiated cells on different differentiation days showed a dynamic expression of the HBO1 complex components (Fig. 7b). Particularly, the expression of JADE paralogs and HBO1 was markedly changed during cardiac differentiation. A decreased expression of JADE1 was observed during mesoderm commitment. In contrast, expression of JADE2 and HBO1 was downregulated when iPSCs differentiated into cardiac progenitors and lineage cells.

To test the expression of the HBO1 subunits in liver, we performed analysis of RNA-seq datasets previously generated by us from liver tissues of young (7-week-old) and aged (52-week-old) male and female mice37. As shown in Fig. 7c, among all JADE paralogs, JADE1 was the most highly expressed in the liver in all groups of animals, whereas expression of JADE2 and JADE3 was substantially lower. Interestingly, the expression level of HBO1 was almost equal to the expression level of JADE1. The expression of ING4 on the other hand was extremely low, suggesting that in the liver, the HBO1 complex contains primarily JADE1 and ING5, rather than JADE2/3 and ING4. Together, these data suggest that the JADE paralogs play essential and distinct roles during cardiac cell differentiation and in differentiated liver cells.

In conclusion, in this work, we describe the distinctive mechanisms of the JADE and BRPF paralogs’ association with chromatin. Our studies reveal a critical role of the multivalent contacts of the JADE1/2/3PZP and BRPF1PZP with the nucleosome that can drive or contribute to different activities of the HBO1 complex. JADE1PZP has a strong intrinsic DNA-binding activity and binds to an almost entire histone H3 tail, resulting in a tight (120 nM binding affinity) interaction with the nucleosome, whereas JADE2/3PZP paralogs show almost negligible DNA-binding but retain H3 binding function. In contrast, the nucleosome association of BRPF1PZP is reduced ~1000-fold due to its inability to engage with the large portion of the H3 tail and a weak association with DNA. These results suggest that the H3 tail is rather shielded from being acetylated by HBO1 (or being posttranslationally modified by other enzymatic complexes in general) when bound by JADE1PZP. Unlike JADE1PZP, BRPF1PZP binds to the small portion of the H3 tail, specifically, to amino acids 1–3 of H3, leaving the rest of the H3 tail accessible for binding of other proteins, including the catalytic HBO1 subunit of the complex, and therefore can undergo acetylation. These mechanistic details help to explain and consolidate the findings that the JADE1 subunit directs enzymatic activity of the HBO1 complex toward acetylation of histone H41,9,23–26, whereas BRPF1/2/3 directs this activity toward acetylation of lysine 14 of histone H37,9,28. It is remarkable that JADE1PZP binds to the unmodified NCP with such a strength, because histone tails, including H3 tails, are not freely accessible within the nucleosome due to their interaction with the negatively charged DNA38. However, the histone H3-DNA interaction can be alleviated through acetylation of lysine residues in H3, and it will be interesting in future studies to assess the role of HBO1-dependent acetylation of multiple lysine residues in H3 in binding of the BRPF1/2/3 subunit and overall function of the BRPF-HBO1 complex.

Methods

Ethics

Human iPSC lines, C62 and C68 were obtained from the Crnic Institute Human Trisome Project (HTP), under a protocol approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (COMIRB 15-2170). The C62 and C68 iPSC lines were characterized previously36. The C62 line was derived from a healthy 27-year-old male donor, whereas the C68 line was derived from a healthy 39-year-old male donor.

Chromatome measurements

Chromatome analyses for mouse ESCs, EpiLCs, and EpiSCs were performed as previously described31. Briefly, mouse ESCs, EpiLCs, and EpiSCs corresponding to naive, formative, and primed pluripotent states were maintained in the following culture media. J1 mESCs (kindly provided by En Li) were cultured in N2B27 (50% neurobasal medium (Life Technologies), 50% DMEM/F12 (Life Technologies)), 2i (1 μM PD032591 and 3 μM CHIR99021 (Axon Medchem, Netherlands)), 1000 U/ml recombinant leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF, Millipore), and 0.3% BSA (Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine (Life Technologies), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Life Technologies), N2 supplement (Life Technologies), B27 serum-free supplement (Life Technologies), and 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Sigma). To differentiate naïve ESCs to EpiLCs, cells were plated in defined medium containing 20 ng/mL Activin A (R&D Systems), 10 ng/mL FGF2 (R&D Systems), and 0.1× Knockout Serum Replacement (KSR) (Life Technologies). The media was replaced after 24 h, and EpiLCs were collected for mass spectrometry (MS) analysis at 48 h. Both mESCs and EpiLCs were cultured on 0.2% gelatin-treated flasks. Mouse EpiSCs (kindly provided by Ludovic Vallier and Derk ten Berge) were maintained in the same defined medium used for EpiLCs, supplemented additionally with 2 µM IWF2. Cells were cultured on plates coated with FBS for 20 min and washed three times with PBS. Chromatin-associated protein levels of JADE1/2/3, ING4/5, MEAF6, and HBO1 from three biological replicates were analyzed using the PRIDE deposited PXD034448 dataset31 and visualized as a heatmap using R Studio. JADE1/2/3 showed statistically significant variation as determined by ANOVA test (FDR < 0.01).

JADE1-HBO1 complex production

JADE1L, HBO1 constructs were described previously39. Plasmids were cotransfected in HEK293T cells with the calcium phosphate method for protein complex overexpression with HA-HBO1 and C-terminal truncated FLAG-JADE1L: amino acids 1–113, 1–198, and 1–509. Cells were harvested 48 h post-cotransfection and lysed in high-salt buffer (450 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1% Triton ×-100, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM ZnCl2, 2 mM EDTA, and 10% glycerol) supplemented with protease inhibitor mixture. The NaCl concentration was reduced to 225 mM, and the whole-cell extract was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 30 min. The FLAG JADE1 was purified from the soluble fraction using anti-FLAG M2 affinity beads (Sigma) on a rotating wheel at 4 °C for 2 h. FLAG beads were next washed with 225 mM NaCl buffer and eluted with 400 μg/ml of FLAG peptide (Sigma).

HAT assays

HBO1 activity in the purified JADE1-HBO1 complexes was measured with 0.125 Ci of 3H-labeled Ac-CoA (2.1 Ci/mmol, PerkinElmer Life Sciences) as described in ref. 40. The amounts of the different purified JADE1 complexes used in the HAT assays were normalized based on the immunoblot data. The HAT reactions were performed in a volume of 15 μl using 0.5 μg of human free histones or native short oligonucleosomes as substrates, respectively, in HAT buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 50 mM KCl, 10 mM sodium butyrate, 5% glycerol, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM dithiothreitol) for 1 h at 30 °C. The reactions were then captured on P81 filter paper. The free 3H-labeled Ac-CoA was washed away, and the paper was analyzed using liquid scintillation. The 3H counts were used to evaluate HBO1 acetyltransferase activity in the complexes containing different JADE1 constructs.

DNA cloning and protein purification

Human JADE2PZP (aa 197–372) and JADE3PZP (aa 198–370) were cloned into the into pGEX-4T vector with a GST tag and a TEV cleavage site. Human JADE1PZP (aa 201–373) was cloned into the into pDEST-15 vector with a GST tag and a TEV cleavage site. All mutants were generated using the Stratagene QuickChange Lightning Site Directed Mutagenesis kit and their sequences were confirmed by DNA sequencing. The proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) pLysS cells grown in Luria Broth or M9 minimal media supplemented with 15NH4Cl (Sigma) and ZnCl2. Protein production was induced at 0.6–0.8 OD600 with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and grown for 18–20 h at 16 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspend in buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 1 mM PMSF, and 10 U/ml DNase), and lysed by sonication. GST fusion proteins were purified on glutathione agarose 4B beads (Thermo Fisher Sci) in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) buffer supplemented with 500 mM NaCl and 5 mM DTT. The GST tag was cleaved with TEV (tobacco etch virus) protease overnight at 4 °C. Proteins were further purified by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) and concentrated in Millipore concentrators.

Mass photometry

Mass photometry (MP) assays were performed on a Refeyn TwoMP mass photometer (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford, UK). Following the protocol41, samples were loaded into the sample wells of the silicon cassettes assembled onto MassGlass UC coverslips (Refeyn, Ltd.). Samples for measurements were prepared by rapid in-drop dilution, and measurements were performed at room temperature in buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT). Before each measurement, 10 µl of buffer was added to a well, and the focus was set and locked. 10 µl of NCP147 alone or a mixture of NCP147 with WT or mutated JADE1PZP, JADE2PZP and JADE3PZP in different molar ratios (1:1, 1:2, 1:4 and 1:6) was added to the drop. Samples were briefly mixed, and movies were captured immediately for 60 s (2800 frames) using AcquireMP software (version 2024 R1, Refeyn Ltd). β-amylase in the same buffer was used as calibration standard. Data were processed using DiscoverMP (version 2024 R1, Refeyn Ltd).

NMR experiments

NMR experiments were performed at 298 K on a Varian 600 MHz spectrometer equipped with a cryogenic probe. 1H,15N HSQC spectra of 0.05 mM uniformly 15N-labeled JADE2PZP and JADE3PZP in 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8 buffer, supplemented with 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT, and 8–10% D2O were collected in the absence and presence of increasing amounts of H3 peptides (synthesized by Synpeptide). NMR data were processed and analyzed with NMRPipe and NMRDraw as described42.

X-ray crystallography

The H3-GSGSS-JADE3PZP construct (aa 1–12 of histone H3, a GSGSS linker, and aa 198–370 of JADE3) was cloned into a pGEX-4T-1 vector with a GST tag and a TEV cleavage site. The linked protein was produced as above. Following cleavage of the GST tag with TEV protease and further purification by SEC, the linked H3-GSGSS-JADE3PZP protein was concentrated in 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.0, 100 mM NaCl buffer to 3 mg/mL. Crystals were obtained using sitting-drop vapor diffusion at 18 °C by mixing 0.8 µL of protein with 0.8 µL of reservoir solution containing 0.1 M HEPES pH 8.5 and 25% PEG 5000. Crystals were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen before collecting data. X-ray diffraction data were collected at 100 K on a Rigaku Micromax 007 high-frequency microfocus X-ray generator at the CU Anschutz X-ray crystallography core facility at a wavelength of 1.54187 Å. The images were indexed and integrated using XDS and scaled and merged using Aimless. The structure was solved by Phaser44 molecular replacement using PDB ID 8GE0 (H3-JADE1PZP) as the search model. Model building was performed with Coot45 and the refinement was carried out with Refmac5. The data processing and refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

NCP assembly

Human H2A, H2B, H3.2, or H3.3 and H4 histone proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) pLysS cells, separated from inclusion bodies, and purified using size exclusion and ion exchange chromatography46. Histones were then combined in 7 M guanidine HCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, and 10 mM dithiothreitol in appropriate molar ratios and refolded into octamer by slow dialysis into 2 M NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) pH 8.0, and 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol. The octamer was purified from tetramer and dimer by SEC. Octamer was then mixed with 147 bp or 207 bp 601 DNA in 5–10 mM Tris pH 8.0, 2 M NaCl, and 0.5–1.0 mM EDTA, and NCPs were reconstituted by slow desalting dialysis into 5–10 mM Tris pH 8.0 and 0.5–1.0 mM EDTA. The 207 bp DNA used in fluorescence anisotropy assays was 147 bp 601 DNA flanked with 30 bp linker DNA on either side and internally labeled with fluorescein 27 bp in from the 5’ end. NCPs were separated from free DNA via sucrose gradient purification. When necessary, NCPs were purified by SEC and peak fractions were pooled. All NCPs were confirmed by SDS and native-PAGE.

Fluorescence anisotropy

Fluorescence polarization measurements were carried out by mixing increasing amounts of JADEsPZP with 5 nM NCP207 in 75 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.00625% Tween20, and 5 mM DTT in a 30 µL reaction volume. The samples were loaded into a Corning round-bottom polystyrene plate and allowed to incubate at 4 °C for 30 min. The polarization measurements were acquired with a Tecan Infinite M1000Pro plate reader by exciting at 470 nm and measuring polarized emission at 519 nm with 5 nm excitation and emission bandwidths. The fluorescence polarization was calculated from the emission polarized parallel and perpendicular to the polarized excitation light47. The data were then fit to a binding isotherm with varying Hill coefficient to determine S1/2. The S1/2 values were averaged over three separate experiments with error calculated as the standard error between the runs.

DNA purification and EMSA

Double-stranded DNA containing the 601 Widom sequence cloned into the pJ201 plasmid (147 bp) was transformed into DH5α cells. The plasmids were purified either as reported in ref. 48 or by the PureLink HiPure Expi Plasmid Gigaprep Kit (Invitrogen K210009XP). Separation of the individual sequences was completed by digestion of the plasmid with EcoRV, followed by PEG and ethanol precipitation. EMSAs were performed by mixing increasing amounts of JADEsPZP, WT, or mutants, with 10 nM 601 DNA/lane in 20–25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5 buffer, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT in a 10 µL reaction volume. Reaction mixtures were incubated for 1 h on ice. Electrophoresis was performed in 0.2× Tris-borate-EDTA (TBE) buffer on ice. The gels were stained with SYBR Gold (Thermo Fisher Sci) for 10 min and visualized on Blue LED (UltraThin LED Illuminator-GelCompany).

RNA-seq of JADEs lacking the PZP domains

Full-length JADE1, JADE2, and JADE3 cDNAs in a modified pcDNA6 vector (Invitrogen) containing an N-terminal V5-tag were described in ref. 49. The JADE1ΔPZP mutant lacking aa 199–373, the JADE2ΔPZP mutant lacking aa 195–369, and the JADE3ΔPZP mutant lacking aa 196–370 were generated via Gibson assembly (primers are listed in the Source Data file). The plasmid containing V5.EPS1–225 cDNA has been previously described50. HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS. For transfection HEK293T cells were seeded in 10 cm dishes and grown to 50–60% confluency. Plasmid DNAs were transiently transfected using the calcium phosphate method. 48 h after transfection cells were harvested in TRI Reagent® (Zymo Research). Total RNA was extracted using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep kit (Zymo Research) following the manufacturer’s instructions, including a DNase1 treatment. The pellet was resuspended in 30 μl of RNase-free water and RNA yield and quality were assessed using a Nanodrop 1000 spectrophotometer. Library preparation and sequencing was performed by the Cologne Center for Genomics (CCG). In brief, libraries were prepared using the Illumina® TruSeq® mRNA stranded sample preparation Kit. Following poly-A selection, mRNA was purified and fragmented using divalent cations at elevated temperature. Fragmented RNA was reverse-transcribed using random primers, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis with DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. After end repair and A-tailing, indexing adapters were ligated. The products were then purified and amplified to create the final cDNA libraries. Library quality and concentration were assesed using a TapStation 4200 (Agilent). Equimolar amounts of libraries were pooled and quantified by using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Peqlab) on an Applied Biosystems 7900HT Sequence Detection System. The pool was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 with PE100 read length and a minimum of 35 million reads per sample. Reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic (v0.39) and mapped to the GRCm39 mouse reference genome with STAR (v2.7.10b), both using default parameters. Differential mRNA expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.49.4) in R (v4.4.2).

hiPSC cardiac differentiation and transcriptomic analysis

Mono-layer based cardiac differentiation of hiPSCs (HTP) was performed using the following protocol36. Briefly, hiPSC lines C62 and C68were dissociated with Accumax (STEMCELL Technologies) and replated in a MatriGel (Corning) coated 24-well plate. Cells were cultured in mTeSR1 for 4 days before induction. 6 μM GSK3 Inhibitor CHIR99021 (Cayman) was applied at induction day 0 to day 1 in RPMI1640 (Life Technologies) with B27 supplement minus insulin (Life Technologies). IWP2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added from day 3 to day 5. On day 5, cells were incubated in fresh RPMI1640 with B27 supplement minus insulin. Cells were then cultured in RPMI1640 with B27 (Life Technologies).

Cells at indicated cardiac differentiation timepoints were collected for total RNA extraction using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA samples were treated with TURBO DNase (Invitrogen) to remove any genomic DNA contaminations. RNA quality was examined using Tapestation (Agilent Technologies) before submission for sequencing libraries preparation and RNA-seq by BGI Genomics on the DNBSEQ (DNBSEQ Technology) platform at a read depth of 40 million reads per sample using paired-end sequencing. Transcriptomic analysis for each component of the HBO1 complex was performed using the GEO-deposited GSE217557 dataset36.

RNA-seq analysis of mouse liver tissue

RNA-seq data were analyzed using data previously collected on young and aged 7- and 52-week-old C57BL6/J male and female mice that were maintained on a low-fat diet for 10 weeks37. The liver was collected and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted from the liver using a RNeasy Midi kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) (n = 4/group)37. RNA integrity was evaluated by RNA ScreenTape analysis (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA library construction and sequencing were performed by the Center for Medical Genomics at Indiana University School of Medicine and deposited to the GEO repository (GSE274914)37. False discovery rate (FDR) was computed from p-values using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Differential expression analyses (FDR < 0.05) were performed separately for male and female mice.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis of binding data was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 and Microsoft Excel software. Binding data are presented as average ± standard deviation (SD) and mean ± standard error (SE) based on at least three independent experiments. MP and EMSA experiments were independently performed at least twice. For chromatome analysis, missing values across the three replicates were first imputed using a Gaussian distribution derived from the standard deviation of the measured values (width = 0.2, downshift = 1.8 SD). Statistical comparisons were then performed using two-sided Student’s t-test, with significance assessed via a permutation-based false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.01 or 0.05. Data are presented as row-normalized values and log2 fold changes.
