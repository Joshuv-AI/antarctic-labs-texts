Physical activity as a moderator of the association between central obesity and Type 2 diabetes in Asian American older adults
Authors: Kevin H. Kim, Mojgan Sami, Kathleen S. Wilson, Koren L. Fisher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsian American older adults (65+) face elevated risks for central obesity and Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). This study tested whether moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA) moderates the association between central obesity and T2DM, hypothesizing that the odds of T2DM linked to central obesity would be lower at higher levels of MVPA.MethodsGeneralized logistic regression models were used to test whether MVPA moderated the association between central obesity and T2DM, adjusting for demographic, cultural, and health-related covariates. Moderation was evaluated by comparing odds ratios from models using uncentered versus centered interaction terms.ResultsMVPA significantly moderated the association between central obesity and T2DM risk in older Asian Americans. Among individuals with central obesity, achieving the recommended threshold of 150 min of MVPA per week was associated with 43 % lower odds of T2DM (p < 0.001), based on comparisons of regression models incorporating uncentered versus centered interaction terms.ConclusionIncreasing time spent in MVPA was associated with a lower likelihood of T2DM among Asian American older adults with central obesity. Further research examining these associations within specific Asian American subgroups will be critical for the development of culturally relevant recommendations and tailored lifestyle interventions with the potential to address diabetes-related health disparities in this population.

Introduction

Obesity is associated with a greater risk to cardiometabolic health, increased risk of physical disability, cognitive impairment and dysfunction, and impaired quality of life, particularly among older adults [1,2]. From 2021 to 2023, the prevalence of obesity among US adults aged 60 years and older, across all ethnicities, was 38.9 % [3]. Asian Americans are the fastest-growing minority population in the US, and current estimates project that by 2060, Asian Americans aged 65 years and older will comprise 8.7 % of the population [4,5]. Previously published data indicate that the prevalence of obesity among non-Hispanic Asian American older adults is 22.4 % when assessed using race-specific criteria. Among Asian subgroups, Filipino Americans (26.7 %) exhibit the highest obesity prevalence, while Vietnamese (13.6 %) and Chinese (13.2 %) Americans show the lowest rates [6]. Although the obesity rates among Asian American populations are lower compared to other racial and ethnic groups, the prevalence of Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), metabolic syndrome, and other cardiometabolic conditions is disproportionately higher [7,8]. Despite higher rates of these chronic conditions, Asian Americans, particularly those over the age of 65, remain understudied. This underrepresentation may stem from researchers’ assumptions that lower obesity rates equate to better overall health [9]. Combined with the limited availability of race-specific and Asian subgroup-specific obesity data, these assumptions contribute to a significant gap in our understanding of obesity and its related cardiometabolic risks within Asian American communities [[7], [8], [9]].

Central obesity is increasingly recognized as a major risk factor for T2DM, suggesting that the overall increase of both central obesity and T2DM over the last 30 years is interrelated [8,10,11,12]. Factors of acculturation, including years since immigrating to the US and cultural assimilation, influence the risk of developing T2DM among immigrant populations [9]. Type 2 diabetes mellitus is highly prevalent (8.1 %) but may be underdiagnosed among US non-Hispanic Asian older adults [13]. Regardless of ethnicity, T2DM prevalence carries strong associations with obesity prevalence [1,14]. Lifestyle patterns more closely resembling an acculturated lifestyle than a traditional lifestyle have been shown to be strongly associated with T2DM risk when comparing second-generation Japanese American men with native Japanese men [15].

Physical activity (PA) is widely recognized as a key strategy in the prevention and treatment of obesity and T2DM across the lifespan through its direct influence on the functioning of the cardiorespiratory, musculoskeletal, and neuroendocrine systems, among others [16]. Physical activity is negatively associated with T2DM incidence in older adults, even after adjusting for BMI and waist circumference (WC) [17]. Additionally, intensive lifestyle modifications that include PA components have been shown to reduce the rates of T2DM in those at risk [18]. Lifestyle interventions that include PA and dietary components can show as much as a 9–10 % improvement in fasting blood glucose and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) in individuals with severe obesity who use insulin within the first year of treatment [19].

Public health research underscores the importance of incorporating cultural and historical factors, such as levels of acculturation, when studying obesity in immigrant communities [20,21,22]. Acculturation is a complex process of socialization through which individuals’ cultural norms, values, behaviors, and practices evolve in response to sustained interaction with a different or dominant culture [23]. Existing research on PA and T2DM among older Asian American adults shows distinct patterns in the relationship between obesity indicators (BMI and WC) and T2DM prevalence, which differ from those in other ethnic groups [24]. While most studies consistently report an inverse association between PA and T2DM across ethnicities, there remains a limited understanding of which specific types of PA are most effective in reducing T2DM risk [17]. Given the rising prevalence of obesity and its related health conditions, the underestimation of obesity-related disease risk poses a significant threat to the health of Asian American populations. The adoption of Western lifestyle patterns characterized by high dietary fat, low levels of PA, and prolonged sedentary behaviors further exacerbates the risk of obesity and obesity-related health conditions in Asian American communities [15]. Interventions aimed at increasing PA and improving dietary habits have demonstrated success in reducing T2DM incidence and enhancing health outcomes in specific Asian American subgroups, including Japanese, Chinese, and Korean Americans [21,25,26]. However, it remains unclear whether these findings can be generalized to the broader population of older Asian American adults. Therefore, this study examined whether PA is a moderator of the relationship between central obesity and T2DM in Asian American adults aged 65 years and older. We hypothesized that the odds of T2DM in those with central obesity would be lower among older Asian Americans reporting higher levels of PA compared to those reporting lower levels of PA.

Methods

This cross-sectional observational study examined the moderating influence of PA on the association between central obesity and T2DM in Asian American older adults through a secondary analysis of data drawn from the 2015-16 and 2017-18 cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey [27]. Data from 2015 to 16 and 2017-18 were combined, and sampling weights were adjusted using an algorithm provided by NHANES to maximize the accuracy of the estimates [27,28].

Participants

Data for this study were drawn from the Asian American sub-sample of the 2015-16 and 2017-18 NHANES cohorts. The inclusion criteria were (1) Asian American ethnicity and (2) age 65 years or older. We defined Asian American ethnicity as the selection of “non-Hispanic Asian” in response to the survey item related to race. Based on the above criteria, the overall sample size for this study was 228 adults aged 65 years and older, which, when weighted, was representative of 2.08 million individuals.

Measures

Dependent variable

The dependent variable in this study was T2DM status, with HbA1c levels ≥6.5 % indicative of being positive for T2DM, in accordance with guidelines from the American Diabetes Association [29].

Independent variables

The independent variables in this study were central obesity and level of moderate to vigorous PA (MVPA). Detailed descriptions of each independent variable are presented below.

Body composition

Central obesity was operationally defined as a WC ≥ 90 cm for men and ≥80 cm for women, in accordance with the International Diabetes Federation-approved Asian-specific WC cut points [30].

Physical activity

Respondents reported the duration (minutes per week and number of days) and intensity (moderate or vigorous) of their physical activity over the past 30 days across three domains: household activities (e.g., cleaning, yard work), transportation activities (e.g., walking, biking), and leisure-time activities (e.g., exercise, sports). Assuming that most household and transportation activities were of at least moderate intensity [31], a single continuous variable representing minutes per week of MVPA was derived by summing the average weekly minutes from household, transportation, recreational, and work activities. A second dichotomous PA variable was derived to indicate whether respondents met the current US Department of Health and Human Services (USDHHS) recommendation of at least 150 weekly minutes of MVPA [31,32].

Control variables

Due to its association with cardiometabolic health, we controlled for BMI in our analysis [33]. Several demographic, behavioral, and health-related variables were also identified a priori as control variables for inclusion in statistical models, based on theoretical relevance and prior evidence linking these factors to T2DM. Demographic variables included age, sex, educational attainment, income, language at home, and length of time in the US [9,34]. Language used at home and length of time in the US were included as proxy measures of acculturation. Behavioral and health-related variables included smoking status, sedentary behavior, and self-reported health status [35].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were completed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics 27. To account for unequal probability of selection due to the complex sampling design of NHANES, sampling weights were applied to all analyses to obtain population-based estimates [28]. Custom 4-year sampling weights were calculated based on NHANES guidelines to account for sampling differences between NHANES 2015-16 and NHANES 2017–18. Unless otherwise indicated, a significance level of p < .05 was applied to all analyses.

Descriptive analyses were performed to describe the overall sample and stratified by central obesity status. All variables were summarized using frequencies and proportions or means ± SD as appropriate. Using a generalized linear modeling approach to conduct a series of with multiple logistic regression, we assessed the relative contributions of time spent in MVPA and central obesity status (based on WC criteria) to the prediction of T2DM, and to assess whether PA moderates the association between central obesity and T2DM in Asian American older adults. The initial model included sex, educational attainment, annual household income, length of time in the U.S., BMI, smoking status, sedentary behavior, and self-reported health status. All covariates were entered simultaneously into the logistic regression models. Multicollinearity diagnostics (variance inflation factors) were performed, and no collinearity was detected. Smoking status did not contribute significantly and was not retained in the final models. An interaction term between central obesity status and MVPA (uncentered) was then added to evaluate moderation. To follow up on a significant interaction, MVPA was centered around the recommended 150 min per week, and a new interaction term with central obesity status was created. We repeated the analysis with the centered interaction term included in the model and odds ratios between the two models were then compared. The difference between the uncentered and centered odds ratios reflects the moderating effect of MVPA on the central obesity and T2DM relationship [36].

Discussion

The findings from our nationally representative sample reinforce previous findings related to PA, T2DM, and central obesity in Asian American older adults [17,19]. In our study, central obesity was strongly associated with T2DM in this population, and time spent in MVPA strongly moderated this relationship, such that when weekly MVPA met or exceeded the USDHHS threshold of 150 min per week, the likelihood that respondents with central obesity were classified as having T2DM was 43 % lower among those meeting the current MVPA recommendation, based on our moderation analysis. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution given the inherent limitations of self-reported PA data, including recall and social desirability biases.

Although the association between PA, obesity, and T2DM risk has been found across different age and ethnic groups, studies investigating moderate-intensity activities, such as walking, are less consistent in their findings, with some studies reporting an inverse association and some reporting no association at all [17]. The equivocal findings reported in the literature may be reflective of differences in demographic characteristics, such as sex, ethnicity, and age of the various study populations. Differences between studies in the use of Asian-specific cut points when examining obesity using body mass index and waist circumference may be an example contributing to these inconsistent findings [6]. The use of Asian-specific cut points is becomingly increasingly common practice, although it has yet to become standard practice in clinical and research settings [8]. Since the Asian American population has yet to be as thoroughly studied as other ethnic groups, the use of Asian-specific cut points in future research is essential in developing a more accurate understanding of the relationship between PA, central obesity, and T2DM in this population.

Given that Asian American older adults tend to engage in less overall PA than older adults of other ethnicities, and in lighter intensity PA and greater sedentary time compared to their younger counterparts [24,27], our findings highlight the potential of targeted public health strategies encouraging Asian American older adults to increase both the time they spend in PA and the intensity of their PA. Although we found that the moderating influence of MVPA on the association between central obesity and T2DM was stronger when weekly MVPA met or exceeded the 150 min per week threshold, any amount of MVPA was associated with lower odds of T2DM. Therefore, encouraging Asian older adults with central obesity to begin by replacing sedentary behaviors with light PA, and gradually increasing time and intensity towards meeting the 150 min of weekly MVPA recommendation may be effective in reducing T2DM risk in Asian American older adults while minimizing the chances of injury. Intervention and education strategies focusing on how to safely increase weekly MVPA to meet USDHHS recommendations for weekly MVPA in the Asian American older adult population could provide considerable value towards improving cardiometabolic health in this population.

Although acculturation was not a primary focus of this study, we included proxy measures (language use at home and length of U.S. residence) as covariates due to their potential influence on health behaviors. These variables showed significant associations with T2DM, suggesting that cultural factors may play a role in shaping diabetes risk. Prior studies have shown that longer U.S. residence is associated with an elevated risk of chronic disease, whereas cultural retention, often reflected by lower English proficiency, may be protective [9,37,38]. These patterns are commonly attributed to greater engagement with American cultural norms, which may negatively affect health-related attitudes and behaviors [9,34]. While it appears that acculturation may influence relationships between physical activity, obesity, and T2DM, further research specifically designed to examine acculturation is needed to explore these relationships in greater depth.

Strengths and limitations

There are several strengths and limitations to consider when interpreting the findings of this study. One notable strength is the use of a nationally representative dataset along with Asian-specific obesity criteria, enabling a more appropriate estimation of risk in an older Asian population that has been understudied. The complex sampling methods and large sample size of NHANES also ensure adequate statistical power and help to increase the generalizability of our findings. In addition, we accounted for a comprehensive set of control variables in our regression models, which helps reduce the risk of confounding.

These strengths notwithstanding, there are several limitations that should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design of this study precludes the determination of causal relationships and temporal patterns. Physical activity and sedentary behavior were assessed through self-reported measures, which are subject to substantial limitations, including recall inaccuracies and social desirability bias that may lead to an overestimation of PA levels. A further limitation is that NHANES records age only up to 80 years, after which respondents' ages are truncated. This presents an issue, as older adults are a diverse group, and their health would be understood in greater depth by continuing to record age past the 80-year threshold. Furthermore, NHANES classifies all Asian respondents as a solitary group (non-Hispanic Asian). Asian Americans are a heterogeneous population with vastly different health profiles from one ethnic subgroup to another. Thus, the findings of our study may not be generalizable across the entire Asian American population. Finally, despite including a comprehensive set of control variables in our analysis, we acknowledge the possibility of unmeasured confounding and misspecification in our statistical models. While this may bring about greater logistical complexity, future studies should consider using disaggregated ethnic subgroups to account for the racial and ethnic diversity of the population to create a better understanding of the relationships between MVPA, central obesity, and T2DM risk in the Asian American population. Consideration should also be given to the broader use of objective measures of physical activity in public health surveillance and analytical approaches such as compositional data analysis, which account the constrained nature of time in a day in research examining physical activity outcomes. Given that the Asian American population has increased by 82 % in the last two decades [5] and the high prevalence of physical inactivity, central obesity, and T2DM among Asian American older adults, studies are urgently needed to better inform the development of culturally relevant public health strategies and interventions. Together, these efforts would strengthen the evidence base needed to guide more precise and effective approaches to reducing T2DM in this growing population.

Conclusions

Our findings show that among Asian American older adults, MVPA moderates the relationship between central obesity and T2DM, with higher levels of MVPA linked to a lower likelihood of T2DM in those with central obesity. To advance our understanding, further research that examines these associations within specific Asian American subgroups will be critical for the development of culturally competent recommendations and tailored lifestyle interventions, particularly for less acculturated populations. The creation of culturally relevant public health policy and interventions is of the utmost importance since such approaches are likely to be more effective in reducing T2DM prevalence and improving overall cardiometabolic health and quality of life in this growing but underrepresented and underserved population.
