A microbiome quantitative trait locus in SLC39A8 modulates disease severity in synucleinopathy-induced models of Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Julianne C Yang, Jamilla Situ, Ryan Troutman, Ruowei Zhu, Margaret Black, Heidi Buri, Arjun Gutta, Fengrui Tian, Allyson Kang, Ezinne Aja, Amber Zeng, Rochelle W Lai, Jia Tan, Fengting Liang, Caitlyn Brahim, Grace Murphy, Aaron Ahdoot, Chao Peng, Jonathan P Jacobs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor deficits, dopaminergic neuron loss, and α-synuclein (α-syn) aggregation. While rare mutations underlie familial PD, around 85% of cases are idiopathic. Emerging evidence implicates common genetic variants and the gut microbiome in PD risk, but their interaction has not been studied. We previously demonstrated that the PD-protective SLC39A8 variant rs13107325 (human A391T, corresponding to A393T in mouse) is associated with microbial compositional shifts in humans and reshapes the microbiome in SLC39A8 A393T knock-in mice. Here, we test whether this SNP modifies PD phenotypes in two α-synucleinopathy mouse models. In the human α-synuclein overexpression model, A393T carrier mice show reduced motor deficits, consistent with a protective role. However, in the α-synuclein preformed fibril (PFF) injection model, A393T carriers exhibit worsened motor deficits, increased dopaminergic terminal loss, and enhanced α-synuclein pathology spread. SNP- and model-specific microbiome changes correlated with motor outcomes. These included enrichment of Lactobacillus and Lactobacillaceae HT002 genera in A393T carriers with α-synuclein overexpression, and enrichment of Erysipelatoclostridium in PFF-injected A393T carriers. These findings suggest that SLC39A8 A393T-induced microbiome alterations are associated with differential disease outcomes depending on context. Our results are consistent with a model in which susceptibility gene SNPs may influence PD progression via the gut microbiome, though direct causal effects remain to be tested.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease, with incidence rates estimated at 90 000 cases per year in North America [1]. Despite significant socioeconomic burden, therapeutic options for PD remain limited due to early-stage diagnostic challenges and complicated etiology. It is diagnosed on presentation of motor symptoms including resting tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia, which are driven by the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons and the accumulation of misfolded α-synuclein (α-syn) [2]. At the time PD patients meet diagnostic motor criteria, an estimated 30% of dopaminergic neurons and 60%–80% of brain dopamine (DA) have been lost, highlighting the need to identify early modulators of disease onset and progression [3].

In earlier disease stages, mood, cognitive, and gastrointestinal dysfunction may also manifest [4]. Gastrointestinal symptoms, in particular, have garnered attention as they have been reported to precede classic motor deficits in a majority of PD patients [5–7]. Preclinical studies have supported a gut-to-brain paradigm in the pathogenesis of PD. Injection of α-syn preformed fibrils into the duodenum of mice induced motor deficits, brain synucleinopathy and DA neuron loss; this effect was prevented by truncal vagotomy [8]. Other studies have demonstrated that microbes and their metabolites can promote disease. Conventionally colonized α-syn-overexpressing mice exhibited exacerbated motor deficits, synucleinopathy, and microglial activation compared to germ-free α-syn-overexpressing (ASO) mice [9]. Colonization of ASO mice with bacterial amyloid curli-producing E. coli resulted in enhanced gut and brain synucleinopathy [10]. These results implicate the gut microbiome in α-syn pathology, though the underlying mechanisms by which this occurs currently remain unclear.

Another factor with a key role in the pathogenesis of PD is gene–environment interactions. PD is primarily an idiopathic disease with only 10–15% accounting for familial inherited cases, indicating environmental components as strong influences in the development of PD [11]. Genome-wide association studies have identified over 90 risk loci in PD [12]. The incomplete penetrance of PD risk variants implicates additional modulating factors. The risk variant-associated microbiome is one such candidate. Supporting this, while Wallen et al. identified fifteen differentially abundant genera between PD patients and their healthy controls, a follow-up study by Wallen et al. incorporating genomic data with microbiome data found that some microbes only distinguished PD from HC in patients carrying certain genetic risk variants [13, 14]. Thus, gene–microbiome interactions may underlie PD susceptibility.

One particularly intriguing variant is rs13107325 in SLC39A8, which causes an A391T missense mutation in the metal transporter ZIP8. While this SNP is associated with reduced risk of PD, it increases susceptibility to Crohn’s disease, schizophrenia, and other systemic disorders [15–18]. In humans, A391T carriage is associated with colonic microbiome compositional shifts in both Crohn’s disease patients and their healthy controls [17]. We previously confirmed a causal relationship of the variant to microbiome shifts by demonstrating that SLC39A8 A393T mice recapitulating the human A391T variant exhibit microbiome alterations and intestinal inflammation, leading us to classify this risk variant as a microbiome quantitative trait locus [19].

In this study, we investigate whether the SLC39A8 A393T variant modulates PD-related behaviors, pathology, and microbiome outcomes. To address this, we compare A393T carriers to their WT counterparts in three PD mouse models: toxin-induced neurodegeneration, injection of preformed α-syn fibrils, and transgenic overexpression of α-syn. The results of this study support a novel model in which common PD-associated genetic variants are associated with microbiome changes that may influence disease progression. More broadly, this study provides a framework for understanding how environmental and genetic risk factors may converge in idiopathic disease.

Results

SLC39A8 A393T variant modulates motor behavior in synucleinopathy models of PD

We first evaluated whether there were effects of the SLC39A8 A393T variant alone on mood, cognitive, and motor behaviors (Fig. 1A). There were no significant differences by genotype in mature 5–6 month old mice on performance on the accelerating Rotarod, object location memory, or time spent in center and total distance traveled in the open field assay (Fig. S1). Due to the positive association between the variant and risk of schizophrenia, we evaluated the acoustic startle response and prepulse inhibition of startle in A393T carriers and WT mice in both the absence and presence of 22q11.2 microdeletion, a strong genetic risk factor for schizophrenia [20]. We did not observe any differences in sensorimotor gating between A393T carriers and their WT counterparts (Fig. S2). We then utilized three mouse models to evaluate whether SLC39A8 A393T modulates PD susceptibility. First, we crossed SLC39A8 A393T/+ mice with ‘Line 15’ mThy1-hSNCA transgenic mice from the Jackson Laboratory to generate SLC39A8 +/+ (WT), A393T/+ (HET), and A393T/A393T (MUT) mice on a hemizygous hSNCA/+ background (‘ASO’ mice) (Fig. 1B). Since transgenic PD mice exhibit overt motor deficits at more advanced ages, we aged mice to 12–15 months prior to performing behavior tests to evaluate mood, motor, and olfactory deficits [21]. We additionally utilized the α-syn preformed fibril (PFF) injection mouse model of PD. In this model, mouse α-syn PFFs were stereotactically injected into the left dorsal striata of WT, HET, and MUT mice prior to longitudinal motor phenotypic evaluation up to 185 days-post-injection (DPI) (Fig. 1C). To model the dopaminergic neurodegeneration observed in PD, we performed intraperitoneal injections of the neurotoxin 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) in combination with the adjuvant probenecid (MPTP/p) twice biweekly for a total of five weeks prior to assessing mood, cognitive, and motor deficits typically induced by MPTP/p [22] (Fig. 1D). Tests included in the motor behavior panel for each mouse model were selected on the basis of previous literature supporting differences between the different PD induction models and their respective controls.

Interestingly, ASO MUT mice exhibited significantly increased latency to fall from the Rotarod compared to ASO WT mice, while PFF-injected MUT mice exhibited significantly reduced latency to fall compared to PFF-injected WT mice (Fig. 2A and B). There were no significant differences by SLC39A8 genotype on the Rotarod or any motor behavior assay performed in the MPTP/p-treated mice (Fig. 2C, Fig. S3). We also observed differences specific to the HET but not MUT mice compared to WT mice. On the buried food pellet test, fasted ASO HET took increased time to find the buried pellet compared to fasted ASO WT mice (Fig. 2D). When measuring forelimb grip strength, PFF-injected HET mice exhibited reduced grip strength compared to PFF-injected WT mice (Fig. 2E). Taken together, these results suggest that the SLC39A8 A393T variant modulates susceptibility to synucleinopathy-induced PD, with reduced susceptibility to hSNCA transgene-mediated motor deficits but not olfactory deficits, and increased susceptibility to PFF-mediated motor deficits. Trends in performance by SLC39A8 on other motor assays support these conclusions, but there were no statistically significant differences in performance by SLC39A8 genotype on other motor behavior assays (Fig. S4).

A393T variant carriers show increased loss of striatal tyrosine hydroxylase and spread of pathological α-synuclein in the PFF model

Given the differential effects of the SLC39A8 A393T variant on motor outcomes in the ASO and PFF synucleinopathy models, and the vulnerability of DA neurons in PD, we next evaluated whether the variant differentially affected DA neuron pathology in these models. The loss of tyrosine hydroxylase immunoreactive (TH-ir) fibrils in the striatum and TH-ir cells in the substantia nigra are commonly used markers of DA neurodegeneration. A recent study reported that the Line 15 ASO model does not exhibit loss of TH-ir cell bodies in the substantia nigra up to 12 months of age, in line with observations from a similar well-studied model, ‘Line 61’ hSNCA transgenic mice, which do not exhibit reductions in the number of TH-ir cell bodies even up to 22 months of age [21, 23]. However, Line 61 mice do exhibit loss of TH immunoreactivity in the striatum [21]. Thus, here we performed immunohistochemical staining to assess TH fiber density in the striata of both ASO and PFF mice (Fig. 3A-C). While there were no significant differences in TH fiber density in ASO mice, PFF-injected HET mice exhibited significantly greater loss in TH immunoreactivity compared to PFF-injected WT mice on both ipsilateral and contralateral sides, suggesting that A393T variant carrier mice experience a greater degree of DA neuron axonal terminal loss in the PFF cohort (Fig. 3D-F). Trends in TH immunoreactivity between PFF-injected MUT and PFF-injected WT mice supported these conclusions.

Since variant carrier status influenced phenotypic outcomes in the two synucleinopathy models (ASO and PFF) but not the MPTP/p-mediated neurodegeneration model, we hypothesized that the effects of SLC39A8 A393T would affect the spread of pathological α-synuclein. To test this, we conducted immunohistochemical staining for Ser129 phosphorylated α-synuclein (pS129 α-syn). Around 90% of α-syn is phosphorylated at the serine 129 residue in Lewy bodies [24]. Compared to PFF-injected WT mice, PFF-injected MUT mice exhibited increased numbers of pS129 α-syn-labeled puncta in the ipsilateral hemisphere, suggesting increased spread (Fig. 4). We did not observe pS129 α-syn staining in the ASO mice, but immunohistochemical staining for the astrocytic marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) revealed infiltration of astrocytes in the striatum of ASO mice that was not observed in the PFF mice (Fig. S5A). We observed HET but not MUT mice possessed significantly increased GFAP+ striatal cell counts compared to WT ASO mice (Fig. S5B). There was no correlation between Rotarod performance and GFAP+ cell counts (Fig. S5C).

Microbiome alterations in A393T mice are associated with motor deficits and spread of pathological α-synuclein in synucleinopathy models of PD

We previously demonstrated that variant carrier mice exhibit microbiome compositional shifts in the colonic lumen [19]. To assess whether variant-linked alterations in the microbiome are associated with differential outcomes in the ASO and PFF models, we performed 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing of colonic content of ASO and PFF mice. In the ASO mouse model, genotype was significantly associated with microbial community structure, accounting for 6% of variation in microbiome composition across mice (Fig. 5A). 17 out of 67 genera were significantly differentially abundant in either HET or MUT relative to WT mice, including HET and MUT enrichment of Lactobacillus and HT002 from the Lactobacillaceae family compared to WT (Fig. 5B). Predictive functional analysis of the colonic luminal samples identified 127 out of 271 pathways that were differentially enriched by genotype in HET and/or MUT mice and were concordant in directionality. These included depletion of fucose degradation in HET and MUT compared to WT mice (Fig. S6). Additionally, average latency to fall from Rotarod was both significantly associated with microbiome composition and positively correlated with Lactobacillus and HT002, suggesting that presence of these two genera in the colonic lumen are protective against ASO transgene-linked motor deficits (Fig. 5C-E). HT002 was also positively correlated with GFAP+ cell count (Fig. S5E).

We previously published microbiome data comparing A393T carriers to WT controls in mice aged to 1 years old, which we refer to as ‘Mature’ in Fig. S8 [19]. Shared A393T-associated genera between Mature mice and ASO mice included increased Lactobacillus in A393T carriers and reduced Peptococcus in A393T carriers in both cohorts (Fig. S6A and B). Lachnospiraceae UCG-006 was increased in A393T carriers in both PFF and Mature mice (Fig. S6D). Bacteroides was increased in PFF A393T carriers, reduced in ASO A393T carriers, and not differentially abundant in Mature mice. These observations support a model in which the A393T variant shapes the gut microbiome in multiple contexts, but its microbial signature is not universal; instead, specific taxa appear uniquely sensitive to the interaction between genotype and disease mechanism.

Discussion

Though earlier research into the genetics of PD focused on rare inherited mutations in familial PD and their effects on α-syn, the role of genetic susceptibility loci in sporadic PD has gained increasing attention in recent years [25–28]. To date, two GWAS have reported that the SLC39A8 A391T variant is associated with reduced risk of PD [15, 16]. Here, we report that mice recapitulating the human SLC39A8 A391T variant are protected against motor deficits in the ASO model, display exacerbated motor deficits and neuropathology in the α-syn PFF mouse model of PD, and do not exhibit motor behavioral differences compared to WT mice in the MPTP/p neurodegeneration model.

The use of three mouse models with different disease induction strategies in this study enables us to propose relationships between the effects of the SLC39A8 variant and disease processes at different stages of PD. In the MPTP/p mouse model, MPTP injected systemically crosses the blood–brain barrier, where it is thought to be converted to its toxic MPP+ ion form by astrocytes [29]. MPP+ is then taken up by dopamine transporter, where it contributes to the mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent death of DA neurons [29]. The addition of probenecid to the MPTP regimen reduces its clearance from the brain and results in near-immediate loss of neurons in the substantia nigra [22]. Therefore, the MPTP/p mouse model may reflect PD in the later stages, where motor deficits occur subsequent to DA neurodegeneration [30]. In contrast, synucleinopathy modeled by PFF or by hSNCA transgene insertion are often used to investigate processes arising from α-syn pathophysiology in earlier stages of PD [31]. In these mouse models, loss of DA neuron terminals in the striatum occurs downstream of α-syn aggregation [21, 32]. Thus, our results across these three mouse models suggest that the SLC39A8 variant affects synucleinopathy-dependent processes upstream of DA neurodegeneration. A limitation of our study is that different behavioral test panels were applied across these three models, making direct comparisons challenging; the Rotarod assay was the only test conducted in all three models and thus provides the most consistent point of comparison.

Consistent with its PD-protective association in human GWAS, we observed that A393T confers protection in the ASO model, which exhibits a cell-autonomous, gradual, and progressive α-syn accumulation mirroring the temporal dynamics of sporadic PD [33]. In contrast, A393T worsens outcomes in the PFF model, where pathology is seeded by exogenous fibrils and rapidly propagated [34]. These results suggest that the effects of the SLC39A8 variant are context-dependent, and differences in synucleinopathy induction and spread are likely why A393T can be protective in ASO mice but detrimental in PFF mice.

Supporting this, in addition to increased motor deficits and reduced striatal TH fiber density, MUT mice in the PFF model exhibited higher numbers of pS129 α-syn-positive puncta compared to WT mice, indicating an exacerbation of synucleinopathy. However, we did not observe any pS129 α-syn-positive puncta in ASO mice striata, which may be due to biochemical differences in the pathological α-syn species between disease models (Fig. S3). Beyond these differences in pS129 α-syn immunoreactivity, we also observed differences between the ASO and PFF models in astrocyte reactivity (Fig. S4), with ASO mice exhibiting increased GFAP+ cell count in the striata compared to PFF-injected mice. In the context of PD, astrocytes may play a protective role in mediating α-syn clearance via lysosomal degradation or contribute to neurodegeneration through pro-inflammatory signaling or even α-syn propagation [35–38]. Whereas midbrain astrocytic cultures exposed to PFF exhibited robust increases in pro-inflammatory signaling and enhanced neurotoxic activity, astrocytes co-cultured with dopaminergic neurons that had increased levels of α-syn due to triplication or genetic mutation—analogous to our ASO model—protected healthy neurons via uptake and degradation of secreted α-syn [39, 40]. While we observed that ASO HET mice exhibited increased GFAP compared to ASO WT, GFAP was not correlated with Rotarod performance. Further testing is required to assess whether astrocytic reactivity contributes to model-specific outcomes by genotype.

We next asked whether variant-induced shifts in the gut microbiome might be associated with the divergent phenotypic outcomes observed in the two synucleinopathy models. Consistent with our previously published findings involving three independent cohorts, we first demonstrate that A393T is associated with global microbiome composition in both ASO and PFF mice [19]. We acknowledge that in both the current and previous studies, mice were housed by sex and genotype, so cage-level effects cannot be fully separated from genotype within a cohort. However, robust replication across independent cohorts strengthens the inference that the genotype effect on the microbiome is genuine and unlikely to be solely attributable to cage or housing conditions. In addition to growing recognition that microbiome composition can influence phenotypic outcomes in disease models, recent studies have supported the concept that genetic risk for neurodegenerative disease can be mediated by variant effects on the gut microbiome [41–43]. As one example, Seo et al. recently demonstrated that the APOE isoform-linked microbiome regulates tauopathy in an Alzheimer’s disease mouse model [43]. We discovered that HET and MUT mice demonstrated enrichment of Lactobacillus, the abundance of which was also associated with protection against motor deficits in ASO mice. Lactobacillus is enriched in PD patients; paradoxically, the administration of probiotics incorporating Lactobacillus species in preclinical mouse PD models protects against motor deficits and neuropathology [44]. These conflicting observations could reflect species-specific effects, underscoring the need for species-level resolution in future studies. Additionally, predictive functional analysis identified depletion of fucose degradation in ASO HET and MUT relative to WT mice. As fucose is a component of intestinal mucus, this is consistent with protection against gut barrier erosion [45]. In the PFF model, microbiome alterations included HET and MUT enrichment of Erysipelatoclostridium, which was negatively correlated with motor deficits and positively correlated with striatal pS129 α-syn staining. A recent meta-analysis of five independent case–control studies demonstrated consistent enrichment of Erysipelatoclostridium in PD patients amongst all five [46]. We also identified HET and MUT depletion of Lachnospiraceae UCG-001, which was positively correlated with motor deficits and negatively correlated with striatal pS129 α-syn staining. Depletion of Lachnospiraceae in PD patients is one of the most robust findings across studies [47]. Additionally, chondroitin sulfate degradation was enriched in HET and MUT mice relative to WT mice, which may suggest weakened barrier integrity, as chondroitin sulfate is a component of intestinal mucus [48]. Further validation using shotgun metagenomics or metatranscriptomics will be necessary to confirm species-level identities and functional activity of the microbial communities associated with variant-specific effects.

Though the microbiome findings highlight a A393T-linked signature associated with protection in the ASO model and susceptibility in the PFF model, the patterns do not converge on a single microbial genus or pathway. Instead, the A393T variant produced partially overlapping but not universally conserved genera across the ASO, PFF, and Mature cohorts. This pattern suggests that microbiome effects of risk alleles are contingent on pathological context, rather than defined by a fixed set of organisms. Existing efforts to identify disease-causing microbes may overlook a more nuanced reality: microbial contributors to neurodegeneration may be dynamic, state-dependent, and emergent only in specific environments.

Collectively, we demonstrated that a protective SNP in SLC39A8 protects against α-syn transgene-induced motor deficits but enhances severity of α-syn preformed fibril-induced motor deficits, with microbiome compositional and predictive functional shifts accompanying the outcomes. While our correlation analyses suggest that a microbiome-mediated component of this effect is possible, future microbiome transplantation or ablation experiments are necessary to assess whether the variant-associated microbiome causes altered PD susceptibility. It is also possible that local effects of ZIP8 transport dysfunction caused by the A391T substitution independent of the gut microbiome influence disease pathophysiology. Prior work found no differences in bulk brain trace element abundances of homozygous A393T mice and WT mice, but region- or cell-type-specific disruptions in metal ion homeostasis remain unexplored [18]. Having identified this relationship between a microbiome-modifying sporadic PD variant and PD susceptibility, this study raises the broader possibility that common genetic polymorphisms may modulate neurodegenerative disease risk through microbiome-dependent mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Animals

The previously described SLC39A8 A393T knock-in line was maintained through heterozygous breeding in specific-pathogen free conditions at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) on a 12:12 light:dark cycle [19]. Mice received autoclaved bedding and were supplied with irradiated food (LabDiet 5053) and water ad libitum. Heterozygous SLC39A8 A393T mice were crossed with hemizygous mice overexpressing human α-synuclein under the control of the murine Thy-1 promoter [Nomenclature: C57BL/6 N-Tg(Thy1-SNCA)15Mjff/J (MGI:5308782)] from the Jackson Laboratory. Offspring which were SLC39A8 A393T heterozygous and hSNCA hemizygous (SLC39A8 A393T/+, hSNCA +/−) were used for an additional round of breeding to generate SLC39A8 +/+ (WT), A393T/+ (HET), and A393T/A393T (MUT) mice that were hSNCA transgene positive (Fig. 1). These ‘ASO’ offspring were used in subsequent experiments. For assessment of schizophrenia-like characteristics, heterozygous SLC39A8 A393T mice were crossed with human 22q11.2 deletion mice [Nomenclature: C57BL/6-Del(16Dgcr2-Hira)1Tac] from Taconic Biosciences to produce offspring which were SLC39A8 A393T heterozygous and hemizygous [Df(h22q11)/+]. These offspring were used for an additional round of breeding to produce WT, HET, and MUT mice with and without the deletion, which underwent startle and prepulse inhibition experiments at 16 weeks of age. Mice of the same sex and genotype were housed in the same cages. All experiments were approved by the UCLA Animal Research Committee (ARC-2003-014).

Preformed fibril (PFF) injection model

Fibrillization of α-syn was accomplished through continuous shaking of purified wild-type mouse α-syn monomers at 37°C at a concentration of 5 mg/ml in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS). Prior to administration, fibrils were diluted with DPBS to a final concentration of 2.5 mg/ml, followed by brief sonication (QSonica Microson XL-2000; 20 pulses; setting 2; 0.5 s per pulse). Mice were stereotaxically injected (Anterior–Posterior: +0.2 mm, Dorsal-Ventral: −3.2 mm, Medial-Lateral: −2.0 mm relative to Bregma) in the left dorsal striatum with 2 ul of 2.5 mg/ml PFF.

MPTP/probenecid intraperitoneal injection model

Animals were treated as described in Petroske et al. [22]. To summarize, MPTP (1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine)/probenecid mice received MPTP hydrochloride (250 mg/kg in saline and 25 mg/kg probenecid in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), sourced from Sigma Chemical Co.) via intraperitoneal injection using a 28G needle. Mice were injected once every 3.5-day interval for five weeks (Fig. 1). A 30-min interval was observed between the first MPTP injection and the probenecid injection into the right abdomen. Mice were weighed the morning of injection, and needles were prepared shortly after. Following injections, heating pads were placed under the cages and replaced at 3 h post-injection to prevent the mice from being too cold since MPTP/p induces a temperature drop.

Mice behavioral tests overview

All behavioral tests were performed during the light phase. Mice were moved to a testing room and habituated for 30–60 min. Gloves used to handle mice were changed between home cages. Behavioral testing apparatuses were cleaned with 70% ethanol and allowed to dry between mice (open field, objects, wire hang, Rotarod) or between home cages (pole test—unless it was urinated on, in which case it was cleaned immediately after). Timelines for behavioral testing are included in Fig. 1. Detailed breakdowns of the sample sizes per sex and genotype and age at behavioral testing have been provided in the Supplementary Materials (Tables S1–S5).

Rotarod

The Rotarod assay was conducted for three consecutive days for ASO, PFF, and Mature mice cohorts, and was conducted for one day for the MPTP/p cohort. Before mice were placed on the Rotarod, the beam and metal pull-out were cleaned with 70% ethanol and dried. Cleaning was repeated in between mice for each round of testing. The Rotarod was set to Accel = 01.1, Accel-Int = 006, S-SP = 05.0, and E-SP = 60.0 for ASO, PFF, and Mature mice cohorts. The MPTP/p mice demonstrated more severe motor dysfunction and used modified Rotarod settings of Accel: 00.5, Accel-Int: 002, S-SP: 01.0, and E-SP: 60.0. Mice received 3 trials per day of testing with at least 15 minutes between trials. On the first day, mice were acclimated on the rotating beam at initial speed for two minutes prior to the beginning of the first trial. After two minutes, all mice were oriented to the same direction prior to starting the trial. Latency to fall in seconds was recorded for each mouse once they had fallen off the beam. For testing days 2 and 3 for ASO, PFF, and Mature mice, there was no acclimation period.

Buried food pellet

The buried food pellet test was used to determine whether olfactory deficits were present in the mice. Food was removed from home cages 24 hours prior to the test. Prior to the start of the test, each mouse was placed into a clean cage with a 3 cm layer of bedding and acclimated for five minutes. In a separate clean cage, a food pellet was randomly placed and covered with bedding. The timer started once the mouse was placed into the cage with the buried food pellet. The timer was stopped once the mouse uncovered the food pellet. The maximum duration of the test was 15 min. If 15 min elapsed and a mouse still had not located the food pellet, the maximum time was recorded and the mouse was returned to its home cage.

Wire hang test

The wire hang test was adapted from a published protocol [49]. Mice were placed on an inverted standard wire cage insert (utilizing only the flat portion of the insert). Paper was taped over other portions of the insert to remove the ability of the mice to cling to other portions. The cage lid was shaken three times to ensure mice were gripping the wire, then inverted and secured 35 cm above the counter. The PFF mice had a maximum trial time of 600 s, while the ASO mice had a maximum trial time of 150 s. If a mouse was not capable of reaching the maximum hang time, it was given an additional trial a minimum of 15 min later. The maximum hang time per mouse was used for subsequent analysis. Mouse effort was also scored as ‘sincere or insincere.’ A mouse was deemed ‘insincere’ if it dangled by its hindlimbs prior to dropping to the counter. A ‘sincere’ mouse let go with all four limbs simultaneously, or dangled by forelimbs prior to dropping down. Insincere mice were excluded from further analysis.

Hindlimb clasp

Quantification of hindlimb clasping was used as a marker for disease progression and motor dysfunction. Each mouse was lifted by the midsection of its tail and held inverted for 10 seconds. Mice received a score of 0, 1, 2, or 3. A score of 0 indicated free movement of both limbs and outward extension. A score of 1 was given if either one limb was clasped inward for the entirety of the suspension, or if both limbs were partially clasped inward. A score of 2 was assigned if both limbs were clasped inward for the majority of the suspension but still showed some flexibility. A score of 3 was assigned for immediate inward clasping, complete immobility of the hindlimbs, and no demonstration of flexibility.

Pole test

To reduce handling anxiety, the mice were handled for 1 min per mouse per day for 5 days prior to performing the pole test. The apparatus consisted of a 50 cm texturized wooden pole with a 1 cm diameter and a metal base, which was deliberately covered in the home cage bedding. The pole test was performed for each mouse for three consecutive days, with five consecutive trials performed on each day. On the first two days, the mice were placed at the top of the pole with the snout oriented towards the ceiling and trained to turn downwards by the experimenter, through grasping the tail and dragging the mouse in a counterclockwise direction. On the third day, mice did not receive any intervention from the experimenter. The time to turn downwards (Tturn) and total time to descend the pole (Ttotal) were recorded for each mouse on the third day. A mouse was defined as having completed the turn if the hindlimbs were on either side of the pole. Completion of descent was defined by contact between the forelimbs and the cage bottom. Mice were additionally scored on the mode of descent. If the mouse completed the turn and descended the pole in a paw-over-paw manner in at least 1 of 5 trials, the mouse was graded ‘C’ for ‘climbed.’ If the mouse failed to complete the turn in all 5 trials and slid or otherwise fell off the pole, the mouse was graded ‘S’ for ‘slid.’ ‘S’ graded mice were assigned a maximum score of 10 seconds for Tturn and 15 s for Ttotal. In rare cases, the mouse failed to complete the turn but was able to descend the pole in a paw-over-paw manner. In these cases, Ttotal was assigned to Tturn.

Grip strength

To assess forelimb grip strength, the grip meter (Chantillon, San Diego Instruments) was positioned at one end of a Plexiglas channel equipped with an adjustable angle wire mesh grip. To record maximum force, the "Mode" button was pressed until "T-PK" appeared on the right side of the screen. Forelimb strength assessment was conducted with the mesh grip set parallel to the testing surface.

Each mouse was lifted by the base of its tail, allowing it to securely grasp the mesh grip using both forelimbs. Subsequently, the mouse was horizontally extended away from the grip by gently applying a constant horizontal force to their tail base until they released their grip on the mesh. The peak pulling force necessary to separate the mouse from the mesh grip was displayed on the meter's screen and recorded. This process was repeated for each mouse in a series of 5 forelimb trials, with a 1-minute interval between trials for proper assessment.

Nesting test

Mice were moved to individual testing cages supplied with 3 grams of nesting material in the form of 4 full Nestlet squares +1 partial Nestlet, scattered around the cage in the hour before the dark phase. Nests were scored 16 h later by an experimenter blinded to the conditions on a scale of 1–5, with 5 representing a perfect nest and 1 representing the Nestlet remaining untouched. The same experimenter evaluated all nests for all mouse models according to previously published scoring criteria [50].

Tail suspension test

The tail suspension test for MPTP mice was performed according to procedures described by Can et al. [51] Mice were suspended from their tails and recorded for six minutes to assess time spent mobile, which is inversely related to severity of depression-related behavior. 17 cm fragments of tape were prepared with a point marked 2 cm from one end. This 2 cm section was utilized for attaching the tape to the mouse's tail, while the remaining 15 cm served for the suspension of the mouse. During tape application, we ensured that the marked end adhered firmly to the tail, leaving approximately 2–3 mm of the tail extending beyond the tape. The same observer assessed time spent mobile with a stopwatch for each mouse while blinded to the experimental conditions.

Novel object recognition and object location memory

Novel object recognition (NOR) and object location memory (OLM) tests were performed as previously described, including increased handling and habituation to the open field apparatus prior to testing [52]. Briefly, the tests involved a training day in which two identical objects are placed 6 centimeters lengthwise and widthwise from each of two corners of the testing apparatus. Mice were placed in the center of the testing apparatus and recorded for 7 min. On the subsequent day (testing), one of the objects was swapped for a novel object (NOR) or was moved to a new corner (OLM), and the mice were recorded for 7 min. Videos were analyzed in AnyMaze 7.0. The mouse was considered as having interacted with the object if its snout entered a 1 cm radius around the object. The interaction time with the novel (NOR) or displaced (OLM) objects was recorded as a percentage of total investigation time, with interaction times greater than 50% indicative of recognition memory [53].

Acoustic startle response and prepulse inhibition of startle

The acoustic startle response and prepulse inhibition of startle was measured as previously described [52]. Briefly, mice were restrained in the startle-PPI apparatus (SR-LAB-Startle Response System, San Diego Instruments, San Diego, CA, USA). After a 5 minute white noise acclimation period, mice underwent six 120 db startle presentations. Next, pseudo-randomized prepulse inhibition phases occurred for 17 min in trial combinations of prepulse (70, 75 or 80 db) + pulse (120 db) and concluded with six 120 db startle presentations.

Open field

The purpose of the open field assay was to better understand locomotion and anxious behaviors among the mice. The assay was conducted in the open field apparatus consisting of four 30 × 30 cm quadrants. Each mouse was individually placed in the center of one of the four quadrants and recorded for 10 min. Video analysis was conducted using AnyMaze software. A 6 cm grid was drawn over the entirety of each quadrant. Using the grid, a 3 × 3 square (18 × 18 cm) was drawn in the center. The amount of time each mouse spent in the center of the quadrant (inversely related to anxiety behavior) and total distance traveled as a measure of gross motor function were measured. The middle of the mouse’s body was the point used to determine its location.

Free-floating brain immunohistochemistry

3–4 coronal sections obtained at intervals along the rostral to caudal axis containing the caudate putamen regions were placed individually in cell culture inserts within a 24 well plate placed on a plate rocker for 3 washes in PBS, 5 min per wash. All wash steps were done at 30 rpm on the plate rocker, while incubation steps were done at 20 rpm. The sections were then steamed in prewarmed sodium citrate buffer pH 6.0 for 10 min, by placing the plate on a stand in a 99°C water bath for the purpose of antigen retrieval, then washed twice with PBST (PBS with 5% Tween-20), 2 min per wash. Samples were then blocked for one hour in 5% normal donkey serum (NDS, Abcam Normal Donkey Serum cat #AB7475) diluted in PBS + 0.3%Triton-X. Three primary antibodies (Aves Labs Chicken Tyrosine Hydroxylase cat #TYH-0020, Cell Signaling Technology Ms GFAP cat #34001, Abcam Rb pSer129 aSyn cat #AB51253) were then added to each well at volumetric ratios of 1:500, 1:500, and 1:1000 diluted in 2% NDS/TBS + 0.3% Triton-X. The plate was placed on a rocker overnight at 10°C. The following day, the plate containing the sections was incubated at room temperature for 2 h to facilitate maximal primary binding. Sections were then washed in PBST for 15 min, followed by 2 washes in PBS for 15 min each. Next, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 conjugated Abcam Goat anti-Mouse cat #ab150113, Alexa Fluor 594 conjugated Abcam Goat anti-Rabbit cat #ab150080, and Alexa Fluor 647 conjugated Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs Donkey anti-Chicken cat #703–605-155) at volumetric ratios of 1:1500, 1:500, and 1:500 for 2 h on the plate rocker at room temperature, protected from light exposure. Sections were then washed in PBST for 15 min, followed by 2 washes in PBS for 15 min each. Sections were then counterstained with DAPI to visualize nuclei (Invitrogen DAPI cat #D1306) at a volumetric ratio of 1:1000 in PBS, for 5 min stationary. Samples were then washed 3 times (PBST, PBS, PBS) for 1 min/wash, and then mounted on slides and allowed to dry. Mounting media (Invitrogen Fluoromount G cat #00495802) and a coverslip was added. Samples were stored in 4°C until image acquisition.

Cryosectioning

Following euthanasia, mice were transcardially perfused with 30 ml of ice-cold PBS. Brains were harvested from ASO and PFF mice and drop-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 hours (ASO) or 5 days (PFF mice). Brains were then cryoprotected in conicals containing 30% sucrose/1X PBS until they sunk to the bottom of the conical (2–3 days), and were subsequently frozen in optimal cutting temperature medium (OCT) until further use. 40 um coronal sections throughout the striatum were obtained from the brains using the LEICA CM 1950 Clinical 146 Cryostat. Sections were stored in PBS + 0.05% sodium azide at 4°C until staining via immunohistochemistry.

Confocal imaging

Sections were imaged using the laser scanning confocal microscopes Zeiss LSM 710 (PFF) or Zeiss LSM 880 (ASO) at 10X magnification for striatum sections. The laser power and gain were held consistent for all images obtained for the same fluorophore. Images were acquired as composite stitched images of the entire brain section with autofocus turned on for every tile.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Image analysis was conducted in a blinded manner. To quantify TH immunoreactivity, TH images were inputted into ImageJ, an image processing software. Four measurements were taken from the cortex to provide an assessment of mean background fluorescence intensity. The caudoputamen was traced using the Allen Mouse Coronal Brain Atlas as a guide. Mean fluorescence intensity for the ipsilateral or contralateral hemispheres (for PFF mice) or for the left or right hemispheres (for the ASO mice) was corrected by subtracting the average background fluorescence intensity. To quantify the total number of pS129 aSyn puncta or GFAP+ cell count in the PFF mice coronal brain sections, images underwent processing in ImageJ. First, the threshold was adjusted to maximize the contrast between background fluorescence and staining, and converted to binary values with [0, 0, 0] for background and [255, 255, 255] for positive staining. The watershed method was used to separate puncta based on circularity. Using the freehand tool and referring to the Allen Brain Atlas, the caudoputamen regions were manually traced in each section. Particle automated count was carried out with the following parameters: particle size—3.5-10 pixel2 and circularity was set to 0.0–1.0. The average TH staining and average number of pS129 aSyn were calculated per mouse (for ASO) or for the ipsilateral or contralateral hemisphere (for PFF).

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Following euthanasia, cecal and colonic luminal content were collected from the same ASO and PFF mice that underwent behavioral testing and frozen at -80C. Samples were then thawed and processed as follows by the UCLA Microbiome Core. Genomic DNA was isolated from each sample using the Zymobiomics DNA Miniprep Kit (cat#D4300), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was then amplified using barcoded 515F and 806R primers, as previously described [54]. PCR products were cleaned with the ZR-96 DNA Clean & Concentrator-5 kit (cat#D4024), and DNA concentrations were normalized before pooling the samples. To purify the DNA library, a ~ 380 bp fragment was excised from a 1.5% agarose gel and extracted using the Thermo Scientific GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (cat#K0691). Sequencing was carried out using 2 × 250 bp paired-end reads on an Illumina MiSeq platform with a v2 reagent kit and a 25% PhiX control spike-in, at the UCLA Technology Center for Genomics & Bioinformatics. Demultiplexed paired-end reads from raw sequencing data were then quality-assessed, filtered, and trimmed using DADA2 in R, followed by dereplication, inference, and merging of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomy assignment of ASVs was performed utilizing the naive Bayesian classifier implemented in the assignTaxonomy function of DADA2 using the Silva v138 database [55–57]. The ASVs were utilized as inputs to the PICRUST2 algorithm for predictive functional analysis [58]. Source code for the analysis and the figures can be found at https://github.com/julianneyang/pdbehavior/. Raw sequencing data has been deposited on NCBI with ID PRJNA1005265 and can be accessed at the following link: https://dataview.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/object/PRJNA1005265.

Statistical analysis

Differences in genotype for ordinal data (hindlimb clasping scores, nest construction scores) or data that was not normally distributed (Tturn and Ttotal on the pole test, acoustic startle, prepulse inhibition) were assessed by Wilcoxon rank-sum tests comparing WT to HET or WT to MUT. For other tests, where body weight influences the outcome (Rotarod, Wire Hang, Total Distance in open field), linear models were used with weight, sex, and genotype (WT as reference) as variables. For all other tests (tail suspension, buried food pellet, grip strength, novel object, object-location memory, time in center in open field), linear models including sex and genotype were utilized. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Microbial community structure was assessed by calculating pairwise distance matrices (root-square Jensen-Shannon divergence) on ASVs prevalence filtered to 15%, and subsequently visualized with principal coordinates analyses. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) on the distance matrices was used to evaluate the effects of genotype on microbial beta diversity after accounting for sex and site (encoding cecum or proximal/distal colon). PERMANOVA analysis was also used to evaluate the association between Rotarod performance and microbial composition. Differential feature (genus or pathway) analysis was performed to assess the effect of genotype on features present in at least 15% of samples using linear mixed effects models implemented in MaAslin2. Log-normalized, total sum scaled data were used and sex and site were included as covariates. A feature was considered significant if the Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value (q-value) was less than 0.25. We used q < 0.25 because the study aims to identify candidates for future validation. Spearman rank correlation was used to evaluate associations between the relative abundance of significantly different genera and Rotarod performance (ASO and PFF mice), ipsilateral α-syn puncta (PFF mice), and GFAP+ cell count (ASO mice).
