The construction of a prognostic nomogram model for colorectal cancer and the prediction of immune characteristics and immune treatment responses based on the bioinformatics analysis of soluble mediator-related genes
Authors: Leilei Yang, Xiuying Yang, Chengfeng Fang, Jiaju Han, Zhiqing Ji, Ruili Zhang, Shenkang Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAccurate prognosis prediction in colorectal cancer (CRC) is essential for personalized treatment. Soluble mediators are promising predictive biomarkers for evaluating outcomes. We sourced transcriptome data of CRC (COAD+READ) from TCGA and GEO. Soluble mediator-related genes (SMRGs) were identified via GeneCards. Through univariate Cox and Lasso regression analyses, prognosis-related feature genes were determined. A prognostic model was created using multivariate Cox regression, categorizing patients into high-risk (HR) and low-risk (LR) groups based on the median riskscore. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis and GSEA were undertaken on groups. ssGSEA assessed immune cell scores, while ESTIMATE analysis evaluated stromal and immune cell scores along with tumor purity. The CellMiner database identified potential drugs for HR patients. Pearson correlation analysis revealed the relationship between mismatch repair (MMR) genes and model genes. We identified 10 SMRGs. Pearson correlation analysis indicated positive correlations among these genes. GO analysis showed that most feature genes were linked to binding functions. KEGG analysis revealed that the HR group was enriched in pathways like Basal cell carcinoma and Glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis. The ssGSEA indicated higher immune cell scores in the LR group, alongside lower stromal scores. LR group also exhibited a lower TIDE score and higher immunophenoscore. Drug sensitivity analysis suggested PF-4708671, PI-103, and XAV939 as potential treatments for HR patients. There was significant correlation between model gene and MMR genes. The CRC prognostic model based on SMRGs effectively predicts patient prognosis and guides treatment strategies.KEYWORDS: Cancer, colorectal cancer, soluble mediator, prognostic features, immunity, bioinformatics, ESTIMATE analysis, nomogram

Introduction

According to the Global Cancer Report by the World Health Organization, colorectal cancer (CRC) has emerged as the third most prevalent cancer and the second major culprit of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with over 1.9 million new cases and approximately 900,000 deaths annually.1 Many factors can trigger CRC, among which unhealthy lifestyle and dietary habits are the main inducers of CRC.2 Worldwide rates of CRC incidence and mortality are steadily rising in tandem with the economic and social development. The International Agency for Research on Cancer predicts that by 2040, the number of new CRC cases will be up to 3.2 million, and the death toll will climb to 1.6 million.3 Surgical resection, targeted therapy, radiotherapy, systemic adjuvant chemotherapy, and immunotherapy are the principal treatment options for CRC.4 Currently, surgical treatment remains the primary treatment method for the majority of CRC patients, but CRC patients with surgical resection are still at risk of recurrence.5,6 Therefore, accurately predicting the prognosis of CRC to help physicians formulate more personalized treatment protocols has become the key to improving patient survival and quality of life.

The current evaluation of CRC prognosis mainly relies on the internationally common TNM staging, which, however, does not take into account the different pathological features of CRC patients at the same clinical stage, making it difficult to accurately reflect the patient’s prognosis.7,8 Since the performance of CRC patients is influenced by multiple factors, the assessment of prognosis based solely on TNM staging is biased. Therefore, further innovation of a more accurate prognostic model for CRC is warranted to improve therapeutic efficacy. In recent years, molecular biomarkers, especially long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), have shown significant potential in the diagnosis, prognosis assessment, and targeted treatment of CRC.9,10 Certain lncRNAs (such as NAMPT-AS) are significantly upregulated in CRC tissues and patient serum exosomes, and are closely related to NAMPT expression. Their high expression is associated with poor prognosis and shortened survival in CRC.9 Moreover, multiple lncRNAs participate in liver metastasis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), inflammatory response, and chemotherapy/radiotherapy resistance through regulating tumor-related signaling pathways during the occurrence and development of CRC. The dysregulation of their expression in tissues and peripheral blood provides new ideas for noninvasive detection and molecular classification of CRC.10 These findings suggest that prognostic models based on molecular biomarkers are expected to complement the limitations of TNM staging and provide more precise risk stratification and individualized treatment plans for CRC patients.

With the continuous advancement of cancer research, a growing body of evidence suggests that soluble mediators may be essential factors influencing cancer prognosis.11 A family of tiny protein molecules known as soluble mediators can dissolve in bodily fluids and is essential for immune system regulation and mediation. They act by binding to specific cell surface receptors, transmitting signals to activate or suppress the functions of immune cells, thus participating in various physiological and pathological processes such as inflammatory reactions, cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis.12 Soluble mediators such as cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors can reinforce or curb the growth of cancer cells by influencing the immune response and cell communication in the tumor microenvironment (TME).13–15 For example, tumor-associated macrophages can modulate the progression of cancer cells by releasing soluble mediators such as CCL22, IL-10, TNF-α, TGF-β, IL-6, and CCL18.16 Tumor cells dampen the anti-tumor activity of T cells by releasing soluble immune checkpoint sPD-L1, thereby facilitating their immune escape.17,18 Recent studies have found that the prognosis of cancer is also affected by soluble mediators. For example, a high concentration of sPD-L1 before treatment may be a marker of poor prognosis in patients with locally advanced or metastatic esophageal squamous cell carcinoma receiving cytotoxic chemotherapy.19 Soluble cytokine IL-8 is overexpressed in CRC and is significantly associated with shorter overall survival and progression-free survival.20 Therefore, the assessment of soluble media expression levels may be helpful for CRC screening and prognostic prediction.

The current tools for evaluating the prognosis of CRC are still quite limited. This study aims to deeply explore the significance of soluble mediator-related genes (SMRGs) in the prognosis and treatment of CRC, and to develop a CRC risk assessment model based on SMRGs using bioinformatics tools, in order to provide a new method for prognosis prediction. By analyzing the TCGA-CRC dataset, we revealed the correlation between the expression pattern of SMRG and the prognosis of CRC. Furthermore, by applying bioinformatics methods such as LASSO regression, Cox analysis and immune infiltration algorithms, 10 reliable SMRGs were selected to construct a model. The model was then verified for its correlation with the immune characteristics of patients and the immunotherapy regimens, providing new perspectives for the prognosis assessment of CRC.

Methods

Data source

This study used the colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD) and rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) datasets from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) as the training set. This dataset contained RNA expression data, mutation data, and clinical data for 650 tumor samples and 51 normal tissues (Table S1). All the samples had complete gene expression information. Combined with the survival data provided by TCGA, 645 samples containing survival information were retained. After further excluding cases with survival time ≤30 days, 599 tumor samples were finally included for analysis. The original count data of RNA-seq were converted into a standardized expression matrix of log-CPM (counts per million) using the edgeR package.

The validation set was selected from the microarray dataset GSE17538 in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) (Table S2). The sample selection criteria for this dataset included: sufficient sample size (n = 222), complete survival time and status information (93 deaths, 129 survivors), balanced survival event proportions, and reasonable distribution of survival time (0–1 year: 31 cases, 1–3 years: 60 cases, 3–5 years: 79 cases, > 5 years: 62 cases). Preprocessing included: 1) Removing genes with an average expression of zero; 2) Standardizing using the normalizeBetweenArrays function from the limma package. The standardized data were used as the external validation set.

To screen for candidate genes related to soluble mediators, we referred to previous research methods11 and searched for genes related to soluble mediators using the keyword “soluble mediator” in the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org.), yielding 12,018 characteristic genes (Table S2).

Differential analysis of SMRGs

In the training set, we used the edgeR package21 to conduct differential analysis on the normal and tumor groups of CRC (the screening condition was |logFC| > 1, FDR < 0.05). Subsequently, we took the intersection of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and the SMRGs to screen out the significantly different SMRGs, and drew volcano plots and expression heatmaps to display their expression characteristics. Then, for the selected genes, we used the clusterProfiler package22 to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses separately to explore their potential biological functions and related pathways.

Screening of prognosis-related features to construct a prognostic model

Clinical data were combined with the expression of DEGs according to the samples, and tumor samples with a survival time greater than 30 days were retained. The survival package23 was employed in the univariate regression analysis of DEGs. Candidate genes were screened based on p-value (p-value < .001). To prevent overfitting of the model, the glmnet package24 was utilized for LASSO regression analysis of candidate genes. We employed cross-validation to select the penalty parameter lambda. Genes with strong correlations were filtered out to reduce model complexity. The survival package was applied in multivariate regression analysis on those candidate genes for modeling.

Based on this model, by combining the expression levels of candidate genes and their corresponding risk coefficients, the risk score of each patient was calculated, and the patients were divided into a high-risk (HR) group and a low-risk (LR) group based on the median of the risk score. The survival curves of the two groups were plotted using the survival package. The ROC curves of the model at 1 year, 3 years and 5 years were drawn using the timeROC package,25 and the AUC values were calculated. Finally, the model was externally validated using the independent GEO dataset GSE17538. The prediction performance and stability of the model were evaluated by calculating the risk score, drawing survival curves and ROC curves.

Correlation analysis of feature genes

To dig out the relation among feature genes, we launched Pearson correlation analysis on the final feature genes. By using the rcorr function from the Hmisc R package,26 we calculated the correlation coefficients and corresponding p-values between genes. Based on the results, a correlation heatmap was mapped to visually display the strength of the associations between genes.

GSEA

To figure out the functions enriched by the feature genes, GO analysis was carried out. Based on the risk score calculated by the prognostic scoring model, the samples were divided into a HR group and a LR group. Combined with the standardized expression matrix processed by the edgeR package and the risk grouping information, the input file for GSEA analysis was constructed. Subsequently, using the GSEA software, the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the samples in the two groups to reveal the significant differences in related pathways and their potential biological significance.

Construction and verification of the nomogram

Combining risk scores and clinical information, including the TMN staging and disease stage (Stage) of CRC, we graphed the violin plot for different clinical groups and launched the Wilcoxon test. To determine whether the model was capable of predicting prognosis independently, clinical information and riskscores were combined, and the samples were subjected to univariate and multivariate regression analyses to generate corresponding forest plots. The rms package27 was applied to plot the nomogram for predicting the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival rates of patients, with corresponding calibration curves generated for verifying the predictive efficacy of the nomogram.

Immune landscape of HR and LR groups

To systematically evaluate the immune-related characteristics of CRC patients, we employed multiple methods to analyze the transcriptome data. Firstly, we conducted ssGSEA analysis on the samples using GSVA28 and the estimate package,29 calculated the levels of immune cell infiltration, immune function score, stroma score, Estimate score, and tumor purity of patients in the HR and LR groups, and then plotted corresponding box plots and violin plots for comparison. The CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to assess the level of immune infiltration in groups with different riskscores, with the box plot graphed. To dig out the sensitivity of patients to immunotherapy in different risk groups, the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) score was worked out from the TIDE website (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/), and the violin plots of TIDE in different groups were generated. We downloaded the immunophenoscore (IPS) for CRC from the Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA, https://tcia.at) and graphed the violin plot for IPS in the two groups.

Somatic mutation analysis

Based on the CRC mutation data from TCGA, the maftools R package30 was used to visually display the overall mutation status of all samples. At the same time, the mutation frequencies and types of candidate genes in the prognostic model were plotted in all samples to facilitate the assessment of the mutation characteristics of these genes.

Drug sensitivity prediction

Through a series of screening processes on the CellMiner database (https://discover.nci.nih.gov/cellminer/), potential drug targets and applicable drugs related to the expression of characteristic genes were identified. Specifically, we first conducted a Pearson correlation analysis to evaluate the relationship between the expression levels of characteristic genes and drug sensitivity, and selected the drugs with significant correlations. At the same time, based on the expression levels of characteristic genes, we divided the high-expression group and the low-expression group using the median value as the threshold, and performed the Wilcoxon rank sum test to screen for drugs with significant differences in drug sensitivity between the two groups. Subsequently, using the pRRophetic software package, we used the standardized gene expression data (log2(TPM +1)) as input and based on the drug sensitivity training model of the GDSC database to predict the IC50 values of various anti-cancer drugs for each sample. Finally, we predicted the candidate drugs with lower IC50 values and potentially higher sensitivity, providing potential treatment references for CRC patients.

Analysis of MMR status in different risk groups of CRC patients and its association with the risk model

To further explore the benefits of immunotherapy in the treatment of cancer cells in different risk groups, based on the previous study,31 we analyzed the expression levels of MMR-related genes (MLH1, MLH2, MLH3, MLH6, PMS1, PMS2) in CRC patients of different risk group in the TCGA dataset, the proportions of microsatellite stable (MSS), microsatellite low instability (MSI-L), and microsatellite high instability (MSI-H), as well as the differences in tumor MSI score. At the same time, Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlation between MMR genes and prognostic model characteristic genes to explore the potential relationship between risk score and MMR status.

Results

Differential analysis of SMRGs

Based on the transcriptome data of the CRC normal group and tumor group in the TCGA database 12,885 DEGs (Table S4) were obtained, among which 2943 DEGs overlapped with the set of SMRGs (upregulated: 1435; downregulated: 1508) (Figure 1(A,B)). Next, we probed into the biological functions and pathways of the DEGs. GO enrichment results manifested that DEGs were greatly concentrated in functions such as metal ion transmembrane transporter activity, amide binding, and endopeptidase activity (Figure 1(C)). The KEGG analysis demonstrated that the DEGs were mainly gathered in the Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway (Figure 1(D)).

Construction and verification of the prognostic model

After combining the expression data of DEGs with clinical data, we launched a univariate regression analysis by using the survival package, screening out survival-linked 16 candidate genes based on a p-value <.001. LASSO analysis of these 16 genes based on the glmnet package yielded 15 feature genes (Figure 2(A,B)). By performing multivariate regression analysis on 15 feature genes, a model based on 10 feature genes was created (Figure 2(C)). The formula is as follows:

Construction of the prognostic model.

(A) Coefficient distribution plot generated in LASSO model for logarithmic (λ) series. (B) LASSO coefficient spectrum of LASSO Cox analysis. (C) Forest plot of multivariate Cox regression.

Riskscore = 0.147*NLGN1 + 0.1823*FJX1 + 0.4391*TIGD1 + 0.2003*ATP2A1 + 0.3226*SEZ6L2 + 0.0963*FOXD1 + 0.0729*FABP4 + 0.0932*LRP2–0.0589*ATOH1 + 0.0984*FTCD.

We thus calculated the riskscore of clinical samples, used the timeROC package to plot the ROC curves, and worked out the AUC values (1-year: 0.73, 3-year: 0.77, and 5-year: 0.78) (Figure 3(A)). Two groups with different risks were generated: the HR group and the LR group, with the median riskscore as the critical value (Figure 3(B)). We applied the survival package in the survival analysis and plotted survival curves, revealing that compared to the LR group, the HR group had a significantly lower survival time (Figure 3(C)). The survival status also exhibited a similar trend, with higher mortality rates among HR patients within 5 years (Figure 3(D)). To examine the predictive performance of the prognostic model, we further validated it on the GEO dataset (Figure 4), revealing that the AUC values of the model for 1 year and 3 years were both above 0.7, which implied good predictive performance of the model on the validation set (Figure 4(A)). The Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival analysis and survival status distribution of the two groups also demonstrated similar trends to those in the training set, with the survival status of the HR group being worse than that of the LR group (Figure 4(B,D)). Given the above results, the CRC prognostic model based on SMRGs established by us performs well in predicting prognosis.

Correlation analysis of feature genes

Further analysis of the 10 identified feature genes in Pearson correlation analysis (Figure 5) revealed that all gene pairs with significant correlations were positively correlated. Among them, the correlation between gene TIGD1 and gene ATP2A1 was the strongest (P < .001). Gene FJX1 was significantly correlated with 7 genes. Genes SEZ6L2 and FOXD1 were significantly correlated with 6 genes. The expression of TIGD1 was tightly linked with 5 genes. The association of these genes appears to explain biological mechanisms, such as co-expression, that contribute to different risks in CRC patients.

GSEA

Based on the TCGA-CRC dataset, GO analysis of the 10 selected feature genes was carried out (Figure 6(A)), revealing that these genes were mostly linked in binding functions, such as amide binding, organic acid binding, and carboxylic acid binding. CRC patients were categorized into HR and LR according to the median riskscore, followed by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis with the use of the GSEA software. The results uncovered (Figure 6(B)) that the HR group was significantly enriched in pathways such as Dilated cardiomyopathy, Basal cell carcinoma, Glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-chondroitin sulfate, and Vascular smooth muscle contraction.

Gene set enrichment analysis.

Construction and validation of the nomogram

The riskscores for T, M, N, and Stage were tallied and differences were analyzed (Figure 7(A)). The results demonstrated that riskscore had significant differences in T, M, N, and Stage (P < .05), indicating that riskscore had the potential to characterize cancer progression. To figure out whether riskscore could independently predict CRC prognosis, we combined clinical features (age, T, M, N, and Stage) with riskscore for univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. The results (Figure 7(B,C)) manifested that in both regression analyses, the Hazard ratios of M stage and riskscore were greater than 1 and statistically significant (P < .05), implying that an elevation in riskscore led to increased risk of death in CRC patients. This suggested that the riskscore was an important independent factor in CRC and was promising in predicting the survival status of CRC. To provide a more comprehensive picture of the riskscore impact on clinical information, a nomogram of the model was further drawn to predict patient survival (Figure 7(D)). The 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year correction curves demonstrated that the predicted survival rate of the model fit the actual survival rate well (Figure 7(E–G)), indicating that the nomogram had excellent performance in survival prediction.

Immune landscape analysis

In ssGSEA, distinct immune cell and immune function scores were determined based on the gene expression in the samples. The results uncovered that among the 29 immune cells and immune functions analyzed, there were significant differences in the scores of 9 of them between the HR group and the LR group (Figure 8(A)). The scores of NK cells and Th1 cells in the LR group were considerably higher than those in the HR group. The Macrophages and Type_II_IFN_response scores in the LR group were significantly lower than those in the HR group. Immune, stromal, tumor purity scores, and estimate scores were also worked out by using the estimate package (Figure 8(B)). The results manifested that the HR group had a significantly higher stromal score than the LR group, while the other three scores showed no significant differences. CIBERSORT analysis demonstrated that in the HR group, T cells regulatory (Tregs) and Macrophages M0 were considerably higher than in the LR group, while Plasma cells, Dendritic cells activated, and T cells CD4 memory resting were significantly lower than in the LR group (Figure 8(C)). Immune checkpoint analysis manifested great differences in the expression levels of 15 immune checkpoint genes between the two groups. Among them, CD14, ADORA2A, LAG3, LAIR1, NRP1, TNFRSF18, TNFSF4, and VTCN1 were greatly upregulated in the HR group, while BTLA, CD244, CD40LG, CD44, CD48, ICOS, and IDO2 were upregulated in the LR group (Figure 8(D)). The HLA box plot manifested the higher expression levels of 2 HLA genes in the LR group (Figure 8(E)). In addition, the violin plot of TIDE scores manifested that the LR group had lower TIDE scores, implying a lower chance of resistance to tumor immune escape, a higher response rate to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy, and a greater likelihood of benefiting from ICB treatment (Figure 8(F)). Statistical analysis of IPS was displayed by violin plot, which demonstrated that among the 3 IPSs, the LR group had significantly higher IPS scores compared to the HR group, with higher IPS scores indicating better response to immunotherapy (Figure 8(G)). Taken together, CRC patients in the LR group may benefit more from immunotherapy.

Somatic mutation analysis

Gene mutations and tumor mutation burden (TMB) are implicated in the pathogenesis of various cancers, including CRC.32 Therefore, we probed into the linkage between feature genes and CRC gene mutations in the TCGA dataset using the maftools package. In TCGA-CRC, a total of 540 tumor samples had mutation data. Since not all tumor samples in the TCGA dataset have complete RNA-seq expression data, clinical follow-up information, and matching mutation data simultaneously, this study only retained samples that had all three types of information to ensure the consistency and comparability of the analysis. Therefore, the number of effective samples for mutation analysis was less than the total number of samples in the RNA expression analysis. By summarizing the mutations (Figure 9(A)), we found that the majority of mutations were missense mutations, with SNP being the most common mutation type, mainly presented as C>T and C>A mutations. Further visualization analysis of the mutations of the 10 characteristic genes in the model revealed that only 128 samples had available mutation detection results for these 10 genes (possibly due to uncovered sequencing, failed quality control, or insufficient sequencing). Moreover, 82.03% of the samples had gene mutations. Among them, the mutation frequency of LRP2 was the highest, accounting for 56% of the mutated samples (Figure 9(B)). The waterfall plots of the top 30 mutations demonstrated that the top 10 mutated genes were basically the same in both groups, including TTN, TP53, MUC16, SYNE1, APC, FAT4, RYR2, KRAS, and РІKЗСА (Figure 9(C,D)). Among them, APC and TP53 were the main mutated genes, with mutation rates exceeding 50%.

Drug sensitivity analysis

We predicted the link between feature genes and drug sensitivity based on the CellMiner database. The results demonstrated that the expression of drugs Vemurafenib, PLX-4720, RAF-265, and NLGN1 gene, as well as the expression of drug ZM-336372 and ATP2A1 gene, were all significantly positively linked (cor coefficients were 0.558, 0.557, 0.431, 0.444, respectively) (Figure 10(A,B)). Due to the significantly poorer prognosis of the HR group, we further used the pRRophetic package to predict the sensitivity of three HR-specific drugs: PF-4708671, PI-103, and XAV939 (Figure 10(C)). The higher estimated sensitivity score implied a lower sensitivity to the drug. We uncovered significantly higher sensitivity of the HR group to the three drugs, which implied that the three drugs may have application potential in CRC patients with poor prognosis.

Analysis of microsatellite instability (MSI) characteristics, mismatch repair (MMR) gene expression and their correlations in different risk groups

Studies have shown that approximately 15% of CRC cases exhibit MSI characteristics, which is the functional manifestation of MMR protein deficiency (dMMR).33,34 Immunotherapy is usually effective for patients with dMMR and MSI-H CRC.35–37 Therefore, in the TCGA-CRC dataset, we compared the MMR gene expression levels, the distribution of MSS/MSI-L/MSI-H, and the MSI score differences among patients in different risk groups. The results showed that among various MMR genes, only MLH1 showed significant differences between the HR and LR groups, with higher expression in the LR group than in the HR group (P < .05, Figure 11(A)). Microsatellite type analysis indicated that both groups were mainly MSS, with similar proportions of MSI-H and MSI-L, and no significant difference was observed (Figure 11(B)). Additionally, the MSI scores of the two groups did not show statistical differences (P > .05, Figure 11(C)). Furthermore, to analyze the relationship between model genes and MMR genes, we conducted correlation analysis on them. The results revealed that most model genes were significantly negatively correlated with MMR genes (P < .05, Figure 11(B)), among which the genes significantly negatively correlated with MLH1 were ATP2A1, FOXD1, and SEZ6L2 (P < .05). Moreover, the model gene TIGD1 was significantly positively correlated with MMR genes (P < .05).

Discussion

Growth factors, chemokines, and cytokines are examples of soluble mediators, which are tiny protein molecules that control immunity and have an impact on the pathogenesis of several illnesses, including cancer.38,39 The concentration of soluble mediators in blood and diseased tissues may have a bearing on the activation of pathways leading to inflammatory responses or disease progression.40 Soluble mediators are connected with the regulation of T cell activation, humoral immune response, lymphocyte activity, and stromal tissue in cancer.17 Therefore, soluble mediators can function as potential biomarkers for various diseases, including cancer. In recent years, studies have identified soluble mediators as predictive biomarkers for CRC and applied them in the early diagnosis and monitoring of CRC patients.40–42 However, reports on innovative, accurate prediction models based on soluble mediators for CRC risk assessment are sparse.

Based on the TCGA-CRC database, we used LASSO and Cox analysis methods to select 10 characteristic genes related to the prognosis of CRC patients from 2943 significantly DEGs (NLGN1, TIGD1, ATP2A1, SEZ6L2, FOXD1, FABP4, LRP2, ATOH1, FTCD), and constructed a prognosis prediction model. These genes have been reported to be closely related to the prognosis and tumor progression of CRC patients. For example, FABP4 is regulated by VEGF-A and bFGF,43 and promotes CRC cell proliferation, stem cell maintenance, and glycolysis through the ROS/ERK/mTOR pathway.44 In addition, the expression deficiency of FABP4 is closely related to the secretion of IL-6 and SerpinE1 by cancer-associated adipocytes in the TME.45 FOXD1 directly interacts with β-catenin, leading to the nuclear translocation and activation of downstream target genes (LGR5 and Sox2).46 At the same time, the high expression of FOXD1 is significantly positively correlated with immune-suppressive related cytokines (such as IL-10, TGFB1, and its receptor TGFBR1), indicating that it may indirectly affect its expression and function by regulating these soluble factors. LRP2 (megaprotein) is a known largest endocytic membrane receptor, and its abnormal expression may affect tumor growth, metastasis and remodeling of the immune microenvironment by regulating ligand clearance in cancer cells and immune and stromal cells.47 In the lipid metabolism-related prognostic risk score model of CRC, patients with high expression of LRP2 have higher infiltration levels of tumor-associated macrophages, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and endothelial cells in tumor tissues, while the infiltration level of anti-tumor immune key effector cells CD8+T cells is lower, indicating that it may promote the malignant progression of tumors by regulating the tumor immune microenvironment.48 The cell surface type I membrane protein SEZ6L2 is involved in the development process of various cancers,49–51 and its increased expression in CRC is associated with poor patient prognosis. Knocking down SEZ6L2 can trigger CRC cell apoptosis through a caspase-dependent manner.52 Moreover, NLGN1 promotes tumor cell penetration of the endothelial monolayer by regulating the APC/β-catenin pathway, thereby enhancing the metastasis and invasion ability of CRC cells.53 Although the specific mechanisms of TIGD1, ATP2A1, ATOH1 and FTCD in CRC are relatively less studied, the screening results of their selection as independent prognostic factors indicate that they may have potentially important functions.54–57 Based on these findings, we believe that the 10 gene prognosis models constructed in this study not only have stable predictive ability statistically, but also may participate in tumor metabolic reprogramming, immune microenvironment regulation, and cell proliferation and apoptosis processes through multiple levels and pathways. This, in turn, can jointly affect the occurrence and development of CRC, as well as the prognosis of patients.

Through analysis of the model on the training set (TCGA dataset) and validation set (GEO dataset), we observed that the risk assessment model demonstrated high accuracy and reliability on two different datasets, proving its potential in practical applications. Both multivariate and univariate Cox regression analyses further manifested that the predictive model can function as an independent factor to effectively assess the prognosis of CRC patients. The nomogram, which combined riskscores with clinical factors, manifested the effectiveness of the model from a more comprehensive perspective. In conclusion, these results confirmed the significant advantages and application value of the risk assessment model based on SMRGs in predicting the prognosis of CRC patients. The TME is composed of cellular components (stromal cells, tumor cells, and immune cells) and non-cellular components (cytokines and metabolites), being crucial for tumorigenesis, progression, and chemotherapy resistance.58,59 Soluble mediators, as regulators of immune function, can modulate cancer progression by influencing the immune status of the TME.60–63 For instance, the expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as LAIR-1 on specific immune cells can serve as a potential biomarker for the progression of chronic inflammation-related cancers, and also participates in regulating the tumor immune microenvironment.61 Moreover, abnormal activation of signaling pathways and their interactions with non-coding RNAs play a significant role in the immune regulation and resistance mechanisms of various tumors, providing new targets for molecular classification and treatment.62 With the development of precision medicine, individualized immunotherapy and combination therapy based on the specific molecular characteristics of patients have become an important direction for improving efficacy and reducing side effects.63 Based on this, this project further analyzed the immune landscape of different risks of CRC using ssGSEA. We unraveled that Tregs had higher levels of infiltration in the HR group, while Th1_cells, T cells CD4 memory resting, NK_cells, Plasma cells, and Dendritic cells activated, had higher levels of infiltration in the LR group. The LR group had higher levels of immune infiltration, which was indicated by different cell types between the two groups. We also analyzed the stromal score in TME, which is an indicator that measures the non-tumor cell components in the TME, including extracellular matrix and immune cells. We discovered that the stromal score in the LR group was considerably lower than that in the HR group, which further supported the idea that the LR group had higher levels of immune infiltration, suggesting that patients in our LR group may have better sensitivity to chemotherapy and immunotherapy. In addition, the TIDE and IPS scores for the two groups also confirmed that the LR group may benefit more from immunotherapy.

CRC exhibits high heterogeneity at the genetic and molecular levels,64 and this difference not only affects the occurrence and development of tumors but also determines the sensitivity of patients to treatment and their prognosis. Molecular typing and risk stratification are important approaches to revealing the heterogeneity of CRC. Among them, MSI testing is one of the most widely used and valuable typing indicators in clinical practice.65 The MSI status reflects the functional status of the MMR system and has significant reference value for the prognosis assessment and immunotherapy decision-making of CRC. Studies have shown that dMMR/MSI-H patients have a better prognosis and can significantly benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors.37,65 In addition, the MMR status can not only predict the clinical benefit of ICB for CRC,35 but also, in tumors lacking MMR, the generation of a large number of new tumor antigens can make them sensitive to this type of treatment, regardless of tissue origin.36 MSI is usually caused by germline mutations of MMR genes (MSH2, MSH6, MLH1, PMS2) in patients with Lynch syndrome or high methylation of the MLH1 promoter.66 Based on the close relationship between MSI and the prognosis and immune treatment response of CRC, we further explored the association between risk models and MMR genes and MSI status. In this study, we compared the expression levels of MMR genes, the distribution of MSS/MSI-L/MSI-H, and the differences in MSI scores among different risk groups. The results showed that among various MMR genes, only MLH1 showed significant differences between HR and LR groups, with significantly lower expression in the HR group compared to the LR group; however, both groups were mainly MSS type, with a lower proportion of MSI-H and MSI-L, and there was no statistical difference in MSI scores. Moreover, correlation analysis revealed significant correlations between most model genes and MMR genes (PMS2, PMS1, MLH3, MLH1). In summary, these results suggest that the risk model constructed in this study has a certain association with the MMR pathway, but its prognostic stratification effect does not depend on the MSI status.

To set up a scientific basis and guide drug treatment of CRC, we screened some drugs with significant connections with feature genes, such as Vemurafenib, PLX-4720, RAF-265, and ZM-336372. Vemurafenib has been shown to prolong the survival of CRC patients and improve treatment response.67 PLX4720 and ZM-336372 have been reported to suppress BRAF mutation.68 Among them, the combination treatment of PLX4720 and PI3K inhibitor can hinder the growth of innate and acquired resistance to BRAF-mutation CRC cells.69 The drug RAF-265 can repress the proliferation of CRC cells and reinforce apoptosis by suppressing the Raf/MEK/ERK signaling pathway.70 Considering the significantly poorer performance of prognosis in HR patient groups, we used the pRRophetic package to predict 3 HR-sensitive drugs (PF-4708671, PI-103, and XAV939), trying to guide drug treatment in CRC patients.

Conclusion

In summary, based on the TCGA database and GEO database, this study screens 10 SMRGs and uses them to construct a novel prognostic model for CRC. CRC patients can be divided into HR and LR groups based on the riskscore of the 10 SMRGs. The survival efficiency of the HR group is significantly worse. The potential mechanism of feature genes in CRC is revealed by the enrichment analysis. In addition, a nomogram based on the prognostic model can effectively predict the 1-year, 3-year and 5-year survival rates of CRC patients. Further immune infiltration analysis suggested that immunotherapy might be more beneficial in the LR group. This study also predicts potential target drugs for model genes. Therefore, this study identifies a novel biomarker based on soluble mediators for CRC patients and can be used to predict prognosis.

Limitations

Although this study constructed a CRC risk assessment model based on SMRGs and demonstrated high potential in predicting patient prognosis and guiding treatment, it still has several limitations. Firstly, as the analysis conclusions mainly rely on retrospective tumor samples and public database data, there may be inherent biases in case selection. Although this study used a sufficient number of CRC samples as a validation set to support the conclusion, it still needs to collect larger-scale and multi-center clinical samples for further verification. Secondly, this study screened and identified SMRGs related to CRC through bioinformatics methods, but lacked sufficient in vitro and in vivo experiments to verify the specific expression patterns of these model genes in CRC and their direct impact on patient prognosis.

Future perspectives

Future research should focus on several key directions: (1) collecting larger-scale, multi-center, and prospective clinical samples to validate the applicability and stability of our model across diverse populations; (2) conducting in vitro and in vivo experiments to elucidate the mechanistic role of SMRGs in CRC progression and immune microenvironment regulation; and (3) integrating multi-omics datasets, including transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, to optimize predictive accuracy and explore the translational potential of the model for personalized therapy.

In addition, from a sustainability perspective, drug repurposing offers a promising strategy to improve CRC management. For instance, GLP-1-based therapies or DPP-4 inhibitors have been shown to modulate cholesterol homeostasis through LXR-α–mediated regulation of ABCA1/ABCG1 expression in adipocytes, thereby improving metabolic profiles with potential anti-cancer benefits.71 Targeting the proteasome, particularly the 20S catalytic core, using clinically approved or novel proteasome inhibitors represents another therapeutic avenue for tumor control.72 Furthermore, natural compounds such as prodigiosin, which exhibits TME – modulating and immuno-regulatory effects with low toxicity to normal cells, hold considerable potential as adjunctive therapies.15 Likewise, hinokitiol has demonstrated the ability to inhibit stemness progression, induce apoptosis, and impair autophagic flux in breast cancer cells, suggesting its prophylactic and therapeutic value.73 Incorporating these approaches could broaden the scope of CRC therapy while enhancing its sustainability and translational impact.
