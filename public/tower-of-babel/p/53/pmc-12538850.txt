Incidence and predictors of dystocia during the active first stage of labor among mothers at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Amhara, Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Tirusew Nigussie Kebede, Getnet Azanaw, Muhabaw Shumye Mihret
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDystocia of the active first stage of labor (AFSOL) significantly contributes to maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality and is a major driver of rising cesarean delivery rates worldwide. Many determinants of AFSOL are potentially modifiable and context-specific, yet evidence from Ethiopia is limited.ObjectiveTo assess the incidence and predictors of dystocia during the active first stage of labor among laboring women at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Northwest Ethiopia, 2022.MethodsA prospective follow-up study was conducted from October 20, 2022, to January 4, 2023, at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Northwest Ethiopia. A total of 656 laboring women were enrolled using systematic random sampling. Of these, 1.7% were lost to follow-up because cesarean delivery was performed before the completion of the active first stage of labor for reasons other than protracted or arrested cervical dilation. The remaining 94.5% of participants who completed follow-up were included in the analysis. Data were collected through direct observation, interviews using a pretested structured questionnaire, and medical record review. Data entry was done using Epi-data version 4.2, and analysis was performed with SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics summarized participants’ characteristics, and binary logistic regression was used to identify predictors of dystocia. Results were considered statistically significant at a p-value ≤ 0.05, and associations were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsThe incidence of AFSOL dystocia was 35.3% (95% CI: 31.9–39.2). Positive predictors included induction of labor (AOR: 2.3; 95% CI: 1.09–4.91), admission with cervical dilation < 4 cm (AOR: 2.7; 95% CI: 1.62–4.63), poor provider support (AOR: 7.3; 95% CI: 4.06–13.39), and caffeine intake ≥ 200 mg/day ( ~ ≥ 3 cups; AOR: 3.4; 95% CI: 1.70–6.84). Protective factors included linseed consumption at labor onset (AOR: 0.25; 95% CI: 0.12–0.51), antenatal care follow-up (AOR: 0.24; 95% CI: 0.09–0.63), and ambulation during labor (AOR: 0.23; 95% CI: 0.09–0.54).ConclusionThe incidence of dystocia during the active first stage of labor is high. ANC follow-up, linseed consumption, and ambulation are protective, whereas caffeine intake, labor induction, early admission, and poor provider support increase risk. Implementing WHO labor care guidelines and promoting evidence-based provider practices may reduce dystocia and unnecessary interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08192-w.Keywords: Dystocia, First stage, Induction of labor, Incidence

Introduction

The active first stage of labor (AFSOL) is the period of rapid cervical dilation, typically lasting about 7 h to reach full dilation (10 cm) according to the WHO partograph [1, 2]. Dystocia in this stage refers to difficult or abnormally slow progress of labor [3]. There are two main types of AFSOL dystocia: protracted AFSOL, when cervical dilation progresses at less than 1 cm per hour, and arrest of AFSOL, when there is no cervical change for 2 to 6 h [3–6]. Abnormal labor can occur at any stage, but dystocia in the active first stage is the most common, accounting for about 48.8% of all abnormal labors [7, 8]. Active-phase abnormalities affect 25–37% of nulliparous and around 15% of multiparous women, while secondary arrest of AFSOL complicates 5–10% of labors [5, 9]. About 20% of all live births are affected by either protraction or arrest during the active first stage of labor [10]. The risk of secondary arrest of dilation is increased fivefold in nulliparous women and eightfold in multiparous women when the active phase is complicated by protracted dilation [11]. Dystocia during the active phase of labor is a significant problem, with reported incidences ranging from 1.3 to 37% [9, 12]. It affects about 20.8% of laboring women overall, occurring in up to 22.5% of primipara and 10.4% of multiparous women [13, 14].

Disorders of the active first stage of labor are strongly linked to higher rates of cesarean delivery, increased vaginal examinations, and frequent use of labor augmentation, all of which carry additional risks [8]. Dystocia alone accounts for 34–50% of primary cesarean deliveries, and nearly two-thirds of cesareans in healthy nulliparous women are attributed to active-phase abnormalities [15–17]. Evidence, including reports from Ethiopia, further indicates that 13–24% of unjustified cesarean deliveries are performed due to abnormal labor progress, particularly arrest of cervical dilation underscoring the major impact of dystocia on maternal care [18–21]. A retrospective observational study conducted in Sweden reported that dystocia during the active phase of labor occurred in 21% of deliveries. Among these cases, 16.7% resulted in cesarean delivery, compared to only 1.7% of deliveries without dystocia [22]. Several studies have concluded that dystocia during the active phase of labor is associated with an increased risk of operative delivery, postpartum hemorrhage and blood transfusion, maternal fever, endomyometritis, chorioamnionitis, shoulder dystocia, neonatal infection, higher admission rates to the neonatal intensive care unit, and neonatal death [9, 20, 23–26].

Previous literature has generally attributed dystocia of the active phase of labor (AFSOL) to abnormalities of the “three Ps”: power, passage, and passenger [1, 5]. Common factors associated with AFSOL include maternal anthropometric characteristics, such as pre-pregnancy body mass index and maternal height; pregnancy-related conditions, such as hypertension, gestational diabetes, and parity; intrapartum factors, including onset of labor, maternal anxiety, and insufficient support from medical staff during labor; and socio-demographic factors [7, 15, 16, 22, 23]. Importantly, the determinants of AFSOL are not necessarily limited to these factors they may vary across different settings and over time and are often context-sensitive. Most of these determinants are modifiable, highlighting a critical opportunity: the burden of dystocia during the active phase of labor could be substantially reduced if evidence-based interventions are implemented, with careful consideration of both the local context and modifiable risk factors.

The WHO developed the partograph as a tool to monitor labor and reduce maternal and neonatal adverse outcomes associated with dystocia of the active phase of labor. WHO also recommends labor augmentation and cesarean delivery as management strategies when indicated [6, 27]. Currently, because of the high incidence of dystocia and the increasing rate of unnecessary cesarean sections and their associated complications, WHO has updated the active phase of labor follow-up chart to allow sufficient time for accurate diagnosis [27]. However, this updated tool has not yet been implemented in Ethiopia. Importantly, the partograph updated or not does not reduce the incidence of dystocia nor identify its contributing factors; it only indicates whether labor is progressing normally. Consequently, dystocia remains a significant concern for many clinicians, particularly in settings where its incidence is unknown [9, 28]. Understanding both the incidence and the preventable risk factors of dystocia during the active phase of labor is crucial to reducing maternal and neonatal complications and avoiding unnecessary interventions. Despite the recognized contribution of dystocia during the active first stage of labor to maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality, there is, to the investigators’ best knowledge, no study addressing this issue in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study is designed to investigate the incidence and pre-labor and intrapartum predictors of dystocia during the active phase of labor, with a focus on modifiable factors, at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. The findings are expected to inform targeted, evidence-based interventions to reduce preventable complications and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Methods and materials

Study design, period, and setting

A prospective follow-up study was conducted from October 20, 2022, to January 4, 2023, at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Northwest Ethiopia. Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital was originally established in 1957 by Emperor Haile Selassie as Liule Mekonnen Primary Hospital. It served as a zonal hospital until 2002 EC, then as a referral hospital until 2011, and is now a comprehensive specialized hospital serving a catchment population of over 5 million people [29]. The Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology includes a labor ward with six beds in the first-stage room, four delivery couches in the second stage, and 37 beds in the maternity ward. The ward is staffed by seven obstetric and gynecological specialists, 46 midwives, one emergency surgeon, two clinical midwifery specialists, 14 general practitioners, and medical interns [30]. According to the hospital reports from 2012 to 2014, the facility provides an average of 969 antenatal care visits and 6,712 deliveries per year, including both regular and referral cases. Among these deliveries, approximately 2,260 were cesarean sections.

Source population

The source population included all laboring mothers who attended at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital in 2022/23.

Study population

The study population consisted of all laboring mothers who were in the active first stage of labor at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Northwest Ethiopia, during the study period.

Eligibility criteria

All laboring mothers who were in the active first stage of labor at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital during the study period were eligible for inclusion in the study. However, women with eclampsia who had not regained consciousness during the active first stage of labor were excluded. This exclusion was necessary because one of the primary data collection methods was direct interview; unconscious women would not be able to respond appropriately, which could compromise the accuracy and completeness of the data. Data from such participants could not reliably capture key variables related to pre-labor and intrapartum predictors. Other data collection methods, including observation and medical record review, were applied to all eligible participants Sample size determination and sampling procedure.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size for this study was calculated using Epi-Info version 7.2.5.0 employing a power-based approach. Variables that were previously identified as significantly associated with dystocia during the active first stage of labor in a study conducted in Israel [12] were used as key determinants for the calculation. For each significant variable, the expected proportion of exposure among cases and controls, the desired confidence level (95%), and the study power (80%) were incorporated into the calculation. This approach ensured that the final sample size was sufficient to detect meaningful differences or associations between the exposure variables and the outcome (dystocia) with statistical reliability. A systematic random sampling technique was used to select study participants from laboring mothers attending DMCSH. The client cards were used as the frame for arranging the participants in chronological order. During the two-month study period, the estimated number of laboring women attending DMCSH was 1,400. Subsequently, every 2nd laboring mother was included in the study until the desired sample size was reached. This systematic approach ensured a representative and unbiased sample of laboring mothers during the study period.

Variables of the study

Dependent variable: Dystocia of active first stage of labor (present/absent).

Independent variables: The independent variables were categorized into six major domains. Socio-demographic factors included maternal age, marital status, occupation, and educational level. Behavioral factors comprised exposure to passive cigarette smoking, coffee consumption, and the intake of linseed or flax. Anthropometric factors included maternal height and height-to-fundal-height ratio. Pregnancy-related factors encompassed parity, gestational age, the presence of obstetric complications such as premature rupture of membranes, hypertension during pregnancy, gestational diabetes, and oligohydramnios or polyhydramnios, inter-pregnancy interval, previous cesarean delivery, and maternal anemia. Intrapartum-related factors included induction of labor, use of labor analgesia, women’s preference for mode of delivery, cervical dilation and fetal descent at admission, satisfaction with healthcare providers (including support during labor), anxiety, ambulation during labor, presence of companionship, and height-to-fundal-height ratio. Finally, healthcare provider-related factors such as the qualification of attending personnel were considered.

Operational definitions

Active phase: The phase of labor beginning from 4 cm of cervical dilation and continuing until full cervical dilation, during which progressive labor changes are expected [31].

Dystocia of active first stage of labor: A condition characterized by slow or arrested progress of labor during the active phase, resulting in delayed cervical dilation or prolonged labor [5].

Protracted disorder of labor: A subtype of dystocia in which cervical dilation progresses at an average rate of less than 1 cm per hour over a 4-hour period, crossing the alert line on the cervicogram, indicating abnormally slow progress during the active first stage of labor [4].

Arrest disorder of active phase of labor: Occurs when labor progress ceases during the active phase, defined as no cervical change for at least 2 h over a 4-hour period, with the cervicogram crossing the alert line [4].

Passive smoker: Is defined as exposure to an average of two or more cigarettes per day during pregnancy [32].Anxiety: Anxiety during labor is measured using the Spielberger State Anxiety Inventory, with scores of 20–40 indicating mild anxiety, 41–54 moderate anxiety, and ≥ 55 severe anxiety [32, 33].

Healthcare providers support: Healthcare provider support is assessed using the Childbirth Satisfaction Rating Scale, a 5-point Likert scale, where scores above the mean indicate good support from healthcare providers [34, 35].

Spontaneous onset of labor: is defined as labor that begins naturally without any artificial stimulation [27], whereas induced labor refers to labor initiated artificially using methods such as oxytocin or prostaglandins, artificial rupture of membranes, or balloon catheter insertion into the cervix [27].

Neonatal asphyxia: is defined as the failure of the newborn to initiate and sustain breathing at birth [36].

Gestational age: is the age of the pregnancy in completed weeks, assessed using the last normal menstrual period (LNMP) or early ultrasound, with preference given to LNMP when both are available and the difference was less than or within the recommended time frame [6].

Ambulation: Ambulation during labor is defined as movement by the parturient for 10-minute intervals every 15 min, repeated at least six times [37, 38].

Inter-pregnancy interval: refers to the duration between the birth of one child and the conception of the subsequent pregnancy, with a short inter-pregnancy interval defined as less than 24 months [2, 39].

Labor companion: a labor companion is any person chosen by the woman to consistently accompany her during labor and childbirth [40].

Data collection tool and procedure

Data were collected using a combination of interviewer-administered structured questionnaires, direct observation, labor follow-up, and chart review. The questionnaire was adapted and modified from previously validated studies to capture relevant socio-demographic, obstetric, and intrapartum-related variables. To assess labor progression, a partograph was employed for diagnosing disorders of the active phase of labor. Maternal anxiety was measured using the Spielberger State Anxiety Inventory, a 20-item self-report instrument with total scores ranging from 20 to 80; each item is rated on a 4-point scale (1 = “not at all,” 2 = “somewhat,” 3 = “moderately so,” 4 = “very much so”), with half of the items reverse-scored (items 1, 2, 5, 8, 10, 11, 12, 16, 19, and 20). Support provided by healthcare providers was assessed using the Childbirth Satisfaction Rating Scale, a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “not at all satisfied” to “completely satisfy.” Additional information, including obstetric history and clinical outcomes, was extracted from patient charts, while maternal height was measured using a standard measuring tape.

Data collection was conducted round-the-clock (day and night) by a team of four midwives with bachelor’s degrees, under the supervision of one MSc and one BSc midwife. Cervical status was assessed by the attending healthcare providers who managed the laboring women. The data collectors then completed the structured questionnaires based on interviews, observations, and chart reviews. This multi-method approach ensured comprehensive, accurate, and reliable data capture throughout the labor process.

Data quality control

To ensure the quality and consistency of the data, the questionnaire was initially prepared in English, translated into the commonly spoken language, Amharic, and then back-translated into English. This process ensured conceptual equivalence and clarity of the items. A pretest was conducted on 5% of the sample (n = 33) at Finote Selam General Hospital to assess the clarity, simplicity, and understandability of the questionnaire, and necessary modifications were made based on the findings. Data collectors and supervisors received comprehensive training on proper data collection procedures, ethical considerations, and close supervision of the process. The principal investigator actively oversaw the entire data collection, working closely with the supervisors to maintain standardization and reliability. Data collection occurred day and night, and supervisors, together with the principal investigator, regularly checked completed questionnaires for completeness and accuracy, allowing prompt corrective measures whenever inconsistencies or gaps were identified. These measures collectively ensured high-quality, reliable, and valid data for the study.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were checked, coded, and entered using Epi-Data version 4.2, and then exported to SPSS version 26 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics and cross-tabulations were initially performed to clean the data, assess completeness, and identify missing values or outliers. Bivariable analysis was conducted to explore the association between independent variables and dystocia of the active first stage of labor. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model to control for potential confounders and identify independent predictors. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in the multivariable analysis were considered statistically significant. The final results were presented using tables, charts, and descriptive text, ensuring clarity and ease of interpretation. This systematic approach allowed for a robust identification of factors associated with dystocia while maintaining data integrity and reliability.

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Review Board of the School of Midwifery, College of Medicine and Health Sciences, University of Gondar (Ref. No. Mid/W.H/…03/2015). Formal permission to conduct the study was also obtained from the administrators of Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. Written and oral informed consent was obtained from each participant after explaining the purpose of the study, the procedures involved, potential risks and benefits, and their rights as participants. To ensure confidentiality, personal identifiers such as names, addresses, and signatures were not included in the data collection instruments. All participants were assured that their information would be kept strictly confidential and used solely for research purposes.

Result

Socio demographic and behavioral characteristic

A total of 656 laboring mothers attending Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital were enrolled in the study, yielding a response rate of 96.2%. Among these, 11 participants (1.7%) were lost to follow-up due to cesarean delivery performed for indications other than dystocia of the active first stage of labor, and before the completion of the active phase; these were considered non-respondents. The remaining 620 participants (94.5%) completed follow-up and were included in the final analysis. The median age of participants was 27 years (IQR = 6), with the majority, 354 (57.1%), falling within the 25–34-year age group. Most participants, 612 (98.7%), reported following the Orthodox Christian religion, and nearly all, 613 (98.9%), were married. A majority of respondents, 420 (67.7%), resided in urban areas (Table 2).

Incidence of dystocia in the active first stage of labor

Among 620 laboring mothers at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, 35.3% (95% CI: 31.9–39.2) experienced abnormal labor progress (dystocia) during the active first stage. Of these, 135 (21.8%) had protracted labor, while 84 (13.5%) experienced arrest of cervical dilation (Fig. 2). Regarding interventions following dystocia, 148 (23.9%) of laboring mothers received labor augmentation, 12 (1.9%) underwent cesarean delivery, and 59 (9.5%) had no intervention after the diagnosis. In terms of delivery outcomes, 427 (68.9%) of participants delivered vaginally, 130 (21.0%) by cesarean section, 56 (9.0%) with instrumental delivery (mainly vacuum), and 7 (1.1%) with destructive delivery. Among those diagnosed with dystocia, 94 (42.9%) ultimately delivered vaginally, 96 (43.8%) by cesarean section, 24 (10.9%) with instrumental assistance, and 5 (2.3%) with destructive delivery. Regarding cesarean delivery indications, 38 (6.1%) were performed due to dystocia of the active first stage of labor, 51 (8.2%) due to non-reassuring fetal heart rate patterns (of which 43 [6.9%] occurred after dystocia and augmentation), 36 (5.8%) due to cephalopelvic disproportion (13 [2%] after dystocia diagnosis), and 5 (0.8%) due to malposition or malpresentation (3 [0.4%] diagnosed with dystocia). Overall, 15.4% of cesarean deliveries were performed following confirmed dystocia and subsequent intervention. The majority of dystocia cases (51.1%) were diagnosed at a cervical dilation of 6 cm, followed by 26.5% at 5 cm (Fig. 3).

Predictors of dystocia in the active first stage of labor

Bivariable logistic regression identified several factors associated with dystocia of the active first stage of labor (AFSOL), including intake of linseed/flax at the onset of labor, ANC follow-up, fetal heart rate monitoring method, labor induction, ambulation, presence of a companion, intravenous fluid use, maternal age, parity, inter-pregnancy interval, caffeine intake, maternal anxiety, cervical dilation at admission, and quality of healthcare provider support.

In multivariable analysis, seven factors remained statistically significant predictors of dystocia of AFSOL. Laboring mothers who consumed linseed at the onset of labor experienced a 75% reduction in the risk of dystocia compared to those who did not (AOR: 0.25; 95% CI: 0.12, 0.51). Conversely, mothers who drank three or more cups of coffee per day were 3.4 times more likely to develop dystocia compared to those who consumed less (AOR: 3.4; 95% CI: 1.7, 6.84). ANC follow-up during the current pregnancy reduced the odds of dystocia by 76% (AOR: 0.24; 95% CI: 0.09, 0.63).

Laboring mothers admitted with cervical dilation < 4 cm were 2.7 times more likely to develop dystocia than those admitted at ≥ 4 cm (AOR: 2.7; 95% CI: 1.62, 4.63). Induced labor was associated with a 2.3-fold increase in dystocia risk compared to spontaneous labor (AOR: 2.3; 95% CI: 1.09, 4.91). Ambulation during AFSOL significantly reduced the risk of dystocia by 77% compared to restricted movement (AOR: 0.23; 95% CI: 0.09, 0.54). Finally, laboring mothers who reported poor support from healthcare providers were 7.3 times more likely to experience dystocia than those satisfied with the care provided (AOR: 7.3; 95% CI: 4.06, 13.39) (Table 5). These findings underscore the importance of early admission, continuous supportive care, ambulation, and lifestyle factors such as linseed consumption and caffeine intake in the prevention of dystocia during the active first stage of labor.

Discussion

The present study found that the overall incidence of dystocia during the active first stage of labor which includes both protracted and arrest disorders of cervical dilation was 35.3% (95% CI: 31.9–39.2). This finding aligns closely with previous studies conducted in Sweden, including a prospective follow-up study reporting an incidence of 37% and a retrospective observational study reporting 32.5% [9, 13]. The similarity of these findings suggests that the burden of active first stage labor dystocia observed in this study is comparable to that reported in high-resource settings, highlighting the clinical relevance of early identification and management strategies.

However, it is considerably higher than rates reported in the United States (20.8%), Sweden (21%), and Israel (1.3%) [12, 14, 20, 28]. Several factors may explain these differences. First, variations in study populations and the qualifications of labor care providers likely play a role. Second, unlike some studies that excluded women with a scarred uterus, our study included them; notably, around 67% of parturient with a scarred uterus were admitted to the labor ward with cervical dilation of < 4 cm. Evidence from this study and others consistently shows that early admission in latent labor (cervical dilation < 4 cm) is a strong predictor of dystocia during the active first stage. Thus, the higher incidence observed here may reflect both the inclusion of high-risk groups and the timing of admission. These findings highlight the importance of tailored labor management, particularly for women with previous cesarean or scarred uterus, to reduce the risk of labor complications.

In this study, the key predictors of dystocia during the active first stage of labor were ANC follow-up, induction of labor, consumption of linseed/flax, quality of health care provider support, drinking more than three cups of coffee per day, cervical dilation at admission < 4 cm, and ambulation during labor.

Laboring mothers who attended ANC during their current pregnancy were 76% less likely to experience dystocia compared to those who did not. This protective effect may be explained by the fact that ANC provides essential counseling on birth preparedness and complication readiness, enabling women to be better prepared psychologically, economically, and practically for childbirth. These findings emphasize the critical role of ANC in promoting optimal labor outcomes and highlight the importance of supporting women throughout pregnancy to reduce labor complications.

This study found that laboring mothers who consumed three or more cups of coffee per day (estimated > 200 mg of caffeine) during pregnancy were 3.4 times more likely to develop dystocia during the active first stage of labor compared to those who consumed less. This finding aligns with previous research conducted in the United States [28]. A plausible explanation is that caffeine can reduce uterine contractility by inhibiting phosphodiesterase activity, which leads to an increase in intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels. Elevated cAMP induces uterine relaxation through activation of specific protein kinases that phosphorylate and inhibit myosin light chain kinase (MLCK), counteracting the calcium-dependent contraction pathway. These alterations in myosin light chain phosphorylation and the reduction in contractile protein content within the myometrium ultimately decrease the force of uterine contractions, potentially contributing to prolonged labor and dystocia [41–43].

Our study found that laboring mothers admitted to the intrapartum ward with cervical dilation of less than 4 cm were 2.7 times more likely to experience dystocia in the active first stage of labor (AFSOL) compared to those admitted with cervical dilation of 4 cm or more. This finding aligns with studies conducted in California, China and Portland, which reported similar associations between early admission during the latent phase and increased risk of labor dystocia [15, 44, 45]. Firstly, early admission during the latent phase may lead to unnecessary interventions, such as oxytocin augmentation, which have been associated with higher cesarean delivery rates [45, 46]. Secondly, women admitted during this phase may experience increased anxiety due to the prolonged duration of labor, which has been identified as a strong predictor of labor dystocia [46]. Furthermore, early admission may limit the mobility of laboring women, as they are often encouraged to remain in bed. Reduced mobility during labor has been linked to longer labor durations and higher cesarean delivery rates. In contrast, encouraging ambulation during labor has been associated with shorter labor durations and improved outcomes. Therefore, our findings suggest that redefining the criteria for the active phase of labor to commence at 6 cm cervical dilation, as recommended by recent studies, may help reduce unnecessary interventions and improve labor outcomes [47, 48]. Additionally, promoting policies that encourage delayed admission until active labor is established could potentially decrease the incidence of dystocia and associated complications [27].

Based on our findings, dystocia of the active first stage of labor was 2.3 times more likely to occur in labor that was artificially induced compared to spontaneous labor. This is consistent with studies conducted in Portland, Israel, Norway, and Iran [12, 15, 32, 49]. One plausible explanation is that induced labor is often more painful than spontaneous labor, which can increase maternal stress. This stress triggers excessive catecholamine release from the adrenal glands, including dopamine, epinephrine, and norepinephrine, which act on the alpha- and beta-adrenergic receptors of the uterus, ultimately leading to ineffective uterine contractions [50, 51]. Additionally, labor induction may be initiated in women with an unfavorable cervix, which can further compromise the efficiency of contractions and contribute to labor dystocia. These mechanisms together may explain the increased risk of dystocia observed in induced labor.

Our study revealed that laboring mothers who received poor support or were dissatisfied with intrapartum care during the active first stage of labor were 7.3 times more likely to experience dystocia compared to those who received good support. This finding aligns with studies conducted in Iran and Portland [15, 32]. A possible explanation is that inadequate support from healthcare providers can increase maternal anxiety and stress during labor. Elevated stress and anxiety levels may impair the effectiveness of uterine contractions by altering the balance of stress hormones such as catecholamine’s, which can inhibit normal myometrial activity and slow labor progress. This highlights the critical role of supportive, respectful, and attentive intrapartum care in facilitating normal labor.

Our study demonstrated that ambulation during the active first stage of labor significantly reduces the likelihood of dystocia. Laboring mothers who ambulated were 77% less likely to experience dystocia compared to those who were restricted to bed rest. This finding aligns with previous studies conducted in Egypt, Tunisia, and India, which reported similar benefits of mobility during labor [37, 38, 52]. Ambulation during labor leverages gravity to facilitate fetal descent, enhancing cervical dilation and promoting effective uterine contractions. Upright and mobile positions help prevent compression of abdominal blood vessels, thereby maximizing uterine blood flow and improving oxygen delivery to the uterus and fetus. Additionally, these positions can increase the strength and frequency of uterine contractions, which are crucial for the progression of labor [53, 54]. Encouraging ambulation during the active first stage of labor can be a cost-effective and non-invasive strategy to reduce the incidence of dystocia. Healthcare providers should consider facilitating mobility for laboring women, as it not only aids in labor progression but also enhances maternal comfort and satisfaction. Implementing policies that support ambulation could lead to improved maternal and neonatal outcomes, as well as a reduction in the need for pharmacological interventions.

Our study observed a striking 75% reduction in the likelihood of dystocia during the active first stage of labor among parturients who consumed linseed (flaxseed) during the third trimester or at the onset of labor pain. This finding aligns with a randomized controlled trial conducted in Iran, which reported that flaxseed intake increased cervical ripening and shortened the first stage of labor [55]. Flaxseed is rich in omega-3 fatty acids, lignans, and fiber, which may contribute to cervical ripening and enhanced uterine contractility. Lignans, such as secoisolariciresinol diglucoside (SDG), possess antioxidant and estrogenic properties that could influence cervical tissue remodeling. Additionally, omega-3 fatty acids are known to modulate inflammatory pathways, potentially promoting uterine contractility and cervical softening [56].

However, it’s important to note that while flaxseed consumption may offer benefits, excessive intake could pose risks. A study from the University of Montreal indicated that consuming flaxseed oil during the last two trimesters of pregnancy was associated with a fourfold increased risk of preterm birth. Therefore, moderation is key, and pregnant women should consult healthcare providers before incorporating flaxseed into their diet [57, 58]. Our findings suggest that flaxseed consumption during the third trimester or at the onset of labor pain may reduce the incidence of dystocia during the active first stage of labor. Nevertheless, further research is warranted to confirm these effects and establish safe consumption guidelines.

Strength and limitation of the study

A key strength of this study is that data were collected continuously, both during the day and at night, which ensured a more representative sample of laboring mothers at Debre Markos Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. This approach allowed for inclusion of participants across different shifts and labor patterns, increasing the generalizability of our findings within the institution. However, this study has some limitations. First, it was conducted in a single comprehensive specialized hospital due to feasibility constraints, which may limit the external generalizability of the results to other settings or regions. Second, the measurement of certain variables relied on participant recall, which may have introduced recall bias. To minimize this, we provided participants with adequate time to reflect on their responses and trained our data collectors thoroughly on how to approach mothers sensitively and systematically to ensure accurate data capture. Despite these efforts, the potential for residual recall bias cannot be entirely excluded. We included both multipara and primigravida mothers in the analysis. Although parity is an important predictor of dystocia and both groups are followed using the same partograph in Ethiopia, their differing labor progression patterns may have influenced the incidence of dystocia, and this should be considered when interpreting our findings.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that the incidence of dystocia (abnormal progress) of the active first stage of labor (AFSOL) is higher than reported in many other countries. Protective factors include antenatal care follow-up, consumption of linseed in late pregnancy, and ambulation during labor, whereas drinking three or more cups of coffee per day, labor induction, poor healthcare provider support, and admission at cervical dilation < 4 cm increase the risk of dystocia. To improve maternal and neonatal outcomes, it is recommended to encourage ambulation during labor, admit mothers in active labor when cervical dilation is > 4 cm, carefully assess the need for induction, and adopt supportive, patient-centered intrapartum care in line with the WHO Labor Care Guide. Further multi-center studies are warranted to explore additional determinants of dystocia and optimize labor management practices in Ethiopia.
