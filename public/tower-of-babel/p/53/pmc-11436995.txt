Identification of blood lipid markers of docetaxel treatment in prostate cancer patients
Authors: Morgan C. Finnerty, Franklin E. Leach, Yousef Zakharia, Kenneth G. Nepple, Michael G. Bartlett, Michael D. Henry, Brian S. Cummings
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Docetaxel is commonly used for treatment of castration-resistant prostate cancer. Unfortunately, many prostate cancer patients develop resistance to docetaxel. Clinical markers less invasive than biopsies, such as blood samples, would be ideal for monitoring and predicting patient treatment outcomes to docetaxel. Lipid alterations are often associated with the progression of many cancers, including prostate cancer. This study investigated the use of lipids from whole blood as clinical markers for docetaxel resistance in a small cohort of patients with prostate cancer. Qualitative lipidomics was performed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry to assess the lipid composition of prostate cancer cells exposed to docetaxel as well as whole blood from prostate cancer patients before, during and after docetaxel treatment. Three patients had castration resistant prostate cancer, three had castration sensitive prostate cancer, and four had de novo prostate cancer during the extent of the study. Mean decrease accuracy and classical univariate receiving operating characteristic curve analyses were performed to identify potential biomarkers. In total, 245 and 221 altered lipids were identified from a second stage of mass spectrometry analysis of prostate cancer cells and clinical blood samples, respectively. Both models indicated that docetaxel treatment altered ether-linked phosphatidylcholines, lysophosphatidylcholine, diacylglycerols, ceramides, hexosylceramides, and sphingomyelins. The results also indicated several lipid changes were associated with sphingolipid signaling and metabolism, and glycerophospholipid metabolism. Collectively, these data suggest the potential usage of identified lipid species as indicators of docetaxel resistance in prostate cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73074-8.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among men in the United States, accounting for 29% of diagnoses1. It is estimated that 288,300 new cases will develop and 34,700 men will die from PCa in 20231. Despite incidence and mortality rates stabilizing for PCa since 2011, diagnosis at a distant stage of PCa has doubled over the past decade1. Most patients diagnosed with PCa are initially treated with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT). At first, ADT is an effective hormonal treatment; however, many PCa patients develop resistance to this therapy within 2–3 years after ADT initiation, developing castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), and another treatment is required2–5. Often taxanes, microtubule stabilizing agents, are then used.

Docetaxel (Taxotere®) is a semisynthetic derivative of paclitaxel (Taxol). It is highly lipophilic in nature, but exhibits better water solubility, less toxicity and rapid absorption properties than paclitaxel6. Thus, docetaxel is commonly used for the treatment of cancer that has failed prior therapy7. Docetaxel treatment is currently approved for breast, gastric, head and neck, esophageal, non-small cell lung, ovary, uterus, and prostate cancers8. As a top approved choice for the treatment of PCa, docetaxel is effective at prolonging the lifespan by a few months of patients with PCa when used with prednisone, as compared to other treatment options9. Although docetaxel is widely approved and used, it has a known mechanism of resistance in PCa5. Many clinical studies are attempting to extend the clinical application of docetaxel by identifying indicators of patient resistance either before or during treatment.

Currently, the most frequent clinical markers used to assess the response of PCa patients to treatment are Gleason score, tumor stage, and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels, all of which are often used in combination10. Although a few parameters, like PSA, have been studied to predict patient overall survivability with docetaxel treatment, its effectiveness have been questioned11,12. Various studies in the past decade have explored the use of lipid species in biofluids, such as plasma and urine, as potential biomarkers for PCa10,13,14. However, lipids have not yet been proposed as biomarkers for docetaxel resistance.

Understanding the mechanisms of docetaxel resistance in CRPC may aid in developing future treatment options or predictors of resistance. Lipid metabolism has become a topic of interest in the realm of drug resistance as it is involved in several cell processes critical to cancer cell survival15,16. Despite these studies, there are few studies testing that lipid alterations can predict drug resistance and even fewer focusing on lipids as clinical markers for docetaxel resistance in PCa. In this study, an exploratory analysis was conducted to determine how docetaxel alters the lipidome in PCa cells in vitro and in blood samples from PCa patients treated with docetaxel. Data from this analysis will be used to propel additional studies identifying how lipids can serve as potential clinical markers of docetaxel resistance.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade chloroform, HPLC grade water, trypsin, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and Corning® Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 media were purchased from Fisher Scientific LLC (Waltham, MA, USA). Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) grade methanol (MeOH), ammonium formate, formic acid, LC-MS grade acetonitrile (ACN), LC-MS grade water, L-ascorbic acid, ammonium molybdate tetrahydrate, sulfuric acid, thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide, phosphorus standard solution, and HPLC grade docetaxel were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) was purchased from Alfa Aesar (Ward Hill, MA, USA). SPLASH® Lipidomix® Mass Spec standards were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids, Inc. (Alabaster, Alabama, USA). The human PCa cell line PC-3, fetal bovine serum (FBS), and a penicillin-streptomycin solution were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). The stable deuterated isotope labeled internal standard, paclitaxel-d5, was purchased from Cayman Chemical Co. (Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA).

In vitro studies

Cell culture

PC-3 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% carbon dioxide (CO2). To assess lipid alterations at an intracellular level, PC-3 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h of incubation, 1 mL of fresh media was added to each well followed by either DMSO or docetaxel. Final concentrations of docetaxel were either 0.4, 1, or 2 nM. Cells were incubated for an additional 48 h before being harvested. After this time, cells were washed with 1× PBS before being incubated with trypsin. Cells were collected, centrifuged for 5 min, and prepared using 0.5 mL MeOH. Whole-cell lysates were then vortexed and frozen at – 80 °C. Samples were replicated with four different passages on different days as suggested (n = 4)17.

MTT assay

Cell viability was assessed using a 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay and cell morphology. In brief, PC-3 cells were seeded in 48-well plates (6 × 104 cells per well) and then treated with various concentrations of docetaxel after a 24-hour incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After an additional 48-h incubation, MTT was added to each well (final concentration: 0.6 mM) before plates were incubated for 2 h. Wells were aspirated, 0.4 mL DMSO per well was added, and then plates were placed on a shaker for at least 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm on a CLARIOstar® plate reader from BMG Labtech (Ortenberg, Germany).

Quantification of docetaxel

PC-3 cells were treated with either DMSO or 1 nM docetaxel for various time points (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 24, and 48 h) to ensure the uptake of docetaxel into the cells. Docetaxel was quantified using a modified extraction and LC-MS method18. In short, samples (250 µL) were dried under vacuum to evaporate MeOH before adding 200 µL water. Docetaxel standards were prepared in ACN before being diluted in water (final concentrations: 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00, 1.50, 2.00, and 4.00 nM). MTBE (1 mL) was added before undergoing vortex (10 min) then centrifugation (10 min, 20 °C, and 21130 rfm). The top layer (0.5 mL) was dried under vacuum before being re-suspended in initial mobile phase (60:40 MeOH: water). An ACQUITY ultra-performance liquid chromatography H-Class PLUS system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) was interfaced to a Waters Xevo Micro TQS mass spectrometer with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The transitions m/z 830.1→304.0 and 881.3→313.0 of the [M + Na] + ions were monitored for docetaxel and paclitaxel-d5, respectively; while the transitions of m/z 830.1→549.0 and 881.3→591.1 were used for confirmation, as suggested18,19.

Patients and sample collection

Thirty blood samples from ten patients with PCa were obtained from the University of Iowa Hospitals & Clinics between October 2020 and November 2022. Ethical approval to report this case was obtained from the University of Iowa Biobank (IRB#201103721). Written informed consent was obtained from the patients for their anonymized information to be published in this article. This information included the patient’s year of birth and diagnosis, race, cancer grade, docetaxel dosage, exposure to other cancer treatments, cancer type, docetaxel response, and more (S. Table 1). Docetaxel response included either sensitivity or resistance and was determined based on PSA values. Following treatment, PSA values greater than those before and during treatment indicate docetaxel resistance. Criteria for selection of subjects included: patients must meet the Genito-Urologic Molecular Epidemiology Resource (GU-MER) eligibility criteria, patient may not have HIV positive status, no prior treatment with docetaxel or any other chemotherapy agent, patient must have a planned (scheduled) but not yet initiated docetaxel treatment as standard of care, and patient must be willing to provide periodic blood samples for research. Each patient agreed to three time points: pre-treatment, mid-treatment, and post-treatment. The various timepoints throughout each patient’s schedule were to determine the lipid alterations in whole blood due to docetaxel, specifically looking to identify potential markers for resistance. The same protocol was used in collection of blood samples from patients at each time point: from each subject, 10 mL whole blood was collected into an EDTA tube. The sample was promptly delivered to the Tissue Procurement Core facility (TPC; within 15 min after collection of whole blood), and either processed immediately or stored at 4 °C for no more than 1–2 h. Processing of samples included placing 100 µL of EDTA-anti-coagulated whole blood in a glass screw cap vial, adding 1 mL 2:0.8 MeOH: water, capping and vortexing for 30 s, and storing at – 80 °C. All samples were transported on dry ice to the University of Georgia for lipid analysis. To reduce degradation effects, the samples were kept frozen at – 80 °C until the start of the lipidomic experiment with only one freeze-thaw20. The study design is shown in S. Figure 1.

Untargeted lipidomics

Lipid extraction

Each sample was extracted using a modified Bligh-Dyer method21 after being thawed on ice. In brief, 0.5 mL of either 2:0.8 MeOH: water (for whole blood blanks), 0.5 mL of MeOH (for in vitro blanks), or sample was added to borosilicate glass 16 × 125 mm tubes sitting on ice. To all but one tube with 2:0.8 MeOH: water (blank), 10 µL of SPLASH Lipidomix Mass Spec standards was added. MeOH (0.5 mL) and chloroform (1.25 mL) were added to each tube and vortexed. Next, chloroform (1.25 mL) and water (1.25 mL) were added to each tube and vortexed. The samples were then centrifuged (300×g; 7 min). The organic (bottom) layer of chloroform was then transferred to a new tube. Chloroform (0.5 mL) was added to the new tubes, and the tubes were vortexed then centrifuged (300×g; 7 min). This process was repeated two more times before the chloroform layer was dried under nitrogen, reconstituted with 50 µL of MeOH: chloroform (3:1 v/v), and stored at – 80 °C until analysis.

Liquid phosphorus assay

A modified Bartlett Assay was used to quantify lipid content by determining the level of inorganic phosphorus22. As such, various amounts of stock phosphorus standard solution (0, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 3.0 and 5.0 µg) were added to borosilicate glass 16 × 125 mm tubes resting on ice. Standards were prepared in duplicate. Lipid extracts (7 µL) and 5 M sulfuric acid (400 µL) were added to separate tubes and heated at 200 °C for 1 h. Tubes were removed from heat and allowed to cool on ice before 5% v/v H2O2 (100 µL) was then added. Tubes were vortexed, centrifuged (300×g; 1 min) and heated again at 200 °C for 1.5 h. At this time, tubes were removed and allowed to cool on ice before molybdate reagent (4.6 mL of 1.1 g ammonium molybdate tetrahydrate in 12.5 mL sulfuric acid and 500 mL diH2O) was added. Next, 15% v/v ascorbic acid (100 µL) was added, followed by vortexing. The solution was then boiled for 7–10 min at 100 °C in a water bath. A 150 µL aliquot was added to a 96-well plate in triplicate and the absorbance was measured at 830 nm on a CLARIOstar® plate reader from BMG Labtech. A standard curve was prepared to determine the phosphorus mass content of each sample. Lipid extracts (500 ρmol/µL) were prepared by reconstitution in chloroform: MeOH (2:1, v/v).

Chromatography and mass spectrometry

A Thermo Scientific Accucore™ C30 HPLC column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.6 μm) was used for reversed-phase chromatography. The mobile phase was water/acetonitrile (40:60 v/v; A), and acetonitrile/2-propanol (10:90 v/v; B). Both mobile phases contained 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid additives to improve peak shape as well as ionization efficiency. A gradient elution was used to separate the analytes (time/minute, % mobile phase B): (0.00, 20), (3.30, 60), (8.67, 70), (9.33, 85), (12.67, 90), (12.93, 100), (21.00, 100), (21.10, 20), (25.00, 20). The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min and the column temperature was 55 °C, while the autosampler was maintained at 4 °C. The injection volume was 2 µL. Separations were performed in both positive and negative ionization modes.

For the mass spectrometry (MS) data collection of the lipids, a Q Exactive HF (ThermoFisher Scientific) equipped with a heated ESI ion source was used. The mass spectrometer was run in full MS mode at a resolution of 60,000 (at m/z 200) for the duration of the chromatographic gradient. An automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1e5 was set with a maximum injection time of 150 ms. Data dependent MS2 was performed including a resolution: 15,000, AGC target: 1e5, loop count: 5, quadrupole isolation width: 2.5 m/z, and normalized collision energy: 25 eV. A tune file with the following source conditions was used for positive and negative mode: spray voltage (+): 3500, spray voltage (−): 2500, capillary temperature: 262.50 °C, sheath gas: 48, aux gas: 11, spare gas: 2, max spray current: 100, probe heater temperature: 425.0 °C, and S-Lens RF level: 50. A m/z scan range of 150–2000 was used. Calibration was conducted using ThermoFisher Pierce™ Negative Ion Calibration Solution and Pierce™ LTQ Velos ESI Positive Ion Calibration Solutions prior to the collection of negative and positive mode data, respectively.

Lipidomic analysis

Spectral deconvolution and peak identification were performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (MS1 analysis)23 and MS-DIAL 4.48 (MS2 analysis)24. Once acquired, full scan raw data files were converted to usable formats (mzXML and ABF) using MSConvert and Reifycs Analysis Base File Converter, respectively. A generalized workflow for the lipidomic analysis is shown in S. Figure 2. In MetaboAnalyst 5.0, default LC-MS spectra processing parameters were selected for a HPLC-orbitrap platform for each ionization mode of the MS1 data. All data uploaded were then filtered by interquartile range, samples were normalized by sum, and features were scaled by autoscaling (standardization or unit variance scaling)25. To visualize the overall grouping trend of all samples, unsupervised principal component analysis was first performed (data not shown). Further analysis of the data involved supervised partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). Additionally, volcano plots of MS1 data were generated with fold change (FC) > 2.0 and p-value < 0.05.

In MS-DIAL 4.48, the negative and positive ionization mode data were also analyzed separately for MS2 analysis. The initial parameters selected included: ionization type: soft ionization, separation type: chromatography, MS method type: conventional LC/MS, data type (MS1): profile data, data type (MS2): centroid data, and target omics: lipidomics. The ion mode selected differed between negative and positive ionization mode appropriately. The following analysis parameters were selected for data collection: MS1 tolerance: 0.01, MS2 tolerance: 0.025, MS1 mass range begin: 275, MS1 mass range end: 2000, MS/MS mass range begin: 275, and MS/MS mass range end: 2000. Parameters selected for peak detection included: minimum peak height: 10,000, mass slice width: 0.05, smoothing level: 3, and minimum peak width: 5. Parameters selected for MS2Dec included: sigma window value: 0.5, MS/MS abundance cut off: 0, and exclude after precursor ion: yes. Parameters selected for identification included: retention time tolerance: 100, accurate mass tolerance (MS1): 0.01, accurate mass tolerance (MS2): 0.05, and identification score cut off: 80. Adducts were selected based on ion mode. Parameters selected for alignment included: reference file: retention time tolerance: 0.1, and MS1 tolerance: 0.025. Once analysis was completed, the results were normalized within MS-DIAL 4.48 to SPLASH® Lipidomix deuterated lipids identified based on extracted ion chromatograms of an aligned spot for each sample and the highest signal-to-noise ratio of duplicates. Alignment results were exported with zero values replaced with 1/10 of the minimum peak height over all samples as text files and saved in Excel.

Identified lipids were filtered by removing features identified with “Unknown”, “w/o MS2”, and “RIKEN” to only include those identified by MS2. These lipids were further redacted via solvent blank subtraction (based on 5-fold greater abundance in sample groups than blanks) and average sound: noise > 10. These filtered lipids from each ionization mode were then combined before repeated lipids were reduced to the first listed lipid species (S. Table 2 and 3). Detected SPLASH standards, with identified adducts, retention times and ESI mode are reported for each experiment in S. Table 4.

MetaboAnalyst 5.0 was used for further analysis of the MS2 data. Mean decrease accuracy (MDA) and classical univariate receiving operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed to identify potential biomarkers. Data files with the reduced lipid list and corresponding peak intensities for each set of samples were inputted for each analysis. Data was normalized by sum and auto scaled before random forest classification was performed. For MDA analysis, one factor and multiple experimental factors were considered to rank lipid features based on their contributions to classification accuracy. Multiple factor analysis was only performed with the clinical data. As such, docetaxel treatment outcome was the primary metadata of interest, while PSA and each time point in which samples were drawn were covariates. Features with a MDA > 0.002 were considered important features to the model’s accuracy. For univariate analysis, the lipid features were ranked based on area under the ROC curve comparing predicted outcomes to “true” outcomes (S. Table 5 and 6). Lipids that had the highest area under the curve (AUC) values (> 0.65) were considered to have the highest predictive ability for docetaxel treatment and used for further analysis.

Biomarker multivariate analysis was performed in MetaboAnalyst 5.0 using random forest classification. Generated models were considered, but the model with the highest AUC and best 95% confidence interval range was used to rank features by the frequency of being selected. Functional analysis of the highest predictive lipids was performed using LIPEA26 to identify significantly perturbed lipid pathways (Bonferroni-corrected p-value < 1.0; S. Table 7 and 8). Lipid ontology trends within each sample group from the highest predictive lipids were determined using LION/web (S. Table 9 and 10)27. Lipids were ranked based on one-tailed T-tests and normalized signals as percentages.

As a validation study is predicted, power analysis was performed in MetaboAnalyst 5.0 with the combined and filtered lipids from MS-DIAL to determine the sample size required for further analysis. To detect significantly different features between the docetaxel resistant and sensitive blood samples with a false discovery rate (FDR) = 0.05 and a power of 80%, a sample size of about 165 patients would be required per treatment arm.

Semi-targeted lipidomics

All extracted blood samples were shipped to the Wayne State University (WSU) Lipidomics Core Facility after untargeted analysis was performed. Samples were spiked with 100 ng each of additional internal standards (diheptadecanoyl phosphatidylcholine (PC), monoheptadecanoyl PC, and N-C17-ceramide from Avanti Polar Lipids) for quantification. Total lipid analysis generally follows the published protocols by LipidMaps consortium with specific details as outlined below28.

The extracted samples were analyzed for PCs by directly infusing into the mass spectrometer (QTRAP5500; ABSCIEX) at 5 µL/min. The samples were scanned from 350 to 1000 Da at 10,000 Da/s and Information Dependent Acquisition (IDA) of MS/MS spectra were collected for 5 min with dynamic exclusion of 5 most intense molecular ions after three occurrences. The total cycle time of each IDA scan was 1.75 s, resulting in a maximum possible detection of over 850 molecules over 5 min. The same IDA experiment is conducted in both positive and negative ion modes. In addition to IDA experiments, the samples were also analyzed by multiple precursor ion scan (MPIS) methods in both negative and positive ion modes29–31. The mass analyzer conditions were as follows: Curtain gas: 30 psi, GS1: 20 psi, Collision gas: High, Ion Spray Voltage: +5500 V or − 4500 V (for positive and negative ion modes, respectively), Declustering Potential: +/−100 V, and Entrance Potential: +/−10 V.

The extracted samples were analyzed for ceramide (Cer) lipids using a precursor ion scan of m/z 264. Sphingoid bases and their phosphates were analyzed by a Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) method. HPLC separation was performed on a Prominence XR system (Shimadzu) using a Targa C8 (5 μm, 2.1 × 20 mm, Higgins Analytical) column. The mobile phase consisted of methanol-water-ammonium formate-formic acid (5:95:1 mM:0.2 v/v; A) and methanol-ammonium formate-formic acid (100:2 mM:0.2 v/v; B). A gradient elution was used to separate the analytes (time/minute, % mobile phase B): (0, 35), (2, 100), and (5, 100). The flow rate was 0.5 mL/min. The HPLC eluate was directly introduced to the ESI source of a QTRAP5500 mass analyzer in the positive ion mode with following conditions: Curtain gas: 35 psi, GS1: 45 psi, GS2: 45 psi, Temperature: 650 °C, Ion Spray Voltage: 5500 V, Collision gas: low, Declustering Potential: 100 V, and Entrance Potential: 10 V. The eluate was monitored by the MRM method to detect unique molecular ion—daughter ion combinations for each of the sphingolipids with 100 ms dwell time for each transition. Optimized Collisional Energies (16–28 eV) and Collision Cell Exit Potentials (10 V) were used for each MRM transition. The data were collected using Analyst 1.6.2 software and the MRM transition chromatograms were quantitated by MultiQuant software (both from ABSCIEX). The internal standard (C-17 analogs of the sphingolipids) signal in each chromatogram was used for normalization for recovery as well as relative quantitation of each analyte. Data were analyzed by LipidView software (ABSCIEX) for the identification and quantitation of lipids. The data was also exported as tab-delimited text file for further statistical analysis.

Statistical analyses

Results are represented as mean ± standard error of measurement. MetaboAnalyst 5.0, LIPEA, LION/web, and GraphPad Prism 9.4 were used for all statistical analysis as previously indicated, and differences with p-value < 0.05 were considered significant. Experiments were replicated at least three times (n = 3) unless otherwise indicated.

Results

Acute influence of docetaxel on PC-3 cell growth

Advanced PCa cell lines have been studied extensively to understand docetaxel resistance. Typically, docetaxel-resistant cells are created through a long-term intermittent regimen with increasing doses32,33. However, few studies have observed alterations during the progression toward resistance, thus an acute study was performed to exam changes in PC-3 cells after exposure to docetaxel. Like the IC50 observed in other studies32–34, the IC50 for PC-3 cells was 1.58 nM after a 48-hour exposure to docetaxel (Fig. 1A). Quantification of docetaxel after exposure to 1 nM docetaxel showed that PC-3 cells had retained the initial 1 nM docetaxel after about 11 h (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, docetaxel continued to concentrate in the cells until levels plateaued about 1.5 nM after 48 h (Fig. 1B). This supports the results of previous studies showing that docetaxel concentrates within cells35. Few morphological changes were observed other than a decrease in confluency as a response to increases in docetaxel concentration (Fig. 1C–F).

Docetaxel impacts PC-3 cells lipid metabolism

Recent lipidomic analysis from our laboratory indicated that docetaxel resistance in multiple PCa cell lines impacts several lipid species, especially those involved in glycerophospholipid (GP) metabolism, sphingolipid (SP) signaling and ferroptosis36. However, these studies were performed in PCa cells in which docetaxel resistance had been established, as opposed to those initially exposed to docetaxel. As such, the lipidome of PC-3 cells treated with 0.4, 1 or 2 nM docetaxel for 48 h was analyzed. PLS-DA plots showed clear separation between docetaxel-treated and DMSO-treated PC-3 cells (Fig. 2A,B). Separation between treatment groups was strengthened with increases in docetaxel concentration (Fig. 2C,H). The number of suppressed or enriched lipid features after docetaxel treatment, as determined from volcano plots, also increased after exposure to higher concentrations of docetaxel (Fig. 3). In the ESI + mode, there were 12 features down-regulated and 16 up-regulated for 0.4 nM, 23 down-regulated and 25 up-regulated for 1 nM, and 174 down-regulated and 268 up-regulated for 2 nM docetaxel, all compared to control (Fig. 3A,C,E). For the ESI- mode, there were 6 features down-regulated and 8 up-regulated for 0.4 nM, 9 down-regulated and 10 up-regulated for 1 nM, and 39 down-regulated and 51 up-regulated for 2 nM docetaxel (Fig. 3B,D,F). The data suggests docetaxel exposure effects lipids within PC-3 cells.

Of the 245 lipids identified in PC-3 cells from the MS2 data, 10 were determined to be significant toward the MDA (Fig. 4A). PC lipids accounted for most of these lipids. However, there were a few exceptions, including hexosylceramide (HexCer), ether-linked phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and ether-linked PC. The most significant lipids showed varying relationships between sample groups, where increases in the normalized peak intensity for PC(O-38:4) and PC(34:4) were exhibited (Fig. 4B,C) and the opposite occurred for PC(40:7) with an increase in docetaxel concentration (Fig. 4D). Pathway analysis demonstrated that lipids aligning with GP and SP metabolism, and SP signaling were enriched in each docetaxel treatment group (Fig. 4E). These lipids were found to be associated with biophysical, chemical, and cell biological features (Fig. 4F). The more significant terms included glycerophosphocholines, headgroup with positive charge, neutral intrinsic curvature, the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and GPs (Fig. 4F).

Lipid variations between models

Our laboratory previously identified that the lipidome differs between PCa cell lines with and without docetaxel resistance36; however, to our knowledge, this has not been investigated in human patients. This lack of data was initially addressed by performing qualitative lipidomic analysis comparing the lipids present in the in vitro and human blood samples from PCa patients. Using similar techniques, 245 lipid species were identified in in vitro samples and 221 in PCa patient blood. Some lipid species were too low in abundance to be measured across models. The identified lipid species included: GPs, glycerolipids (GLs), SPs, fatty acyls (FAs), and sterol lipids (SLs; Fig. 5). PC-3 cells treated with either DMSO or docetaxel contained a higher quantity of GPs (158 vs. 84) and SPs (63 vs. 33) and a lower quantity of GLs (15 vs. 71) and FAs (5 vs. 23) than the blood of PCa patients before and regardless of docetaxel treatment, whereas a more similar number of SLs was found (4 vs. 10). Of the GPs, PC was the most abundant in both sample sets, and PE was the second most abundant, but of the PEs identified in the PCa patient blood only consisted of ether-linked PE species. These data suggest that while similar lipids are present in PC-3 cells and human blood exposed to docetaxel, differences exist in the abundance of these lipids. Although the similarities are of interest, differences were expected as the in vitro model identified intracellular lipids while the clinical model identified circulating lipids.

Demographics of subjects

The entire study included 10 male subjects all diagnosed with PCa. Among the patients, 8 (80%) were Caucasian (“white”), 1 (10%) was African American (“black”), and 1 (10%) was Hispanic. Nearly one-third of patients had castration-sensitive PCa, CRPC, or de novo metastatic PCa. Only 1 patient had local PCa while the remaining patients had metastatic sites in the bone, with only some in the lungs or lymph nodes. All patients were receiving concurrent treatments, including hormone therapy, androgen inhibitors, and a receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa beta ligand (RANKL) inhibitor. Clinical information on other conditions that might influence lipid metabolism, such as hyperlipidemia, diabetes, other malignancies and medications, was available. The average age at diagnosis was 69.8± 6.7 years old, and the average age at time of initial sample collection was 72.2 ± 7.9 years old. The average duration from pre-treatment to mid-treatment was 1.5 ± 0.6 months whereas the duration from pre-treatment to post-treatment was 6.2 ± 1.5 months. Gleason score information was available for 9 out of 10 patients: 5 patients had Gleason score within 5–7, 6 patients had Gleason score within 8–10, with 2 patients having overlapping scores within both ranges. After docetaxel treatment, 3 patients had no response and were considered resistant. The other 7 were sensitive to docetaxel treatment. Overall after treatment, 6 patients had stable disease, 2 were in remission, and 2 had recurrence of the disease. The generalized patient information is shown in Table 1.

Pathway analysis demonstrated that lipids aligning with SP metabolism and signaling were abundant in the docetaxel treatment groups (Fig. 7E). Docetaxel resistance in PCa patients

Of the 10 patients involved in this pilot study, 3 were determined to not have responded over time according to PSA values and deemed resistant to the chemotherapeutic. To determine if there is a correlation between the lipids of these patients and docetaxel outcome, the three time point samples per patient were pooled together and patient samples were compared according to docetaxel outcomes. PLS-DA plots showed clear separation between sensitive and resistant patients, regardless of docetaxel treatment (Fig. 8A,C). Volcano plot analysis identified several significant lipid features, including 143 features in the ESI + mode and 158 features in the ESI- mode (Fig. 8B,D). MS2 analysis of the sample groups showed correlations between docetaxel outcome and the intensity of N-acyl ethanolamine (NAE), PC-O, lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), sterol ester (SE), diacylglycerol (DG) and PC species (Fig. 8E). Those lipid species with a positive correlation to docetaxel resistance, as determined by both univariate and MDA analyses, included LPC(18:0) and DG(40:4). Pathway analysis showed correlations between docetaxel outcome and lipids aligning with SP and GP metabolism and SP signaling (data not shown). However, no ontology terms were significant.

PSA values are typically used to assess treatment outcome in PCa patients. On average, the PSA levels of the patients assessed in this study decreased during and after docetaxel treatment (Table 2; Fig. 9A). However, there clearly was no improvement for 2 of the patients deemed resistant, based on their PSA levels, and the third patient displayed an increased PSA value six months after their post-treatment sample (Fig. 9B). Multivariate analysis comparing docetaxel treatment outcome as the primary metadata of interest (resistant or sensitive) to PSA and each time point (pre-, mid- and post-treatment) suggested 14 lipid species whose intensities correlated to docetaxel resistance (Table 3; Fig. 9C,D,F). Two other types of analyses were performed on the docetaxel outcome groups, one-factor and multi-factor MDA analysis, to determine lipid species that have an MDA > 0.002. Of those lipids identified from the MDA analyses, 7 were similar with the multivariate analysis (Figs. 8E and 9C). These included NAE(26:5), PC(O-37:0), LPC(18:0), DG(40:4), PC(27:1), SE(29:1/18:2), and PC(O-54:8). It should be noted that the intensity of PC(O-54:8) also correlated to docetaxel resistance when only assessing across time points (Fig. 7A). Of the 14 lipids from the multi-factor analysis, there were 7 not accounted for in either of these MDA comparisons (SM(d34:1), Cer(d18:0/18:0), SM(d38:4), PC(32:1), Cer(d18:2/22:0), TG(16:0/18:0/d18:3), and DG(43:6)). These seven lipid species may account for correlations between PSA and docetaxel outcome, as PSA was not accounted for with the MDA analyses.

Quantified lipids of whole blood samples from prostate cancer patients

Through the untargeted lipidomic approach, the most predominant lipid classes found to differentiate between blood PCa sample groups were PC and Cer. To determine the clinical relevance of these findings, the concentrations of PC, Cer, and HexCer lipids in each of the patient blood samples were measured. These concentrations showed that on average, the PCa patients who did respond to docetaxel treatment (sensitive) had higher levels of PC (Fig. 10), Cer and HexCer (Fig. 11) than those who did not respond to treatment (resistant), regardless of the three time points. The concentration of the specific lipids did fluctuate during and after docetaxel treatment started as compared to before. For patients sensitive to docetaxel treatment, the concentration of PCs and Cers generally decreased with docetaxel treatment, as compared to their pre-treatment samples.

Discussion

PCa patients undergoing docetaxel treatment often experience resistance over time, or sometimes even no response. As a result, clinical markers are needed to predict patient outcomes to treatment. Cancer cells depend upon lipids for their survival and alterations in lipid metabolism is often considered a hallmark of cancer cells. This study provides evidence that docetaxel treatment alters the lipidome of PCa cells as well as those in PCa patient blood. Furthermore, this study identified several of these lipid species. Lastly, this study is the first to suggest that lipidomic profiles are distinct between patients who are docetaxel sensitive and those who are non-responsive or resistant to docetaxel.

It’s important to note that this study is not the first to suggest that lipids could be used as biomarkers for PCa. A study examining the lipidome of urinary exosomes from healthy controls and PCa patients showed distinctions between these groups using a combination of three lipid species, two phosphatidylserines (PS(18:1/18:1) and PS(18:0/18:2)) and lactosylceramide (d18:1/16:0)10. As this study demonstrated, biomarker tests for cancer often involves multiple individual markers otherwise considered “weak” (AUC < 0.7) can be combined into a single multivariate model to strengthen the discrimination and confidence25. It is worth noting that similar GP species were identified in the study and our own data.

Identification of GPs in this study correlated to another study that identified five lipids from plasma as potential biomarkers for detecting PCa from benign prostatic hyperplasia14. These lipids consisted mainly of PEs and PCs. A previous study from our laboratory showed increases in PC and LPC species in PC-3 cells, as compared to normal prostate cells36. These lipid species have been shown to be elevated in PCa with docetaxel exposure in our study as well as in ovarian cancer patients’ plasma37.

Results from our study demonstrated several connections across varying analyses of the in vitro and clinical samples. Differences were seen in the MS2 identified lipid compositions (Fig. 5) as well as between significantly important lipids separating the control groups from treatment groups. Some of these differences could be attributed to the lipid environments, intracellular or circulating whole blood, for the two models. Despite these differences, pathway analysis findings were similar between the in vitro and clinical samples. As the in vitro portion suggests, glycerophosphocholines, headgroups with positive charge, neutral intrinsic curvature, and GPs accounted for many lipids shown to differentiate between groups. PCs and PEs are the most abundant GPs within cell membranes and contain a positive charge on their headgroups. A strong increase in neutral intrinsic curvature could be explained with the increase in ether-linked PEs (Fig. 4A,B,F) due to its wedge-like shape when in membranes38. As several studies have demonstrated, even subtle changes in the lipidome of PCa effects several aspects of cellular mechanisms, including membrane fluidity and curvature, signaling pathways, and even chemotherapeutic responses.

While this study suggests that lipid alterations occur after docetaxel treatment in PCa, it is necessary to mention several limitations. Both of the in vitro and clinical models indicated that ether-linked PCs, Cers, DGs, SMs, LPC and HexCer align with docetaxel resistance in PCa and could be used as complements to PSA. However, further validation of the identified lipid species is necessary, such as through quantification. Future studies are focusing on such validations.

Another limitation of the current study is that the in vitro portion used an acute paradigm that lacked an intermittent regimen with docetaxel treatment. Although this intermittent model is commonly accepted with the development of docetaxel resistance, it is time consuming, taking from 6 months to a year to develop resistance32,33. While the acute regimen used in this study allowed a quick turnaround in preparation for the clinical pilot study, an intermittent regimen may better reflect the clinical situation. Additionally, the use of one cell line is less than ideal. PC-3 cells were used as they were isolated from a bone metastasis of a grade IV prostatic adenocarcinoma from a 62-year-old white male. This aligned with the fact that majority of the patients in this pilot study were white males having metastatic PCa in the bone. Additionally, previous studies from our laboratory screened several different prostate cell lines, including PC-3, and identified similar lipid species and classes36. However, this does demonstrate that the clinical study suffers not only in size, but also from a lack of diverse patients.

There are additional limitations with the clinical aspect of this study. The clinical model was intended to be a pilot study; thus, the number of patients was too small for any major conclusions to be made. Additionally, the cohort of patients was very heterogeneous, which challenged the ability to compare the setting of the patients. Many patients had hyperlipidemia or high cholesterol (S. Table 1), which may impact lipid alterations due to docetaxel treatment or general baseline levels. All patients had concurrent treatments with docetaxel (Table 2, S. Table 1), which can impact PSA values. This set another challenge with depending on PSA values to determine docetaxel response and supports the need to have other measurements, like tracking personal lipid alterations, to determine a patient’s response to docetaxel.

For trial feasibility, blood samples were drawn from patients before starting, during and after ending docetaxel treatment. These were surrogate endpoints to determine whether lipid changes occur with treatment and then also upon resistance to treatment. Ideally, clinical endpoints should involve at least two additional blood samples drawn during treatment39. Patients from this pilot study had a minimum of six treatments, so this should be more than feasible. Additionally, there exists some uncertainty in the predictive accuracy of the potential biomarkers presented due to the size of this study. Clinical biomarkers cannot be determined solely from these results as there weren’t enough patients for a strong power assessment. There is a sample stability risk over time when samples are frozen20. A better practice would be to analyze the whole blood fresh without being frozen; however, this is not always practical. Additionally, there is the matter of the imbalance of population groups in respect to docetaxel outcome. Since only 3 of the 10 patients were determined as resistant to docetaxel, a larger cross-sectional study is needed to assess this imbalance in target populations. Lastly, while lipid alterations are reported to occur upon docetaxel treatment, it is unclear whether this is a result of alterations to or from lipid metabolism pathways or other docetaxel effects. Docetaxel has been associated with altering the lipidome of PCa36. However, the mechanisms linking docetaxel resistance to lipid alterations in PCa are still unclear.

A preliminary power analysis was performed to determine the sample size needed to expand these findings to a larger clinical study. A power analysis of the docetaxel response groups suggested that at least 165 patients would be required for each treatment arm to achieve 80% power with an FDR < 0.05. Thus, a future clinical study with more patients could assess the conclusions made in this study, including verification of the lipids quantified.

Conclusions

In summary, we applied lipidomics to identify perturbations in lipid species in PC-3 cells and clinical patients undergoing docetaxel treatment. The results indicate several lipid changes associated with important biological processes, such as SP signaling and metabolism, and GP metabolism. Moreover, this is the first study to identify lipidomic changes with the development of docetaxel resistance in PCa patients. This study therefore progresses beyond previous laboratory cell line-based approaches to demonstrate that docetaxel treatment alters lipid metabolism. There are only a few studies conducted thus far that have identified lipids as biomarkers for PCa10,13,14,40 and even fewer for docetaxel resistance36. These results indicate several lipid species that show potential as biomarkers for the progression toward docetaxel resistance in PCa. However, further analysis is necessary to determine concentration thresholds for said lipids in a larger cohort.
