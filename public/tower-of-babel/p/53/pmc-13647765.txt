Temporal changes in clinical practice and mortality in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage following the 2012 AHA/ASA guidelines: a retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC database
Authors: John H. Kanter, Akshay I. Kelshiker, Pablo Martinez-Camblor, Adam C. Glaser, Lauren Buhl, Christopher S. Ogilvy, Myles D. Boone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/objectiveAneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) carries high morbidity and mortality. The 2012 AHA/ASA aSAH management guidelines introduced recommendations for standardized monitoring, vasospasm prevention with nimodipine, blood pressure control, early aneurysm repair, and selective use of antiseizure medication (ASM) in the immediate post-hemorrhagic period. The overall impact of the guideline period on patient outcomes in real-world ICU practice remains incompletely characterized.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults admitted to the ICU with aSAH between 2001 and 2019 using MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV, linked de-identified electronic health record databases from a single academic center. Patients were grouped into Period 1 (pre-guideline, 2001–2011) and Period 2 (post-guideline, 2012–2019). Outcomes included mortality at 1, 6, and 12 months; ASM use; seizure occurrence; and ICU/hospital length of stay (LOS). Logistic regression was used for binary outcomes and quantile regression for median LOS, with unadjusted, demographic-adjusted, and fully saturated models. Sensitivity analyses stratified by Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Hunt and Hess (HH), and modified Fisher grade (mFG) were performed.ResultsA total of 516 patients (279 pre-guideline; 237 post-guideline) were included. ASM use increased from 63.3% to 92.0% post-guideline. In fully saturated models, mortality was significantly lower in the post-guideline cohort at 1 month (OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.04–0.67), 6 months (OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.05–0.64), and 1 year (OR 0.16, 95% CI 0.04–0.61). Seizure occurrence was numerically higher in Period 2 in unadjusted analyses but this difference was not significant in the saturated model. LOS effects were mixed. The largest mortality reductions occurred in patients with low GCS, high HH, and high mFG scores.ConclusionsIn the period following publication of the 2012 AHA/ASA aSAH guidelines, ICU mortality decreased significantly and ASM use increased substantially at a single academic center. These temporal associations are consistent with guideline-driven practice change, but cannot be attributed exclusively to guideline implementation given the observational pre–post design and the many concurrent advances in neurocritical care. The mortality reduction was most pronounced in patients with high-severity aSAH. These findings highlight the value of systematic outcomes monitoring following major guideline updates and identify high-severity aSAH as a priority population for future prospective evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05175-y.

Introduction

Spontaneous aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) accounts for approximately 5% of all strokes, with a global incidence of 6.1 cases per 100,000 people per year [1]. aSAH is associated with high morbidity, mortality, and long-term healthcare costs, particularly because patients are typically younger, leading to a significant loss of productive life years [2]. The pathophysiology of aSAH is complex, involving factors such as seizures [3], delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) and vasospasm [4], and hydrocephalus [5], each independently linked to poor functional and cognitive outcomes [6].

In 2012, the American Heart Association and American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) published updated guidelines for the management of aSAH [7]. Key recommendations included standardized monitoring, vasospasm surveillance and prevention with nimodipine, blood pressure targets, early aneurysm repair, and selective use of ASM in the immediate post-hemorrhagic period (Class IIb, Level of Evidence B). Despite the widespread dissemination of these guidelines, whether their publication was associated with changes in real-world clinical practice and patient outcomes at the population level remains incompletely characterized. Prior studies have largely examined isolated interventions rather than the collective impact of a major guideline update [8–10].

Additionally, the 2023 AHA/ASA aSAH guidelines updated the ASM recommendation, advising against routine prophylactic ASM use except in patients with high-seizure-risk features (ruptured MCA aneurysm, high-grade SAH, intracerebral hematoma, hydrocephalus, or cortical infarction) [11]. The present study therefore also provides a baseline characterization of ASM practice patterns during the 2012 guideline era that may inform evaluation of the transition to the 2023 recommendations.

We conducted a retrospective cohort study comparing mortality, ASM use, seizure occurrence, and length of stay before and after the 2012 guideline publication among adults admitted to the ICU with spontaneous aSAH. We hypothesized that the post-guideline period would be associated with changes in practice patterns and improved outcomes, while recognizing that causal attribution to guideline implementation specifically is not possible from a pre–post observational design.

Methods

The MIMIC (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care) database is a freely accessible, de-identified electronic health record repository derived from patients treated at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC), a quaternary academic medical center in Boston, Massachusetts [12, 13]. MIMIC-III contains records from ICU admissions between 2001 and 2012, while MIMIC-IV extends coverage through 2019, together providing a continuous longitudinal dataset spanning the pre- and post-guideline periods under study. Both versions were accessed via Google BigQuery, a cloud-based SQL query platform. Access to this corpus was granted to three of the authors (JHK, AIK, and ACG). The database includes detailed information for over 65,000 patients admitted to the ICU, including demographic data, laboratory results, medication history, and clinical notes.

We included adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to the ICU at BIDMC with spontaneous aneurysmal SAH between 2001 and 2019, identified using International Classification of Disease (ICD) codes; ICD-9 codes were used for records predating the 2015 U.S. transition to ICD-10, and ICD-10 codes were used thereafter. Patients were excluded if clinical records were incomplete or if diagnostic criteria for spontaneous aSAH were not clearly met. Patients were divided into Period 1 (pre-guideline, 2001–2011) and Period 2 (post-guideline, 2012–2019). The year 2012 was included in the post-guideline period because MIMIC data are grouped by multi-year anchor periods that prevent unambiguous identification of individual admission years. As the 2012 AHA/ASA guidelines were published in June 2012, patients admitted in early 2012 were exposed to pre-guideline practice but are classified as post-guideline in our analysis. This study was conducted using de-identified data from the publicly available MIMIC databases. Use of the databases was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRB) of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, with a waiver of informed consent granted given the retrospective nature of the study and the de-identified status of all patient data. No patient consent was required. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all applicable institutional and national guidelines and regulations.

Primary exposure

The primary exposure is the calendar period of ICU admission relative to the 2012 AHA/ASA guideline publication date (Period 1: pre-guideline; Period 2: post-guideline). We acknowledge that this exposure represents a time-period comparison and does not directly measure adherence to individual guideline recommendations.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is mortality at one month, six months, and one year.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes include ASM use, seizure occurrence, hospital LOS, and ICU LOS.

Clinical and demographic data were extracted from MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV using SQL via Google BigQuery. The extracted dataset comprised 75 variables capturing patient demographics, discharge location, date of mortality, surgical procedure (coil embolization or surgical clipping), aneurysm location, and clinical indicators including seizures, vasospasm, and comorbidities.

Clinical records were manually reviewed by two authors (JHK and AIK) to confirm aneurysm presence and location and to assign Hunt-and-Hess (HH) [14] and modified Fisher Grade (mFG) [15] scores. Scores were absent from the structured clinical record in 78% (n = 407/516) of cases for HH and 83% (n = 426/516) of cases for mFG. Complete-case analysis was not used as the primary approach for these variables because doing so would have excluded the majority of the cohort from severity-adjusted models and risked introducing selection bias, as missingness was likely related to illness severity and documentation practice rather than occurring at random. Missing values were instead supplemented using Google Gemini, a large language model, which analyzed accompanying clinical notes and assigned scores based on textual descriptions of neurological examination and imaging findings. Each LLM-assigned grade was independently reviewed alongside its supporting rationale by two authors (JHK and AIK), who determined the final score. Approximately 20% of LLM-generated scores required correction during human review, with most corrections involving a change of only one grade.

Additional variables including comorbidities and seizure occurrence were identified through further manual chart review. Mortality at 6 months and 1 year was ascertained from the MIMIC database’s linkage to the Social Security Death Master File. Patients with unknown vital status at follow-up were treated as censored in time-to-event analyses. Final datasets were merged and cleaned in a Google Colab Python environment. SQL scripts and Python code are publicly available (https://github.com/akelshiker/MIMIC-aSAH-25; https://github.com/adamcglaser/MIMIC_SAH_ASM_dataset).

Statistical analyses

We follow the STROBE reporting guidelines. Continuous variables are summarized as mean (SD) or median (IQR); categorical variables as frequencies and proportions. Welch’s t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, or Chi-squared tests were used for between-group comparisons. Variables with more than 5% missing data were excluded from complete-case analyses.

Three models were constructed for each outcome: (1) unadjusted; (2) demographics-adjusted (age, sex, race); and (3) fully saturated, adjusting for age, sex, race, surgical treatment (clip/coil), GCS, aneurysm location (anterior communicating [AComm] or posterior communicating [PComm] artery), comfort-measures-only (CMO) status, HH grade, mFG, rebleeding, vasopressor use, renal replacement therapy (RRT), mechanical ventilation, and Charlson comorbidity index. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported for binary outcomes; beta regression coefficients with 95% CIs from quantile regression are reported for median LOS. All analyses were performed using R 4.4.1 (www.r-project.org). Two-sided p-values are reported; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) [16] was dichotomized into 3–13 (lower) and 14–15 (higher) based on the a priori clinical distinction between obtunded/comatose and alert/oriented presentations, corresponding to established thresholds used in prior aSAH severity stratification literature [17]. This categorization was pre-specified before analysis.

Results

A total of 516 patients admitted to the ICU with aSAH were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics for Period 1 (pre-guideline, n = 279) and Period 2 (post-guideline, n = 237) are presented in Table 1. The cohort was predominantly female (65.9%) and White (61.8%). Most patients presented with anterior circulation aneurysms (80.0%) and were treated with coil embolization (72.2%). Age, sex, race, WFNS grade, minimum GCS, and rates of coil and clip treatment were similar between periods (all p > 0.05).

There were notable differences in aSAH severity between periods. Based on HH classification: 34.9% of patients in Period 1 had HH grade II versus 21.8% in Period 2 (p = 0.002), while 16.7% in Period 2 had HH grade IV versus 8.3% in Period 1 (p = 0.006), indicating a shift toward greater illness severity in the post-guideline cohort. This severity shift is important context for interpreting the primary outcome: the post-guideline group achieved better survival outcomes despite, on average, more severe presentations at baseline.

In total, 394 patients (76.4%) received ASMs. ASM use increased significantly between periods (63.3% in Period 1 vs. 92.0% in Period 2; p < 0.0001). Seizure occurrence was numerically higher in Period 2 than Period 1 in unadjusted analyses (Table 2). While this may appear counterintuitive, it may reflect ascertainment bias — increased monitoring intensity and EEG surveillance in the post-guideline period may have identified seizures that were not detected pre-guideline — or it may reflect survivor bias in a cohort with higher proportions of severely ill patients who survived longer and were therefore at greater risk for seizure.

Table 2 presents regression model results. All three model specifications showed statistically significant increases in ASM use and reductions in 1-month, 6-month, and 1-year mortality in the post-guideline period. In the fully saturated model, post-guideline mortality was lower at 1 month (OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.04–0.67; p = 0.012), 6 months (OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.05–0.64; p = 0.009), and 1 year (OR 0.16, 95% CI 0.04–0.61; p = 0.007). Absolute mortality counts by period and time point are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Seizure reduction was significant in unadjusted and demographics-adjusted models but not in the saturated model (OR 2.05, 95% CI 0.92–4.55; p = 0.078). LOS effects were mixed and did not reach consistent statistical significance. As a pre-specified sensitivity analysis to assess model stability given the high rates of missing HH and mFG data, we fitted a reduced saturated model excluding these two variables but retaining all other covariates. Mortality ORs in this reduced model were approximately 0.19 (95% CI 0.05–0.71) at 1 month, 0.19 (95% CI 0.05–0.70) at 6 months, and 0.18 (95% CI 0.05–0.68) at 1 year — essentially unchanged from the primary estimates — indicating that the wide confidence intervals in the fully saturated model reflect genuine uncertainty from sparse events rather than instability introduced by LLM-imputed covariates.

In the sensitivity analyses (Fig. 1), associations between time period and ASM use, seizure occurrence, and mortality were generally consistent with primary results. Mortality reductions were most pronounced in patients with low GCS (3–13), high HH grade (III–V), and high mFG (III–IV). Among patients with mFG grades I–II, effect estimates were imprecise due to small event counts; among patients with HH grades I–II, there were insufficient events to generate stable estimates. For LOS, the post-guideline period was associated with shorter stays in less severely ill patients and longer stays in the most severely ill patients, consistent with improved survival and longer recovery time in that group.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 516 ICU patients with aSAH at a single academic center, we observed three principal findings. First, the post-guideline period (2012–2019) was associated with a significant reduction in mortality at 1, 6, and 12 months, with the largest effects seen in patients with severe aSAH. Second, ASM use nearly doubled between periods, rising from 63% to 92%. Third, neither LOS nor seizure occurrence showed consistent directional changes across adjusted models. Taken together, these results characterize a period of meaningful temporal improvement in aSAH survival that coincided with the dissemination of the 2012 AHA/ASA management guidelines.

The magnitude of the mortality reduction observed in our study — an 83–84% lower odds of death at all time points in the fully adjusted model — is striking and warrants contextual interpretation. Population-level improvements in aSAH outcomes have been reported in multiple prior studies examining temporal trends over the past two decades. A systematic review by Etminan et al. documented a global decline in aSAH case fatality rates from approximately 50% in the 1980s to below 35% by the 2010s, attributed to improved neurocritical care and endovascular technique [1]. Our findings are broadly consistent with this trajectory.

The mortality benefit in our cohort persisted after adjustment for illness severity, comorbidity burden, surgical treatment, and mechanical ventilation, lending some robustness to the association. The severity-shift between periods — with the post-guideline cohort containing a higher proportion of HH grade IV patients — further suggests that the observed survival improvement is not an artifact of treating a milder case-mix after 2012. A pre–post design, however, cannot isolate the contribution of the 2012 guidelines from the many concurrent developments in aSAH and general critical care that occurred over this period. These include advances in endovascular technique, the development of dedicated neurocritical care teams, transitions in vasospasm management from triple-H therapy to euvolemia, more judicious approaches to withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST) in severe aSAH [18], and general ICU bundle improvements in prevention of ventilator-associated pneumonia, VTE, and glycemic dysregulation. The relative contribution of each of these factors to the observed mortality reduction cannot be determined from this analysis, and our findings should be interpreted as characterizing temporal trends during the guideline era rather than as causal evidence of guideline effectiveness.

WLST deserves specific attention as a potential confound. Early WLST in high-grade aSAH is a major determinant of in-hospital mortality and has historically accounted for a substantial proportion of deaths in observational datasets [19]. If WLST was more prevalent in Period 1 — reflecting a period of greater prognostic nihilism in severe aSAH — this alone could explain a portion of the observed mortality difference independent of any change in active management. While CMO status was included as a covariate in our saturated model, granular WLST decision data were unavailable, and this represents an important residual confound.

The near-doubling of ASM use between periods — from 63% to 92% — represents a substantial shift in clinical practice. Whether this reflects guideline-driven behavior change or other institutional factors cannot be determined from our data; the 2012 guidelines assigned prophylactic ASM a Class IIb recommendation with Level B evidence, meaning it was a permissive rather than directive recommendation. Changes in provider staffing, local protocol evolution, or academic influence from the concurrent literature may have independently contributed.

The evidence base for prophylactic ASM in aSAH has been mixed. A 2025 analysis by Kanter et al. found that early ASM administration was associated with lower mortality and seizure rates in spontaneous aSAH [10], and a prior study in traumatic brain injury found ASM associated with reduced mortality, though not with reduced seizure rates [19]. Conversely, a meta-analysis examining ASM in acute intracerebral hemorrhage found no association with improved outcomes [20], and a single-institution study by Smith et al. found no improvement in seizure rates or patient outcomes after prophylactic ASM use in aSAH [8, 9]. This heterogeneity in the evidence likely reflects differences in patient populations, ASM agents used, timing of administration, and outcome ascertainment across studies.

Importantly, the 2023 AHA/ASA aSAH guidelines revised the ASM recommendation, moving away from routine prophylaxis and recommending ASM only in patients with specific high-seizure-risk features: ruptured MCA aneurysm, high-grade SAH, intracerebral hematoma, hydrocephalus, or cortical infarction [11]. The 92% ASM use observed in Period 2 of our cohort substantially exceeds what would be appropriate under current 2023 guidance for many patients. This finding provides a historical benchmark of pre-2023 practice and underscores the importance of prospective quality improvement studies to evaluate how ASM prescribing patterns are evolving in response to the updated recommendations. Unnecessary ASM exposure carries real costs — including cognitive side effects, drug interactions, and systemic toxicity — making de-implementation an important clinical target [21].

The observation that seizure occurrence was numerically higher in Period 2 — and significantly so in unadjusted and demographics-adjusted models — is counterintuitive given the increase in ASM use. This apparent paradox likely reflects several interacting factors. Increased monitoring intensity in the post-guideline period, including more systematic EEG surveillance, may have increased ascertainment of seizures — particularly nonconvulsive events — that would previously have gone undetected. Survival bias may also be operative: the post-guideline cohort included higher proportions of severely ill patients who survived long enough to be at risk for in-hospital seizure. That the seizure effect was attenuated to non-significance in the fully saturated model — which adjusts for illness severity — is consistent with this interpretation. The MIMIC database does not include continuous EEG monitoring data or detailed seizure characterization, so a definitive explanation is not possible from this dataset.

A clinically important finding of this study is the severity-dependent pattern of mortality benefit. The largest reductions in mortality were observed in patients with low GCS (3–13), high HH grade (III–V), and high mFG (III–IV). For patients with mild aSAH, who already face a lower baseline risk of death, the potential for measurable survival benefit from any guideline-level change is inherently constrained — a floor effect that is expected and does not diminish the significance of the finding in severe cases.

This pattern has two implications. First, it supports the clinical priority of aggressive, guideline-concordant management in high-grade aSAH, where the evidence for benefit is most compelling. Second, it suggests that quality improvement efforts should be particularly focused on ensuring high-severity patients receive the full constellation of recommended interventions — early aneurysm securement, nimodipine, vasospasm surveillance, and appropriate critical care monitoring — rather than applying a single intervention such as ASM uniformly across all grades.

Mortality, while an important endpoint, is an incomplete picture of aSAH outcomes. Functional recovery, cognitive outcomes, and DCI rates are arguably more relevant to patients and families, yet these data were not available as structured variables in the MIMIC database. Functional outcome measures such as the modified Rankin Scale are not systematically captured in MIMIC, and DCI — a major driver of delayed morbidity in aSAH — could not be reliably ascertained from administrative codes alone. Similarly, rebleeding was captured as a covariate but not analyzed as a primary outcome. These gaps are an inherent limitation of database research and restrict the scope of conclusions that can be drawn about the full clinical impact of the guideline period. This is a particularly important gap in the clinical context of aSAH, where survival with severe neurological disability may represent an outcome that patients’ families and clinicians weigh as heavily as death itself. The inability to distinguish meaningful functional recovery from survival with significant disability limits the clinical applicability of our mortality findings and represents a priority for future work. Prospective registries such as Get With The Guidelines–Stroke, which report discharge functional status and longer-term outcomes, would be better positioned to characterize functional recovery in the post-2012 and post-2023 eras.

Strengths

This study has several notable strengths. The linkage of MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV provides one of the larger single-center aSAH cohorts available for a pre–post analysis spanning nearly two decades of ICU admissions. The availability of long-term mortality data at 1, 6, and 12 months allows assessment of survival benefit well beyond the index hospitalization, which is relatively uncommon in database studies. The application of three regression model specifications — unadjusted, demographics-adjusted, and fully saturated — provides transparency about how effect estimates change with progressive confounder adjustment. The stratified sensitivity analyses by GCS, HH, and mFG offer clinically meaningful granularity about which patient populations showed the greatest temporal changes in outcomes, and the publicly available analysis code supports reproducibility.

Limitations

Several limitations require acknowledgment. The most significant is the pre–post observational design: our findings represent temporal associations and cannot establish that the 2012 guidelines caused the observed improvements. The many concurrent advances in neurocritical care and neurosurgical practice described above represent plausible alternative explanations, and we did not have access to documentation of adherence to specific guideline components. The MIMIC database assigns patient records to multi-year anchor periods, and individual admission years cannot be unambiguously identified within those periods. As a result, patients admitted in early 2012 — prior to the June 2012 publication of the AHA/ASA guidelines — are misclassified as post-guideline. This misclassification introduces bias toward the null: our estimates of the mortality difference between periods are likely conservative, and the true post-guideline mortality reduction may be larger than reported.

This is a single-center study at a quaternary academic referral center, limiting generalizability to community hospitals and settings with differing neurocritical care resources. WLST practice differences between periods represent an important residual confounder that cannot be fully accounted for with available data. Finally, HH and mFG scores were supplemented using Google Gemini applied to clinical notes, an approach not independently validated for aSAH severity classification. Approximately 20% of LLM-generated scores required correction during human review, with most corrections involving a change of only one grade.

Conclusion

In a retrospective cohort study of ICU patients with aSAH treated at a single academic center between 2001 and 2019, the period following publication of the 2012 AHA/ASA aSAH guidelines was associated with significant reductions in mortality at 1, 6, and 12 months and with a marked increase in ASM use. These temporal changes were most pronounced in patients with high-severity aSAH. We cannot conclude from a pre–post observational design that the guidelines caused these improvements; concurrent advances in neurocritical care and neurosurgical technique represent plausible alternative explanations. Nevertheless, these findings are consistent with the hypothesis that the 2012 guideline era coincided with meaningful improvements in aSAH outcomes, and they provide a historical baseline for evaluating the ongoing transition to the 2023 AHA/ASA recommendations. The near-universal ASM use observed in the post-2012 cohort substantially exceeds current 2023 guidance, highlighting de-implementation of routine prophylaxis as an important quality improvement target. Prospective, multicenter studies with explicit measures of guideline adherence are needed to establish whether and which specific guideline components drive survival benefit in aSAH.
