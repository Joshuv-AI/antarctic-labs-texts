Association of lymph node ratio with adjuvant radiotherapy benefit in human papillomavirus-positive oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma: a population-based analysis
Authors: Zhen-Zhen Lu, Xin-Jia Chen, Zi-Qing You, Yi-Min Li, San-Gang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo analyze the prognostic value of lymph node ratio (LNR, number of metastatic nodes divided by total nodes excised) and to determine whether LNR can guide treatment decisions in human papillomavirus (HPV)-related oropharyngeal carcinoma (OPC).MethodsWe retrospectively included HPV-related OPC who underwent surgery and lymph node dissection between 2010 and 2020 using the data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results program. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, Kaplan-Meier analysis, and Multivariate Cox regression models were used for statistical analyses.ResultsA total of 2922 patients were included. The ROC curve analysis showed that LNR has significantly better efficacy than pathological nodal (pN) staging in predicting overall survival (OS) in patients without adjuvant radiotherapy (area under the curve 0.726 vs. 0.681, P = 0.047). Using OS as the endpoint, the optimal cutoff values for predicting OS were determined by ROC curve analysis to be 4.0% for LNR. Multivariate analysis confirmed LNR ≥ 4.0% as an independent prognostic factor for worse cancer-specific survival (CSS) and OS in the entire cohort and in those without adjuvant radiotherapy. In the subgroup with LNR < 4.0%, there was no significant difference in CSS (P = 0.788) and OS (P = 0.327) between patients who received radiotherapy and those who did not. However, in the group with LNR ≥ 4.0%, radiotherapy resulted in better 3-year CSS (95.2% vs. 85.9%, P < 0.001) and OS (93.9% vs. 82.7%, P < 0.001) than those who did not. LNR-based stratification could potentially spare 24.4% of patients from unnecessary adjuvant radiotherapy.ConclusionsLNR is a significant prognostic factor in HPV-related OPC and can help refine risk stratification and treatment decision-making. Introduction

Oropharyngeal cancer (OPC) is one of the common malignant tumors in the head and neck. In recent years, the incidence of OPC has been gradually increasing. In 2020, there were approximately 98,412 newly diagnosed cases of OPC and 48,143 deaths globally [1]. The etiology of OPC is multifactorial. Smoking and alcohol consumption have long been recognized as traditional risk factors. In addition to these well-established factors, human papillomavirus (HPV) infection has emerged as a crucial player in the development of OPC. Increasing evidence from epidemiological studies across different geographical regions has indicated a remarkable rise in the incidence of HPV-positive OPC in recent years [2]. Clinically, the primary tumors are relatively small, but the lymph node stage is often advanced, suggesting early lymphatic metastasis. However, compared with HPV-negative OPC, patients with HPV-positive OPC usually have a better prognosis [3–5]. This may be related to the changes in the biological behavior of tumor cells caused by HPV infection, such as increased sensitivity of tumor cells to radiotherapy and chemotherapy [6]. The 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system made a significant step forward by establishing different staging systems based on HPV status [7]. It also incorporated the number of lymph node metastases into the nodal staging, which reflects the importance of lymph node involvement in OPC prognosis. However, the current treatment guidelines have not fully caught up with these new staging concepts.

Despite the distinct biological behavior and favorable prognosis of HPV-positive OPC, the optimal treatment strategy remains a subject of ongoing debate. While primary radiotherapy (with or without chemotherapy) is widely accepted as the standard of care for locally advanced disease, surgical resection followed by risk-adapted adjuvant therapy has gained traction, particularly in the context of transoral robotic surgery [8]. Following surgery, the decision to administer adjuvant radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy is traditionally guided by pathological features such as positive margins, extracapsular extension, lymph node involvement, and tumor stage. However, the threshold for adjuvant treatment in HPV-positive OPC remains controversial. Recent data, including those from the MC1675 and ECOG-ACRIN 3311 trials, suggest that de-intensification of adjuvant therapy, such as omitting chemotherapy or reducing radiation dose, may not compromise oncologic outcomes in selected HPV-positive patients with favorable pathological features [9, 10]. Conversely, the identification of patients at high risk of recurrence who would still benefit from standard or intensified adjuvant therapy remains imprecise. This underscores the urgent need for more refined prognostic tools to guide postoperative treatment decisions in HPV-positive OPC.

The lymph node ratio (LNR), defined as the ratio of the number of metastatic lymph nodes to the total number of lymph nodes removed during surgery, has been extensively investigated in various cancers. In oral cancer, LNR has been shown to be a reliable prognostic indicator, with higher LNR values associated with poorer survival rates [11]. Similar findings have been reported in vulvar cancer [12], cervical cancer [13], and breast cancer [14]. The rationale behind the prognostic significance of LNR is that it takes into account both the presence of metastatic lymph nodes and the adequacy of lymph node dissection. However, the application of LNR in OPC, especially in HPV-related OPC, has yielded inconsistent results [15, 16]. This may be related to the small sample size, differences in study design, differences in HPV status assessment, and different selections of LNR cut-off values. Therefore, further exploration of the prognostic role of LNR in HPV-related OPC using a large cohort study has important clinical significance. In light of this, this study aimed to analyze the role of LNR in prognostic assessment and treatment decision-making in HPV- related OPC.

Materials and methods

Patients

We retrospectively included OPC patients who were diagnosed between 2010 and 2020 using the data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program. Our data were derived from two sources: (1) the 2010–2018 SEER Research Plus database (with specialized head-and-neck fields), which records HPV status as HPV-positive, HPV-negative, or unknown [17]; and (2) the 2000–2022 SEER Research Data, in which HPV status is classified as p16-positive, p16-negative, or unknown [18]. The SEER database is a project of the National Cancer Institute in the United States. Patients included in this study had to meet the following criteria: (1) diagnosed with HPV-positive oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC); (2) available data on tumor (T) stage and nodal (N) stage; (3) having undergone surgery and lymph node dissection; (4) available data on the number of removed lymph nodes and positive lymph nodes. Patients diagnosed with second malignancies and those lacking treatment data were excluded. Those with de novo metastatic stage disease were also excluded. To ensure the accuracy of the data, the SEER data quality team reviewed this HPV dataset [19]. Since this study used the publicly available SEER database, approval from the institutional review board was waived.

Data collection

The following variables were included for this study: age at diagnosis, gender, race, tumor grade, T stage, N stage, the number of removed lymph nodes, the number of metastatic lymph nodes (NMLN), and postoperative adjuvant treatment strategies. For patient selection, cases involving the soft palate, base of the tongue, pharyngeal tonsils, and oropharynx (unspecified) were included. For cancer staging classification, the 8th edition system of the AJCC staging was used for pathological TNM staging (pTNM). The LNR was defined as the number of NMLN divided by the number of lymph nodes dissected. The primary endpoints of this study were cancer-specific survival (CSS) and overall survival (OS). Specifically, CSS was defined as the time from the initial diagnosis of OPSCC to death due to OPSCC. OS was defined as the time from the initial diagnosis of OPSCC to death from all causes.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient clinical characteristics. The cutoff values for the LNR were calculated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The predictive capabilities of LNR and NMLN for prognosis were compared with the AJCC 8th edition pN stage. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to plot survival curves, and the log-rank test was used to compare the differences in survival outcomes between different groups. Multivariate Cox regression models were constructed to screen out independent factors significantly associated with CSS and OS. Sensitivity analyses were performed to investigate the effect of adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy on survival outcomes after stratification by different risk stratifications of lymph nodes. To evaluate whether the survival benefit associated with adjuvant radiotherapy varied with increasing LNR, Cox proportional hazards models were fitted, incorporating an interaction term between adjuvant radiotherapy (yes vs. no) and LNR (as a continuous variable). Data analysis was performed using the SPSS statistical software (version 25.0, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and the R program (version 4.4.2) (Vienna, Austria). All tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 2922 eligible patients were included in this study (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). The baseline characteristics of patients are presented in Table 1. Among them, 59.7% (n = 1745) were aged between 50 and 64 years. The majority of the patients were male (85.5%, n = 2499), White (92.8%, n = 2712), with poorly differentiated tumors (44.3%, n = 1294), and T1-2 stage (78.4%, n = 2290). In terms of the pN stage, 13.6% (n = 396) of the patients were at the pN0 stage, 70.9% (n = 2071) at the pN1 stage, and 15.6% (n = 455) at the pN2 stage. Of these patients, 1969 (67.4%), 856 (29.3%), and 97 (3.3%) were staged as I, II, and III diseases, respectively. The median LNR was 0.056 (range, 0–1). Regarding postoperative treatment, 2186 (74.8%) received adjuvant radiotherapy and 1081 (37.0%) received adjuvant chemotherapy.

The predictive ability of different risk stratifications of lymph nodes on overall survival

To mitigate the potential confounding effect of postoperative radiotherapy on survival outcomes, we estimated the optimal cutoff value for the LNR exclusively among patients who did not receive postoperative radiotherapy (n = 736), with the aim of establishing a threshold that can safely guide treatment-decision algorithms for omitting adjuvant radiotherapy. We subsequently compared the prognostic accuracy of LNR as continuous variables, the NMLN as continuous variables, and pN stage for OS by constructing ROC curves and quantifying their discriminative capacity. The AUC values were 0.726 for LNR, 0.715 for NMLN, and 0.681 for pN stage (Fig. 1). While LNR and NMLN demonstrated comparable predictive ability (P = 0.562), LNR exhibited significantly superior predictive performance relative to pN stage (P = 0.047). Similarly, NMLN also significantly outperformed the pN stage (P = 0.044). Collectively, these findings indicate that both LNR and NMLN offer improved prognostic refinement over conventional pN staging, with LNR showing a non-significant trend toward the highest discriminative ability. Using OS as the endpoint, ROC curve analysis identified an LNR of 4.0% as the optimal cutoff value. Based on this threshold, 1109 patients (38.0%) had an LNR < 4.0%, while 1813 patients (62.0%) had an LNR ≥ 4.0%.

Survival and prognostic analysis in the entire cohort

The median follow-up time was 34 months (range, 0-107 months). The 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year CSS were 98.6%, 95.5%, and 93.5%, respectively. The 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS were 98.0%, 93.9%, and 90.5% respectively.

Patients with a higher pN stage had significantly worse CSS (the 3-year CSS for pN0, pN1, and pN2 patients were 99.2%, 96.4%, and 88.2% respectively, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A), and also had a significantly worse OS (the 3-year OS for pN0, pN1, and pN2 patients were 97.7%, 94.7%, and 87.6% respectively, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2B).

Compared with patients with LNR < 4.0%, patients with LNR ≥ 4.0% had significantly worse CSS (the 3-year CSS was 97.9% and 94.1%, respectively, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2C) and significantly worse OS (the 3-year OS was 96.4% and 92.5%, respectively, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2D).

The results of the multivariate analysis showed that an LNR ≥ 4.0% (hazard ratio [HR] 1.838, 95%confidence interval [CI] 1.067–3.167, P = 0.028), a higher pN stage (pN1 vs. pN0: HR 3.786, 95%CI 1.388–10.325, P = 0.009; pN2 vs. pN0: HR 9.878, 95%CI 3.340-29.213, P < 0.001) were associated with worse CSS. Receiving radiotherapy (HR 0.280, 95%CI 0.186–0.421, P < 0.001) was associated with better CSS. Additionally, the pT stage (pT2 vs. pT1: HR 1.831, 95%CI 1.164–2.879, P = 0.009; pT3 vs. pT1: HR 3.060, 95%CI 1.834–5.107, P < 0.001; pT4 vs. pT1: HR 7.790, 95%CI 4.317–14.057, P < 0.001) was also a prognostic factor for CSS (Table 2).

Regarding OS, an LNR ≥ 4.0% (HR 1.633, 95%CI 1.066–2.502, P = 0.024), a higher pN stage (pN1 vs. pN0: HR 2.658, 95%CI 1.344–5.259, P = 0.005; pN2 vs. pN0: HR 5.255, 95%CI 2.428–11.375, P < 0.001) were associated with worse OS. Prognostic analysis in the subgroup without adjuvant radiotherapy

To further assess whether an LNR ≥ 4.0% independently predicts poor prognosis among patients not receiving adjuvant radiotherapy, we conducted a multivariate Cox regression analysis in this subgroup (n = 736). After adjustment for age, gender, race, tumor grade, pT stage, and pN stage, an LNR ≥ 4.0% remained significantly associated with worse CSS (HR 5.188, 95%CI 2.374–11.338, P < 0.001) and worse OS (HR 4.394, 95%CI 2.556–7.553, P < 0.001) (Table 3). These results showed that an LNR exceeding 4.0% serves as an independent predictor of adverse outcomes even in the absence of adjuvant radiotherapy.

In the overall cohort, adjuvant radiotherapy provided significant OS benefits (3-year OS: 94.7% vs. 91.6%, P < 0.001). In the group with LNR < 4.0%, there was no significant difference in 3-year OS between patients who received radiotherapy and those who did not (96.8% vs. 95.9%, P = 0.327) (Fig. 4C). In the group with LNR ≥ 4.0%, adjuvant radiotherapy provided a significant survival advantage over the no adjuvant radiotherapy group (3-year OS: 93.9% vs. 82.7%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4D). Adjuvant radiotherapy showed comparable OS in patients with pN0 stage compared to those who did not (97.5% vs. 97.7%, P = 0.487) (Fig. 5D), while had better OS in patients with pN1stage (95.9% vs. 89.9%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5E) and pN2 stage (89.3% vs. 64.0%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5F).

The cohort included 396, 2071, and 455 patients staged as pN1, pN2, and pN3, respectively. Additionally, 1109 and 1813 patients had an LNR of < 4.0% and ≥ 4.0%, respectively. Among patients stratified by pN staging, 2526 (86.4%) exhibited a survival benefit from adjuvant radiotherapy. In contrast, based on LNR stratification, only 1813 (62.0%) derived such a benefit. Consequently, adopting the LNR-based stratification may allow for the safe omission of adjuvant radiotherapy in an additional 713 patients (24.4%).

In the overall cohort, there was no significant difference in 3-year CSS between patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy and those who did not (94.8% vs. 95.9%, P = 0.293). In both the subgroups with LNR < 4.0% (P = 0.501) and LNR ≥ 4.0% (P = 0.547), there were no significant differences in CSS between patients who received chemotherapy and those who did not. In the pN0 subgroup, chemotherapy showed inferior CSS (P = 0.027), while it did not show significant survival advantages in the pN1 (P = 0.160) and pN2 subgroups (P = 0.706).

In the overall cohort, there was no significant difference in 3-year OS between patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy and those who did not (94.0% vs. 93.9%, P = 0.970). In both the groups with LNR < 4.0% (P = 0.775) and LNR ≥ 4.0% (P = 0.256), there were no significant differences in OS between patients who received chemotherapy and those who did not. The receipt of chemotherapy also did not improve OS regardless of pN stage (pN0, P = 0.501; pN1, P = 0.056; pN2, P = 0.706).

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the prognostic role of the LNR in HPV-related OPC and its potential implications for treatment decision-making. Our findings provide valuable insights into the utility of LNR as a prognostic marker and its interaction with adjuvant radiotherapy in this patient population.

It is well established that the absolute number of pathologically positive lymph nodes strongly predicts survival in HPV-driven OPC and that current consensus guidelines recommend adjuvant radiotherapy ± chemotherapy for any patient with ≥ 2 metastatic nodes or extracapsular extension [20, 21]. However, pN staging alone does not account for the denominator of the lymphadenectomy. The same “pN2” label may represent 2 positive nodes out of 15 harvested or 2 out of 40. This denominator-agnostic approach may over- or under-estimate tumor burden, especially in minimally dissected necks or after extensive dissections driven by surgical protocol rather than disease extent. This suggests that a subset of patients, typically those with limited nodal burden despite their pN classification, may be overtreated with current standard guidelines driven solely by pN status. The LNR integrates both tumor burden and surgical adequacy, providing a continuous, reproducible metric that can be calculated in every specimen.

The results of this study confirm that LNR is a significant prognostic factor in HPV-related OPC. Patients with an LNR ≥ 4.0% had significantly worse CSS and OS compared to those with an LNR < 4.0%. This finding is consistent with previous studies in other cancers, such as oral cancer, vulvar cancer, and breast cancer, where LNR has been shown to correlate with survival outcomes [11–14]. In recent years, several studies have also found the prognostic value of LNR in OPC [22–24]. Jacobi et al. conducted a single-center retrospective study and found that patients with LNR ≤ 10% had significantly better OS and disease-specific survival than those with LNR > 10% in patients with HPC-related OPSCC, while LNR had no prognostic value in HPV-negative OPSCC [23]. Choi et al. retrospectively analyzed 90 patients with HPV-related OPSCC and found that LNR had a significantly higher predictive value for OS and distant metastasis than the pN staging [24]. Nocon et al. analyzed the data of 4166 patients with HPV-related OPSCC from the National Cancer Database (NCDB) and found that pN classification demonstrated good prognostic ability (5-year OS% for pN0, pN1, pN2 were 91.3%, 90.1%, 78.8%, P < 0.001), but poor separation among stages with pN0 and pN1. In addition, survival analysis showed significant improvement for LNR ≤ 6% versus > 6% (5-year OS% 92.7% vs. 83.7%, P < 0.001) [25]. However, the NCDB database lacks data on cancer-related deaths of patients, so it is difficult to evaluate the impact of LNR on CSS.

The ROC curve analysis in our study demonstrated that LNR had significantly better efficacy than pN stage in prognostic stratification, suggesting that LNR could serve as a complementary or alternative prognostic tool to pN staging. This is particularly relevant in HPV-related OPC, where the biological behavior of the tumor and the response to treatment may differ from HPV-negative OPC [6]. LNR should be considered as a valuable prognostic marker in HPV-related OPC, particularly in conjunction with traditional pN staging. The inclusion of LNR in prognostic models could help refine risk stratification and identify patients who may benefit from more aggressive treatment strategies. In addition, although HPV-driven OPC carries a more favorable prognosis than tobacco-related head-and-neck cancer, cervical nodal metastasis remains the dominant determinant of survival in patients. Recent studies have shown that a high nodal-to-primary tumor standardized uptake value max ratio (NTR) predicts worse outcomes across head and neck cancers [26–28], and HPV-positive OPC commonly presents with hyper-metabolic nodes [29]. We hypothesize that NTR supplies prognostic information that is incremental to both LNR and pN-category, and could be used to individualize decisions on systemic therapy, postoperative radiotherapy, or surveillance intensity.

According to the ASTRO treatment guidelines, in patients with HPV-related OPSCC who have received curative surgical treatment, if there are pathological risk factors, such as positive surgical margins or certain tumor characteristics for high-risk recurrence, postoperative radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy is recommended [30]. Although the 8th edition staging system has adopted distinct staging strategies based on the HPV status of patients, there is currently no evidence to suggest that the new staging criteria published in the 8th edition of the AJCC staging system should drive clinical decision-making. The primary difference between HPV-positive and HPV-negative OPC lies in their prognosis [31]. Several studies have reported that patients with HPV-related OPCSS have a better prognosis and response to radiotherapy [32–35].

In our study, adjuvant radiotherapy was associated with significant improvements in CSS and OS in the overall cohort. However, the benefits of radiotherapy were not uniform across all subgroups. In our study, we found that in patients with LNR < 4.0%, radiotherapy did not significantly improve CSS or OS, suggesting that this subgroup may have a lower risk of disease progression and may not require adjuvant radiotherapy. Conversely, in patients with LNR ≥ 4.0%, radiotherapy provided substantial survival benefits, highlighting the importance of tailoring treatment based on LNR. Similarly, the benefits of radiotherapy varied by pN stage. While radiotherapy did not improve outcomes in patients with pN0 disease, it significantly enhanced CSS and OS in patients with pN1 and pN2 disease. This finding underscores the need for personalized treatment approaches based on both LNR and pN stage. In our study, under the conventional pN-based framework, the majority of patients (86.4%) were stratified into subgroups where adjuvant radiotherapy conferred a survival benefit. This finding theoretically implies that nearly all patients require treatment. In contrast, LNR-based stratification indicated that only 62.0% of patients may benefit from adjuvant radiotherapy. Consequently, an LNR-guided strategy could potentially spare 713 patients (24.4% of the cohort) from unnecessary radiotherapy. This reclassification underscores a critical limitation of the current pN staging system. Specifically, the system fails to distinguish between patients with a high nodal yield but a low metastatic ratio (true low risk) from those with few examined nodes yet a high metastatic burden. By accounting for variability in lymph node dissection, the LNR provides a more individualized assessment of recurrence risk.

Several studies have explored the effect of LNR in the decision-making of adjuvant radiotherapy for HPV-related OPC. A previous study on the NCDB database found that patients with LNR ≤ 6% did not benefit from radiotherapy in terms of survival (5-year OS 93.1% vs. 92.0%, P = 0.10), while patients with LNR > 6% could significantly improve their survival after postoperative radiotherapy (5-year OS 85.4% vs. 74.9%, P < 0.001). However, the pN staging could not identify subgroups that did not require radiotherapy [20]. Therefore, using the LNR may offer a more refined approach to radiotherapy decision-making, potentially enabling a greater number of patients to avoid unnecessary adjuvant radiotherapy and, consequently, the potential side effects associated with radiotherapy, including mucositis, xerostomia, and taste disorders, as well as late adverse reactions such as dysphagia and subcutaneous tissue fibrosis, and improving the quality of life of these patients [29]. Because LNR is readily calculated from the routine pathology report without additional cost or special stains, it can be immediately implemented in any center performing standard neck dissection, thus addressing a practical unmet need for precision adjuvant decision-making in HPV-related OPC.

Our study has some limitations. First, it is a retrospective analysis, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the SEER program lacks data on the exact time of radiotherapy initiation and specific adjuvant radiotherapy doses or target volume, limiting our ability to accurately analyze the impact of adjuvant radiotherapy on outcomes. Third, the SEER database lacks data on performance status, which is known to influence treatment decisions such as postoperative radiotherapy and to be strongly associated with prognosis. Moreover, the follow-up duration was relatively short, and longer-term outcomes could provide additional insights into the prognostic role of LNR and the impact of adjuvant therapies. The primary reason for the relatively short follow-up time is the inherent limitation of the 2010–2018 SEER Research Plus database, which was one of the two data sources used in our study. This database has a fixed follow-up period ending in 2020, and we were unable to extend the follow-up beyond this date due to data availability constraints. Despite the shorter follow-up time, our study provides valuable insights into the characteristics and outcomes of OPC patients, particularly in relation to HPV status. Finally, the optimal threshold for the LNR in HPV-positive OPC remains unclear, with a range of 5% to 17% [17, 18, 28].

Conclusions

In conclusion, LNR is a significant prognostic factor in HPV-related OPC and can help refine risk stratification and treatment decision-making. Adjuvant radiotherapy provides substantial survival benefits in patients with LNR ≥ 4.0%. Future research should focus on prospective studies and the development of personalized treatment strategies based on LNR and other prognostic markers.
