Pharmacological Therapy for Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease in a New Era for Obesity and Metabolic Medicine: A State-of-the-Art Review
Authors: Wah-Kheong Chan, Hak-Keith Leung, Guo-Jeng Tan, Quan-Hziung Lim, Lee-Ling Lim, Jeyakantha Ratnasingam, Shireene Ratna Vethakkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most common chronic liver disease worldwide and a major cause of cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and liver-related mortality. Weight loss via positive health behavioral changes is the cornerstone of management of MASLD. However, this is a huge challenge for many patients, highlighting the need for effective pharmacological therapies. Two pharmacological agents have received accelerated approval for the treatment of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), the progressive inflammatory form of MASLD, namely resmetirom, an oral, liver-directed, selective thyroid hormone receptor-β agonist, and semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist. Despite recent advances, effective pharmacological therapy for MASH-related cirrhosis remains an unmet need, underscoring the importance of early identification and intervention. This narrative review summarizes the current pharmacological therapy landscape of MASLD, including emerging incretin-based therapies and fibroblast growth factor-21 analogues, as well as current guidance on the use of non-invasive tests for treatment selection and monitoring for treatment response. It also provides a background on recent advances in obesity and metabolic medicine, highlighting the evolving paradigm of complication-centric obesity management and the potential role of incretin-based therapies as the backbone of the management of obesity and obesity-related conditions, including MASLD.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most common cause of chronic liver disease, affecting an estimated 38% of the global adult population, with the prevalence expected to increase to over 55% by 2040.1 It is diagnosed based on the presence of hepatic steatosis and at least one cardiometabolic risk factor in the absence of significant alcohol intake, and is a recognized liver manifestation of metabolic syndrome.2,3 A change in dietary environment favoring high-calorie ultra-processed foods and a more sedentary lifestyle in developed nations are among factors fueling the high and increasing prevalence of obesity and obesity-related diseases, including MASLD.4,5 Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) is the more severe form of MASLD, and is defined histologically by the presence of hepatic steatosis, lobular inflammation, and hepatocyte ballooning, which can lead to progressive fibrosis and cirrhosis, with increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). MASH has emerged as a major cause of liver transplantation and mortality due to decompensated cirrhosis and HCC, and its burden has been projected to continue to increase substantially in the coming years.6

The asymptomatic nature of the disease means many affected patients are not aware even when they have severe liver disease and may present only when they have developed decompensated cirrhosis or HCC. Late diagnosis translates to a missed opportunity for intervention that could meaningfully alter the natural course of the disease and improve patient outcomes. Therefore, screening of at-risk populations—such as people with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), pre-diabetes, or obesity with cardiometabolic risk factors for liver fibrosis—is paramount to identify people with significant liver disease who are at higher risk of future cirrhosis.7 Positive health behavioral changes that can achieve and maintain a healthier body weight and metabolic profile, including healthy eating and increased physical activity, are essential. While a comprehensive lifestyle program and weight loss can lead to resolution of steatohepatitis and improvement in liver fibrosis, only a small proportion of people reach these goals with lifestyle interventions alone.8 In this context, the accelerated approval of two pharmacological agents by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of MASH with moderate to advanced (F2–F3) fibrosis represents an important milestone. This review focuses on these newly approved pharmacological agents and provides insight into the evolving landscape of therapeutics in MASLD in the context of parallel and rapid developments in the field of obesity and metabolic medicine.

RESMETIROM: THE FIRST U.S. FDAAPPROVED PHARMACOLOGICAL AGENT FOR MASLD

In March 2024, resmetirom (Rezdiffra; Madrigal Pharmaceuticals) was the first pharmacological agent to receive accelerated approval from the U.S. FDA for the treatment of MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis.9 Resmetirom is an oral, liver-directed, selective thyroid hormone receptor-β receptor agonist that acts predominantly in the liver to enhance lipid metabolism and reduce hepatic steatosis without systemic thyromimetic effects.10 In the pivotal Phase 3 Study to Evaluate the Efficacy and Safety of Resmetirom in Patients With NASH and Fibrosis (MAESTRO-NASH) (Table 1),11 966 patients with biopsy-proven MASH and F1–F3 fibrosis were randomized to receive resmetirom 80 mg daily, resmetirom 100 mg daily, or placebo. At 52 weeks, repeat liver biopsy revealed significantly greater resolution of steatohepatitis with no worsening of liver fibrosis in both groups of patients who received resmetirom. In addition, significantly greater improvement in liver fibrosis without worsening of steatohepatitis was achieved in patients who received resmetirom (Fig. 1). Furthermore, a significant reduction in lowdensity lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol from baseline was recorded in the resmetirom group (–14% to –16%) compared with the placebo group (0.1%). Overall, resmetirom was well tolerated. Diarrhea and nausea were observed more frequently in patients receiving resmetirom, especially at the initiation of treatment, but were mild to moderate in severity and lasted a median of 15 to 20 days. Other less common but important side effects of resmetirom included hepatotoxicity and gallstone-related disorders, namely cholelithiasis, cholecystitis, and biliary pancreatitis. While it has been recommended that liver profile be checked 12 weeks after initiation of resmetirom to assess for hepatotoxicity,12 some clinicians may check the liver profile earlier at 4 or 8 weeks. It is noteworthy that mild increases in serum aminotransferase levels, usually <1.5 times the upper limit of normal, may be observed in the first 4 weeks after initiation of resmetirom, especially in patients treated with statins at baseline, and these increases usually return to normal within 8 weeks after initiation of resmetirom without discontinuation of resmetirom.12 As resmetirom has been shown to increase plasma concentrations of statins, it has been recommended that the daily dosage of rosuvastatin and simvastatin be limited to 20 mg per day, and the daily dosage of pravastatin and atorvastatin be limited to 40 mg per day, to minimize the theoretical risk of statin-related adverse events.12 Resmetirom is a cytochrome P450 2C8 (CYP2C8), organic anion transporting polypeptide 1B1 (OATP1B1) and OATP1B3 substrate, so its concomitant use with a strong CYP2C8 inhibitor (e.g., gemfibrozil) or OATP1B1 and OATP1B3 substrate (e.g., cyclosporine) is not recommended. The use of resmetirom with a moderate CYP2C8 inhibitor (e.g., clopidogrel) may be considered at a lower dosage with careful monitoring.12 While the effects of resmetirom on liver histology were independent of weight, a separate post hoc analysis reported that resmetirom 100 mg had higher efficacy than 80 mg for both primary endpoints in patients ≥100 kg,13 supporting the recommended dosage of 80 mg in patients weighing <100 kg and 100 mg in patients weighing ≥100 kg. In the extended Phase 3 Study to Evaluate Safety and Biomarkers of Resmetirom in Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease Patients (MAESTRO-NAFLD-1) open-label cirrhosis cohort, following 2 years of resmetirom, >50% of patients achieved a sustained ≥25% decrease in liver stiffness measurement (LSM) with consistent improvements in multiple fibrosis biomarkers and in clinically significant portal hypertension risk categories.14 The ongoing Phase 3 MAESTRO-NASH-OUTCOMES study enrolled patients with MASH and compensated cirrhosis, aiming to provide data on composite liver-related outcome events, including all-cause mortality, liver transplant, hepatic decompensation, and Model for End-Stage Liver Disease score, that could lead to its full approval (ClinicalTrials. gov ID: NCT05500222). While the safety and effectiveness of resmetirom in patients with compensated cirrhosis is being evaluated, its use in decompensated cirrhosis should be avoided because of the markedly elevated area under the curve and maximum concentration of the drug in moderate and severe hepatic impairment, which may increase the risk of adverse events.12

GLUCAGON-LIKE PEPTIDE-1 RECEPTOR AGONIST: A BACKBONE PHARMACOLOGICAL THERAPY FOR MASLD?

In August 2025, semaglutide (Wegovy; Novo Nordisk) became the second pharmacological agent and the first glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist to receive accelerated approval from the U.S. FDA for the treatment of MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis based on positive results from part 1 of the Research Study on Whether Semaglutide Works in People With Non-alcoholic Steatohepatitis (ESSENCE) (Table 1).15 In the planned interim analysis at week 72, which involved the first 800 patients enrolled in the study, semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly resulted in significantly greater resolution of steatohepatitis without worsening liver fibrosis and significantly greater improvement in liver fibrosis without worsening of steatohepatitis compared with placebo (Fig. 1). Histological benefits were observed in patients with and without T2DM and across different body mass index (BMI) categories. In addition, patients receiving semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly had significantly greater weight loss and improvements in metabolic profile, including blood pressure, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol, triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein. As expected, the most common side effects were gastrointestinal, namely nausea, diarrhea, constipation, vomiting, and decreased appetite, which were mostly mild to moderate in severity (see section on ‘Side effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists and management’). While the results from part 2 of the ESSENCE trial have yet to be released, emerging data support the potential of GLP-1 receptor agonists being the backbone of a larger group of pharmacological treatments for MASLD, either as part of dual or triple receptor agonist therapy or with an add-on therapy, based on their benefits across multiple indications related to obesity and metabolic health.

Several other incretin-based therapies have shown promising results in phase 2 trials, including survodutide (a dual GLP-1 and glucagon [GCG] receptor agonist) and tirzepatide (a dual GLP-1 and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide [GIP] receptor agonist) (Table 1), both showing significantly greater resolution of steatohepatitis with no worsening of liver fibrosis and significantly greater improvement in liver fibrosis with no worsening of steatohepatitis compared with placebo (Fig. 1).16,17 In a sub-study of a phase 2 trial on retatrutide (a triple GLP-1/GIP/GCG receptor agonist) for obesity that included participants with ≥10% liver fat content based on magnetic resonance proton density fat fraction, all patients who received retatrutide 8 or 12 mg weekly achieved ≥30% liver fat reduction (a widely used surrogate marker for histological response) after 24 weeks.18 At the time of preparation of this article, phase 3 trials of survodutide, tirzepatide, and retatrutide were ongoing (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06632444, NCT06632457, and NCT07165028).

In a phase 2 trial on patients with MASH-related cirrhosis (Table 1), semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly for 48 weeks failed to achieve the primary endpoint of improvement in liver fibrosis and no worsening of MASH (Fig. 1), raising concerns about lack of efficacy in patients with more severe liver disease.19 This emphasizes the importance of identifying patients with F2–F3 fibrosis for intervention to prevent them from progressing to cirrhosis. On a positive note, efruxifermin (a fusion protein of human immunoglobulin G1 fragment crystallizable domain linked to a modified human fibroblast growth factor-21 [FGF-21]) has shown promising results. In the phase 2b Study of Efruxifermin in Non-Cirrhotic Subjects With Histologically Confirmed Nonalcoholic Steatohepatitis (HARMONY), efruxifermin 50 mg weekly for 96 weeks in patients with biopsy-proven MASH and F2–F3 fibrosis resulted in significantly greater improvement in liver fibrosis with no worsening of MASH compared with placebo.20 In a separate study on patients with biopsy-proven MASH-related cirrhosis, a Study of Efruxifermin in Subjects With Compensated Cirrhosis Due to Nonalcoholic Steatohepatitis (SYMMETRY), efruxifermin 28 or 50 mg weekly failed to achieve the primary endpoint of improvement in liver fibrosis with no worsening of MASH after 36 weeks, but efruxifermin 50 mg weekly did result in significantly greater improvement in fibrosis after 96 weeks, the first pharmacological agent to have been shown to do so in patients with MASH-related cirrhosis.21 At the time of preparation of this article, phase 3 trials of efruxifermin were ongoing (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06215716 and NCT06528314). A pilot study demonstrated the tolerability of the addition of efruxifermin 50 mg weekly to patients with T2DM and MASH with F1–F3 fibrosis receiving a GLP-1 receptor agonist with improvements in liver fat, FibroScan aspartate aminotransferase score, insulin sensitivity, and glycemic control.22 In the phase 2b Study Evaluating the Safety, Efficacy and Tolerability of BIO89-100 in Subjects With Biopsy-confirmed Nonalcoholic Steatohepatitis (ENLIVEN), pegozafermin, a long-acting glycopegylated FGF-21 analogue, at 15 or 30 mg weekly or 44 mg every 2 weeks for 24 weeks, resulted in significantly greater resolution of steatohepatitis with no worsening of liver fibrosis, while 30 mg weekly or 44 mg every 2 weeks, resulted in significantly greater improvement in liver fibrosis with no worsening of steatohepatitis, compared with placebo.23 In post hoc analysis of 12 patients who were reclassified as having compensated cirrhosis at baseline in the study, pegozafermin resulted in ≥1 stage fibrosis improvement in nine of the 11 treated patients (82%) and ≥1 stage fibrosis improvement with no worsening of steatohepatitis five of the 11 treated patients (45%), which was not observed in patients who received placebo.24 At the time of preparation of this article, phase 3 trials of pegozafermin were ongoing (ClinicalTrials. gov ID: NCT06318169 and NCT06419374).

WHO TO TREAT AND HOW TO MONITOR TREATMENT RESPONSE?

Following the U.S. FDA approval of resmetirom and semaglutide for the treatment of MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis, the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases published guidance on using non-invasive tests to identify candidates for treatment and to monitor treatment response (Fig. 2).25,26 The use of non-invasive tests is widely accepted in routine clinical practice due to the many limitations of liver biopsy (e.g., invasive procedure with small risk of serious complications affecting acceptance and feasibility for repeated assessment, sampling variability, and observer variability).27 The rationale for targeting patients with MASH and F2–F3 fibrosis in the development of pharmacological therapy for MASLD was based on the recognition of the significantly increased risk of liverrelated complications and mortality in this group of patients compared with those with no or mild fibrosis.28 However, non-invasive tests are increasingly recognized as being similarly prognostic,29 and risk stratification based on the two-step approach is particularly relevant in this context30 because the two-step approach has been recommended to identify patients with more severe liver disease or higher risk of future cirrhosis in major guidelines.3,7,31-33 On the other hand, patients with MASH-related cirrhosis represent a unique population that is more difficult to treat with no existing approved pharmacological therapies. As for the recommended threshold of non-invasive tests for indicating beneficial response to treatment, studies have shown a high likelihood of histological response among patients achieving the thresholds on repeat testing (Fig. 2).34-37 Noteworthy is a 30% change in LSM in patients stratified into intermediate or high risk based on baseline LSM using either 8–12 or 10–15 kPa, which was shown to be associated with significant change in risk of liver-related events during long-term follow-up.38

SIDE EFFECTS OF GLP-1 RECEPTOR AGONISTS AND MANAGEMENT

The adverse effects of GLP-1 are dominated by gastrointestinal symptoms linked to delayed gastric emptying and central satiety signaling. Nausea (15%–30%), vomiting (10%–15%), and diarrhea (5%–10%) are most common and are dose-dependent, emerge early, and typically lessen with physiological adaptation.39 Constipation rates increase with longer treatment.40 Gastroparesis and intestinal obstruction are a concern, with longer duration of use associated with higher risk, though evidence remains inconsistent.40,41 Cholelithiasis risk is 20%–30% higher in real-world studies among patients with T2DM on GLP-1 receptor agonists compared to other glucose-lowering drugs, although less so with dulaglutide.41 Biliary adverse events are attributable to gallbladder cholesterol supersaturation during rapid weight loss and delayed gallbladder emptying from GLP-1 agonism.41 Pancreatic adverse events were an early concern, but recent large analyses showed no convincing association with pancreatitis or pancreatic cancer, and transient elevations in pancreatic enzymes are expected but rarely clinically meaningful.41 Hypoglycemia is rare unless combined with insulin or sulfonylurea for diabetes treatment. Transient heart rate increases, injection-site reactions, fatigue, and headache are mild and self-limiting. Ocular adverse events, particularly non-arteritic ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) were recently flagged by reports from a matched cohort analysis suggesting a 4- to 7-fold increase in risk among GLP-1 receptor agonist users.42 However, NAION is exceptionally rare (0.002% to 0.01%), its mechanism is unclear, and subsequent larger meta-analyses were contradictory.43 However, patients with T2DM and existing diabetic retinopathy are at a 23% increased risk of retinopathy progression due to rapid glycemic shifts.41 Sarcopenia can be substantial, with lean mass losses of up to 10% accompanying fat loss, raising concerns of premature aging.44 A family history of medullary thyroid cancer and multiple endocrine neoplasia are contraindications due to pre-clinical animal data, although human evidence for oncogenicity is inconclusive.41

Management of gastrointestinal adverse events centers on patient education, judicious dose escalation, and effective management of accompanying symptoms.39 Patients should be advised to maintain smaller meal portions, avoid fatty and spicy foods, and practice mindful eating. Gradual dose escalation is prudent, with consideration for slower titration among patients who report more apparent initial symptoms. Short-term use of antiemetics, histamine-2 blockers, prokinetics, or osmotic laxatives may be considered. Dose reduction or temporary cessation is appropriate for moderate symptoms, whilst severe gastroparesis warrants discontinuation. Preemptive adjustment of insulin or secretagogues may be necessary to prevent hypoglycemia among patients with T2DM, especially if glycemic control is already near-normal. Resistance exercise should be routinely recommended to mitigate sarcopenia.44

EMERGING CONCEPTS IN OBESITY MEDICINE

Advances in understanding the pathobiology of obesity have led to the increasing awareness of obesity as a complex, chronic, relapsing disease that requires multidisciplinary management and holistic personalized care.45 From the initial recognition of obesity as an actual disease by the American Medical Association (as recently as 2013), to the introduction of the adiposity-based chronic disease model by the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists and American College of Endocrinology in 2017,46 to the Lancet Commission 2025 identifying clinical obesity as an entity encompassing not just excess adiposity but concomitant organ dysfunction and/or limitation of daily activities, there has been significant recent progress in the field of obesity medicine.45 Obesity is a condition characterized by excessive and/or dysfunctional adipose tissue, which can be located in ectopic sites such as the liver, pancreas, and skeletal muscle.47 Revised diagnostic criteria for obesity require an elevated BMI plus at least one other elevated anthropometric measure such as waist circumference, waist hip ratio, or waist height ratio (Fig. 2).45,48 Importantly, the development of obesity management medications for chronic treatment, in particular incretinbased therapies, has been a game-changer for patients.

Emerging concepts in obesity medicine are summarized in Table 2 and the highlights are: (1) The importance of risk stratification that goes beyond BMI or waist circumference alone, and includes a clinical component comprising medical, functional, and mental domains. (2) Application of a chronic disease management model using a structured framework with treatment targets, regular review, and intensification of therapy.48 (3) The transition from a sole focus on short-term weight loss to a complication-centric model targeting prevention, improvement, and resolution of obesity complications. (4) The selection of evidence-based pharmacological therapy that concurrently targets both weight reduction and multiple obesity complications in patients with obesity, including MASLD. These shifts echo changes in the management of another obesity-linked chronic disease with similarly wide-ranging complications, T2DM.

Patients with obesity must be screened concurrently for other obesity complications such as dysglycemia, cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, MASLD, obstructive sleep apnea, knee osteoarthritis, psychological symptoms, and functional limitations, among others. After considering patient preferences, side-effect profiles and cost constraints, evidence-based pharmacological treatment that targets both weight loss and as many downstream complications of obesity as possible should be selected. For example, for a normoglycemic patient with MASLD and cardiovascular disease (CVD), the medication of choice would be a GLP-1 receptor agonist with proven efficacy in both MASLD and CVD (i.e., semaglutide) rather than alternative agents like naltrexone-bupropion, which results in weight loss but has little to no efficacy in reversing CVD or MASLD.15,47 Another important consideration is the magnitude of weight loss that has been demonstrated to reverse a particular complication, for example, weight loss of ≥5% for hepatic steatosis and ≥10% for steatohepatitis.49,50

Finally, there are other novel classes of agents that have demonstrated weight-loss benefits in people with obesity, some of which have also shown evidence of efficacy with regards to MASLD, including dual GLP-1/GCG receptor agonists (e.g., survodutide), dual GLP-1/GIP receptor agonists (i.e., tirzepatide), and triple GLP-1/GIP/GCG receptor agonists (i.e., retatrutide).51 Cagrilintide, an amylin analogue, led to significant weight loss in the large phase 3 trial, a Research Study to See How Well CagriSema Helps People With Excess Body Weight Lose Weight (REDEFINE 1), both on its own (11.5% weight loss) and in combination with semaglutide (20.4% weight loss).52 At the time of preparation of this article, a phase 2 trial of various combinations of cagrilintide, semaglutide, and zalfermin (a long-acting analog of FGF-21) for the treatment of MASH with F2–F4 fibrosis has been completed but the results have not yet been released (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05016882).

INCRETIN-BASED THERAPIES FOR OBESITY AND DIABETES

Dulaglutide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide are the three most widely used incretin-based therapies for T2DM.53 Among people with T2DM on metformin therapy over 40 weeks, reductions in HbA1c and body weight were greater with semaglutide (up to 1 mg weekly) than with dulaglutide (up to 1.5 mg weekly).54 Superior reductions in HbA1c and greater weight loss were reported with tirzepatide than with semaglutide 1 mg weekly over 40 weeks in people with T2DM.55 Higher doses of semaglutide (2 mg weekly) and dulaglutide (4.5 mg weekly) have also been approved for T2DM. Apart from effective glycemic control and weight loss in people with T2DM, incretin-based therapies also offer cardiovascular-kidney benefits (see section on ‘Cardio-renal benefits of incretin-based therapies’), which are discussed in the following section.56

The observations of modest (up to 5%) weight loss in people with T2DM treated with incretin-based therapies motivated the investigation of whether higher doses might offer greater weight loss in people with obesity. In 2014, liraglutide 3 mg once daily was the first GLP-1 receptor agonist approved for chronic weight management in people with overweight (BMI ≥27 kg/m2) and at least one weight-related comorbidity, or those with obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2).57 This was followed by the approval of semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly and tirzepatide 5 to 15 mg weekly for similar indications in 2021 and 2023, respectively.53,58 In the Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with Obesity (STEP) trials, semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly demonstrated a placebo-subtracted weight loss of 12%–15% in people without T2DM.59,60 Consistent with results from other weight loss strategies, people with overweight or obesity and coexistent T2DM have been shown to have approximately 20% attenuation in weight loss. Key landmark trials on incretin-based therapies for obesity and T2DM are summarized elsewhere.59-61

In the A Study of Tirzepatide in Participants With Obesity or Overweight (SURMOUNT) trials with similar eligibility criteria as the STEP programs, tirzepatide 5 to 15 mg weekly demonstrated a placebo-subtracted weight loss of up to 20% in people with obesity without T2DM.60 Of note, approximately 60% of those treated with 15 mg weekly attained ≥20% weight loss.60 In the SURMOUNT-2 trial involving people with BMI ≥27 kg/m2 and T2DM (mean HbA1c 8.02%, mean duration of diabetes approximately 8 years), tirzepatide 15 mg weekly demonstrated placebosubtracted weight loss of 11.6% after 72 weeks.62 Nearly half of the trial participants achieved HbA1c <5.7%, with a mean end-of-trial HbA1c of 5.9%, and one-third of participants achieved >20% weight loss after 72 weeks.62

In the SURMOUNT-3 trial, an initial mean weight loss of ≥5% after 12 weeks of intensive lifestyle intervention (a healthy balanced diet with a 500 kcal deficit daily and ≥150 minutes of physical activity weekly) was achieved,63 with additional placebo-subtracted weight loss of 24.5% reported after 72 weeks of tirzepatide at a maximum tolerated dose of 10 to 15 mg weekly.63 Of note, among people with overweight or obesity who achieved a mean weight loss of 20.9% after 36 weeks of tirzepatide, 46.2% sustained a mean weight loss of ≥10% 1 year after discontinuation of tirzepatide.64 In Asian populations, the Study of Tirzepatide in Chinese Participants Without Type 2 Diabetes Who Have Obesity or Overweight (SURMOUNT-CN) and the Study of Tirzepatide in Participants With Obesity Disease (SURMOUNT-J) demonstrated that a placebo-subtracted weight loss of 11.3% to 21.1% was achieved with tirzepatide 10 to 15 mg weekly after 52 to 72 weeks.65,66 Among people with overweight or obesity, 48.4% of those treated with tirzepatide 10 to 15 mg weekly achieved ≥20% weight loss versus 27.3% with semaglutide 1.7 to 2.4 mg weekly, with corresponding reductions in waist circumference of 18.4 and 13.0 cm.67 Despite greater weight loss, the rates of gastrointestinal adverse events with tirzepatide in the SURMOUNT trials appear lower than those for semaglutide in the STEP trials, likely due to the actions of GIP to attenuate central GLP-1–induced aversive responses.68

CARDIO-RENAL BENEFITS OF INCRETINBASED THERAPIES

We have come to recognize that obesity and T2DM share a common pathophysiology that includes metabolic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, which are key drivers of atherosclerosis, heart failure, and renal function decline. Interventions to improve each of these cardio-renal-metabolic endpoints have now been integrated, as mechanistically they are intertwined, and improving one endpoint often leads to improvement in other endpoints as well.69 Incretin-based therapies have shown significant benefit in glycemic control and weight reduction in phase 3 trials (see section on ‘Incretin-based therapies for obesity and diabetes’), and these benefits have been postulated to improve harder outcomes in target organs by virtue of improvement in metabolic inflammation and classical cardio-vascular risks such as weight, glucose, blood pressure, and lipids. As a result, several randomized controlled trials have explored the cardio-renal effects of these agents. These trials also aim to fulfill the U.S. FDA requirements for cardiac safety data prior to registration.70

Numerous studies have shown major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) benefits that include non-fatal myocardial infarction, non-fatal stroke, and cardiovascular death versus placebo in addition to standard of care, beginning with the GLP-1 receptor agonist in the LEADER trial.71 Following this, GLP-1 receptor agonists were proven to show similar benefits in people living with T2DM in the Trial to Evaluate Cardiovascular and Other Longterm Outcomes With Semaglutide in Subjects With Type 2 Diabetes (SUSTAIN-6), Effect of Albiglutide, When Added to Standard Blood Glucose Lowering Therapies, on Major Cardiovascular Events in Subjects With Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (HARMONY OUTCOMES) trial, Effect of Efpeglenatide on Cardiovascular Outcomes (AMPLITUDE-O) trial, Researching Cardiovascular Events With a Weekly Incretin in Diabetes (REWIND) trial, and A Heart Disease Study of Semaglutide in Patients With Type 2 Diabetes (SOUL).72-76 Different GLP-1 receptor agonist molecules, both short acting-daily as well as long acting-weekly and both subcutaneous and oral versions, have been tested.71-78 Cardiovascular outcome trials (CVOTs) with GLP-1 receptor agonists have also been expanded in populations without diabetes but with obesity and high cardiovascular risk in the Semaglutide Effects on Heart Disease and Stroke in Patients With Overweight or Obesity (SELECT) trial, which showed a robust 20% relative risk reduction in MACE endpoints versus placebo.79 The SURPASS CVOT showed tirzepatide to be non-inferior to dulaglutide, which was a first of its kind CVOT comparing the molecule of interest with an active comparator.80 GLP-1 receptor agonists have now been combined with other receptor agonists or analogues, e.g., survodutide (GLP-1/GCG receptor agonist), retatrutide (GLP-1/GIP/GCG receptor agonist), and cagrilintide-semaglutide (amylin analogue+GLP-1 receptor agonist) with early results showing greater weight loss and glycemic benefits with these combinations. Dedicated cardio-renal trials of these agents have begun, and results are eagerly awaited in the coming years (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05556512, NCT0607 7864, NCT05882045, and NCT05669755).

An area of growing interest is heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, in which very few therapies have shown benefit. Semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly in the STEP-heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) diabetes mellitus (DM) and STEP-HFpEF trials and tirzepatide up to 15 mg weekly in the Study of Tirzepatide in Participants With Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction and Obesity (SUMMIT) trial showed significant improvement in symptoms in both diabetes and those without diabetes with obesity.81-83 In addition, renal endpoints have also recently become an important focus. The possible renal benefits of these agents were noted from their respective CVOTs as secondary endpoints; the only dedicated renal outcome trial to date with kidneyspecific hard endpoints is the Research Study to See How Semaglutide Works Compared to Placebo in People With Type 2 Diabetes and Chronic Kidney Disease (FLOW) trial, which showed a significant benefit of semaglutide 1 mg weekly.84 Many of these trials for the heart and kidney showed that beneficial effects occur independent of glucose or weight reduction and regardless of sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor use, repurposing incretinbased therapies towards organ protection among people living with obesity and T2DM. CONCLUSION

The treatment landscape of MASLD is entering a transformative era. U.S. FDA approval of resmetirom represents a landmark achievement in MASLD therapeutics.11 In parallel, incretin-based therapies act as the backbone of metabolic therapy with proven benefits in MASLD beyond weight reduction, including glycemic control and improvement in cardiorenal outcomes, among others.85,86 Despite these recent advances, MASLD, being a heterogeneous multisystem disease where no single therapy can fully target the metabolic, inflammatory, and fibrotic mechanisms, will remain a challenge. Further studies are needed to establish the long-term efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness of these emerging pharmacological agents, alone or in combination, and in real-world clinical practice. Ultimately, obesity and obesity-related conditions including MASLD will require a broader public health approach to strengthen preventive measures in addition to a multidisciplinary framework integrating positive health behavioral changes, pharmacological therapy, and cardiovascular risk reduction to achieve meaningful and sustainable control at the population level.
