Therapeutic Inertia in Type 2 Diabetes in Indian Primary Care: A Scoping Review
Authors: Lokesh Shanmugam, Siva Ranganathan Green, A Sathya, Joshua Chadwick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTherapeutic inertia (TI) is a significant barrier to achieving optimal glycaemic control in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), particularly in primary health care (PHC) settings in India. Despite growing global attention, evidence from Indian PHC remains limited and fragmented.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following PRISMA-ScR guidelines. A systematic search of electronic databases and relevant policy documents was performed to identify studies published between 2010 and 2025. Studies addressing therapeutic inertia in T2DM at global and Indian levels were included and thematically analysed.ResultsThe findings indicate that global evidence on therapeutic inertia is extensive, highlighting delays in treatment intensification, clinical decision-making challenges, and health system inefficiencies. In contrast, Indian evidence is relatively limited and predominantly derived from urban and tertiary care settings. Across studies, therapeutic inertia was influenced by multiple factors, including patient-related barriers (eg, fear and lack of awareness), provider-related factors (eg, clinical conservatism), and system-level constraints (eg, resource limitations). Notably, there is a lack of longitudinal and interventional studies focusing on PHC in India.ConclusionTherapeutic inertia remains a multifactorial challenge in T2DM management, particularly in Indian PHC settings where evidence gaps persist. Strengthening primary care systems, promoting implementation research, and developing context-specific interventions are essential to address this issue and improve diabetes outcomes.Keywords: therapeutic inertia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, primary health care, glycemic control, NPCDCS, PRISMA-ScR

Introduction

Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) has emerged as one of the most critical public health concerns in India. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF 2021), there are 537 million adults in the world with diabetes currently estimated to increase to 783 million by 2045, with India in itself estimated to be home to 101 million cases, second only to China.1,2 The early age of onset, aggressive disease progression, and high incidence of complications make diabetes management particularly challenging.

Several Indian registries report that more than half of patients with T2DM fail to achieve HbA1c targets (<7%), indicating delays in timely treatment adjustment.3,4

One of the key contributors to poor glycaemic control is “therapeutic inertia” (TI), defined as the failure to initiate or escalate glucose-lowering therapy when appropriate.5 Although often used interchangeably with clinical inertia, TI specifically refers to delays in treatment modification despite evidence of inadequate glycaemic control, whereas clinical inertia is a broader concept encompassing overall deficiencies in disease management.6

This failure to intensify therapy leads to persistent hyperglycaemia, increased risk of micro- and macrovascular complications, and higher healthcare costs.7–12 Studies have reported delays of 1–2 years in treatment intensification and even longer delays in the initiation of insulin therapy.9,10

Multiple factors contribute to therapeutic inertia, including healthcare provider-related barriers such as concerns about hypoglycaemia, polypharmacy, and time constraints;13 patient-related factors such as cost, fear of injections, and sociocultural beliefs;14 and healthcare system-level challenges including care delivery patterns and limited access to resources.15

Therapeutic Inertia in India

The specific challenges of the health system aggravate the therapeutic inertia in India. Primary Health Centres (PHCs) and Community Health Centres (CHCs) are the pillars of diabetes care under the National Program for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases, and Stroke (NPCDCS).16 However, these clinics are overloaded, underequipped, and attended by general practitioners who may not have specialised training in diabetes management.17 Furthermore, drug shortages, weak referral mechanisms, and restricted laboratory access can lead to system-level inertia.18

Evidence from India further highlights this issue. The DiabCare India 2011 survey of a large metropolitan population drew attention to the fact that, although there was uncontrolled HbA1c. Similarly, a multicentre registry study documented median intervals of 12–18 months for delays in starting insulin, even in patients with established poor glycaemic control.19 Recent qualitative-quantitative research conducted in rural PHCs also revealed that doctors prefer symptomatic treatment and are hesitant to intensify therapy owing to resource limitations and patient resistance.20 These results demonstrate that therapeutic inertia in India is not only a clinical problem, but also a structural and systemic problem.

Why Primary Care Settings are Important

For consistency, this study used the term primary health care (PHC) to encompass all community- and outpatient-level facilities providing first-contact diabetes management in India. More than 70% of Indian T2DM patients are treated at the primary level.21 PHC physicians are typically the first and primary point of contact. In these contexts, inertia delays treatment intensification and increases the risk of complications. For instance, inappropriately delayed initiation of insulin is associated with increased risk of complications such as nephropathy and retinopathy.22 Additionally, the fragmented referral system leads to poor continuity of care across PHCs, private clinics, and tertiary hospitals.23

Unlike in high-income countries, which have well-structured primary care systems in place, electronic decision support systems, and a multidisciplinary model of care delivery, primary healthcare settings in India may be challenged by issues such as workforce limitations and fragmented referral systems. Such structural variations may have a substantial impact on the degree and determinants of TI in this country.

At the international level, interventions, such as structured care models, electronic reminder systems, patient education programs, and multidisciplinary team approaches, have been proposed to reduce therapeutic inertia.24 Nonetheless, few studies have been conducted on the feasibility and effectiveness of these interventions in PHC settings in India. Indian studies tend to be either hospital- or urban-centred, thereby creating a large knowledge gap concerning rural and low-resource settings.20,25

Therefore, this scoping review, guided by PRISMA-ScR methodology,5,9,26 aims to map the existing evidence on therapeutic inertia in T2DM within Indian primary healthcare settings, identify key contributing factors, and highlight gaps to inform future research and policy.

The primary research question guiding this review is: What is the current evidence on the prevalence, determinants, and impact of therapeutic inertia in T2DM management within Indian primary healthcare settings?

Methodology

Study Design and Structure

The Arksey and O’Malley (2005) framework was used to define scoping reviews as a systematic process for mapping existing literature to determine the nature, range, and extent of research activity on a given topic.26 This framework was further developed by Levac et al (2010) with a focus on bridging the research purpose and study selection, taking an iterative team-based approach to data extraction, and extending beyond descriptions to interpret practice, policy, and future research implications.27 This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for the Scoping Reviews Checklist (PRISMA-ScR).28 Finally, guidance by Munn et al (2018) was used to clarify the rationale behind selecting a scoping review over a systematic review.29 Using these parallel frameworks, we ensured that our review was systematic, comprehensive, and consistent with international standards for scoping review methodology. Editorial consistency in terminology and formatting was maintained in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR recommendations.

Operational Definition of Therapeutic Inertia

In this review, TI was operationalised as the delay or failure to initiate or increase pharmacological treatment in people with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), despite the existence of indicators for suboptimal glycaemic control. For example, HbA1c values were higher than the target values. This operational definition of TI corresponds to that proposed by Khunti et al (2018) and the ADA/EASD Consensus (2018), which considers TI an outcome in the treatment timeframe.

Clinical inertia encompasses the overall failure to modify management, including lifestyle counselling, diagnostic evaluation, or non-pharmacological interventions. As the literature contains inconsistent usages of both terms, we included studies using either “therapeutic inertia” or “clinical inertia” when their scope was defined from a pharmacological intensification perspective regarding T2DM.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

Searches were conducted using the following three electronic databases: Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed/MEDLINE. Searches were performed in March 2025. National policy documents, such as the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases, and Stroke (NPCDCS) operational guidelines (2018),16 were also manually searched for contextualising system-level factors.

A key limitation of this review is that the available Indian evidence is largely derived from studies conducted between 2006 and 2014, which may not fully reflect the current scenario. Additionally, most studies were retrieved from major international databases (Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed), with limited inclusion of Indian-specific databases. Furthermore, a substantial proportion of the included studies were conducted in urban tertiary care settings, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to rural primary healthcare contexts.

The following search strategy was modified for each database (eg. PubMed):

(“therapeutic inertia” OR “treatment inertia” OR “clinical inertia”)

AND (“type 2 diabetes” OR “T2DM”)

Data Extraction and Synthesis

A structured process for extracting and synthesising data was adopted, following the PRISMA-ScR framework. Data were independently abstracted from all included reports by two researchers using Microsoft Excel prepared in advance. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus. Quantitative data were presented descriptively, whereas for qualitative research, thematic synthesis was conducted using an inductive code approach, where evidence was grouped through node convergence within the themes of the patient, provider, and system levels.

Patient and Public Involvement

Patients and the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans for this research. Individualised data were not included in the analysis. All data were derived from publicly available sources, ensuring the complete protection of patient privacy and confidentiality.

Thematic Barriers to Overcoming Therapeutic Inertia

Patient-Level Barriers

Fear of Insulin and Stigma

Fear of insulin therapy and stigma were frequently reported barriers. Studies described misconceptions regarding insulin use and its association with advanced disease. Findings from the Gujarat KAP survey (Shah et al, 2009) revealed widespread misconceptions, with many patients perceiving insulin as a last-resort measure associated with advanced disease or impending complications. Similarly, the DiabCare India study (Mohan et al, 2014) reported that despite persistently high HbA1c levels (>8%), numerous patients were reluctant to start insulin owing to needle anxiety, perceived lifestyle disruption, and social stigma. Insulin is often viewed as a sign of personal failure rather than an effective therapeutic tool.

Financial Cost of Therapy

Financial constraints were reported as barriers to treatment intensification, particularly for advanced therapies such as insulin analogues and GLP-1 receptor agonists. Advanced therapies, such as insulin analogues, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors, are prohibitively expensive for a large proportion of patients, particularly those from rural or economically disadvantaged backgrounds. Even when clinicians recommend escalation of therapy, the additional costs associated with insulin delivery systems and glucose monitoring equipment often hinder uptake. Unlike patients in high-income countries, who benefit from insurance coverage, the predominance of out-of-pocket expenditures in India further reinforces therapeutic inertia.

Low Awareness of HbA1c Targets

The Gujarat KAP survey (Shah et al, 2009) revealed that many patients had limited awareness of HbA1c as an indicator of long-term glycaemic control. A frequent misconception equates to the absence of symptoms with adequate control, leading patients to overlook persistently elevated glucose levels. This knowledge gap often resulted in a disconnect between physician advice and patient adherence, as individuals were reluctant to intensify therapy when they perceived themselves to be “normal”. Therefore, limited HL contributes significantly to therapeutic inertia, particularly in resource-constrained primary health care settings.

Provider-Level Barriers

Hypoglycemia Fear

Findings from the DiabCare India study (Mohan et al, 2014) suggest that physicians often delay initiating insulin therapy due to concerns regarding patient safety. Hypoglycaemia was perceived as a significant risk with potential implications for medicolegal liability and increased emergency visits. As a result, clinicians tend to weigh the immediate risks of hypoglycaemia more heavily than the long-term consequences of sustained hyperglycaemia, contributing to therapeutic inertia.

Non-Guideline Adherence

The DEDICOM study (Nagpal and Bhartia, 2006) and FIT India guidelines (2015) highlighted substantial gaps in provider adherence to the recommended treatment protocols. Physicians often opt to intensify lifestyle interventions or increase oral antidiabetic drug dosages rather than transition patients to insulin therapy. This pattern of therapeutic conservatism appears to stem from limited hands-on training, reliance on traditional prescription habits, and inadequate opportunities for continuing medical education in PHC settings.

System-Level Barriers

Drug and Laboratory Stockouts

The NPCDCS operational guidelines (2018) acknowledge that PHCs often face recurrent shortages in insulin, oral hypoglycaemic agents, and diagnostic supplies. Such gaps in availability restrict both patients and physicians from implementing timely treatment escalation even when clinically warranted. The absence of routine HbA1c testing further compounded this challenge, as therapy adjustments are frequently based only on fasting glucose values, delaying the recognition of poor glycaemic control.

Referral Fragmentation

Nagpal and Bhartia (2006) reported that fragmented referral pathways in Delhi often result in patients being lost during the transition between PHCs, private clinics, and tertiary hospitals. The absence of standardised referral and follow-up systems has led to inconsistent and poorly coordinated therapeutic decisions across different levels of care.

Overburdened PHCs

The NPCDCS operational guidelines (2018) also note a critical shortage of trained physicians and nurses in PHCs. Providers are required to manage both communicable and non-communicable diseases, leaving limited time for proactive diabetes management. Under these constraints, treatment intensification is often deprioritised, thereby perpetuating therapeutic inertia.

Collectively, these challenges demonstrate how patient-, provider-, and system-level factors interact in mutually reinforcing ways to create a self-sustaining cycle of therapeutic inertia.

Summary of Clinical Outcomes Associated with Therapeutic Inertia

Increased HbA1c at Intensification

The international literature (Shabnam 2023; McDaniel 2024) describes HbA1c values of 8.7–9.3% at the time of therapy intensification. Indian evidence also supports this, with Mohan et al (2013, Archive) and Mohan et al (2014, DiabCare) reporting HbA1c values of > 9%. These values exceed the recommended HbA1c target of 7%.

Long Delays in Therapy

For high-income countries, the time frame reported regarding the delay in therapy intensification is generally between 12–24 months (McDaniel, 2024), with most people being treated within the lower part of the time frame, on average. For the registry in the Indian setting, the time frame for the delay in therapy intensification was between 15–18 months (Mohan et al 2013), with a relatively higher percentage of achieving HbA1c values above 9% at the threshold of starting insulin therapy.

Complications

Delayed intensification promotes microvascular and macrovascular complications. McDaniel et al (2024) demonstrated an increased incidence of nephropathy, retinopathy, and cardiovascular hospitalisation in patients with extended TI. Indian studies also reported increased rates of nephropathy and retinopathy.

Costs and Healthcare Utilisation

Therapeutic inertia in the US is associated with increased healthcare use and expenses from hospitalisation and complications (McDaniel, 2024). Limited data were available from Indian studies on healthcare costs. Out-of-pocket expenditure increases as complications advance, adding to the financial burden on families.

Important Points

Global Evidence

Between 2010 and 2015, research related to therapeutic inertia in Indian diabetes care was limited, averaging only approximately two–three studies per year. Since 2016, there has been a marked increase in the number of publications, reflecting a growing interest in the topic. The years 2020 to 2022 saw a significant surge, with more than eight studies published annually, many of which adopted registry-based methodologies and incorporated structured-care models. This trend clearly indicates an increased awareness of therapeutic inertia as a critical barrier to effective diabetes management in India’s health care system.

Indian Evidence

There was minimal research related to therapeutic inertia in Indian PHC before 2013. The field’s first significant outputs were the A1chieve study conducted in 2013 and the DiabCare India survey conducted in 2014. Progress was slow, with only gradual increases in surveys, registry data, and qualitative studies conducted after 2017. While there was a moderate increase in research publications and initiatives from 2019 onwards, the evidence base remains heavily dominated by studies conducted in urban and tertiary care settings, indicating that comprehensive data from rural PHC are still lacking.

Critical Appraisal

No formal risk of bias assessment was performed according to scoping review practice. However, we documented methodological limitations and advantages of the included studies during charting. For instance, although registry studies yield high-quality quantitative data, qualitative studies add richness to the understanding of contextual barriers.

Discussion

Main Findings in Context

This scoping review demonstrates that therapeutic inertia (TI) in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) remains a persistent and multifactorial barrier to optimal glycaemic control in India, particularly within primary healthcare (PHC) settings. Across the included studies, delays in treatment intensification were consistently reported, typically ranging from 12 to 24 months globally42,43 and approximately 15–18 months in Indian settings.16,44,45

Reported HbA1c levels at the time of treatment intensification were frequently above recommended targets, often exceeding 9% in Indian studies. A descriptive comparison indicates broadly comparable delays between Indian and global studies; however, differences in healthcare infrastructure, access, and patient-related factors were observed across settings. This review also adopts a focused definition of therapeutic inertia as delay in initiation or escalation of pharmacological therapy despite evidence of inadequate glycaemic control, distinguishing it from the broader concept of clinical inertia.

Comparison with Global Evidence

Findings from international studies consistently report delays in treatment intensification and their association with adverse clinical outcomes, including cardiovascular and microvascular complications.42,43 Large observational studies have documented substantial gaps between guideline recommendations and real-world practice.46

In addition, global evidence highlights multiple contributing factors, including patient reluctance, provider-related decision delays, and system-level constraints.47,48 Interventions such as structured care models, digital decision-support systems, and guideline-based algorithms have been described in international settings. These findings are consistent with the patterns identified in this review, where similar delays and contributing factors were observed across studies.49

Guidelines from ADA/EASD (2015, 2018) emphasise early and patient-centred treatment intensification,50,51 although persistent hyperglycaemia continues to drive complications.52 These findings are consistent with the patterns identified in this review, where similar delays and contributing factors were observed across studies.

Therapeutic Inertia in the Indian Context

The Indian evidence base reflects similar patterns of delayed treatment intensification, with additional contextual challenges. Studies report a high proportion of patients with suboptimal glycaemic control, including elevated HbA1c levels at the time of therapy escalation.44,45 Patient-level factors such as fear of insulin, cost concerns, and limited awareness were frequently reported.44,53 India’s large diabetes burden further compounds this issue.54 Provider-level factors included concerns regarding hypoglycaemia and delayed treatment modification.55 System-level constraints, including limited availability of medications, diagnostic facilities, and workforce shortages, were also described.56

Provider-level factors included concerns regarding hypoglycaemia, conservative treatment approaches, and delayed therapy modification.44,56,57 System-level constraints, including limited availability of medications, diagnostic facilities, workforce shortages, and inadequate training of PHC staff, were also described.58–60

Most available studies are based in urban and tertiary care settings, with limited representation of rural PHC systems. This indicates a gap in context-specific evidence for primary care settings in India.

Health and Economic Implications

Delayed treatment intensification has been associated with an increased burden of diabetes-related complications, including nephropathy, retinopathy, and cardiovascular disease.42,43 Evidence from both global and Indian studies reports higher complication rates among patients with prolonged delays in therapy escalation.

Economic implications have been more extensively documented in international studies, where therapeutic inertia is associated with increased healthcare utilisation and costs. Although Indian data are limited, similar patterns may be expected given the high out-of-pocket expenditure and limited insurance coverage.9

Policy and Programmatic Implications

The findings highlight the need for strengthening diabetes management strategies within PHC systems in India. Existing national programs, such as NPCDCS, identify gaps related to drug availability, diagnostic access, and healthcare workforce capacity. International models, including structured care programs and coordinated management approaches, provide potential frameworks that may be adapted to the Indian context.61 Evidence from global burden studies in Europe, Turkey, and Kazakhstan further emphasises the importance of data-driven policy interventions.62–65 Strengthening guideline implementation, improving access to essential medicines and diagnostics, and enhancing provider training may support timely treatment intensification.

Future Research Directions

This review identifies several gaps in the current evidence base. There is limited research focused on PHC settings, particularly in rural areas. Future studies should include longitudinal cohort designs to assess delays in treatment intensification and associated outcomes.

Interventional studies evaluating digital decision-support tools, structured care models, and task-sharing approaches are also needed. Additionally, economic evaluations and studies exploring behavioural and sociocultural determinants of therapeutic inertia would provide further insights.

Strengths and Limitations

This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of global and Indian evidence on therapeutic inertia in T2DM, with a focus on primary healthcare settings. It includes diverse study designs and highlights key patient-, provider-, and system-level factors. However, several limitations should be considered. The available Indian evidence is limited and largely derived from studies conducted between 2006 and 2014, which may not fully reflect current practice. Many studies were conducted in urban or tertiary care settings, limiting generalisability to rural PHC contexts.

Additionally, the search was restricted to major international databases (Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed) and English-language publications, which may have excluded relevant national or regional studies. Variability in study design and reporting across included studies should also be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

This scoping review highlights that therapeutic inertia remains a persistent barrier to optimal glycaemic control in T2DM, particularly within Indian primary healthcare settings. Delays in treatment intensification and elevated HbA1c levels were consistently reported across both global and Indian studies, with additional context-specific challenges in India. Key contributors include patient-, provider-, and system-level factors, compounded by gaps in PHC infrastructure and evidence. Addressing these barriers through targeted interventions and context-specific research is essential to improve diabetes management outcomes in India.
