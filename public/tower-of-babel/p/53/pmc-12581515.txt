Diffusely metastatic glioblastoma with FGFR3::TACC3 fusion: cell-free DNA fragmentation analyses and molecular characterization of matched primary and metastatic tumor sites
Authors: Miguel A Hernandez-Rovira, Alicia Vagnozzi, Tyler Bales, Keerthana N Prabhu, Noushin Niknafs, Milan Chheda, Jiayi Huang, Albert H Kim, Michelle Miller-Thomas, Omar Butt, Katie D Vo, Bhargavi S Sovani, Ashwin Singh Parihar, Suzanne Crumley, Sonika Dahiya, Dimitrios Mathios
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtracranial metastasis of IDH-wildtype glioblastoma is very rare and poorly understood at the molecular level. We report a case of FGFR3::TACC3 fusion IDH-wildtype glioblastoma in a 61-year-old male, whose preoperative blood sample showed highly aberrant cfDNA fragmentation patterns, which could be suggestive of early systemic dissemination, undetected by standard-of-care imaging of his body. Eleven months post-resection and adjuvant therapy, he developed widespread extracranial metastases. Comprehensive molecular profiling of matched primary and metastatic tumors revealed broadly conserved genomic, transcriptomic, and copy number landscapes, with the metastasis harboring an additional ERCC6 deletion and enriched expression of receptor tyrosine kinase signaling genes. These findings provide rare insight into the genetic continuity and evolution underlying IDH-wildtype glioblastoma metastasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-025-02142-5.Keywords: Glioblastoma, cfDNA, Fragmentation, Liquid biopsy, Metastasis, FGFR3::TACC3, Systematic review

Introduction

IDH-wildtype glioblastoma is the most common primary malignant tumor of the CNS with an exceedingly aggressive course and a median survival of 15 months [37]. Despite its highly aggressive and invasive nature, IDH-wildtype glioblastoma usually remains confined to the CNS. It is estimated that only 0.4–0.5% of patients experience extracranial metastasis [35], with the most common sites being to bone, lung, lymph nodes, and liver [8].

The rarity of systemic metastases despite the almost universal brain invasion and occasional craniospinal metastasis in IDH-wildtype glioblastoma is puzzling and has not been well-characterized. It is hypothesized that short patient survival and the unique brain microenvironment supporting the growth of these tumors may play a major role in spatially confining the tumor in the CNS [43]. Additionally, the route of metastasis is unknown. Researchers have identified circulating tumor cells in IDH-wildtype glioblastoma patients, suggesting hematogenous spread [47]. It is believed that surgical intervention represents the greatest risk factor for metastatic spread due to blood–brain barrier disruption; however, distant metastasis and circulating tumor cells have been observed in the absence of any surgical intervention [3, 34]. The recent discovery of CNS lymphatic vessels also creates the potential for lymphatic spread of intracranial to peripheral tissues [29], especially in the context of noted lymphatic remodeling in cases of intracranial gliomas [17]. Given the primary drainage of lymphatics through the dura, dura-adherent tumors (as in our patient) may find this a suitable route for metastasis.

Given the rarity of metastatic IDH-wildtype glioblastoma—particularly with FGFR3::TACC3 fusion and primitive neuronal features—and the limited molecular data from matched primary and metastatic sites, we detail the clinical course, treatment, preoperative liquid biopsy findings, and comprehensive molecular profiling of both the primary brain tumor and a pulmonary lymph node metastasis.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old male presented (Fig. 1) with an acutely worsening headache, as well as two months of word-finding difficulty, decreased language comprehension, and right homonymous hemianopsia. An MRI of his brain showed a 6.5 cm enhancing temporal mass with central necrosis and vasogenic edema extending to the left lateral ventricle, highly concerning for glial neoplasm (Fig. 2A). A CT of his chest, abdomen, and pelvis was negative for any concerning masses at the time. Immediately prior to surgery, following induction of anesthesia, a preoperative blood draw was collected for cfDNA analysis. Given the size of the tumor and amount of brain swelling, an asleep craniotomy to remove the bulk of the tumor, followed by an awake craniotomy the next day for language mapping and removal of the tumor edge was performed (Fig. 2B), leading to a near total resection of the mass except for the ependymal enhancement component.

Histopathology showed an extensively necrotic tumor, demonstrating the high-grade glial nature of the lesion. The tumor exhibited significant pleiomorphism, brisk mitotic activity, and robust microvascular proliferation (Fig. 3A and B). There was an infiltrative growth pattern, including extensive adherence to and involvement of the dura (Fig. 3C). A subset of tumor cells had a poorly differentiated embryonal appearance. Immunohistochemical stains showed strong GFAP positivity, confirming the glial nature of the tumor (Fig. 3D). IDH1 (R132H) was wild type by immunohistochemistry, and ATRX expression was retained, suggestive of a CNS WHO grade 4 IDH-wildtype glioblastoma. Whole-genome next-generation Illumina sequencing confirmed the IDH1/2 wild-type status, and revealed an FGFR3::TACC3 fusion, + 7/−10 chromosomal alteration, TERT promoter mutation (c.-124C > T), and a likely pathogenic TP53 V218L mutation. MGMT promoter methylation was absent.

The patient began radiation therapy with 60 Gy in 30 fractions delivered over 39 days with concomitant temozolomide. Given a concurrent testicular infection managed with a ten-day course of levofloxacin, adjuvant chemotherapy with temozolomide was interrupted and completed after completing the antibiotic course. Approximately four months after diagnosis, MRI revealed interval marked vasogenic edema, consistent with pseudoprogression, and the patient was started on four cycles of bevacizumab 7.5 mg/kg every two weeks. The fourth cycle was complicated by hypertension and osteomyelitis of the right great toe with subsequent amputation, preventing further treatment with bevacizumab.

The patient remained stable until 9.6 months after diagnosis, when he presented to the emergency department after acutely developing slurred speech and confusion. A brain MRI nine days prior revealed progression of irregular and peripherally enhancing lesions in the white matter of the left peritrigonal parietal deep region and the periventricular left inferior temporal lobe (Fig. 2C). As part of the stroke protocol, a CT angiogram captured extensive lymphadenopathy of the mediastinum and right hilar lung mass concerning for concomitant lung cancer. Endobronchial ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration of an enlarged subcarinal lymph node revealed numerous discohesive and pleomorphic malignant cells with enlarged nuclei and scant cytoplasm, as well as nuclear molding, necrosis, and apoptosis (Fig. 3E-I). While clinical suspicion was initially high for synchronous lung carcinoma, immunohistochemical evaluation of the lymph node biopsy was negative for the neuroendocrine markers chromogranin and INSM1 and the lung marker TTF-1. Instead, GFAP staining was diffuse and strong (Fig. 3J), consistent with metastasis of the patient’s primary IDH-wildtype glioblastoma. Whole-genome next-generation Illumina sequencing of the subcarinal lymph node redemonstrated IDH1/2-wild-type status, + 7/−10 chromosomal alterations, TERT promoter c.-124C > T mutation, TP53 V218L mutation, and the FGFR3::TACC3 fusion. The genetic profiles of both lesions were nearly identical apart from an additional ERCC6 deletion in the metastatic lesion, as shown in Table 1, although there were additional genes identified with changes in expression of over 20% when comparing the primary to the metastatic lesion, as shown in Table 2. Gene set enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes revealed significant expression changes in receptor tyrosine kinase genes, particularly those associated with the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway (Fig. 4). Interestingly, expression of the PIK3CA gene, was low in both the primary and metastatic samples (in the 11th and 1st percentiles of the analyzed Caris Life Sciences® samples), which suggests an attempt by the tumor cells to compensate for the overactivation of the receptor tyrosine kinase via negative feedback. TACC3 expression in the primary and metastatic tissues was in the 98th percentile, again consistent with the known mechanism of FGFR3::TACC3 fusions, where loss of the FGFR3 3’ untranslated region leads to uncontrolled expression [25].

FDG PET/CT obtained for staging revealed widespread metastatic disease involving the axial and appendicular skeleton, mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes, and the lungs (Fig. 5). The patient was prescribed CCNU (lomustine) 110 mg/m2 every 6 weeks, of which he received the first dose. At 11.6 months from diagnosis, the patient presented to the emergency room with bruising, increased lethargy, shortness of breath, and pain. The complete blood count showed a low hemoglobin level (7.1), for which the patient was transfused. The MRI was stable compared to the prior one. During the hospitalization, the patient expressed a desire to transition to hospice care, prompting discharge to a skilled nursing facility. The patient passed away exactly 12 months from the initial date of presentation. Autopsy revealed mildly decreased brain mass (1200 g, normal 1250–1400 g) with residual high-grade glioma in the left temporal lobe in a background of marked treatment effect. Notably, the residual tumor demonstrated dural invasion.

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) fragmentation analyses

Preoperative blood draw was collected in cfDNA Streck tubes from the current patient and 53 additional patients with untreated IDH-wildtype glioblastoma. All blood draws were collected right after induction of anesthesia and well before any skin incision was made. cfDNA extraction and genomic library preparation were performed following the protocol described in Mathios et al., 2025 [32]. Shallow (1–2 × coverage) whole genome sequencing of the cfDNA library from each sample was carried out on the Illumina NovaseqX platform. DELFI (DNA EvaLuation of Fragments for early Interception) and ARTEMIS (Analysis of RepeaT EleMents in dISease) cfDNA genomic features—such as short-to-long fragment ratios, coverage, z-scores of chromosomal arm changes, repeat elements, and epigenetic features—were calculated as described [32], and the locked machine-learning model presented in Mathios et al. was used to calculate ARTEMIS-DELFI scores for each patient in a cohort of patients with IDH-wildtype glioblastoma prospectively collected through our CNS Tumor Bank. ARTEMIS-DELFI scores, thus, compare genome-wide differences in fragmentation profiles among the patients in the cohort and represent the probability of a patient having brain cancer. Due to the low availability of circulating tumor DNA in comparison to leukocyte cfDNA, ARTEMIS-DELFI scores have low sensitivity for specific genetic alterations. This includes chromosomal alterations: despite the inclusion of arm changes as a feature in the ARTMEIS-DELFI model, the identified z-scores for chromosomes 7 and 10 (7p: − 2.235222528, 7q: − 0.793902807, 10p: 0.175591263, 10q: − 1.852855960). This patient’s blood draw was analyzed and plotted against all the other patients in the cohort. The ARTEMIS-DELFI score was found to be the highest among all other IDH-wildtype glioblastoma samples (0.95 vs median score: 0.49, Fig. 6) and close to 1, the score representing absolute certainty that the patient has brain cancer.

Boxplot showing ARTEMIS-DELFI score distribution of IDH-wildtype glioblastoma samples, with the current patient sample with the highest score in the cohort highlighted in red

Our patient’s highly elevated ARTEMIS-DELFI score may reflect more than an increased tumor burden. It can be hypothesized that with multiple tumor sites and escape from the blood–brain barrier, this would increase the ctDNA contribution and increase the probability of brain cancer being detected, encapsulated in a higher ARTEMIS-DELFI score. If this is true, serial liquid biopsy analysis could provide valuable prognostic information and aid in risk stratification, informing decisions about whether to reoperate or proceed directly to systemic therapy. A significant limitation remains, however, in that the ARTEMIS-DELFI score currently does not provide insight into specific genetic changes [32]. It is, therefore, not possible to track specific genetic alterations throughout the clinical course.

Discussion

Herein, we present a case of diffusely metastatic IDH-wildtype glioblastoma harboring an FGFR3::TACC3 fusion. This molecular alteration is estimated to be present in ~ 3% of IDH-wildtype glioblastomas and arises from the fusion of the FGFR3 N-terminus to the TACC3 C-terminus. The most common variants of this fusion involve splicing of 5’ FGFR3 exon 17 to different exons of 3’ TACC3. This fusion results in the elimination of the FGFR3 3’ untranslated region, eliminating gene regulation by miR-99a and leading to uncontrolled expression. The fusion induces constitutive activation of the receptor tyrosine kinase domain of the FGFR3 component of the fusion protein, activating FGF pathway signaling. The TACC3 domain is believed to promote fusion protein dimerization, autophosphorylation and FGFR3 activation [25]. The prominence of FGFR::TACC fusions in various cancers, combined with a relatively well-understood mechanism of action provides an opportunity for personalized medicine in the form of FGF receptor inhibitors such as erdafitinib, a small molecule that inhibits the tyrosine kinase domain of FGFR1-4 [28]. This medication was considered for our patient, but was not administered in light of the well-controlled intracranial disease with more traditional chemotherapy regimens and the patient’s decision to pursue hospice after he was found to have metastatic IDH-wildtype glioblastoma. A prior case of metastatic IDH-wildtype glioblastoma to the cervical lymph nodes with FGFR3::TACC3 fusion exists in the literature, with the authors noting a very similar molecular profile across all lesions that included an additional PDZT2-TERT fusion of unknown clinical significance and a + 7/− 10 chromosomal arm signature [22].

Extracranial metastasis is an exceedingly rare presentation of IDH-wildtype glioblastoma. The phenomenon of systemic metastases of a tumor known to be confined to the CNS has not been thoroughly studied, and the only known facts about it stem from association studies indicating a younger median age and longer overall survival in patients with metastatic IDH-wildtype glioblastoma compared to non-metastatic cases. Our patient survived twelve months, which is mildly below the mean overall survival (OS) for IDH-wildtype glioblastoma (15 months) [37] and lower compared to the overall survival of FGFR::TACC fusion IDH-wildtype glioblastomas (26.7 months) [31]. Likewise, prior case series have reported greater OS for IDH-wildtype glioblastoma patients with extracranial metastases [35]. Explanations for this observation remain inconclusive, yet longer survival may allow more time for malignant cells to develop appropriate adaptations to survive in environments outside the CNS. This theory remains disputed, and others suggest metastatic potential is determined concurrently with disease initiation [35]. The very close similarity of the genetics of the primary pre-treated tumor with the patient’s lymph node metastasis points to a common ancestor, with perhaps the accumulation of more epigenetic rather than genetic changes, allowing for the tumor cells to develop a metastatic niche over time outside the CNS. Highly aberrant cfDNA fragmentation profiles, as implied by their highly increased ARTEMIS-DELFI score, in the preoperative blood draw from this patient, support the notion that hematogenous spread of this tumor had already occurred at the time of presentation. GSEA analysis of gene expression profiles [10] of the matched tissues indicated the pathways that differ in the two tumor locations are involved in receptor tyrosine kinase signaling and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. This pathway is involved in the regulation of cell growth via promotion of macromolecule and organelle synthesis, as well as the management of cellular homeostatic activity [42]. Because we have not analyzed tumor tissue from the brain at time of patient’s death it is difficult to ascertain whether these adaptation pathways are related to the treatment the patient received during his disease course or these pathways are providing a survival advantage to the exiting the nervous system tumor cells, allowing them to survive in the hostile environment of other immune rich organs in the body.

The genetic analysis of our paired samples indicates little genetic adaptation of the tumor cells found in the mediastinal lymph node with the only new genetic abnormality being ERCC6 deletion. ERCC6 is found on chromosome 10 and encodes for the CSB protein involved in DNA repair. CSB is a rare autosomal recessive disorder associated with postnatal neurodegeneration and growth failure. The ERCC6 gene plays a crucial role in the transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair (NER) pathway, which specifically targets and repairs DNA damage in actively transcribed genes. The assay for clinical sequencing of the tumor specimens includes a 221-gene panel of cancer-relevant genes. No new somatic nucleotide variants were detected in the metastatic site biopsy specimen compared to sequencing of the primary tumor, and the tumor mutational burden remained stable at 2 mutations/MB between the two specimens, suggesting that loss of ERCC6 may be a passenger aberration and its function is not necessary or sufficient to lead to a hypermutator phenotype.

Our patient’s lower survival is likely multifactorial and was potentially contributed to by the incomplete treatment with temozolomide chemotherapy due to the patient’s orchitis requiring antibiotic treatment. While this was the appropriate course of action considering the patient’s concurrent orchitis, it may have had negative effects on long term survival compared to following standard of care [46].

The tumor microanatomy was characterized by dural adhesion, which was observed in an additional case in the literature [49], with two additional cases presenting with adherence to metastatic sites [2, 33]. Notably, our patient’s tumor displayed a primitive neuronal component, which has been observed in prior cases of dura-adherent IDH-wildtype glioblastoma [20, 39, 49].

Our patient’s intracranial tumor was very vascular, with various thrombosed veins observed entering the tumor and multiple vessels observed during tumor dissection. The tumor was likewise in close proximity to the sigmoid sinus, a feature shared by four additional cases in the literature [24, 41, 51, 52]. Increased vascularity and proximity to the sigmoid sinus may suggest the potential for hematogenous spread, as prior reports have described the presence of GFAP-positive cells in the peripheral blood of IDH-wildtype glioblastoma patients [34]. This notion is strengthened by the highly aberrant cfDNA fragmentation signature in the preoperative blood draw of this patient at the time of initial diagnosis. There is a dearth of studies exploring the possibility of lymphatic spread, however, the multitude of reports describing lymph node metastasis complement the observations in our patient. In our patient, there were multiple intensely FDG-avid lymph nodes along the upper mediastinal, paratracheal, prevascular, subcarinal, and bilateral hilar stations, encompassing a significant portion of the lymphatic drainage to the lungs. This presentation may suggest concomitant lymphatic in addition to hematogenous spread.

To further characterize the behavior of metastatic IDH-wildtype glioblastomas, we performed a comprehensive review of the literature to identify prior cases. We identified 35 reports containing a total of 50 unique cases (Supp Table 1) [1, 2, 4–7, 9, 12–16, 18, 19, 21, 23, 24, 26, 27, 30, 33, 35, 36, 38, 40, 41, 44, 45, 48–55]. Following the exclusion of two cases without clinical information, we identified 37 male patients and 11 female patients. 47 patients underwent surgery, with 17 confirmed gross total resections and eight confirmed subtotal resections. Likewise, most patients underwent adjuvant radiation (n = 29) and chemotherapy (n = 32). A significant number (n = 21) of patients experienced intracranial recurrence, oftentimes preceding or identified concurrently with metastasis. Most patients experienced local recurrence, with a second subset experiencing limited recurrence in an adjacent lobe. Two patients experienced multifocal recurrence throughout the brain parenchyma. The median time from diagnosis to metastasis was 17 months, with the most common sites for metastasis, aside from the lungs, being the liver, pancreas, vertebral and sacro-iliac bones, and hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes.

Limitations

The additional cases included in our study were obtained from case reports or case series, which limits the rigor of the conclusions drawn. Likewise, many of the reports included were published prior to 2021, when the modern guidelines for the definition of IDH-wildtype glioblastoma were detailed. Given that only a minority of studies presented molecular information, it is impossible to determine whether these older cases fit the current definition of IDH-wildtype glioblastoma based on the information provided. This limited the number of cases available with which to properly describe the population. Lastly, two cases did not have the full text available; as such, no clinical information could be gathered from them. Finally, we were unable to compare molecular data between the CNS and lymph node tumors at the time of death; however, we did observe remarkable similarity between the evolved primary tumor and the lymph node tumor after therapy.

Conclusion

We here present an unusual case of diffusely metastatic IDH-wildtype glioblastoma with an FGFR3::TACC3 fusion and primitive neuronal features in the absence of intracranial disease progression. This case presents unique features compared to the one FGFR3::TACC3 IDH-wildtype glioblastoma case presented recently in the literature, as our case was accompanied by histological features of primitive neuronal differentiation that are known to be associated with leptomeningeal spread but not-extra-CNS manifestation. We provide evidence to support the notion that spread of cells to extracranial sites through the circulation is occurring at the time of initial presentation and not due to mechanical reasons related to surgical removal. Liquid biopsy analyses in the blood of patients with IDH-wildtype glioblastoma can provide a non-invasive assessment of hematogenous spread in this case. Extensive molecular characterization of both the intracranial and metastatic lesions in our patient, showed similar genetic profiles further supporting the theory of hematogenous dissemination at the time of presentation. Epigenetic and metabolic/immune adaptations are likely to be contributing to development of metastatic foci during the patient’s treatment allowing tumor cells that exclusively thrive within the CNS to adapt to a non-CNS environment.

Likewise, this finding implies minimal selection pressure from medical therapies employed. This case reaffirms and emphasizes the potential for metastatic spread of IDH-wildtype glioblastoma, hints to potential tumor microenvironment, tumor intrinsic and anatomic characteristics of tumors with elevated potential to exhibit this unusual behavior and provides insights into the molecular differences between the primary brain tumor and metastatic sites.
