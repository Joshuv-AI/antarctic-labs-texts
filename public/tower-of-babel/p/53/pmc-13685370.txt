Neglected tropical diseases in the Arabian Peninsula: a systematic review of leishmaniasis and rickettsioses
Authors: Shuaibu Abdullahi Hudu, Mohamed Soliman, Naglaa M Shalaby, Emad A Morad, Ghusun M Alhazimi, Abdulgafar Olayiwola Jimoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVector-borne neglected tropical diseases are still an underrecognized public health problem in the Arabian Peninsula. Of these, leishmaniasis is endemic in multiple regions, while the epidemiology of rickettsial infections remains incompletely understood. This systematic review synthesizes existing evidence on the epidemiology, distribution, and surveillance of these two diseases in the region.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines, a systematic review was performed. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, EMBASE, and relevant grey literature for studies published from January 2000 to December 2025 that describe human cases of leishmaniasis or rickettsioses in countries of the Arabian Peninsula. Two reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data, and assessed methodological quality (risk of bias) using validated tools.ResultsA total of 44 studies were included. The majority of studies investigated leishmaniasis (n = 31), with fewer on rickettsioses (n = 9) or mixed febrile illness (n = 4). Most publications originated from Saudi Arabia. In the Arabian Peninsula, particularly in Saudi Arabia, cutaneous leishmaniasis remains endemic, with localized transmission hotspots and the coexistence of multiple Leishmania species, including L. major and L. tropica. Evidence for rickettsial infections remains sparse, primarily derived from case reports and serologic surveys.ConclusionLeishmaniasis remains a public health challenge in the Arabian Peninsula, particularly in Saudi Arabia. In contrast, rickettsioses are likely underdiagnosed due to limited surveillance and diagnostic capacity. Improved, integrated surveillance systems, paired with febrile illness investigations that include diagnostic testing for rickettsial pathogens, will lead to fewer missed cases, contributing to disease detection and control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14012-z.Keywords: Arabian Peninsula, Leishmaniasis, Neglected tropical diseases, Rickettsioses, Vector-borne diseases

Background

Neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) remain a persistent public health challenge in many parts of the world, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. Among these, vector-borne infections such as leishmaniasis and rickettsioses are increasingly recognized as important yet underreported causes of morbidity in the Arabian Peninsula [1–3]. Despite the ecological suitability for transmission and increasing regional mobility, the epidemiology of these two diseases in the region remains poorly characterized.

Arthropod-borne diseases account for a significant portion of the NTD burden, especially in arid and semi-arid regions, where epidemiologically important vectors exhibit impressive ecological adaptability [4, 5]. Throughout the Arabian Peninsula, landscape alterations and population mobility, combined with rapid urbanization and environmental changes, including desertification, human migration across borders, and climate variability, have contributed to modifications in vector breeding sites and changes in human–vector exposure [6, 7]. Although regional control activities have inevitably prioritized ‘headline’ vector-borne diseases, such as malaria and dengue, relatively little attention has been paid to other high-morbidity endemic infections, such as leishmaniasis and rickettsioses, both of which can be transmitted in outbreak settings and may be difficult for clinicians to recognize.

Leishmaniasis, a protozoal infection caused by Leishmania species and transmitted by infected Phlebotomine sand flies, remains endemic in some Arabian countries [8, 9]. Cutaneous form is the most common clinical type, although visceral leishmaniasis has been reported occasionally [10]. In parallel, emerging infections caused by obligate intracellular bacteria, namely rickettsiae, transmitted mainly by ticks and fleas, are increasingly recognized as significant yet neglected pathogens among patients with acute febrile illnesses. Their lack of pathognomonic symptoms, limited availability of advanced diagnostic resources, and low clinical suspicion predispose these infections to substantial underdiagnosis.

Although localized epidemiological studies and sporadic outbreak reports are available, a comprehensive synthesis of evidence on the burden, distribution, and determinants of leishmaniasis and rickettsioses in Arabia remains lacking. This deficiency is particularly worrying in view of the region’s specific epidemiological context, which includes mass events and foci of rural–urban development, as well as ecological changes that may affect vector distribution and disease transmission dynamics. Filling these knowledge gaps is critical for both enhancing surveillance and guidance on targeted control measures and for evidence-based public health policy.

Given the scarcity of information on epidemiological aspects and the geographic distribution of leishmaniasis and rickettsioses in the Arabian Peninsula, this systematic review was conducted to gather data and provide measurable insights into these neglected diseases. This systematic review focuses specifically on leishmaniasis and rickettsioses, two vector-borne NTDs with ecological plausibility and emerging epidemiological signals in the Arabian Peninsula.

Methodology

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420261297312. Included studies had to present human data on leishmaniasis or rickettsioses from populations living in and/or exposed to the countries of the Arabian Peninsula: Saudi Arabia, Yemen, Oman, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Bahrain, and Kuwait. Eligible studies for review included observational articles, surveillance reports, outbreak investigations, hospital-based epidemiological reports, and diagnostic accuracy studies, and were used to estimate disease burden. Although the inclusion criteria focused on studies published between 2000 and 2025, earlier surveillance data were referenced only to provide historical epidemiological context for long-term transmission patterns. Studies published in English were included. Articles published in Arabic were also considered when a reliable translation was available. Studies involving only animal hosts or arthropod vectors were excluded unless they contained direct evidence of pathogen detection relevant to human transmission. Entomological or reservoir studies that reported only ecological observations without pathogen identification or linkage to human infection were excluded.

An extensive literature search was conducted across several databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and EMBASE. To reduce publication bias and obtain locally relevant data, the search also included grey literature: reports from the World Health Organization Eastern Mediterranean Regional Office, country Ministry of Health epidemiological bulletins, and Index Medicus for the Eastern Mediterranean Region. Search strategies used both controlled vocabulary terms and synonyms, including those for leishmaniasis, rickettsioses, vector-borne diseases, and the Arabian nations. All identified records were imported into reference management software, and duplicates were removed prior to screening. The selection process was documented following a PRISMA flow diagram, including reasons for exclusion. The complete search strategies for each database are provided in Appendix S1. Because many included studies reported overlapping epidemiological findings, Supplementary Table S1 presents representative studies selected to illustrate the principal evidence base across countries, diseases, and study designs.

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against predefined eligibility criteria. Studies were included if they reported human epidemiological data on leishmaniasis or rickettsioses in the Arabian Peninsula. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by a third-party adjudicator.

A risk-of-bias assessment was performed for all included studies using validated tools appropriate to their study designs. Study-level risk-of-bias assessments for representative studies are presented in Supplementary Table S2, whereas Fig. 4 provides an aggregate graphical summary of risk-of-bias domains across included studies.

Graphical summary of risk-of-bias assessment across included studies

Data synthesis was predominantly descriptive because of the expected diversity in study designs, outcome measures, and diagnostic methods. The quantitative summary, based on pooled estimates of incidence or prevalence with corresponding confidence intervals, was possible when homogeneity was deemed adequate. The results were classified by disease category, country, and study period to enable spatial and temporal mapping of the disease burden in the Arabian Peninsula. Because the review was conducted only with publicly available data, no formal ethical approval was required. Formal assessment of publication bias was not conducted due to the absence of a meta-analysis and substantial heterogeneity; grey literature searches were used to mitigate reporting bias. A formal GRADE assessment was not performed. Overall certainty of evidence was considered moderate for leishmaniasis and low for rickettsioses.

Results

Study selection

In total, 1,146 records were retrieved from database searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase. Sixty-four additional records were identified from grey literature sources (WHO-EMRO) and the national Ministry of Health publications. Following the removal of 312 duplicates, 898 records remained for title and abstract screening. A total of 742 records were excluded as non-relevant to the target diseases, lacking human data, or not relevant to the population studied. Full-text review was performed on 156 articles, of which 112 were excluded due to non-epidemiological focus, only animal data available, or lack of reporting for outcomes. In the end, 44 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The largest number of studies were from Saudi Arabia (n = 26), followed by Yemen (n = 8), Oman (n = 5), and the United Arab Emirates (n = 3). No human epidemiological studies that satisfied all the included criteria were found in Qatar, Bahrain, or Kuwait. Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using validated tools appropriate to each study design: the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for observational studies; the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist for prevalence studies; and the QUADAS-2 tool for diagnostic accuracy studies. In general, the risk of bias was considered moderate across studies, primarily due to non-probability sampling methods and insufficient reporting of study populations. Epidemiology of leishmaniasis in the Arabian Peninsula

Leishmaniasis, especially cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), is endemic in Saudi Arabia and represents an important public health problem for the country despite effective national control efforts. Tens of thousands of CL cases were identified across the country under historical surveillance (1986–92), with Leishmania major and L. tropica responsible for most cases, transmitted by sand flies (Phlebotomus papatasi and P. sergenti) and involving rodent reservoir hosts, particularly Psammomys obesus and Meriones libycus, while humans serve as important reservoirs for anthroponotic transmission of L. tropica. During this period, 83,947 cases of CL and 1881 cases of visceral leishmaniasis (VL) were recorded, with regional variation across Saudi Arabia [11, 12]. CL predominated in the central and southern regions, while VL was observed predominantly in the south among younger individuals [11]. Enrolled vector control, along with other control measures, prevented CL at the local level, but low-level disease transmission persisted in several regions [10].

This latter assertion remains valid, as subsequent work continues to support the concept of endemic transmission in Saudi Arabian regions. Data from the Ministry of Health for 2020–2022 were analyzed at the national level, and a total of 2,280 CL cases were detected, with notable regional variability [13]. The highest incidence rates were observed in the Qassim, Aseer, Ha’il, and Madinah regions, with non-Saudis accounting for nearly 87% of cases during the study period [12]. The disproportionate number of cases among non-Saudi persons (87%) probably also reflects the demographic composition of the workforce in Saudi Arabia, where most of the resident population comprises migrant workers. A large proportion of migrant workers have outdoor-exposure occupations, such as construction, agricultural work, and animal handling, which may increase their contact with sandfly vectors and reservoir hosts. Migrant populations might also experience barriers to accessing healthcare and delayed diagnosis, leading to an underestimation of disease burden in this population. Cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) remains prevalent in both rural and urban settings, and the disease predominantly affects young adults, with rural exposure and proximity to rodent-infested environments reported as major risk factors [14]. The district of Al-Ahsa had more than 245 confirmed CL cases between 2017 and 2023; L. major was the most abundant species (75% of lesions were predominantly clinically identified), and rural residency and the presence of rodents/rats around residences were among the most frequently reported exposures [14].

Molecular studies from the Qassim region showed that 49.5% of the skin biopsies were positive for L. major; 28.6% for L. tropica, and 3.9% for L. infantum/donovani complexes. This shows species diversity and a potential risk of atypical clinical forms in Saudi Arabia [15]. Based on World Health Organization (WHO) endemicity maps, cutaneous leishmaniasis is classified as hyperendemic in several regions of Saudi Arabia. CL is classified as hyperendemic in Saudi Arabia, including Al-Hassa, Riyadh, Aseer, Ha’il, and Madinah, while sporadic cases of VL have been reported from Jazan and Asir governorates [16, 17]. A marked decline in reported cutaneous leishmaniasis was observed over time following the implementation of control measures, with persistent low-level transmission in recent years [12]. Temporal trends in reported cases of cutaneous leishmaniasis across the study period are illustrated in Fig. 5. Rickettsial infections in the Arabian Peninsula context

Contrary to leishmaniasis, rickettsioses are less well known among populations in the Arabian Peninsula. Vectors that can transmit Rickettsia species (ticks and fleas) are widespread in the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMRO), but human epidemiological studies from Saudi Arabia, Oman, or Yemen are scarce [18]. A young adult with acute tick-borne spotted fever group (SFG) rickettsial infection, serologically confirmed and doxycycline-responsive, from Saudi Arabia is reported; this represents one of the very few laboratory-confirmed cases of human SFG rickettsiosis acquired in Saudi Arabian soil. The study highlighted limited epidemiological information on local vector distribution and transmission dynamics, as few cases have been reported and clinical suspicion is low [19].

Several Rickettsia species, including R. typhi, R. felis, R. prowazekii, and R. conorii, have been reported in countries within the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region, including countries of the Arabian Peninsula [18]. These findings support the presence of rickettsial pathogens within the region and suggest that rickettsioses may be underrecognized rather than absent. Arguably, the best indirect evidence for rickettsial exposure in the wider peninsula region comes from serosurveys in Dhofar, South Oman, where earlier studies dating back to 2000 demonstrated antibodies to rickettsiae in human sera, indicating continued but unreported circulation of zoonotic pathogens between arthropod vectors and humans with minimal recourse to formal healthcare [20, 21].

Further entomological surveys in other countries of the Arabian Peninsula, for example, the UAE, also identify DNA from SFG Rickettsia in tick species that parasitize dromedaries (Hyalomma dromedarii), further corroborating its role as an additional reservoir and source of competent vectors for pathogen transmission to humans [22, 23]. Nevertheless, population-based surveillance data on rickettsioses in Arabia are scarce; most of our knowledge has come from isolated clinical reports, entomological vector studies, and extrapolation from seroprevalence data in neighboring areas.

Vectors and transmission ecology

For leishmaniasis, vectors are well defined. L. major is transmitted by P. papatasi and L. tropica by P. sergenti in Saudi Arabia. Rodent species (Psammomys obesus and Meriones libycus) are important reservoirs in central and eastern foci, perpetuating zoonotic transmission cycles [11]. In the case of rickettsial infections, ticks like Rhipicephalus, Hyalomma, and others can also act as vectors; serological and PCR research in the wider, however similar to ours, EMRO area evidence many different species of Rickettsia circulate among tick populations, thus indicating these pathogens might be even more widely spread than estimated in humans [23].

Knowledge gaps and surveillance limitations

Although leishmaniasis has been well described throughout Saudi Arabia, there are significant information gaps regarding VL in other Arabian countries and regarding disease characteristics in broader populations. In the case of rickettsioses, systematic population surveys are generally not available, and data are obtained from case reports and seroprevalence studies conducted in nearby areas where specific reporting systems may not exist. The level of evidence for leishmaniasis was moderate, supported by several surveillance and molecular studies, whereas the level of evidence for rickettsioses was low due to limited data from case reports and seroprevalence surveys with restricted geographical coverage.

Discussion

In this regard, the aim of this systematic review is to summarize existing knowledge on leishmaniasis and rickettsioses in the Arabian Peninsula and to provide insights for public health policy. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review synthesizing epidemiological evidence on both leishmaniasis and rickettsioses in the Arabian Peninsula. The results show that although leishmaniasis is well documented as a vector-borne infection in Saudi Arabia and neighboring areas, rickettsioses remain underrecognized in this region despite the region’s conducive ecosystem for their transmission.

Leishmaniasis is still a major public health concern in Saudi Arabia. Evidence of long-term endemic transmission over decades is available from several epidemiological studies of CL in central, southern, and eastern provinces. Tens of thousands of cases were registered by the historical national surveillance system in the Kingdom over several years, highlighting the long-standing nature of CL in Saudi Arabia [11, 24]. More recently, another nationwide observational study reported 2,280 confirmed CL cases from 2020 to 2022, with geographical hotspots in Qassim, Asseer, and Ha’il, and a vastly higher burden among non-Saudi individuals [12]. Molecular studies also indicate that L. major and L. tropica are the principal aetiological agents, reflecting the coexistence of multiple Leishmania species maintained through distinct transmission cycles involving specific sand fly vectors and reservoir hosts [15]. These data align with narrative reviews, which deem CL a neglected disease in predominantly rural areas with occasional urban associations, spread by well-known vectors, i.e., Phlebotomus papatasi and Phlebotomus sergenti [10].

In comparison with leishmaniasis, however, the literature on rickettsioses in the Arabian Peninsula is scarce, with almost exclusive focus on case reports and regional seroprevalence data. A confirmed human case of SFG rickettsiosis was diagnosed in Saudi Arabia following a tick bite [19]. The patient presented with fever, rash, and an eschar, and responded rapidly to doxycycline, consistent with the classical clinical course of a tick-borne rickettsial infection. Moreover, serological surveys in the Dhofar region of Oman have detected antibodies to Rickettsia conorii in 59% of rural participants, suggesting significant exposure among people residing in close proximity to livestock and ticks [20]. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because immunofluorescence assays may cross-react with other spotted fever group rickettsiae, limiting species-specific attribution. Although these observations do not constitute formal national surveillance data, they provide indirect evidence that rickettsial pathogens circulate within the region. Together with entomological findings and isolated clinical reports, these data support the need for expanded surveillance and improved diagnostic capacity. There are limitations to this review, including underreporting of leishmaniasis and rickettsioses, differences in study designs, and the use of surveillance and case-based evidence. Language limitations and the lack of complete regional data may have led to the omission of studies. In addition, studies that focused exclusively on animal reservoirs or arthropod vectors, without accompanying human epidemiological data, were excluded. While this approach maintained a human-health focus, it may have limited a more comprehensive assessment of transmission dynamics within a One Health framework.

Quantitative synthesis was not feasible due to the lack of population-based rickettsial studies. The interpretation of the available epidemiological evidence should take into account important diagnostic limitations. Several studies on leishmaniasis relied in part on clinical diagnosis, which may be subject to misclassification because other dermatological conditions can mimic cutaneous leishmaniasis. Likewise, serological methods used in rickettsial investigations, particularly indirect immunofluorescence assays, may exhibit cross-reactivity among spotted fever group rickettsiae, limiting species-level identification and potentially influencing estimates of exposure.

The differences in the evidence bases for these two diseases reflect broader challenges in public health surveillance. Leishmaniasis and rickettsioses are already recognized as neglected tropical diseases and are subject to formal reporting procedures and control programs for their insect vectors [10, 25]. In contrast, rickettsioses are not commonly considered in the differential diagnosis of acute febrile illnesses and are not reported systematically to national reporting systems. This knowledge gap is surprising in light of tick vectors that transmit several Rickettsia species and serological signals from adjacent human populations indicating active circulation of these pathogens.

Although Saudi Arabia contributed the majority of available evidence, studies from Yemen, Oman, and the United Arab Emirates provide important regional context. Evidence from Yemen confirms the continued endemicity of cutaneous leishmaniasis, while studies from Oman suggest human exposure to spotted fever group rickettsiae. Entomological investigations from the United Arab Emirates have also identified rickettsial DNA in ticks collected from livestock. Collectively, these findings indicate that vector-borne neglected diseases are distributed across multiple countries of the Arabian Peninsula, although surveillance capacity and research output vary considerably between countries.

At the policy level, these outcomes highlight the importance of broad, comprehensive frameworks for vector-borne diseases in the Arabian Peninsula. An enhanced surveillance platform that encompasses leishmaniasis and rickettsioses would enable timely responses and targeted interventions, as well as better estimates of both the burden of illness and the risk factors for infection. Incorporating rickettsial diagnostics into common febrile illness algorithms, particularly in areas with known vector activity and human contact, could facilitate case detection and prevent misdiagnosis. Doxycycline is an effective therapy for most rickettsial infections and is already recommended in algorithms for clinicians treating febrile illness in many locations; refining diagnostic guidance may help reduce unnecessary antibiotic use while providing the best care to patients.

The One Health approach is particularly relevant. Both leishmaniasis and rickettsioses are zoonotic diseases that require One Health collaboration among human, animal, and environmental health programs [26]. For leishmaniasis, this would include integrated surveillance of canine and rodent reservoirs and of adult sand flies [27]. For rickettsioses, given the high seroprevalence in rural communities in Oman and the presence of known vectors, combining livestock health data with human surveillance may improve early warning [28].

Despite the limited number of studies identified, rickettsioses were included because sporadic clinical reports, serological evidence, and entomological data suggest ecological suitability for transmission in the region. Highlighting these data gaps is important to stimulate future epidemiological research. Molecular surveillance may improve understanding of species and strain diversity, vector competence, and how climate change or land-use modification could alter the risk of infection. For leishmaniasis, sustained molecular characterization and long-term monitoring are required to track transmission dynamics and assess the effectiveness of control measures. Beyond informing national and local policies, filling these gaps will expand global knowledge of neglected vector-borne diseases. No formal assessment of reporting bias across studies was conducted. However, the inclusion of grey literature and Ministry of Health reports was intended to reduce the risk of selective publication.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates substantial variability in the evidence base for leishmaniasis and rickettsioses in the Arabian Peninsula. Leishmaniasis, predominantly the cutaneous form, has been well described and is still endemic in several areas, especially in Saudi Arabia, with continuous transmission over decades of control attempts. The presence of different Leishmania species, vectors, and intra-sylvatic reservoirs requires constant vigilance and the adaptation of control measures. Compared with rickettsioses, the regional literature has long neglected them. Overall, the available information is highly suggestive, based mostly on anecdotes from clinical cases, serological surveys, and entomological studies, that these infections are present and underdiagnosed. This knowledge deficit is probably due more to a lack of regular surveillance and poor clinical awareness than to true epidemiological rarity. Filling these gaps will require enhanced, unified surveillance capacities that encompass a broader range of VBDs (e.g., other vectors and pathogens), the integration of rickettsial diagnostics into febrile illness algorithms, and increased collaboration among professionals across human, animal, and environmental health. Focused epidemiological studies with integrated molecular and ecological surveillance are imperative to delineate the true burden of rickettsioses for evidence-based policy. Improving recognition of neglected vector-borne diseases in the Arabian Peninsula is critical to enhancing public health preparedness and regional NTD control programs.
