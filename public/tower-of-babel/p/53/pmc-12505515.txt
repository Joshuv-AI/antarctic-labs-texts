The impact of very-low-calorie ketogenic diets on gut microbiota in individuals with obesity: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shun Wang, Zihan Bao, Ziyang Li, Mengqi Zhao, Xunling Wang, Fenghu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to systematically review and meta-analyze the effects of very-low-calorie ketogenic diets (VLCKD) on gut microbiota in individuals with obesity.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across four electronic databases—PubMed, EBSCOhost, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science—up to June 2025. Outcomes included changes in gut microbial diversity and the relative abundance of key taxa. Subgroup analyses were performed based on body mass index (BMI), age, and intervention duration.ResultsA total of 14 studies were included. Random-effects meta-analysis revealed that VLCKD interventions significantly improved gut microbial α-diversity, as indicated by increased Shannon index (SMD: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.03 to 1.04, P = 0.0378) and Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity (PD) index (SMD: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.36 to 1.18, P = 0.0002). In addition, VLCKD significantly increased the abundance of Akkermansia (SMD: 1.76, 95% CI: 0.48 to 3.03, P = 0.0069) and the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio (SMD: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.67 to 1.34, P < 0.0001), while significantly reducing the probiotic genus Bifidobacterium (SMD: −1.23, 95% CI: −1.81 to −0.64, P < 0.0001). Subgroup analyses indicated that the increase in Shannon index was more pronounced in participants with BMI ≤ 30 kg/m² and age >30 years. Akkermansia showed a greater increase in those with BMI 30–35 kg/m², age >40 years, and intervention duration ≤6 weeks. Conversely, Bifidobacterium abundance declined significantly in individuals with BMI 30–35 kg/m², age >40 years, and within an intervention period ≤12 weeks.ConclusionVLCKD appears to be a promising dietary intervention for modulating gut microbiota in individuals with obesity. However, its bidirectional effects on microbial ecology warrant caution. Future studies should further investigate its long-term safety and explore personalized strategies for microbiota-targeted interventions.KEYWORDS: Very-low-calorie ketogenic diet, obesity, gut microbiota, meta-analysis, systematic review

Introduction

In recent years, the global obesity crisis has become increasingly severe. According to data released by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2024, over one billion individuals worldwide are classified as obese.1 Obesity represents a state of low-grade chronic inflammation2 and is closely associated with numerous chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, certain types of cancer, and mental disorders, substantially increasing the global disease burden.3 Among the various intervention strategies for obesity, dietary control and physical activity are widely recognized as the most common, safe, and effective methods, especially in the context of long-term weight management.4 Compared with single-modality interventions, the combination of scientifically guided nutrition and regular physical exercise is more effective in regulating energy intake and expenditure, improving metabolic indicators, and reducing both body fat and the risk of associated complications.5

The ketogenic diet (KD), a dietary approach that has attracted considerable attention in recent years, is widely adopted by individuals aiming for rapid weight loss due to its potential benefits in fat reduction and metabolic improvement.6 However, several studies have indicated that the classical KD may pose risks to renal function, endocrine regulation, and lipid metabolism, potentially promoting atherosclerosis and triggering health concerns such as “keto flu.” These risks have raised frequent clinical concerns.6–8 With continued advances in dietary and nutritional research, the classical KD has undergone notable refinements. The very-low-calorie ketogenic diet (VLCKD) has emerged as a more structured and controllable variant of the ketogenic approach and is now regarded as a safer and more efficient dietary strategy for weight reduction.9 VLCKD is typically characterized by a total daily caloric intake of less than 800 kcal, including fewer than 50 grams of carbohydrates per day (approximately 13% of total energy), protein intake of 1–1.5 g/kg body weight (about 44%), and fat accounting for around 43% of total energy. This strict carbohydrate restriction drives a metabolic shift from glucose to ketone bodies (β-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate, and acetone) as the body’s primary energy source.10 Such a shift effectively promotes fat oxidation and ketogenesis, thereby contributing to rapid reductions in body fat, improved insulin sensitivity, and better glycemic control in the short term.11 Simultaneously, increased levels of ketone bodies have been associated with enhanced anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity, immune regulation, gut motility and barrier function, cellular growth and differentiation, mineral absorption, and the prevention of distal ulcers, Crohn’s disease, and colorectal cancer.9

However, the gut microbiota (GM), a critical “invisible organ” involved in human metabolism, immunity, and energy regulation, is influenced by a wide range of factors, including genetics, age, environment, diet, and lifestyle.12,13 A substantial body of research has demonstrated that gut dysbiosis is closely linked to metabolic disorders such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.14–16 Among these influencing factors, diet is considered the most important external modulator of the gut microbial ecosystem, capable of rapidly altering microbial diversity, metabolite production, and host inflammatory responses.17 Although existing studies have shown that VLCKD exerts beneficial effects on weight control, insulin resistance, and lipid metabolism,18 its specific impact on the gut microbiota remains inconclusive and, in some aspects, controversial, warranting further investigation9 (Figure 1). In particular, no clear consensus has been reached regarding changes in microbial diversity, the responsiveness of key functional genera, and the mechanisms linking these alterations to metabolic improvement.

The interaction between VLCKD and the gut microbiota.

Therefore, the present study aims to systematically evaluate and meta-analyze the effects of VLCKD on the gut microbiota in individuals with obesity. This work seeks to synthesize current evidence on gut microbial changes following VLCKD intervention and to provide a more precise theoretical basis and practical guidance for nutritional strategies in obesity management.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.19 The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) [ID: CRD420251075467].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligibility was assessed based on a predefined set of inclusion and exclusion criteria under the PICOS framework: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study Design. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) Reviews, meta-analyses, conference abstracts, or theses; (2) Interventions that did not involve a very-low-calorie ketogenic diet (VLCKD); (3) Populations that were not obese; (4) Studies with incomplete outcome data, data that could not be extracted, or those involving interventions combined with dietary supplements, probiotics, or antibiotic treatments; (5) Animal or in vitro studies.

Included studies were randomized or non-randomized crossover or parallel trials investigating the effect of VLCKD on the gut microbiota in obese populations. Additional inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Participants were obese (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m²), regardless of age, sex, or training status; (2) Interventions consisted of a very-low-calorie ketogenic diet. Where additional exercise interventions were included, details such as exercise duration, energy expenditure, or supplement use were reported; (3) The gut microbiota was assessed using metagenomic sequencing or 16S rRNA gene sequencing, with reported alpha diversity indices and relevant microbial measures, and sufficient data available for extraction; (4) Participants were adults aged >18 years; (5) Studies were published in English.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted by two independent reviewers (WS and BZH) across four electronic databases—PubMed, EBSCOhost, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science—for articles published up to June 2025. A repeat search was performed in July 2025 to identify any newly published relevant studies.

Search strategies were designed using Boolean logic and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) combined with free-text terms to ensure comprehensive retrieval. All search terms are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Reference lists of eligible studies and previous reviews were also manually screened to identify additional relevant literature and ensure all subject-specific keywords were captured.

Data extraction

Retrieved articles were imported into EndNote X9 for de-duplication. Two independent reviewers (WS and BZH) screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment. Data were independently extracted using a standardized Excel spreadsheet. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (ZMQ).

Extracted data included: authorship, publication year, country, participant age and sex, sample size, BMI, intervention type and duration, target parameters, and both primary and secondary outcome measures. Primary outcomes focused on changes in alpha and beta diversity of the gut microbiota (note: beta diversity could not be described quantitatively due to lack of available data). Secondary outcomes included changes in the relative abundance of specific bacterial taxa (e.g., Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Akkermansia, Bifidobacterium) and microbial ratios (e.g., Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes). For meta-analysis, means and standard deviations (SD) of outcomes before and after intervention were extracted. When only graphical data were available, numerical values were obtained using WebPlotDigitizer. Authors were contacted for raw data when necessary.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias in included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers (WS and BZH) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool.20 Six domains were evaluated: selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias, and other potential sources of bias. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “high risk,” or “unclear risk.” Disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (LZY).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and figure generation were performed using R software, while study quality assessments were conducted using RevMan 5.3. Effect sizes were calculated using standardized mean differences (SMD). If studies reported standard errors, these were converted to SD using standard formulas.21 When only medians, ranges, or interquartile ranges were reported, mean and SD were estimated using the method proposed by Wan et al.22 A 95% confidence interval (95% CI) was applied in all comparisons. SMD was chosen to standardize effect sizes across studies with differing measurement units and sample sizes, thereby enabling comparison and synthesis of overall intervention effects. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, categorized according to the Cochrane Handbook: 0–40% may not be important; 30–60% may represent moderate heterogeneity; 50–90% may indicate substantial heterogeneity; and 75–100% suggests considerable heterogeneity.20 In cases of high heterogeneity, subgroup analyses and meta-regression were performed to explore potential sources. The evidence of small-study effects was assessed using Egger’s regression asymmetry test,23 and publication bias across studies was evaluated using funnel plot analysis.

Results

A total of 28696 records were identified through electronic database searching. No additional records were identified by screening reference lists of included articles, previous systematic reviews, or other sources. After removing duplicates, 16432 studies remained for screening. Of these, 849 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 14 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis, as illustrated in Figure 2.

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection process.

Study characteristics

A total of 14 studies published between 2009 and 2025 were included in this review. These studies were conducted in Sweden (n = 1), the United States (n = 1), Italy (n = 4), Australia (n = 1), Spain (n = 2), Turkey (n = 1), Germany (n = 1), Finland (n = 1), and China (n = 2). The combined sample size was 286 participants, with individual study sizes ranging from 7 participants24 to 48 participants.25,26 The average participant age ranged from 18 to 56.2 years,25,27 and mean BMI ranged from 29.81 to 48.67 kg/m2.28,29 All 14 studies reported participant sex. Two studies included only female participants,29,30 while the remaining 12 studies included both male and female participants.24–28,31–37 Most studies (n = 9) employed 16S rRNA gene sequencing for gut microbiota analysis,25,29–31,35 targeting different variable regions (V2–V4–V8, V3–V4, V4–V5) to assess α-diversity, β-diversity, and microbial composition changes. Five studies did not report the targeted region.24,26,27,34,36 An overview of the included studies is presented in Table 1.

Risk of bias and publication bias

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool. Among the 14 studies included in the analysis, 7 employed blinded outcome assessment, and all 14 reported complete outcome data. One study was rated as having a low risk of bias, 10 studies had a moderate risk, and 3 studies were considered at high risk of bias (Supplementary Figure S1). Overall, based on risk of bias evaluation, 7 studies (50%) were considered high quality, 4 studies (28%) were of moderate quality, and 3 studies (22%) were of low quality. Funnel plot analysis and Egger’s test were conducted to assess publication bias. The funnel plot showed an approximately symmetrical distribution, suggesting a low risk of publication bias or small-study effects.

Results of meta-analysis

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes focused on alpha diversity measures, including Shannon index, Simpson index, OTUs, Chao1, and Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity (PD) index (Figure 3). Six studies (9 effect sizes, 256 subjects) reported Shannon index data. The random-effects meta-analysis indicated that VLCKD significantly improved the Shannon index in individuals with obesity (SMD: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.03 to 1.04, P = 0.0378), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (I² = 71.5%). Three studies (3 effect sizes, 102 subjects) provided PD index data, and the meta-analysis showed a significant improvement (SMD: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.36 to 1.18, P = 0.0002). Although we assessed OTUs, Chao1 and Simpson indices separately, no significant effects were detected, suggesting that VLCKD may have limited influence on the richness of rare species and the dominance structure of the gut microbiota. The significant improvement in the PD index implies that VLCKD may enhance evolutionary diversity and ecological complexity in the gut.

Forest plot of the effects of VLCKD on gut microbiota diversity in individuals with obesity.

To explore sources of high heterogeneity, we first conducted sensitivity analyses. Egger’s test and funnel plot analysis were used to assess potential publication bias (Figure 4a). Subsequently, subgroup analyses and meta-regression were performed to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity (Figure 5a–c). Subgroup analyses were stratified by BMI, age, and intervention duration (Supplementary Table S2). Results showed that age significantly moderated the intervention effect, with statistically significant between-group differences (Pd < 0.01), indicating that age may be a key source of heterogeneity. Specifically, the effects were more pronounced in individuals aged >30 years and those with BMI ≤ 30 kg/m² (P < 0.05). Meta-regression revealed a positive regression slope with age, suggesting that intervention efficacy may increase with age.

Secondary outcomes

For Akkermansia, four studies (5 effect sizes, 108 subjects) showed that VLCKD significantly increased its abundance under a random-effects model (SMD: 1.76, 95% CI: 0.48 to 3.03, P = 0.0069), with high heterogeneity (I² = 85.8%). Regarding Bifidobacterium, meta-analysis indicated a significant negative effect (SMD: -1.23, 95% CI: -1.81 to -0.64, P < 0.0001), suggesting that VLCKD may markedly reduce this key probiotic, potentially posing health risks—a point discussed further in the discussion section. As for the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio, VLCKD significantly increased the ratio (SMD: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.67 to 1.34, P < 0.0001), indicating stability and consistency in the observed direction of change (Figure 6).

Forest plot of the effects of VLCKD on the abundance of key gut microbial taxa in individuals with obesity.

To further investigate sources of heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis was performed. Egger’s test and funnel plot analysis were used to assess publication bias (Figure 4b). Subgroup analyses (Supplementary Table S3) and meta-regression (Figure 5d–f) were conducted using BMI, age, and intervention duration as moderator variables. For Akkermansia, BMI significantly moderated the effect of the intervention. The most notable effects were observed in individuals with BMI between 30–35 kg/m². Although effects were also significant in those with BMI > 40 kg/m², the limited number of studies in this subgroup may reduce the robustness of the evidence; thus, this result was excluded from the main conclusions. Regarding age, individuals over 40 years exhibited significant increases in Akkermansia abundance. In terms of intervention duration, only interventions ≤ 6 weeks showed significant effects in subgroup analyses.

For Bifidobacterium, subgroup analyses (Supplementary Table S4) indicated a significant negative effect in individuals with BMI between 30–35 kg/m², suggesting stronger suppression of this probiotic in this weight category. Similarly, significant reductions were observed in those with BMI > 40 kg/m²; however, due to the small number of studies, this finding was not considered robust. In age-stratified analysis, significant negative changes were observed in participants older than 40 years, implying that age may be an important moderator of the intervention effect. However, in the 40–50 year subgroup, only one study was included, limiting the reliability of the finding. With respect to intervention duration, significant effects were observed in both ≤ 6-week and 6–12-week subgroups. Meta-regression revealed a mildly positive trend with age, suggesting that while the intervention effect may improve with increasing mean age, the enhancement remains modest.

Discussion

This meta-analysis aimed to systematically evaluate the effects of VLCKD on gut microbiota in individuals with obesity, and to further explore potential moderating factors. The main findings indicate that VLCKD interventions significantly enhanced gut microbial diversity, as evidenced by notable increases in the Shannon index and Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity (PD) index. Additionally, VLCKD significantly increased the abundance of Akkermansia, while reducing the abundance of Bifidobacterium, along with an elevated Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio. These results suggest that VLCKD exerts bidirectional effects on microbial structure. Our findings support the growing view that VLCKD can effectively modulate the gut microbial ecosystem and lead to short-term improvements in microbial diversity. However, the significant reduction in Bifidobacterium may indicate potential risks to gut barrier integrity and probiotic balance. This observation has important implications for long-term VLCKD interventions in obese populations. It highlights the need to monitor probiotic stability and gut health during implementation, and suggests that individualized supplementation strategies—such as dietary fiber or probiotics—may be necessary to maintain microbial stability and safety.38

Changes in gut microbiota diversity indices

Gut microbial diversity, particularly alpha diversity, is a key indicator of intestinal health and has been closely associated with the development of various diseases. According to a review by Li et al.,39 gut microbial diversity may serve as a potential biomarker for the early prediction of multiple diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease, schizophrenia, and multiple sclerosis. Higher microbial diversity is typically linked to greater intestinal resilience, enhanced immune function, and reduced levels of inflammation.40 Our meta-analysis demonstrated that VLCKD significantly increased alpha diversity indices in individuals with obesity, including the Shannon index and PD index. These metrics reflect improvements in gut health across different dimensions: the Shannon index captures species evenness, and PD index reflects phylogenetic and evolutionary complexity. In contrast, the Observed OTU, Simpson and Chao1 indices did not show significant changes, likely due to their lower sensitivity to changes in dominant or low-abundance taxa. VLCKD alters the ratio of dietary fats to carbohydrates, which may substantially shift the availability of microbial substrates in the gut. This shift could favor the growth of fat-tolerant and mucin-degrading bacteria, thereby driving changes in microbiota structure and diversity.41,42 It is worth noting that VLCKD appears to primarily affect common or dominant microbial taxa, while its influence on low-abundance or rare species is limited. This may partly explain why some diversity indices did not reach statistical significance.43,44

Moreover, subgroup analyses showed that VLCKD had a more pronounced effect on microbial diversity among individuals aged over 30 years. This may be due to age-related microbial dysbiosis and the decline of gut microbial diversity observed in older adults. Aging is associated with greater gut microbial instability, which may render the microbiota more responsive to dietary interventions. Therefore, VLCKD may induce more substantial structural shifts in populations with baseline dysbiosis, particularly in older individuals.45,46 Additionally, the findings revealed that VLCKD had stronger effects in individuals with BMI ≤ 30 kg/m², suggesting that the gut microbiota of mildly obese individuals may be more responsive to dietary interventions. This may be explained by the greater ecological plasticity of the microbiome in individuals with Class I obesity or lower BMI. Conversely, with higher BMI, the gut microbiota often exhibits dominant taxa fixation, resulting in reduced plasticity to dietary interventions and decreased sensitivity to their effects.47,48 Specifically, these dominant taxa have been reported in some studies to play a central role in metabolic pathways related to high-efficiency energy utilization, such as starch degradation, aromatic amino acid biosynthesis, and nucleotide biosynthesis, thereby establishing a relatively fixed metabolic pattern that limits microbiota changes induced by dietary interventions.27,49 Moreover, individuals with higher BMI frequently present with impaired intestinal barrier function, increased intestinal permeability, and metabolic endotoxemia. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) can activate the TLR4–NF-κB signaling pathway, triggering sustained elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which not only exacerbates metabolic dysregulation but also inhibits the colonization and expansion of beneficial microbes such as Bifidobacterium and butyrate-producing bacteria.50

These results indicate that the microbial modulatory effects of VLCKD may vary across individuals. However, BMI has consistently been identified as a key mediator in the relationship between dietary intervention and microbiome composition.51 Future research with larger sample sizes and more diverse populations is warranted to validate these findings.

Alterations in characteristic gut microbiota

Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes are the two dominant bacterial phyla in the adult gut microbiota, together accounting for approximately 90% of its composition.52 F/B ratio reflects the relative abundance between these two major phyla and is often used as an indicator of microbial balance. A higher F/B ratio has been associated with obesity, metabolic dysfunction, and gut dysbiosis.53,54 In the present study, VLCKD intervention did not significantly alter the individual abundance of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes in obese individuals; however, a significant increase was observed in the F/B ratio. This may be due to the F/B ratio’s heightened sensitivity to subtle community shifts, especially in the context of obesity and intestinal inflammation. In contrast, changes in single phyla may be masked by inter-individual variability or sequencing methodology.52,55 Firmicutes dominate in lipid metabolism and can efficiently utilize fat-derived metabolites, leading to their rapid expansion in high-fat dietary environments. On the other hand, Bacteroidetes specialize in metabolizing dietary fiber and complex carbohydrates. The VLCKD’s severe carbohydrate restriction reduces substrate availability for Bacteroidetes, potentially contributing to their relative decline. Prior research has suggested that the F/B ratio is more statistically sensitive than single phyla abundance in detecting dietary intervention effects, and more closely linked to obesity- and metabolism-related phenotypes.56 Traditionally, an elevated F/B ratio is associated with increased energy harvest efficiency, enhanced fat storage, and low-grade chronic inflammation—features characteristic of obesity, metabolic syndrome, and type 2 diabetes. However, under VLCKD intervention, an increase in the F/B ratio may not necessarily reflect pathological dysbiosis. Rather, it may indicate an adaptive microbial restructuring in response to the dietary regimen. This is consistent with findings from Tanca et al.,41 who observed that VLCKD induced short-term increases in both F/B ratio and Akkermansia abundance, suggesting that the observed microbial shifts likely reflect a favorable adaptive response.

Akkermansia is widely recognized for its anti-obesity, anti-inflammatory, and gut barrier-enhancing properties, and is considered one of the key taxa responsive to ketogenic diets.57 VLCKD alters intestinal substrate availability by reducing carbohydrates, thereby increasing reliance on mucus—the primary substrate for Akkermansia—which promotes its proliferation.58 Subgroup analysis revealed a significant effect in individuals with BMI between 30–35 kg/m². This may be attributed to greater ecological plasticity in this group, where the microbiota remains more modifiable and responsive to dietary shifts.47 Additionally, Akkermansia appears to exert more pronounced anti-obesity effects in individuals over 40 years of age. Its abundance is inversely associated with obesity risk, suggesting that older individuals, who typically exhibit greater baseline dysbiosis and reduced microbial diversity, are more sensitive to VLCKD-induced microbial shifts.59 As aging is associated with decreased gut diversity, weakened barrier function, and greater susceptibility to dysbiosis, Akkermansia may more easily occupy ecological niches during intervention.58 During short-term dietary interventions, the gut microbiota is highly sensitive to acute dietary changes, enabling rapid expansion of Akkermansia within days. This bacterium relies on host-secreted mucin polysaccharides as its primary carbon source and is extremely sensitive to changes in substrate availability and mucin layer metabolism. Dietary patterns characterized by a marked restriction of carbohydrates and an increased proportion of lipids can accelerate mucin layer metabolism in the gut, leading to a rapid rise in Akkermansia abundance over a short period, accompanied by enhanced expression of functional proteins related to stress responses and neurotransmitter metabolism (such as γ-aminobutyric acid synthase).41,60 However, during long-term ketogenic dieting, the gut micro-ecology gradually undergoes compensatory restructuring. Other commensal species, through metabolic interactions and resource competition, suppress the over-proliferation of a single species, while host immunity and mucin layer renewal rates may also be feedback-regulated, causing Akkermansia abundance to gradually decline. In addition, long-term interventions may lead to dietary fatigue and reduced compliance, resulting in Akkermansia abundance returning toward a new steady state.58,61,62 Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify these dynamic patterns and their clinical significance.

Bifidobacterium is a well-established core probiotic that contributes to host health by producing short-chain fatty acids (e.g., acetate), modulating immune responses, enhancing intestinal barrier integrity, and maintaining microbial homeostasis.63,64 A decline in Bifidobacterium abundance has been linked to increased risk of several chronic diseases, including allergic asthma, colitis, type 2 diabetes, and depression.38,65,66 SCFAs are key metabolites produced by the fermentation of dietary fibers by the gut microbiota. They serve as an important energy source and exert regulatory effects on metabolism, immunity, and the nervous system through the activation of G-protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) and the inhibition of histone deacetylases (HDACs), thereby contributing to energy supply, immune modulation, and metabolic improvement.67,68 Bifidobacterium is recognized as an important producer of SCFAs.69 Salazar et al.70 reported that in obese women, an increase in Bifidobacterium was accompanied by a reduction in SCFA levels. In contrast, Canfora et al.71 found that in overweight or obese men and women with prediabetes, higher Bifidobacterium abundance was not associated with significant alterations in SCFA concentrations. Similarly, Solito et al.72 demonstrated that in obese adolescents, supplementation with Bifidobacterium breve BR03 and B632 for 8 weeks improved insulin sensitivity, yet SCFA levels, especially acetate, did not significantly increase. Collectively, these findings highlight the lack of consistency across studies investigating obese populations of different ages, sexes, and comorbidity status. Future research should focus on these issues to achieve greater consensus.

Our subgroup analysis revealed that VLCKD significantly reduced Bifidobacterium abundance, with the most pronounced effect observed in individuals with BMI 30–35 kg/m². This may represent a critical “window of plasticity,” during which the gut microbiota remains modifiable and responsive to dietary stressors.73 The severe carbohydrate restriction and high-fat nature of VLCKD markedly reduce the availability of fermentable fibers and oligosaccharides—substrates essential for Bifidobacterium—thus favoring the dominance of fat-tolerant microbial communities and suppressing Bifidobacterium growth.74 Additionally, subgroup analyses by age and intervention duration showed that Bifidobacterium significantly declined in individuals aged >40 years undergoing VLCKD for 0–12 weeks. This may be due to age-related changes in the gut environment, including decreased gastric acid secretion, reduced digestive enzyme activity, and slower intestinal transit, all of which impair probiotic colonization, particularly for Bifidobacterium.75,76 In the short term, the strict restriction of carbohydrates and fermentable fibers in the diet reduces the substrates available for Bifidobacterium.77 At the same time, acute VLCKD induces rapid ketogenesis, elevating ketones such as β-hydroxybutyrate. High concentrations of ketones may indirectly inhibit certain anaerobic probiotics by reducing gut pH or destabilizing bacterial membranes.17 Notably, ketone bodies selectively inhibited bifidobacterial growth.74 In contrast, during long-term interventions, compensatory remodeling of the gut ecosystem appears to occur. Evidence suggests that the microbial community may partially recover or stabilize, likely due to cross-feeding interactions among microbes and host feedback mechanisms regulating mucosal barrier renewal and immune tolerance.35 However, cautious evaluation is required regarding the long-term nutritional safety and potential risks to gut health.78,79

In summary, VLCKD exerts dual effects on characteristic gut microbial taxa in individuals with obesity: On the one hand, the increase in Akkermansia reflects an adaptive and potentially beneficial microbial response to the dietary shift. On the other hand, the marked suppression of Bifidobacterium raises concerns about gut barrier integrity and chronic disease risk. These findings underscore the need for careful monitoring and individualized dietary planning during VLCKD interventions. Future large-scale, long-duration randomized controlled trials are warranted to further elucidate the underlying microbial mechanisms and evaluate the long-term safety of VLCKD across diverse populations.

Limitations of the study

First, the number of included studies for certain outcomes was limited. Although this review adhered strictly to systematic review procedures, the current body of research on the effects of VLCKD on gut microbiota in individuals with obesity remains sparse. Some microbial outcomes were derived from only 3 to 5 studies, potentially compromising the precision and robustness of effect size estimates. For example, When conducting subgroup analysis, only the Shannon index, Akkermansia, and Bifidobacterium were analyzed. Meanwhile, when analyzing Bifidobacterium, the levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) should be reported simultaneously, as there is a relevant association between them; however, due to a lack of eligible studies (fewer than three), meta-analysis of SCFA data could not be conducted. Second, there was inconsistency in microbial sequencing regions and analytical approaches across studies. Although most studies used 16S rRNA sequencing, the choice of hypervariable regions (e.g., V3–V4, V4, V4–V5) and data processing platforms varied considerably, which may have affected the comparability of microbial abundance and diversity indices, introducing methodological bias. Third, despite being uniformly labeled as "VLCKD," the intervention protocols varied substantially in total caloric intake, fat-to-protein ratios, use of medium-chain triglycerides (MCTs) or exogenous ketones, and dietary supplementation. These differences may have altered the gut substrate environment and contributed to heterogeneity in microbiota responses and effect estimates. Finally, the included studies were conducted across diverse geographic regions (including Europe, North America, and Asia), where differences in genetic background, dietary habits, gut microbial baseline composition, and lifestyle factors could systematically influence individual responses to VLCKD. These contextual factors may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that VLCKD can significantly enhance gut microbial diversity in individuals with obesity, as evidenced by increases in the Shannon index and PD index. Additionally, VLCKD significantly increased Akkermansia abundance and the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio, while concurrently reducing the abundance of Bifidobacterium. These findings highlight the dual ecological effects of VLCKD—potentially beneficial modulation of microbial diversity, but also possible risks of dysbiosis due to probiotic depletion. Subgroup analyses revealed that improvements in microbial diversity (Shannon index) were more pronounced in individuals aged >30 years and with BMI > 30 kg/m². The most notable increases in Akkermansia were observed in individuals with BMI between 30–35 kg/m², aged over 40, and within intervention periods ≤ 6 weeks. In contrast, reductions in Bifidobacterium were most significant in those with BMI 30–35 kg/m², age >40 years, and intervention durations ≤ 12 weeks. Future research should place greater emphasis on preserving core probiotics, exploring the potential of co-administration with probiotics, and assessing the long-term ecological safety of VLCKD. Large-scale, high-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to further validate these findings and guide safe, personalized dietary interventions for obesity management.
