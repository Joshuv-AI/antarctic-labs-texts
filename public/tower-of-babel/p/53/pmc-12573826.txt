Comprehensive metagenomic analysis of the giant panda’s oral microbiome reveals distinct taxonomic and functional characteristics
Authors: Lin-Fan Xiao, Yusi Li, Huan Lian, Xiaoning Liu, Yifan Wen, Xin Chen, Wenjun Huang, Bi Li, Li Luo, Xiaolan Wang, Cedric Tutt, Jun Zheng, Vivien Ya-Fan Wang, Ning-Yi Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHost-associated microbiomes play a crucial role in various biological processes, including host physiology, health, and evolution. However, despite its importance, the oral microbiome of wild animals such as the giant panda (Ailuropoda melanoleuca), remains poorly studied. Here we presented the initial comprehensive metagenomic analysis of the giant panda’s oral microbiome using shotgun sequencing, generating the valuable data for this endangered species. Our study included 118 samples, comprising 28 supragingival samples (11 giant pandas and 3 Asiatic black bears (Ursus thibetanus), also known as a moon bear, collected by us, along with 14 human (Homo sapiens) samples originating from public datasets.) as well as 90 fecal samples from public databases. Our findings indicated that the oral and gut microbiomes of Ursidae were different from those of humans, both taxonomically and functionally. These results suggest that phylogenetic factors, rather than ecology, play an important role in shaping the oral and gut microbiomes (phylosymbiosis). Notably, periodontal opportunistic pathogens in humans coexisted with giant pandas, other bears and gorillas without dental issues. This suggests immune tolerance mechanisms may increase with long-term coevolution in host-microbiome, enabling pathogen coexistence without inflammatory harm. Lastly, giant pandas exhibited a high abundance of nitrate-reducing oral taxa, which may contribute to their adaptation to the environment and promoting oral health. This initial study not only enhances our understanding of host-microbiome interactions but also demonstrates the importance of metagenomic approaches in conservation biology, which could contribute to the long-term conservation of giant pandas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-025-00475-z.Keywords: Oral microbiome, Giant pandas, Metagenome-assembled genomes, Shotgun metagenomics

Introduction

Published research has highlighted the vital role of microbial communities in the mouth, influencing biological processes like lipid A biosynthesis, beta-lactam degradation and cellular stress responses. These microbes also contribute to oral diseases, with pathogens such as Tannerella forsythia, Treponema denticola, and Porphyromonas gingivalis [1–3]. Current research in this field is primarily focused on how these microbes interact with human oral health [4]. Although research on non-primate oral microbiomes is relatively scarce compared to studies conducted on humans, they have the potential to reveal insights into the dynamic interactions between the internal and external microbial environments of the body, especially in digestive and respiratory systems. Recently, shotgun metagenomic sequencing of oral microbiota has emerged as a powerful tool to conduct a detailed exploration of microbial diversity [5]. So, investigating the oral microbiomes of non-primate species through supragingival sample analysis provides a promising avenue to understand these dynamics.

The key concept of phylosymbiosis is that the similarity in microbial communities reflects the host phylogenetic relationships. This phenomenon has been reported in primates, but studies on non-primate oral microbiomes are still limited [6, 7]. Previous studies suggested that microbiome niches are primarily influenced by two factors: phylogeny and diet [8–10]. However, it remains unclear which factor plays the dominant role. A promising model organism for investigating this question is the giant panda, a flagship species belonging to the order Carnivora but is known for its dietary specialization in bamboo. Given its unique phylogenetic position and specialized bamboo diet, the giant panda provides a valuable model system for examining the relative contributions of host phylogeny (evolutionary history) and ecology (dietary adaptation) in shaping microbial communities. While gut microbiome studies on giant pandas are increasing, the corresponding research on its oral microbiome remains limited [11–13].

Here, we present the comprehensive metagenomic analysis of the giant panda’s oral microbiome using fresh supragingival plaque samples. In this study, metagenomics was utilized to analyze the microbial communities in the oral and gut microbiomes of three host species: giant panda, Asiatic black bear, and human to determine which factor -nature (phylogeny) or nurture (ecology) - is more influential in shaping these communities. Both the Asiatic black bear and human are omnivores (noting that modern human diets, unrepresented here, include vegetarianism and veganism), their dietary patterns differ markedly from the giant panda’s specialized bamboo consumption. Our results indicate that more closely related species, more similar community composition, showing the patterns of phylosymbiosis. In addition, human-related periodontal pathogens were found in asymptomatic giant pandas. The result was also reported in other bears and gorillas in another paper, which may indicate the coevolution in host-microbiome [5]. Finally, we identified distinctive taxonomic and functional signatures within the giant panda’s supragingival microbiome, including an enrichment of nitrate-reducing bacteria. These features may support bamboo digestion and could contribute to the low incidence of dental caries observed during veterinary assessments. Collectively, these findings advance our understanding of host-microbe coevolution in specialized herbivores and offer potential applications for the conservation management of this threatened species.

Results

Host phylogeny and sites may drive microbiome composition

Supragingival samples from giant pandas were collected using high-temperature-sterilized disposable cotton swabs, with five repetitive swabs performed at each designated site to ensure sampling consistency. All samples were immediately stored in EDTA-containing preservation medium to maintain DNA integrity, followed by cryopreservation at -80 °C at the Chengdu Research Base of Giant Panda Breeding (Methods). Using identical protocols, supragingival samples from Asiatic black bears were obtained from Animals Asia, Vietnam. Publicly available supragingival and fecal metagenomic datasets (giant panda, Asiatic black bear, human) were downloaded from the NCBI database. The microflora distribution was analyzed based on the relative abundances of supragingival and fecal samples from three species (Fig. 1 and supplementary Fig. S1). Taxonomic profiling and metagenomic binning were performed using SqueezeMeta and data visualization was subsequently conducted using the R packages phyloseq and MicrobiotaProcess (Methods) [14–16]. Collectively, metagenomic sequences were predominantly bacterial across all samples at the domain level.

At the phylum level, the top 10 taxa in the giant panda oral microbiome were dominated by Proteobacteria (39.84%), followed by Bacteroidetes (26.92%), Actinobacteria (24.95%), Firmicutes (5.12%), Fusobacteria (2.58%), and various other phyla were also identified in lesser proportions (Fig. 1A, supplementary Fig. S1A and S1C). Although the proportion of the top six major phyla (around 98%) was similar to the composition of the oral bacterial community in the other two host species [17], Actinobacteria and Firmicutes were less abundant and Proteobacteria were more abundant in the Ursidae oral microbiome compared to the human oral microbiome. Additionally, Spirochaetes were detected in the human oral community, constituting approximately 2.22% of its composition. At the genus level, 288 bacterial genera were identified in the study. The genus-level taxa appeared to be more dispersed than the phyla, with an average percentage of 77.41% in oral cohorts (Fig. 1B, supplementary Fig. S1B, S1d and S1E). Furthermore, Neisseria, Lautropia and Moraxella were more prominent in the composition of oral bacteria in the Ursidae oral microbiome, while Actinomyces, Prevotella, Leptotrichia, Unclassified Actinomycetaceae, Selenomonas and Tannerella were scarcely observed compared to the prevalence of human oral microflora.

In oral samples, the Ursidae oral microbiome exhibited a significantly distinct compositional profile compared to humans, harboring nine unique microbial species. Seven of these were from the Proteobacteria phylum, while one each came from Bacteroidetes and Fusobacteria (Fig. 1C). Analysis of the host-specific core oral microbiome showed that most taxa spanned four primary phyla: Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes (Supplementary Fig. S2A). The human core oral microbiome additionally contained representatives from Fusobacteria and Spirochaetes. Comparative analysis revealed consistent phylum-level differences: relative to humans, the number of the Ursidae oral microbiome exhibited reduced Actinobacteria and Firmicutes but increased Proteobacteria (Supplementary Fig. S2A).At the genus level, each species harbored distinct taxa. Of note, Aggregatibacter, Arachnia, Chryseobacterium and two unclassified genera were exclusively identified in the giant panda-specific oral core microbiome (Supplementary Fig. S2C).

The analyses of bacterial alpha and beta diversity were conducted to investigate the similarities and differences in oral microbiome community structures among the three host species (Fig. 1D and E; Table 1). Chao1 is used to estimate the total richness, and higher values indicate larger numbers of species; whereas Shannon index is used to estimate the diversity of species within a community, and higher values indicate higher diversity. The analyses by Chao1 and Shannon index suggested that oral microbial richness had dramatic differences between the three hosts (wilcox test, p < 0.001; Fig. 1D). In terms of evenness, dramatic differences were observed in oral community diversity (wilcox test, p < 0.001 for the comparison between giant pandas and humans; wilcox test, p < 0.05 for the comparison between Asiatic black bears and humans), but not within Ursidae species (wilcox test, not significant).

Gut microbiomes displayed greater compositional variability compared to oral communities. The giant panda gut microbiome was mainly comprised of Proteobacteria, accounting for 69.27%. In contrast, the top two taxa of gut microbiomes in Asiatic black bears and humans were Proteobacteria and Firmicutes, and Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, respectively, which was consistent with Arumugam’s findings (Fig. 1A) [18]. At the genus level, taxonomic dispersion was more pronounced than at the phylum level: the top 20 genera accounted for 74.86% in gut cohorts. Within Ursidae, gut microbiomes showed conserved phylum-level patterns, with minor interspecific variations in Unclassified Enterobacteriaceae (Fig. 1B). These findings suggest greater microbiome similarity among Ursidae hosts compared to humans.

Two core microbiota (species shared across all three hosts) was identified in gut samples. Among these, Escherichia coli and an unclassified Neisseriaceae are found in all three host species in gut samples (Fig. 1C) [19]. Comparative analysis of the core microbiota revealed similar phylum-level differences between the Ursidae and human gut microbiomes: the number of Ursidae core microbiome exhibited reduced Actinobacteria but increased Proteobacteria (Supplementary Fig. S2B). At the genus level, distinct taxonomic profiles were observed between hosts. Notably, Campylobacter, Pseudomonas, Shigella, and Yersinia were exclusively identified in the giant panda-specific oral core microbiome (Supplementary Fig. S2D).

A clear segregation in community structures was observed using beta diversity index (based on Hellinger transformed abundance metrics (Bray-Curtis) and PERMANOVAs (PERMutational ANalysis of VAriance) among the three host species (Fig. 1E). The effect size (R²) values, when stratified by sampling sites, were approximately one and a half times greater than those stratified by individual animals (Stratifying by sampling sites (oral/gut) explained 38% of total variation (R² = 0.38, p = 0.002) and stratification by host species accounted for 27% of variation (R² = 0.27, p < 0.002). This result indicated that microbial niche (site) had a stronger effect than host phylogeny. Furthermore, oral samples exhibited tighter clustering in ordination space compared to gut samples, reflecting lower inter-individual variability in oral communities. Pairwise PERMANOVA showed significant differentiation between Ursidae (giant pandas and Asiatic black bears) and humans (p < 0.05 for both comparisons; Table 1). These results align with prior cross-species microbiome studies and suggest that microbial community patterns are influenced by both host evolutionary history and ecological niches.

Kinships and living habitats may harbor distinct functional communities

To investigate the functional characteristics of gene families, we conducted comparative analyses of normalized gene family abundances using DIAMOND alignments against three functional databases: KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes), CAZy (Carbohydrate-Active enZYmes), and CARD (Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database) [20–23]. The beta diversity index and PERMANOVAs of KEGG pathways, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes, and CAZys exhibited similar patterns to those of the microflora communities, where sampling sites (oral vs. fecal) and host species were the two main factors (all adjusted p value was 0.002 and the R2 value of sites was comparable to that of the three animal hosts.) (Fig. 2A; Table 2, supplementary Fig S3A, 3B, supplementary Table S1 and S2). Notably, these results indicate a conserved carnivorous functional profile in giant pandas, similar to black bears, despite their dietary shift to bamboo.

Furthermore, a total of 157 statistically significant enrichment levels in KEGG pathways were identified, and the top 10 KEGG pathways, which differed between the two largest microbial ecosystems, indicated that oral symbiotic bacteria are likely to be involved in detoxification and antioxidant functions, such as " ko00785 Lipoic acid metabolism " and “ko00480 Glutathione metabolism”, whereas gut microorganisms were more enriched in functions related to carbohydrate digestion, including “ko00511 Other glycan degradation” and “ko00052 Galactose metabolism” (Fig. 2B).

The prevalence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes has escalated with the abuse of industrial antibiotic production and has been detected across various host species, encompassing humans, livestock, and natural ecosystems [24, 25]. To investigate the differences in AMR gene abundance across sampling sites, the Welch test was conducted, and the top 11 AMR families were enriched in oral microbiomes for “ancient” ARGs (antibiotic resistance genes) of “resistance-nodulation-cell division (RND) antibiotic efflux pump” and “major facilitator superfamily (MFS) antibiotic efflux pump”, which are encoded on the chromosome and play a crucial role in bacterial survival [25]. On the other hand, AMR families of gut microorganisms were more diverse and included some “novel” AMR families, plasmid-borne genes, such as “trimethoprim resistant dihydrofolate reductase dfr” (Fig. 2C).

Considering that the giant panda mostly lives on bamboo [26], carbohydrate-active enzymes are crucial. Interestingly, our results demonstrated that among the CAZy families present in the oral microbiome of giant pandas, enzymes from the Glycosyltransferase Family (GT) were more abundant, whereas those from the Glycoside Hydrolase Family (GH) were less abundant (Fig. 2D).

Unique features of the giant panda oral microbiomes

The combination of three methods, ALDEx2, LEfSe and DESeq2, was used to identify overlapping significant biomarkers of the oral microflora of the giant panda [16, 27–30]. The study revealed 56 species-level taxa that varied in abundance among host species (Fig. 3A, supplementary Table S3 and supplementary Fig. S5A), and 41 significantly enriched genera. The top three enriched genera were Neisseria (including Neisseria chenwenguii, Neisseria dentiae, Neisseria shayeganii, Neisseria sp., and Neisseria animaloris), Capnocytophaga (including Capnocytophaga canimorsus, Capnocytophaga canis, and Capnocytophaga cynodegmi), and Prevotella (including Prevotella buccae, Prevotella fusca, and Prevotella intermedia) [31–37]. In addition, some opportunistic pathogens including Prevotella intermedia and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans were also identified in the oral cavity of giant pandas, even in individuals with healthy oral status. This observation aligns with previous findings demonstrating that species associated with periodontal diseases in humans are commonly present in brown bears and gorillas [5].

Functional diversity analysis yielded 40 differentially enriched KEGG pathways (supplementary Fig. S3C and supplementary Fig. S5B). One of these pathways was associated with flavonoid biosynthesis (designated as “ko00941 Flavonoid biosynthesis”), which might contribute to the reproduction and immune response of the host [38–40]. Interestingly, enrichment of the microbial ‘ko00941 Flavonoid biosynthesis’ pathway may be related to food conversion in giant pandas. This functional pattern differed from that of the ‘ko00052 Galactose metabolism’ pathway (Supplementary Fig. S4A), suggesting a potential association with their specialized bamboo-dominated diet (a notable dietary shift given that giant pandas transition from milk to bamboo consumption around 1.5 years of age).

The distinctive CAZy model of the giant panda oral niche revealed the presence of two significant CAZy enzymes, namely “β-glucosidase [EC 3.2.1.21]” and “β-galactosidase [EC 3.2.1.23]”, which may be involved in the two key steps of flavonoid bioconversion in the giant panda fecal microbiomes [40]. Additionally, two CAZy families, “AA4 (glycolate oxidase)” and “CE 3 (acetylxylan esterase [EC 3.1.1.72])”, were found to be associated with cellulose and lignin degradation. These CAZy families may contribute to the uptake of flavonoids. (supplementary Fig. S4B-D and supplementary Fig. S5C) [41]. 72 specifics AMRs genes were also identified in the giant panda oral microbiomes (supplementary Fig. S5D and S5E), which were classified into different AMR families. The top 11 AMR families in the giant panda oral microbial community were predominantly “ancient” AMR families, including the “MFS antibiotic efflux pump” and “RND antibiotic efflux pump”. However, in the comparison of specific antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes among the three host species, a higher number of ‘novel’ AMR families were detected in humans such as ‘chloramphenicol acetyltransferase (CAT)’. On the other hand, the top AMR families exhibited similar patterns between the Ursidae species (Fig. 3C, supplementary Fig. S5F and supplementary Fig. S5G) [25].

Reconstruction of metagenome-assembled genomes

De novo metagenome-assembled genome (MAG) assembly techniques were employed, resulting in the recovery of 26 medium quality MAGs (>50% completion, < 10% contamination) and two high quality MAGs (>90% completion, < 10% contamination) (Fig. 4a). A total of 27 out of 28 MAGs were annotated at least at the genus level, and 18 of them were reported to be part of common oral taxa associated with supragingival communities, including Actinomyces, Aggregatibacter, Campylobacter, Capnocytophaga, Corynebacterium, Lautropia, Neisseria, Ottowia, Porphyromonas, Prevotella, Streptococcus, and Veillonella. In addition, four MAGs were identified as belonging to different complex pathogens, namely the “orange complex” (including Prevotella intermedia), “yellow complex” (including Streptococcus oralis), “green complex” (including Capnocytophaga), and “purple complex” (including Veillonella parvula) [42]. Previous studies have reported the four nitrate-reducing oral symbionts including Actinomyces, Corynebacterium, Neisseria and Veillonella were present in the mammal oral microbiomes [43, 44]. In this study, these genera were identified among the 18 MAGs associated with supragingival communities in the giant panda, including a high-quality MAG of V. parvula. Given that V. parvula is a well-characterized oral nitrate reducer, we focused on this species to investigate its potential role in nitrogen metabolism and nitrate reduction in giant pandas using its high-quality MAG [45]. Six nitrate reductase genes (nar) were found, three genes involved in providing the cofactor molybdenum to the nitrate reductase enzymes (mo), and one additional gene involved in the regulation of nitrate reduction (norB) in the oral V. parvula recovered from the giant pandas (Fig. 4B). The regulator (norB) was conserved as it was found in the genomes of all the species of V. parvula, and the reconstructed genes in V. parvula from the giant panda oral metagenome showed a unique pattern: the absence of one molybdenum cofactor (moeA) and the absence of two nitrate reductases (narX_1 and narY). These genetic characteristics suggest that V. parvula may play a crucial role as a nitrate-reducing oral symbiont in the giant panda oral microbiome.

Discussion

Oral microbiomes play key roles in health and disease, but the oral microbes of wild animals remain understudied. Compared with previous analyses based on 16 S rRNA of dental plaque microbiota in panda cubs with caries, our work significantly advanced the field by establishing a multidimensional comparative framework encompassing taxonomic resolution, functional potential, and cross-species analyses among giant pandas, Asiatic black bears, and humans [13]. Despite the small sample size (only three Asiatic black bears), which caused cautious interpretation of interspecific comparisons, the rare dataset is of exceptional significance due to the challenges of sampling vulnerable and endangered species. As a result, this dataset provides novel insights into the complex tripartite relationships among host phylogeny, dietary specialization, and the dynamics of microbial communities across sampling sites.

Based on our findings, regardless of their divergent bamboo-carnivorous diets, the interesting phylogenetic clustering patterns between giant pandas and Asiatic black bears revealed host evolutionary history as the dominant driver of oral microbiome composition (Figs. 1 and 2; Tables 1 and 2). This conclusion was reinforced by two observations: (1) Core microbiome shared across Ursidae lineages even with the 12-million-year evolutionary divergence [46]. (2) Deep taxonomic divergence between Ursidae and humans at both species-level and functional pathway resolutions (Supplementary Table S1 and Table S2). These results reveal an intriguing phenomenon: the oral microbiome of giant pandas, an endangered species, also conforms to the concept of phylosymbiosis [6, 9, 25, 47, 48].

Giant pandas, including those without signs of oral disease, contained some well-known human oral opportunistic pathogens

Our analysis of biomarkers within the oral microbiome of giant pandas has identified notable taxonomic and functional differences (Fig. 3 and supplementary Table S3). Giant pandas even in asymptomatic pandas contained some well-known human oral opportunistic pathogens, such as Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and Prevotella intermedia (Fig. 3A). It is worth noting that certain human oral opportunistic pathogens, such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, have been detected in a significant number of brown bear and gorilla samples without oral diseases [25, 42]. These results unveil a paradox: how these wild animals maintained oral homeostasis while hosting putatively pathogenic taxa. One plausible explanation is that through long-term coevolution with microbes, hosts have developed deeply conserved tolerance mechanisms, such as immune modulation, which may inadvertently extend the infectious period of pathogens by allowing their persistence [49]. These adaptations likely enable giant pandas, other bears, and gorillas to tolerate the presence of these microbes without triggering detrimental inflammatory responses, thereby preserving oral health. However, our current research is constrained by factors such as small sample sizes, reliance on human-biased microbial reference databases, and the fact that the detection of wild animals’ potential oral pathogens via more state-of-art methods, does not necessarily imply causation between giant pandas’ oral diseases and these human oral pathogens.

In our analysis of functional biomarkers, we identified an enrichment of the ‘ko00941 Flavonoid biosynthesis’ pathway, which is a key metabolic route for flavonoid production. Flavonoids are secondary metabolites found in bamboo and are known to have antibacterial, antiviral, and anti-inflammatory effects, as well as antioxidant properties. They have been used as a pharmaceutical intermediate, dietary supplement, and food additive [40, 50, 51]. Additionally, we found significant activity of two key CAZy enzymes: β-glucosidase (EC 3.2.1.21) and β-galactosidase (EC 3.2.1.23). These enzymes are integral to the breakdown of complex carbohydrates. Furthermore, we detected the presence of two other CAZy families, AA4 (glycolate oxidase) and CE3 (acetylxylan esterase, EC 3.1.1.72), which are known for their involvement in the degradation of lignocellulose (supplementary Fig. S4B-D). Interesting, this flavonoid pathway enrichment coincides temporally with the giant panda’s dietary transition from milk to bamboo consumption at ~ 1.5 years of age. The concurrent presence of flavonoid biosynthesis pathway and lignocellulose-degrading enzymes suggests the oral microbiome may potentially contribute to flavonoid metabolism during this critical dietary adaptation phase.

Previous studies indicated that the giant panda lacks a cecum, and they rely on gut microbiota for the breakdown of hemicellulose [50]. While our study does not establish direct involvement of oral microbiota in lignified bamboo digestion, we provide evidence suggesting a potential contributory role. Specifically, our research indicates that the oral microbiomes of giant pandas possess CAZy enzymes that are known to facilitate the degradation of hemicellulose, a major component of lignified plant material. This finding aligns with the observation that lignified ingesta are digested by giant pandas in previous studies [51].

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has been on the rise and now poses a major threat to all beings on earth due to the misuse of antimicrobials since the mid-20th century [52]. Animals can serve as a resistance gene pool and transfer AMR elements into humans, and vice versa [53]. However, research related to antimicrobial resistance in giant pandas is scarce [54, 55]. Differential AMR genes were found in the giant pandas’ supragingival regions (Fig. 3B). AMR genes can be classified into “ancient” and “novel” categories. “Ancient” AMR genes contain well-established chromosomal genes like the RND family of efflux pumps and the ABC-F ribosome-binding proteins. These genes have been repurposed over time to enhance bacterial survival mechanisms and have been identified in the microbiomes of ancient humans and brown bears, highlighting their evolutionary significance [25, 56–60]. Conversely, “novel” AMR genes, such as aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (ANT, APH, AAC) and beta-lactamases (OXA), target specific antibiotics like streptomycin and penicillin, respectively [25, 61]. These genes are frequently located on plasmids and other mobile elements, promoting interbacterial horizontal gene transfer. Some ‘novel’ aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, as well as beta-lactamases that target cephamycin, have been identified in the oral microbiome of giant pandas. The presence of these newly recognized resistance genes indicates that the microbial communities of wildlife including captive giant pandas may be influenced by human activities such as antibiotic treatments, either directly through environmental interventions or indirectly through ecological changes like water or antibiotic-contaminated food. Although giant panda oral microbiomes had these new genes, most antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes within these communities still belong to “ancient” families, emphasizing the persistent nature of these resistance mechanisms and their extensive evolutionary history.

The discovery of nitrate-reducing oral symbionts in the supragingival regions of giant pandas may provide new insights into their potential adaptation to dynamic environments and their ability to withstand oral diseases. The reduction of nitrate to nitrite by oral bacteria could contribute to the production of nitric oxide, which plays an important role in promotion of oral health [44]. Recent research has emphasized the significant role of nitrate, a naturally occurring molecule found in plants, which may affect vascular health in animal models such as mice [62]. These studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of nitrate on the oral microbiome and its potential impact on overall host health. Considering that the giant panda primarily consumes bamboo, investigating the interaction between nitrate and the oral microbiome in this species could offer valuable insights into how the giant panda benefits from the bamboo-rich diet. Understanding the specific mechanisms and consequences of nitrate metabolism in the oral microbiome of giant pandas may contribute to our knowledge of their unique dietary adaptations and their overall well-being. Interesting, a study by Moraitou et al. (2022) reported higher abundances of nitrate-reducing oral taxa in gorilla species, which has been hypothesized to be associated with high-altitude adaptation [10]. Similarly, our data also detected elevated abundances of nitrate-reducing oral taxa in giant pandas. We propose that methodological and taxonomic distinctions may underlie the observed differences between these datasets: (1) The gorilla study utilized ancient dental calculus, capturing long-term environmental exposures and microbial signatures preserved over time. In contrast, our analysis of modern dental plaque reflects contemporary, host-specific microbial interactions. (2) Gorillas (Primates) and giant pandas (Carnivora: Ursidae) occupy distinct phylogenetic positions with unique evolutionary histories, potentially shaping divergent oral microbiome adaptations despite similar functional traits. Regarding the intriguing parallel in nitrate-reducing taxa: While our data similarly revealed enriched nitrate-reducing oral communities in giant pandas, we suggest Moraitou et al. high-altitude adaptation framework may not be directly applicable given fundamental ecological differences. Critically, field observations from our collaborators (Dr. Cedric and Chengdu Research Base staff) indicate wild giant pandas routinely traverse a broad altitudinal range (1,200–4,100 m) rather than residing continuously at high elevations [63]. Critically, current evidence remains insufficient to establish a definitive link between nitrate-reducing microbiomes and high-altitude adaptation in giant pandas. Further mechanistic studies are needed to clarify this potential association.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this study provided the initial comprehensive metagenomic analysis of the oral microbiomes of two threatened bear species: the endangered giant panda and the vulnerable Asiatic black bear. By using shotgun sequencing and comparative approaches, we investigated how host phylogeny and diets shape microbial communities. Our findings reveal three key insights: (1) Phylogenetic factors, rather than ecological factors, primarily shaped oral and gut microbiomes in giant pandas. This discovery extends the concept of phylosymbiosis to the oral microbiomes of endangered species, offering fresh perspectives on microbial evolution (2) We observed some human periodontal opportunistic pathogens coexisting with oral homeostasis in pandas and other species, such as other bears and gorillas without dental health issues. This intriguing balance may suggest that immune tolerance mechanisms possibly forged through long-term coevolution with their microbiota, enabling these wild animals to harbor pathogens while minimizing inflammatory harm. (3) Giant pandas exhibited a notably higher presence of nitrate-reducing oral taxa, which may reflect adaptations to their distinctive environment and could carry implications for maintaining oral health.

However, this study has two main limitations: 1. primarily due to the scarcity of non-primate supragingival microbiome data and reliance on microbial reference databases biased toward human-associated taxa. To expand our sample scope, we sought to incorporate data from published ancient dental calculus datasets. Beta diversity analysis revealed that ancient samples introduced significant confounding variation (PC1: 33.2% of microbiome variance and 38.3% of functional variance; data not shown), indicating that ancient samples may obscure distinction of a genuine signal from a likely contaminant. To mitigate potential bias arising from the inclusion of published animal oral metagenomes from dental calculus or other samples, we applied stringent inclusion criteria, restricting the analysis to fresh supragingival plaque samples. Consequently, the final number of qualified samples included in this study was limited. 2. While phylogeny is a well-established driver of microbiota structure—consistent with our observation of divergent oral communities between Carnivora (giant panda) and Primates (human)—this study’s significance extends beyond taxonomic comparisons. Critically, we established a standardized pipeline for low-biomass, non-model wildlife samples and validated here on managed-care pandas, to address reproducibility challenges in cross-species microbiome research. This methodological advance enabled collection of pristine, in vivo oral specimens from this flagship endangered species, mitigating degradation and contamination issues common in ancient or proxy samples. Analysis of these rare biospecimens revealed previously obscured functional adaptations: significant enrichment of flavonoid-metabolizing pathways (e.g., KEGG ko00941) and specialized enzymes (e.g., EC 2.4.1.265) within the panda’s relatively conserved oral microbiome. These findings suggest how metabolic innovation—rather than structural divergence—may support a carnivorous lineage in exploiting a bamboo niche, shifting the focus from ‘what differs?’ to ‘how does function evolve under constraint?‘. Thus, in addition to confirming expected phylogenetic patterns, this work provides: (i) a reproducible framework for studying inaccessible species, (ii) novel functional insights derived from high-quality samples, and (iii) mechanistic perspectives on host-microbe coadaptation where traditional experimentation is infeasible.

Nonetheless, our study highlights the potential of metagenomics in uncovering previously unknown microbial communities and their functional capabilities. The identification of novel microbiome communities in the supragingival regions of nonhuman animals not only paves the way for future investigations but also enriches our understanding of microbial ecology, particularly in the context of conservation and endangered species.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The supragingival samples were collected from giant pandas (Ailuropoda melanoleuca) (n = 11) during dental checkups conducted by specialist veterinary dentist Cedric Tutt and from Asiatic black bears (Ursus thibetanus) (n = 3) during dental checkups by staff at Animals Asia. The samples were obtained from Giant Pandas at the Chengdu Research Base of Giant Panda Breeding and Animals Asia in Vietnam, respectively. It is important to note that no animals were harmed during the collection of these samples. Sample collection was carried out in a manner that adhered to ethical practices and the well-being of the animals.

Two teeth were selected from each of the four quadrants (upper left, lower left, upper right, lower right) for the sampling process. Separate samples were taken from the buccal and palatal/lingual aspects of all sampled teeth. To ensure sample uniformity, each designated tooth position was gently wiped five times using high temperature sterilized disposable swabs high-temperature disposable sampling swabs (Huachenyang, CN). Contact between the sampling swabs and tongue and cheeks was prevented to eliminate contamination. The supragingival samples were collected from the junction of the tooth and gingiva. Following collection, all samples were placed in EDTA-containing storage medium (Huachenyang, CN). and were stored at -80℃ in the Chengdu Research Base of Giant Panda Breeding for further analysis.

Sample processing and DNA extraction

Total microbial DNA was extracted via the CTAB method and quantified using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA). Library construction followed the manufacturer’s protocol for the TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, FC-121-4001, USA). This involved seven sequential steps: 1. Fragmentation: Genomic DNA was randomly sheared into fragments of 200–500 bp. 2. End Repair: DNA fragment ends were enzymatically blunted and phosphorylated. 3.Adenylation: A single ‘A’ base was added to the 3’ ends of repaired fragments to facilitate adapter ligation. 4. Adapter Ligation: Illumina-compatible indexing adapters were ligated to adenylated fragments. 5. Purification: Ligated products were purified using magnetic beads to remove reaction components. 6. PCR Amplification: Adapter-ligated DNA was PCR-amplified with Illumina primers to enrich library molecules. 7. Final Cleanup: Amplified libraries underwent bead-based purification and were quantified by fluorometry for quality control. Libraries passing QC thresholds were subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Data processing

Sequencing pair-end WGS data of gingival regions and additional pair-end WGS data from previous studies downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/) (human supragingival samples PRJNA932553, human gut PRJNA539933, black bear gut PRJNA407583, captive giant panda gut PRJNA358755 and PRJNA407583, respectively) were processed together via kneaddata v0.10.0, which is a wrapper package to do quality control, adapter removal, host contaminated reads filtering and quality assessment in one command [12, 64–68]. The kneaddata workflow executed two key steps: (1) Adapters and low-quality sequences were removed using Trimmomatic v0.39 with default parameters. (2) To eliminate contamination from host DNA, reads were aligned to species-specific reference genomes (RefSeq GCF_002007445.2 [Ailuropoda melanoleuca], GCA_009660055.1 [Ursus thibetanus thibetanus] and RefSeq GCF_000001405.40 [Homo sapiens]) with Bowtie2 v2.4.4 [69–72]. Raw sequencing data are archived at the China National Center for Bioinformation under the project accession CRA018931.

Clean reads from samples of the same host species were merged and processed using the SqueezeMeta pipeline (v1.5.1, January 2022) with default parameters, an integrated workflow incorporating PICRUSt2 v2.5.0 [14, 73]. SqueezeMeta was executed in co-assembly mode: reads from all samples were pooled for single assembly, followed by individual sample read mapping to quantify gene abundances. Metagenomes from matching anatomical sites and hosts underwent co-assembly via Megahit v1.2.9 with succinct de Bruijn graph algorithm, where pooled reads were assembled into contigs [74]. The resulting co-assembled contigs underwent quality filtering via PRINSEQ v0.20.4 to remove fragments shorter than 200 bp [75]. Subsequently: ribosomal RNAs were predicted with Barrnap v0.9; 16 S rRNA genes were taxonomically classified using the RDP classifier v2.11; and tRNA/tmRNA sequences were identified with Aragorn v1.2.36 [76–78]. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using Prodigal v2.6.3, followed by comprehensive annotation through DIAMOND v2.0.14 BLASTp searches (e-value ≤ 1e-5) against GenBank nr, eggNOG v5.0, KEGG, CAZyDB (May 2019), and CARD v3.2.4, complemented by HMMER3 v3.1b1 homology searches against Pfam v35.0 [20–22, 79–83]. Taxonomic assignment of genes was performed using the LCA algorithm applied to nr database results [14, 80]. Lastly, individual sample reads were then mapped to co-assembled contigs using Bowtie2 v2.4.4 to calculate coverage and TPM-normalized abundances [69].

The generated raw KO abundance Table from the SqueezeMeta were analyzed from the previous study [84]. Normalized KO abundances were transformed into KEGG pathways via PICRUSt2 v2.5.0 with the script “pathway_pipeline.py” with the “–no_regroup” setting.

Taxonomic and functional analysis

Raw abundances were analyzed on the RStudio [85]. Taxa abundance was filtered via the threshold of relative abundance < 0.01% with at least annotated in the level of family with R packages phyloseq v1.41.0 [15]. All Figures were drawn by ggplot2 v3.3.6, ComplexHeatmap v2.13.1 [86, 87].

Composition

To assess composition in microbiome by species and sites, the top 10 most abundant phylum-level and genus-level taxa were illustrated with microViz v0.9.2 and MicrobiotaProcess v1.9.2 [16, 88].

Core microbiome

Microbiome v1.19.0 was used to detect core taxa in each cohort with the parameters of detection >0.001 & prevalence >75%, and UpSetR V1.4.0 was used to show in an upset plot [89, 90].

Alpha diversity and beta diversity

Alpha diversity index was calculated with two different methods: Chao1 estimator, which evaluates species richness and the Shannon index, which reflects species evenness. Effects on alpha diversity were tested by Wilcox test. Beta diversity values were calculated as Bray–Curtis dissimilarity on the transformed data. Distance metrics were performed PERMANOVAs with the ‘adonis2’ function of the vegan v2.6-2 and adjust p value with ‘p.adjust’ function of the stats v3.6.2 [91, 92]. In addition, pairwise PERMANOVAs were run with the ‘pairwise.adonis’ function of pairwiseAdonis v0.4 [93].

Differentially abundant taxa and functional analysis

Three methods, namely ALDEx2 v1.29.1, LEfSe function of microbiomeMarker 1.3.2, and MicrobiotaProcess v1.9.2 were used to analyze the differentially abundant taxa following the previous description to get conserved biomarkers (adjusted p values < 0.05) [16, 27, 30]. Biological processes among cohorts in composition, beta diversity and differential abundance analysis were done using the same method.

Metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs)

Bins were combined by DAS Tool v1.1.1 and evaluated using CheckM v1.0.11 after performing MaxBin2 v2.2.6 and Metabat2 v2.15 [94–97]. The generated medium-quality MAGs (>50% completion, < 10% contamination) were taxonomically identified with GTDB-TK v2.1.1 and a phylogeny tree containing these MAGs was constructed with GTDB-TK v2.1.1 and decorated with iTOL v6.6 [98, 99]. Species-level taxonomic phylogeny trees were assigned based on the majority rule of all closest reference genomes using PhyloPhlAn 3.0 v3.0.3 and decorated with iTOL v6.6 to achieve high-quality MAGs (>90% completion, < 10% contamination) [100].
