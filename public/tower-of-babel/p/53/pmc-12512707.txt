Transcriptomic profiling of thrombus-derived extracellular vesicles reveals PECAM-1 as a potential inflammatory marker for cardioembolic stroke patients
Authors: Florencio J D Machado, Juan Marta-Enguita, María Herrera, Javier Marta-Moreno, José A Páramo, Tomás Segura, Gemma Serrano-Heras, Francisco Hernández-Fernández, Lourdes Arias-Salazar, Blanca Yélamos-Sanz, Beatriz Zandio, Nuria Aymerich, Roberto Muñoz, Rebeca Bermejo, Carmen Roncal, Josune Orbe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate etiological diagnosis ensures appropriate secondary prevention of ischemic stroke (IS) patients. However, about 25–40% of IS cases remain of undetermined origin (referred to as ESUS if an unknown embolic source is suspected), highlighting the need to improve diagnostic precision. To identify potential biomarkers of IS etiologies, we analyzed the transcriptomic content of extracellular vesicles (EVs) from thrombi obtained after thrombectomy, including patients with cardioembolic (CE, n = 10), atherothrombotic (AT, n = 10) and ESUS (n = 10) IS (discovery cohort). mRNA levels of those differentially expressed genes in thrombus EVs were determined in microvascular brain endothelial cells after stimulation, revealing PECAM-1, as the most promising candidate. Next, PECAM-1 levels were determined in thrombi (n = 65) and serum samples (n = 95) of an independent validation cohort of IS patients, using immunohistochemistry and ELISA respectively. The transcriptome profiling and bioinformatics analysis revealed 1,514 gene transcripts in thrombus EVs, of which 114 were differentially expressed in AT vs CE etiologies. The genes upregulated in EVs from CE thrombi were enriched in pathways related to cell surface interactions at the vascular wall, leukocyte migration and neutrophil degranulation. Consistent with the increased PECAM-1 expression in thrombus EVs from CE vs AT etiologies, the endothelial expression of PECAM-1 increased after thrombin exposure and diminished upon atherogenic stimulation (oxLDL and TNFα). Moreover, thrombus expression of PECAM-1 was localized predominantly in platelet rich areas containing or surrounded by inflammatory cells and fibrin, and was higher in CE stroke thrombi. Serum levels of PECAM-1 were associated [OR (95%CI) 1.52 (1.14–2.03), p = 0.004] with the severity of stroke at Hospital admission. These findings collectively suggest that the transcriptional study of thrombus EVs may be useful to unravel the molecular pathways behind thrombus formation, and to explore local biological differences between IS etiologies. Specifically, PECAM-1, an adhesion molecule implicated in immuno-inflammatory processes, was increased in thrombi EVs and thrombi of patients with CE IS. Regarding the use of PECAM-1 as systemic biomarker, it did not discriminate IS etiologies, but was positively correlated with worse neurological outcome, suggesting a possible role of PECAM-1 in processes leading to brain injury post-IS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03555-8.Keywords: Ischemic Stroke, Diagnostic, Thrombus, Extracellular vesicles, Transcriptomes, And inflammation related pathways

Introduction

Stroke is the leading cause of long-term disability and the second leading cause of death worldwide. Although large vessel occlusion acute ischemic strokes (LVOS) account for approximately 40% of ischemic strokes, they are strongly associated with severe disability and mortality [1]. Currently, treatment options for LVOS are limited to intravenous alteplase (tPA) or Tenecteplase (TNK) administration within the first 4.5 h after symptom onset, and/or mechanical thrombectomy within the first 24 h [2]. Despite the success of early reperfusion in improving functional outcomes, over 35–40% of patients treated with endovascular intervention and/or tissue plasminogen activators (tPA or TNK), remain severely disabled, and approximately 10% die within 90 days [3]. In this context, little is known about the main trigger of the ischemic event and the primary therapeutic target; the thrombus. Indeed, a significant proportion (roughly 25–40%) of all ischemic strokes are of undetermined etiology, commonly referred to as cryptogenic strokes or ESUS if an unknown embolic source is suspected, despite increasingly advanced and comprehensive diagnostic evaluations [4]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the dynamic processes of thrombus initiation and propagation are necessary in order to translate these findings into proper etiology classification of stroke, which would lead to improved clinical treatment methods, prognosis, and a reduced risk of recurrent events. Previous studies attempted to use computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to predict clot density and its relationship with stroke etiology [5, 6]. In parallel, numerous investigations have aimed to associate the relative composition of the thrombus constituents with the underlying cause of stroke [7]. Although, cerebral thrombus histopathology reveals high heterogeneity in the pattern of distribution and quantity of the most common components, including fibrin, platelets, leukocytes, and red blood cells, these histological features may provide some insights into thrombus´s origin [8, 9]. As such, it has been reported an increased presence of erythrocytes in thrombi of atherosclerotic origin, while thrombi of cardiac origin were enriched in fibrin and platelets [8, 10]. However, the observed variability is high enough to prevent a feasible etiological classification based on these parameters alone. Thus, additional large-scale studies are needed to further clarify the association between thrombus composition, organization, and structure with stroke etiology, and to guide treatment using anticoagulant therapy, antiplatelet therapy, or other strategies to prevent secondary events [11].

Beyond the predominant thrombus components (fibrin, platelet, leukocytes and erythrocytes), extracellular vesicles (EVs) also represent other important constituents of the thrombus. EVs are a group of membrane-derived particles that are characterized by their lipid bilayer structure, which are released into the extracellular environment or circulation in response to cell activation or apoptosis. The diversity of EVs lies in differences in the mechanisms of their biogenesis as well as their cellular origin. EVs have been identified as mediators of cell-to-cell communication due to their ability to contain and transfer bioactive molecular cargo to both distant and neighboring cells. They participate in normal homeostatic processes as well as pathological disease states, reflecting the biological processes occurring in the organism under various conditions, which makes them ideal as biomarkers and also highlights their potential as therapeutic vehicles [12, 13]. During thrombosis, vascular and blood cells interact directly, and additionally, they communicate and connect through EVs released from the different cell types, whose cargo depends largely on the mechanism of EV formation and the stimuli triggering their release [14]. In this regard, it was reported that the release of EVs from activated endothelial cells, platelets and neutrophils, but also from monocytes and erythrocytes, may transfer prothrombotic ‘message’ and promote blood clot formation in the arterial and the venous system [14].

With the aim of improving diagnosis, thereby refining the management and treatment of patients with ischemic stroke (IS), we have studied the transcriptomic content of EVs obtained from thrombi of IS patients and identified differentially expressed genes between EVs of CE and AT origins. The selected candidates have been validated in vitro, in brain endothelial cells, and the protein expression of one of them, PECAM-1, has been further studied in human IS thrombi and in serum samples to establish its association with stroke etiology, outcome, and other clinical parameters.

Material and methods

IS patients

The discovery cohort included 30 patients with LVOS from the General University Hospital of Albacete (Ethical approval number:2019/03/031), and the validation cohort included 122 patients recruited in 2 different hospitals [80 from the Stroke Unit of the Navarra University Hospital (Ethical approval number: 84/2018), and 42 from Miguel Servet University Hospital (Ethical approval number: 16/2021)]. For all participants in the study, a previous written informed consent was obtained directly from them or from an authorized person. Samples and data from patients included in the study were provided by the Biobank of the University of Navarra, the Aragon Health Sciences Institute within the framework of the Biobank of Aragon, and the General University Hospital of Albacete. All samples were processed following standard operating procedures, with the approval from the Human Ethics Committees and Scientific Review Boards, and in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The decision to perform combined treatment [mechanical treatment plus intravenous tissue-type plasminogen activator (tPA)] was made following current standardized recommendations [2]. In these patients, a detailed medical record including age, sex, history of cardiovascular disease, cardiovascular risk factors, glucose level at admission, use of antithrombotic drugs (antiplatelet agent and anticoagulants), and thrombolytic treatment with tPA was registered. IS severity was assessed by the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), a widely recognized tool for assessing neurological deficits in IS patients at hospital admission. Cardiovascular risk factors included: (1) hypertension defined as patients taking antihypertensive drugs or with blood pressure > 140/90 mmHg on repeated measurements, (2) type-2 diabetes: patients on antidiabetic drugs, fasting blood sugar ≥ 126 mg/dL or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or a casual plasma glucose > 200 mg/dL, (3) hypercholesterolemia understood as patients receiving lipid-lowering drugs or with an overnight fasting cholesterol ≥ 240 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥ 200 mg/dL, or low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol ≥ 160 mg/dL, (4) current smoking habits, and (5) atrial fibrillation (AF) before admission or during 3-months after event. We applied the etiological classification of the Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) for IS patients [15]. To evaluate the recanalization rate after endovascular therapy angiographic study was performed and mTICI score applied. A successful recanalization was defined as mTICI 2b, 2c or 3 [16].

To establish the 90 days modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score, a face-to-face interview with a stroke specialized neurologist was performed to determine the following clinical outcomes: a) 3-month all-cause mortality; b) 3-month-functional independence, defined as 90-day mRS < 3.

Medium/large size EVs separation from IS thrombi

All EVs related techniques have been set up based on the recommendations of the International Society of Extracellular Vesicles [17, 18] and our previous experience [19].

EVs separation was performed from frozen thrombus retrieved by mechanical thrombectomy and stored at −80ºC. Briefly, the thrombus was digested with Accutase (StemPro® Accutase® Cell Dissociation Reagent, GIBCO) adding 1µL accutase per 1 mg of thrombus – minimum 250µL of accutase, and was incubated during 60 min at 37ºC at 600 rpm for cell detachment. Then, the digested thrombus was centrifuged at 900 xg (Mikro 22R, Hettich Zentrifugen) for 10 min at room temperature (RT). The obtained supernantant was collected and centrifuged a second time at 12,500 × g for 5 min at RT (Mikro 22R, Hettich Zentrifugen). Finally, the obtained cell-free supernatant was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 70 min at 4ºC (Mikro 22R, Hettich Zentrifugen), and the resulting pellet (enriched in thrombus-derived EVs) was homogenized in 100 µL of filtered PBS (1 × 0.22 µm, followed by 1 × 0.1 µm filter) and stored at − 80ºC.

EVs characterization

Thrombus EVs size distribution and concentration (n = 3 in triplicate) was assessed by Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (NanoSight NS300, Malvern Instruments Limited).

In order to determine the vesicular nature of separated vesicles, thrombi EVs were subjected to carboxyfluorescein N-succinimidyl ester (CFSE, Sigma) staining and its uptake assessed by nanoscale flow cytometry [20]. A total of 10 μL of isolated EVs from 3 patients were labeled with 0.2 mM CFSE for 30 min at 37 °C and washed with 450 μL of wash buffer (2% BSA, 10 mM HEPES, 0.9% NaCl and 7.4 pH) for 70 min at 20,000 × g (Mikro 22R, Hettich Zentrifugen). The supernatant was carefully removed, and the pellet diluted in 100 μL of PBS (filtered 1 × 0.22 µm, followed by 1 × 0.1 µm) and subsequently analyzed on the CytoFlex cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and the CytExpert 2.3 software (Beckman Coulter). To define the analysis gate for EVs, we compared the violet side scatter (Violet-SSC) to the standard SSC using calibrated polystyrene beads of 0.25, 0.58, 0.79, and 1.34 μm (Spherotech).

Flow cytometry assessment of the cellular origin of thrombus EVs

To determine the cellular origin, 5 µl of EVs form 15 patients were labeled with specific antibodies: 20 µg/ml PE antihuman CD62E for endothelium, (clone HCD62E, Biolegend), 1 µg/ml APC anti-human CD41/61 for platelets (clone A2A9/6, Biolegend), 1:10 dilution PC7 anti-human CD11b (clone Bear1, Beckman Coulter) for leukocytes and 5 µg/ml FITC anti-human CD235a for erythrocytes (clone KC16, Beckman Coulter). Isotype control anti-bodies PE mouse IgG2a, ĸ (clone MOPC-173, Biolegend), APC mouse IgG2a,ĸ (clone MOPC-173, Biolegend), PC7 mouse IgG1 (clone 679.1Mc7, Beckman Coulter), FITC mouse IgG1,ĸ (clone MOPC-21, BD) were used as negative controls. Antibodies and corresponding isotype controls were diluted in PBS filtered two times and centrifuged 5 min at 17,400 × g (Mikro 22R, Hettich Zentrifugen) before labeling the EVs (20 min, at RT in darkness). The samples were run in the CytoFlex cytometer and the results were analyzed with CytExpert 2.3 software.

Transcriptomic analysis of Thrombus EVs: RNA-Seq library construction

RNA-Seq was performed in EVs isolated from thrombi (n = 30, discovery cohort) of IS patients from different etiologies following the TOAST classification [CE (n = 10), AT (n = 10) and ESUS (n = 10)] as previously described [20–22]. Thrombus EVs were resuspended in 100 μL of lysis/binding Buffer (Invitrogen). RNA transcripts were captured with dynabeads oligo (dT) (Invitrogen) and reverse transcribed. RNA samples underwent a linear amplification by in vitro transcription followed by a fragmentation into 250–350 bp. Partial Illumina adaptor sequences were used, and a second reverse transcription reaction was performed. Full Illumina adaptor sequences were successfully added and the libraries sequenced (NextSeq 2000, Illumina).

Bioinformatic analysis

Quality of the data was evaluated with FastQC software (v0.11.8) and processing of the reads was carried out using Trimmomatic (v0.38) [23]. The resulting reads were aligned with STAR using GRCh38 human assembly and Gencode v38 as genome annotation reference [24]. Then, duplicated reads were removed applying UMI-tools dedup function [25]. Finally, expression levels were calculated with featureCounts software (v1.6.0) [26]. The obtained gene expression data was normalized using the analysis pipeline available in LIMMA package (TMM normalization and logCPM calculation using Limma voom method) [27]. After quality assessment and outlier detection with R/Bioconductor [28], a filtering process was performed. Genes with read counts lower than 6 in more than the 50% of the samples were excluded. LIMMA was used to identify the genes with significant differential expression and considering the age and sex of each patient in the linear model. The selection of differentially expressed genes was based on a p-value cut off (p < 0.05), and further functional and clustering analyses and graphical representations were performed using R/Bioconductor (v4.1.0) [28].

Gene Enrichment and Protein–protein interaction analysis

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Reactome gene sets pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEG) were performed based on Metascape database v3.5.20240101 (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1).

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) pairs of DEGs were obtained from Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING, http://string-db.org/, version 12.0). In our study, a PPI high confidence score (0.7) was considered the threshold of significance to construct the PPI network.

RNA expression of candidate genes by RT-qPCR in thrombus EVs and cultured microvascular brain endothelial cells

RNA isolation from thrombus EVs

Fifty µL of thrombus EVs from 24 patients with IS were incubated with 3.5 ng/μL Proteinase K (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min at 37ºC, and neutralized with 17.6 μM of Proteinase K inhibitor (Merk) for 10 min at RT. Next, 1 ng/μL RNase A (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was incubated with EVs for 20 min at 37ºC, and neutralized with 1.5 U/μL RNaseOUT (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 5 min at RT. Then, RNA was extracted using ReliaPrep RNA Tissue Miniprep System Kit (Promega) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA was reverse transcribed with random primers (Agilent) and Oligo-dT (Agilent), and the AffinityScript Multiple Temperature Reverse Transcriptase (Agilent).

RNA isolation from cell culture

Total RNA from a human brain microvascular endothelial cell line (hCMEC/D3) was extracted using semi-automated Maxwell RSC simplyRNA tissue kit (Promega) following the manufacturer instructions. cDNA from cell culture was obtained following reverse transcription with 1 × first-strand buffer (Invitrogen), 10 mM dNTPs (Invitrogen), 6 µg random primers (Invitrogen), 10 mM dithiothreitol-DTT reducing agent (Invitrogen), 10 U/µL Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen), and 2 U/µL RNase Out (Invitrogen). The mixture was incubated for 60 min at 37 °C and 5 min at 95 °C in a thermocycler (Veriti™, Thermo Fisher).

Real-time qPCR of thrombus EVs and hCMEC/D3 cell

Real-time qPCR was performed on QuantStudio Real time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using commercially available qPCR gene expression assays (IDT) for PECAM-1 (Hs01065282_m1), IL-8 (Hs.PT.58.39926886.g), ITGB1 (Hs.PT.58.40244627), CD44 (Hs.PT.58.3636696), SLC3A2 (Hs.PT.58.40962829), and GAPDH (Hs.PT.39a.22214836). Data were expressed as raw Ct values for EVs samples. Cell culture data are presented as fold change relative to baseline after expression normalization with GAPDH.

Western blot of thrombus EVs

15–20 μL of isolated thrombus EVs were lysed by thermal shock (3 × 37ºC-liquid N2), loaded in SDS-PAGE gel (4–20% Mini- PROTEAN TGX Stain-Free, Bio-Rad) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Trans-Blot Turbo, Bio-Rad). Blots underwent an overnight incubation with primary antibodies: 0.125 μg/mL Alix (mouse Anti-AIP1, clone 49/AIP1, #611,620, BD Bioscience), 1 μg/mL EMMPRIN (mouse monoclonal antihuman EMMPRIN/CD147, clone IT10C5, #21,451,471, Immunotools), and 1 μg/mL ApoB100 (goat polyclonal anti human Apolipoprotein B100, AF3260, Novus Biologicals), 2 µg/mL anti-PECAM-1 (M0823, Dako), 0.5 µg/mL anti-IL8 (HPA057179, Sigma), 0.5 µg/mL anti-ITGB1 (HPA059297, Sigma), 2 µg/mL anti-CD44 (MA5-13,890, Invitrogen), and 1:5000 anti-SLC3A2 (15,193–1-AP, Invitrogen). Peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied as needed. Peroxidase activity was detected with a chemiluminescent substrate (TMA-6, Lumigen) and images acquired with Chemidoc imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis of retrieved thrombi

A total of 65 retrieved thrombi from the validation cohort were fixed in formaldehyde (PanReac AppliChem, Spain) for 24 h for PECAM-1 analysis. Then, samples were embedded in paraffin using a tissue automatic processor (Tissue-Tek VIP) and cut into 3 µm sections with a rotatory microtome (HM-340E). Serial slides from each thrombus were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (PanReac AppliChem); Martius scarlet Blue (MSB), and antibodies for specific cell types as previously described [9]. In addition, PECAM-1 immunochemistry was performed with the primary antibody anti- PECAM-1 (2 µg/mL, M0823, Dako). Mouse secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (Envision System HRP labeled polymer, Dako) was used as required. Slides were scanned using Aperio CS2 system (Leica Biosystems) and analyzed with Fiji ImageJ Software. The brown regions and tissue area were automatically detected using a macro designed for this purpose. Data are presented as the percentage of positive stained area in total tissue area.

Analysis of circulating PECAM-1 by ELISA

Serum levels of PECAM-1 from a total of 95 patients (validation cohort) were measured by ELISA (ELH-PECAM-1, Raybiotech), with intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation of 10% and less than 12%, respectively, following the manufacture’s protocols.

Microvascular brain endothelial cell culture and stimulation

Human brain microvascular endothelial cell line (hCMEC/D3, SC0066, Merck), was seeded on 0.7 mg/mL rat collagen type I in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich, 08–115) coated 75 cm2 flask and cultured in complete EndoGROTM-MV medium (Merck, SCME004) supplemented with 1% penicillin and streptomycin (PS, 100 U/mL and 100 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were maintained in a humidified environment at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and the medium was renewed every 2–3 days.

Then, confluent cells were washed with PBS and incubated overnight in serum-free media endothelial cells (SFMEC, 11,111–044, GIBCO) and stimulated for 6 h and 12 h with 0.5 or 1 U/mL Thrombin (T4393, SIGMA), 10 ng/mL Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-alpha, GF023, Merck), and 10 ng/mL Interleukin 1 beta (IL1beta, IL038, MERK), and for 24 h with 100 µg/ml oxidized low-density lipoprotein (LDLO13-N-1, Alpha Diagnostic). After stimulation, cells were collected in 200 µL homogenization buffer (Maxwell RSC simplyRNA tissue kit, Promega), and stored at −80ºC until RNA isolation.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical data are presented as number (percentage). The normality of the data was assessed with Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Differences between categorical data were analyzed by Chi square test. Differences between two independent groups were determined with the unpaired Student’s T-test for parametric, or Mann–Whitney U test for the non-parametric variables. Multiple comparisons were assessed with ANOVA followed by Tukey’s and Dunnett's test or Kruskal–Wallis followed by Mann–Whitney U test for parametric and non-parametric variables respectively. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed by Pearson or Spearman tests. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were used to assess the utility of the candidates in predicting stroke etiology. The variable with best predictive performance was dichotomized at the cutoff point maximizing Youden’s index and analyzed using multivariable binary logistic regression. We selected variables with p ≤ 0.10 in the univariate analysis to enter as covariates into the multivariate model. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and only two-sided p values were used. Statistical analysis was performed by IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM Corporation, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., California, USA).

Results

Characterization of thrombus EVs

Prior to the transcriptomic experiments, routine laboratory analyses were performed to confirm the vesicular nature of the separated thrombus EVs [18]. Flow cytometry analysis showed thrombus EVs within the working gate, and the majority of them (71%) were able to uptake and process CFSE (Fig. 1A-C). By NTA, thrombus EVs showed a polydisperse distribution with particles with a mean size [SD] of 158 [± 2] nm, and a mean concentration of 1.4 × 1010 ± 1.2 × 109 particles/mL (Fig. 1D) and by western blot the presence of the EVs markers Alix and EMMPRIN was detected while APOB100, commonly associated with co-precipitated contaminants, was not detected (Supplementary Fig. 1). Finally, the cellular origin of thrombus EVs, determined by flow cytometry, showed a predominance of platelet and leukocyte derived EVs followed by EVs from endothelial cells and erythrocytes (Fig. 1E).

Clinical characteristics of the discovery cohort

As described in Table 1, thrombi EVs were separated from a total of 30 patients (discovery cohort) with AT, CE, and ESUS IS (n = 10 per group) with ages ranging from 65 to 78.5 years and no female patients in AT group. Tobacco use was reported exclusively in AT patients (50%), while 100% of ESUS presented hypertension. Dyslipidemia and diabetes were observed across all groups, the former being higher in ESUS. As expected, atrial fibrillation was predominant in the CE patients (100%), and previous IS was 10% for all groups. Regarding treatment, 40% of ESUS patients had antiplatelet therapy and 60% of CE were on anticoagulants, while statin use was comparable across groups. In acute treatment, fibrinolytic therapy was most frequently used in ESUS patients (40%), with successful recanalization observed in nearly all patient groups (96.6%). Severe baseline NIHSS scores were highest in ESUS patients (90%), while functional independence at three months was achieved exclusively in CE patients (100%). No mortality was reported across any of the groups.

Differentially expressed molecules are detected by other methods in EVs

Based on the bioinformatics analysis, we selected PECAM1, IL-8, ITGB1, CD44, and SLC3A2, decreased in AT thrombi EVs compared with CE EVs, to check by other techniques their presence in EVs.

We performed RT-qPCR for the selected genes on EVs from an independent cohort of IS thrombi (n = 24), although, as shown in Fig. 3A, the scarce RNA content of EVs, and the lower sensitivity of this technique vs RNAseq, prevented detection of the signal in most of the tested samples or genes, except for IL-8. SLC3A2 was undetermined in all samples. Qualitatively, the number of samples with detectable vs undetectable mRNA for PECAM-1, ITGB1, CD44 and IL-8 was higher in EVs from thrombi of CE origin compared with AT etiology. For CE etiology detection frequency was above 30% for all 4 genes, while it was lower than 10% for AT etiology, except for IL-8.

Increased PECAM-1 expression in CE thrombi revealed by immunological analysis

The expression pattern of PECAM-1 in brain endothelial cells mimicked that of thrombus EVs, decreasing in response to atherogenic and proinflammatory molecules (oxLDL and TNFα), while increasing after thrombin stimulation. These results prompted us to determine its expression morphologically in an independent larger cohort of thrombi (n = 65, validation cohort).

Thrombus median expression of PECAM-1 was 12.0% (7.75–23.6) of total area, and correlated with the platelet marker CD42b (Fig. 5A). In detail, PECAM-1 was localized predominantly in platelet rich areas containing or surrounded by inflammatory cells and fibrin (Supplementary Fig. 3, and Fig. 5). In addition, thrombus PECAM-1 expression was lower in dyslipidaemic patients and on those taking anti-platelet or statin therapies (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we performed a transcriptomic analysis of thrombus EVs from patients with IS, revealing important insights into EVs composition and the biological pathways associated with different stroke etiologies. Following RNA-seq analysis of thrombus EVs, 5 potential candidate genes related to IS etiology were identified as differentially expressed showing higher expression levels in CE as compared to AT strokes. These genes are involved in cell surface adhesion and leukocyte migration: PECAM-1, IL-8, ITGB1, CD44, and SLC3A2. Among these, thrombus PECAM-1 stood out as a potential biomarker of CE stroke etiology, supported by its higher expression in thrombi of cardioembolic origin. Consistently, in brain microvascular endothelial cells PECAM-1 expression increased in response to thrombin stimulus and decreased upon atherogenic triggers. Furthermore, our study revealed an independent association between increased serum PECAM-1 levels with IS severity, although no correlation was found with etiology.

To gain insights into the cellular composition of IS thrombi, we analyzed the cellular origin of thrombi EVs. Our results showed a predominance of platelet and leukocytes-derived EVs and lower levels of vesicles from endothelial or erythrocyte origin, that did not completely match previous anatomopathological analysis of IS thrombi where erythrocytes account for one third or half of the total area [11]. However, in line with our results, Christoph Klatt et al. reported that less than 2% of RBCs were present in thrombi formed with whole blood under flow conditions [29]. The question remains on whether the enzymatic treatment applied prior to the separation of thrombus EVs could introduce a bias in the distribution of the epitopes used to identify EV cell types [13, 30]

The content of EVs, including RNAs, protein, etc., depends on the cellular activation status. Therefore, the analysis of the transcriptional content of EVs post-IS could be a valuable tool for delving into the molecular pathways of IS, enabling the identification of novel biomarkers for IS etiology and prognosis. The bioinformatics analysis revealed the enrichment in gene sets related to cellular response to stress, and respiratory electron transport between upregulated genes in AT vs CE thrombi EVs, whereas genes related to cellular interactions and inflammatory responses were downregulated. Consistent with our findings, other authors reported an enrichment in molecules related to inflammation in the blood of CE stroke patients [31]. The possible involvement of neutrophils in CE IS was also suggested after proteomic analysis, where CE thrombi presented an upregulation of 20 proteins belonging to the innate immune system compared to AT thrombi [32]. In line with this data, other proteomic studies have reported differences on clot-endothelium interaction pathways [33, 34], and on the expression of molecules involved in platelet-immune cell communication processes between CE and AT thrombi. RNAseq of IS clots reinforce the hypothesis of an overall neutrophil, platelet, and innate immune system dependent activation in CE clots, while AT clots had greater enrichment in the oxygen transport, the oxidoreductase activity, and the T-cell activation processes [35].

Among the differentially expressed genes, PECAM-1, ITGB1, CD44, SLC3A2 and IL-8 emerge as potential candidates for CE IS etiology, being all of them previously associated to molecular and cellular processes potentially related to the pathophysiology of IS [36–43]. The response of the candidates to inflammatory, atherogenic and prothrombotic molecules in brain endothelial cells was dissimilar, although PECAM-1 behaved as if responding to a CE insult, augmenting after thrombin stimulation and decreasing upon oxLDL and TNFa. In contrast to ECs from other vascular beds [44], oxLDL promoted a downregulation of PECAM-1 in brain ECs, suggesting that the brain endothelial cells could display different characteristics in the context of atherogenic stimulation, and highlight the complexity associated with the functions of this molecule depending on the vascular bed. Thrombin on the other hand, upregulated PECAM-1 expression in brain ECs. Thrombin is a key enzyme in coagulation and thrombosis whose role in AF and CE stroke can be inferred based on the effectiveness of oral anticoagulants in the primary and secondary prevention of IS [45]. Our results reinforced previous findings reporting elevated PECAM-1 expression after thrombin stimulation in microvascular ECs [36], and in the surface of brain EC microvesicles [46]. Regarding inflammation, our data are in line with other studies reporting that certain cytokine combinations, ie, TNFα and IFNγ, can reduce the expression of PECAM-1 on ECs [44]. Such a reduction may occur as a result of a true downregulation of PECAM-1 expression or as a redistribution of PECAM-1 molecules away from the junctions. Moreover, data obtained by intravital microscopy in rats and in PECAM-1–deficient mice indicated that leukocyte migration is elicited by local administration of IL-1β, but not TNFα [44], arising the possibility that the role of PECAM-1 in transmigration might depend on which cell type is activated, either leukocytes or endothelium.

To dissect the role of PECAM-1 in IS, also at a local level, we performed immunohistochemistry in IS thrombi. CE thrombi presented a significant and independent increase in PECAM-1 expression when compared to AT thrombi, being localized in platelet rich areas containing or surrounded by inflammatory cells and fibrin which suggest a possible role in platelet-leukocytes interaction. In fact, activated platelets may adhere to leukocytes and form circulating mixed aggregates that are considered a marker of a prothrombotic state in several cardiovascular conditions. The molecular mechanisms responsible of this cellular interaction include platelet P-selectin and P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1), its counter receptor in leukocytes, which result in the activation of the beta-2 integrin Mac-1 and in the firm adhesion between the two cell types. The interaction of P-selectin with PSGL-1 also induces upregulation of leukocyte tissue factor, biosynthesis of several cytokines and other inflammatory reactions, thereby contributing to the thrombotic progression [47].

Finally, we explored serum levels of PECAM-1 in IS patients. Although we did not find an association with stroke etiology, augmented PECAM-1 concentrations were independently associated with IS severity. In this context, PECAM-1 protein levels were markedly increased in the ischemic hemisphere of mice brains after transient middle cerebral artery occlusion, and the loss or blockade of PECAM-1 significantly dampened leukocyte invasion to the ischemic area, attenuating lesion volumes [48]. The results of the present study nicely integrate with these prior findings, suggesting that PECAM-1 could be a promising target for improving stroke outcomes.

This study has certain limitations. Firstly, the total number of samples was limited, specifically in the case of AT IS samples, which are less prevalent compared to CE IS. Secondly, the RNA content of EVs is scarce thereby compromising the sensitivity of conventional PCR in detecting specific transcripts within EVs. Thirdly, the results of this study are for generation of new hypothesis for further study, and that a larger sample size should be used in future validation studies.

In conclusion, through the transcriptomic analysis of thrombus EVs, we have identified altered molecular pathways capable of distinguishing between AT or CE etiologies. These pathways involve processes such as cell–cell and cell-surface interactions, as well as leukocyte migration and inflammatory response, which are upregulated in CE IS. Among the differentially expressed molecules, thrombus PECAM-1 correlated significantly with CE etiology, and its serum levels were associated with stroke severity at hospital admission. These findings suggest a potential role of this adhesion molecule, involved in leukocyte migration during the inflammatory response, in the pathophysiology of IS, with greater implications in the development of cardioembolic thrombi and its possible future use as potential target for specific treatments.
