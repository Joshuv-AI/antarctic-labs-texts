Genomic and lipidomic analysis reveals lipid mediation of genetic risk in gestational diabetes
Authors: Yao Dong, An-qun Hu, Bai-xue Han, Qing Li, Hai-yan Liu, Jin-jin Liu, Zong-guang Li, Zi-qiang Qian, Meng-ting Cao, Long-nan Pan, Yun-yun Xu, Yao Pei, Fang Wu, Yan Tan, Yong-fu Yu, Ying-jie Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a complex metabolic disorder with a substantial genetic component; however, whether lipid species mediate the impact of genetic susceptibility on GDM risk remains unclear. This study aimed to characterize genetic architecture of GDM and evaluate whether lipid species mediate the genetic risk.MethodsA cohort and case-cohort design were implemented within the Anqing Pregnant Women Cohort. Maternal blood was collected at 15–20 gestational weeks, and GDM was diagnosed at 24–28 weeks. A Meta-GWAS of GDM was performed using regression-based methods, and polygenic risk score (PRS) was constructed. Lipidomic analysis was performed to identity differential lipid species. Risk Ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated via weighted Poisson regression. Weighted co-abundance network analysis (WGCNA) was applied to cluster lipids into co-abundance modules. Mediation analyses were used to estimate the extent to which lipid species statistically accounted for the association between genetic risk and GDM. A nested 5-fold cross-validation framework was used to explore predictive models and to evaluate the incremental value of PRS and lipid features.ResultsThe Meta-GWAS of 536 GDM and 2,051 controls identified one genome-wide significant risk locus (LIPF) and eight suggestive loci, including two novel signals (IL17B, RPL15P2). Implicated genes were enriched in glycerolipid metabolism pathways. Lipidomic analysis in a case-cohort of 102 GDM and 111 controls identified 134 differential lipid species. WGCNA yielded 14 distinct co-abundance modules, five of which were significantly associated with GDM. Lipid mediators, including specific triacylglycerols, phosphatidylethanolamines, and hexosylceramides, collectively accounted for approximately 31.10% of the overall genetic effect on GDM. Modules enriched for these lipids showed varying degrees of statistical mediation for rs16885782 (CDKAL1), rs11236507 (DGAT2) and rs10830963 (MTNR1B), with mediation effect ranging from 10.30% to 22.07%. The incorporation of lipid biomarkers significantly improved GDM prediction (AUC = 0.849, P = 0.001), whereas further adding PRS did not show significant improvement (AUC = 0.858, P = 0.095).ConclusionsGenes involved in lipid metabolism are associated with GDM susceptibility. Lipids potentially mediate the genetic risk of GDM, and lipid biomarkers showed potential for improving predictive performance. These findings require further validation in independent cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-05083-5.

Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as new-onset hyperglycemia during pregnancy in women without pre-existing diabetes, affecting approximately 14.0% of pregnancies worldwide [1]. Beyond its high prevalence, GDM increases risks of multiple adverse pregnancy outcomes, including gestational hypertension, preterm birth, and macrosomia [2]. Moreover, GDM plays a central role in the intergenerational cycle of “diabetes begetting diabetes”: women with GDM exhibit a notably elevated risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) postpartum, while their offspring are predisposed to T2DM, obesity and cardiometabolic disorders [2]. Thus, prevention, early diagnosis, and timely management of GDM are crucial for reducing subsequent adverse health consequences for both mothers and children.

GDM has well-documented associations with demographic, behavioral, and clinical risk factors [3]. However, the pathogenesis mechanisms, particularly in Chinese populations, remain incompletely understood due to its multifactorial etiology. Also, prediction models based solely on these established risk factors achieve only modest predictive ability [4]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) provide a hypothesis-free approach to dissect the biological mechanisms underlying complex phenotypes. Our prior GWAS on GDM, using a suggestive significance threshold of P < 1e-5, revealed gene sets enriched for lipid metabolism pathways [5], and other genetic studies of GDM have reported risk loci such as PPARG [6] and FTO [7], both involved in adipogenesis and lipid metabolism. These findings provide evidence for the hypothesis that genetic variation contributes to GDM development by affecting lipid metabolism during pregnancy.

Nevertheless, the understanding of the genetic architecture of GDM remains limited. While multiple GWASs have identified distinct genetic loci associated with GDM, studies focusing on Chinese populations remain limited. Apart from the large-scale work utilizing non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) data [8, 9], most prior investigations, including our earlier work in the Anqing Pregnant Women Cohort (APWC) [5], were critically constrained by small sample sizes (< 200 GDM cases). This limited statistical power likely explains the absence of genome-wide significant loci. Moreover, lipid metabolism is mechanistically linked to insulin resistance [10] and growing evidence indicated that lipid metabolism disturbances precede GDM onset [11]. This provides biological plausibility for the hypothesis that genetic variation contributes to GDM through its effects on lipid metabolism. However, it remains unclear whether and to what extent specific lipid species mediate the genetic influence on GDM. Elucidating such mediation effects may help identify potential intervention targets for pregnant women with a high genetic susceptibility to GDM.

To address these gaps, we conducted an GDM GWAS with over 500 GDM cases to uncover novel susceptibility loci. Additionally, we conducted targeted lipidomic analysis to select potential lipid biomarkers, and integrated genomic and lipidomic data to investigate the mediating role of lipid species and to explore the utility of combined biomarkers for GDM prediction.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed both cohort and case-cohort designs based on the APWC. A total of 4,498 pregnant women were recruited from Anqing Municipal Hospital in two stages, divided due to the COVID-19 epidemic (Stage 1: February 2018-December 2020; Stage 2: February 2022-October 2024). Eligible participants were those who underwent Down syndrome screening in early mid-pregnancy and met the inclusion criteria (Detailed in Additional File 1: Sect. 1). Questionnaire survey and blood sample collection was constructed at baseline enrollment (15 ~ 20w), and participants were followed till pregnancy termination.

A combined design was adopted for cost-effectiveness, with Stage 1 serving as full cohort and Stage 2 as a case-cohort study. In Stage 2, a subcohort was selected from baseline participants using a 1:4 systematic sampling strategy. After exclusion of ineligible participants, eligible subcohort members, together with incident GDM cases identified outside the subcohort, were jointly included in the Stage 2 case-cohort. Two analytical subsets were defined: (1) Analytical subset I, comprising the Stage 1 full cohort (200 GDM cases vs. 1,701 controls) and the Stage 2 case-cohort (336 GDM cases vs. 350 controls), used for the meta-GWAS of GDM; and (2) Analytical subset II, consisting of 102 GDM cases and 111 non-GDM controls enrolled after October 2023, used for the lipidomic mediation analyses (Fig. 1). In addition, participants from Stage 1 and the Stage 2 subcohort with both gestational glycemic measurements and genomic data were included in GWASs of three glycemic traits, including 2,051 participants with fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 1,709 with oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at 1 h (1 h-OGTT), and 1,661 with 2 h-OGTT measurements (Fig. 1).

Phenotype definition

The diagnostic criteria for GDM are based on the IADPSG standards [12]. Pregnant women without a pre-existing diabetes diagnosis underwent a 75 g OGTT at 24–28 gestational weeks. Plasma glucose levels were measured fasting and at 1-hour, and 2-hours post-glucose load. GDM was diagnosed if one or more test results met the following thresholds: (1) FPG: 5.1-7.0 mmol/L, (2) 1 h-OGTT ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or (3) 2 h-OGTT: 8.5–11.1 mmol/L. Individuals meeting the diagnostic criteria for pre-pregnant diabetes were reclassified and excluded [13].

Genome-wide association study of GDM and glycemic traits

Genotyping was performed using the ASA-CHIA (Infinium Asian Screening Array-China Health Industry Alliance) Bead chip (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, California, USA). Details on imputation and quality control are presented in Additional File 1: Sect. 2 [14–18]. GWAS for GDM was performed separately for each stage using PLINK 2.0 [19] and GCTA (v1.94.1) [20], followed by standard error-based fixed-effects meta-analysis using METAL [21]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to address potential population stratification. Covariates included significant principal components, body mass index (BMI), and cohort stage (for glycemic traits only). Associations with P < 1e-5 were considered suggestive significant, and P < 5e-8 as genome-wide significant. SNP-based heritability was estimated using genome-based restricted maximum likelihood (GREML) method in GCTA (v1.94.1) [20].

Functional annotation was performed using the FUMA GWAS platform [22] (see Additional File 1: Sect. 3). Gene mapping was based on expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL), position, and chromatin interactions. Candidate genes identified by at least two mapping methods were subjected to overrepresentation analyses using WebGestalt (version 2024) across the KEGG, GO, and WikiPathways databases. Trait enrichment and tissue-specific expression (30 GTEx v8 tissues) were also assessed using FUMA [22].

Construction of genetic risk score and polygenic risk score

After performing internal linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning (window = 250 kb, r2 = 0.1), a genetic risk score (GRS) was constructed from 16 SNPs with P < 1e-5 from the GWAS result to characterize the genetic architecture of GDM and serve as the exposure in subsequent mediation analyses. To more effectively evaluate the predictive performance of genetic variation, the dataset was split into source and target subsets for the construction of polygenic risk scores (PRSs). The source population included all participants except those in Analytical Subset II, while Analytical Subset II served as the target population. GWASs for GDM and three glycemic traits were repeated in the source population using the same analytical procedures. The LD reference panel was derived from the East Asian (EAS) population of the 1000 Genomes Project. PRSs were generated using PRS-CS [23], which applies Bayesian regression to estimate posterior effect size for each SNP, and individual PRSs were subsequently calculated in Analytical Subset II using PLINK 2.0.

Lipidomic profiling

Serum lipid metabolites were quantified using LC-MS as detailed in Additional File 1: Sect. 4. Concentrations below the limit of detection were treated as missing. Lipids with > 5% missingness were excluded, and remaining missing values were imputed using the half-minimum method [24]. Prior to statistical analysis, lipid concentrations were log-transformed and Z-score standardized to reduce skewness and improve comparability [25]. Candidate lipids were prioritized based on the following criteria: (i) a variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 derived from orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) using the ropls R package [26], and (ii) false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted-P < 0.05 in both Student’s t-test and multivariate logistic regression adjusted for potential confounders. The robustness of the OPLS-DA model was evaluated using 1,000 permutation tests. Candidate lipids were submitted to the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 for pathway enrichment and selection for potential biomarkers.

Weighted co-abundance network analysis

Given that lipids exhibit highly coordinate variations driven by shared enzymatic pathways or common transcriptional regulation, univariate analyses may not adequately capture correlated metabolic patterns. To address this limitation, Therefore, weighted co-abundance network analysis (WGCNA) was applied to group lipids into modules based on pairwise correlation structure. After excluding outlier samples identified via hierarchical clustering, a weighted network was constructed. The soft-thresholding power (β) was selected based on the scale-free topology criterion. Modules were identified using a dynamic tree-cutting algorithm. For each module, the module eigengene (ME), defined as the first principal component of the standardized lipid levels within the module, was calculated to represent its overall profile. Associations between MEs and GDM as well as gestational glycemic traits were evaluated using multivariable regression models. WGCNA was performed using the “WGCNA” R package, with detailed algorithmic parameters provided in the Additional File 1: Sect. 5.

Mediation analysis and sensitivity analysis

Genetic exposures included GRS and individual SNPs demonstrated suggestive significance (P < 1e-5) in the primary GWAS and were subsequently replicated in Analytical subset Ⅱ (P < 0.05) with consistent effect directions. Potential mediators were defined as lipids significantly associated with genetic variations, and independently associated with GDM after adjusting for corresponding genetic exposures. Lipids were incorporated into the mediation analysis in three complementary forms: (i) individual lipids to estimate lipid-specific mediating effects; (ii) principal components explaining > 80% of cumulative variance of potential lipid mediators to assess collective mediation estimates; (iii) MEs of lipid co-abundance modules to capture the aggregate mediation estimates of co-regulated lipid groups. Multiple testing was controlled using the FDR, applied separately for analyses of individual lipids (n = 718) and lipid modules (n = 14), with FDR-P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Mediation effects were estimated using the quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo method with 1,000 simulations using the mediation R package [27]. Mediator models (Exposure to mediator) were fitted using linear regression, while the outcome models (Exposure + mediator to outcome) were fitted using Poisson regression with robust variance estimation to approximate risk ratios. Mediator and outcome models were adjusted for maternal age, education level, pre-pregnancy BMI, baseline gestational weight-gain, age at menarche and menstrual cycle. To account for the case-cohort design, inverse probability weights based on sampling fractions were applied to obtain estimates representative of the source population [28].

To evaluate robustness, an alternative inverse probability weighting (IPW) strategy was used to re-estimate proportion mediated. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses for unmeasured confounding were conducted using the product of partial R² for the mediator and outcome models (R2M*R2Y), representing the strength of a hypothetical unmeasured confounder jointly associated with the mediator and outcome [29]. We calculated the magnitude of R2M*R2Y required to attenuate the observed mediation estimate to the null and compared it with the variance explained by measured covariates.

Development of GDM prediction model

To explore potential biomarkers and evaluate the incremental predictive value of PRSs and lipid candidates, a nested five-fold cross-validation framework was implemented. The dataset was partitioned into five stratified sets to preserve the case-control ratio. Within each outer cross validation fold, lipid feature selection and model development were conducted exclusively using the training set. Specifically, lipid features were selected via stability selection with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression. Within each training set, 1,000 bootstrap iterations were performed, and the optimal regularization parameter (lambda.1se) was determined via internal 5-fold cross-validation. Lipids features selected in ≥ 85% across bootstrap iterations were retained for the predictive models. LASSO was performed using glmnet R package [30].

Four predictive models were constructed: (i) Base model including maternal age, education level, pre-pregnancy BMI, baseline gestational weight-gain, age at menarche, menstrual cycle, (ii) Genetic model, comprising the base model plus PRSs, (iii) Lipid model, comprising the base model plus selected lipids, (iv) Combined model, comprising the base model, PRSs, and selected lipids. Model performance was assessed in the held-out test sets using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with overall performance summarized by averaging AUCs across all folds. Predictive models were constructed using logistic regression. Statistical differences in predictive performance were evaluated using resampling-based comparisons across cross-validation folds. Predictive improvement was quantified using integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and category-free net reclassification improvement (NRI) [31].

Furthermore, lipids with a selection frequency exceeding 80% across the outer cross-validation folds were identified as potential lipid biomarkers. The linearity of associations between potential lipid biomarkers was evaluated using restricted cubic splines (RCS). For model interpretability, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) [32] were applied to the a combined model incorporating baseline predictors, PRSs, and potential lipid biomarkers fitted in Analytical Subset II. SHAP values were used to reflect model-specific feature contribution of the combined model.

Statistics and reproducibility

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR). Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using Student’s t-tests. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The required sample size and post hoc power analysis are provided in Additional File 1: Sect. 6 [33–35].

Baseline characteristics showing significant differences between GDM and non-GDM groups, together with factors from prior knowledge, were considered potential confounders. These included maternal age, education level, pre-pregnancy BMI, baseline gestational weight-gain, age at menarche and menstrual cycle. Missing values were imputed using random forest method. Unless stated specified, all statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Development Core Team).

Results

Baseline characteristics of pregnant women

Among the 4,498 participants enrolled, 4,053 were followed up to pregnancy outcomes, of whom 536 (13.2%) were diagnosed with GDM. The median baseline gestational age was 16.43 weeks [IQR: 16.00-16.86]. Women who developed GDM were characterized by higher maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, baseline gestational weight-gain, and younger age at menarche, and a greater proportion reported a family history of diabetes or irregular menstruation before conception (see Additional file 2: Table S1). No significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between subcohort in Analytical dataset II and all eligible participants from the same period (see Additional file 2: Table S2), indicating its representativeness.

Genome-wide association study of GDM and glycemic traits during pregnancy

After imputation, a total of 3,852,467 SNPs were retained for association analyses, identifying 76 significant variants for GDM (P < 1e-5) (see Additional file 2: Table S3). A genome-wide significant locus (LIPF) was detected, along with eight loci showing suggestive significance. Among these, three loci (MTNR1B, CDKAL1, LIPF) were previously associated with both GDM and T2DM, four (LINC01098, DGAT2/MOGAT2, HCFC2P1, MACROD2) were linked to T2DM but not GDM, and two (IL17B, RPL15P2) were suggestively novel (Fig. 2a; Additional file 2: Table S4). The SNP-based heritability of GDM (hg²) was estimated at 15.1% (95% CI: 5.6–24.6%, P = 0.04). Gene mapping identified 75 genes, with 21 candidate genes mapped by at least two methods (Additional file 2: Table S5). Enrichment analysis revealed significant overrepresentation of lipid metabolism-related biological processes, including glycerolipid metabolism, triglyceride lipase activity, lipoprotein lipase activity and triglyceride synthesis (FDR-P < 0.05; Fig. 2b, Additional file 2: Table S6, Additional file 3: Fig. S1). Enrichment in the GWAS Catalog further indicated shared genetic architecture between GDM and lipid-related traits, particularly HDL cholesterol levels (Fig. 2c). Differentially expression analysis showed upregulated in adipose tissue (Additional file 3: Fig. S2).

A total of 25 risk loci were identified for glycemic traits (P < 1e-5), including 13 suggestively novel associations. Notably, intronic variants rs10830963 in MTNR1B reached genome-wide significance for 1 h-OGTT, while showing consistent associations across GDM, FPG, and 1 h-OGTT (Additional file 2: Table S4). Colocalization analysis yielded a PP.H4 > 0.6 at rs10830963 among GDM, FPG, and 1 h-OGTT (Additional file 2: Table S7).

Lipidome profiling of GDM

A total of 753 lipid metabolites were quantified by LC-MS; 718 passed quality control and were categorized into 5 classes, 17 categories, and 31 subclasses (Fig. 3a). OPLS-DA identified 184 lipids with VIP > 1, 1,000 permutation tests suggested no substantial overfitting (Q2 intercept < 0) (Additional file 3: Fig. S3). Of these, 134 lipids were associated with GDM in t-tests and multivariable logistic regression (Fig. 3b; Additional file 2: Table S8; Additional file 3: Fig. S4). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed glycerophospholipid metabolism as the most significantly enriched pathway (FDR-P < 0.05) (Fig. 3c; Additional file 2: Table S9).

One outlier was identified during quality control and excluded from subsequent analyses. WGCNA yielded 14 distinct co-abundance modules, each labeled by a unique color, while 33 lipids remained unassigned and were categorized into the grey module (Additional file 3: Fig. S5; Additional file 2: Table S10). The constituent lipids of each module and their respective associations with phenotypes were detailed in Additional file 3: Fig. S6. Among these modules, the brown-colored module was negatively associated with GDM, FPG and 1 h-OGTT glucose level. This module was predominantly enriched in hexosylceramides (HexCers), phosphatidylcholines (PCs) and sphingomyelins (SMs). Conversely, the cyan-, green-, and tan-colored modules showed significant positive associations with both GDM and 1 h-OGTT level. Additionally, the black- and pink- colored modules were positively associated with GDM and 1 h-OGTT level, respectively. The green-, tan- and pink-colored modules were predominantly composed of triacylglycerols (TGs), the black-colored module consisted primarily of diacylglycerols (DGs), and the cyan-colored module was enriched in phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs). Except for the brown module, no other modules were significantly associated with FPG, and no modules demonstrated significant associations with 2 h-OGTT glucose (Fig. 3d).

Mediation analysis

In Analytical subset Ⅱ, the GRS was significantly associated with GDM risk (RR [95% CI] = 1.92 [1.59–2.31], P < 0.001). Eleven lipids were linked to the GRS and 134 were associated with GDM after GRS adjustment (FDR-P < 0.05), with 10 overlapping as potential mediators (Fig. 4a, Additional file 2: Table S11). Nine lipids demonstrated significant mediation estimates (P for indirect effect < 0.05), including higher levels of one DG, five TGs, two PEs, and lower level one HexCer. Among these, HexCer d18:2/23:0 showed the largest estimated indirect effect (Fig. 4b-c, Additional file 2: Table S12). Furthermore, multivariable mediation analysis based on principal components indicated a significant collective mediation effect, with a total mediation proportion of 31.1% (Fig. 4d, Additional file 2: Table S13).

Among 45 SNPs replicated in Analytical subset Ⅱ (Additional file 2: Table S14), one lead SNP per locus was analyzed for mediation. These included established GDM risk loci, such as rs16885782 (CDKAL1) and rs10830963 (MTNR1B), as well as suggestive novel loci identified in the current study including rs11236507 (DGAT2). After FDR correction, no individual SNP showed significant associations with individual lipid species (Additional file 2: Table S11). When evaluating lipid co-abundance modules as mediators, four potential mediation pathways were identified: GRS-Tan-GDM, rs16885782-Cyan-GDM, rs10830963-Brown-GDM, and rs11236507-Tan-GDM, all showing significant mediation estimates with mediation proportions of 9.77%, 10.30%, 15.84%, and 22.07%, respectively (Fig. 4e-f, Additional file 2: Table S15).

Sensitivity analysis using IPW yielded mediation proportions consistent with primary findings (Additional file 2: Table S16). Assessment of potential unmeasured confounding indicated that mediation effects were relatively robust (Additional file 2: Table S12). Furthermore, incorporating interaction terms of genetic variants and lipid mediators revealed no simultaneous significance in both mediation and interaction effects (Additional file 2: Table S17).

Prediction model of GDM

Compared with the base model (AUC = 0.620), inclusion of PRSs did not significantly improve predictive performance (AUC = 0.653, P = 0.183), although significantly improved discrimination with an NRI of 0.370 (95% CI: 0.103–0.637) and an IDI of 0.046 (95% CI: 0.012–0.080). In contrast, incorporating lipid biomarkers yielded improvements across all indexes, with an AUC of 0.849 (P = 0.001), an NRI of 1.117 (95% CI: 0.891–1.343), and an IDI of 0.435 (95% CI: 0.352–0.519). Adding PRSs to the lipid model did not further increase the AUC (AUC = 0.858, P = 0.095), although improved discrimination with an NRI of 0.581 (95% CI: 0.323–0.840) (Fig. 5a, Additional file 2: Table S18).

Four potential lipid biomarkers were consistently selected in at least four cross-validation folds, including PC(P) 36:1, Cer d18:1/24:1, LPE 18:0 and PS 36:2 (Fig. 5b, Additional file 2: Table S19). RCS analysis revealed significant linear associations between these four lipid biomarkers and GDM risk (Fig. 5c). In the combined model integrating baseline predictors, PRSs, and potential lipid biomarkers fitted in Analytical subset II, the four potential biomarkers showed more contributions to model predictions than PRSs, accounting for 8.43–27.87% of the total contribution as based on SHAP values (Fig. 5d, Additional file 2: Table S20).

Discussion

This study integrated genetic, lipidomic, and phenotypic data from the APWC to elucidate determinants of GDM, and assess the potential mediating role of lipid species in genetic susceptibility. We identified one genome-wide significant locus (LIPF) and eight suggestive loci, with enrichment analyses implicating alterations in lipid metabolism, particularly glycerolipids, as a potential mechanism underlying GDM in genetically predisposed individuals. Lipidomic profiling revealed distinct alterations in glycerolipids, sphingolipids, and phosphatidylglycerols. Mediation analyses further suggested that potential lipid mediators collectively accounted for about 31.3% of the observed association between genetic risk and GDM. Lipid features demonstrated potential improvement of model discrimination, whereas inclusion of PRS did not show significant improvement.

While multiple GDM GWAS have been conducted using both birth cohorts or large-scale biobanks (Additional file 2: Table S21), large-sample studies remain predominantly focused on European populations. To date, the largest GDM GWAS in a Chinese population, which utilized NIPT data (3,317 cases and 19,565 controls) [8], identified 19 genome-wide significant loci. Apart from the unpublished TPMI study result [36], our study represents the largest array-based GWAS of GDM in a Chinese population to date. We replicated three established loci including MTNR1B and CDKAL1 [8, 9, 37, 38], as well as LIPF reported in our previous work [5]. We also identified several suggestive novel associations, including DGAT2, MACROD2 and IL17B. The association of DGAT2 with GDM is consistent with its reported role in T2DM [39]. Biologically, DGs are critical lipotoxic mediators linked to impaired insulin signaling [40], and a accumulation of TGs and DGs associated with DGAT2 variants may contribute to GDM pathophysiology [41]. MACROD2 may act via vascular adhesion protein-1 to exert insulin-like effects [42]. It has been associated with T2DM [43], although evidence in GDM remains limited. IL17B, implicated in immune pathways in T1DM [44], may link immune-inflammatory responses with insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction [44]. Nevertheless, given the relatively modest sample size and reliance on a suggestive threshold, these findings are exploratory and require independent replication. Detailed assessments regarding the reliability and evidence levels for these loci are provided in Additional file 2: Table S22.

We further bridged the gap between genetic association and biological function by demonstrating that candidate genes are significantly enriched in gelycerolipid metabolism pathways. Genes mapped in our study, including DGAT2, MOGAT2, LIPF, LIPN, and LIPK are involved in triglyceride synthesis and lipid processing. LIPF encode enzymes regulating TG lipase and lipoprotein lipase activity [45], while MOGATS and DGATS catalyze sequential steps in TG synthesis [46]. The end products of these pathways (TGs and DGs) are well-established contributors to insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction, two hallmarks of GDM [40, 47]. These findings collectively suggest that genetic susceptibility to GDM may be expressed through disruption in lipid metabolism, especially glycerolipid.

Our lipidomic profiling and mediation analyses provide quantitative evidence of statistical mediation by lipid dysregulation in the association between genetic risk and GDM. In our study, TGs and DGs were among the most prominent lipid subclasses associated with GDM and were identified as potential mediators of the GRS-GDM association, consistent with pathway enrichment results. At the individual SNP level, tan-colored module, which was primarily enriched in TGs, showed statistically significant mediation effect between rs11236507 at DGAT2 and risk of GDM, consistent with the gene’s known biological functions [46]. The presence of mediation at the module level, despite limited associations at the individual lipid level, likely reflects aggregation of correlated lipid signals.

Beyond glycerolipids, we also found that sphingolipids and glycerophospholipids emerged as potential mediators despite their absence in genetic enrichment findings. For example, cyan-colored module, enriched in PEs, exhibited statistically significant mediation effect for rs16885782 (CDKAL1). While direct associations between CDKAL1 and PEs have not been reported, its broader class, phospholipids, is known to be associated with CDKAL1 [48], and PEs are among the lipids most closely linked to GDM risk [49, 50]. Similarly, the brown-colored module, enriched in ceramide derivatives (HexCers and SMs) and PCs, exhibited significant mediation effects for rs10830963 (MTNR1B). Although associations between MTNR1B and these specific lipid species have not been previously reported, their biological relevance with GDM is well-supported. PCs are generally inversely associated with GDM risk [51], and an elevated PE/PC ratio has been linked to endoplasmic reticulum stress [52]. This stress response can inhibit insulin signal transduction, thereby contributing to insulin resistance [52]. Additionally, ceramides are established drivers of insulin resistance [53]. The observed reduction in HexCers and SMs may reflect redistribution within the ceramide pool, potentially indicating accumulation of more bioactive ceramide species [54]. These interpretations remain speculative and require further mechanistic validation.

From a predictive perspective, our study showed that lipid biomarkers yielded significant improvements in prediction, consistent with previous studies [11, 55]. In contrast, PRS did not provide a statistically significant improvement in model discrimination in this study. Although GDM is recognized as a heritable and polygenic condition [56], and PRS has demonstrated predictive utility in larger cohorts [9], the relatively modest GWAS sample size in this study may have constrained the identification of significant loci and, consequently, the predictive performance of the PRS. In addition, PRS represents inherited genetic susceptibility and is typically used to estimate long-term or lifetime disease risk [57]. Pregnancy, however, represents a relatively short and dynamic physiological window, during which the impact of genetic predisposition on disease onset may be more modest. In contrast, the lipidome may better capture intermediate metabolic states, and thus provide a more immediate representation of physiological status. SHAP analyses also indicated that lipid features contributed more to model predictions than PRS; however, SHAP values reflect model-specific feature contributions rather than biological causality.

To our knowledge, this study is among the first in a Chinese population to integrate genomic and lipidomic data to evaluate potential mediation of genetic risk for GDM. While prior studies have suggested biological plausible of genetic variation influences GDM through its effects on lipid metabolism, few studies have integrated genetic and lipidomic features of GDM, and there is a lack of validation and quantification in population-based studies. The HAPO study was the first to conduct such an integrative analysis [38, 58], but the cross-section design limited the ability to evaluate the mediating role of lipid metabolites.

Several strengths distinguish this study. The prospective study design ensures a clear temporal sequence among genetic variants (fixed before birth), early mid-pregnancy lipid profiles (14–20 gestational weeks), and subsequent GDM diagnosis (24–28 weeks), supporting the temporal order of exposure, mediator, and outcome. Furthermore, the case-cohort design provides a cost-efficient framework for multi-omics analysis and enables direct estimation of RR without relying on the rare-disease assumption [28].

However, certain limitations should be noted. The single-center setting and relatively homogeneous population reduces confounding but may limit generalizability. Although the sample size met pre-specified power requirements (see Additional File 1: Sect. 6), it remains modest for detecting subtle genetic effects, conducting complex mediation analysis, as well as prediction modeling, and identified novel loci require independent replication. Furthermore, despite a clear temporal sequence supporting potential causality, mediation analysis in an observational study cannot establish causality and relies on assumptions that are not fully testable. Although sensitivity analyses were performed, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Besides, statistical mediation may identify associations but cannot fully elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms, underscoring the need for experimental and multi-omics validation. Finally, despite stable performance in internal validation, external validation in larger and more diverse populations is required to confirm predictive performance.

Conclusions

Genes related to lipid metabolism are associated with GDM in Chinese women. Specific lipid species, including subclasses of glycerolipids, sphingolipids and glycerophospholipids demonstrated significant mediation effects in the association between genetic variation and GDM. The inclusion of lipid biomarkers showed potential to improve predictive performance for GDM. These results underscore the potential of lipid species as modifiable targets for early intervention. Nevertheless, these findings require external validation, and the precise biological mechanisms remain to be further elucidated.
