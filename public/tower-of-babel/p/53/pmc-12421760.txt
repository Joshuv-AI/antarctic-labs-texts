Barriers and facilitators of the implementation for the antimicrobial Social Norm Feedback (SNF) intervention strategy in primary care institutions: a qualitative study based on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)
Authors: Yuxing Yan, Junli Yang, Yun Lu, Yutong Fu, Jiao Chen, Xinchen Li, Yue Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough current evidence supports the effectiveness of social norm feedback (SNF) interventions, their sustained integration into primary care remains limited. Drawing on the elements of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy identified through the Delphi-based evidence applicability evaluation, this study aims to explore the barriers and facilitators to its implementation in primary care institutions, thereby informing future optimization.MethodsBased on the five domains of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), we developed semi-structured interview and focus group discussion guides. Employing purposive sampling, we selected health administrators, hospital managers, physicians, and pharmacists for semi-structured interviews, while patients who had received antimicrobial therapy in primary care institutions participated in focus group discussions. Data collection continued until saturation was achieved, ultimately gathering insights from 36 participants across 5 health administration departments and 7 primary care institutions in 5 locations of Guizhou Province, Southwest China. Textual data were coded and categorized using NVivo 12.ResultsWe identified 33 barriers, 38 facilitators, and 1 mixed determinant influencing the persistent implementation of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy in primary care institutions. The highest number of factors influencing implementation was found in the inner setting domain. Key barriers included insufficient available resources, insufficient accessibility of knowledge and information, lack of relative priority (referring to the perception that the intervention was less important than competing organizational tasks), organizational culture that prioritizes short-term efficiency, lack of organizational incentives and rewards, inadequate implementation climate, insufficient readiness for implementation, insufficient oversight and leadership engagement, lack of clear goals and feedback, lack of networks and communications, and absence of Pharmacist Council in organizational structure. Improving these factors would promote continuous implementation. Furthermore, facilitators within this domain also included the tension for change (stakeholders' perception that current prescribing practices urgently need improvement), excellent learning climate, excellent compatibility, and organizational structure in newly established large-scale hospitals.ConclusionsLong-term implementation of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy necessitates synergistic effects among intervention characteristics, organizational setting, characteristics of individuals, outer setting, and dynamic implementation process. The study provides critical evidence and references for the sustainable implementation of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy and the optimized design of complex interventions in regions with comparable socioeconomic contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-025-01617-3.Keywords: Social norm feedback, Antimicrobial intervention strategy, Barriers and facilitators, Primary care institutions, Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research, Qualitative study

Background

Antimicrobial agents represent one of the most significant scientific breakthroughs in human health, as they prevent and treat infectious diseases, extending life expectancy [1]. However, overuse and misuse have led to antimicrobial resistance (AMR), resulting in prolonged illness, increased hospitalization costs, therapeutic failure, and, in some cases, the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria with limited or significantly reduced treatment options [2–5]. Countries and international organizations have issued strategies and action plans to combat AMR. Scholars have also explored various policy-based interventions, such as education and training programs [6], delayed antimicrobial prescriptions [7], and point-of-care C-reactive protein testing [8]. However, the inappropriate antimicrobial prescription rate (IAPR) remains high. In the United States, nearly half of patients use unnecessary antimicrobial agents [9]. In England, the IAPR is 69.2% [10]. And a national survey in China also shows that 51.4% of outpatient antimicrobial prescriptions are inappropriate [11]. The phenomenon is more prominent in primary care institutions, where the IAPR can exceed 70% [12–14]. These indicate that policy regulations and policy-based interventions alone are insufficient to curb the inappropriate use of antimicrobial agents, highlighting the critical need for effective intervention strategies that gently change the situation.

Research suggests that behavioral interventions can effectively address AMR challenges [15]. Social norm feedback (SNF) is a behavioral improvement tool [16], which contains multiple elements. It employs an evaluation and feedback mechanism to compare individual behaviors against specific targets. When individuals realize their practices deviate significantly from their peers, peer pressure motivates them to adjust their behaviors to align with social norms [17, 18]. Current evidence confirms the effectiveness of SNF interventions in controlling inappropriate antimicrobial use [19–21]. However, during the promotion process, due to its complex and diverse constituent elements, coupled with the lack of attention from higher-level departments, insufficient adherence of medical staff [20, 22, 23] and other problems, the SNF intervention strategy faces barriers in primary care institutions and lacks sustainability [24–26]. The World Health Organization, in its "Health Implementation Research: Practice Guidelines", also states that integrating an intervention into routine practice typically requires 15–20 years, with approximately 60% ultimately failing [27]. Previous various interventions may temporarily modify individual behaviors but often fail to achieve long-term organizational-level behavioral change [28]. Thus, to facilitate effective and lasting application in real-world settings, there is an urgent need to identify the key barriers and facilitators to the sustainable implementation of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy.

The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) is one of the most commonly used theoretical frameworks for investigating determinants of intervention implementation in real-world settings due to its comprehensiveness and systematism [29]. It encompasses five key domains (intervention characteristics, inner setting, characteristics of individuals, outer setting, and implementation process) with 39 associated constructs [30]. Its strength lies in enabling multidimensional analysis of factors influencing implementation, thereby assisting researchers in overcoming implementation barriers and developing context-specific strategies to promote clinical translation of evidence [31]. Therefore, this study employs the CFIR to develop guides for semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions based on the elements of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy formed by the Delphi method evidence applicability evaluation. The aim is to deeply analyze barriers and facilitators in implementing the SNF intervention strategy in outpatients of primary care institutions. This work will provide theoretical support for applying the CFIR to AMR prevention and control, and laying the groundwork for sustaining the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy in primary care institutions.

Methods

Antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy

Based on comprehensive review of the domestic and international antimicrobial SNF intervention strategies, combined with our preliminary research findings [25, 26], our research team employed the Delphi method to engage experts in scientifically validating the applicability of SNF intervention elements. This process initially identified 3 foundational elements of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy suitable for primary care institutions:

The first element is peer comparison. Through the Health Information System (HIS), private or public feedback report is periodically sent to physicians (e.g., every 10 days, fortnightly, monthly, quarterly, or irregular intervals). The report contains detailed information such as antimicrobial prescription rate (APR), IAPR, regional or institutional precise/approximate/averaged ranking, the top N antimicrobial agents and their usage proportions, the top N diseases associated with antimicrobial prescriptions and their proportions, and the best and non-best performers for antimicrobial use.

The second element focuses on education and training for physicians and patients. Education and training for physicians will be carried out through: (1) regular online and offline training sessions, (2) distribution of clinical application guidelines and educational manuals on antimicrobial agents, and (3) educational videos on appropriate antimicrobial use. For patients, they include: (1) distribution of educational pamphlets on antimicrobial agents, (2) display of educational videos in hospital waiting areas, corridors, and lobbies, and (3) posters promoting appropriate antimicrobial use in hospital bulletin boards.

The third element introduces supportive implementation mechanisms. These primarily encompass: designating an intervention coordinator at each institution to promote the implementation of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy; presenting in the feedback report, contraindications and precautions for the top N antimicrobial agents, along with signatures from authoritative institutions or experts; displaying real-time pop-up warnings for potentially inappropriate antimicrobial use (e.g., unnecessary use, incorrect antimicrobial spectrum, combination of antimicrobial agents without indication) at the bottom left or right corners or in the center of the computer screen; and simultaneously showing an appeal window, allowing physicians to document justifications for maintained antimicrobial prescriptions.

Study design

This qualitative study is based on the CFIR, which serves as the core theoretical foundation. Through semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions, the study systematically analyzes barriers and facilitators to implementing the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy in primary care institutions.

Study sites and participants

Purposive sampling, guided by the principle of maximum variation, was employed to select 7 primary care institutions in Guiyang city (the capital city of Guizhou Province) and its surrounding counties. Site selection aimed to ensure diversity across urban–rural settings and institutional characteristics. Practical considerations such as geographic accessibility also informed final site selection. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with managers, physicians, and pharmacists from these institutions, depending on staff availability and role relevance. Additionally, to incorporate perspectives from multiple levels of the healthcare governance system, interviews were held with health administrators from the provincial and municipal health departments of Guizhou Province, as well as from four county/district-level health administrative departments. Patients who had received antimicrobial treatment at the sampled institutions were recruited to participate in the focus group discussion.

The sample size for this study was determined based on data coding principles, data saturation criteria [32], and the practical feasibility of the research. While power analysis is not applicable in qualitative inquiry, we ensured that the sample captured a range of perspectives across roles, sites, and administrative levels to support analytic depth. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for participants are detailed as follows. Inclusion and exclusion criteria for healthcare providers:

Hospital managers, physicians and pharmacists: Eligible participants must hold a management position (hospital managers)/be directly involved in clinical or pharmacy work (physicians and pharmacists) in a primary care institution, have at least one year of experience in their current role, possess an intermediate or higher professional title, and voluntarily agree to participate. Excluded individuals include off-job managers/physicians/pharmacists and those pursuing advanced studies.

Health administrators: Eligible participants must work in health administrative departments, have at least one year of experience in primary care management, and voluntarily agree to participate. Exclusions include individuals on leave, resigning, or in training during the study period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for healthcare recipients:

Patients were required to be aged 18 or older, have a documented record of antimicrobial treatment at a sampled institution during the study period, provide informed consent, and be willing to cooperate. Individuals with cognitive impairments, severe hearing/visual disabilities, or speech disorders were excluded.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with healthcare providers (health administrators, hospital managers, physicians, and pharmacists) by one interviewer (YY) and two note-takers (JY and YF). Focus group discussions with healthcare recipients (patients) were moderated by one facilitator (YC) and recorded by three note-takers (YL, JC and XL). The interview and discussion guides for the five stakeholder groups were developed based on the CFIR, literature review, and the research team's expertise. Additional file 1 presents the universal framework for the guides across all groups, with population-specific guides provided in Additional file 2. Prior to formal data collection, all guides underwent pilot testing and refinement. Each interview or discussion lasted 25–90 min and was conducted face-to-face in Mandarin or the Guiyang dialect. The variation in duration primarily reflected differences in participants' roles, the complexity or relevance of their experiences, and familiarity with the research topic. For instance, interviews with hospital managers were generally longer due to their broader understanding of institutional practices and policy contexts. Key points were confirmed with participants to prevent misunderstandings. Data confidentiality and participant anonymity were strictly maintained. Participants were assured of their right to withdraw at any time or decline to answer questions without adverse consequences. Data collection was deemed sufficient once saturation was achieved (no emergence of new themes).

Data analysis

All interviews and discussions were audio-recorded and transcribed into text within 48 h. During transcription, strict measures were taken to remove any personally identifiable information (e.g., names, institutional affiliations). Participants, interviewers, and moderators were anonymized using coded identifiers (e.g., HA1 for health administrator 1, HM1 for hospital manager 1, Phy.1 for physician 1, Pha.1 for pharmacist 1, and Pat.1 for patient 1), with only dialogue content retained for subsequent analysis. To ensure data accuracy and consistency, a second researcher independently reviewed the transcripts.

Thematic analysis, combining inductive and deductive approaches, was employed to identify facilitators, barriers, and mixed determinants influencing the implementation of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy. The process included the following steps: Two researchers independently read the transcripts to identify and establish an initial coding system. Codes were categorized based on their semantic meanings, forming preliminary themes (inductive approach); Guided by the CFIR codebook template (Additional file 3), the themes were contextualized within the antimicrobial SNF intervention in primary care institutions and linked to CFIR constructs (deductive approach). Continuous comparison and contrast were conducted until no new themes emerged. All codes were mapped to the five CFIR domains; In the third phase, two analysts independently assigned ratings to each identified theme to assess its potential impact (positive, negative, or mixed) on implementation. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with a third researcher involved if consensus could not be reached. All code names, definitions, and ratings were discussed and finalized through research team meetings.

All quoted excerpts underwent rigorous forward–backward translation: professional translators first rendered Chinese quotes into English, followed by independent back-translation into Chinese for cross-verification. Each quote was labeled only with the participant's role and study code (e.g., HA1, Pat.1) to prevent identification. Coding, categorization, and analysis of Chinese text were performed using NVivo 12 software (QSR International).

Trustworthiness

The study was led by Dr. YC, who has extensive expertise in qualitative research. Prior to data collection, Dr. YC conducted a one-week systematic training session for all team members, covering interview and focus group discussion techniques and textual data management (including transcription and coding). Throughout the entire research process, the researchers maintained a neutral and objective stance. To further ensure the rigor and credibility, interviewers had no prior contact or relationship with participants, minimizing potential biases. During all interviews and discussions, only the interviewers and participants were present, ensuring privacy and minimizing external influences. Notably, no participants withdrew from the study at any stage. The transcripts were shared with participants by researchers YC and YY for review, feedback, and corrections. And revised versions were finalized for analysis. Our study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [33], with the populated checklist provided in Additional file 4.

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews (30 interviews) and one focus group discussion. Participants' ages ranged from 23 to 83 years (mean ± SD: 43.4 ± 14.2 years), with a balanced gender distribution (male: 20 (55.6%); female: 16 (44.4%)). Barriers

Barriers identified in semi-structured interviews

A prominent barrier was the design limitations of the SNF intervention strategy, reported by 11 participants from three stakeholder groups — health administrators (66.7%), hospital managers (16.7%) and physicians (30.8%). Health administrators and hospital managers widely agreed that restricting feedback reports to physicians' private access will undermine managerial efficacy. One of them remarked:

"From a management perspective, I hope that the feedback reports can, at a minimum, be shared with the deputy deans in charge"(HA8).

Physicians raised concerns that the ranking mechanism could provoke resistance. They also criticized the standardized online training materials for lacking personalization for diverse clinical needs, leading to low utilization rates. As one physician remarked:

"The general training materials are designed for everyone, but physicians differ in their levels of knowledge and diagnostic skills. It would be more effective if a more individualized and differentiated education model could be designed to address the specific issues that physicians frequently encounter. If a physician keeps hearing repetitive content unrelated to their problems, it becomes redundant and disengaging"(Phy.13).

Additionally, according to the report from healthcare administrators, the second major barrier was the high level of complexity, particularly due to the heterogeneous HIS platforms across primary care institutions, which complicated multi-departmental coordination. One health administrator explained:

"The challenge lies in the fact that HIS varies between hospitals, with different vendors used across institutions within the same district. Integrating and aggregating data across these disparate systems is highly complex"(HA2).

"Multi-departmental coordination—involving entities such as healthcare insurance and market regulation—is noted as a persistent difficulty"(HA2).

Barriers identified in the focus group discussion

From the perspective of healthcare service recipients (patients), barriers primarily center on the complexity of educational materials. Patients reported that pamphlets are overly technical and difficult to comprehend, while participation in educational activities incurs high time costs, reducing adherence. One patient stated:

"I don't want to read them, I don't understand. I can barely use a smartphone, let alone navigate platforms like TikTok"(Pat.1).

Facilitators

Facilitators identified in semi-structured interviews

Healthcare providers (100.0% of hospital managers, 84.6% of physicians, 55.6% of health administrators and 50.0% of pharmacists) unanimously acknowledged the high-quality design and optimized packaging. The combined packaging of feedback reports, education and training for physicians and patients, and real-time pop-up warnings was perceived as more effective than any single component alone. Managers also underscored that design features such as authoritative signatures and streamlined appeal channels enhanced the intervention's authority, standardization, and acceptability. As one hospital manager expressed:

"The feedback report is signed by the government department. First, this indicates the government's attention to and emphasis on this area of work. Additionally, it may serve as a deterrent for physicians"(HM1).

Similarly, the intervention's low implementation costs in terms of time, staffing, and workload, its low complexity and convenient operation, and its relative advantage over existing retrospective antimicrobial stewardship systems (such as antimicrobial prescription review) were commonly highlighted as facilitators. A pharmacist noted:

"I think you should be able to intervene beforehand... Intervening beforehand would be more effective, as it would provide immediate reminder"(Pha.1).

Facilitators identified in the focus group discussion

The adaptable patient education formats were especially emphasized by patients (100.0%). They expressed strong acceptance of video-based education, citing its clarity and flexibility. When asked if they were willing to read the brochure, one patient remarked:

"I'm not very keen on reading it because I don't really understand it. If I'm sitting here for an infusion, watching a video would make it clearer"(Pat.2).

Inner setting

Barriers

Barriers identified in semi-structured interviews

The determinants of inner setting domain are detailed in Table 3. Providers widely face insufficient available resources (reported by 100.0% of hospital managers, 77.8% of health administrators, 69.2% of physicians and 50.0% of pharmacists). These included pharmacist staffing shortages, lack of testing and promotional equipment, insufficient time and funding, lack of available training resources and the restricted variety of medications—which constrain the intervention's sustainability. As one hospital manager commented:

"In township health centers, we lack the capacity for drug sensitivity tests or blood cultures, forcing us to rely on empirical antimicrobial selection"(HM6).

Another manager added:

"Many primary care institutions lack the economic, infrastructural support to implement such plans, particularly in terms of human resources"(HM2).

The second most commonly mentioned barrier was insufficient accessibility of knowledge and information, raised by three stakeholder groups, especially by pharmacists (100.0%) and physicians (53.8%). They consistently reported that information sources on antimicrobial agents were limited in primary care institutions. A physician explained:

"Accessing guidelines for antimicrobial use can vary widely online, with many inconsistent versions. Moreover, the means of obtaining official guidelines are quite limited—often relying on platforms like Baidu Wenku"(Phy.7).

Additionally, health administrators, hospital managers and physicians highlighted competing priorities in daily workflows, which relegated the intervention strategy to a lower relative priority. A health administrator noted:

"Antimicrobial intervention, while critical for public health, remains a minor concern within the broader healthcare governance landscape"(HA2).

Moreover, they also cited the organizational culture that prioritizes short-term efficiency and entrenched traditional practices as barriers to long-term quality improvement in prescribing. One hospital manager expressed:

"The promotion of new technologies and things in township settings is quite challenging. The entrenched mindset of 'the old leading the new' is deeply rooted and difficult to change"(HM3).

Another barrier to the implementation of the intervention strategy, emphasized by provider groups except hospital managers, was the lack of organizational incentives and rewards. As one physician stated:

"Without rewards or penalties, physicians won’t pay attention to the feedback reports"(Phy.7).

Facilitators

Facilitators identified in semi-structured interviews

The most widely endorsed facilitator within the inner setting was sufficient leadership engagement, mentioned by all 4 provider groups (100.0% of hospital managers, 88.9% of health administrators, 53.8% of physicians, and 50.0% of pharmacists). Respondents emphasized that strong institutional support, particularly from department directors, encouraged frontline compliance. A hospital manager observed:

"When a dedicated leader is assigned to oversee the work, implementation tends to be more effective. Their authority in making arrangements is more widely recognized, and others are more willing to cooperate"(HM5).

Other highly prevalent facilitators included tension for change (stakeholders' perception that current prescribing practices urgently need improvement), excellent learning climate, implementation climate, and compatibility with existing workflows. One physician commented:

"I've noticed that antimicrobial prescriptions are indeed quite frequent in our township. I think this kind of intervention is necessary, as it can help standardize our prescribing practices. Sometimes, we're not even aware that certain prescriptions we issue are inappropriate"(Phy.9).

Notably, sufficient networks and communications were identified as key facilitators by 100.0% of hospital managers, physicians, and pharmacists. As one pharmacist remarked:

"When we encounter uncertain situations, we discuss them together, which also helps promote the implementation of the intervention strategy"(Pha.2).

In addition, 100.0% of hospital managers and 92.3% of physicians emphasized that organizational incentives and rewards, along with the availability of training resources, dedicated spaces, and multimedia equipment, can facilitate the implementation of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy.

Characteristics of individuals

Barriers

Barriers identified in semi-structured interviews

Table 4 analyzes the influencing factors raised by healthcare service providers and patients in the characteristics of individuals domain. Providers identified entrenched prescribing habits (66.7% of hospital managers, 55.6% of health administrators, and 23.1% of physicians) and insufficient professional technical skills among physicians (66.7% of hospital managers, 33.3% of administrators and 15.4% of physicians) as primary barriers. Stakeholders described a tendency among some physicians to rely on empirical practices:

"In primary care, physicians often rely solely on their clinical experience to judge the type of infection and select antimicrobial agents, which may not always be accurate. The lack of prescribing standardization, stemming from deeply ingrained prescribing habits, can be particularly difficult to change"(HM5).

Barriers identified in the focus group discussion

From the healthcare recipients' perspective, patients' deeply rooted traditional medication beliefs and behavioral habits, lack of initiative in self-directed learning, and low willingness to cooperate with brochures and posters hinder long-term adherence. Facilitators identified in semi-structured interviews

Providers universally reported that their knowledge and beliefs about the SNF intervention strategy, along with high self-efficacy—defined as confidence in successfully applying the intervention—contributed to its sustained implementation. As one hospital manager emphasized:

"This intervention strategy is beneficial for the hospital, particularly in promoting the appropriate use of antimicrobial agents. If something contributes to our development, it is more likely to be sustained"(HM1).

A physician also noted:

"The principles behind the intervention are sound, and the direction of its development is positive. We feel confident in applying the intervention successfully, therefore, we are willing to actively give it a try"(Phy.11).

Facilitators identified in semi-structured interviews

Patients consistently emphasized that they benefited from clear medical instructions during external collaborations, stating:

This contributes to enhancing acceptance and long-term adherence.

Mixed determinants

Mixed determinants identified in semi-structured interviews

In the domain of outer setting, insufficient external collaboration resources constitute a mixed determinant. Healthcare service providers perceived that while the lack of external collaboration resources poses barriers, it also creates opportunities for introducing and scaling the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy. One physician noted:

"In the past, we haven't had any collaboration with external intervention teams. We hope to bring in some external resources to facilitate mutual learning and promote future development—this is something we truly need"(Phy.12).

Discussion

This study systematically analyzed the implementation barriers and facilitators of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy in primary care institutions in Guizhou Province, China, through semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions. We maintained continuous reflections on attitudes, assumptions, and positions during the interview and focus group discussion processes and analyses to ensure our understanding authentically reflected participants' perspectives. The findings revealed divergent focuses of attention among different stakeholders. Health administrators emphasized macro-level regional promotion and ideological enhancement. Hospital managers concentrated on organizational resource-environment compatibility. Physicians and pharmacists prioritized operational feasibility, while patients valued information accessibility and treatment experience optimization. These multidimensional perspectives collectively illuminated the determinants of implementation and sustainability for the intervention strategy within complex healthcare ecosystems.

Intervention characteristics

The thematic analysis revealed a divergence between managers and physicians regarding whether the feedback report should be designed as private or public. Managers preferred public feedback to facilitate accountability, while physicians expressed concerns that ranking-based public reports may trigger resistance. This paradox manifests the "double-edged sword effect" of the feedback. In China's primary care settings, where professional hierarchies are clearly defined, peer comparison can effectively drive behavioral change [34], but it may also evoke defensive reactions [22, 35]. This duality aligns with two key domains of the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [36]: social influence, which underscores how peer norms shape compliance, and social/professional role and identity, which highlights how perceived threats to one’s professional image may hinder engagement with externally imposed evaluations. To mitigate these tensions, it is recommended to implement a dual-track feedback of "public–private". A public report (only showing the organization code) is generated for the organization as a whole to meet the regulatory needs of managers. Individual physicians' prescription data are subject to private feedback in order to alleviate potential psychological resistance. Concurrently, physicians criticized the standardized training content for lacking personalization, echoing a prior study which showed that generic feedback could prove frustrating, whereas personalized feedback demonstrated greater potential for behavioral guidance[37]. Tailored strategies should include modular [22] training structures (e.g., foundational to advanced tracks) to accommodate varying competency levels across stakeholder groups, along with iterative content updates based on recipient feedback—strategies consistent with adaptive implementation principles.

Furthermore, health administrators highlighted that, in China's primary care institutions, heterogeneous HIS platforms pose integration challenges and increase informational burden. This supports findings from an international survey identifying fragmented information systems as barriers to healthcare interventions [38]. Establishing regional medical information platforms could effectively enable cross-institutional data exchange [39]. Leveraging county-level medical consortiums ("medical communities") may support this process by providing a foundation for unified data standards and shared service interfaces. Additionally, standardized feedback plugins should be developed for integration with heterogeneous HIS, capturing the real-time data.

Notably, patient feedback demonstrated that video-based education was far more acceptable than text-based materials, especially in China's primary care regions where a large proportion of the population is older or has limited literacy, suggesting the need to accommodate diverse groups' media preferences in educational form [40]. Mois G et al. further indicated that differentiated content design could enhance intervention precision and adaptability [41], providing guidance for optimizing health education.

Inner setting

Within the inner setting, insufficient available resources were universally recognized as the major barrier, particularly for China's primary care institutions that faced situations like pharmacist staffing shortages and the lack of testing equipment. This is consistent with the findings of Qin A et al. [42] on healthcare resource disparities driven by uneven economic development. Another study has also pointed out that physicians in primary care institutions may struggle to implement interventions due to resource constraints such as inadequate technical and information support, as seen in disruptions related to electronic medical record vendor changes that affect data quality and feedback processes [43]. This study further revealed the cascading effects of resource shortages on sustainability. For instance, physicians in China's township health centers are forced to rely on empirical medication due to the inability to conduct antimicrobial susceptibility testing. The phenomenon closely parallels the dilemma observed in diabetes prevention programs that fail to integrate into routine primary care due to resource limitations [44]. These findings also reflect the "environmental context and resources" sdomain of the TDF [36], as limitations in surrounding infrastructure directly restricted opportunities to develop and apply appropriate prescribing skills.

Both hospital managers and physicians emphasized the catalytic role of sufficient leadership engagement and collaborative culture in driving implementation. However, a structural conflict exists between organizational culture that prioritizes short-term efficiency and the long-term quality objectives of antimicrobial stewardship. Physicians, pressured by time constraints and short-term goals to avoid clinical deterioration risks, tend to prescribe broad-spectrum antimicrobial agents for rapid symptom relief, exacerbating antimicrobial misuse. Broom A et al. [45] similarly noted that performance targets demanding measurable short-term progress may ultimately undermine the goals of reducing AMR effectively. Managers and physicians acknowledged the lack of relative priority of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy in the healthcare industry's work. A study from India also indicated that antimicrobial stewardship efforts are often sidelined due to shifting priorities and limited human resources [46], as non-urgent tasks are easily marginalized when multiple objectives compete [45].

To address these barriers, we recommend establishing policy-level resource allocation mechanisms, such as dedicated funding for antimicrobial susceptibility testing equipment, and employing participatory approaches to engage local stakeholders, thereby achieving contextualization of the intervention strategy and elevating its priority [47]. Concurrently, reinforce leadership engagement through incentive mechanisms, such as incorporating indicators of the effectiveness of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy into performance appraisals. At the organizational level, creating cultural symbols like "Antimicrobial Stewardship Day" and ritualized activities (e.g., selection of annual "Best Prescribing Physician") may reshape organizational values and foster the quality-oriented culture. These recommendations correspond to two implementation strategies defined by the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) [48]. The first, conduct local consensus discussions, involves engaging frontline stakeholders to determine whether the intervention addresses locally important problems and fits the implementation setting, thereby enhancing local support. The second, alter incentive structures, refers to modifying financial or performance-based incentives to promote adoption and sustain engagement.

Characteristics of individuals

In the domain of characteristics of individuals, physicians' entrenched prescribing habits and insufficient professional technical skills emerged as key barriers. Especially for primary care physicians, it is difficult to adapt to evidence-based interventions due to the experience-dominated prescribing model. This further validates that even with interventions, the habits of some physicians are hard to change completely in a short period of time [46, 49, 50]. Additionally, physicians lacking relevant knowledge may find it difficult to interpret and apply feedback reports [43]. The study also found that the lack of proficiency in computer operation among senior physicians hindered the implementation of the SNF intervention strategy. These barriers also reflect deficits in the capability domain of the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) model—the core of the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) framework [51]—including gaps in psychological capability (e.g., limited clinical knowledge) and physical capability (e.g., difficulty using digital tools). These limitations reduce physicians' ability to engage effectively with the SNF intervention.

Notably, groups with high self-efficacy and a strong sense of responsibility demonstrated enhanced intervention adherence. This suggests that strengthening automatic motivation—as framed in COM-B [51]—may support implementation sustainability in complex primary care settings. One promising approach is implementing structured "peer mentor-ship programs", in which highly adherent physicians mentor lagging peers through targeted guidance and role modeling.

Outer setting

Pressures from patients and lack of supervision of external policies constituted dual barriers. Patients exhibited cognitive biases toward antimicrobial agents [52], viewing them as a "cure-all" and thereby demanding prescriptions for various infections, potentially forcing physicians to compromise under patient pressure [53]. On the other Hand, patients may discontinue medication without following medical advice, leading to incomplete treatment. A clinical trial about real-time feedback revealed that nearly 40% of patients failed to Fully adhere to the 3-h bundled treatment protocol [54]. Additionally, patients' excessive antimicrobial needs and insufficient primary-level drug reserves have led to a reliance on loosely regulated private clinics. Despite policy incentives, weak enforcement, particularly in pharmacies, still persists widely [55]. The lack of coordination between health authorities overseeing care institutions and market regulators managing pharmacies has resulted in a "strict hospital control versus unregulated pharmacy access" gap. A study from Ghana similarly noted that in developing and middle-income countries, antimicrobial agents are easily obtained without prescriptions from pharmacies and licensed drug stores due to the lack of pharmaceutical regulation [56], serving as alternative supply channels after hospital restrictions.

It is worth mentioning that insufficient external collaborative resources were identified as both a barrier and a facilitator. Although it may potentially undermine long-term sustainability by limiting access to continued support, shared learning, and technical assistance from external partners, it also promoted institutions to adopt the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy. This coincides with the research showing how market constraints can catalyze reform in public–private collaboration [57], and aligns with the "environmental context and resources" domain of the TDF [36], wherein structural limitations not only hinder action but can also serve as behavioral catalysts under conditions of urgency or competition. Physicians reported that peer pressure contributes to antimicrobial overuse, and, within the context of China's kinship-based society, patients also indicated that community-driven misinformation and beliefs influence their behavior, resonating with the conclusion of Han L et al. [58] that peer support may not always promote positive behavioral outcomes.

Based on the aforementioned discoveries, regional antimicrobial alliances should be established to strengthen cross-sector coordination. Additionally, we recommend developing multi-departmental data-sharing platforms to integrate hospital prescriptions and pharmacy sales data for full traceability [59, 60]. To reduce patients' inappropriate antimicrobial demand, public education could first target schools and health-literate groups, who can then influence their families and social circles—an approach well suited to a kinship-oriented society, where information often spreads through trusted personal networks [61]. Meanwhile, the intervention strategy should be extended to communities by engaging opinion leaders (e.g., retired healthcare workers or family physicians) to establish social norms and spread proper medication knowledge. And they can also help to mitigate the influence of traditional misconceptions through the social network effects. Moreover, flexible policy design is critical, such as setting differentiated outpatient APR thresholds for primary care institutions to alleviate compliance pressures caused by resource disparities.

Implementation process

The study found that stratified and categorized execution, such as prioritizing groups with higher educational level, can effectively reduce the initial resistance and enhance sustainability. This aligns with Price L et al.'s [62] recommendation to segment populations for tailored education to improve adherence to antimicrobial interventions. Moreover, the support from opinion leaders (e.g., department heads) was viewed as essential to accelerating behavioral diffusion and mobilizing collective participation [63]. Tilson JK et al. [64] emphasized that local opinion leaders' participation in community-building meetings may be pivotal for the success of interventions. This is consistent with the Knowledge to Action (KTA) model, which posits that involving respected peers in implementation helps bridge the gap between knowledge and practice by reinforcing contextual relevance, facilitating collective commitment, and enhancing practical uptake.

However, a prominent implementation barrier is the deviation between top-level design and grassroots execution, often stemming from China's top-down policy structure, where centrally designed strategies may not fully align with the practical realities of primary care settings. This situation was equally validated in a comparative study of two hospitals using identical digital intervention system, which revealed differences in their implementation effectiveness [65]. This highlights the necessity of dynamically adjusting strategies to adapt to local contexts [47], as emphasized by the Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services (PARIHS) framework [66], which conceptualizes successful implementation as the interplay of high-quality evidence, supportive context, and skilled facilitation.

These findings advocate for implementing real-time monitoring systems to track the progress of execution, and convening regular multidisciplinary review meetings (e.g., Quarterly SNF Intervention Strategy Forums) to facilitate experience-sharing and corrective actions [67]. Furthermore, the prefecture-level coordinators overseeing the "three-network" system—namely, the Bacterial Resistance Surveillance Network, the Clinical Antimicrobial Use Monitoring Network, and the Hospital Infection Control Surveillance Network—could be designated as dedicated coordinators to strengthen supervision of the implementation process.

Our study has some limitations. Firstly, the relatively small sample sizes for certain stakeholder groups—particularly pharmacists—may limit the transferability of the findings to other contexts. This potential underrepresentation of pharmacists, who are key stakeholders, reflects their limited number in primary care institutions. However, by adhering to the principle of data saturation and ensuring diversity in institutional types and participant roles, we aimed to minimize the potential impact on the results. Secondly, voluntary participation may have introduced self-selection bias. Nevertheless, diversity was captured to the fullest extent by including participants from multiple roles (health administrators, hospital managers, physicians, pharmacists, and patients). Thirdly, the subjectivity of qualitative data could affect the generalization of conclusions. However, researchers reduced the risk of bias through consistency checks in coding and reflexive analysis (e.g., repeatedly verification against original statements). Finally, all factors were qualitatively identified without causal inference. Future studies should employ mixed methods to quantify barrier weights and validate strategy universality across regions with varying economic levels.

Despite these limitations, this study systematically integrated the perspectives of various stakeholders from both the supply and demand sides through the CFIR, conducting a multi-dimensional and context-specific analysis of the barriers and facilitators to implementing the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy. During data collection, measures such as creating a private survey environment, turning off audio recording at any time based on participants' wishes, and consistently emphasizing confidentiality, were taken to enhance the authenticity and reliability of responses from primary care participants. The findings provide valuable references for other regions with comparable socioeconomic contexts.

Conclusions

Guided by the CFIR, this qualitative study conducted semi-structured interviews and a focus group discussion with stakeholders from both the healthcare supply and demand sides. The aim is to identify determinants influencing the sustainable implementation of the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy in primary care institutions in Southwest China. Barriers and facilitators were found across all five CFIR domains. The factors most frequently reported by various stakeholder groups pertained to the inner setting, particularly concerning the organizational available resources. And in the outer setting domain, a mixed determinant of insufficient external collaboration resources was identified. The findings of this study provide valuable insights and evidence support for optimizing the antimicrobial SNF intervention strategy in countries and regions with similar socioeconomic contexts, laying a foundation for promoting the long-term sustainable implementation in the real-world setting.
