Unusual Imaging Findings of Epithelioid Hemangioma: Case Report of Single Intramedullary Sclerotic Bone Lesion
Authors: Yun Chul Hwang, Tae Eun Kim, Jae Hyuck Yi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Epithelioid hemangioma (EH) is an uncommon, benign vascular tumor of mesenchymal origin. It mainly presents as a tumor with a lytic appearance and septations. However, no case reports have documented the predominantly sclerotic appearance of EH. Here, we present the imaging findings, including X-ray, CT, MRI, and histopathological findings of a 24-year-old female with EH.

INTRODUCTION

Epithelioid vascular tumors of the bone are formed by endothelial cells, which are morphologically similar to epithelial cells. These are rare and represent an overlapping spectrum of tumors ranging from low to high malignant potential. These include epithelioid hemangiomas (EHs), epithelioid hemangioendotheliomas, and epithelioid angiosarcoma (1). The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies bone epithelioid vascular tumors into two categories: EH and a more malignant category, including epithelioid hemangioendothelioma and epithelioid angiosarcoma (2).

Conventionally, the imaging findings of EH on X-ray and CT scans appear as lytic tumors, and the differential diagnosis includes epithelioid hemangioendothelioma, epithelioid angiosarcoma, and cystic angiomatosis (3). To date, no case reports showing the predominantly sclerotic appearance of EH have been documented. Herein, we describe the radiological features of a single intramedullary sclerotic bone lesion in the limb extremities.

CASE REPORT

A 24-year-old female presented to our orthopedic surgery clinic with left shoulder pain for the past 6 months. The frequency of pain was continuous, with an intensity of approximately three points on the visual analogue scale (VAS) score. The patient had no specific underlying conditions other than asthma. The patient is a violinist, but there seems to be no specific correlation between the pain felt by the patient and her profession.

Her vitals were stable. Physical examination revealed no notable abnormalities. Laboratory findings, including C-reactive protein levels and erythrocyte sedimentation rate, were within normal ranges. The X-ray of the left humerus revealed a single round sclerotic mass-like opacity in the metadiaphysis of the proximal humerus (Fig. 1A). For further evaluation, a CT and MRI were performed. A CT scan revealed an intramedullary hyperdense mass in the proximal humeral metadiaphysis (Fig. 1B). Contrast-enhanced MRI revealed an enhancing mass with high signal intensity on T2-weighted images and low signal intensity on T1-weighted images, with multiple internal septations in the metadiaphysis of the proximal humerus (Fig. 1C). Lymphadenopathy was not observed on CT or MRI.

Based on imaging findings, the presumed initial diagnosis was osteoblastoma. As the patient desired proactive treatment, a decision was made to proceed with surgery. She was admitted to the orthopedic ward and underwent a wide excision two days later. Histopathologically, cellular lesions were noted on a thick haphazard trabecular bone and loose connective tissue background. At higher magnification, these lesions showed lobular proliferation of small capillary-sized vessels. Some epithelial cells contained intracytoplasmic vacuoles (Fig. 1D). Based on the pathological findings, the final diagnosis was an EH.

The patient was discharged six days after surgery and regularly visited the outpatient clinic. She was followed up for 4 years with no evidence of recurrence.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution. The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study (IRB No. DFH 2024-02-002).

DISCUSSION

An EH is a benign vascular tumor in which more than 50% of the neoplastic vessels are lined with epithelioid endothelial cells (4). When EH arises within bone tissue, it predominantly presents in the metaphysis and diaphysis of the long bones of the extremities, with subsequent occurrences in the short tubular bones of the distal lower extremities and flat bones (5). However, the cause of EH remains unclear. Patients typically exhibit a gradual onset of symptoms characterized by a slow-growing soft-to-bony swelling of the extremities, often accompanied by impairment in joint mobility. Occasionally, clinical manifestations include a pathological fracture resulting from osteolysis within these lesions (2).

X-ray imaging is typically unreliable for the diagnosis of bone EH because of the absence of specific radiographic characteristics. On conventional radiography, EH typically appears as a lucent lesion with well-defined expansile margins. Additionally, it may present with lytic appearance, septation, thick periosteal reactive bone formation, and partial cortical destruction (2). CT findings of EH typically include expansile, septate, lytic lesions, and trabeculations, which are easily visualized using this imaging modality. The lesions and their well-limited contours inside and outside the bone were enhanced by contrast injection. MRI typically reveals EH as lesions with low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and a high signal intensity on T2-weighted images. The limits of the lesions, both within and outside the bone, were distinctly delineated. According to established imaging findings, the differential diagnoses of EH would include eosinophilic granuloma, aneurysmal bone cyst, non-ossifying fibroma, and solitary bone cysts, which are represented as lytic tumors. Additionally, angiosarcoma, hemangioendothelioma, and cystic angiomatosis, although rare, may be included in the differential diagnosis (3).

However, in this case, the EH exhibited a considerably sclerotic appearance on X-ray and CT images, which is in clear contrast to the established imaging findings for EHs reported to date. This can lead to significant confusion in the diagnosis of the lesions. In cases of focal intramedullary sclerotic bone lesions, benign lesions such as fibrous dysplasia, osteoid osteoma, and osteoblastoma can be included in the differential diagnosis (6). Furthermore, other mesenchymal tumors that can lead to reactive bone formation, including intraosseous lipomas and intraosseous hibernomas, should be considered in the differential diagnosis, as they may appear similar to this case (78). It is also crucial to include malignancies, such as osteosarcoma, sclerotic bone metastasis, and lymphoma (6). This approach differs markedly from that for the diagnosis of lytic tumors.

To date, no case reports have documented the predominantly sclerotic appearance of EH. The fact that EH can exhibit a sclerotic appearance in contrast to a typical lytic appearance can lead to significant confusion among radiologists and pathologists. In our case, there was a discrepancy between the radiological and pathological findings, which was confusing. If there had been an awareness that EH can also manifest with a predominantly sclerotic appearance, it might have clarified this confusion.

Currently, it is challenging to explain the sclerotic appearance of the EH observed in this case. The pathological stage of EH may be associated with the imaging features. For instance, Milgram categorized intraosseous lipomas into three types, indicating that each pathological stage has distinct imaging features (8). However, owing to the current lack of sufficient research to distinguish the specific pathological stages of EH, it is challenging to determine a radiologic-pathological correlation. More case reports documenting the pathological features of EH, allowing for the classification of pathological stages, may shed light on why the EH exhibited a predominantly sclerotic appearance in our case.

Currently, there are no standardized treatment protocols for EH. However, primary treatment options include thorough curettage or en bloc surgical resection. Other available therapies include surgical resection, radiotherapy, endovascular embolization, and pharmacological management. EH of the bone carries a recurrence risk of approximately 10%. When classified as a benign tumor, EH rarely exhibits widespread metastasis or results in fatalities. Although regional lymph node metastases and soft tissue extensions may occur, they do not necessarily indicate increased aggressiveness of the disease (3).

In summary, EH is typically observed radiologically as a lytic tumor. However, as seen in our case, it does not commonly present with predominantly sclerotic changes. Therefore, radiologists, clinicians, and pathologists should consider this possibility when distinguishing sclerotic tumors as confusion may otherwise arise.
