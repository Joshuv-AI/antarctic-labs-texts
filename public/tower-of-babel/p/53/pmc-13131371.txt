Rare Coexistence of Uncommon Congenital Valve Duo in an Adult: Double Orifice Mitral Valve and Bicuspid Aortic Valve
Authors: Shazia Rasheed, Kiran Zahra, Mushkbar Fatima, Wajahat Rehman, Khawaja Muhammad Aarij, Saima Khawaja Khail, Parveen Akhtar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Hypertension, Atrial myxoma, Rheumatic mitral stenosis, Diastolic murmur, Palpitations

Introduction

Double orifice mitral valve (DOMV) is an uncommon congenital anomaly characterized by a fibrous band that creates 2 openings in the mitral valve (MV). It is often associated with other congenital cardiac anomalies and may present with mitral regurgitation (MR) or mitral stenosis (MS) or remain asymptomatic.

Case Presentation

We report the case of a 46-year-old female patient who presented with a 2-month history of palpitations and mild shortness of breath (New York Heart Association class II). During the current visit, the patient’s Glasgow Coma Scale score was 15 on presentation. Physical examination revealed warm extremities, good volume regular pulses, no arm-leg pulse delay, no jugular venous distention or pedal edema, and both heart sounds audible in all 4 cardiac areas with a grade 3/4 diastolic murmur best heard at apical area and no systolic murmur appreciated on cardiac auscultation. Chest auscultation revealed normal vesicular breathing. Vital signs on presentation included a blood pressure of 100/60 mm Hg equal in upper and lower limbs, temperature of 36° C, heart rate of 102 beats/min, respiratory rate of 19 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air.

The patient's medical history was significant for hypertension for 6 years, which was controlled on beta-blocker (bisoprolol 5 mg daily) as per records. They had 10 children, all delivered by unassisted normal vaginal delivery at a local facility near their hometown. All children were alive and healthy, and the patient tolerated all deliveries well. Based on clinical assessment, the initial differential diagnoses considered were rheumatic MS and benign atrial myxoma.

Laboratory investigations were within normal limits except for hemoglobin was 8.4 gm/dL, hematocrit was 26.1%, and mean corpuscular volume was 67.6 fL. The patient’s electrocardiogram showed normal sinus rhythm with no significant ST-T changes. Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed severely dilated left atrium (left atrial volume index, 65 mL/m2) and normal biventricular systolic function. The MV was thickened with doming and restricted diastolic opening of leaflets with suspicion of DOMV and mild MR. Pressure gradient across the MV was mildly increased (max, 10 mm Hg; mean, 7 mm Hg). It was technically difficult to obtain MV area on initial TTE. The aortic valve (AV) showed thickened cusps with mildly restricted mobility; however, precise characterization of AV morphology remained elusive. Pressure gradient across the AV was mildly increased (max, 27 mm Hg; mean, 18 mm Hg), with mild aortic regurgitation (Figure 1, Video 1). There was no evidence of coarctation of aorta on TTE (Figure 2, Video 2).

Two-dimensional TTE, apical 4-chamber view with color-flow Doppler-guided continuous-wave spectral Doppler display, positioned across the DOMV, demonstrates a spectral Doppler profile with abnormal pressure gradients at rest (A) and at peak exercise on supine bicycle stress (B).

Two-dimensional TTE, suprasternal long-axis view with color-flow Doppler-guided continuous-wave spectral Doppler, demonstrates the cursor positioned within the descending thoracic aorta and high resistance, normal velocity, and systolic flow profile without a diastolic tail (no evidence of coarctation).

Transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) was performed to further evaluate the valvular pathology with the help of three-dimensional (3D) TEE, and it confirmed thickened DOMV with 2 distinct asymmetric MV orifices and diastolic doming of leaflets. There was a presence of a fibrous ridge or tissue bridge dividing the MV (complete bridge type). The combined MV area of both orifices was 1.2 cm2 on 3D planimetry. The MV gradient on TEE was unchanged from the TTE. There was also mild MR and evidence of 4 unequal papillary muscles attached to the 2 separate MV orifices (Figures 3 and 4, Videos 3-6).

Two-dimensional TEE, transgastric basal (A) and midventricular (B) short-axis views, demonstrates 2 distinct diastolic orifices (stars) with a spectacles-like morphology of the DOMV and 4 unequal papillary muscles (stars).

Three-dimensional TEE, multiplanar long-axis views (left side of each panel) and volume-rendered reconstruction, diastolic en face surgeon’s view of the DOMV with conventional 3D image (A) and photorealistic 3D image (B), demonstrates the 2 distinct orifices (stars) and the existence of a fibrous ridge (tissue bridge; arrows) dividing the MV into 2 separate orifices consistent with a complete bridge-type classification.

Assessment of the AV showed a thickened bicuspid AV (BAV; fused noncoronary and right coronary cusps) with doming of cusps and mild aortic stenosis and mild aortic regurgitation. The AV area by planimetry was 1.7 cm2; the AV gradient on TEE was unchanged from the TTE (Figure 5, Video 6). The pulmonic and tricuspid valves were normal.

Three-dimensional TEE with 2D multiplanar short-axis (top left), 2D long-axis (bottom left), and volume-rendered reconstruction short-axis (from the perspective of the aorta) systolic views of the BAV demonstrates the thickened leaflets with ovoid opening (star) and fused noncoronary and right coronary cusps.

Since the patient was experiencing only mild symptoms, the heart team decided to perform a supine bicycle stress echocardiogram for functional assessment and evaluation of pulmonary artery pressure during exertion. The patient reached 80 watts on bicycle stress, and there was no increase in MR during exercise. The mean pressure gradient across the MV increased from 7 to 19 mm Hg (Figure 1), and the patient's pulmonary artery pressure increased to a maximum of 40 mm Hg with only mild tricuspid regurgitation. More importantly, the patient remained asymptomatic, reporting only leg fatigue, and further exercise was stopped at the patient's request due to leg cramps.

After multidisciplinary discussion and counseling, the absence of significant hemodynamic deterioration during exercise and the presence of minimal symptoms justified a conservative management strategy. Left atrial enlargement warranted concern for atrial arrhythmia, especially atrial fibrillation, but advanced rhythm monitoring is unavailable at our resource-limited hospital, and the patient's brief palpitations resolved with anemia correction. Nonetheless, given the markedly enlarged left atrium, symptoms of palpitations, and underlying MS, oral vitamin K antagonist (warfarin) was initiated to mitigate thromboembolic risk along with continuation of low-dose beta-blocker. The patient was advised to have regular follow-up with clinical assessment and repeat two-dimensional (2D) TTE imaging for surveillance.

At the 6-month follow-up conducted via teleconsultation, the patient reported symptomatic relief, remained clinically stable, and continued to perform routine activities with previously troubling palpitations abated significantly, an improvement plausibly linked to the repletion of iron stores and correction of anemia. The patient is scheduled for an in-person visit to hospital soon, as their family lives in a remote area, to obtain follow-up TTE imaging as well.

Discussion

Double orifice mitral valve is an uncommon congenital malformation involving the subvalvular MV apparatus including the chordae tendineae and papillary muscles. It is characterized by the presence of an accessory fibrous tissue bridge that partially or completely divides the MV into 2 openings, producing a spectacles-like configuration.1 Only a small proportion of cases (approximately 15%) demonstrate 2 openings of nearly symmetrical size.2 Double orifice mitral valve rarely occurs in isolation and is frequently associated with other congenital malformations such as atrioventricular septal defects, coarctation of the aorta, BAV, sinus venosus atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, double orifice tricuspid valve, tetralogy of Fallot, and Ebstein's anomaly, highlighting a potential shared embryologic origin.3 An acquired form of DOMV may develop following surgical repair of MV prolapse, when the midportion of a prolapsing leaflet is sutured to the opposite leaflet edge, the so-called edge-to-edge or Alfieri double-orifice repair.4

Clinical presentation of DOMV ranges widely, from an incidental finding in asymptomatic individuals to significant dysfunction with MS or MR, often compounded by additional congenital abnormalities.2,5

There are 2 principal classification systems described in the literature for congenital DOMV. The first is an echocardiography-based classification, which depends on ultrasound probe imaging planes of the MV and categorizes the DOMV into a complete bridge type (DOMV visible in all planes), an incomplete bridge type (DOMV visible only in the leaflet-edge plane), and hole type.6 Another classification derived from autopsy data describes DOMV according to the location of the accessory orifice in an anterolateral commissure, a posteromedial commissure, or absence of an accessory orifice.2 Approximately half of the patients with DOMV maintain normal valvular function, while the other half exhibit some degree of valvular dysfunction including MS or MR. Mitral regurgitation may result from the abnormal orifice of MV itself or may occur secondary to ruptured chordae tendineae.5

Given its availability and ease, TTE remains the initial investigation of choice for the diagnosis and evaluation, with parasternal short-axis view being the optimal view for identifying DOMV, while long-axis and apical views are important for evaluating the subvalvular apparatus and quantifying regurgitation and stenosis.6,7 The TTE is used not only for diagnosis and classification but also for the assessment of associated anomalies.

Three-dimensional TEE can provide additional precision in delineating MV morphology and serve as a key modality in advanced multimodality imaging. It offers additional information because of its superior spatial resolution and an en face “surgical view” of the MV, allowing accurate visualization of DOMV morphology. It enables precise localization of the accessory orifice and detailed assessment of leaflet motion and subvalvular apparatus. Compared with 2D imaging, 3D TEE enhances diagnostic accuracy and aids in surgical planning. Therefore, it has become an invaluable tool in the comprehensive evaluation of complex MV anomalies.7

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging, although not an essential investigation, can be helpful in cases of diagnostic uncertainty and when there is a suspicion of associated anomalies.8

Supine bicycle stress echocardiography plays an important role in the functional evaluation of valvular lesions particularly when symptoms do not correspond to resting echocardiographic findings. It provides valuable information beyond resting studies and allows exercise-induced hemodynamic assessment by simultaneous Doppler data acquisition of transmitral gradients, pulmonary artery pressures, and overall functional capacity under physiological stress. By revealing exercise-induced obstruction or symptoms, stress echo helps determine the true hemodynamic significance of DOMV and guides appropriate management decisions.9

A multidisciplinary approach and individualized case-by-case decision-making remain essential to optimize outcomes in patients with this rare congenital abnormality. Treatment strategies are tailored based on each patient’s symptoms. Conservative management with regular clinical and imaging follow-up is the strategy of choice for the patient with no or minimal symptoms. Surgical intervention is indicated when the valvular dysfunction results in significant symptoms. Furthermore, correction of associated cardiac anomalies is frequently necessary during surgical management.1,5,10

Our case is of unique value as the patient is minimally symptomatic, multiparous with a surprising history of bearing 10 children and tolerating all normal deliveries without any complication, despite severely stenotic DOMV and mildly stenotic BAV with fused noncoronary cusp and right coronary cusp, which is not the common fusion pattern in the case of BAV. This case provides a comprehensive anatomical and hemodynamic evaluation using advanced multimodality imaging at rest and with exercise. These exercise data are scarcely documented in DOMV reports and provide important clinical insight into the physiological adaptability of this rare congenital valve combination in patients with minimal symptoms. Furthermore, by integrating anatomical precision with functional assessment on exercise hemodynamics, this case offers a practical and evidence-based imaging model to optimize diagnosis, risk stratification helping longitudinal management, and individualized decision-making in patients with rare congenital valve anomalies.

Conclusion

This case highlights the diversity of DOMV manifestations and the importance of comprehensive anatomical and functional evaluation, particularly through echocardiography including 3D TEE, to assess valvular pathology along with supine bicycle stress echocardiography for hemodynamic and functional assessment in minimally symptomatic patients, supporting an individualized treatment strategy.
