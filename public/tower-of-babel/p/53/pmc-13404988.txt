Spirit-based ready-to-drink cocktails and public health law: modernization without deregulation
Authors: Y Tony Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe United States alcohol-regulatory system classifies beverages by base ingredient—spirits, malt, or wine—rather than alcohol content. As a result, a canned vodka soda and a malt seltzer with the same alcohol content can be taxed, distributed, and sold under different rules. An industry-led alcohol-equivalency movement is pressing states to classify low-alcohol spirit-based ready-to-drink cocktails by alcohol content, producing tax reductions, expanded retail access, and federal container modernization. Drawing on state statutes, fiscal notes, Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau rulemaking, and public health evidence, this Policy Inquiry argues that alcohol-content-based classification is defensible but should not become deregulation without safeguards. Among the measures identified, none pairs spirit-based ready-to-drink tax or retail-access liberalization with minimum unit pricing, dedicated harm-reduction revenue, modernized warning mechanisms, and alcohol-availability controls. The article proposes a framework combining standard-drink-based access limits, minimum unit pricing, dedicated revenue, point-of-sale alcohol information, retail-marketing guardrails, and combined state and local licensing authority.Keywords: alcohol policy, spirit-based ready-to-drink cocktails, public health law, alcohol equivalency, excise tax, alcohol outlet density, health-warning mechanisms, federal preemption

Introduction

The ready-to-drink (RTD) alcohol category rose from 4% of US beverage-alcohol servings in 2019 to 8% in 2024 and is projected to reach 9% by 2029. Spirit-based products are a major driver: cocktails and long drinks account for 42% of new global RTD launches, US spirit-based products are forecast to grow about 6% annually through 2028, and most US RTD consumers identify as Millennial or Gen Z.1,2

This article uses “ready-to-drink alcoholic beverages” for the broader category of premixed alcoholic products, including malt-, wine-, and spirit-based products. Its focus is narrower: low-alcohol cocktails made with distilled spirits, such as vodka sodas or canned margaritas, that have historically been taxed and distributed as spirits. Malt- and wine-based products serve mainly as comparators; “RTDC” refers to Pennsylvania's statutory permit category.

This growth has collided with a regulatory architecture built for a different era. Under the federal-state system established by the Twenty-First Amendment and the Federal Alcohol Administration (FAA) Act, beverages are classified by base ingredient—distilled spirits, malt, or wine—rather than by alcohol by volume (ABV), producing a striking anomaly: a 5% ABV canned vodka soda is taxed and restricted as “hard liquor,” while a 5% ABV malt-based hard seltzer is treated as “beer.” This triggers cascading differences in federal excise taxation, state retail access, container rules, and labeling. At the federal level, a 12-ounce, 5% ABV spirit-based RTD faces roughly $0.13 in federal excise tax vs about $0.05 for a comparable malt beverage under the $16-per-barrel reduced beer rate—about 260% of the malt-beverage figure, or roughly 160% higher; under the $18-per-barrel general beer rate the ratio is about 230%—gaps that widen sharply at the state level.3-6

The distilled spirits industry has advanced a unified policy agenda built on the principle of “alcohol equivalency”—the argument that beverages of equivalent ABV should receive equivalent regulatory treatment regardless of base ingredient.7 Pennsylvania's Act 86 of 2024 created a regulated Ready-to-Drink Cocktail (RTDC) permit pathway for eligible retailers and distributors selling spirit-based RTDs of 0.5%-12.5% ABV in containers up to 16 ounces.8,9

Maryland and Texas considered tax reductions and retail-access expansions for spirit-based RTDs, examined below.10,11 At the federal level, the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau (TTB) issued Treasury Decision T.D. TTB-200 on January 10, 2025, adding 15 distilled-spirit standards of fill and eliminating the can-vs-other-container distinction.12 In a separate proposed rule, Notice No. 237, TTB proposed standardized “Alcohol Facts” statements covering serving size, servings per container, alcohol by volume, fluid ounces of pure alcohol per serving, calories, and specified nutrient information; these would provide alcohol-content and nutrition information, not cancer-specific health warnings.13

This Policy Inquiry makes 2 claims. First, ABV-based classification can correct a real mismatch because malt- and spirit-based ready-to-drink products with the same ABV and serving size deliver the same ethanol dose. Second, tax or retail-access parity should be conditioned on safeguards for price, availability, information, marketing, and youth access. The article proposes a framework consistent with Twenty-First Amendment authority and the Alcoholic Beverage Labeling Act (ABLA).

Methods and scope

The author reviewed state legislation, fiscal notes, agency guidance, and official bill histories concerning spirit-based ready-to-drink cocktails from 2021 through June 2026. Official state legislative, fiscal, and agency sources were treated as authoritative. Trade summaries and bill-tracking services were used only for context and were not relied on unless confirmed against official sources. Provisions were classified by tax treatment, retail access, direct-to-consumer (DTC) shipping, container or alcohol-by-volume limits, labeling, dedicated revenue, and outlet-density or sales-point controls. Inclusion required an enacted law, an introduced bill with official bill text or fiscal note, or agency action specifically addressing spirit-based ready-to-drink beverages, ready-to-drink cocktails, or comparable premixed distilled-spirit products. Bills addressing only ordinary cocktails-to-go, general alcohol licensing, or direct-to-consumer shipping without a spirit-based ready-to-drink provision were screened but not classified as ready-to-drink-specific. The scan is illustrative rather than exhaustive, and claims about which measures include or omit safeguards refer to the measures identified here.

Regulatory background

At the federal level, TTB exercises primary authority over labeling, advertising, and formulation under the FAA Act, 27 USC §§ 201 et seq. Producers of spirit-based RTDs must obtain formula approval under 27 CFR Part 5, Subpart J, and a Certificate of Label Approval before products reach the market. Many are treated as distilled spirits specialty products under TTB labeling rules, allowing simplified composition statements (eg, “made with vodka and triple sec”) rather than full ingredient lists. Federal excise taxes differ by base ingredient: $13.50 per proof gallon for spirits; for beer, $18.00 per barrel at the general rate or $16.00 per barrel at a reduced rate available in specified circumstances; and $1.07 per wine gallon for still wine.3-6 Under the reduced beer rate, the per-ounce-of-pure-alcohol burden on a low-alcohol spirit-based RTD is about 260% of the comparable malt-beverage figure, or roughly 160% higher; under the general beer rate it is about 230%.

Section 2 of the 21st Amendment grants states broad authority over the importation and sale of intoxicating liquors. In Granholm v Heald (2005), the Court held that the Amendment does not permit states to discriminate against out-of-state producers in violation of the dormant Commerce Clause, while affirming that states retain “virtually complete control over whether to permit importation or sale of liquor and how to structure the liquor distribution system.”14 Seventeen “control” states maintain government monopolies over wholesale spirits distribution; the remaining 33 “license” states delegate sales to private licensees under state oversight.15 Both models permit wide variation in spirit-based RTD tax rates, retail-channel access, and direct-to-consumer (DTC) shipping eligibility.16

This architecture creates 2 principal dimensions of base-ingredient disparity—taxation and retail access—plus a narrower e-commerce dimension for products eligible for direct shipment. “Discrimination” here carries its dormant Commerce Clause meaning, applied to in-state vs out-of-state shipping; differential treatment by base ingredient is more accurately termed regulatory disparity than constitutional discrimination.

Dimensions of regulatory disparity

States that have changed their rates have not converged, each adopting a different ABV threshold, container limit, and tax floor.20-22 The resulting burden on a 12-ounce, 5% ABV canned cocktail varies sharply with whether it is taxed as a low-ABV mixed beverage, wine-equivalent, or distilled spirit, and with layered sales taxes and control-state markups.

Retail access

Industry advocacy materials report that roughly 18 states bar spirit-based RTDs from grocery stores and 20 from convenience stores, while malt- and wine-based RTDs of equivalent ABV circulate in both.7 This segregation is a legacy of post-Prohibition architecture reserving “liquor” sales for specialized outlets. Pennsylvania's Act 86 created a regulated RTDC permit pathway within the Commonwealth's three-tier system, imposing a $2500 fee, mandatory Responsible Alcohol Management Program (RAMP) certification, transaction-scan age verification for anyone appearing under 35, designated-register sales, and locked storage. Compared with ordinary distilled-spirit sales in Pennsylvania, Act 86 liberalizes access by creating an RTDC permit pathway outside the traditional state-controlled spirits channel; compared with beer and wine retailing, however, it remains more conditional, adding RTDC-specific permit, storage, register, training, and age-verification rules, while some broader alcohol-license obligations may apply across license categories.8,9

Direct-to-consumer shipping

Direct-to-consumer (DTC) shipping is a secondary illustration, not the central public-health issue. Most states permit some form of wine DTC, while spirits DTC remains far more restricted.16,23 In states that permit wine DTC but not spirits DTC, a wine-based RTD meeting the statutory definition of wine may be eligible for direct shipment, whereas a spirit-based RTD generally is not.24 Where states open spirits DTC, they do so more narrowly than for wine—New York's S2852A (2024) and California's AB 1246 (2025) rely on small-distillery thresholds, volume limits, and reciprocity.25,26 After Granholm and Tennessee Wine v Thomas (2019), such limits must rest on public-health or enforcement justifications rather than protectionism, leaving reciprocity's durability uncertain.27,28

The legislative momentum began in the Midwest and has since spread (Table 2). Michigan's SB 144 (Public Act 19 of 2021) cut the tax on “mixed spirit drinks” by 37.5% and raised the canned-product ABV ceiling to 13.5%;20 Nebraska's LB 274 (2021) created a “ready-to-drink cocktail” category taxed at the wine rate, a 74.7% cut;21 and Vermont's H 730 (2022) cut the spirit-based RTD tax by 85.7%; industry materials estimated that the law expanded eligibility from 81 state stores to more than 1000 private beer-and-wine retailers.22 North Carolina's SB 527 (2024) addressed premixed-cocktail tax treatment at or below 13% ABV and separately made cocktails-to-go permanent.30

The pace accelerated in 2025-2026. Maryland's HB 736 would cut the spirit-based RTD tax from $1.50 to $0.60 per gallon (60%) for products of 12% ABV or less in containers up to 16 ounces; one fiscal-note scenario projected a general-fund loss of at least $2 million annually from FY 2027.10 Because such notes estimate general-fund effects rather than downstream budgets, a tax cut can erode substance-use-disorder prevention and treatment funded by that revenue, absent replacement or maintenance-of-effort provisions. New York's S9225 would create reduced excise treatment for certain low-alcohol farm-distillery RTDs.31 Texas SB 2225, engrossed by the Senate in April 2025, would have authorized spirit-based RTDs up to 10% ABV for malt-beverage retailers but was not enacted in the 89th Regular Session.11 Not every jurisdiction has liberalized: in November 2025 the Oregon Liquor and Cannabis Commission (OLCC) opposed privatizing canned-cocktail sales; OLCC estimated privatization would have generated $7.7 million rather than $11.4 million over 2020-2025, a 32% decrease and a $3.7 million loss for public programs.32 In parallel, TTB's T.D. TTB-200 modernized wine and distilled-spirits standards of fill in ways relevant to single-serve spirit-based ready-to-drink products.12

Equivalency is strongest when it corrects base-ingredient rules that treat identical ethanol doses differently. The public-health concern is its translation into tax cuts and broader access without measures that preserve price floors, monitor cumulative availability, and communicate risk. Across the enacted measures identified in this scan, none included modernized health-warning mechanisms, minimum unit pricing, or outlet-density caps.

Public health evidence

Price and consumption

Alcohol taxation is among the most evidence-based instruments in public health law. Wagenaar et al.'s 2009 meta-analysis of 112 studies and 1003 estimates found that alcohol prices and taxes are inversely associated with consumption, with an average price elasticity of approximately −0.50.33 Subsequent reviews concluded that doubling alcohol taxes would be associated with substantial reductions in alcohol-related mortality, traffic-crash deaths, and other harms, and that the price–consumption relationship is robust across study designs and jurisdictions.34,35 Younger drinkers are more price-responsive: a 10% price increase has been estimated to reduce consumption among 11- to 18-year-olds by roughly 5.3%, compared with 4.4% for adults.36 The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies alcohol taxation as a “best buy” intervention.37

The state tax reductions documented above move in the opposite direction: Michigan, Nebraska, and Vermont enacted cuts of 37.5%-85.7%, and Maryland's HB 736 would cut spirit-based RTD taxes by 60%. Heavy drinkers tend to respond to price increases by substituting cheaper products rather than drinking less.36 Scotland's minimum unit pricing (MUP) policy addressed this by setting a floor price per unit of alcohol; subsequent evaluation associated it with a 13.4% reduction in alcohol-attributable deaths, with larger benefits in more deprived communities.38 None of the US measures identified in this scan paired tax cuts with MUP floors, tax-indexing, or dedicated harm-reduction revenue.

Sales-point expansion and outlet density

The Campbell Collaboration's systematic review found that greater alcohol outlet density is consistently associated with increased consumption and related harms—medical harms, injury, crime, and violence—a conclusion echoed by a parallel review of 44 studies.39,40 Longitudinal evidence from Melbourne linked an increase of 1 packaged outlet per 1000 residents to 1.36 additional domestic violence incidents per 1000 residents.41 A national study of 32 482 census areas in England found the highest-density areas had 13% higher acute-alcohol and 22% higher chronic-condition admission rates.42 The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) concludes there is “strong scientific evidence” that regulating outlet density is among the most effective strategies for reducing excessive consumption and related harms.43

These changes rarely create new stores or licenses; more often they let existing beer, wine, grocery, convenience, restaurant, or distributor outlets sell spirit-based RTDs previously barred to them. The public-health issue is therefore best described as sales-point expansion for spirit-based RTDs rather than an automatic increase in physical outlet density. Pennsylvania's Act 86 authorized such sales in restaurants, distributors, supermarkets, and convenience stores statewide, and Texas SB 2225 would let malt-beverage retailers, including grocery and convenience settings, sell these products—settings that generally lack the controlled entry and liquor-specific environment of dedicated liquor stores. Outlet-density controls remain relevant nonetheless: new permits and the cumulative concentration of availability still matter—particularly given evidence that neighborhoods with higher proportions of Black residents and families in poverty already carry higher outlet density, with redlining a strong predictor of clustering.43,44

Younger-adult marketing and information asymmetry

Spirit-based RTDs, like the broader category, disproportionately attract younger adults, and their marketing actively engages this group through health-oriented marketing, including “health-halo” claims implying lower risk because a product is low-calorie, low-sugar, or wellness-oriented.1 A systematic audit of RTDs in Québec City grocery stores found 68% displayed at least 1 nutrition cue, with hard seltzers especially likely to carry nutrient-content claims (NCCs).45 Hobin et al randomized 5063 Canadian consumers to RTD labels varying NCCs and health-warning labels (HWLs): claims such as “0 g sugar” raised perceived healthiness, while HWLs reduced intentions to try, buy, and binge.46,47 These Canadian and online studies identify mechanisms relevant to US retail. Because spirit-based RTDs skew young, moving them into grocery and convenience channels increases early, repeated exposure to distilled-spirit brand names—including co-branded products pairing a spirits brand with a familiar mixer—which the recommendations below address.

Conceptually, 3 categories of information are at issue: (1) “Alcohol Facts” disclosures (serving size, servings per container, alcohol by volume, fluid ounces of pure alcohol per serving, calories, carbohydrates, fat, and protein); (2) container warning labels (cancer, pregnancy, impaired driving, liver disease, addiction); and (3) marketing restrictions (limits on “0 g sugar,” “low calorie,” and wellness imagery). The US framework treats these unevenly. The Alcoholic Beverage Labeling Act (ABLA), 27 USC § 216, expressly preempts state-mandated container statements relating to alcohol and health beyond the federal warning, essentially static since 1988.48 That preemption is container-specific; non-container interventions—point-of-sale warnings and signage, licensing conditions, shelf-placement rules, outlet-density controls—may remain available, subject to independent First Amendment, state-law, and federal constraints, including doctrines governing commercial-speech restrictions and compelled factual disclosures.49,50

The January 2025 Surgeon General's advisory found that alcohol contributes to nearly 100 000 cancer cases and about 20 000 cancer deaths annually in the United States, the third leading preventable cause of cancer after tobacco and obesity, and that fewer than half of US adults are aware of the alcohol-cancer link.51 The advisory called on Congress to update the federal container warning, a power only Congress holds under ABLA. Ireland's Public Health (Alcohol) Act 2018 will require comprehensive cancer warning labels from 2028.52 As of 2018, 47 WHO Member States required some form of alcohol warning label, but that figure is not a count of cancer warnings; the United States already requires a federal alcohol warning that simply does not mention cancer, making Ireland's forthcoming label the clearer cancer-warning comparator.53

Limits of the evidence

This analysis has limits. The evidence on alcohol taxation, outlet density, and labeling is robust, but the state spirit-based RTD measures examined here are recent, and direct evaluations of their effects on consumption, substitution, younger-adult exposure, youth-access risks, and alcohol-related harms remain limited. The public-health concern is therefore prospective and precautionary: these measures operate through mechanisms—price, physical availability, and marketing exposure—repeatedly linked to alcohol-related harm in the broader literature, but empirical post-change evaluation is urgently needed.

A model public health framework for spirit-based RTD modernization

ABV-based classification is a rational starting point, but it is not sufficient. The United Kingdom's August 2023 shift to alcohol-by-volume duty shows that parity can reduce arbitrary category distinctions.54 The public-health task is to attach safeguards to spirit-based ready-to-drink tax or retail-access changes, using point-of-sale signage, licensing conditions, and shelf-placement rules where container mandates are preempted.48

Standard-drink-based limits for grocery and convenience access. Pennsylvania's Act 86 restricts RTDC permit products to 12.5% ABV. That ceiling keeps the strongest products out of grocery channels but is too high as a public-health model for single-serve formats: a 16-ounce container at 12.5% ABV holds roughly 3.3 US standard drinks (one US standard drink being 0.6 fl oz, or 14 g, of pure ethanol). States should instead set a lower ABV ceiling for grocery and convenience access than for full spirits channels and tie single-serve eligibility to standard drinks per container. A 16-ounce container is reasonable only for lower-ABV products—about 7.5% ABV yields roughly 2 standard drinks—while higher-ABV spirit-based RTDs should be confined to smaller containers or more controlled channels.

Dedicated excise revenue and maintenance of effort. WHO identifies alcohol taxation as a “best buy” intervention.37 States should statutorily dedicate a portion of spirit-based RTD excise revenue to alcohol prevention, treatment, enforcement, and surveillance rather than depositing all proceeds into general funds—and, where alcohol taxes already fund those programs, should include maintenance-of-effort language so that a tax reduction does not quietly defund prevention and treatment.

Point-of-sale information, retail-display rules, and limits on youth-facing and health-halo marketing. Because ABLA preempts state-mandated container statements on alcohol and health, states should work through non-container tools. They should require point-of-sale signage modeled on and supplementing Alcohol Facts disclosures—serving size, servings per container, alcohol by volume, fluid ounces of pure alcohol per serving, and calories—plus separate cancer-risk information where legally permissible, and condition spirit-based RTD permits on shelf tags or digital disclosures. Permits should also be conditioned on retail-display rules that separate alcoholic RTDs from soft drinks, energy drinks, and nonalcoholic mixers; prohibit youth-oriented cross-promotional displays pairing spirit-based RTDs with familiar nonalcoholic brands; and regulate misleading health-halo claims—including nutrient-content claims implying alcohol is health-promoting—where consistent with First Amendment doctrine and not otherwise preempted.49,50 These are display and disclosure conditions, not brand-name bans, addressing youth-facing normalization while avoiding commercial-speech and trademark overreach. These interventions can counteract the health-halo marketing documented above.46,47

Alcohol-availability monitoring and combined state-and-local licensing authority. The CDC finds “strong scientific evidence” that regulating outlet density reduces excessive consumption and related harms.43 Because availability is driven by the number of licenses rather than the products themselves, states expanding spirit-based RTD access should require availability monitoring and preserve both state and local authority to deny new licenses or permits in over-saturated and historically disadvantaged neighborhoods. Federal action is needed on 3 fronts. First, TTB should finalize Alcohol Facts disclosures covering serving size, servings per container, alcohol by volume, pure alcohol per serving, calories, and specified nutrients, and consider a shorter compliance period than the proposed 5 years.13 Second, Congress should update the federal container warning to reflect contemporary alcohol-cancer evidence because ABLA preempts additional state-mandated container health statements.48,51 Third, states should use tools outside the preempted container space, including point-of-sale warnings, licensing conditions, shelf-placement rules, and outlet-density controls.

The policy window is narrow. Once spirit-based RTDs achieve grocery-store parity with malt beverages, retroactive safeguards will face stronger industry opposition. The 2025 Surgeon General alcohol-cancer advisory and the less specific 2025-2030 Dietary Guidelines make this an important moment to pair market-access changes with safeguards.51,55

Conclusion

The spirit-based ready-to-drink reclassification movement is a restructuring of American alcohol policy. Equivalency is sound at the ethanol-dose level: a 5% alcohol-by-volume vodka soda and a comparable malt seltzer deliver the same alcohol. The population-level question is different: whether broader retail access, lower prices, and grocery-aisle normalization increase total exposure and harm. Existing price, outlet-density, and health-halo evidence suggests that risk is real.33,39,46,47

Conditional equivalency and harm reduction can coexist. States can modernize classification while requiring standard-drink-based access limits, coordinated container limits, price floors, dedicated revenue, retail-display and marketing guardrails, point-of-sale information, and preserved state and local licensing authority. The objection is not to alcohol-content classification, but to using equivalency as deregulation.
