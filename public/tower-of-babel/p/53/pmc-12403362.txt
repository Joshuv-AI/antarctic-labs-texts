Barriers to implementing clinical practice guidelines for managing patients with pressure injuries in China: a qualitative study using the theoretical domains framework
Authors: Jiyuan Shi, Hongshuang Chen, Xuelian Wang, Feiyu Fang, Chao Sun, Zheng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPressure injuries (PIs) are a common challenge faced by healthcare institutions worldwide. Evidence-based practice is crucial for improving the quality of patient care, but its implementation often faces many difficulties. This study aims to explore the barriers to implementing clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) to manage patients with PIs in Chinese tertiary hospitals.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using the theoretical domains framework to identify barriers to implementing CPGs. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 22 healthcare professionals, including nurses and doctors with experience in managing patients with PIs, across multiple departments in tertiary hospitals. Data were analyzed using content analysis, supported by NVivo 11.0 software, to identify key themes.ResultsThis study identified several barriers to implementing high-quality CPGs for the PIs in China, including insufficient knowledge and skills among healthcare workers. Additionally, challenges such as a lack of multidisciplinary collaboration and information technology support, absence of incentive systems, resource limitations, unfavorable reimbursement policies, and insufficient leadership support were highlighted. Addressing these complex issues requires a multi-faceted system intervention strategy. These findings will provide important references for promoting evidence-based practices in the management of patients with PIs in developing countries.ConclusionsThe findings of this study highlight the ongoing challenges in translating evidence-based CPGs into clinical practice for managing patients with PIs in China. This study systematically identifies key barriers related to healthcare providers’ ability, opportunity, and motivation, emphasizing the complexity of factors that hinder effective CPGs implementation. The insights gained lay the groundwork for developing targeted intervention strategies to foster the adoption of best practices in wound care and evidence-based practices to manage PIs in the future.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-03785-4.Keywords: Pressure injuries, Evidence-based guidelines, Clinical practice guidelines, Theoretical domains framework

Background

Pressure injuries (PIs), also known as pressure sores, are a common challenge faced by healthcare institutions worldwide [1, 2]. Although some achievements have been made in the prevention of PIs, the occurrence of PIs in the elderly, bedridden patients, diabetes, and other high-risk groups is still unavoidable [2, 3]. Li et al. showed that the global prevalence of PIs in hospitalised adult patients is estimated to be 12.8%, with a hospital-acquired PIs incidence rate of up to 8.4% [2]. The prevalence of PIs in hospitalized adults in Asia is estimated at 3.0%, with China reporting a lower rate of approximately 1.67%, potentially influenced by prevention efforts, statistical methods (such as whether to include stage I of PIs), or data inaccuracies [2–5]. Although the hospital-acquired PIs incidence has decreased in recent years, given the large total number of hospitalizations, the overall number of patients with PIs is bound to be a quite staggering number [1, 2, 6].

PIs usually bring serious harm and impact to patients, such as pain, infection, and decreased quality of life [6]. In addition, if PIs are not effectively treated, it may lead to delayed wound healing and even progress to cellulitis and osteomyelitis, posing serious physical and mental health risks [6–8]. Managing PIs requires not only the appropriate selection of dressings and debridement, but also careful attention to nutrition, positioning, infection, and pain, all of which demand comprehensive and ongoing patient evaluation [8–11]. Bergstrom’s study found that 45.4% of Stage 2 PIs healed within a 12-week period, with a median healing time of 46 days [12]. Palese’s study found that the average healing time for Stage II PIs is approximately 23 days, with an overall healing rate of 56.7% within a 10-week follow-up period [13]. Although the healing rate for adult patients with PIs is typically low, effective treatments, such as nutritional support, the use of wound dressings that maintain a moist healing environment, timely debridement, and multidisciplinary teamwork, can significantly improve healing rates and shorten recovery time [16].

As an important tool for narrowing the gap in current clinical practice and providing the best evidence, high-quality clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) have played a pivotal role in improving the standardization of overall healthcare [17, 18]. In recent years, in order to promote the dissemination and application of PIs - related evidence, multiple institutions or associations have developed CPGs, including high-quality CPGs such as the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel, the European Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel, the Pan Pacific Pressure Injury Alliance (NPUAP/EPUAP/PPPIA), the Registered Nurses Association of Ontario (RNAO), Wound Ostomy Continence Nurses Society (WOCN) [10, 11, 18]. These CPGs provide comprehensive recommendations for the assessment and management of patients with PI, including nutrition, position change, infection control, pain, dressings, biophysical therapy, debridement, and other aspects [10, 11, 18]. Two versions of the CPGs have been translated and interpreted by Chinese researchers (NPUAP/EPUAP/PPPIA 2019 and RNAO 2017) and widely disseminated. However, the transition from evidence-based innovation to successful clinical practice often requires a considerable amount of time, and the promotion of non-localized CPGs often faces greater challenges [19, 20].

The challenges and complexity of implementing CPGs far outweigh the production process of evidence, but applying CPGs is a necessary bridge to bridge the gap between theory and practice [19, 21, 22]. The lack of theoretical basis makes it difficult to understand and explain the reasons for successful or failed implementation, thereby limiting the formulation of implementation strategies and missing opportunities for successful change [23]. In order to facilitate the successful translation of complex and multi-component evidence into clinical practice, researchers have developed dozens of theories, models, and frameworks to assist in implementing research [23–25]. The theoretical domains framework (TDF) integrates 33 behavioral change theories, aiming to help identify and understand the factors that influence behavior [26, 27]. This framework has been proven useful in medical settings, such as identifying obstacles in evidence-based practices for preventing PIs and venous leg ulcer [28–30]. A comprehensive evaluation of the barriers in the application of CPGs is a core step in promoting the dissemination and implementation of knowledge in the real world [24, 26, 31, 32]. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to explore the barriers to medical staff implementation of CPGs for patients with PIs based on TDF.

Methods

Ethical issues

Prior to the start of the study, approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the School of Nursing, Peking Union Medical College, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Ethics Number: PUMCSON-2022-13).

Study participants

Participants were recruited via the Chinese Nursing Society, participants were purposefully sampled to represent a range of medical staff with wound management experience, familiarity with CPGs recommendations prior to the interview, and willingness to share their views on factors affecting the implementation of the CPGs. We have established the minimum inclusion criteria: (1) Having worked in a tertiary comprehensive hospital for more than 5 years; (2) Informed consent and voluntary acceptance of interviews; (3) The professional title is nurse-in-charge/attending physician or above (associate chief physician/nurse or associate chief physician/nurse), and having participated in the clinical management of PIs patients. We excluded medical staff who have not been involved in PIs patient management for over a year.

Interview guideline

The interview guideline is based on the TDF, which encompasses 14 theoretical domains: (1) Knowledge, (2) Skills, (3) Social/Professional Role and Identity, (4) Beliefs about Capabilities, (5) Optimism, (6) Beliefs about Consequences, (7) Reinforcement, (8) Intentions, (9) Goals, (10) Memory, Attention, and Decision Processes, (11) Environmental Context and Resources, (12) Social Influences, (13) Emotion, and (14) Behavioral Regulation. This framework has been widely used to assess barriers in the knowledge transfer process [27, 29]. At least one question was formulated for each domain. The guideline was developed and revised by three researchers (JYS, XLW, and ZL), and its reliability was validated through a pretest conducted with two senior specialist nurses (detailed definitions of the 14 theoretical domains and the interview questions have been added in Appendix 1).

Data collection

We sent the two recently released CPGs to participants one week in advance, both of which were rated as high in method quality and have Chinese translations or interpretations [10, 18, 33]. When the collected data does not provide any new information about the impact of the implementation of high-quality CPGs, it is considered that the data is saturated and the interview ends. The first author conducted face-to-face or online interviews with the interviewees based on their preferences and obtained informed consent through written or electronic signatures. The interview recording was transcribed into text within 48 h, while data analysis and collection were conducted simultaneously. The transcribed text was entered, organized, and analyzed using NVivo 11.0 software. After verifying the accuracy, the interview records were reviewed multiple times and analyzed through content analysis.

Data analysis

Specific themes and codes were categorized based on the TDF framework [30]. If data related to different fields, it was organized into the code that most accurately reflected the key topic [30]. Two researchers (JYS and HSC) participated in coding comparison and discussion. In cases of disagreement or unclear content, the information was verified or supplemented by the respondents through phone or offline interviews. Both audio and text materials were securely stored throughout the research process. All respondent names and their organization names were anonymized to ensure their privacy was protected.

Results

Participant characteristics

The study included 22 participants (Table 1), with an average interview duration of 31 min. Of these, 12 participants were aged 40 years or older, and the majority had over 10 years of work experience (n = 17). The participants were from 6 provinces in China, representing 12 different hospitals, and were distributed across 9 departments, with the largest groups coming from the intensive care unit (n = 4), geriatrics (n = 4), and Surgery(n = 4).

Barriers to implementation of the clinical practice CPGs recommendations

Barriers to implementing high-quality CPGs recommendations were identified across the 14 theoretical domains of the TDF:

Knowledge

Mastering the knowledge of CPGs faces challenges, influenced by various factors such as the complexity of CPGs, lack of localization, and language barriers. N4: “As you know, the guide is a very thick book with a lot of sources and content of evidence. We may not read it in such detail. Moreover, the knowledge of traditional Chinese medicine is not reminded in the guide.” N7: “It’s better to have a more concise content. I really don’t have the energy to study the entire guide, and I prefer a concise process and operational standard based on the guide and hospital context.” N13: “The foreign version of the guide does not conform to the characteristics of the Chinese language in terms of language construction and description. Although the 2019 international guides have translations, they are difficult to read and affect my understanding and application.” D4: “I didn’t specifically study the CPGs for PIs. Instead, I would read the CPGs for infection, chronic wounds, and surgical debridement. Breaking them down is actually interconnected, with more details and levels involved.”

Skills

A commonly mentioned theme was the lack of sufficient skills related to the evaluation and treatment of patients who have already developed PIs. The lack of specialized training and the interdisciplinary nature of the required skills may be key factors contributing to this issue. N4: “Some inexperienced young surgeons and specialized nurses may lack the operational skills to manage PIs, such as debridement and proper dressing application, which requires further training.” N12: “I’m not sure how to assess certain scales that aren’t included in the information system, and we haven’t received training on some of the techniques mentioned in the CPGs.” D8: “As a geriatric doctor, I may lack expertise in managing PIs, but I believe it requires collaboration across multiple disciplines, with the nursing team playing a critical role.”

Social/professional role and identity -

Unclear role positioningThere are significant differences between China and other countries in terms of medical models, institutional settings, organizational culture, and personnel allocation. There is still a lack of effective multidisciplinary role positioning for PIs patients. N4: “Currently, China’s Tertiary Grade A Hospital mostly adopts a PIs consultation model led by nursing teams, with patients scattered across different departments. There are significant differences in the ability and resources of departments to handle PIs, and there is usually a lack of effective interdisciplinary cooperation mechanisms, resulting in low efficiency and potential delays in treatment opportunities”. N9: “For example, some pain and nutritional assessments are just done for the sake of doing it, without delving into it. What I know seems to lack a professional and multidisciplinary approach, and there is basically no such collaborative model.” D5: “Wounds are usually first treated by nurses. If the wound is severe or the nursing team is unable to handle it, they can seek our involvement, but the division of responsibilities in each area is unclear and there is insufficient interdisciplinary cooperation.”

Beliefs about capabilities

Some medical staff lack confidence in managing patients with PIs. N8: “Sometimes when I ask a young nurse to handle her, she doesn’t dare to take action.” N12: “I am not a specialist nurse, and I lack confidence when dealing with PIs.” D8: “When I encounter PIs in the elderly, I often feel uncertain and I still need the help of other professionals.”

Optimism

The respondents hold an optimistic attitude towards gradually advancing evidence-based practices in managing patients with PIs. N9: “We should definitely use the latest evidence to standardize our clinical work. This may be a slow improvement process, but we still have confidence.”

Beliefs about consequences

All respondents acknowledge the importance and necessity of evidence-based practice. N3 “I highly endorse the CPGs, but it may be that I am not familiar enough with the new CPGs. However, I believe it will definitely benefit patients and healthcare professionals.” N4: “Following the CPGs for treating PIs will result in better outcomes, there’s no problem with that.”

Reinforcement

There is a lack of evaluation indicators and incentive measures in clinical practice. N4: “Currently, there is indeed a lack of corresponding incentive measures, and certain rewards and feedback need to be given based on workload or performance.” D4: “I think it should be the senior management of the medical or nursing department, as well as the hospital, who give enough recognition and trust to such work, give you the economic compensation you deserve, and make you willing to do it.”

Intention

Lack of motivation to follow the CPGs may affect the implementation of CPGs by healthcare workers. N11: “I think many healthcare professionals don’t attach much importance to pressure ulcer CPGs. With so much clinical work, why do I have to follow the CPGs to carry out my work? I lack motivation.” D5: “I have too much knowledge to learn, and I don’t have the motivation to learn the PIs CPGs. It’s best for you to tell me directly how to do it.”

Goal

Sometimes, in clinical settings, it is not possible to fully comply with CPGs. N6: “Some patients are discharged after controlling their underlying diseases, and you cannot follow the CPGs. The bed resources are very tight now For some patients, our core goal is not to cure pressure ulcers, and many departments focus more on specialized diseases.” D4: “Following the CPGs for PIs is not my main goal, I have many other considerations.”

Memory, attention, and decision process

There are certain obstacles to fully mastering knowledge, and knowledge is easily forgotten when there is little contact with patients. N3: “Different people have different perceptions. If there is less exposure to departments, and you don’t put it into clinical practice, you will soon forget.” N13: “I can’t remember, sometimes I go to read and interpret, but a large guide may seem inconvenient.” D8: “If you say you let me think for myself and then sort out every item so clearly, I don’t think I have that ability.”

Each hospital and department has different equipment and dressings. In the application of CPGs, we should combine what our hospital has to make the best recommendation.” D6: “In terms of equipment, we may also lack it. You see, when it comes to electrical stimulation, ultrasound, and air flow beds, we definitely don’t have them.”

Discussion

Some developing countries, like China, have yet to establish their own CPGs for PIs [18, 33]. While high-quality international CPGs have been translated and disseminated by local researchers, it is essential to understand the barriers to effectively implementing these international CPGs in developing countries [19, 34]. Therefore, based on the interview CPGs developed using the TDF, this study conducted semi-structured interviews with 16 nurses and 8 doctors. We investigated the barriers to implementing the relevant CPGs and identified several obstacles consistent with evidence-based practices in other fields, such as poor operability, poor accessibility, lack of resources, organizational culture, and lack of leadership. Grimshaw et al. examined the effectiveness of implementation strategies tailored to CPGs, and the results showed that these customized strategies can improve nursing outcomes by an average of 4.3–12.0% [35]. However, successfully implementing CPGs requires more than a single intervention. Instead, a comprehensive intervention strategy is necessary to address the various barriers to change, which demands a systematic approach and careful planning during the implementation process [25, 35, 36]. If the interventions are based on principles derived from evidence and behaviour change theory, the effectiveness of behavior change will be significantly enhanced [36]. The findings of this study provide valuable insights for this approach.

Lack of knowledge and skills is a commonly reported barrier among respondents, and the reasons for this lack are complex [37, 38]. CPGs in this field are mostly developed by non-local organizations, leading to difficulties in localization, complexity, access, and training, which hinder healthcare professionals from systematically mastering relevant knowledge and skills [29]. CPGs developers should carefully evaluate the applicability of recommendations across different countries and regions. Involving experts from various geographical areas in the development process can enhance the relevance and inclusivity of these CPGs. Additionally, it is essential to consider the specific medical environment and resource availability in the local context. If conditions allow, countries can develop their own CPGs to ensure they are more localized. For example, in China, recommendations might include the use of traditional Chinese medicine. If local conditions do not permit the development of independent CPGs, appropriate tools can be used to modify international CPGs to ensure their effective implementation in the local context. Having CPGs alone is not enough; efforts should also be made to strengthen their dissemination and training through various means, such as courses and seminars.

CPGs emphasize the importance of interdisciplinary cooperation, but in practical work, interdisciplinary cooperation faces many challenges, which are an important factor affecting the implementation of the CPGs. Patients with PIs are often dispersed across various departments, leading to complex medical environments and difficult management. Several interviewees noted that interdisciplinary collaboration is hindered by the absence of effective localized models and communication barriers. Some interviewees also expressed that doctors’ insufficient attention to PIs can also affect the development of interdisciplinary collaboration [39]. In the future, interdisciplinary collaboration can be improved by placing greater emphasis on and implementing effective measures. Furthermore, improving the level of interdisciplinary cooperation requires the establishment of a professional multidisciplinary team, including surgeons, nurses, nutritionists, rehabilitation experts, etc. Several interviewees have mentioned that leadership can help improve the environment for evidence-based practice, and previous studies have also shown that leadership is an important predictor for nurses to continue using CPGs and recommendations [40]. Qualified leaders not only provide necessary resources and support for evidence-based practice, but also play a key role in interdisciplinary collaboration, knowledge dissemination and evaluation, quality control, and other aspects of PIs treatment [41]. The enhancement of leadership’s positive impact on PIs management is also a topic worthy of further exploration in future research.

The study also revealed insufficient implementation resources, including shortages of equipment and dressings, mismatches between information systems and CPGs content, and restrictions on PIs management imposed by medical insurance policies. Some interviewees pointed out that after the implementation of DRG reimbursement model in China, the reimbursement ratio of treatment expenses for PIs (as complications) is very low, and hospitals’ pursuit of shortening hospitalization time has also led to incomplete evidence-based practice, which is a major obstacle [42]. In addition, information system mismatch and lack of information technology support can also affect the development of evidence-based practices in PIs patient management [43]. It is necessary to comprehensively update information systems based on CPGs to make them an important tool for promoting behavior change and continuous quality control. The government can suggest that professional associations or societies establish a dedicated working group to implement PIs CPGs and provide policy support, especially in terms of the responsibilities and authorities of specialized nurses (most specialized nurses in China do not have prescription authority) [44]. At the same time, each hospital should establish its own multidisciplinary evidence-based practice team to comprehensively plan the implementation of CPGs and continuously overcome difficulties in practice. Addressing issues in interdisciplinary collaboration, organizational culture, medical insurance reimbursement policies, human resources, law, and other fields requires support from the government and legislative departments. In the future, government and legislative departments should develop policies, laws, and regulations to support evidence-based practice for patients with PI, and continuously improve the current status of wound management practices.

Our research further confirms the complexity of barriers to evidence-based practice in managing patients with PIs, from the perspectives of ability, opportunity, motivation, and behavioral patterns (COM-B), only by overcoming multiple barriers such as skills, knowledge, interpersonal relationships, time, resources, motivation, and attitude can the best evidence be effectively followed in evidence-based clinical management of PIs patients [27, 45]. At present, in addition to discovering some common problems, research has also identified personalized issues in the implementation of international high-quality CPGs for the PIs in China. Future research should focus on developing implementation strategies that address the barriers identified in this study, based on established theoretical frameworks such as the behavior change wheel, to improve the uptake of high-quality CPGs in clinical practice.

Limitation

The study has several limitations. One limitation of this study is that the varying number of doctors and nurses participating in the study could introduce variability and affect the consistency of the data. Additionally, the interview structure presents another limitation. Although the aim was to capture comprehensive insights, the interview questions were loosely based on the TDF and were open-ended, focusing more on general obstacles faced by medical staff in implementing CPGs rather than addressing specific barriers for each recommendation. While participants were asked to review the CPGs before the interview, we cannot guarantee that they fully understood all the details. Lastly, the study was conducted within a single Chinese healthcare context, limiting the external validity of the results, and the findings may not be directly applicable to medical practices in other countries or regions. Further validation in different settings is needed to assess the broader international applicability.

Conclusion

The treatment of patients with PIs is a significant challenge for healthcare institutions worldwide. Adhering to scientifically sound CPGs is crucial for improving the care quality for PIs patients, minimizing complications, optimizing resource utilization, and reducing medical costs. The research results indicate that following CPGs for managing PIs patients in China still face many challenges, and the obstacles to translating the best evidence into wound care practices are multifaceted, influenced by different levels of factors such as ability, opportunity, and motivation, which may also interact with each other. TDF provides a systematic and effective framework for this study to comprehensively identify and understand the barriers to evidence-based practice in PI patient management. This study lays a key foundation for developing corresponding intervention strategies, and future research can use tools such as behavior change wheels to improve evidence translation in clinical practice based on these results.
