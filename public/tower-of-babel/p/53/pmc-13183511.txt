Conservative management of cranial cruciate ligament injury in an Asian small-clawed otter (Aonyx cinereus)
Authors: Mizuki TOMIHARI, Shoki MURAKAMI, Toshiyuki TANAKA, Takashi HASEGAWA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 9-year-old male Asian small-clawed otter (Aonyx cinereus) kept at NIFREL exhibited acute left hindlimb lameness. Under general anesthesia, physical examination revealed a positive cranial drawer sign on the left stifle, and cranial tibial displacement with osteoarthritic changes was observed on the stifle radiograph, leading to a diagnosis of cranial cruciate ligament (CCL) injury. The left hindlimb lameness improved with conservative management consisting of meloxicam and exercise restriction. Over a follow-up period of 953 days, the left gait remained stable with minimal radiographic progression of degenerative joint disease. In Asian small-clawed otters, this case adds to the limited body of knowledge on CCL injuries and demonstrates the viability of conservative treatment depending on clinical presentation.Keywords: Asian small-clawed otter, conservative management, cranial cruciate ligament injury, hindlimb lameness, zoo animal medicine

The Asian small-clawed otter (Aonyx cinereus) is a small otter species belonging to the family Mustelidae and subfamily Lutrinae, distributed throughout Southeast Asia and surrounding regions [6, 8]. This species is classified as “Vulnerable” on the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List [17], and faces threats from habitat destruction, illegal trade, and a reduction in prey biomass. In recent years, captive numbers in zoos and aquariums have increased, but information regarding health management and age-related diseases in captivity remains limited. Necropsy surveys of captive individuals in zoos and aquariums occasionally identify degenerative joint disease (DJD) as a concurrent age-related condition [14]. In wild California sea otters (Enhydra lutris), osteoarthrosis with bony changes in the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is documented [1, 13] while cranial cruciate ligament (CCL) rupture is also reported in the Cape clawless otter (Aonyx capensis) [10]. However, there is little information on orthopedic diseases in Asian small-clawed otters. Here, we report a case of acute hindlimb lameness in a captive Asian small-clawed otter, diagnosed as a CCL injury, and describe the clinical course obtained at diagnosis and during the follow-up.

A 9-year-old, 2.5-kg male Asian small-clawed otter (Aonyx cinereus) was kept at NIFREL (Osaka Aquarium, Japan). Lameness of the left hind limb was first observed on the morning of December 26, 2022 (day 1). The clinical signs appeared acutely with no previously noted gait abnormalities. The initial lameness was progressive, and within several days, the animal became non-weight bearing on the left hind limb, exhibiting apparent pain. Oral administration of robenacoxib (Onsior tablets; Elanco Japan, Tokyo, Japan, 3 mg/head, once daily) was initially applied to treat left hindlimb lameness. However, due to poor palatability and difficulty in administration, the medication was changed to meloxicam (Inflacam tablets; Virbac Japan Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan; 0.2 mg/kg oral dose on the first day and 0.1 mg/kg orally once daily) and misoprostol (Cytotec tablets; Pfizer Japan Inc., Tokyo, Japan; 5 μg/kg orally twice daily) on day 51. These oral medications were discontinued on day 64 due to improvement of clinical signs, and the animal kept under close observation. In addition to the medication, the animal was housed in a flat indoor cage (114 × 79 × 48 cm) for movement/exercise restriction, and warm bath therapy using water at about 20°C was applied twice daily by placing a plastic tub (32 × 24 × 12 cm) inside the cage until day 79. Since the animal was kept indoors with the ambient room temperature maintained at approximately 25°C, continuous heating of the water was not performed.

The animal had a history of heart disease (mitral regurgitation, MR) and was continuously administered an angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor (Rizuminarl; Asuka Animal Health Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan; 0.5 mg/head oral dose once daily). Although no clinical signs of MR were observed and the otter exhibited no difficulties in daily life, frequent ventricular premature contractions occurred during a previous sedation with a combination of medetomidine (Dolbene injectable solution; Kyoritsu Seiyaku Corp., Tokyo, Japan; 100 µg/kg) and ketamine (Ketamine Injection 10%; Fujita Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan; 1.3 mg/kg), leading to discontinuation of the procedure.

The case received a comprehensive examination including gait observation, orthopedic assessment, and radiographic examination on day 30. Ultrasonographic examination of the stifle joint, which has been reported as a diagnostic modality for cranial cruciate ligament injury in dogs [15], was not performed in the present case. During gait observation, mild left hindlimb lameness and slight abduction of the left knee were noted. Additionally, intermittent lifting of the left hindlimb was observed after continuous movement. Anesthesia was induced using a chamber filled with sevoflurane (Sevoflurane Inhalation Solution “VTRS”; Viatris Pharmaceuticals G.K., Tokyo, Japan) and maintained with inhalation anesthesia at a concentration of 2.0–3.0% after endotracheal intubation. Under general anesthesia, orthopedic examination revealed reduced muscle mass around the left femur. Pain response, indicated by an increase in heart rate, was consistently elicited during flexion and extension of the left stifle joint, with a positive cranial drawer test. In contrast, no marked abnormalities were detected in the tibial compression test, and no crepitus, clicking, or evidence of meniscal injury was noted. No remarkable findings were detected in the hip joint, femur, patella, tibia, fibula, or distal limb. Radiographic examination of both stifle joints revealed cranial displacement of the tibia under stress in the left stifle (Fig. 1E). In the lateral view of the left stifle joint, mild osteoarthritic changes were also observed around the femoral trochlear groove (Fig. 1D). No abnormal findings were detected in the right stifle joint (Fig. 1A–C) or other joints including forelimbs.

On the left stifle joint, cranial displacement of the tibia under stress conditions was observed (red arrow of E). In the mediolateral view of the left stifle joint, mild osteoarthritis-like changes were detected around the femoral trochlear groove (yellow arrowheads on D).

Based on the acute onset of clinical signs and clinical examinations, a diagnosis of left CCL injury was made. Surgical repair was considered as a treatment option; however, several concerns were raised, including the animal’s small body weight (2.5 kg) and the potential anesthetic risk associated with pre-existing heart disease. Also, stifle instability appeared to improve, and lameness showed gradual amelioration after application of analgesic and anti-inflammatory medications alongside exercise restriction. Considering these factors, conservative management was deemed effective, and surgical intervention was not pursued. The animal continued under conservative management with close monitoring.

Subsequently, the gait and physical activities of the animal showed gradual improvement and maintained a good clinical condition without apparent lameness. On Day 79, the animal was transferred from the indoor cage to the regular exhibition enclosure, and the warm bath therapy was discontinued; follow-up observation was continued thereafter. In addition to routine daily visual observations by the staff, the animal’s behavior, feeding activity, and general health status were closely monitored twice daily during feeding. At reevaluation on day 953, gait assessment revealed no obvious lameness. Under general anesthesia, orthopedic palpation showed a slightly reduced range of motion in the left stifle joint (a decrease of approximately 10–15 degrees in maximum extension compared to the right), but the cranial drawer sign was less pronounced than on day 1. Pain response under anesthesia persisted; however, no other joint abnormalities were detected. Radiographic examination revealed mild periarticular bone proliferation around the trochlear groove and sesamoid region in both stifles (Fig. 2C and 2E), but findings were limited and showed only slow progression compared with day 30. No deformities of the joint space or articular surface were observed, and no apparent asymmetry was noted between the left and right sides (Fig. 2A–F). In stress radiographs of the left stifle joint, cranial displacement of the tibia was mild (Fig. 2F). The clinical course of the case was deemed to be relatively favorable.

In the left stifle joint, cranial displacement of the tibia under stress conditions was mild (F). In both stifle joints, periarticular bone proliferation was observed around the trochlear groove and near the sesamoid bones (yellow arrowheads on C and E), though no distinct difference was noted between the left and right stifles.

With conservative management and without surgical intervention, this case of CCL injury showed sustained progress. The efficacy of this treatment is expected given the animal’s low body weight (2.5 kg), contributing to the favorable outcome of conservative management for CCL injury in an Asian small-clawed otter. Similar conservative treatment is also applied to light-weight animals such as cats in companion animal medicine, where cases of CCL injury have demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes [2, 9]. Correspondingly, otters with greater weight have benefited from surgical intervention; A Cape clawless otter (Aonyx capensis) weighing approximately 10 kg underwent tibial plateau leveling osteotomy (TPLO) and bone anchor fixation, resulting in a favorable outcome [10]. In the present case, as in the previous report, the diagnosis of CCL injury was based on a history of acute lameness, a positive cranial drawer sign, and radiographic findings [12]. Although no surgical intervention was performed, the observed clinical progression suggests that conservative treatment may be viable depending on body weight and clinical condition.

In dogs with CCL injury, marked stifle joint instability is typically observed, with secondary osteoarthritis (OA) or DJD developing over time [3, 4, 16]. However, in the present case, despite the diagnosis of CCL injury and long-term observation, no remarkable bony changes were detected at day 953, approximately 2.6 years after CCL injury. On day 953, the animal was 11 years 7 months old and considered elderly; nevertheless, there was little progression of OA/DJD. The animal’s low body weight is presumed to have contributed to the mild degree of joint changes observed. Moreover, as no distinct asymmetry was observed between the left and right stifles, it is suggested that in Asian small-clawed otters, unlike in dogs, joint instability following CCL injury may not necessarily have a major influence on the progression of OA/DJD. It is possible that factors other than body weight, such as anatomical characteristics, joint instability following injury, and lifestyle, contributed to the lack of progression of CCL injury to OA/DJD in this case. However, anatomical and pathophysiological information in this species remains limited, and further studies are required to clarify these factors.

In the case presented here, a stable clinical course was achieved through conservative management incorporating temporary administration of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs: meloxicam). While the potential benefit of supplements cannot be ruled out, there is currently limited information regarding their efficacy in this species. Further investigation of conservative treatment options for Asian small-clawed otter remains necessary.

In canine and feline veterinary medicine, arthroscopic examination is frequently performed, and its diagnostic accuracy for detecting CCL injuries and meniscal lesions is well documented [5, 7, 11]. Although arthroscopy was not applied in the present case, in future cases involving smooth-coated otters, combining detailed evaluation with arthroscopy or needle arthroscopy may improve diagnosis reliability and support treatment planning.

This study has several limitations, including that it involved only a single case, lacked advanced imaging diagnostics including ultrasonographic assessment of the stifle joint, arthroscopic, and/or magnetic resonance imaging examinations, and that therapeutic options such as multi-drug combination therapy or surgical intervention were limited by the case management in a zoological setting. Future cases, along with the use of arthroscopy and surgical interventions, will contribute to a better understanding of the pathophysiology and aid in establishing optimal CCL injury management strategies for both smooth-coated otters and Asian small-clawed otters.
