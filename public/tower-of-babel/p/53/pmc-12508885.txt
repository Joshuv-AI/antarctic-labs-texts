Cortical tortuosity as a potential clinical biomarker for Alzheimer’s disease: A 3D MRI analysis of brain morphological changes
Authors: Maria-Julieta Mateos, James J. Lah, Deqiang Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by progressive brain morphological changes that are not fully captured by traditional volumetric measures. Cortical tortuosity, a novel measure of cortical folding complexity, provides complementary insights into these changes and has the potential to serve as a biomarker for AD.Objective:To evaluate cortical tortuosity as a biomarker for brain morphological changes in AD, and to establish whether it provides independent information beyond cortical thickness and volume, in relation to disease severity and cognitive performance.Methods:Using data from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), we analyzed 228 AD dementia patients, 620 individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and 506 healthy controls (CN). Cortical tortuosity was computed across multiple brain regions using a 3D slope chain code method. Statistical analyses adjusted for study site, ADNI phase, sex, age, regional brain volume, cortical thickness, and white matter hyperintensities (WMH) were conducted to assess group differences and correlations with cognitive performance.Results:Significant differences in cortical tortuosity values were identified in 37 brain regions among the diagnostic groups. AD patients exhibited lower tortuosity compared to controls, especially in the paracentral (AD: 7.87±0.11, CN: 8.59±0.01, p<4.49×10−10), lingual (AD: 12.42±0.11, CN: 12.86±0.08, p=7.28×10−6), and frontal pole (AD: 7.44±0.09, CN: 7.9±0.07, p<4.77×10−4) regions. Lower tortuosity in the left inferior temporal region was associated with higher Clinical Dementia Rating Scale-Sum of Boxes (CDRSB) and Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive Subscale (ADAS) scores, indicating a link between reduced tortuosity and greater cognitive impairment.Conclusion:Cortical tortuosity provides a complementary measure of brain morphological changes in AD, with potential as a biomarker for disease progression and cognitive decline. These findings highlight the clinical utility of cortical tortuosity in understanding and monitoring AD-related brain changes.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) is a globally prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, presenting substantial clinical and societal challenges. It is marked by progressive cognitive decline, memory loss, and behavioral shifts (DeTure and Dickson, 2019). The pathophysiological underpinnings of AD, including the hallmark amyloid-β plaques and Tau neurofibrillary tangles, have been extensively studied over the years (Braak and Braak, 1991). While cerebrospinal fluid analysis and Positron Emission Tomography (PET) provide a quantitative assessment of amyloid-β and Tau burdens in the brain Frisoni et al. (2010), MRI is non-invasive in nature and sensitive to soft-tissue contrasts, which makes it the preferred tool for tracking structural and functional changes associated with AD progression (Braskie and Thompson, 2014). The most analyzed morphological features to study and quantify the effects of AD on brain surface are cortical thickness and regional brain volume (Braskie and Thompson, 2014). Cortical thickness, determined through MRI scans, refers to the distance between the outer surface of the brain and the boundary between gray and white matter. Studies have consistently revealed that as the disease stage of AD advances, the cortical thickness and regional brain volume diminish, especially in crucial regions such as the bilateral parietal and temporal lobes, including the precuneus (Du et al., 2006, Fischl and Dale, 2000, Das et al., 2007, Du et al., 2007). Recent studies using machine learning techniques have also successfully studied AD using structural MRIs (Fan et al., 2008, Vemuri et al., 2008, Schwarz et al., 2016). The presence of amyloid-β pathology, detected through PET imaging, is now considered a key diagnostic criterion for AD and a crucial factor in understanding its progression (Chapleau et al., 2022).

While regional brain volumetry has provided insights, it might not fully capture the complex morphological changes in AD. Clinically, there is a need for more sensitive biomarkers that can detect early-stage changes and track disease progression. Cortical tortuosity, a measure of the complexity of cortical folding patterns, holds potential as a more sensitive biomarker for AD-related brain morphological changes. Bribiesca (Bribiesca, 2013) introduced a 2D method for quantifying tortuosity based on chain coding, providing a foundation for exploring this measure in the context of neurological disorders.

The 3D tortuosity method proposed by Mateos et al. (2024) was chosen as the tool for analysis in this study. This method extends the concept of tortuosity into three dimensions, providing a more comprehensive and robust way to quantify the intricate folding patterns of the cortex. By incorporating three-dimensional information, this method captures a wider range of morphological features that might be missed by traditional 2D approaches.

Previous research has demonstrated the utility of tortuosity as a marker of brain changes in AD. Barbará-Morales et al. (2020) examined alterations in tortuosity measures in AD using ADNI database. Their study introduced a tortuosity descriptor described as “3D”, but their method relied on angular deviations along skeletonized meshes, effectively reducing cortical surfaces to a lower-dimensional representation. Subsequently, Mateos et al. (2020) proposed a three-dimensional adaptation of the slope chain code to cortical contours extracted in three orthogonal orientations (axial, coronal, sagittal), to compute tortuosity and demonstrated that this approach could distinguish between AD dementia patients and controls based on morphological differences in the central sulcus.

This study aimed to investigate cortical tortuosity, quantified using a 3D slope chain code approach, as a clinically relevant biomarker for AD. Our goal was to use cortical tortuosity as a novel morphological descriptor to characterize population-level differences across diagnostic groups and cognitive performance, and to determine whether it provides information that is independent from, and complementary to, cortical thickness and regional volume. Specifically, we sought to:

•Examine the relationship between regional cortical tortuosity and disease stage (Cognitively Normal, Mild Cognitive Impairment, and AD dementia) in a large cohort of ADNI participants.•Assess the correlation between regional tortuosity values and regional brain volume to evaluate whether tortuosity provides additional information beyond volumetric measures.•Investigate the association between cortical tortuosity and neuropsychological test performance to explore its potential as a biomarker for cognitive decline.

Materials and method

We analyzed the brain’s morphological features by measuring the three-dimensional tortuosity in various cortical regions as defined by the Desikan–Killiany atlas (Alexander et al., 2019) (Fig. 1). This atlas was selected for cortical parcellation due to its extensive validation and widespread use in ADNI-based neuroimaging studies. Its standardized parcellation scheme ensures compatibility with prior research, facilitating comparisons across datasets and enhancing the reproducibility of findings. We used data from the ADNI project, which encompasses patients from all four distinct stages of the project (ADNI-1, ADNI-GO, ADNI-2, ADNI-3), accessible at http://adni.loni.usc.edu/. This database comprises a cohort aged 55 to 90 years, including cognitively normal (CN) individuals, those diagnosed with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), and those with AD dementia. We selected MRI scans that were performed within six months of any neuropsychological test. Scans that failed segmentation were also excluded. A final dataset including scans from 1,354 participants, was included in the analysis. Table 1 presents the distribution of participants across different diagnostic groups (CN, MCI, AD), along with their mean age, years of education, MMSE score, and sex.

The analyzed dataset includes participants from four distinct phases of the ADNI project: ADNI-1, ADNI-GO, ADNI-2, and ADNI-3. While acknowledging the potential for heterogeneity across these phases due to variations in imaging protocols, participant recruitment, and data collection procedures, we have carefully considered these factors in our analyses. ADNI has implemented rigorous quality control procedures and standardized image processing pipelines to minimize variability across phases (Jack, Jr. et al., 2008, Wyman et al., 2013). However, we found that ADNI phase was significantly associated with tortuosity in our analyses, even after controlling for “study site”. This results suggests that additional factors related to the ADNI phase may contribute to variations in tortuosity. We, therefore, chose to control for phase as a covariate in all of our statistical models to account for these potential confounding effects.

T1-weighted image acquisition and post-processing

T1-weighted images were utilized to characterize the morphological characteristics of the brain’s surface. These images were collected across a variety of scanners with protocols individualized for each scanner, the specific acquisition parameters are available in the ADNI’s official documentation, which can be accessed at https://adni.loni.usc.edu/methods/documents/. The ADNI pre-processing protocol provides T1w image volumes that have been intensity normalized, gradient un-warped, and subjected to an on-scanner non-uniformity correction. For the post-processing of the images, we employed the FreeSurfer recon-all pipeline (Dale et al., 1999, Fischl et al., 2004).

3D tortuosity

We used a three-dimensional adaptation of the slope chain code method to compute tortuosity. This method was recently introduced by Mateos et al. (2020) (see Eq. (1)). Briefly, a chain code is a technique employed to represent the boundary or contour of an object in a binary image. It accomplishes this by encoding the object’s shape through a sequence of directional steps taken along its perimeter, with each step defined as a chain code element (Bankman et al., 2000, Bribiesca and Bribiesca-Contreras, 2014). In our context, xni, yni, and zni are the chain code elements, where “n” corresponds to the index of a specific segment, and “i” denotes the slice within the 3D structure under analysis. The three different directions represented by x, y, and z, respectively. Additionally, SX, SY, and SZ indicate the number of slices in each direction (Mateos et al., 2020). The results suggest that the tortuosity estimation for the central sulcus could be capturing brain structural changes related to AD dementia diagnosis. More specifically, the calculation of τ3D comprises the following steps:

1.Contour Extraction: The contour represents the boundary or outline of the structure within a specific slice. The contour was extracted from every individual slice that constitutes the 3D image data.2.Straight-line Segment Definition: based on the defined contours, we defined straight-line segments as ‘markers’ or ‘guidelines’ that trace the structure’s shape.3.Slope Variation Calculation: Evaluate the changes or variations in slope between each consecutive segment. This step is crucial as these variations provide insights into the contour’s twists and turns, the key component for the tortuosity value.4.Aggregation of Variations: All the slope variations identified in the previous step are collectively summed up. This aggregate number corresponds to the value of that particular structure.

This entire process is carried out independently in three directions (X,Y,Z) for every cortical region, see Eq. (1). By doing so, we ensure that aspects of the cortical region’s folding patterns are captured. A previous study showed that this approach to estimate the tortuosity minimizes the discretization artifacts due to voxelization (Mateos et al., 2020). This method allows the quantification of the complexity of twists and turns present in different voxelized three-dimensional objects.

This quantification of tortuosity was performed for each brain region defined in the Desikan–Killiany atlas.

Statistical analysis

We first evaluated the correlation coefficient of the τ3D between the two hemispheres for each region to determine whether the expected symmetry between the two hemispheres exists. To assess the relationship between regional tortuosity (τ) and other morphological measurements the regional brain volume and regional cortical thickness, we conducted correlation analyses, including all participants in the CN, MCI, and AD dementia groups for each measurement. We also performed a general linear model (GLM) analysis to determine whether tortuosity values differ between different stages of diagnosis (CN, MCI, and AD dementia) after accounting for the effects of regional brain volume, regional cortical thickness, white matter hyperintensities (WMH) age, sex, ADNI phase, and study site. This analysis aimed to determine whether tortuosity measures provide additional information in differentiating diagnostic stages, beyond regional brain volume and regional cortical thickness.

To control for type I errors arising from multiple comparisons, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure for false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001). This procedure controls the expected proportion of false positives among all rejected null hypotheses. For group comparisons across brain regions, we performed separate ANOVAs for each region, followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons, with regional brain volume, regional cortical thickness, age, sex, ADNI phase, and study site included as covariates. We then applied FDR correction to the p-values obtained from these pairwise comparisons.

Similarly, for the correlations between regional tortuosity values and neuropsychological scores, we calculated correlation coefficients for each region and each cognitive test. FDR correction was applied to the p-values of these correlations, controlling for the multiple comparisons across regions and cognitive tests. For both analyses, we set the desired FDR level to 0.05.

Scanner variability and ADNI phase, which are recognized confounders in multi-center neuroimaging studies, were explicitly controlled for in all analyses. By including these variables as covariates, we aimed to mitigate their potential impact on observed associations, ensuring the robustness and interpretability of our findings.

To study the relationship between brain cortical tortuosity and cognitive functions, we also used GLM with the different neuropsychological tests scores as dependent variables. Tests included Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Everyday Cognition (Ecog), Clinical Dementia Rating Scale-Sum of Boxes (CDRSB), Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) immediate recall and forgetting, Trail Making Test B-A difference score (TRABSCOR), Logical Memory - Delayed Recall (ldel total), Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ), modified Preclinical Alzheimer’s Cognitive Composite with digit substitution (mPACCdigit) and with Trails B (mPACCtrails) and the Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive Subscale (ADAS). Tortuosity measure served as our key predictor, while regional brain volume, regional cortical thickness, WMH, age, sex, ADNI phase and study site were included as covariates. One model was performed for each anatomically defined cortical region, and FDR correction was performed.

To further explore the potential mediating role of regional brain volume in the relationship between tortuosity and cognitive performance, we conducted a comprehensive mediation analysis for each combination of the 14 significant neuropsychological tests (identified from a previous analysis) and the 68 brain regions (Wager et al., 2008, Wager et al., 2009). This analysis was performed using the mediation function from the M3 Mediation Toolbox (Wager et al., 0000). Specifically, we examined both the direct effect of tortuosity on cognitive test scores (path c’) and the indirect effect (path ab) mediated by regional brain volume (see Fig. 2). Significance was evaluated using thresholds for p-values, and confidence intervals were assessed to determine if they included zero. For each brain region, p-values across the neuropsychological tests were combined using Fisher’s method (Fisher, 1970). This method combines independent p-values from separate tests to provide a more comprehensive significance measure, offering insight into the overall effect. Additionally, we visualized the results through a heatmap, which illustrated regions where both direct and indirect effects were significant. This allowed us to identify key brain regions where tortuosity influences cognition, either directly or through its effect on brain volume. For each test and region, we estimated five path coefficients.

Finally, we explored the potential impact of amyloid pathology on cortical tortuosity and conducted an ANOVA analysis with tortuosity as the dependent variable and amyloid presence as the independent variable. Amyloid positivity was determined using a t−ταβ ratio cutoff of 0.26, derived from CSF biomarkers. This ratio has been shown to have a strong association with amyloid PET positivity, serving as a reliable proxy for amyloid-PET burden in the brain (van Harten et al., 2022). Age, sex, regional brain volume, regional cortical thickness, WMH, ADNI phase, and site were included as covariates in the model. Regional volume & regional cortical thickness vs τ3D

We evaluated the correlation between regional brain volume and tortuosity across all participant groups (CN, MCI, and AD Dementia), which is essential to assess the redundancy between these metrics (Table S1). The correlation coefficients range from about 0.5 to about 0.1, which translates into 25% to 1% of the variance explained. These suggest that regional tortuosity and regional brain volume are moderately correlated, and regional tortuosity captures morphological variations of the brain that are not captured by regional brain volume.

Similarly, we assessed the correlation between regional cortical thickness and tortuosity across all participant groups. We found that the correlations were weak (R-values close to 0), but several regions showed statistically significant correlations (p<0.05) Table S1. This indicates that, while the overall correlation between cortical thickness and tortuosity is weak, there may be region-specific variations in the relationship between these measures.

These findings underscore the complementary role of tortuosity as a biomarker in neuroimaging studies. Whereas cortical thickness primarily reflects macroscopic atrophy associated with neurodegeneration, tortuosity captures the intricacies of cortical folding, quantifying subtle morphological variations that are not accounted for by thickness alone. The observed region-specific correlations between cortical thickness and tortuosity highlight their distinct contributions, suggesting that tortuosity provides additional insights into brain structural alterations and their relationship to the pathological processes underlying Alzheimer’s disease.

GLM analyses were performed to investigate whether cortical tortuosity was statistically different between diagnoses after accounting for the effects of regional brain volume, regional cortical thickness, WMH, age, sex, study site, and ADNI phase. These analyses revealed significant differences in tortuosity values among different diagnostic groups. The brain regions showing significant differences are illustrated in Fig. 3. All regions showed higher τ3D values for control subjects and lower values for AD dementia patients. Also, we can contrast the results between hemispheres and notice five regions that appeared in one of the hemispheres. After the FDR correction, 9 of the 68 cortical regions showed statistically significant differences in τ3D values among the AD dementia patients, MCI and CN. These regions are located in the frontal, temporal, and occipital lobes, as shown in Fig. 4.

Neuropsychological test scores correlation with regional τ3D

Using the GLM, we identified significant relationships between cortical tortuosity values of certain regions and different neuropsychological test scores after accounting for the effects of regional brain volume, regional cortical thickness, WMH, age, sex, age, ADNI phase, and study site and doing the FDR correction. Some brain regions revealed a significant association of tortuosity with the neuropsychological test scores.

The regression coefficients and the p-values for all the neuropsychological tests and brain regions can be found in Table S3, and Table S4. We found both negative and positive correlations between cortical tortuosity and neuropsychological test scores. Negative correlations observed in tests like the CDRSB, ADAS, and TRABSCOR suggest that decreased tortuosity in certain regions correlates with greater cognitive impairment, as indicated by higher scores on these tests. Lower tortuosity in the left inferior temporal gyrus was associated with higher scores on both the CDRSB and ADAS. Similarly, decreased tortuosity in the left isthmus cingulate and the bilateral paracentral regions was linked to higher ADAS and TRABSCOR scores, respectively.

Discussion

We applied a novel measure of cortical morphology, 3D tortuosity (τ3D), using a slope chain code-based method, to study Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Across diagnostic groups, τ3D was significantly reduced, with cognitively normal (CN) subjects exhibiting higher values than AD dementia patients and MCI patients showing intermediate values. These findings demonstrate that τ3D is sensitive to disease stage and may serve as a clinically relevant biomarker.

Our approach differs from prior definitions of tortuosity, most notably the descriptor presented by Barbará-Morales et al. (2020), which was described as “3D” but in practice computed from angular deviations along skeleton trajectories derived from cortical meshes, reducing cortical geometry to simplified paths. In contrast, our method applies the slope chain code to cortical boundaries evaluated in three orthogonal orientations, retaining folding information across directions. More broadly, most prior tortuosity measures have been defined in two dimensions, such as vascular tortuosity (Bullitt et al., 2003), curvature descriptors in retinal vessels (Turior et al., 2013), or parameterization-based vessel tortuosity metrics (Johnson and Dougherty, 2007). While effective in their domains, these 2D descriptors are not tailored to cortical folding complexity. Building upon the slope chain code framework introduced in Mateos et al. (2024), the present study represents the first large-scale application of this method to cortical folding in AD.

Although our analyses revealed an overall reduction of tortuosity along the AD continuum, the effects were regionally heterogeneous. Fig. 3, Fig. 4, together with Table S2, show that the bilateral paracentral gyri, left isthmus cingulate, left lingual gyrus, and right frontal pole exhibited the strongest reductions, whereas the postcentral, pericalcarine, and lateral occipital regions showed more moderate effects. In contrast, the transverse temporal cortex displayed minimal change. Importantly, no region showed increased tortuosity in AD compared to CN. Several effects were hemisphere-specific: the left paracentral, lingual, isthmus cingulate, and lateral occipital were significant only on the left, while the frontal pole and pericalcarine were significant only on the right. These findings highlight widespread but asymmetric alterations in cortical folding, consistent with posterior parietal and cingulate atrophy in AD (Rabinovici et al., 2007), early lingual involvement (Chiari-Correia et al., 2023), and progressive frontal and cingulate decline (Scahill et al., 2002).

Associations with cognition further emphasize the clinical relevance of τ3D. Decreased tortuosity was significantly associated with worse performance across multiple neuropsychological tests, particularly Logical Memory Delayed Recall and MMSE, and also with executive and functional measures including CDRSB, MoCA, mPACCdigit, mPACCtrailsB, TRABSCOR, and FAQ (Fig. 5). Mediation analyses (Fig. 6) showed both direct effects of tortuosity on cognition and indirect effects mediated by volume, especially in frontal and temporal regions. These results suggest that tortuosity influences cognition independently of, and in part through, atrophy.

White matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume did not significantly affect group or regression outcomes when included as a covariate, suggesting that differences in cortical tortuosity are independent of WMH burden. Likewise, no significant differences in τ3D were observed between amyloid-positive and amyloid-negative individuals after covariate adjustment, implying that tortuosity may capture neurodegeneration downstream of amyloid rather than amyloid deposition itself.

The biological interpretation of reduced tortuosity likely reflects a loss of cortical folding complexity driven by intracortical disorganization, synaptic loss, and degeneration of underlying white matter architecture. This is consistent with prior evidence: de Moraes et al. (2024) showed that cortical folding indices correlate with cognition and CSF markers independently of thickness, Ruiz de Miras et al. (2017) reported reduced cortical fractal dimension in AD converters, Bachmann et al. (2023) found surface complexity tracked decline better than thickness, and Liu et al. (2012) showed reduced gyrification and widened sulci in early AD. Together, these studies support tortuosity as a complementary morphometric marker beyond volume and thickness.

Finally, an important question is whether tortuosity is specific to AD. Aging itself has been linked to reduced cortical complexity (Liu et al., 2012, Ruiz de Miras et al., 2017), and our study design cannot establish disease specificity. Comparative analyses in non-AD dementias such as frontotemporal and vascular dementia, and in healthy aging cohorts, will be essential to determine whether tortuosity reflects AD-specific pathology or a more general cortical vulnerability.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, demographic differences in age, sex, and education across diagnostic groups in ADNI represent potential confounders, even though all analyses adjusted for these variables. In addition, ADNI participants are relatively homogeneous and highly educated compared to the general population of individuals with AD, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to more diverse clinical settings. Second, tortuosity depends on cortical parcellation: here we used the Desikan–Killiany atlas, but other atlases with finer or coarser parcellations may yield different results. Third, tortuosity measures are sensitive to segmentation quality; inaccuracies in FreeSurfer outputs could bias results, particularly in regions with complex anatomy. Fourth, although the validity and robustness of the 3D tortuosity descriptor have been established in our earlier methodological work (Mateos et al., 2024), we did not evaluate test–retest or cross-scanner reproducibility in the present dataset. Additional reliability studies across acquisition platforms are therefore warranted. Fifth, while we considered amyloid status, we did not evaluate tau or other biomarkers, limiting mechanistic interpretation. Finally, we did not directly compare AD with other dementias or with normal aging, leaving the disease specificity of tortuosity unresolved.

Future work

Future studies should assess the test–retest and cross-scanner reliability of τ3D to establish robustness, and extend analyses to more demographically and clinically diverse cohorts to evaluate generalizability beyond the ADNI framework. Comparative studies across multiple sites and acquisition protocols, as well as in non-AD dementias (e.g., frontotemporal dementia, vascular dementia) and age-matched healthy controls, will be critical to determine whether cortical tortuosity is specific to AD pathology or reflects broader processes of aging and neurodegeneration. Exploring alternative cortical parcellations and more advanced segmentation methods could improve precision, and extending tortuosity analyses to subcortical structures or to MCI subtypes (amnestic vs. language-predominant) may further clarify disease trajectories. Integration with tau PET, multimodal MRI, and functional measures will help elucidate underlying mechanisms. Although prediction and classification are beyond the scope of the present work, future studies could test whether incorporating τ3D alongside conventional measures improves diagnostic or prognostic performance. The present study establishes the foundation by demonstrating its independent contribution to the characterization of disease stage and cognitive impairment.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that cortical tortuosity is significantly reduced along the AD continuum, correlates with cognitive performance, and exerts both direct and volume-mediated effects on cognition. By establishing that τ3D provides information that is complementary to, and independent from, cortical thickness and volume, our results show that tortuosity can be used to characterize population-level morphological differences and their cognitive impact in AD. These findings highlight a dual aspect of AD pathology: widespread symmetry in cortical tortuosity alongside region-specific asymmetries that affect cognition. Overall, τ3D emerges as a promising complementary biomarker of cortical morphology in AD, with potential applications in early detection, disease monitoring, and future clinical translation.
