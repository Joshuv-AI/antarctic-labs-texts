Defining physician-AI collaboration in pulmonary and critical care medicine: concepts and illustrative examples for clinical reasoning
Authors: Atefeh Vaezi, Tracy Ashby, Sajid Kadir, Michael Schweitzer, Anas Hadeh, Parijat Sen, Vandana Seeram, Mehdi Mirsaeidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe exponential growth of medical data and complexity in Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine (PCCM) necessitates a paradigm shift in clinical reasoning and education. This paper proposes a structured framework for collaborative decision-making between physicians and artificial intelligence (AI) that emphasizes the integration of critical thinking with AI-enhanced capabilities. Critical thinking is defined as the systematic and objective analysis of information accompanied by logical reasoning and evidence‑based judgment. Traditional medical education often neglects explicit instruction in these cognitive skills, leaving clinicians vulnerable to diagnostic error and cognitive biases. Meanwhile, AI excels at data aggregation, pattern recognition, and predictive analytics, offering complementary capabilities that can support evidence-based decision-making. By distinguishing two modes of interaction, AI‑assisted tools that provide recommendations and AI‑enhanced systems that simulate complex scenarios, we propose a conceptual model in which AI reinforces rather than replaces physician judgment. The article outlines how critical‑thinking skills map onto phases of diagnostic and management reasoning and illustrates the roles of physicians and AI. We discuss applications of AI in diagnosis, radiology, pulmonary function interpretation, and decision support, and outline ethical considerations including algorithmic bias, data privacy, and HIPAA compliance. This framework reimagines the physician–AI relationship as a cognitive partnership essential for the future of PCCM.

Introduction

Contemporary physicians face an unprecedented volume of clinical information. Medical knowledge is doubling at a rate measured in months rather than years, with one study projecting a doubling time of approximately 73 days by 2020.1 Given this information explosion and the complexity of pulmonary and critical care medicine, clinicians cannot rely on memorization alone. Physicians must synthesize data, evaluate its reliability, and apply it to individual patients. Critical thinking, systematic and objective analysis of information accompanied by logical reasoning and evidence‑based judgment, provides a cognitive framework for managing this complexity. This skill encompasses interconnected processes such as analysis, inference, evaluation, interpretation, explanation, and self‑regulation.2,3

Although some clinicians develop critical thinking through experience, relying solely on informal learning leads to variability and leaves practitioners vulnerable to cognitive biases.4 Bridging experiential and formal learning requires intentional educational design. Therefore, medical education must intentionally cultivate critical‑thinking skills through structured instruction. Evidence‑based strategies for promoting critical thinking include problem‑based learning, collaborative learning, reflective writing, and concept mapping. For example, studies show that concept mapping promotes critical‑thinking skills compared with traditional methods,5 and problem‑based learning enhances critical thinking and problem‑solving across biomedical fields.6

Artificial intelligence has rapidly entered health care, excelling at data aggregation, pattern recognition, and predictive analytics. In PCCM, AI algorithms can analyze imaging, laboratory data, and electronic health records to assist in diagnosis and treatment planning. However, AI tools are not replacements for clinical judgment. Instead, they serve as cognitive extenders that augment the physician’s ability to process complex information. Recent evidence highlights the risk that AI systems trained on biased or incomplete data may propagate disparities, underscoring the need for human oversight.7

Despite growing interest in artificial intelligence (AI), no model currently integrates AI into the pedagogy of critical thinking in medicine.8 A clear framework for leveraging AI tools, including large language models (LLMs), could help educators design curricula that foster and adapt clinical reasoning skills. We propose a model that distinguishes between AI-assisted and AI-enhanced interactions: AI-assisted tools provide recommendations that physicians interpret and contextualize, while AI-enhanced systems simulate complex clinical scenarios and offer adaptive training environments that enable learners to practice judgment in high-stakes settings. This conceptual framework may guide the development of future curricula in pulmonary and critical care medicine and beyond.

Critical thinking in clinical practice

Clinical decision‑making can be conceptualized as drawing on two cognitive systems: a fast, associative system informed by experience and pattern recognition, and a slower, rule‑based system that relies on analytical reasoning.9,10 Critical thinking aligns with the rule‑based system and requires open‑mindedness, tolerance for ambiguity, and willingness to consider alternative hypotheses.6,11 By the time physicians begin residency, they must be adept at recognizing distractions and cognitive biases, questioning assumptions, evaluating evidence, and managing their thought processes.12

Critical thinking is a teachable skill. Educational interventions such as problem‑based learning, concept mapping, reflective writing, and collaborative discussion have been shown to improve critical‑thinking skills in medical and nursing students.5,13,14 Despite these strategies, diagnostic errors remain common. Beyond knowledge deficits, cognitive biases, including premature closure, anchoring, confirmation bias, and availability bias, contribute substantially to diagnostic errors. Critical thinking functions as a metacognitive skill that helps clinicians recognize their own biases, generate broader differential diagnoses, and evaluate assumptions.

AI can support critical thinking by quickly retrieving evidence and highlighting patterns, but it also carries the risks of reinforcing biases present in training data. Models that draw on incomplete or unrepresentative datasets may misclassify symptoms or underestimate disease prevalence in minority populations.15,16 Therefore, clinicians must strengthen their critical thinking skills to effectively evaluate AI outputs and reduce the risk of perpetuating bias, at least until more advanced generations of AI emerge.

Decision-making: Artificial intelligence vs. Human

Human decision‑making draws on cognition, emotion, experience, and social context, allowing for moral reasoning and empathy.17 However, human judgment is subject to fatigue, inconsistency, and cognitive bias.18 Conversely, AI systems excel at processing large datasets with speed and consistency, generating evidence‑based recommendations and predictive analyses. Yet AI lacks consciousness and moral agency, and its outputs reflect the quality of the input data and the design of its algorithms.19

In the early days of AI, rule‑based systems predominated; these models were interpretable but limited in handling uncertainty. Probabilistic models expanded capabilities but required accurate prior probabilities and were computationally demanding.20 Modern AI leverages machine learning, including supervised learning, reinforcement learning, and generative models such as large language models. These systems can learn from data, adapt to new information, and generate novel output, but they can also be unpredictable.21 Our framework distinguishes between deterministic models (eg, decision trees, logistic regression) and generative models (LLMs). While the latter can provide rich educational experiences, they can also hallucinate false statements or amplify biases, requiring critical evaluation by humans.

In the context of PCCM, AI tools can assist with image interpretation, pulmonary function analysis, and risk stratification. However, clinicians must remain actively engaged, verifying AI recommendations against clinical knowledge and patient context.

A framework for physician–AI collaboration

We propose a framework that differentiates two forms of physician–AI interaction:

AI‑assisted interaction: AI provides recommendations or synthesizes data, and physicians interpret and contextualize these outputs. Users must engage critical thinking to evaluate the reliability of the data, recognize potential biases, and integrate AI insights with patient values and clinical experience.

AI‑enhanced interaction: AI systems perform defined tasks, such as simulating clinical scenarios or executing structured reasoning pathways. Physicians oversee the process, validate the AI’s decisions, and use the simulation to practice complex decision‑making. AI‑enhanced systems can serve as high‑fidelity educational tools that allow learners to experience rare or high‑stakes situations in a safe environment.

Integration of AI into pulmonary and critical care medicine

Pulmonary and critical care medicine encompasses airway diseases, pulmonary vascular diseases, interstitial lung diseases, lung transplantation, lung cancer, sleep medicine, and multiple intensive care contexts. AI tools can help trainees and practitioners synthesize specialized data, improve diagnostic accuracy, and support personalized management strategies.26 For instance, machine‑learning algorithms outperform pulmonologists in interpreting pulmonary function tests and radiographs.22 Natural language processing algorithms can extract relevant clinical features from electronic health records to suggest diagnostic possibilities that may be overlooked by trainees. Deep‑learning tools detect subtle lesions on imaging, facilitating early diagnosis. Decision‑support systems use probabilistic reasoning to calculate the likelihood of diagnoses based on real‑time patient data and provide risk–benefit analyses for therapies. However, AI integration must be approached cautiously. Bias in training data can exacerbate inequities.29 Transparent reporting of algorithm performance across diverse populations is essential. Implementing AI‑based scribes or decision‑support tools also raises questions about patient privacy and HIPAA compliance. These tools should be treated as third‑party service providers, with rigorous vetting, business associate agreements, and patient consent.30 Institutions must educate clinicians about appropriate use, de‑identification, and data governance.

When used responsibly, AI can serve as a “cognitive extender” that reduces information overload, mitigates cognitive biases, and enables clinicians to focus on complex decision‑making and empathy in patient care. Our framework provides a roadmap for incorporating AI into PCCM education by distinguishing between AI‑assisted tasks that require critical appraisal and AI‑enhanced simulations that offer immersive experiential learning.

Limitations and ethical considerations

Integrating AI into clinical reasoning carries significant limitations and ethical considerations. Algorithmic bias arising from unrepresentative training data may lead to misdiagnosis and unequal care.31–33 Underrepresented populations must be included in datasets, and open‑science practices such as participatory data collection, inclusive data standards, and code sharing can help mitigate bias. Additionally, AI systems often act as black boxes, making it challenging to interpret their reasoning. Clinicians should prioritize interpretable models when possible and maintain oversight over AI outputs, at least for now.

Patient privacy is another major concern. AI scribes and decision‑support tools capture sensitive health information and therefore fall under HIPAA regulations. Providers must secure business associate agreements with vendors and obtain patient consent when recording clinical encounters.34

Educational prompts should avoid including protected health information; instead, educators should use hypothetical or de‑identified scenarios when training learners. Dependence on AI could erode clinician autonomy and the therapeutic alliance if users blindly accept AI recommendations. Therefore, AI outputs should always be considered advisory, not authoritative. Educators must teach trainees to maintain situational awareness, to critically appraise AI recommendations and to integrate evidence with patient values and preferences. Lastly, legal liability remains poorly defined: mismanagement of AI recommendations, or reliance on flawed algorithms may expose clinicians and institutions to legal risk. Clear regulatory guidance and institutional policies are necessary to navigate these challenges.

Conclusion

The rapid expansion of medical knowledge and the complexity of pulmonary and critical care medicine require new approaches to clinical reasoning and education. Critical thinking, characterized by systematic analysis and evidence‑based judgment, is a learned skill that must be deliberately cultivated. Artificial intelligence, when ethically designed and responsibly implemented, can augment clinicians’ cognitive capabilities by synthesizing data, mitigating cognitive biases, and simulating clinical scenarios. Our proposed framework differentiates between AI‑assisted tools that support decision‑making and AI‑enhanced systems that provide immersive learning experiences. By mapping critical‑thinking skills onto phases of diagnostic and management reasoning and delineating physician and AI roles, we offer a practical model for integrating AI into PCCM education. Future research should test this framework in educational interventions, evaluate AI tools across diverse patient populations, and develop guidelines that ensure fairness, privacy, and human oversight. Ultimately, a collaborative partnership between physicians and AI has the potential to enhance clinical reasoning and improve patient outcomes in the evolving landscape of PCCM. Future work should focus on translating our conceptual model into educational practice. Specifically, curricular design initiatives could explore how AI-assisted and AI-enhanced approaches can be systematically incorporated into simulation-based training and bedside teaching. Additionally, empirical studies are needed to assess how these interactions influence learners’ critical thinking, diagnostic accuracy, and decision-making over time. Such evidence will be essential to refine the model and guide sustainable integration of AI into pulmonary and critical care education.
