Transcription initiation drives dosage compensation establishment and maintenance in male Drosophila
Authors: Ronald Oellers, Anastasios Alexiadis, Yidan Sun, Mohit Navandar, Tanvi Kulkarni, Herbert Holz, Plamen Georgiev, Leily Rabbani, Maria Shvedunova, Niyazi Umut Erdogdu, Patrick Cramer, Michael Lidschreiber, Thomas Manke, Asifa Akhtar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dosage compensation (DC) in Drosophila equalizes X chromosome–linked gene expression via the male-specific lethal complex, which acetylates histone 4 lysine-16 on the male X chromosome. How DC is established during early embryogenesis remains obscure, largely due to the difficulty of obtaining sufficient sex-specific material at early embryonic stages. Here, we developed a high-throughput embryo sorting strategy and combined newly synthesized RNA-sequencing and RNA polymerase II (Pol II) profiling to investigate the mechanisms underlying the onset of DC in vivo. We found that changes in transcription initiation occur before detectable differences in transcriptional pausing or elongation and represent the primary determinant of enhanced mRNA output during DC establishment. It had hitherto been unclear whether DC establishment and maintenance exerted similar or distinct effects on transcriptional kinetics. Extending genome-wide profiling to S2 cells, we uncovered Pol II initiation as a general principle underlying both inception and perpetuation of DC-driven transcriptional up-regulation.

INTRODUCTION

Transcription mediated by RNA polymerase II (Pol II) constitutes an essential cellular process underlying cellular identity, differentiation, and responses to environmental changes. In metazoans, Pol II transcription is initiated by the assembly of Pol II with general transcription factors into the preinitiation complex at the promoters (1). Upon initial RNA synthesis and escape from the promoters, Pol II frequently undergoes promoter-proximal pausing, 30 to 60 nucleotides (nt) downstream of the transcription start site (TSS) (2). At this stage, paused Pol II is stabilized by the association of negative elongation factor and DRB-sensitivity inducing factor (3–5). The release of paused Pol II into productive elongation requires the kinase activity of the positive transcription elongation factor b (6–8). Pol II transcription is tightly regulated at the level of transcription initiation, promoter-proximal pausing, and early elongation (9, 10).

Epigenetic mechanisms are tightly coupled with transcriptional control at the chromatin level (11–14). In particular, histone tail acetylations such as H4K16ac have a profound influence on transcription (15–18). However, how distinct steps in the Pol II transcription cycle are regulated by epigenetic machineries is an area of active research. Dosage compensation (DC) in Drosophila melanogaster is a fundamental model for epigenetic regulation, in which the single male X chromosome is bound by the male-specific lethal (MSL) complex to achieve chromosome-wide up-regulation of transcription to equalize the output of the single male X with that of the two X chromosomes found in females (19–21). The precise transcriptional enhancement exerted by the MSL complex (approximately twofold increase in production of X-linked transcripts) provides the ideal paradigm to investigate the influence of chromatin-based epigenetic changes on Pol II kinetics in mechanistic detail.

In flies, maternally supplied proteins and transcripts are crucial for early development and are replaced by embryonic proteins and transcripts after zygotic genome activation (ZGA) (22, 23). Most histone acetylation and methylation marks only rise in prominence after ZGA (22, 23); however, H3K27me3, H3K9me3, and H4K16ac form prominent exceptions to this (24–26). H3K27me3 is inherited from the maternal germline and is crucial in limiting the expression of developmental genes (25, 27). Similarly, H4K16ac is present before ZGA and is deposited maternally, where its maintenance into the offspring is essential for male viability. Maternal depletion of the lysine acetyltransferase MOF and consequent loss of H4K16ac is lethal for male offspring and cannot be rescued by subsequent zygotic expression of MOF (26). This underscores the unique and early established roles of maternally inherited epigenetic marks in shaping the chromatin landscape of the developing genome. In particular, H4K16ac, deposited in the oocyte and maintained after fertilization, promotes chromatin accessibility and primes a subset of genes for future activation, including X-linked loci, leading to DC in male embryos (26, 28, 29).

While dissection of the mechanisms of transcriptional regulation in Drosophila DC has received considerable attention (28, 30–40), precisely how the MSL complex modulates transcription of X-linked genes remains unresolved. Current models debate two nonmutually exclusive mechanisms: One posits enhanced transcriptional elongation mediated by increased RNA Pol II processivity, supported by elevated Pol II occupancy and broad H4K16ac enrichment across gene bodies on the male X chromosome (26, 28, 30, 31, 33, 34, 41, 42). In contrast, alternative evidence favors a recruitment-based mechanism, as reflected by increased Pol II occupancy at promoters of male X-linked genes relative to autosomal genes (33, 42). Moreover, synergy and genetic interaction between MSL1 and CDK7 implicate yet more complexity at the level of transcription initiation (43, 44).

The data generated to date trying to discriminate between these models suffer from variability across different systems as well as technical and resolution limitations and do not consider the developmental stage. In particular, it is now unclear whether MSL complex–based regulatory mechanisms differ during the establishment versus maintenance of DC. Critical questions remain about the timing, chromatin context, and molecular processes by which the MSL complex directs transcriptional up-regulation of X-linked genes. Addressing these gaps is essential for a comprehensive mechanistic understanding of DC in Drosophila.

In our earlier work, we performed time-resolved sex-separated transcriptomic profiling and identified stage 15 embryos as a critical window for the establishment of DC, characterized by pronounced sex-specific effects following maternal depletion of males absent on the first (MOF). Therefore, in this study, we also leverage stage 15 embryos, which provide not only fully competent zygotic transcription but also a well-established chromatin landscape (26, 45). This system enables the characterization of DC during its establishment, before the onset of male lethality in larvae (46). In parallel, we use S2 cells as a model of DC maintenance, reflecting a steady-state cellular context in which DC is already fully established. By combining high-resolution nascent transcriptomics with chromatin profiling in both systems, we provide a direct comparison of transcriptional kinetics and chromatin states during the establishment and maintenance phases of DC. This unique approach allows us to dissect the progression of DC at base-pair resolution, thereby overcoming the limitations of previous studies. We demonstrate that the establishment and maintenance phases of DC converge on initiation-driven up-regulation of X-linked transcription.

RESULTS

Experimental pipeline for nascent transcriptomics in early Drosophila embryos

To directly characterize the establishment of X-chromosome transcriptional up-regulation in vivo, we sought to apply high-resolution nascent transcriptomics approaches in Drosophila embryos, specifically transient transcriptome sequencing (TT-seq) and mammalian native elongating transcript sequencing (mNET-seq). TT-seq uses brief metabolic labeling of live cells with the nucleoside analog 4-thiouridine (4-sU), which is incorporated into newly synthesized RNA. Isolation and sequencing of 4-sU–labeled RNA capture genome-wide RNA synthesis and enable the calculation of important transcriptional parameters such as RNA synthesis and degradation rates by adding temporal resolution to the expression profiling (47). Complementarily, mNET-seq provides genome-wide localization of Pol II with single-nucleotide precision by capturing nascent RNAs associated with actively engaged Pol II (48). In addition, mNET-seq is able to profile Pol II and the different phosphorylation states of its C-terminal domain (CTD) (for antibodies, see Table 1), notably Ser5p, which is associated with transcription initiation, and Ser2p, which is associated with elongation (48).

The integration of these two approaches provides a powerful multiomics framework for dissecting transcriptional dynamics (49, 50): TT-seq enables estimation of the productive initiation frequency, defined as the number of Pol II molecules that initiate transcription and successfully enter productive elongation. mNET-seq, in turn, allows the measurement of Pol II occupancy in the promoter-proximal region. According to a previously established model (49), the apparent pause duration, i.e., the total time Pol II spends in the promoter-proximal pausing window between two successful initiation events, can be estimated from the ratio of mNET-seq signal to TT-seq signal. Precise quantification of these transcriptional parameters is essential for establishing a mechanistic understanding of DC from a transcriptional perspective. Once we have identified the regulatory step from the changes in transcriptional kinetics, we confirm that these are consistent with changes in Pol II occupancy, these are expected to be cohesive with changes in Ser5p modulations at the TSS should initiation be affected, or Pol II occupancy changes, especially in Ser2p on the gene bodies if elongation was affected.

These methodologies impose stringent experimental requirements, most notably large quantities of input material (~150 to 200 μg of total RNA per sample for TT-seq, at least 3 × 107 cells for mNET-seq) together with reliable sex separation. Given the minute RNA yield of individual embryos (~100 ng of total RNA per embryo), manual embryo handling is inherently incompatible with these approaches. Furthermore, the implementation of nascent transcriptomics in Drosophila embryos generally presents substantial technical challenges intrinsic to the embryonic system. The protective chorion and the size of the embryos limit 4-sU permeability, thereby limiting the efficiency and temporal resolution of nascent RNA labeling. The high sensitivity of the technologies used requires rapid experimental processing including developmental staging to minimize cellular stress and preserve embryo viability.

To overcome these limitations, we focused on stage 15 embryos that uniquely provide a suitable system satisfying the technical constraints of nascent transcriptomics. MOF and H4K16ac have been shown to be intergenerationally transmitted in Drosophila, where maternal depletion of MOF results in compromised DC detectable as early as stage 5 in male embryos (26). However, stage 15 embryos exhibit more pronounced dysregulation of dosage-compensated genes and H4K16ac patterns. Critically, robust Sxl expression at this stage enables fluorescence-based sorting using embryos stably expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP)–tagged Sxl, thereby permitting reliable and scalable separation of male and female embryos. Furthermore, we developed a protocol for stage 15 embryos that allows their rapid and efficient processing and high throughput sorting while maintaining viability of the embryos (see Materials and Methods). Together, these features establish stage 15 embryos as the most suitable system for investigating early DC dynamics within the experimental constraints of nascent transcription profiling (Fig. 1A) (for fly lines, see Table 2).

Using this workflow, we were able to collect and sex sort tens of thousands of precisely staged embryos per experiment (Fig. 1B), taking advantage of GFP-tagged Sxl to reliably separate males and females (Fig. 1C). We independently confirmed the sex of each sample by visual inspection under fluorescent microscope and using the counts per million of chromosome Y reads before downstream analysis (Fig. 1D). TT-seq profiling of these embryos showed robust intronic RNA enrichment (Fig. 1E), indicating efficient capture of nascent transcripts and validating the reliability of our experimental pipeline.

MOF-dependent regulation of X-chromosome transcription rates in early Drosophila development

Having established a robust pipeline for nascent transcriptomics in Drosophila embryos, we next sought to determine how maternal MOF depletion affects the establishment of X-chromosome transcriptional up-regulation during early development. To this end, we performed TT-seq and mNET-seq with sex-sorted stage 15 embryos upon maternal MOF knockdown (KD; fig. S1, A and B). MOF depletion in the germline of mothers led to male-specific lethality in the progeny as previously described (fig. S1C) (26).

Within this experimental framework, TT-seq (47) allowed comparisons between newly synthesized and total RNA levels. To increase the robustness of our analysis, we compared the X chromosome and autosomes within the same samples, where possible, to use autosomal signals as internal control throughout this study. In male embryos, MOF RNA interference (RNAi) led to down-regulation of genes in both labeled and total RNA fractions. Using DESeq2 to normalize and analyze the gene level counts, we found 491 down-regulated genes localized on the X chromosome and 357 on the autosomes in the total RNA fraction. In the labeled fraction, we detected 451 down-regulated genes on the X chromosome and 177 down-regulated genes on autosomes (Fig. 1, F to I). The newly synthesized RNA provided a stronger enrichment of down-regulated genes on the X chromosome (Fig. 1H). Up-regulated genes were sparse, with a singular up-regulated gene on the X chromosome and 55 on the autosomes in the total RNA fraction and one up-regulated gene on Chr X and 30 on the autosomes in the labeled fraction (Fig. 1, F to I). When comparing X-chromosome transcriptional output to that of autosomes, female embryos displayed no major differences in nascent transcription levels following maternal MOF depletion (Fig. 1, J and K, top). The median fold change (FC) between X and autosomes from TSS to transcription end site (TES) was stable at 1.03 for females in control conditions and 1.03 after maternal mof depletion. However, although male embryos displayed identical profiles to females under control RNAi conditions (Fig. 1J, bottom; median X/autosome FC, 0.96), maternal mof depletion strongly reduced the median nascent transcriptional output from the X chromosome relative to that of autosomes (Fig. 1K, bottom; median X/autosome FC, 0.62).

Similar to TT-seq, chromosome-wide metagene plots generated from mNET-seq data for total Pol II demonstrated efficient DC in male embryos. Pol II occupancy from both the X chromosome and autosomes in females was equal (median FC, 1.08), and their ratio remained unaffected by mof depletion (median FC, 1.06) (Fig. 2, A and B). In male control embryos, the Pol II occupancy of the X chromosome similarly equaled that of the autosomes (median FC, 0.94) (Fig. 2C). In contrast, maternal depletion of MOF reduced X-chromosome Pol II occupancy in males to ~75% that of the autosomal level (median FC, 0.74) (Fig. 2D). When comparing the Pol II occupancy across the length of the metagene, we could observe a visible reduction in the FC profile already starting from TSS. The observed reduction was uniform across the length of genes (Fig. 2, C and D), underscoring the specificity of MOF in male DC.

After confirming the robustness of our in vivo model, we calculated transcriptional rates (Fig. 2, E to G; for mathematical details, please see Materials and Methods). The productive initiation rate represents the number of polymerases that initiate and successfully exit the pause window. Comparing FCs in productive initiation rates between mof KD and control conditions revealed that male X chromosomes exhibited a significant reduction of ~35% in productive initiation rates compared with autosomes upon mof depletion (Fig. 2E). However, no differences between X chromosomes and autosomes were detected in female embryos.

Synthesis rates, representing the rate at which complete mRNAs are synthesized, mirrored these findings. Male X chromosomes exhibited significantly lower synthesis rates than autosomes under maternal MOF RNAi conditions, displaying ~37% lower synthesis rates of X chromosomal genes (Fig. 2G). Females remained unaffected when compared between X versus autosomal genes, displaying only a ~4% reduction in median synthesis rate (Fig. 2G).

Integrating the mNET-seq and TT-seq data enabled the calculation of Pol II apparent pause duration, representing the total time Pol II spends in the promoter-proximal pausing window between two successful initiation events (49, 50). Here, male X chromosomes showed prolonged apparent pause duration under MOF depletion relative to autosomes with a ~24% increase in median apparent pause duration, whereas female pausing dynamics remained unaffected and equivalent between X chromosomes and autosomes (Fig. 2F). We also see this reflected in the correlation of gene expression and transcription kinetics, where gene expression changes in the labeled and total fraction do correlate much better with changes in productive initiation and apparent pause duration on the male X chromosome (figs. S2 and S3).

To interrogate whether mof depletion affects most genes evenly or the effects are more varied in strength, we plotted the FC of the median spike-in–normalized TT-seq coverage per 50–base pair (bp) bin for expressed genes longer than 2 kb on the autosomes and X chromosome. The heatmaps in Fig. 2H show the unscaled coverage from 1 kb upstream of the TSS to 30 kb downstream.

In both male and female stage 15 embryos, maternal depletion of mof led to a global reduction in gene expression, visible as a homogeneous FC of less than one across autosomes and the X chromosome. However, in males, we see that the X chromosome is affected much more strongly as compared to autosomes in males or the X chromosomes in females. The reduction in absolute coverage on the male X chromosome presents itself very evenly with the down-regulation uniform across the X-linked genes (see Fig. 2H), further supporting that DC strictly regulates genes to approximately double gene expression from the X, with strict restraints.

The reduction of autosomal expression in both sexes is broadly consistent with the role of the NSL complex, which up-regulates expression of target genes genome-wide in both males and females, and would be expected to be affected by maternal depletion of mof. In addition, some contributions also could arise from sex-independent targets of the MSL complex, which are also found on autosomes (34, 43).

We then quantified the median coverage per gene from the median spike-in–normalized TT-seq signal and the median DESeq2-normalized mNET-seq coverage in the stage 15 embryos. Here, we see that male embryos display a significantly stronger reduction in TT-seq coverage from the X chromosome relative to the autosomes (~29% reduction on autosomes and ~55% reduction on chromosome X) in comparison with the females (~28% reduction on autosomes and ~29% reduction on chromosome X) (Fig. 2I). The mNET-seq coverage, while not showing absolute values due to the DESeq2 normalization, is in line with these reductions, with males displaying a ~9% reduction in median mNET-seq coverage on the autosomes and a ~45% reduction on chromosome X (Fig. 2J), which represents a significant reduction of Pol II occupancy on the X chromosome as compared to the autosomes (P = 2.83 × 10−203). Females, on the other hand, displayed a ~18% reduction of median mNET-seq coverage across autosomal genes and a ~21% reduction on chromosome X (Fig. 2J). Although this difference is also significant in females (P = 0.00011), the effects in males are much stronger.

These findings demonstrate that maternal MOF depletion disrupts DC in male Drosophila embryos by significantly reducing productive transcription initiation and synthesis rates on the X chromosome. This disruption results in transcriptional output levels dropping by ~36% compared with those in the autosomes (Fig. 1K), a reduction of Pol II occupancy matching the reduction in productive initiation rate, and is accompanied by an increase in Pol II apparent pause duration on the X chromosome. The effect on male X-linked genes appears to be very consistent, down-regulating most X-linked genes evenly to ~50% expression, compared to the control dosage compensated state (Figs. 1, I and K, and 2, H and I).

MSL complex–mediated regulation of transcription initiation and synthesis rates on the male X chromosome in Drosophila S2 cells

Next, we sought to further dissect the mechanism by which transcription is enhanced during DC and compare the maintenance of DC to the establishment phase. In addition, we aimed to elucidate the roles of different MSL complex members in DC. Because male flies experience lethality in the third instar larval stage upon disruption of DC (46), blocking us from obtaining material from adult males, we performed additional experiments in differentiated cell lines. To mimic the established DC phase, we used S2 cells to interrogate MSL complex–mediated transcriptional regulation, which allow for flexible and scalable KD of targets. Using double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) against mof, msl-1, and msl-2, we successfully depleted core components of the MSL complex, leading to a reduction in the H4K16ac histone mark (Fig. 3A) (for antibody details, see Table 1). The pronounced reduction in H4K16ac levels following MSL1 KD is consistent with previous studies identifying the MSL1 dimer as the structural core of the MSL complex, providing the scaffold for MSL2, MSL3, and MOF binding (32, 51–53). Thus, MSL1 KD results in a near-complete disruption of MSL complex formation and a corresponding loss of H4K16ac on the X chromosome (32).

We performed both TT-seq and mNET-seq in S2 cells, observing a reduction of nascent transcription and Pol II occupancy over dosage compensated genes upon KD of MSL complex members (Fig. 3B). All KDs resulted in down-regulation of genes in both total and labeled mRNA fractions (Fig. 3C). In all cases, the differentially expressed genes [abs(FC) > 1.5 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05] were mainly localized on the X chromosome (table S1). The extent of transcriptional down-regulation supports a successful KD for each target, which is further intensified in the labeled RNA fraction (Fig. 3C). Under control conditions, labeled RNA output was comparable between the X chromosome and autosomes (Fig. 3, D to F, top). However, depletion of msl-1, msl-2, or mof led to a substantial decrease in X-chromosome transcription relative to autosomes (Fig. 3, D to F, bottom). The MSL1 KD exhibited the strongest phenotype, reducing X-chromosome transcriptional output by ~27%, from an X/autosome median FC of 0.92 in control conditions to 0.67 in the msl-1 KD (Fig. 3D). We next compared the initiation and synthesis rates for both X chromosomes and autosomes between control and KD conditions. KD of MSL complex components caused a significant reduction in the median productive initiation rates on the X chromosome relative to autosomes, with the most pronounced effect (~29% reduction on Chr X) observed upon MSL1 depletion (Fig. 3, G to I). Similarly, median synthesis rates were reduced by ~30% on the X chromosome following msl-1 KD (Fig. 3G). We observed that the effect of msl-2 KD on initiation (median of ~23% down on Chr X) and synthesis rates (median of ~24% down on Chr X) was almost identical to that of msl-1 (Fig. 3H). The mof KD displayed much smaller changes comparatively, displaying a ~15% reduction in median productive initiation rate on Chr X and a ~13% reduction in median synthesis rate relative to autosomes (Fig. 3I).

mNET-seq experiments upon the msl-1 and mof KD in S2 cells allowed us to calculate the effect of MSL complex depletion on apparent pause duration. A significant increase in median apparent pause duration in the msl-1 KD was observed on X-linked genes (~16%), relative to autosomal genes (Fig. 3G, middle). KD of mof did not lead to a statistically significant increase in median apparent pause duration on the X chromosome (~3%); nevertheless, an upward trend on X-chromosomal genes could be observed (Fig. 3I, middle). Furthermore, chromosome-wide metagene plots generated from mNET-seq data in S2 cells demonstrated efficient DC in the control condition, while the msl-1 KD shows uniform reduction of Pol II occupancy on X chromosomal genes by ~20% (median FC) (fig. S4A, left). This reduction is also visible at the prominent pausing peak, indicating that the transcriptional defect observed lies upstream of the pausing site. Upon KD of mof, the same trend is visible (average reduction of ~10%), however much weaker compared with the msl-1 KD (fig. S4A, right). Collectively, these findings imply that the maintenance of DC in S2 cells is also regulated at the level of transcription initiation.

The MSL complex modulates transcriptional kinetics in Drosophila S2 cells, independent of gene length or expression level

We next explored whether nascent transcriptional down-regulation coincides with gene architecture in S2 cells. To address this, we plotted FCs in transcription initiation or synthesis rates against gene length and expression levels.

Genes on the X chromosome with reduced productive initiation or synthesis rates upon MSL complex KD exhibited length distributions similar to unaffected autosomal genes (fig. S4, B and C). Similarly, no correlative trend was observed between gene expression levels in the control condition and changes in initiation or synthesis rates upon MSL complex depletion. The distribution of expression levels for X-linked genes closely resembled that of autosomal genes, with differences observed only in initiation and synthesis rates (fig. S4, D and E). The uniform shift of gene populations along the rate axis (y axis), together with the comparable distributions between X-linked and autosomal genes, suggests that DC operates independently of gene length and modulates transcription through factors that are largely independent of individual gene expression profiles.

Absolute quantification of transcriptional changes upon abolishment of MSL complex mediated DC

To assess whether the effects of MSL complex loss on transcription are uniform across genes or gene specific, we again plotted the FC of the median spike-in–normalized TT-seq coverage per 50-bp bin for expressed genes longer than 2 kb on the autosomes and X chromosome (Fig. 4, A and D). The heatmaps in Fig. 4 (A and D) show the unscaled coverage from 1 kb upstream of the TSS to 30 kb downstream of the TSS. The msl-1 KD cells display a strong reduction in X-linked expression with milder autosomal down-regulation (Fig. 4A). When we quantified the median coverage per gene using the median spike-in–normalized TT-seq signal, we observe a ~23% reduction from the autosomes and a ~45% reduction from the X chromosome (Fig. 4B), which represents a significant difference between X-chromosomal and autosomal genes (P = 2.42 × 10−282), again confirming the separate regulation of transcription on the X chromosome by the MSL complex. The median DESeq2-normalized mNET-seq coverage showed a median of ~13% increase for autosomal genes and a ~18% decrease for genes on chromosome X (Fig. 4C). This still represents a significant reduction for X-chromosomal genes compared with autosomal genes (P = 4.39 × 10−159).

The mof KD in S2 cells displayed much more light effects on genome-wide transcription (Fig. 4D). Quantifying the per-gene median effect of mof KD on TT-seq coverage in S2 cells, it displays the weakest effects. We observe a ~6% reduction of spike-in–normalized median TT-seq coverage on autosomes and a ~20% reduction on the X chromosome after KD of mof (Fig. 4E). This still represents a significant reduction for X-chromosomal genes as compared to autosomal genes (P = 7.56 × 10−64). In turn, the DESeq2-normalized median mNET-seq coverage increases by ~10% for autosomal genes and reduces by ~7% on the X chromosome following mof KD (Fig. 4F). Although the magnitude of this difference is smaller for the mNET-seq data, the difference between X chromosomal genes and autosomal genes is still significant (P = 8.35 × 10−64). In general, the mof KD caused much weaker changes to both expression of X-linked genes and transcriptional rates as compared to the msl-1 KD. However, both do affect X chromosomal genes more strongly than autosomal ones, setting X chromosomal genes apart from autosomal ones (figs. S5 and S6).

Together, these results demonstrate that loss of MSL complex function causes a robust and uniform transcriptional down-regulation of the X chromosome in males, consistent with the MSL complex acting as a global amplifier of X chromosomal transcription in males. These findings highlight that male DC through the MSL complex modulates transcription in an even manner across the male X chromosome.

The MSL complex does not affect transcription termination

To determine whether disruption of DC affects transcription termination, we quantified transcriptional readthrough by calculating the ratio of TT-seq signal in the region spanning TES to 2 kb downstream of the TES to the TT-seq signal in the region spanning 2 kb upstream of the TES to the TES, as previously described (Fig. 4G) (54). We then compared the log2 FCs of this readthrough index (RTI) for all KDs in stage 15 embryos and S2 cells in comparison with their corresponding controls. The log2 FCs of RTI were highly similar between the conditions and showed no systematic shifts for either the X-chromosomal or autosomal genes (Fig. 4, H to J). Furthermore, independent of our experimental model or the KD performed, we did not observe a significant alteration in transcriptional readthrough, supporting the conclusion that DC does not modulate transcriptional output through regulation of transcription termination.

Pol II initiation limits transcriptional up-regulation on the male X chromosome

Having established that loss of DC is associated with a significant decrease in productive initiation rate, we next aimed to resolve the earliest stage of transcription influenced by MSL complex depletion. We rationalized that the most upstream genomic position at which a significant change in Pol II occupancy is observed is likely to be the most important for stimulating the most sizable enhancement of RNA output, because downstream regions would benefit from the upstream enhancement in transcription. To study this, we analyzed Pol II occupancy around pausing sites using mNET-seq to determine the most upstream genomic location showing a significant decrease in Pol II occupancy following MOF depletion. Keeping the regions the same size, we defined three regions relative to the pausing site: −60 to −21 bp upstream of the pausing site (prepause window), −20 bp to +20 bp around the pausing site (pause window), and +21 to +60 bp downstream of the pausing site (postpause window). These promoter-proximal transcription windows allowed us to distinguish between Pol II initiation (prepause window), promoter-proximal pausing (pause window), and productive elongation/Pol II released from pause (postpause window).

In female embryos, Pol II occupancy on the X chromosome was higher than on autosomes and showed minimal alteration upon mof KD (Fig. 5A, top). In males, however, mof depletion led to a slight but noticeable reduction in X-chromosome Pol II occupancy relative to the autosomes across prepause, pause, and postpause windows (Fig. 5A, bottom). Furthermore, quantification of the FC between the X chromosome and autosomes revealed that mof depletion in males leads to a pronounced reduction in Pol II occupancy both prepause (~29% reduction) and postpause site (~35% reduction) on chromosome X (Fig. 5B, left). In contrast, mof depletion in females caused no relevant changes (difference in median of <5%) in these windows (Fig. 5B, right). Pol II occupancy in male embryos around the pausing site is reduced to a lesser extent (~23%) than in the regions immediately upstream and downstream of the pause window (Fig. 5B, left). Our data indicate that the earliest transcriptional events affected by maternal mof depletion in males occur upstream of the pausing site. This was evidenced by the reduction in Pol II occupancy in the prepause region and the absence of a compensatory accumulation at the pause, consistent with previous mNET-seq studies showing that inhibition of pause release leads to promoter-proximal Pol II accumulation (49). These findings imply that the transcriptional up-regulation underlying DC is primarily achieved through enhanced Pol II initiation at promoters, which is impaired upon mof depletion.

Further evidence supporting this conclusion arises from comparing the FC in productive initiation rate against apparent pause duration under control and mof KD conditions in male and female embryos (Fig. 5, C and D). In females, the distribution of X-chromosome data points (red dots) is indistinguishable from autosomes (blue dots) (Fig. 5D), indicating that X-chromosomal and autosomal genes are likely to be regulated by common mechanisms. However, in males, MOF depletion results in a marked reduction in productive initiation rate accompanied by a slighter increase in apparent pause duration (Fig. 5C). This inverse relationship between initiation rate and pause duration is known as the pause initiation limit; however, here, the earliest effect that we find is a strong reduction of productive initiation rate and Pol II recruitment on the male X chromosome and only a moderate increase in apparent pause duration. This leads us to conclude that the transcriptional regulation through DC appears to be primarily driven by an increase in Pol II initiation.

Changes in transcription initiation appear independent of changes in H4K16ac during establishment of DC despite moderate correlation with RNA synthesis

We were also interested in the relationship between H4K16ac and transcriptional rates during the establishment of DC. We reanalyzed a previously published H4K16ac chromatin immunoprecipitation–sequencing (ChIP-seq) dataset by Samata et al. (26), using the same maternal mof depletion fly line and stage 15 embryos. Comparing the changes in H4K16ac levels in stage 15 embryos over gene bodies and promoters with the dynamics in transcriptional rates, we found that H4K16ac changes over promoter regions did not correlate or only very weakly correlated with the changes in transcriptional rates (fig. S7, A to F). Changes in H4K16ac enrichment on the gene body appeared to only weakly correlate with changes in productive initiation rates for X-linked genes in male embryos [correlation coefficient (r) = 0.13], while apparent pause duration did not correlate with changes in H4K16ac enrichment (fig. S8, A to D). We could, however, detect a moderate correlation between synthesis rates of genes on the X chromosome and H4K16ac enrichment changes in male embryos (r = 0.31). As an internal control, we found no correlation between synthesis rates and H4K16ac enrichment changes for autosomal genes in male embryos (r = −0.07) (Fig. 5E). While we also observed a higher correlation between synthesis rate and H4K16ac on X chromosomal genes compared with autosomal genes in female embryos, it was much less pronounced and they lacked a strong change in synthesis rate (Figs. 5F and 2G). To test the correlations between H4K16ac and transcriptional rates using a different and chromosome-agnostic analysis, we subsetted all genes on the basis of their H4K16ac enrichment levels in control samples into four clusters using k-means clustering (fig. S8, E to H). Cluster 1 showed predominantly gene-body H4K16ac, cluster 3 with predominantly promoter-associated H4K16ac, cluster 2 a mixture of gene body and promoter H4K16ac, and cluster 4 serving as a negative control owing to its lack of H4K16ac signal. By comparing the FCs in transcriptional rates of X-chromosomal and autosomal genes within each H4K16ac cluster, we consistently observed male specific effects on X-chromosomal genes, regardless of the cluster. In male embryos, the magnitude of the effect, especially on synthesis rate, gradually decreases from clusters with high to low H4K16ac signal (fig. S8, F to H), indicating that higher H4K16ac levels are associated with stronger increases in synthesis. Together with the lack of a corresponding effect on autosomal genes, this supports a role for H4K16ac in the establishment of DC on the male X chromosome.

MSL complex–mediated enhancement of initiating Pol II during establishment and maintenance of DC

To investigate whether the decrease in Pol II occupancy before the pausing site is caused by a decrease of initiating Pol II, we performed mNET-seq for total, Ser5p, and Ser2p Pol II in stage 15 embryos (figs. S9 and S10), as well as total and Ser5p Pol II in Drosophila S2 cells (for antibodies, see Table 1). By comparing occupancy patterns of total, Ser2p, and Ser5p Pol II, we can determine whether the pattern that we observe is specifically linked to transcription initiation, because Ser5p marks Pol II engaged in transcription initiation and early promoter escape, but not later elongation. Ser2p, on the other hand, is linked to promoter proximal pause release and elongation. Generating a matched mNET-seq dataset for the embryos, with total, Ser2p, and Ser5p done in parallel, allows us to further dissect early transcriptional events and reproduce our results from total Pol II experiments.

For the embryos, we performed a new and matched mNET-seq experiment to compare total, Ser5p, and Ser2p datasets and avoid batch effects. Performing the quantification of prepause, pausing, and postpause region Pol II occupancy as before, we confirmed our results found in Fig. 6A, where mof depletion in males reduces Pol II occupancy at both prepause (~18% reduction) and postpause (~18% reduction) sites on the chromosome X (Fig. 6A). On the other hand, mof depletion in females caused no meaningful changes (difference in median of <3%) in these windows (Fig. 6B).

As Ser2 phosphorylation is deposited by CDK9 during release from promoter proximal pausing, we restricted our analysis to the pausing and postpause sites. Changes in early transcriptional kinetics appear to be independent of changes in H4K16ac during maintenance of DC

Last we also used ChIP-seq in S2 cells to check whether we could identify a relationship between the transcriptional rates and H4K16ac. Our msl-1 KD results in a ~75% reduction of H4K16ac on X-linked gene bodies (Fig. 7G), whereas the KD of mof results in a ~50% reduction of H4K16ac over the gene body of X-linked genes (Fig. 7H). Both KDs displayed similar (~25%) reductions of H4K16ac on X-linked promoter regions (Fig. 7, G and H). Correlating H4K16ac changes on promoters with changes to transcriptional rates in S2 cells, we were unable to detect noticeable correlation (fig. S11). When we correlated the log2 FC of H4K16ac over promoter or gene body regions against changes in the transcription rates in msl-1 and mof KD, we were also unable to detect any meaningful correlation between promoter or gene body H4K16ac changes and initiation, apparent pause duration, or synthesis rate changes (Fig. 7, I and J; and figs. S11, A to F, and S12, A to D). These findings imply that the relationship between H4K16ac status and transcriptional rate is less direct than anticipated. This finding was also confirmed when using k-means clustering as a chromosome agnostic approach to group genes on X chromosome and the autosomes by their H4K16ac pattern, which demonstrated that rate changes did not seem to greatly depend on the amount of H4K16ac in each group (fig. S12, E to I).

Generally, genes on the X chromosome with the greatest loss of H4K16ac showed the strongest and most consistent down-regulation across all KDs (fig. S13A, top left). Autosomal genes with H4K16ac displayed less variable total RNA expression but were down-regulated in the mof KD. In contrast, nascent expression of H4K16ac-positive autosomal genes increased in the msl-1 KD but decreased in the mof KD (fig. S13A, bottom left). The msl-2 KD showed intermediate effects, causing pronounced nascent down-regulation in H4K16ac-positive X-linked genes, but little dysregulation in autosomal H4K16ac-positive genes.

Given the observed relationship between H4K16ac loss and transcriptional down-regulation, we next assessed whether other chromatin modifications, such as H3K36me3 and H3K79me2, also change in response to MSL complex depletion. We found the ChIP-seq signal for these chromatin marks in msl-1 and mof KDs (fig. S13, B and C) to be unchanged on both the X chromosome and autosomes. This indicates that, while H4K16ac is substantially reduced, the overall chromatin environment appears to be stable, allowing potential regulators to still access these genes.

DISCUSSION

This study provides the first nucleotide-resolution analysis of Pol II occupancy dynamics during the establishment and maintenance of DC in Drosophila. We advance our understanding of the DC mechanism by integrating genome-wide multiomic datasets to infer transcriptional kinetics (productive initiation, synthesis, and apparent pause duration rates) along with Pol II CTD phosphorylation states. By combining mNET-seq, TT-seq, and H4K16ac profiling across embryos and S2 cell models, we demonstrate that the MSL complex drives transcriptional up-regulation primarily through enhanced Pol II initiation, updating current models, which place elongation as the dominant regulatory step in male X-chromosomal up-regulation (30, 31, 55). Because DC is one of the most prominent biological examples of substantial (approximately twofold) chromosome-wide transcriptional enhancement, our study has considerable consequences for our understanding of large-scale mRNA transcriptional modulation. Our work reveals that increasing the initiation rate appears to be both an efficient and highly tunable step in guiding enhanced RNA output of a large gene set. This would suggest that Pol II initiation could represent a promising target to restore large-scale expression of aberrantly silenced genes in cancer or neurodevelopmental disorders.

Technologies used in previous studies to analyze effects of DC on Pol II (30, 31, 33), such as ChIP-seq and global run-on sequencing (GRO-seq), lack the resolution to precisely distinguish the promoter-proximal pausing sites and the TSS (average distance, 20 to 80 bp), as well as being unable to resolve the phosphorylation status of Pol II. In this work, we reassessed Pol II occupancy at single base-pair resolution (Figs. 2, A to D; 5, A and B; and 6) and took advantage of mNET-seq to precisely map both initiating/paused and elongating Pol II by resolving the phosphorylation status of the Pol II CTD (48). We show that MSL complex depletion reduces Pol II occupancy upstream of pausing sites, implicating initiation, but not elongation, as the critical step modulated to achieve the increased RNA output accomplished by the DC process. In control conditions, the X/Autosome ratio for Pol II occupancy stays around 1 across the gene body. However, it drops to about 0.75 when maternal mof is depleted in embryos or when msl-1 is knocked down in S2 cells. This reduction is uniform across TSS, promoter-proximal pausing site, and gene body, indicating that the transcriptional defect arises upstream of the pausing site and is not specific to elongation or gene body processivity.

Our study is the first to shed light on how DC influences transcriptional kinetics genome wide as previous studies only offered static Pol II occupancy and gene expression snapshots (30, 33). It has been shown that Pol II occupancy can remain unchanged if one transcriptional parameter compensates for another (56, 57). This is problematic because Pol II profiling methods such as GRO-seq, ChIP-seq, and mNET-seq take snapshots of Pol II occupancy of the genome. However, by combining our mNET-seq–based Pol II occupancy data with TT-seq, we gain temporal resolution of transcription, allowing us to compare changes in transcriptional kinetics much more reliably (47).

The ~25% reduction in mNET-seq signal (total Pol II) observed on the X chromosome following maternal mof depletion in embryos or msl-1 KD in S2 cells and the ~29 to 35% reduction in productive initiation rate in these conditions closely match the previously reported 1.2 fold (or 20%) increase in Pol II promoter occupancy at the X chromosome over autosomes in wild-type salivary gland cells (33, 39, 40). The observed reductions in synthesis rates and TT-seq signal of ~24 to 37% come close to the previously observed ~40% increase in Pol II density over gene bodies using GRO-seq (30, 31). While this gene body Pol II density change was interpreted as enhanced elongation by the authors of the study, our study now helps to reinterpret this in light of improved insight into Pol II dynamics (30, 31). Discerning Pol II occupancy changes at base-pair resolution allowed us to better resolve the TSS–pausing site area, and adding temporal resolution with TT-seq allowed us to study the changes in transcriptional kinetics. Current models of transcription describe gene body Pol II densities to be proportional to I/V (initiation rate/elongation velocity) at steady state (49, 58), so discrepancies between occupancy and initiation rates (I) would indicate changes in elongation velocity (V). Increased initiation is required for increased mRNA output (59), but processivity changes can modulate output.

Looking at mNET-seq occupancy changes (total Pol II) around pausing sites in male embryos, we observe reductions of ~29% in the prepause and ~35% in the postpause occupancy (Fig. 5B), matching the ~35% drop in productive initiation (Fig. 2E). This also holds true for our experiments in S2 cells, where, e.g., upon msl-1 KD, we observe a ~29% reduction in productive initiation rate for X-linked genes (Fig. 3G), which closely matches the ~28%/~34% reduction in pre/postpause mNET-seq coverage in the same condition (Fig. 7A). The changes to promoter adjacent Pol II occupancy agree with the changes in productive initiation, indicating the absence of notable elongation changes (constant V).

Last, we have shown that Ser5p Pol II occupancy upstream of the pausing site is affected upon maternal mof depletion in stage 15 embryos as well as msl-1 KD in S2 cells, linking MSL complex activity to Pol II initiation and promoter escape. This observation aligns with prior work showing that MSL1 interacts with CDK7, the kinase responsible for Ser5 phosphorylation, and that CDK7-mediated phosphorylation stabilizes MSL1 chromatin binding (33, 43). We took advantage of high-throughput technologies to capture genome-wide effects on Pol II occupancy, but our data are substantiated by the findings of Forbes et al. (36) whose MS2 tagging of selected dosage-compensated genes in Drosophila embryos around stage 5, revealed that the initiation rate, defined as number of Pol II molecules per transcriptional burst (burst amplitude), is increased in male embryos, whereas elongation rate/Pol II dwell time was not male-specifically perturbed. In line with this, we also did not observe any tilt in the X chromosomal TT-seq or Ser2p mNET-seq signal over gene bodies (60, 61) or changes in biases of cotranscriptionally deposited histone marks like H3K36me3 or H3K79me2, which, together with the agreement of Pol II occupancy changes and changes to productive initiation and pausing, indicates an absence of elongation velocity changes (59, 62–64). While imaging- and sequencing-based readouts of transcriptional rates cannot be compared directly, the findings of Forbes et al. (36) agree with our findings that enhanced transcription of dosage compensated genes is driven by increased transcription initiation.

Whereas the observed initiation defects upon disruption of DC explain the largest part of the transcriptional reduction, they do not linearly account for the full 50% reduction in transcriptional output. Mathematical models of DC show that small changes across multiple kinetic parameters can synergize to yield a twofold increase in mRNA output (57). Moreover, they demonstrate how changes in initiation and promoter escape could produce ChIP-seq profiles seen in prior studies, where reduced Pol II density at initiation regions might be obscured, making reductions on gene bodies more apparent (57). This indicates that, due to the limitations of ChIP-seq and GRO-seq to study Pol II turnover, Pol II densities may have been interpreted as the absence of initiation effects and more notable effects during elongation (56, 57). The latest Pol II mapping technologies have forced us to reassess the robustness of comparing broad occupancy peaks spanning the promoter region to draw conclusions on the earliest transcriptional events.

We observed that maternal KD of mof leads to strong reductions in productive initiation and synthesis rates for X-linked genes in males; this is recapitulated in the maintenance phase by KDs of msl-1 and msl-2, whereas mof KD displays weaker effects (summarized in fig. S13D). In the light of these data, we propose the following model of DC: Establishment phase: The maternal MSL subcomplex consisting of MSL1, MSL3, and MOF establishes broad domains of H4K16ac in the early embryos, increasing accessibility of the chromatin to allow later canonical MSL complex binding at gene promoters. MSL complex binding enhances Pol II initiation and phosphorylation of Pol II CTD Ser5 specifically on the male X chromosome, enabling efficient release from the promoter (65). Maintenance phase: Once DC is established, MSL1 and MSL2 sustain enhanced Pol II initiation, on the active genes. In the absence of H4K16ac, other active histone marks, which have been established at transcriptionally active loci independent of DC mechanisms, can also contribute to maintaining chromatin accessibility at actively transcribed genes. This is consistent with the continued presence of other activating histone marks, such as H3K36me3 or H3K79me2 even after MSL complex KD (fig. S13, B and C).

Our study using in vivo stage 15 embryo and in vitro S2 cell data implies that establishment of DC requires maternal H4K16ac-mediated chromatin priming (26), whereas its maintenance relies on preestablished transcriptional continuity and euchromatic chromatin marks. Both systems converge on initiation as the core driver of increased male X-linked expression, underscoring its role as a universal regulatory node regardless of developmental stage.

S2 cells are a widely used model to study transcription and DC in Drosophila because they express the MSL complex and allow rapid, scalable KD experiments. However, they are highly aneuploid, with extensive copy number variation across the genome, which may influence absolute expression levels. Our analysis focuses on the comparison of relative FCs of transcriptional parameters per gene that helps minimize the impact of the aneuploidy on our conclusions. Unlike our in vivo experiments, the RNAi-mediated KD of MSL complex members in S2 cells does not represent a full abrogation of DC; however, the close agreement between the S2 cell and embryo phenotypes demonstrates the robustness of our main conclusions, despite the limitations of the S2 cell system.

An additional factor that may contribute to DC-related regulatory mechanisms is the inverse dosage effect, in which changes in the dosage of regulatory components produce genome-wide shifts in gene expression. In this model, compensated expression of genes on a varied chromosome, such as the male X chromosome, can arise because inverse dosage effects partially offset the direct consequences of altered gene copy number, as has been documented in other eukaryotic aneuploid contexts (66–69). The inverse effect is thought to result from a stoichiometric imbalance of dosage-sensitive components of the transcriptional machinery between the varied chromosome and the remainder of the genome. The resulting increased or decreased dosage of one chromosomal segment can thereby lead to opposite-direction changes in gene expression elsewhere. In Drosophila males, the MSL complex is proposed to sequester MOF and other chromatin regulators to the X chromosome, where they promote X-linked transcription while limiting broader genome-wide inverse effects that would, otherwise, alter autosomal expression (70). MOF (35), JIL1 kinase (71), and heterochromatin protein 1 (HP1) (72) are among the factors implicated in this regulation, and their redistribution or sequestration on the X chromosome may help prevent overcompensation and support normal development. According to this model, loss of MSL complex members (msl-1, msl-2, msl-3, mle, or roX1/roX2 double mutants) is expected to reduce X-linked expression and allow chromatin regulators such as MOF to redistribute to the autosomes, where they can influence autosomal gene expression.

However, our data do not support a model in which inverse dosage effects alone account for DC. Using absolute quantifications of nascent gene expression (spike-in–normalized TT-seq; Figs. 2, H to J, and 4, A to F), we do not observe a substantial global up-regulation of autosomes upon disruption of DC. Instead, our results indicate that DC is primarily driven by increased transcriptional output of the X chromosome mediated by the MSL complex, with any inverse dosage responses likely playing a secondary or modulatory role. This interpretation is consistent with the prevailing model of DC in D. melanogaster, in which enhanced transcription of the male X chromosome is a central mechanism (20, 28, 33, 34, 43, 73–80).

By integrating single-nucleotide resolution Pol II profiling with the temporal resolution of nascent RNA profiling and modeling of transcriptional kinetics, we provide a framework for dissecting global regulation in vivo. Future studies applying this approach to other systems will reveal additional conserved principles of gene dosage control, advancing our understanding of epigenetic control of gene regulation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fly work

D. melanogaster flies were reared on corn flour-molasses medium containing 1.2% w/v agar, 1.8% (w/v) dry yeast, 1% (w/v) soya flour, 8% (w/v) corn flour, 2.2% (w/v) molasses, 8% (w/v) malt extract, 0.24% (w/v) 4-hydrobenzoic acid methyl-ester, and 0.625% propionic acid. Fly maintenance, crosses, and embryo collections were conducted at 25°C, 70% relative humidity, and 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle. See Table 2 for the fly lines used.

For maternal RNAi, control RNAi;sxl-GFP or mof RNAi;sxl-GFP females were crossed with mata-Gal4;sxl-GFP males and the female progeny were fertilized by their siblings. These flies were transferred in cages and after two consecutive 2-hour synchronization collections, embryos were collected on agar apple juice plates for 2 hours. The collected embryos were dechorionated with a solution of 3 to 7% NaClO for 90 s and washed thoroughly with distilled water and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with 0.1% Triton X-100. Stage 15 male and female embryos were separated on the basis of GFP intensity using a Union Biometrica Inc. BioSorter.

S2 cells culture and RNAi

S2 cells were a gift from M. Boutros. They were cultured at 25°C in Express Five SFM medium (Gibco, no. 10486025) supplemented with GlutaMAX (100 ml/liter; Gibco, no. 35050061). Shaking cultures were maintained at a cell concentration of 1.5 × 106 to 1.5 × 107 cells/ml.

dsRNAs against msl-1, msl-2, mof, or gst (nontargeting control) were prepared according to the DRSC protocol (https://fgr.hms.harvard.edu/dsrna-synthesis) via standard T7 in vitro transcription of cDNA templates using the HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit [New England Biolabs (NEB), no. E2040S].

Transfection took place in six-well plates seeded with 1.5 × 106 cells in 1 ml of medium. For each well, 10 μg of dsRNA and 9 μl of Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, no. 13778150) were diluted separately in 150 μl of Express Five SFM medium and mixed for 30 min before added dropwise to the cells. After 2 to 4 days of incubation, the cells were collected for further processing.

Mammalian native elongating transcript sequencing

For Drosophila embryos, ~6000 embryos were transferred in 1-ml glass douncer in Express Five SFM medium and dounced with six to eight strokes on ice with the loose pestle. The homogenate was passed through a 40-μm filter to remove debris, and the cells were pelleted by spinning at 500g, 4°C for 5 min. For S2 cells, ~108 cells were collected by spinning at 500g, 4°C for 5 min. From this point onward, embryo and cell processing was identical, and all steps and buffers were on ice unless otherwise indicated.

The above pellets were washed twice with PBS and dissolved in 1 ml of hypotonic lysis buffer [15 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 7.6), 10 mM NaCl, and 5 mM EDTA] supplemented with protease inhibitors (cOmplete Mini EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail; Roche, no. 11836170001) and incubated on ice for 15 min. After addition of 1% NP-40, the samples were vortexed for 30 s, and the nuclei were collected by spinning at 2000g for 2 min. The nuclear fraction was lysed in 1 ml of HMGT-K350 buffer [15 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 7.6), 12.5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 0.2% Tween 20, and 350 mM KCl] supplemented with protease inhibitors and incubated for 30 min rotating. The chromatin was collected by centrifugation at 10,000g for 5 min and washed once with HMGT-K350 buffer and then with 100 μl of micrococcal nuclease (MNase) wash buffer [1× MNase buffer (NEB, no. M0247S) and 1× bovine serum albumin (BSA)]. After a short spin for 10 s at 10,000g, the supernatant was removed, and 100 μl of MNAse reaction buffer [1× MNase buffer (NEB, no. M0247S), 1× BSA, and 4000 gel units of MNase (NEB, no. M0247S)] was added. The MNase reaction was incubated on a thermomixer at 37°C 1400 rpm for 2 min, and the reaction was stopped by adding 5 μl of 500 mM EGTA. After spinning for 5 min at 16,000g, the soluble fraction was transferred to a new tube and diluted three times with NET-2 buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% NP-40]. For each immunoprecipitation (IP) reaction, 30 μl of protein G dynabeads (Invitrogen, no. 10003D) were used. Dynabeads were washed once with NET-2 buffer, incubated with 3 μg of the corresponding Pol II antibody for 3 hours at 4°C and then washed again twice with NET-2 buffer. The antibody-conjugated magnetic beads were resuspended in 100 μl of NET-2 buffer and mixed with the MNase-digested supernatant. The digested chromatin was incubated with the antibody-conjugated beads for 2 hours, rotating at 4°C. Afterward, the samples were washed four times with the ice-cold NET-2 buffer. After washing with 300 μl of PNKT buffer [1× PNK buffer (NEB, no. M0236S) and 0.1% Tween 20], the beads were incubated in 100 μl of PNK reaction buffer [PNKT buffer, 100 U of T4 PNK, 3′-phosphastase minus (NEB, no. M0236S), and 1.5 mM adenosine 5′-triphosphate] for 6 min at 37°C in a thermomixer. Last, the beads were washed with 1 ml of NET-2 buffer and then resuspended in 250 μl of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, no. 15596026). RNA was isolated with the Direct-zol RNA Microprep Kit (Zymo Research, no. R2062). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced using the NovaSeq 6000 SP (XP) sequencer. At least two replicates were used per condition.

Transient transcriptome sequencing

Sixty million S2 cells were collected by centrifugation at 500g for 5 min. Six thousand embryos were transferred in 1-ml glass douncer containing 1 ml of Express Five SFM medium and processed with six to eight strokes using the loose pestle. After filtration through a 40-μm filter, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 500g for 5 min. Collected material from cells or embryos was diluted in 1 ml of Express Five SFM medium, and 4-sU was added to a final concentration of 500 μM. After rotating the samples for 5 min at 25°C in the dark, the cells were collected by spinning at 2000g for 1 min and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen.

The frozen pellets were resuspended in 1 ml of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, no. 15596026). Upon addition of 50 μl of 3 M sodium acetate and 0.5 ml of isopropanol, the samples were incubated at least for 2 hours at −20°C. After centrifugation at 22,000g, 4°C for 20 min, the RNA pellets were washed with 80% ethanol and resuspended in 300 μl of Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, no. 93283). Following the addition of 18 ng of a custom spike-in mix (consisting of four unlabeled and four 4-sU–labeled spike-in sequences) per 100 μg of total RNA for the embryo experiments and 48 ng of spike-in per 20 million cells for the S2 cell experiments, the samples were fragmented by sonication in Bioruptor Plus (Diagenode) for 1 cycle (30 s on/30 s off, high-power mode). After keeping 2 μg of the samples as input for total RNA sequencing, the sample volumes were adjusted to 300 μl with TE buffer, and 80 μl of HPDP-biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. 21341; 2.5 μg/μl in N,N′-dimethylformamide) was added. The samples were incubated at 25°C in the dark, rotating for 2 hours. Following phenol-chloroform extraction, the biotinylated RNA was dissolved in 45 μl of water, denatured at 65°C for 10 min, and kept on ice.

MyOne Streptavidin C1 beads (40 μl; Invitrogen, no. 65001) per sample were washed twice with 1× high-salt wash buffer [HSWB; 100 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 10 mM EDTA, 1 M NaCl, and 0.1% Tween 20] and resuspended in a mixture of 50 μl of 0.1× HSWB and 5 μl of 1× HSWB. Afterward, the biotinylated denatured RNA was mixed with the beads and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature (RT), rotating in the dark. The beads were then washed twice with 1× HSWB, and the RNA was eluted with 100 μl of 5% β-mercaptoethanol. Both total and labeled fractions were subjected to TURBO DNase (Invitrogen, no. AM2238) treatment and purified using Oligo Clean & Concentrator kit (Zymo Research, no. D4061). The sequencing libraries were constructed using the Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep, Ligation with Ribo-Zero Plus kit and sequenced with the NovaSeq 6000 sequencer. At least three replicates were used per condition.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing

Cells (108) were collected by centrifugation at 500g for 5 min and washed with PBS supplemented with 0.016% (v/v) Triton X-100. The samples were fixed in 1% methanol-free formaldehyde in PBS supplemented with 0.016% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 10 min at RT. The fixation reaction was quenched by addition of glycine to a final concentration of 0.125 M. Following an incubation at RT for 2 min, the samples were washed twice with PBS supplemented with 0.016% (v/v) Triton X-100. The fixed cells were resuspended in 1 ml of lysis buffer [10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 10 mM NaCl, and 0.2% NP-40] supplemented with protease inhibitors, and the nuclei were collected by centrifugation at 2000g, 4°C for 5 min. The pellet was dissolved in 1 ml of shearing buffer [10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 0.1% SDS, and 1 mM EDTA] and transferred into 1-ml Covaris tubes for sonication (Covaris E220; peak power, 140 W; duty factor, 5%; 200 cycles per burst) at 4°C for 10 min. Unsonicated debris was removed by centrifugation at 10,000g, 4°C for 5 min, and the sonicated chromatin was stored at −80°C. Five percent of each sample was processed for chromatin concentration and quality by treatment with 2 μl of proteinase K (20 mg/ml) first at 50°C for 30 min and then at 65°C for 30 min followed by DNA isolation using the ChIP clean and Concentrator kit (Zymo Research, no. D5205). For each IP, 3 μg of chromatin was diluted with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer [140 mM NaCl, 25 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 7.5), 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, and 0.1% Na-DOC] to 550 μl. After keeping 10% of the samples as input, 3 μg of antibody was added, and the mixture was incubated overnight rotating at 4°C. Next day, Protein A or Protein G Dynabeads (Invitrogen, nos. 10001D and 10003D) were washed three times with RIPA buffer, mixed with the samples, and incubated for 3 hours, rotating at 4°C. Beads were washed four times with RIPA buffer and once with LiCl buffer [250 mM LiCl, 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, and 0.5% Na-DOC] and rinsed twice with TE buffer. After dilution to 100 μl with TE buffer, both input and IP fractions were decrosslinked overnight at 65°C, shaking at 1400 rpm. Following ribonuclease A treatment (1 μl of 10 mg/ml) at 37°C for 30 min, 5 μl of 10% SDS and 1.3 μl of proteinase K (10 mg/ml) were added, and the samples were incubated at 55°C, shaking at 1400 rpm for 2 hours. Last, the DNA was purified using the ChIP Clean and Concentrator kit (Zymo Research, no. D5205). Libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina and sequenced with NovaSeq 6000. At least three replicates were used per condition.

TT-seq data processing

The data were processed through the snakePipes mRNA-sequencing (mRNA-seq) pipeline (modified version of v.2.1.2). Adapters and low-quality bases (<Q20) were removed using Cutadapt (v.2.8; https://github.com/marcelm/cutadapt) (81). For all of the samples, reads were then mapped to the dm6 reference genome with STAR (v.2.7.4a), enforcing to only keep reads that map for at least 33% of their length (aligner opts: --outFilterMatchNminOverLread 0.33 --outFilterScoreMinOverLread 0.33) (82). We then used Samtools (v.1.20) to remove alignments with mapping quality (MAPQ) smaller than 7 and only kept proper pairs (83). The reduced MAPQ cutoff was chosen to accommodate for potential T > C mismatches in 4-sU–labeled reads (84). Then, reads per gene were counted using featureCounts (v.2.0.0) (85). The gene-level counts obtained from featureCounts were then used for differential expression analysis using DESeq2 (v.1.26.0) (86). Nascent and total RNA fractions of TT-seq data were analyzed separately for differential expression due to the large enrichment differences of RNAs in the two sample groups. Differentially expressed genes were identified on the basis of an FC cutoff of 1.5 and an adjusted P value of ≤0.05 using the standard DESeq2 workflow and normalization instead of spike-in–derived scale factors. Bigwig files were created from coverage values with the GenomicRanges (87) package and normalized using a scale factor derived from median spike-in counts per sample, which was additionally corrected for cross-contamination and antisense bias, as described previously (47, 49, 87).

mNET-seq data processing

The data were processed through the snakePipes mRNA-seq pipeline (modified version of v.2.1.2). Adapters and low-quality bases (<Q20) were removed using Cutadapt (v.2.8; https://github.com/marcelm/cutadapt) (81). For all of the samples, reads were then mapped to the dm6 reference genome with STAR (v.2.7.4a), enforcing to only keep reads that map for at least 33% of their length, and allowing only two mismatches. In addition, we allowed for introns of up to 500 kb to be mapped and enforced high alignment confidence (aligner opts: --outFilterMismatchNmax 2 --outFilterMultimapScoreRange 0 --alignIntronMax 500000 --outFilterMatchNminOverLread 0.33 --outFilterScoreMinOverLread 0.33) (82). We then used Samtools (v.1.20) to remove alignments with MAPQ smaller than 7 and only kept proper pairs (83). Then, reads per gene were counted using featureCounts (v.2.0.0) (85). We used the counts data to generate normalization factors with DESeq2 to normalize the mNET-seq data (86). We then reduced the coverage tracks to the last nucleotide incorporated by Pol II; all continued analysis uses this 1-nt coverage data. For combined bigwig tracks, the sum of all sequencing depth normalized coverage was used.

Promoter-proximal pause site detection

For all expressed transcriptional units (TUs), we extracted the coverage on the first exon (excluding the last five bases) and detected the local coverage maximum. This signal needed to exceed two times the median signal in this area to be detected as a pausing site. Pausing sites further downstream than 350 bp were discarded.

Calculation of the number of transcribed bases from TT-seq data

Using only uniquely aligning fragments with a positive inner mate distance, we calculated the number of transcribed bases (tbj) per sample j. tbj was calculated as the sum of coverage using all fragments smaller than 500 bp, which did not entirely overlap a Refseq annotated intron from dm6.

Estimation of productive initiation frequency I

We calculated the antisense bias corrected number of transcribed bases per condition tbicondition from TT-seq data on all expressed TUs, excluding the first exon from calculations as described (47, 49, 87). The productive initiation frequency I per TU Ii was calculated for each sample using the labeling time t = 5 min, length L, and a conversion factor to RNA amount per cell k (47, 49, 87)Ii=1k×tbisamplet×Li

Estimation of synthesis rate

Using sequencing depth (spike-in based) and cross-contamination–normalized counts from TT-seq data, we estimated labeled and unlabeled RNA amounts per TU as αi and βi, respectively. We used these parameters to calculate synthesis and degradation rates μi and λi, assuming first-order kinetics as previously described (88)αi=μiλi·(1−ⅇ−λit)αi+βi=μiλi

With labeling time t = 5 min, the RNA synthesis and degradation rates can be derived as followsλi(t)=−1t·log(βiαi+βi)μi(t)=(αi+β)·λi(t)

Estimation of apparent pause duration

The apparent pause duration was calculated integrating Pol II occupancy from mNET-seq P and the productive initiation rate I derived from TT-seq data as previously described (49). The apparent pause duration d as the residing time of a polymerase in the pausing window (±10 bp around the pausing site) per TU i was calculated as followsdi=s·∑±10PiIi

Processing of transcriptional rates

After obtaining the rate estimates per replicate using the spike-in–normalized TT-seq coverage, we performed variability stabilization using inter-quantile range normalization through the normalizeBetweenArrays function of the limma package (89). This yields well-normalized rate estimates in arbitrary units, which can be used to compare relative rate changes in different conditions. We then filtered out measurements for TUs that contain multiple genes, as well as genes that are shorter than 2 kb. In addition, we filtered out non–protein-coding genes and genes that are expressed lower than 5 reads per kilobase (RPK) in our experiments. FCs were calculated from these filtered values, and differences in rate-FCs between X chromosome and autosomes were tested using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test.

Metagene analysis of TT-seq and net-seq coverage

We used deepTools (v.3.5.5) to compute the coverage profiles for protein-coding genes with an RPK ≥ 5 based on accompanying TT-seq data (90). Only nonoverlapping genes located on autosomes or chromosome X were included. Coverage values were extracted using computeMatrix with a bin size of 1 bp. We used the import_deepToolsMat and convertToEnrichedHeatmapMat functions of the profileplyr package (https://github.com/RockefellerUniversity/profileplyr) to extract the profile values.

For each condition, we added a pseudocount that was set as 1% of the smallest nonzero value observed. From this matrix, we calculated the mean coverage per group (e.g., Chr X or autosomes) over all genes with a 5% trim on the mean to remove outliers and then calculated the 95% confidence intervals using bootstrapping, resampling 1000 times.

Detection of nascent enrichment

We used featureCounts (v.2.0.0) to obtain the counts for both exonic reads per gene and whole gene per sample. Intronic counts were calculated by subtracting the exonic counts from the whole-gene counts for each gene and sample. Intergenic counts were obtained from the featureCounts summary and the “Intergenic” category in the whole-gene counts. We then calculated the fraction of each count category out of the total counts (whole-gene + intergenic counts) per sample.

Sex determination of embryos TT-seq samples

We counted the number of reads aligned to Chr Y or the scaffolds related to Chr Y using Rsamtools (v.2.14.0) using the bam files for each sample (91). To account for differences in sequencing depth between samples, Y chromosomal read counts were normalized by dividing the number of Y chromosomal reads by the total number of mapped reads and then multiplied by 106.

Pol II initiation analysis around pausing sites

We used deepTools (v.3.5.5) computeMatrix reference-point function to aggregate the per gene coverage in a 120-bp window around the pausing site (±60 bp) with a bin size of 1 (90). This window size was chosen on the basis of the mean distance of the pausing site from the TSS. To avoid signals before the TSS, we choose the window range to be smaller in one direction than the mean pausing site distance from TSS. We calculated the mean coverage over chromosome X or autosomal genes with a 5% trim of the mean and then calculated the 95% confidence intervals using bootstrapping, resampling 1000 times. We then defined three regions relative to the pausing site, −60 to −21 bp upstream of the pausing site, ±20 bp around the pausing site, and +21 to +60 bp downstream of the pausing site, for which we aggregated the FC for each nucleotide within that area to compare promoter Pol II enrichment between chromosome X and autosomes.

RTI calculation

For each annotated TU that was longer than 2 kb and expressed in our TT-seq data, we defined a pretermination window, extending from 2 kb upstream of the TES to TES −1 bp, and a posttermination window that extended from TES to 2 kb downstream of the TES. We calculated the mean coverage per pre- and posttermination window from the spike-in–normalized median coverage bigwigs for the labeled TT-seq samples. Pretermination windows that had a mean coverage of less than 0.01 were adjusted to a minimal mean coverage of 0.01 to avoid divisions by near zero. The RTI was then calculated asRTI=TT2kbDownstream TESTT2kbUpstream TES

ChIP-seq data processing

Our data or previously published datasets were processed using the DNA-mapping pipeline of snakePipes (v.2.8.1) (92). Adapters were removed using TrimGalore (v.0.6.5; https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) and multimapping reads (mapq < 3) were filtered out using samtools (v.1.19.2) (83). Duplicate reads were marked with sambamba markdup (v.0.8.0) and removed if any sample in an experiment exceeded 30% duplicate reads (93). We kept only properly paired reads with a mapq higher than 3 for further analysis.

In the case of public data, we replicated the published analysis workflow as close as possible while using the same version of snakePipes (v.2.8.1) that was used for all other analysis, as such we removed duplicate reads in the analysis of H4K16ac ChIP-seq data from GSE130335 (26). For the H4K16ac, H3K36me3, and H3K79me2 ChIP-seq data from S2 cells, we used a H3 ChIP as input and for the H4K16ac data in embryos from GSE130335 (26), we used immunoglobulin G input to subtract the input signal from the ChIP with deepTools bamCompare (v.3.5.4) (90). Heatmaps of histone marks were plotted from H3 or input-subtracted bigwig files using deepTools (v.3.5.5) plotHeatmap and k-means clustering was performed using the –kmeans option (90).

Quantification of ChIP signal over gene bodies and promoter

Input or H3 subtracted replicates were merged with Wiggletools (v.1.2.11) mean and converted back to bigwig format using ucsc-wigtobigwig (v.472) (94). We used the GenomicRanges package to extract the mean coverage over a region spanning from the TSS of each TU to 350 bp downstream of TSS for the promoter signal and the position 351 bp downstream of TSS to the TES as the gene body signal (87). Not available (NA) values were excluded from the calculations.

ChIP-seq coverage and transcriptional rate correlation

The mean coverage over promoter or gene body regions was used to test for correlation between changes in the transcriptional rates and changes in the H4K16ac coverage. Before the calculation of FCs, a pseudocount of 1 was added to the mean signal over promoter or gene body. We used Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ, marked as r), as implemented in the cor.test() function from the stats package in R (v.4.2.3). Statistical significance was tested against the null hypothesis that the true Spearman correlation is zero (two-sided test).
