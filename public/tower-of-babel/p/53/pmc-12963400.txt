Integrated electrochemical porous solid electrolyte reactor and packed bed reactor for efficient synthesis of nylon-6 precursor
Authors: Shou-Kun Zhang, Yuge Feng, Shaoyun Hao, Ziang Xu, Sushanth Ashokkumar, Juan Wang, Haotian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe current production of cyclohexanone oxime (CyO), an essential precursor in the global nylon-6 industry, relies on processes that utilize toxic substrates and hazardous oxidizing agents. Here we present a continuous process and reactor design, with integrated electrochemical porous solid electrolyte reactor (PSER) and packed bed reactor (PBR), for an electrolyte-free, scalable, and efficient generation of high-purity CyO product from cyclohexanone (CyH), oxygen and ammonia. By electrochemically converting oxygen into high-purity hydrogen peroxide in a PSER, followed by a direct feeding into a downstream PBR for CyH ammoximation without any intermediate purification steps, we are able to obtain electrolyte-free CyO products. In an integrated 25-cm² PSER (six times larger than our previous design) and a titanium silicate-1 filled PBR system, we successfully demonstrate high CyO product selectivity of 97.2%, high yield of 93.6%, as well as high peroxide utilization efficiency of 96.3%. By operating the PSER under 125 mA/cm2, up to 28.3 mmol/h of CyO production rate can be achieved. Efficient conversions of a broad range of other aliphatic and aromatic ketone substrates are also demonstrated in this integrated PSER-PBR reactor system.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Chemical engineering, Chemical engineering

Introduction

Cyclohexanone oxime (CyO), a vital precursor for producing nylon-6, is globally manufactured on a scale of approximately six million tons annually1. This compound is synthesized primarily through the rapid reaction between cyclohexanone (CyH) and hydroxylamine (NH2OH), as illustrated in Fig. 1. In traditional CyO production, the major challenges stem from the instability of the intermediate NH2OH, which is prone to hazardous decomposition2. To mitigate these risks, processes typically incorporate acid stabilization using sulfuric or phosphoric acid and subsequent neutralization steps, which not only increase production costs but also result in the generation of low-value byproducts (Fig. 1a)3,4. Recent research has increasingly focused on the in-situ generation of NH2OH and its direct conversion with CyH to CyO, thereby simplifying the manufacturing process and reducing byproduct formation. Typically, two main pathways, including nitrogen oxides reduction and ammonia oxidation have been identified for synthesizing hydroxylamine (NH2OH) using various nitrogen sources and the in-situ production of CyO (Fig. 1a–c). The first pathway involves the chemical or electrochemical reduction of NOx, such as NO, NO2−, or NO3− from different nitrogen sources5–15. These methods, however, often suffer from over-reduction to undesired ammonia (NH3), presenting significant challenges in achieving satisfactory conversion rates and efficiencies towards NH2OH and CyO production rate. To achieve a higher current density, additional salts are required as NOx is consumed, which will lead to an increase in the separation process steps and investment costs. Additionally, nitrogen oxides predominantly stem from the oxidation of NH3 in industry, suggesting the low overall energy efficiency of this synthetic route. The second pathway, which directly utilizes NH3 to synthesize NH2OH in a single oxidation step, could significantly improve the energy efficiency by avoiding multiple oxidation and reduction steps16,17, and promise a greener and more sustainable pathway towards CyO synthesis.

One of the primary challenges in industrializing the NH3 oxidation pathway for CyO production revolves around the selection and method of applying the oxidants18. Among different types of oxidants, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) emerges as a particularly promising candidate, owing not only to its ability to facilitate efficient and rapid conversion of NH3 to NH2OH and subsequent in-situ reactions, but also due to its inherent environmental benignity and no harmful byproducts19–21. However, the transportation, handling, and storage of concentrated H2O2 from centralized production facilities to end-users pose significant challenges. Given its rapid decomposition, especially under high concentrations, H2O2 is typically stabilized with acids (e.g., phosphoric or phthalic), chelating agent (e.g., nitrilotriacetates, citrates), or colloidal silica, etc.22,23. These stabilizers not only introduce impurities in the chemical synthesis of CyO but also potentially limit the reactivity and the stability of the catalyst, reducing the reactor’s lifespan due to corrosion24. Additionally, H2O2 is mainly produced through the energy-intensive anthraquinone process, which emits roughly three tons of carbon dioxide per ton of H2O2, accounting for hydrogen usage, on-site energy consumption, and off-site energy losses due to decomposition25. In 2022, Lewis, Liu, and Hutchings introduced a groundbreaking method, leveraging the in-situ generation of H2O2 from a mixture of H2 and O2 using a gold-palladium catalyst for the oxidation of NH3 during the CyH ammoximation process, which presented a promising approach to mitigating the challenges of long-distance oxidant transport (Fig. 1c)26. Despite their innovative reaction process design, the high-pressure requirements for the mixed H2 and O2 gases, the expensive Au-Pd catalyst, and the relatively low oxime production rate (0.68–1.4 mmol/h) still need further improvement for large-scale applications. Very recently, Yuan and co-workers described an electrocatalytic ORR for CyO synthesis by purging oxygen gas into the NH4HCO3 solution under a relatively low current density, which has an inherent limitation due to the oxygen mass transfer issue (Fig. 1c)27.

Here, we present a continuous process and reactor design, integrating an electrochemical porous solid electrolyte reactor (PSER) with a packed bed reactor (PBR), for a direct, continuous, and highly efficient generation of high-purity CyO from CyH ammoximation (Fig. 1d). The PSER electrochemically generated electrolyte- and impurity-free H2O2 solutions with tunable concentrations directly from oxygen and water. Meanwhile, the downstream PBR, a widely used industrial reactor for heterogeneous catalytic systems, facilitated the subsequent ammoximation of CyH28. The electrochemically generated H2O2 (eH2O2) was directly co-fed with NH3 and CyH into a PBR filled with TS-1 catalyst, a widely used commercialized catalyst for ketone ammoximation reactions in industry3, enabling highly selective and high-yield production of CyO. Compared to batch reactors and continuous-stirred tank reactors (or continuous slurry reactors) reported for CyO production29, our PSER-PBR system offers several key advantages, including continuous operation, lower operating costs, a more compact design, and the elimination of catalyst-liquid separation, which simplifies downstream processing. Importantly, no impurity of sulfuric acid, phosphoric acid or other H2O2 stabilizers are involved in the entire process, eliminating the need for extensive downstream separation steps.

Through our PSER-PBR system, CyO can be continuously produced with a high CyH conversion of 96.3%, CyO selectivity of 97.2%, CyO yield of 93.6%, and H2O2 utilization efficiency of 96.3%, at a H2O2 electrolysis current density of 12 mA/cm2 in the PSER. The current density can be increased to an industrially-relevant level of 125 mA/cm2 (a total of 3.1 A with 25 cm2 electrode), where CyO was produced at a rate of 28.3 mmol/h (1.132 mmol/(h·cm2); 3.2 g/h) from our lab-scale prototype reactor while delivering a 68.1% CyH conversion, 96.9% CyO selectivity, 65.9% CyO yield, and 60.6% H2O2 utilization efficiency. The reduced performance under higher current condition could come from the increased amount of O2 bubbles generated from the decomposition of H2O2, which decreases the reactant remaining time on the surface of TS-1 catalyst. Remarkably, this CyO production rate (at 125 mA/cm2, FE of 50.3%, 1.132 mmol/(h·cm2)) greatly surpassed prior benchmarks (Supplementary Table 1), suggesting its potential for practical applications. A techno-economic analysis (TEA) demonstrated the PSER-PBR strategy for CyO production is profitable with a rather low cost of 2196 $/ton under a 0.1 $/kWh electricity fee (CyO market price is around 9700 $/ton)30. The use of cheapest nitrogen source—NH3 (except N2) and low cost of H2O2 production from our PESR reactor are the main reason for benefits.

Results

PBR optimization

To ensure the seamless integration of the two units in our system, we first optimized the PBR and electrochemical H2O2 synthesis sections independently, focusing on the operating range of mutual interest. As shown in Fig. 2a, our system utilizes a PBR with a 6.4 mm diameter, packed with commercial TS-1 catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 1), which is renowned for its effectiveness in ammoximation reactions. Within the reactor, TS-1 catalyzes the reaction between NH3 and H2O2 to form active NH2OH, which subsequently reacts with CyH to yield CyO. Key ammoximation factors, including reactant concentrations, reaction rate (governed by intrinsic kinetics, temperature, catalyst loading, etc.), and residence time (determined by the reactor length and feeding rates), are analyzed in our PBR system. During the process, the H2O2 utilization efficiency is determined by KMnO4 titration (based on excess H2O2); the substrate (CyH) conversion, target product (CyO) selectivity, yield and production rate are analyzed by 1H-NMR spectroscopy; the NH3 and other nitrogen sources were analyzed by GC and UV–vis (see detailed parameter definitions in “Methods”, Supplementary Figs. 2–15).

The optimization reaction started with a mixture of 0.2 M CyH, 0.4 M NH3 · H2O and 0.26 M commercial H2O2 (cH2O2) at a constant flow rate of 0.5 mL/min into a PBR containing 1.5 × g TS-1 at 80 °C with a two-hour continuous detection, resulting a stable ammoximation performance after 0.5 h with a CyH conversion up to 91.7%, a CyO selectivity over 96.0%, production rate around 5.0 mmol/h and yield of 88.0%, and an approximately H2O2 utilization efficiency of 74.8% (based on 0.26 M H2O2 usage) (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 16). Without specific instruction, we selected the one-hour reaction for further comparison. Although the reaction scale increased from 0.2 moles to 0.8 moles, high performance was maintained with a CyO selectivity of above 96%, a CyO concentration up to 0.23 M, and high production rate of 18.2 mmol/h, a slightly decreased CyH conversion from 91.7% to 85.9% and a CyO yield from 88.0% to 84.3% (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Figs. 16–18). It was noteworthy that the pre-added carbon source CyH goes into three parts: the target product CyO, the detected unreacted CyH, and the missing part perhaps coming from the 1H-NMR measurement error, the evaporation of CyH, and the precipitation of over-concentrated CyO. The analysis comes from the optimization of temperature when using a high input concentration of 0.8 M CyH. Theoretically, higher temperatures enhance reaction rates, leading to greater conversion and yield. As expected, the CyO yield was low at approximately 36.8% at 25 °C, increased to above 78.9% at 40 °C, and reached a maximum yield of 84.3% at 80 °C (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20). Notably, in addition to the detected hydrocarbons CyH and CyO, Fig. 2d shows a “carbon loss” fraction (yellow column), initially attributed to measurement errors from 1H-NMR analysis observing no other byproducts (Supplementary Fig. 2). This “carbon loss” increased from 1.6% at 80 °C (possibly due to the measurement error) to 8.7% at 40 °C and further to 14.6% at 25 °C, showing the factor of increasing CyO deposition (mainly because of the decreased CyO solubility) (Supplementary Fig. 21). Although 40 °C was sufficient for a high yield of CyO, we used 80 °C in subsequent tests to minimize the loss fraction unless otherwise specified.

Catalyst loading is also a crucial factor in the PBR system. As shown in Fig. 2e, the CyO yield remained stable at around 80% when reducing TS-1 usage from 1.5 to 0.1 × g, with the highest catalytic efficiency of 161 mmol·h−1·gcat−1 achieved at 0.1 × g TS-1, albeit with a slightly decreased yield of 74.3% (Supplementary Figs. 22–24). To assess the impact of catalyst diffusion within the PBR, we mixed 0.2 × g TS-1 with varying amounts of structurally similar SiO2, presenting a different PBR length from 4 cm to 14 cm. The result confirmed that TS-1 diffusion does not significantly affect catalytic performance (Supplementary Figs. 25 and 26).

To further enhance the CyO productivity, we screened commonly used alcohols from the nylon-6 production industry to improve CyH and CyO solubility (Fig. 2f, g and Supplementary Fig. 27). Among them, t-BuOH demonstrated a superior performance, yielding 75%, slightly lower than in water but still the most effective among tested alcohols. With t-BuOH, the CyO yield increased from 75% at 0.8 M CyH to over 80% at 3.2 M CyH (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 28). Therefore, to optimize performance, we used H2O as the solvent for tests with CyH concentrations below 0.8 M and t-BuOH for the concentrations above 0.8 M. A slight improvement in performance was observed with increased of H2O2 concentration. As shown in Fig. 2h, i, when processing 3.2 M CyH, reducing the H2O2: CyH ratio from 1.3:1 to 1:1 led to a slightly decreased yield from 80% to 71% but an improved H2O2 utilization efficiency from 64% to 73% (Fig. 2e, f). At 80 °C, when 4.1 M H2O2/CyH (1:1) reacted with 7.6 MNH3 · H2O, we could achieve a conversion of 82.8%, a yield of 82.1%, H2O2 utilization efficiency of 82.1% and productivity of 91.8 mmol/h (10.4 g/h) (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 28d, e). To maximize H2O2 utilization efficiency and improve overall Faradaic efficiency (FE), we maintained an H2O2/CyH ratio of 1:1 in subsequent tests unless otherwise specified.

The scaled-up PSER for H2O2 production

Having a clear understanding of the PBR parameters, we next investigated the electrochemical 2e−-oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) for H2O2 production. To guarantee the high purity of H2O2, we adopted the three-chamber PSER, where the middle chamber was separated by an anion exchange membrane (AEM) and a cation exchange membrane (CEM). In the PSER, as illustrated in Fig. 3a, the middle chamber was filled with PSE instead of traditional liquid electrolyte, significantly reducing the ionic impurities in the H2O2 product. On the cathode side, a gas diffusion layer (GDL) bearing carbon black served as the cathodic catalyst, facilitating the reduction of gaseous O2 into HO2− species via a two-electron transfer and further avoiding the gas mass transfer issue. The generated HO2− penetrated through the AEM into the middle chamber. On the anode side, a commercial IrO2 onto GDL was used to produce H+ through water oxidation, which subsequently reacted with the HO2− to form H2O2. The combination of the stable and low cost carbon black catalyst and the long-term operability of commercial IrO2 substantially reduced H2O2 production costs. Additionally, the tunable current density allowed for on-demand H2O2 generation at the required concentration, eliminating the need for dilution from high commercial concentrations. Most importantly, since H2O2 was produced with high purity, no additional separation steps were required between the PSER and PBR.

To enable direct integration with the PBR, the H2O2 production rate in the PSER need to be closely matched with the H2O2 input rate required for the ammoximation reaction, as discussed in the previous section. To ensure sufficient supply of H2O2, we scaled-up the PSER reactor to a reaction area of 25 cm2 and evaluated its electrochemical performance (Supplementary Fig. 29). We optimized the electrochemically in-situ generated H2O2 concentration by tunning the current density with a fixed a DI water flow rate of 0.5 mL/min in our PSER for better integration with CyH ammoxidation reaction in the PBR. At a current density of 12 mA/cm2, the PSER continuously produced H2O2 at a concentration of approximately 0.2 M, corresponding to a production rate of 5.6 mmol/h, with a FE of 96.0% at a cell voltage of only 1.8 V. Increasing the current density to 56 mA/cm2 yielded 0.8 M H2O2 (23 mmol/h) with a high FE of 87.9%. Further scaling to 125 mA/cm2 (the current goes to 3.1 ampere) resulted in a high H2O2 concentration of 1.6 M with a H2O2 production rate of 45 mmol/h at 2.7 V, although with a slightly decreased FE of 75.5% possibly due to the high product concentration and rising temperature (Fig. 3b–d). Although higher H2O2 concentration (up to 30 wt%, 9.8 M) in PSER reactor has been demonstrated in our previous work31, we still use 1.6 M eH2O2 to perform the following CyH ammoximation without regulating the water flow rate to coordinate feeding rate of NH3 and CyH (Supplementary Fig. 30).

The PSER-PBR system for CyH ammoximation with electrochemically in-situ generated H2O2

Having established the electrochemical H2O2 generation part and the following CyH ammoximation with H2O2, we further coupled the PSER with the PBR to validate the complete on-site ammoximation process. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, the tandem reaction was initially investigated with co-feeding electrochemically generated 0.2 M H2O2 (eH2O2) at 12 mA/cm2, 0.2 M CyH (in water), and 0.4 M NH3 · H2O with the same flow rate into a PBR with 1.5 × g TS-1. High performance was achieved (Fig. 3e–g): 96.3% H2O2 utilization efficiency, 96.3% CyH conversion, 93.6% CyO yield, and 97.2% CyO selectivity. Notably, this surpassed the performance obtained using excess commercial cH2O2 (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 16): 74.8% H2O2 utilization efficiency (calculation based on 1.30 equivalents of cH2O2), 91.7% CyH conversion, 88.0% CyO yield, and 96.0% CyO selectivity. According to previous experimental results (Fig. 2h, i), more amounts of oxidants were beneficial for the CyH ammoximation, however, the electrochemically generated H2O2 (1.0 eq eH2O2) by PSER achieved even better performance than that with commercial H2O2 (1.3 eq cH2O2). This result evidenced that the electrochemically in-situ generated pure eH2O2 is more reaction-positive than cH2O2 bearing stabilizer, which could partially sacrifice the oxidation ability or interfere the activity of the catalyst. A more direct contraction is from the reaction of 0.8 M CyH (in t-BuOH) with 1 equivalent of cH2O2 (Fig. 2h, i) and 1 equivalent of eH2O2 at 56 mA/cm2 current density (Fig. 3e–g), as below: c63.7% vs. e81.2% H2O2 utilization efficiency, c64.3% vs. e81.2% CyH conversion, c63.6% vs. e79.0% CyO yield, c13.9 mmol/h vs. e16.9 mmol/h CyO production rate and c99.0% vs. e97.4% CyO selectivity.

When applying an industrial current density of 125 mA/cm2 (3.1 A) in PSER, the obtained eH2O2 (1.6 M) showed a similar CyH ammoximation performance with 1.6 M cH2O2: c69.0% vs. e60.6% H2O2 utilization efficiency, c69.6% vs. e68.1% CyH conversion, c67.8% vs. e65.9% CyO yield, c29.6 mmol/h vs. e28.3 mmol/h CyO production rate and c97.4% vs. e96.9% CyO selectivity. The decreased performance may come from the higher decomposition rate of concentrated eH2O2 into lots of O2 bubbles over cH2O2 bearing stabilizer, which could lead to the contact opportunity between catalyst and liquid and also the reduced amounts of oxidants—H2O2 (Supplementary Fig. 31 and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Another important parameter, total Faradaic efficiency (namely, an electron-to-oxime efficiency) was also calculated, achieving a high FECyO of up to 92.4% with 0.2 M eH2O2 at 12 mA/cm2, a good FECyO value of 72.5% with 0.8 M eH2O2 at 56 mA/cm2, and a still acceptable FECyO of 50.3% with 1.6 M eH2O2 at 125 mA/cm2 (Fig. 3g), which outperformed the overall CyO Faraday efficiency of electrochemically generated NH2OH from NOx (NO/NO2−/NO3−) reduction (Table S1)5–13. Although previous reports showed high FECyO (26% to 68%) when applying low current density (<60 mA/cm2), the CyO production rates were low from 0.02 mmol/(h·cm2) to 0.28 mmol/(h·cm2). Previous studies also showed the improved CyO production rates from 0.19 to 0.59 mmol/(h·cm2) at high current density from 200 mA/cm2 to 500 mA/cm2, but delivered a lower FECyO (1.5%–20%) than 50.3% in our PSER-PBR system at 125 mA/cm2 with 1.13 mmol/(h·cm2). High current density would exacerbate the competitive electroreduction of NOx into NH4 instead of NH2OH, which further reduced the CyH ammoximation performance5–13. Very recently, Liu and Chen reported high FECyO (>90%) during the NOxRR process above 200 mA/cm2, however, minor by-products of NH3 need to be removed14,32. Compared to blubbing O2 into NH4HCO3 at a relative low current density (<15 mA/cm2)27, our one-pass and electrolyte-free PSER design for 2e−-ORR without any O2 mass transfer issue showed its powerfulness in industry.

The PSER-PBR system application evaluation for other ketone oximes production and equipment stability

Beyond cyclohexanone oxime, other organic oximes also play essential roles in chemical and industrial synthesis. To explore the versatility of the PSER-PBR system, we tested its performance in producing various oximes (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figs. 32–36). Specifically, other cycloketones (5-membered and 7-membered ketones), acyclic ketones, and benzyl ketones were reacted with eH2O2 to yield their corresponding oximes. As shown in Fig. 4b, high selectivity was achieved across all tested substrates, exceeding 98%. Acetone and 3-pentanone exhibited high conversion and yield of above 80%. cycloketones and PhCHO showed relatively lower yields and conversions, and further optimization of reaction conditions may be necessary for these species.

Delivering good stability of this integrated system is more challenging than each of the independent reactor. Since the PSER was enlarged to 25 cm2 in our work, it was more challenging to produce H2O2 stably than the smaller size of reactors we demonstrated before33–35. Encouragingly, the FE of H2O2 maintained above 80% at 60 mA/cm2 for over 100 h, with cell voltages stabilizing at 2.0 V (Supplementary Fig. 37). When coupled with a PBR, the system maintained a H2O2 utilization efficiency of around 80%, consistent CyO yield of around 80%, CyO selectivity above 95%, CyO production rate of 16 mmol/h for 30 h (Fig. 4c). This represents 0.384 mol of total ketone oxime production every day, which is higher than previous reports, such as 0.216 mol/day9 and 0.158 mol/day10 using NOx, 0.032 mol/day27 using NH4HCO3 and 0.034 mol/day26 using NH3. Over extended operation, the TS-1, a robust commercial catalyst, may still experience activity loss and require regeneration, traditionally necessitating process interruptions for catalyst separation and reactivation29,36. However, our modular design ensures continuous operation by allowing seamless PBR replacement. The used TS-1 could be replaced, reactivated, and reused, analogous to swapping a fully charged battery in electric device, eliminating downtime for recharging.

Moreover, to clarify the economic potential of the PSER-PBR setup for producing CyO, we performed a preliminary techno-economic analysis (TEA) based on the performance of our PSER-PBR reactor at 125 mA/cm2 (Fig. 4c and Supplementary TEA Note). We also considered our previous PSER TEA for H2O2 production31 and Shi’s work27 for evaluation of CyO economic benefits. Our strategy was demonstrated to produce CyO with a rather low cost of 2196 $/ton under a 0.1 $/kWh electricity fee (2533 $/ton, even with 0.2 $/kWh), which is very profitable in consideration the market price of CyO (~9700 $/ton)30. The main reason comes from the use of cheapest nitrogen source—NH3 and low cost of H2O2 producing strategy from our PESR reactor.

Discussion

In summary, we designed a PSER-PBR system for the production of the nylon-6 precursor-CyO. Our system features a 25 cm2 scaled-up PSER (6 times larger than our previous design of 4 cm2) for the on-site H2O2 synthesis via 2e−-ORR from water and oxygen under an industrial current density as high as 125 mA/cm2 (3.1 A). The eH2O2 was directly fed to a PBR with NH3 · H2O and CyH to obtain electrolyte-free CyO. This coupled reactor achieves a cyclohexanone oxime (CyO) production rate of 28.3 mmol/h (1.13 mmol/(h·cm2)), which is higher than that of existing methods. Furthermore, it boasts optimized selectivity, conversion rate, and oxidant utilization, all exceeding 95%. Unlike traditional continuous stirred-tank and batch reactors, the PBR component functions as an autonomous unit that can be initiated and halted without detriment, which ensuring the straightforward replacement of the ammoximation catalyst, enhancing operational flexibility and efficiency. This reactor also accommodates a diverse spectrum of alkyl or aryl ketone monomers for ammoximation, indicating that further investigation could uncover its broader industrial relevance for various oxidative transformation processes. By using one-pass PSER-PBR system for cascade reaction, our research described slightly higher H2O2 utilization efficiency than commercial H2O2, successfully avoided the liquid electrolyte interruption and electrolyte separation in commonly used electrochemical devices and greatly reduced the risk of potential side reaction from the reactants recycling. Moreover, according to the TEA, the use of cheapest nitrogen source (NH3) and electrochemically in-situ generated H2O2 could largely decrease the CyO production cost to 2196.9 $/ton (0.1 $/kWh electricity cost)

Methods

Chemicals and materials

Titanium Silicalite-1 (TS-1) was purchased from ACS Material. was purchased from Sigma. Oxygen (>99.9%) was purchased form Airgas. Solid electrolyte (Amberchrom®50WX8, 200–400 mesh, hydrogen form), DMSO, 0.1 N KMnO4 standardized solution, Nafion 117 solution (5%), methanol, ethanol, isopropanol (IPA), and tert-butyl alcohol (t-BuOH) were all purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. D2O (99.9% D) was purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc. SiO2 (70–230 mesh) was purchased from Beantown Chemical. Sigracet 28 BC gas diffusion layer (GDL) and Nafion 1110 was purchased from Fuel Cell Store. Carbon black (BP2000) was purchased from Cabot Corporation. Anion exchange membrane (AEM) (Sustainion® X37-50 Grade T) and IrO2 on carbon paper was purchased from Dioxide Materials.

2e−-ORR catalyst preparation

15 mg BP2000, 120 μL 5% Nafion 117, and 4 mL IPA were added to a 10 mL glass vial and sonicated for 1 h. Subsequently, this ink was sprayed onto a GDL (6.0 × 6.0 cm2) via a spray gun. Finally, it was dried and cut into 5.0 × 5.0 cm2 (density of ORR catalyst is around 0.41 mg/cm2) species as cathode.

Ammoximation catalyst (TS-1) characterization

The three-chamber solid electrolyte cell was composed of a cathode chamber, an anode chamber, and a middle layer filled with the mixture of solid electrolyte, which was separated by an anion exchange membrane and a cation exchange membrane. The cathode chamber was fed with an O2 flow rate of 20 mL/min and the anode chamber was fed with a 1.0 mL/min DI water flow rate.
