Impedance Pneumography in Healthy and Sick Children Aged Between 2 and 35 Months
Authors: Nicole Beydon, Enora Le Roux, Priscilla Boizeau, Iulia Ioan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRationalImpedance pneumography (IP) non‐invasively records tidal breathing flow‐volume curves over long durations. It measures the ratio of time to peak tidal expiratory flow relative to the total expiration time (T PTEF /T E ) and the Expiratory Variability Index (EVI), which assesses the variability of expiratory flow in the early phase of expiration with a decrease in case of clinical symptoms of bronchial obstruction.ObjectiveTo establish reference values for EVI and T PTEF /T E , recorded at home during sleep using IP.MethodsWe conducted a bi‐centre prospective study to recorded EVI and T PTEF /T E over at least 4 h of sleep, using IP measurement Ventica® (Icare Finland Oy, Finland), in healthy and sick (non‐wheezy and wheezy) children, aged 2 to 35 months.ResultsAmong the 67 healthy children included, 59 were successfully recorded by parents (median [Q1; Q3] (range) age 15.4 [8.6; 26.0] (2.3−35.9) months) and showed median [Q1; Q3] (5th; 95th percentiles) EVI of 16.7 [14.9; 18.9] (12.7; 20.7); and T PTEF /T E (n = 51) of 0.18 [0.16; 0.22] (0.13; 0.26).In 9 non‐wheezy children with respiratory symptoms and 12 wheezers, EVI (13.5 [9.6; 14.6] and 7.0 [6.0; 12.5], respectively) and T PTEF /T E (0.16 [0.13; 0.18] and 0.12 [0.09; 0.18], respectively) were significantly lower compared to healthy.The values of EVI and T PTEF /T E in 68 healthy and sick children with both measurements (12 missing data) were concordant (> or < 5th percentile) in 62 cases (91%).ConclusionsWe provide reference values of EVI and T PTEF /T E measured at home by parents using IP in young children. IP shows lower values in wheezy and non‐wheezy sick young children.

Introduction

Wheezing is a common symptom in infancy. In Europe, the prevalence of wheezing before the age of two is 30.7%, with recurrence occurring in 12.3% of cases [1]. The diagnosis and management of wheezing in these young children are hindered by the lack of objective tools to assess reversible airflow limitation, such as those available in older children [2, 3]. Non‐invasive lung function tests (LFTs) are easy to perform in routine in awake children as young as 3 years old, but testing children between 2 and 3 years of age can be challenging [4, 5]. In contrast, infant LFTs can only be conducted in a few highly specialized centres and are not performed routinely [6]. Obtaining objective LFT indexes is crucial in this young age group, as diagnostic and outcome assessment often relies on parents' reports of symptoms, which may be affected by recall bias and imprecision [7, 8].

In recent years, impedance pneumography (IP), a non‐invasive technique to record tidal breathing flow‐volume curves, has been tested in both healthy and sick infants and young children [9, 10, 11, 12]. The ratios of time or volume to peak tidal expiratory flow (T PTEF and V PTEF ) relative to the total time or volume of expiration (T E and V E ) recorded using IP have been found to correlate significantly with the same indexes recorded using a pneumotachograph (PNT) during mouth breathing in awake young children or sleeping infants [10, 12]. Another flow‐volume curve index, derived from overnight sleep recordings in young children, has been developed to evaluate the variability of expiratory flow in the early phase of expiration, which is expected to decrease in case of bronchoconstriction [13]. This index, called the Expiratory Variability Index (EVI), was found to decrease in the presence of clinical symptoms of bronchial obstruction and to increase when these symptoms were alleviated following specific treatment in preschool children [14, 15, 16]. In two studies, each including 40 healthy preschool children, the median EVI values recorded were similar (17.3 and 17.4), with 5th percentiles of 12.0 and 12.1 [14, 15]. In one of these studies, there was a weak but significant correlation between age and EVI (r = 0.28, p = 0.005) raising questions about the applicability of these reference values to younger children [15]. Given the limited data on EVI in healthy infants and the challenges in obtaining routine non‐invasive LFTs at this age, we aimed to establish reference EVI values for healthy children before 3 years of age. We also recorded T E and T PTEF using IP to calculate their ratio. Finally, we compared EVI and T PTEF /T E results obtained in healthy and sick children.

Methods

Subjects

For this two‐centre prospective study (NCT04702620) we recruited (September 2022 – December 2023) children through advertising in nurseries and by word of mouth, few wheezy inpatients were also recruited. All children must be between 2 and 35 months, have a gestational age of at least 37 weeks and birth weight above the third percentile. Additional inclusion criteria for healthy (H) children can be found in the Data Supplements. We also included some symptomatic healthy (SH) children who met the same criteria, except for presenting with acute non‐wheezy respiratory symptoms at the time of inclusion. Lastly, we included wheezy (W) children of the same age, gestational term, and birth weight, provided they had no significant diseases other than wheezing observed by the investigator at the time of inclusion (within 12 h prior to the IP recording).

Exclusion criteria for all subjects were as follows: cutaneous disease hampering electrodes use; patients using implanted and/or connected devices, including ECG electrodes.

The parents or legal guardians were informed of the study and signed a written consent form. The study was approved by an institutional review board (Comité de protection des personnes Sud‐Est I, 2020‐62).

Impedance Pneumography (IP) Recording (See Details in the Data Supplements)

The IP recordings (Ventica®, Icare Finland Oy, Finland) involve measuring the electrical impedance of the thorax by applying a small high‐frequency current through one pair of electrodes (one on each side of the thorax) and measuring the resulting voltage signal with another pair of electrodes (one on each inner surface of the arms, positioned in front of the thorax electrodes). The technique allows the measurements of the time to peak tidal expiratory flow (T PTEF ), the total time of expiration (T E ) and the variability of impedance (Expiratory Variability Index, EVI) in the early phase of expiration (between 15% and 45% of exhaled volume). We used the Ventica reading software (Ventica® Analytics v2.1.0) for data analysis, which automatically cleans data from breathing cycles with poor‐quality signals or from periods with child's movement and gives the EVI over a minimum of 5 h. This minimum duration is deemed appropriate as it is achievable in 90% of preschool and school‐aged children. However, since the signal‐to‐noise ratio may differ in younger children, we also calculated EVI from recordings with a shorter validated duration (> 4 h). To increase success rate of recordings, we invited the parents of healthy children to repeat the recording on two separate nights [16].

We extracted raw data (SciPy), presented as 5‐min intervals measurements, from individual files of the entire night recording. We selected the 5‐min intervals with a mean respiratory rate (RR) and heart rate (HR) equal to the entire night mean ± 1.645 standard deviations (SD), and calculated mean T E , T PTEF , and T PTEF /T E values across all selected 5 min intervals.

The recordings were conducted at home, except for some hospitalized wheezy children who were recorded on the last night before discharge. All parents performed the recording after being instructed on how to place the four electrodes and connect them to the device. They were asked to report any issues with the device or any side effects.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables are given as mean (SD) or median first and third quartile [Q1; Q3] depending on distribution. Between‐group comparisons of continuous variables were analysed using the non‐parametric Wilcoxon two‐sample test. Qualitative variables are reported as proportions with their percentages and compared using the Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

The repeatability (agreement) and reliability of EVI over two nights have been studied using Bland and Altman graphs and the intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICC), respectively [17]. Two SD of the EVI mean difference (coefficient of repeatability, CR) are also provided to define the limits above and below an individual measurement within which 95% of second measurements will lie.

Univariate and multivariate analysis were conducted to study the relationships between EVI or T PTEF /T E and children characteristics or clinical features.

Results

We recruited 95 children (53 females and 42 males) with a median [Q1; Q3] age of 12.7 months [6.3; 25.7]. After excluding 15 children (median age 23.8 months [6.4; 31.8]) due to either the absence of recordings, insufficient validated recording time (< 4 h) or non‐compliance with an inclusion criterion, the analysis included 80 children (46 females and 34 males) with a median age 12.1 months [6.3; 24.0] (Figure E1), not significantly different from that of the 15 children not analysed (p = 0.07). Among them, 59 were non‐symptomatic healthy (H) children, 12 were non‐wheezy symptomatic but otherwise healthy (HS) children (all had rhino‐pharyngitis, and 5 had bronchitis), and 9 were wheezy (W) children. Results in Healthy Children

The repeatability and reliability of EVI measurements were assessed in 17 H children, with repeated recordings taken with a maximum interval of 3 days. Among the 11 children with two recordings exceeding 5 h, the mean bias (limits of agreement; CR) between the two EVI results was minimal (0.4, (−2.7 to 3.5; 1.5), and the between‐EVI concordance was good (ICC: 0.84 (95%CI: 0.54;0.95)) (Figure E2a). This concordance remained good after including the 6 children with at least one 4‐to‐5 h recording (ICC: 0.77 (95%CI: 0.48 to 0.91)), with a similar agreement (limits of agreement; CR) (0.5, (−3.5 to 4.5; 2.0)(Figure E2b). Given the good repeatability of EVI, we included all EVI measurements obtained from recordings of at least 4 h in H child to determine the reference values. When two measurements were available for a child, the recording with the highest value was selected. Results of EVI are shown in Table 2.

In the 59 H children, mean (SD) [5th; 95th percentiles] EVI were 16.8 (2.5) [12.7;20.7] (Table 2, Figure 2). The univariate model did not show a significant relationship between EVI and sex, birth weight, current tobacco exposure, history of eczema, or Vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy or after birth. However, ethnicity (Caucasian vs. non‐Caucasian) and asthma in parents or siblings showed significant relationships with EVI (p < 0.05) which was confirmed in the multivariate analysis (Caucasian, beta coefficient −1.81 [−3.52; −0.10], p = 0.038; familial asthma, 1.27 [0.01; 2.53], p = 0.048). The generalized linear model did not found any effect of sex (p = 0.55), age (p = 0.14), age adjusted for sex (p = 0. 09), height (p = 0.14), and height adjusted for sex (p = 0.09) on EVI value.

In 51 H children, for whom raw data files were available, RR, T E , T PTEF and T PTEF /T E were recorded. As expected, age and height were significantly and negatively correlated with RR (r = −0.72 and r = −0.74; respectively, both p < 0.0001), but not with T E , T PTEF or T PTEF /T E (all p values > 0.05). The linear model did not show any correlation between T PTEF /T E and sex (p = 0.60), height (p = 0.70), age (p = 0.40), and height or age adjusted on sex (p = 0.60 and p = 0.20, respectively). Median [Q1; Q3] T PTEF /T E was 0.18 [0.16;0.22] with 5th and 95th percentiles of 0.13 and 0.26 (Table 2, figure 2).

Discussion

In this prospective study we found that the measurement of tidal breathing indices during at least 4 h of night sleep was feasible from 2 months of age, with a success rate of 90% after a single night recording. We did not find any effect of age, height or sex on the EVI values in H children, which were not different from those record in preschool children (approximately 17 for the mean and 12 for the 5th percentile). The T PTEF /T E was concordant with EVI in most cases, and both indices were significantly impaired in sick children, supporting the ability of IP to detect disease.

EVI has been previously studied in preschool children. After the age of 3, most children can cooperate with non‐invasive lung function techniques, making the youngest ones the most challenging to test [4]. As with any lung function index, reference values must be established for the age range of the patients being measured, which explains our interest in conducting this study.

The advantage of using IP to record tidal breathing in children under 3 years of age is enormous compared to classical tidal breathing recordings using a face mask and a PNT. The main advantages include the absence of additional dead space or PNT resistance to the respiratory system (no alteration of respiratory control), no risk of leaks from the face mask, no need for compensation between cold, dry inspired gas and warm, vapour‐saturated exhaled gas, no volume drift, and no need for signal alignment [18]. Moreover, the possibility to perform the measurement during long period, gives the opportunity to study the respiratory system of young children and gather valuable information.

First, the feasibility of IP measurements is high, and we demonstrated that young age is not a factor associated with a lower success rate. In predominantly preschool‐aged children, the feasibility of overnight IP recording ranged from 75% to 97% [14, 15, 16]. We found that 83% of the first recordings were acceptable for the commercial software, increasing to 90% when a shorter recording time was accepted for analysis. One child woke up more than once during the night. However, the result was correct for this recording, supporting the previous finding that movements did not alter EVI measurements [11]. The main reason for not obtaining results was that parents did not perform the measurement, mostly among outpatients W children, or that the accepted recording time was insufficient, likely due to a poor signal. The minimum required recording time of 5 h, set by the commercial software, is reasonable for preschool children in whom approximately 28% of the recording time is excluded during signal processing [9, 19]. However, in younger children, IP may differ, requiring adapted recording materials, particularly electrodes (size, resistance). In this regard, we included traces of at least 4 h to increase the feasibility while slightly decreasing the between‐test repeatability. In the case of repeated measurements, we kept the night with the higher EVI as the final result, rather than the average value [16]. Finally, even though tidal breathing expiratory flow–volume curves vary with sleep stages, since we included all sleep time in our recordings, shortening the recording duration by 1 h in infants would not significantly affect the number of sleep cycles or the time spent in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep [11]. No age‐related effect was observed in the values recorded in healthy children possibly because sleep cycle length increases with age, while the proportion of total sleep time spent in REM sleep markedly decreases (from 34% at 3 months to 26% at 24 months) [20].

Second, when comparing our results with previous values obtained in older healthy children, the difference is minimal (mean EVI from 16.8 to 17.4), although variability was slightly lower in our study, resulting in a higher 5th percentile value of 12.7 compared to 12.0 or 12.1 [14, 15]. We did not confirm the assumption that age influences EVI under 3 years of age. EVI reflects the variability of the flow‐volume curve in the initial part of the expiration (15%–45% of tidal expiration). At this stage, post‐inspiratory inspiratory activity (PIIA) is still present, counteracting passive lung and thoracic recoil to prevent the lungs from emptying too abruptly. This respiratory muscle activity may not vary significantly with age, as in healthy adult PIIA ceased at 79% of expiratory time, whereas in new‐borns it ceased at a mean (SD) of 83% (25%) of expiratory time [21, 22]. Movements of vocal cord to decrease glottis aperture also occur during early expiration in young children and prolong expiration [23, 24]. Another reason why we did not find a significant difference in EVI between our study population and older children is that, thanks to the placement of two electrodes on the arms and two on the thorax, we maintained a linear relationship between impedance change and volume change. Even though linearity using this electrode conformation across all lung volumes has only been demonstrated in adult subjects, there is no obvious factor that would be likely to interfere with this relationship [25].

Third, we reported reference values for T PTEF , T E , and T PTEF /T E which are indexes previously reported in infants using PNT technique, where the dead space limits prolonged measurements [26]. Results of T PTEF /T E recorded using IP have been validated against PNT measurements in awake preschool children performing methacholine challenge [12]. The good agreement between T PTEF /T E measured using IP and PNT has been confirmed in sedated infants (5 to 28 months), although IP measurements yielded slightly lower in this population (mean bias (limits of agreement) −0.025 (− 0.113–0.063)) [10]. This important validation step for IP was conducted using 1 min recordings, but as with EVI, sleep stages influence T PTEF /T E , making it important to record total night sleep [19].

Our study has some limitations, the main one being that we included a limited number of sick children. However, IP measurements was significantly impaired in sick children and more so in wheezy children than in non‐wheezy children (Table 2, Figures 2 and 3). EVI could be normal on the last night before discharge in some hospitalised wheezy children, which is consistent with previous findings from a study where EVI was significantly and negatively correlated with the number of days to discharge in wheezy children [14]. Additionally, although none of the included children had enlarged tonsils or signs of obstructive sleep apnoea, and the HS children with rhinopharyngitis showed no clinical signs of laryngitis, we cannot exclude the potential effect of mild upper airway inflammation or obstruction on our results, even though we focused on the expiratory phase of tidal breathing. Finally, we did not perform repeated measurements in sick children to assess improvement or treatment effects in those with wheezing, which may represent the most relevant application of IP but was beyond the scope of our study [15, 19, 27]. However, from the repeated measurements performed in H children, we have found that a change of less than 2 between two EVI measurements is unlikely to reflect a true change.

In conclusion, in infants, overnight IP measurement is easy to record by parents at home. EVI reference values are close to those recorded in older children. Further studies, including sick children are needed to confirm our findings on the effect of respiratory illness on IP outcomes.
