Empowering fertility: Integrating indicators into personalised reproductive care
Authors: Michaela S. Olabisi, Sunni L. Mumford
Published: 2024

Before 2000, initial studies on fertility indicators primarily focused on their utility in natural family planning and menstrual cycle monitoring for contraceptive purposes. 1 , 2 However, starting in 1999, researchers began to shift their focus towards understanding the relationship between these indicators and the likelihood of conception among women attempting pregnancy. Early methods, including basal body temperature tracking, cervical fluid monitoring and urinary ovulation tests, were explored for their potential to predict fertile windows and optimise chances of conception. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6

As research advanced into the 2010s, particularly with the rise of digital health technologies, studies increasingly examined the role of fertility indicators in improving fertility outcomes among women actively trying to conceive. By 2020, comprehensive reviews and meta‐analyses underscored the potential of smartphone fertility apps to accurately predict the fertile window, though questions remained about the consistency and reliability of these methods across diverse clinical contexts. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Despite significant progress, a crucial research gap persists in understanding how effectively these indicators translate into enhanced conception rates in real‐world settings.

In this recent issue of Paediatric and Perinatal Epidemiology, Pedersen and colleagues 7 provide crucial insights into which fertility indicator methods are most effective in increasing the likelihood of conception among women attempting pregnancy without fertility treatments. Using data from two Danish preconception cohorts, they found that the use of any type of fertility indicator was associated with an increase in fecundability (adjusted fecundability ratios [aFR] 1.14, 95% CI 1.08, 1.19). This effect varied across different fertility indicators, with an overall pregnancy rate of 84.8%. Women who used fertility indicators had a higher estimated pregnancy rate of 86.3% compared to 82.5% among those who did not use any fertility indicators.

Cervical fluid monitoring emerged as the most effective method, with the highest increase in fecundability (aFR 1.46, 95% CI 1.03, 2.07), likely due to its direct indication of ovulation, enabling precise timing for intercourse. Urinary ovulation testing also showed a significant improvement in fecundability (aFR 1.35, 95% CI 1.16, 1.58), offering ease of use and clear results. While counting days proved moderately effective (aFR 1.18, 95% CI 1.09, 1.29) and remains popular for its simplicity, fertility apps, despite their convenience, exhibited variable effectiveness (aFR 1.08, 95% CI 0.96, 1.22). This discrepancy between the effectiveness of fertility apps and traditional methods like counting days raises important questions about the reliability of algorithm‐driven predictions versus more standardised approaches. Fertility apps tend to rely on complex algorithms to predict fertile windows, which can be influenced by the accuracy of user input and the app's underlying technology. In contrast, counting days uses a more standardised approach based on cycle length and ovulation timing, which is less susceptible to technological errors but may lack personalisation.

In their study, Pedersen et al. 7 advances the field by addressing critical gaps and leveraging robust methodological strengths. A key strength is minimising selection bias by including only participants attempting conception for ≤6 cycles at baseline. However, a significant limitation of the study is its reliance on self‐reported data, which introduces potential inaccuracies due to recall bias. This bias can lead to incomplete or inaccurate reporting of fertility indicator use and the timing of conception attempts, as participants may forget details or misremember frequencies. Additionally, the consistency of each method's use during each cycle is unknown, which could significantly affect the perceived effectiveness of these indicators. Moreover, most of the women in the study were using more than one method, making it challenging to disentangle the effectiveness of individual methods. The study also noted a low prevalence of use for basal body temperature tracking and fertility apps, which could limit the generalizability of findings related to these specific methods.

Why has it taken so long to investigate and document the impact of fertility indicators on fecundability among women attempting pregnancy? Despite advancements in understanding biological mechanisms and predictors of fertility, the direct influence of fertility indicators on conception rates has remained largely underexplored until recently. This delayed recognition underscores broader challenges in reproductive health research, where the focus has often lagged behind the needs and experiences of those trying to conceive. For example, while traditional research largely centred on clinical outcomes, recent efforts have begun to address more inclusive aspects of care, such as recognising the unique needs of diverse family dynamics and socioeconomic backgrounds. This shift highlights a growing awareness that effective reproductive health research must align more closely with the varied realities of those seeking to conceive, yet there is still considerable progress to be made.

Moving forward, closing the gap between theoretical fecundability and practical outcomes will necessitate a proactive shift in clinical practice among physicians and healthcare providers. It requires integrating fertility indicators more comprehensively into routine reproductive health assessments, moving beyond traditional contraceptive counselling to include proactive discussions on enhancing fertility. This shift entails equipping clinicians with updated training on the interpretation and application of fertility indicators, fostering a deeper understanding of how these indicators can optimise the timing and frequency of intercourse for conception. It highlights the need for personalised fertility management plans that consider individual factors. By embracing a proactive and informed approach, clinicians can empower individuals attempting pregnancy with the tools and support needed to navigate their fertility journey effectively. In return, patients striving to conceive may experience a sense of optimism at the potential of accessible and cost‐effective interventions like fertility indicators to improve reproductive outcomes.

Despite the progress outlined in Pedersen et al.'s study, 7 several critical questions remain unanswered, highlighting the need for further research in this area. Have we fully explored which fertility indicators are most effective and how best to utilise them? The variability in efficacy across different methods suggests that more research is needed to determine the optimal combination of indicators and the contexts in which they are most beneficial. Additionally, future studies should aim to provide robust data on the long‐term outcomes of using fertility indicators, particularly in diverse populations and real‐world settings. These studies should address the reliability of algorithm‐based predictions versus traditional methods and explore how different approaches might be integrated to enhance their overall effectiveness. Moreover, understanding the psychological and behavioural impacts of using fertility indicators on couples trying to conceive is essential for developing comprehensive fertility management strategies. By addressing these outstanding questions, future research can help to further refine fertility indicators as a critical tool in reproductive health, ultimately improving outcomes for individuals and couples seeking to conceive.
