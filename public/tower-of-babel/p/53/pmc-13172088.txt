A Comparative Effectiveness Study of Lorazepam or IVIg Versus no Treatment for Down Syndrome Regression Disorder
Authors: Jonathan D. Santoro, Saba Jafarpour, Panteha Hayati Rezvan, Mellad M. Khoshnood, Benjamin N. Vogel, Lina Nguyen, Lilia Kazerooni, Noemi A. Spinazzi, Nidhiben Anadani, Kristen S. Fisher, Robyn A. Filipink, Melanie A. Manning, Cathy Franklin, Eileen A. Quinn, Michael S. Rafii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDown syndrome regression disorder (DSRD) is manifested by acute or subacute onset of bradykinesia, catatonia, mutism, and neuropsychiatric symptoms. Both benzodiazepines and immunotherapy have been effective in treating DSRD, although no study has compared these treatments directly. This observational study compares the response of lorazepam and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) to no therapy over 6 months.MethodsThis prospective, non-randomized, observational natural history study assessed therapeutic responses in 212 patients meeting international criteria for DSRD. Outcomes were measured at baseline, 12, and 24 weeks using the Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale (BFCRS), 25-foot walk (25FW), Clinical Global Impression-Severity (CGI-S), and Neuropsychiatric Inventory Total (NPIT) and Severity (NPIST) scores. Linear mixed-effect models were used to examine changes in outcomes.ResultsPatients received lorazepam (n = 85, 40%), IVIg (n = 68, 32%) or no therapy (n = 59, 28%). By 12 weeks, both active therapies outperformed no therapy on all outcomes (all p < 0.001), with IVIg showing additional advantages over lorazepam on BFCRS [mean difference (95% CI): − 2.24 (− 3.72, − 0.75)], NPIT [− 9.22 (− 11.22, − 7.23)], and NPIST [− 3.16 (− 4.07, − 2.26)]. By 24 weeks, IVIg was superior to lorazepam on all outcomes [25FW: − 1.80 (− 2.58, − 1.02); BFCRS: − 7.09 (− 8.58, − 5.60); CGI-S: − 0.63 (− 1.01, − 0.25); NPIT: − 12.23 (− 14.22, − 10.23); NPIST: − 3.80 (− 4.71, − 2.89)]. Treatment response varied by lumbar puncture abnormality (BFCRS), any neurodiagnostic study abnormality (CGI-S, NPIT), and serum cytokines and MRI abnormality (NPIT), with IVIg demonstrating greater benefits among patients with such abnormalities.ConclusionsAt the endpoint of 24 weeks, both IVIg and lorazepam were superior to no treatment for DSRD but IVIg was superior to lorazepam for all five outcomes. Between 12 and 24 weeks of treatment, IVIg showed greater improvement in all outcomes than did lorazepam, suggesting that at least a 24-week treatment course is necessary for maximum IVIg benefit.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-026-00906-2.

What was learned from the study? Introduction

Down syndrome (DS) is a common genetic condition caused by trisomy of chromosome 21, with an incidence of 1 in every 700 births [1]. Individuals with DS are at risk for a variety of medical issues over the lifespan, including neurological conditions such as autism, intellectual disability, epilepsy, and Alzheimer’s dementia later in life [2–4]. A unique symptom cluster, known as Down syndrome regression disorder (DSRD), has been increasingly recognized over the last decade [5–9]. Individuals afflicted by DSRD develop acute or sub-acute onset of agitation, autistic-like behaviors, bradykinesia, catatonia, encephalopathy, neuropsychiatric disease, and mutism, often preceded by stress or mild infectious disease [7, 10–12]. The onset of symptoms tends to be in the second or third decade of life and occur in individuals traditionally thought to be too young to develop Alzheimer’s dementia [13, 14].

Emerging evidence has found that some individuals with DSRD have higher rates of autoimmunity and demonstrate abnormalities on inflammatory cytokines and neurodiagnostic studies during work-up with electroencephalogram (EEG), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and lumbar puncture (LP) [10, 15, 16]. These individuals have been previously found to be very responsive to immunotherapy, particularly with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) [10, 15, 17, 18]. Psychotropics and benzodiazepines (e.g., lorazepam), meant to treat the symptoms of the condition, have also been found to be beneficial in this patient population [10, 19]. To date, no study has directly evaluated the two most commonly utilized therapies, IVIg and lorazepam, nor evaluated the possibility of improvement without therapy.

This study sought to: (1) determine the comparative effectiveness of lorazepam, IVIg, and no treatment at 24 weeks, using five established outcome measures, (2) determine the duration of treatment necessary for maximum benefit at 12 and 24 weeks, and (3) determine by stratified analyses how the effectiveness of IVIg compares to lorazepam for following adjustment for potential effect modification of clinical and diagnostic factors. The authors’ primary hypothesis was that IVIg has maximum effect at 24 weeks as opposed to 12 weeks compared to lorazepam and no treatment.

Methods

Compliance with Ethics Guidelines and Consent to Participate

This study was approved by the Children’s Hospital Los Angeles (CHLA) institutional review board (IRB) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964. Individuals evaluated for DSRD in clinical care were enrolled into a prospective observational natural history study where their data were, systematically tracked and stored in a database. Given that symptoms of DSRD are neurocognitive and neuropsychiatric in nature, informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians in all circumstances as no individual had capacity to consent for this study. Assent was obtained when possible and waived when neurocognitive impairment was too profound for the patient to reasonably assent. Individuals required enrollment at the primary site (CHLA) but could have follow-up at other sites if endpoint measures could be completed in the necessary timeframe.

Individuals enrolled had their outcomes tracked as they proceeded with clinical care. Patients were not randomized to treatment but were observationally followed for responses to treatments they had been started on as part of their clinical standard of care. As this study reported observed clinical outcomes, there was no consent or assent for therapeutic interventions as these were not assigned. The clinical review of outcomes and data extraction in this study was thus retrospective in nature.

Data Extraction and Data Availability

Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and neuroimaging data were collected from the electronic medical record. All data points were extracted and then verified before entry into the study database by members of the study team. Anonymized datasets used during and/or analyzed during the current study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request following IRB approval.

Patient Populations and Inclusion Criteria

Individuals included in this study required a confirmed diagnosis of possible or probable DSRD per international consensus criteria made between July 1, 2022, and August 1, 2025, [20]. In addition, all subjects had to have completed standardized diagnostic studies, which included EEG, MRI, and LP. Patients were eligible if they were aged 10–30 years at symptom onset and had at least three consecutive visits, each separated by 12 weeks (± 2 weeks), where a Bush–Francis Catatonia rating scale (BFCRS), timed 25-foot walk (25FW), Clinical Global Improvement Scale (CGI-S), and Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI-Q, broken down into total (NPIT) and severity (NPIST) scores) could be administered. Initial (baseline) visits included a comprehensive 90-min physical, neurological, and psychiatric examination. Life events (LEs) defined broadly as psychological, medical, or environmental stressors that may have preceded the onset of symptoms and were also collected.

Catatonia was diagnosed by clinical assessment and the use of the BFCRS. Individuals were systematically evaluated by clinicians familiar with the BFCRS and assessed for catatonia at each visit. Using the BFCRS as a tool for diagnosis, individuals were diagnosed with catatonia using Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Version 5 (DSM-5) standard of having a score of ≥ 3 on the BFCRS.

Individuals could receive only one therapy and were required to be on this therapy for a minimum 24 weeks. Individuals who had previously received any therapy more than 12 weeks prior to the study period were eligible. Exclusion criteria included: baseline weight < 40 kg, history of neurosurgical intervention (e.g., shunt placement), prior or active receipt of radiotherapy or chemotherapy, or use of any immunotherapy. Individuals could have existing obstructive sleep apnea if it was either treated and/or their apnea hypopnea index was unchanged from values prior to the onset of their DSRD. Individuals receiving a combination of therapies (IVIg and lorazepam) were excluded, as were any patients who were concomitantly taking antipsychotics, anticonvulsants, mood stabilizers, antidepressants, or other benzodiazepines. Individuals obtaining concomitant ECT were also excluded. Finally, each individual included in the study had their clinical data verified by blinded study physicians (MMK and SJ) prior to data analysis to ensure that: (1) they continued to meet clinical criteria for DSRD, and (2) had not had another mimic of DSRD (e.g., epilepsy) identified during diagnostic work-up or management.

Cohort Stratification

Individuals enrolled in this study were not randomized to treatments and were observationally followed and included only after clinical treatment selection was made by the care team. As there are no standards for therapeutic selection in DSRD, treatment choice was fully at the discretion of the evaluating physician and caregiver/guardian. Individuals included were then categorized based on their clinical treatment regimen but only after a total period of 24 weeks, as no other concomitant treatments could have been administered for inclusion. A priori sample size estimates indicated that 50 subjects per group were required to achieve 80% power to detect significant inter-group differences at an alpha level of 0.05. Due to the logistical challenges of identifying monotherapy-exclusive patients within the larger clinical population, the final enrollment was restricted to those meeting strict inclusion criteria. Ultimately, the study exceeded the minimum threshold in all three arms (no therapy, lorazepam, and IVIg), providing sufficient data for robust linear mixed-effects model analysis.

A cohort of individuals receiving no therapy during the study period were classified as the “No therapy” group. These individuals received no therapy for one of three reasons: (1) the individual met criteria for DSRD, but the full diagnostic work up had not been performed, (2) caregiver preference, and/or (3) delays in obtaining insurance authorization for therapy. Individuals who were treated with only lorazepam or only IVIg for a period of 24 weeks were classified into two intervention groups: “lorazepam” and “IVIg”, respectively.

Behavioral/Neuropsychiatric Assessment

Each patient underwent a standardized frequency of clinical assessment. Each patient had to have a baseline assessment (day 0) followed by two follow-up visits at 12- and 24-week intervals which were aligned with clinical care follow-up. A ± 14-day range was provided for the two follow-up visits to accommodate scheduling for variability in scheduling. As there is no one singular validated objective metric for improvement in individuals with DSRD, multiple primary outcome measures were utilized and included assessments of catatonia using the BFCRS, motor function via the 25FW, neuropsychiatric symptoms with the NPI-Q (including NPIT and NPIST), and overall clinical status using the CGI-S. A lower score on the BFCRS, 25FW, NPIT, NPIST, and CGI-S were considered improvement.

Therapeutic Dosing: Standardized regimens for therapeutic interventions were utilized.

1) Lorazepam: all individuals were started on 0.5 mg three times daily (TID) of lorazepam and titrated up by 0.5 mg TID weekly to a goal of 2 mg TID. Individuals could receive a maximum dosage of 2 mg TID at 12 weeks, although could titrate up to a maximum of 3 mg TID at 24 weeks if tolerated, using the same titration schedule. Individuals not tolerating titration (e.g., somnolence, balance issues, altered mental status) were titrated back to the highest tolerated dose by the assessing physician.

2) IVIg: the same protocol was followed for all individuals regardless of therapeutic response. Each individual initially received an induction dose of 2 g/kg of IVIg over 2 days, followed by 1 g/kg monthly thereafter. No changes were made to the dosing regimen or frequency of administration during the study.

Definition of Abnormal Neurodiagnostic Studies

Reports of EEG, MRI, and LP were identical to prior manuscripts published by the authorship group [10, 15, 16]. This is also presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared across therapy arms using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and one-way ANOVA for continuous variables (Table 1). Linear mixed-effects models with restricted maximum likelihood estimation were used to examine changes in primary outcomes (25FW, BFCRS, CGI-S, NPIT, NPIST) from baseline to 12 weeks through 24 weeks. Models included therapy arm (no therapy, lorazepam, and IVIg), time (baseline,12 weeks, and 24 weeks), and their interaction as fixed effects, with a random intercept for each subject to account for within-subject correlation across repeated measurements. From these models, we estimated: (1) between-group differences, and (2) within-group changes for each therapy arm, at all time intervals (baseline to 12 weeks, baseline to 24 weeks, and 12 to 24 weeks), using linear combinations of model parameters (Table 2). Results

Of 783 individuals reviewed for inclusion, 366 (46.7%) were excluded due to receiving multiple therapies during the 6-month time epoch (e.g., IVIg and lorazepam), 157 (20.0%) had insufficient or incomplete data including delayed or missing follow-up, and 48 (6.1%) met at least one exclusion criteria. For those meeting exclusion criteria, the most common reason for exclusion was active use of immunotherapy (n = 41, 85.4%), weight < 40 kg (n = 5, 10.4%), and prior receipt of radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy (n = 2, 4.2%).

A total of 212 patients (27.1%) met inclusion criteria and were categorized by therapy received: no therapy (n = 59, 27.8%), lorazepam (n = 85, 40.1%), and IVIg (n = 68, 32.1%). The most common reasons for no therapy were caregiver preference (n = 40, 67.8%) and incomplete diagnostic work-up despite meeting DSRD criteria (n = 16, 27.1%). Nearly all patients (n = 149, 70.2%) were treatment-naïve at baseline, though some had received SSRIs (n = 37, 17.4%) and/or antipsychotics (n = 28, 13.2%) more than 12 weeks prior to baseline evaluation.

Patient Characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the 212 patients are presented in Table 1. Mean age was 16.3 years (SD = 3.7) at symptom onset, 18.3 years (SD = 3.8) at diagnosis, and 18.8 years (SD = 3.8) at initiation of therapy. Just over half were male (n = 109, 51.4%), half identified as Hispanic (n = 106, 50.0%), and three-quarters were white (n = 160, 75.5%). Most patients (n = 135, 63.7%) met probable DSRD criteria. Catatonia was present in 73.6% (n = 156) of patients. Specific neurodiagnostic findings indicated that any neurodiagnostic study abnormality was present in 50.0% of patients: 29.2% with abnormal EEG, 35.4% with abnormal MRI, and 16.5% with abnormal LP with detailed breakdown of findings in Table 1. Of note, there were no baseline demographic or clinical features that were statistically different between cohorts.

Only six individuals (2.8%) had received prior immunotherapy which was steroids (oral prednisone) in three patients (50.0%), mycophenolate mofetil in two patients (33.3%) and azathioprine in one (16.7%). These therapies were administered an average of 17.7 months, 14.5 months, and 21.0 months prior to study participation, respectively. Among patients receiving lorazepam (n = 85), steady-state dosing was: 1.5 mg TID (n = 14, 16.5%), 2 mg TID (n = 61, 71.8%), 2.5 mg TID (n = 4, 4.7%), and 3 mg TID (n = 6, 7.1%). No patients discontinued therapy during the 24-week study period.

Changes in Primary Outcomes by Therapy Arm Over Time

Between-Therapy Differences. As summarized in Table 2 and illustrated in Fig. 1, significant treatment-by-time interactions were evident for all five primary outcomes, indicating differences in treatment response across therapy arms over time. IVIg demonstrated the largest observed improvements, followed by lorazepam, with both therapies showing greater observed improvement than no therapy. During the initial period from baseline to 12 weeks, IVIg showed superior improvement compared to no therapy across all outcomes [25FW: mean difference (95% CI): − 2.44 (− 3.29, − 1.58); BFCRS: − 10.34 (− 11.96, − 8.71); CGI-S: − 1.64 (− 2.06, − 1.22); NPIT: − 12.13 (− 14.32, − 9.95); NPIST: − 3.89 (− 4.88, − 2.90); all p < 0.001], and outperformed lorazepam on three outcomes [BFCRS: − 2.24 (− 3.72, − 0.75), p = 0.003; NPIT: − 9.22 (− 11.22, − 7.23), p < 0.001; NPIST: − 3.16 (− 4.07, − 2.26), p < 0.001]. Lorazepam showed intermediate benefits, outperforming no therapy on four outcomes [25FW: − 1.82 (− 2.63, − 1.00), p < 0.001; BFCRS: − 8.10 (− 9.65, − 6.56), p < 0.001; CGI-S: − 1.39 (− 1.79, − 0.99), p < 0.001; NPIT: − 2.91 (− 4.99, − 0.83), p = 0.006]. From 12 to 24 weeks, IVIg remained superior to both lorazepam and no therapy on most outcomes, with robust continued advantages for BFCRS and NPIT. Lorazepam’s benefit over no therapy also persisted across multiple outcomes during this period. Between baseline to 24 weeks, IVIg maintained superiority over no therapy [25FW: − 3.48 (− 4.33, − 2.62); BFCRS: − 16.79 (− 18.41, − 15.16); CGI-S: − 1.61 (− 2.03, − 1.19); NPIT: − 21.38 (− 23.57, − 19.20); NPIST: − 6.75 (− 7.74, − 5.76); all p < 0.001] and lorazepam [25FW: − 1.80 (− 2.58, − 1.02); BFCRS: − 7.09 (− 8.58, − 5.60); CGI-S: − 0.63 (− 1.01, − 0.25); NPIT: − 12.23 (− 14.22, − 10.23); NPIST: − 3.80 (− 4.71, − 2.89); all p < 0.001] across all outcomes. Lorazepam also surpassed no therapy on all five outcomes [25FW: − 1.68 (− 2.49, − 0.86); BFCRS: − 9.70 (− 11.25, − 8.15); CGI-S: − 0.98 (− 1.38, − 0.58); NPIT: − 9.16 (− 11.24, − 7.08); NPIST: − 2.95 (− 3.90, − 2.01); all p < 0.001], though with smaller advantages than IVIg.Fig. 1Treatment responses by group

Treatment responses by group

Within-Therapy Changes. Patients treated with IVIg demonstrated robust gains across all outcomes from baseline to 12 weeks, with continued improvement from 12 to 24 weeks for all outcomes except CGI-S. By 24 weeks, substantial changes from baseline were observed across all measures [25FW: − 4.11 (− 4.69, − 3.52); BFCRS: − 16.74 (− 17.84, − 15.63); CGI-S: − 1.32 (− 1.61, − 1.04); NPIT: − 21.37 (− 22.86, − 19.88); NPIST: − 7.18 (− 7.85, − 6.50); all p < 0.001]. Patients treated with lorazepam showed significant gains in four outcomes from baseline to 12 weeks (all except NPIST), with additional improvement from 12 to 24 weeks limited to NPIT and NPIST. Overall changes from baseline to 24 weeks were significant across all outcomes [25FW: − 2.31 (− 2.83, − 1.79); BFCRS: − 9.65 (− 10.64, − 8.66); CGI-S: − 0.69 (− 0.95, − 0.44); NPIT: − 9.14 (− 10.47, − 7.81); NPIST: − 3.38 (− 3.98, − 2.77); all p < 0.001] but of smaller magnitude than IVIg. Patients receiving no therapy demonstrated minimal change over time. The only significant change from baseline to 12 weeks was slight worsening in CGI-S [0.51 (0.20, 0.82); p = 0.001]. By 24 weeks, modest improvement from baseline was observed only for 25FW [− 0.63 (− 1.25, − 0.004), p = 0.049]. Therapeutic responses in the NPI-Q sub-fields, including total, severity, frequency, and distress scores are presented in Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively.

Of 45 three-way interaction tests examining whether clinical characteristics influence treatment response over time, 11 (24.4%) were nominally significant (p < 0.05) and 5 (11.1%) remained significant after FDR correction (Supplementary Table S2; Table 3). LP and any neurodiagnostic study abnormalities were the most common factors among nominally significant interactions, followed by serum cytokines, meeting probable DSRD criteria, MRI abnormality, and EEG abnormality. The five FDR-significant interactions were: three for NPIT (MRI abnormality, any neurodiagnostic study abnormality, and serum cytokines), one for CGI-S (any neurodiagnostic study abnormality), and one for BFCRS (LP abnormality). Discussion

In the first comparative efficacy study of therapeutics in individuals with DSRD, participants were noted to respond well to both lorazepam and IVIg, with significant clinical improvement over time compared to individuals receiving no therapy. Lorazepam demonstrated significant benefits across multiple domains by 12 weeks, including motor-based assessments (25FW, BFCRS), CGI-S, and NPIT. However, continued improvement from 12 to 24 weeks was limited to NPIT and NPIST, with little additional change in other domains. In contrast, IVIg showed robust and sustained benefits across all outcomes. Most of the improvement occurred from baseline to 12 weeks, with continued gains from 12 to 24 weeks for all outcomes except CGI-S. By 24 weeks, IVIg demonstrated significantly greater benefits than lorazepam across all five primary outcomes. These findings suggest IVIg was associated with greater improvement than lorazepam but required 24 weeks to demonstrate maximum benefit. Further, the therapeutic benefit realized for individuals receiving either lorazepam or IVIg in this study occurred several years after the onset of clinical symptoms (average 2.46 years), indicating that patients may benefit from treatment beyond the acute or subacute phases of the disease. This potential for later clinical improvements supports the importance of diagnosis and clinical intervention even in patients with longer disease durations.

Lorazepam produced the expected motor-specific clinical benefits on the 25FW and BFCRS as early as 12 weeks but did not yield improvements in neuropsychiatric pathology until 24 weeks. As lorazepam is the first-line treatment for catatonia in individuals [8–10, 12, 19] with and without DSRD (21), these findings are not entirely surprising. The delayed improvement in neuropsychiatric function potentially details the clinical phenomenon of “unmasking”, where the treatment of motor impairment in DSRD makes underlying neuropsychiatric symptoms more noticeable, and thus “worse” at early time points. Neuropsychiatric worsening, particularly emotional lability, has been observed anecdotally in other populations [22], although it had never been reported in DSRD. The exact mechanism for neuropsychiatric improvements on the NPI-Q by 24 weeks is unclear, although sub-analysis demonstrated that these improvements were primarily driven by motor-related factors, raising the question whether patients treated only with lorazepam were improved at all from a neuropsychiatric standpoint. Although unanswered at this time, lorazepam may represent symptomatic therapy for DSRD, whereas IVIg may be treating the underlying cause of DSRD akin to the treatment of autoimmune encephalitis [23, 24]. However, future studies will need to assess the utility of dual therapy of lorazepam and IVIg, which is commonly utilized in clinical practice. In addition, further evaluation of electroconvulsive therapy should also be explored, given the potential adjunct use for treatment of catatonia in refractory cases [8, 9, 12, 25].

IVIg yielded improvement at both timepoints across multiple objective measures. Impressively, IVIg was able to improve catatonia (25FW and BFCRS), neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPIT and NPIST scores), and global improvement (CGI-S) in a proportional and sustained manner. This finding is important, as the depth of response on motor-based measures was only slightly superior to lorazepam by 12 weeks and outperformed it more significantly by 24 weeks. As such, this is the first study to directly compare these two standard therapies in DSRD, highlighting that IVIg was associated with larger improvements in motor and neuropsychiatric outcomes than lorazepam by 24 weeks. Although we observed continued improvement through 24 weeks, this study did not evaluate durability of benefit after treatment discontinuation or longer-term trajectories. Longer follow-up and randomized trials are needed to determine the optimal duration of therapy with IVIg [15]. These data add to published studies demonstrating the early and sustained therapeutic benefit of IVIg in DSRD [10, 12, 15, 16].

The use of demographic and clinical predictors of therapeutic response remains a critically important topic of discussion in the management of DSRD. As with prior studies, demographic and clinical factors were found to be poor predictors of outcomes, while neurodiagnostic abnormalities consistently predict response to IVIg [10, 12, 15, 16]. Specifically, abnormal neuroimaging and LP were tightly associated with more dramatic responses to immunotherapy but not lorazepam, which may provide evidence for early intervention with immunotherapy such as IVIg in cases where these studies are abnormal. As such, these findings add to the growing literature on greater clinical responses to immunotherapy in individuals with DSRD who have neurodiagnostic abnormalities [10, 15, 16, 26]. This observation should be made with caution as diagnostic abnormalities in DSRD may not be sensitive or specific to this condition as EEG and MRI findings like these can also be observed in the DS population [16, 26]. However, an important factor to appreciate in this study is that individuals with prolonged duration of untreated symptoms still responded to both lorazepam and IVIg.

Effect modifications analysis are critical to review in the context of the findings reported. Most interactions did not survive FDR correction, suggesting no strong evidence that these biomarkers consistently alter the IVIg–lorazepam treatment difference in this sample, while recognizing limited power for interaction testing. Five interactions remained significant after FDR correction: NPIT (cytokines, MRI abnormality, any neurodiagnostic abnormality), CGI-S (any neurodiagnostic abnormality), and BFCRS (LP abnormality), and each pointed toward larger observed IVIg-associated improvement in biomarker-positive strata. Differences from prior reports [12, 15, 16] may reflect that earlier studies assessed absolute response to immunotherapy, whereas we tested the incremental benefit of IVIg over an active comparator (lorazepam), which may reduce detectable heterogeneity. Importantly, these subgroup findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

This study is not without limitations. Treatment of all patients occurred at tertiary academic medical centers, and, as such, both severity and ascertainment biases are likely present in this sample. These biases my have skewed the rates of neurodiagnostic abnormalities and therapeutic responses in the setting of evaluating a non-representative cohort of individuals with DSRD. Additionally, the use of consensus criteria developed by medical professionals for the diagnosis of DSRD [20] contains inherent limitations due to the lack of objective diagnostic biomarkers. As this study was observational and not randomized, residual confounding and confounding by indication are likely; therefore, between-group differences should be interpreted as associations rather than causal treatment effects. Further, individuals receiving no therapy could have had a higher likelihood for treatment bias given the multiple reasons that individuals could have been placed in that arm, including, but not limited to, caregiver preference and insurance status. Bias of the assessors is also possible, as evaluators and clinicians were not blinded to the therapy received. In addition, the primary outcomes have not been assessed or validated for minimum clinically important differences scores, making interpretation of meaningfulness of effect unclear. In addition, a subjective “percentage back to baseline” was not available for all patients. In future studies, the use of a composite score, incorporating the multiple clinical biomarkers of improvement, may also be beneficial in lieu of utilizing multiple primary endpoints. While there were no reports of relapse of symptoms on treatment or treatment discontinuation in this study, it is possible that individuals may have symptomatic worsening after the 24-week observation period or with withdrawal of treatments, and this will need to be further studied. The effect modification analyses were exploratory and involved several three-way interaction tests. We applied Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure to control for multiple testing; however, the risk of false-positive findings remains given the exploratory nature of these analyses. Additionally, post hoc stratified analyses were conducted without additional multiplicity adjustment. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory approach, modest sample size limiting statistical power for detecting complex interactions, and potential for type I error. Randomized study design benefits from eliminating bias for assigning treatments by clinicians, limiting potential bias in assessing outcome measures and allowing for intention to treat analyses for individuals who dropped out of the study. The latter is particularly important, as moving on to a new treatment (indicating a failure of existing therapy) was exclusionary in this study and limits the interpretation of the results.

Conclusions

In this observational efficacy study in DSRD, both IVIg and lorazepam were associated with greater improvement than no therapy, and IVIg was associated with larger improvements than lorazepam at 24 weeks; randomized studies are warranted to estimate causal effects and optimal treatment duration.
