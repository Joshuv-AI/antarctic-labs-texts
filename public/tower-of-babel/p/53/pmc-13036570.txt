Mitochondrial quantity–quality imbalance in cellular senescence: practical readouts and minimal assay bundles
Authors: Myeongwoo Jung, Seongho Cha, Eun Kyung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cellular senescence is an irreversible program of cell-cycle arrest that accumulates with age, contributing to chronic inflammation and various age-related diseases. A key feature of senescence paradigms is mitochondrial dysfunction, which involves not just a single defect but a series of coordinated changes in bioenergetics, redox homeostasis, mitochondrial quality control, and organelle interaction. Senescent cells often display a “quantity–quality imbalance” in their mitochondria: while the mitochondrial mass may increase, their efficiency in oxidative phosphorylation decreases, leading to a destabilized membrane potential (∆Ψm) and elevated levels of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS). These interrelated changes can exacerbate senescence through persistent stress signaling, impaired turnover of damaged mitochondrial components, and alterations in organelle contacts, such as those between endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria, and between mitochondria and lysosomes. Given that these phenotypes differ depending on cell type, triggering factors, and timing, no single assay can adequately define senescence-associated mitochondrial dysfunction. In this review, we present practical, complementary strategies that include extracellular flux–based respiration profiling, ATP output measurement, ∆Ψm and ROS assessments, flux-based mitophagy reporters, quantitative network imaging, and contact-site assays. We propose minimal assay bundles that allow for a thorough multidimensional analysis. By establishing standardized, orthogonal measures of mitochondrial quantity and quality, we aim to enhance mechanistic understanding and facilitate the rational evaluation of mitochondria-targeted senolytic and senomorphic therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Cellular senescence is defined as an irreversible cell cycle arrest triggered by various intrinsic and extrinsic stresses, including telomere shortening, DNA damage, oncogene activation, and metabolic disturbances (1, 2). Senescent cells accumulate with age and are increasingly linked to tissue dysfunction and a wide range of age-related diseases (3, 4). Despite undergoing proliferative arrest, senescent cells remain metabolically active and develop the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). This phenotype leads to the release of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and extracellular matrix (ECM) factors, which remodel the tissue microenvironment and influence neighboring cells (4, 5). Accordingly, the progressive build-up of senescent cells is associated with chronic inflammation, impaired tissue repair, and the progression of age-related disorders, such as metabolic diseases, cancer, and neurodegenerative diseases (1, 6). Across various forms of cellular senescence, such as replicative senescence (RS), oncogene-induced senescence (OIS), and stress-induced premature senescence (SIPS), mitochondria undergo convergent yet multi-layered remodeling. This remodeling involves a decline in bioenergetics, increased oxidative stress, disrupted dynamics and mitophagy, and altered inter-organelle communication (7, 8). A recurring theme is the imbalance between mitochondrial quantity and quality. While mitochondrial mass can increase alongside respiratory remodeling, mitochondrial performance may deteriorate. This decline is evidenced by reduced OXPHOS coupling, ATP-generating capacity, dysregulation of mitochondrial ∆Ψm, and excessive production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) (9, 10). These alterations may reinforce senescence programs. For instance, mtROS can promote oxidative damage and stress signaling, while ∆Ψm dysregulation can compromise ATP synthesis and disrupt quality control pathways. Meanwhile, an imbalance in fission and fusion reshapes the mitochondrial network, affecting bioenergetic efficiency and redox homeostasis. Additionally, insufficient mitophagy allows damaged mitochondria to persist, further amplifying dysfunction (11, 12). Furthermore, since mitochondria operate within a broader network of organelles, exchanging metabolites and signals with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and lysosomes through membrane contact sites (MCSs), this indicates that senescence-associated remodeling may extend beyond the organelle itself to include inter-organelle communication (13). As these features are context-dependent and can vary across models and cell types, no single assay can definitively capture senescence-associated mitochondrial dysfunction. This underscores the need for an integrated, multi-parametric assessment that combines complementary assays of bioenergetics, redox state, ∆Ψm, mitochondrial dynamics/turnover, and mitochondria–organelle contacts. This mini-review offers a comprehensive overview of the methods used to assess senescence-associated mitochondrial dysfunction. It emphasizes key areas such as bioenergetic decline, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dynamics, mitophagy, and inter-organelle communication. We compare traditional biochemical assays with emerging imaging and biosensor techniques, outlining the strengths and limitations of each approach. Additionally, we propose a framework for selecting assays based on complementary readouts, facilitating a multidimensional analysis of mitochondrial function in senescence research.

BIOENERGETIC DECLINE IN CELLULAR SENESCENCE

Bioenergetic decline across senescence models

Bioenergetic decline is a common characteristic of cellular senescence observed in various experimental models, including RS, OIS, and SIPS. For instance, in replicative senescence, senescent fibroblasts display altered respiration, marked by increased proton leak and decreased respiratory control, indicating impaired OXPHOS coupling (14). Telomere-dependent senescence has also been linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, changes in ∆Ψm, and increased oxidative stress. This suggests that mitochondrial impairment may accompany and influence cellular senescence (15). In OIS, mitochondrial dysfunction has been identified as a contributing factor. Pharmacological or genetic interference with the electron transport chain can promote senescence-associated phenotypes (16). Similarly, SIPS models induced by genotoxic or oxidative stress often show reduced ATP-linked respiration and mitochondrial depolarization, further highlighting the prevalence of bioenergetic impairment in senescence (17, 18). Notably, senescent cells often show an imbalance in both the quantity and quality of mitochondria. While mitochondrial mass may increase, their performance decreases when adjusted for mitochondrial content (19, 20). This restricts the interpretability of individual metrics like basal OCR or total ATP. Instead, respiration should be analyzed using coupling-related parameters—ATP-linked respiration, proton leak, and maximal capacity—along with measures of mitochondrial content and changes in cell-state. This multi-parameter assessment is crucial, as energetic insufficiency and inefficient coupling can impact stress signaling, proteostasis, and secretory programs (21).

Practical assays to quantify bioenergetic decline

A practical assessment of senescence-associated bioenergetic decline is best achieved by combining respiration profiling, ATP output, and ∆Ψm as complementary measurements. This approach is particularly important in cellular senescence, as mitochondrial abundance can increase while the energetic efficiency per mitochondrion declines. Consequently, relying on single endpoints, such as basal OCR or total ATP, may be misleading.

Respiration profiling using extracellular flux analysis (e.g., Seahorse) measures OCR in intact cells and allows for coupling-relevant interpretations through standard inhibitor and uncoupler sequences (oligomycin, FCCP, and rotenone/antimycin A) (22-24). Across various cellular senescence models, a consistent pattern has emerged: a decrease in ATP-linked respiration and an increase in proton leak. This finding suggests impaired OXPHOS coupling, even when basal OCR remains stable or undergoes changes. Therefore, reporting should focus on ATP-linked respiration and proton leak, along with maximal respiration and spare capacity, rather than relying solely on basal OCR. Since cellular senescence affects cell size and composition, OCR should not be presented without normalization (e.g., cell number, DNA content, or total protein) and should ideally be interpreted alongside a mitochondrial content metric to distinguish between quantity and performance. ATP output can be measured using luciferase-based ATP kits, providing a convenient orthogonal endpoint (25, 26). However, these assays typically report total cellular ATP and are influenced by production, consumption, and compensatory glycolysis. Therefore, ATP measurements should be interpreted alongside OCR decomposition–particularly ATP-linked respiration–and under tightly standardized handling conditions. ∆Ψm provides a mechanistic readout that links electron transport to ATP synthesis and can support coupling defects inferred from OCR and ATP assays (9). ∆Ψm can be quantified by flow cytometry or live-cell imaging to track spatial and temporal changes at the single-cell level. While JC-1 provides a ratiometric signal, it is sensitive to mitochondrial content and dye behavior across different cell types. In contrast, TMRM/TMRE can offer a more controlled readout, especially when staining conditions, such as non-quench mode, are clearly defined (27-29). In either case, a depolarizing control (e.g., FCCP) is essential for validating the dynamic range. Collectively, a minimal assay bundle for assessing bioenergetic decline in senescence includes (i) Seahorse OCR profiling with coupling-focused parameters, (ii) a luciferase-based ATP assay, and (iii) ∆Ψm measurement (preferably TMRM with JC-1 as an alternative), ideally complemented by a straightforward measure of mitochondrial abundance (e.g., TOM20/VDAC or mtDNA copy number).

OXIDATIVE STRESS AND MTROS ELEVATION IN CELLULAR SENESCENCE

mtROS elevation as a functional signature of senescence-associated mitochondrial dysfunction

In cellular senescence, mitochondrial oxidative stress, particularly elevated mtROS, is commonly observed and indicates a decline in mitochondrial function. Increased proton leakage and reduced or heterogeneous mitochondrial ∆Ψm can lead to electron leakage from the electron transport chain, resulting in excessive mtROS generation (8, 10, 30). Importantly, the elevation of mtROS is not merely a downstream consequence of cellular senescence. Multiple lines of evidence indicate that mtROS can participate in self-reinforcing circuits that stabilize and amplify senescence programs by sustaining oxidative damage and stress signaling (9, 12, 31). Consistent with section 2, changes in mtROS have been reported across major senescence paradigms. In replicative and telomere-dependent senescence, progressive mitochondrial dysfunction and increased ROS production are associated with the progression and variability of senescence development. Delaying mitochondrial dysfunction may also delay replicative senescence (15, 32, 33). In OIS, mitochondrial dysfunction serves as an effector pathway. Genetic or pharmacological interference with the electron transport chain can trigger senescence-associated phenotypes, supporting a direct relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction and the development of cellular senescence (16, 34, 35). In SIPS, various stresses, such as chemicals and ionizing radiation, frequently trigger mitochondrial depolarization and an oxidative stress response. MtROS often result in persistent damage signals and contribute to the stabilization of the phenotype (18, 36, 37). These observations collectively support the view that mtROS are a common functional signature of senescence-associated mitochondrial dysfunction and, in certain contexts, act as a driver that reinforces senescence by perpetuating oxidative damage and pro-senescent signaling.

Practical assays to quantify global and mitochondrial ROS in senescence

A variety of fluorescent probes have been developed to measure intracellular ROS, and these assays are widely used in senescence research. Since mitochondria-derived ROS can play a role in both the induction and maintenance of senescence, it is often beneficial to assess global intracellular oxidative stress in conjunction with mitochondria-enriched ROS, rather than relying on a single measurement (38). DCFH-DA is one of the most commonly used probes for measuring global ROS. After cellular uptake and de-esterification, the non-fluorescent DCFH is converted into the fluorescent DCF through oxidation in the presence of cellular oxidants. This process makes DCFH-DA a valuable indicator of intracellular oxidative stress (39). However, DCFH-DA lacks source specificity and has well-known limitations in interpretation. Due to its non-specificity and susceptibility to probe chemistry and redox cycling, DCFH-DA should be interpreted as a global indicator of oxidative stress rather than a specific readout of mtROS (38, 40). Mitochondria-targeted dyes, such as MitoSOX, are ethidium-derived probes designed to detect mitochondrial superoxide in live cells. They are commonly used to assess ROS in mitochondria (41-43). This method is practical and widely adopted; however, careful interpretation is necessary because ethidium-based probes can produce non-specific oxidation products, and fluorescence may vary between proliferating and senescent cells. When it is essential to attribute specific ROS to particular subcellular compartments, genetically encoded sensors offer a complementary option. For example, HyPer reports on the dynamics of hydrogen peroxide in living cells, while roGFP2–Orp1-type relays provide sensitive H2O2-linked redox reporting in defined compartments (44, 45). The most important aspect is to select a method that aligns with the question you wish to investigate (for example, screening versus mechanism). Your conclusions will be strongest if they are supported by at least two different tests. In summary, a minimal assay bundle for assessing oxidative stress in senescence includes (i) DCFH-DA to capture global intracellular ROS, (ii) MitoSOX to evaluate mitochondria-enriched superoxide, and (iii) a mitochondrial functional anchor, such as ∆Ψm, with biosensor-based validation (e.g., HyPer or roGFP2–Orp1) when higher specificity is needed.

MITOCHONDRIAL QUALITY CONTROL FAILURE IN CELLULAR SENESCENCE

Mitochondrial quality control failure in cellular senescence: mitochondrial dynamics–mitophagy coupling and its functional consequences

Mitochondrial quality control depends on the coordinated remodeling and turnover of the mitochondrial network (46). Mitochondrial dynamics, which refers to the balance between fission and fusion, regulate network architecture and compartmentalization. Meanwhile, mitophagy selectively removes damaged mitochondrial segments that are delivered to the lysosome. Together, these processes maintain mitochondrial integrity and adaptability under various stresses (47-49). When this quality-control axis is compromised, dysfunctional mitochondria can persist and affect various cellular processes, including mtDNA distribution and maintenance, respiratory performance, apoptotic susceptibility, calcium homeostasis, and mtROS production, ultimately shaping the senescence phenotype (8, 9, 47). In various senescence models, the balance between fission and fusion, as well as impaired mitophagy flux, are often disrupted, indicating a failure to effectively segregate and eliminate damaged mitochondrial compartments (12, 47). In numerous models, mitochondrial networks appear more elongated or hyperconnected, while mitophagy flux is attenuated. This provides a mechanistic framework for the accumulation of dysfunctional, ROS-producing mitochondria, despite an increase in mitochondrial content (18, 50-53). In conjunction with Sections 2-3, this supports a quantity–quality mismatch in cellular senescence, characterized by an increase in mitochondrial abundance alongside a reduction in ∆Ψm stability and redox control.

It is important to note that these trends do not occur universally. The shift in mitochondrial remodeling towards fragmentation rather than elongation depends on the specific cell type and the trigger for senescence. Furthermore, the regulation of mitophagy can vary in both direction and magnitude, particularly under stress-induced conditions (54-58). Consequently, mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy should be viewed as regulated, stimulus-dependent processes. Conclusions must be supported by complementary functional assessments of bioenergetics, ∆Ψm, and oxidative stress.

Practical assays to quantify dynamics and mitophagy

Live-cell fluorescence imaging is essential for assessing mitochondrial dynamics, as it captures network behavior in real time. Mitochondria can be labeled with mitochondria-targeting dyes, such as MitoTracker-type reagents, or with genetically encoded mitochondrial fluorescent proteins like mt-GFP and mt-RFP. This labeling allows for the quantification of changes in network connectivity and architecture (9, 59). Dye-based labeling is experimentally straightforward; however, signals can decay and are sensitive to loading conditions and mitochondrial state. In addition, an excessive concentration of dye can promote non-specific cytosolic or off-target organelle accumulation and dye aggregation, generating false-positive puncta that may be misinterpreted as mitochondrial structures. To minimize these artifacts, dyes should be titrated to the lowest effective concentration and appropriate background controls (e.g., unstained cells) should be included. In contrast, fluorescent protein labeling supports longer-term imaging with a more stable signal, although expression levels must be carefully controlled to minimize perturbation. To reduce subjective interpretation, mitochondrial morphology should be reported using quantitative network metrics (e.g., length, branching, and connectivity) derived from standardized analysis protocols (60, 61).

Mitophagy measurements are most informative when they capture flux rather than relying on single time-point co-localization. While conventional co-staining strategies, such as staining mitochondria with LC3 or lysosomal markers, can provide supportive evidence, they may overestimate productive mitophagy. This is because punctate LC3 signals do not always indicate lysosomal delivery (62, 63). pH-sensitive reporters, such as mt-Keima, provide a more direct flux readout by distinguishing mitochondria in neutral and acidic environments. This capability allows for the quantification of mitochondria delivered to lysosomes in intact cells (64, 65). Commercial mitophagy dyes and probes can also be used for rapid screening; however, the interpretation of the flux is most reliable when supported by pH-based reporters and complementary controls, such as lysosomal inhibition, which verify lysosome-dependent delivery (66).

Electron microscopy (EM) offers high-resolution structural validation of mitochondrial morphology and mitophagy-associated structures in fixed specimens, complementing live-cell and flux-based assays. However, conventional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) relies on single sections, limiting its ability to capture the complete shape of individual mitochondria or the complexity of mitochondrial networks within cells (63). To address this limitation, volume approaches like electron tomography, which reconstructs 3D architecture from stacked serial images, have been adopted. Additionally, high-resolution 3D EM methods, including focused ion beam–SEM (FIB-SEM), allow for more precise analysis of mitochondrial morphology and network organization (67). Quantitative interpretation can be enhanced by using image-analysis tools, such as Mitochondrial Analyzer (MiNA), to extract single-cell network parameters like length, branching, and connectivity, followed by computational analysis (61, 68). Recently, multi-dimensional morphology features have been integrated with AI-based machine learning approaches to provide a more objective, reproducible, and high-throughput evaluation of mitochondrial dynamics (48, 69). Taken together, a minimal assay bundle for studying dynamics and mitophagy in cellular senescence includes: (i) live-cell mitochondrial network imaging with quantitative morphology metrics (e.g., length, branching, and connectivity); (ii) a mitophagy flux readout, preferably mt-Keima, with pH-sensitive alternatives as options; and (iii) one functional anchor from Sections 2-3, such as ∆Ψm and/or coupling-focused respiration, with mtROS included as needed. Ideally, this should be complemented by optional high-resolution structural validation using EM when required.

INTER-ORGANELLE COMMUNICATION FAILURE IN CELLULAR SENESCENCE

ER–mitochondria and mitochondria–lysosome contact remodeling across senescence models

Inter-organelle communication is increasingly recognized as a modifiable factor in senescence-associated mitochondrial dysfunction (13, 70, 71). Although mitochondria form contact sites with multiple organelles, this section focuses on ER–mitochondria contact sites (MERCs/MAMs) and mitochondria–lysosome interfaces. These hubs directly facilitate Ca2＋ and lipid exchange, coordinate fission and turnover, and influence mitochondrial energetics and stress signaling (70, 72-75).

As noted in earlier sections, changes in organelle interactions have been reported in major senescence paradigms, although their direction and functional consequences can vary. In replicative or telomere-dependent senescence, alterations in the association between the ER and mitochondria, along with inter-compartmental Ca2＋ handling, have been linked to changes in mitochondrial metabolism and stress signaling. This suggests that rewiring of the contact site can influence mitochondrial performance during the progression of senescence (15, 76). In OIS, perturbations in ER–mitochondria Ca2＋ signaling and contact architecture have been identified as modulators of senescence-associated programs. Additionally, experimental manipulation of ER–mitochondria coupling has been shown to influence senescence phenotypes under specific conditions (77). In SIPS, contact remodeling has been observed, with reports indicating that the structural expansion of MERCs can occur alongside a reduced Ca2＋ flux from the ER to the mitochondria. This underscores that physical proximity does not necessarily lead to increased functional transfer (78).

Functionally, the remodeling of MERCs can alter mitochondrial Ca2＋ uptake and phospholipid exchange, contributing to the initiation or stabilization of cellular senescence (13). In parallel, mitochondria-lysosome contacts have also been associated with mitochondrial turnover and inflammatory responses (79). This provides a mechanistic link to the quality control defects described in Section 4. However, these observations require careful interpretation. Reported changes in MERCs (whether increases or decreases) depend on cell type, the senescence trigger, and timing, and are also sensitive to methodological choices. Furthermore, “more contact” does not necessarily imply greater Ca2＋ or lipid transfer, as the composition of contact-sites can change independently of the contact surface area. Thus, conclusions are most reliable when structural contact measurements are combined with functional transfer assays and mitochondrial readouts.

Practical assays to quantify organelle contacts and link them to function

A practical assessment of contact remodeling in cellular senescence is best achieved by combining a structural proximity readout with a functional transfer assay, rather than relying solely on morphology. EM-based analysis offers a high-resolution benchmark for organelle apposition in fixed specimens and enables quantification of contact distance and coverage. However, conventional TEM is limited by single-section sampling and cannot capture dynamic remodeling. To enhance network-level interpretation, 3D approaches such as electron tomography or volume EM (e.g., FIB-SEM) can serve as confirmatory endpoints when ultrastructural resolution is necessary (80, 81).

Live-cell proximity reporters provide scalable alternatives for quantitative contact measurements. FRET-based sensors can indicate close-range proximity but are sensitive to photophysics and background noise. In contrast, BRET-based sensors minimize excitation-related autofluorescence and have been used as MERC distance indicators (82). SPLICS offers split-fluorophore contact site sensors that produce punctate signals when two organelles come within specific distance ranges. This capability allows for the quantification of contact distribution and dynamics in intact cells (83). Reporter geometry and expression can influence apparent proximity, so structural conclusions are strengthened by additional validation.

Immunodetection-based approaches, such as proximity ligation assay (PLA) provide an antibody-based method to visualize the proximity between known contact-enriched protein pairs in cells and tissues. This offers an independent readout that complements genetically encoded reporters and EM (84, 85). As in earlier sections, confidence in contact remodeling is maximized when findings are validated with independent structural assays, such as combining SPLICS/BRET with PLA or EM.

Collectively, a minimal assay bundle for studying organelle-contact remodeling in cellular senescence includes: (i) a live-cell proximity reporter (SPLICS preferred; BRET/FRET as alternatives); (ii) an orthogonal structural validation method (PLA and/or EM); and (iii) a functional transfer readout (mitochondrial Ca2＋ uptake and/or a lipid-transfer proxy), interpreted alongside mitochondrial outputs (∆Ψm and/or coupling-focused respiration, with mtROS as needed).

INTEGRATIVE PERSPECTIVE & FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Mitochondrial dysfunction in cellular senescence is best understood as a systems-level breakdown rather than a defect in a single pathway. Key interconnected issues include bioenergetic inefficiency, loss of redox control, impaired dynamics and mitophagy, and rewiring of organelle contacts. Disruption in one area can impact the others, reinforcing long-term characteristics of senescence. This perspective highlights a recurring theme: the imbalance between the quantity and quality of mitochondria. While mitochondrial abundance may increase, their performance and stress control per mitochondrion may decline, complicating the interpretation of individual endpoints.

Recent advancements in tools have made multidimensional, intact-cell profiling more feasible. Genetically encoded biosensors, quantitative live-cell imaging, and computational pipelines now allow for the simultaneous tracking of ATP, ∆Ψm, mtROS, network architecture, and flux-based quality control. This approach helps to elucidate heterogeneity within senescent populations and differentiate adaptive metabolic adjustments from persistent dysfunction. However, a remaining challenge in fully characterizing senescence-associated heterogeneity is the mismatch in measurement scales. Biochemical assays of mitochondrial bioenergetics typically generate population-averaged readouts, capturing global shifts such as reduced ATP production or increased proton leak. While informative, these bulk measurements obscure cell-to-cell variation within senescent populations. By contrast, advanced imaging approaches provide single-cell or even sub-organelle resolution, revealing features such as heterogeneous network architectures and variable mitophagy flux. As a result, interpretations can diverge between bulk and single-cell analyses, and reliance on either approach alone may yield an incomplete view of mitochondrial dysfunction in senescence. Bridging this gap will require integrative study designs that explicitly connect population-level energetics with single-cell structural and flux readouts to enable a more comprehensive, multidimensional evaluation of senescence-associated mitochondrial function. Additionally, the remodeling of ER–mitochondria and mitochondria–lysosome interfaces is receiving attention as a coordinating layer that influences Ca2＋ and lipid exchange, fission-site specification, and turnover. However, the precise role of these interactions–whether as a driver, consequence, or feedback mechanism–remains unclear and requires paired structural and functional validation. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of senescence-associated mitochondrial dysfunction and the development of targeted therapeutic strategies can lay the groundwork for next-generation senolytics and senomorphics. This knowledge can also facilitate biomarker-guided stratification of senescent states, high-content screening for context-matched vulnerabilities, and more quantitative evaluations of serotherapies across various disease models.
