Vitamin D, allergen sensitization, and atopic dermatitis☆
Authors: Öner Özdemir
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

I have read the article titled by Shen et al with great interest.1 However, while reading and discussing this study with our fellows in the pediatric allergy division during one of our routine morning meetings, some questions arose in our minds. And clarification of these questions of this study will help understand better of the study.

First, while it is plausible that vitamin D deficiency may increase allergen sensitization,2 it is an ambitious and controversial hypothesis to suggest that its deficiency increases susceptibility to atopic dermatitis development.1 Atopic dermatitis is known to be a genetically inherited skin disease. The pathophysiology of atopic dermatitis is complex and multifactorial. It also embraces a defect in the epidermal barrier, a changed immune response, and dysbiosis in the skin's microbiota.2, 3, 4 Allergen sensitization may be caused by this skin disorder on its own as well, independent of serum vitamin D levels. It is also possible that the vitamin D deficiency or low vitamin D levels seen in this disease may be the result of these patients avoiding sun exposure or being protected from going outside by their families because of the disease.5

Although it is logical that vitamin D deficiency may increase allergen sensitization through the immune system, it is very difficult to believe that it will cause atopic dermatitis on its own, as mentioned many times in the text.1, 2, 3, 4 Low vitamin D levels may trigger clinical manifestations of atopic dermatitis such as skin inflammation and increasing disease severity by causing sensitization or through the immune system, but it does not cause disease on its own.6,7

It would have been nice if this article had more explicitly discussed the effects of vitamin D levels on the severity and course of atopic dermatitis, as well as whether vitamin D levels can actually cause the development of atopic dermatitis per se.

Second, the phrase "IgE sensitization" appears in the main text and in some places, including figures, but this term is not an accurate description as used in the article.1 The terms food, inhalant, mite, and allergen sensitization are meaningful and appropriate, but "IgE sensitization" does not mean anything to us as allergists.

IgE does not cause sensitization, but the sensitizations that do occur raise total IgE levels. Also here, sensitization should have been described based on specific IgE values, not total IgE. Specific IgE is something that shows the presence of sensitization, not total IgE level. In the studies reported in the literature on "IgE sensitization",8,9 specific IgE values were always studied and evaluated in the subjects in contrast to this reported study.1

Third, a total serum IgE level above 100 kU/L may be significant under the age of 6 years but may not always indicate IgE sensitization or allergen sensitization as it is discussed. However, it should be investigated further and sensitization should be suspected.10

Food and mite sensitizations lead to an increase in specific IgE. Not every increase in specific IgE leads to an increase in total IgE, and an increase in total IgE may not always indicate an increase in specific IgE. These situations should have been taken into account when evaluating the results of this study.

Fourth, although it is thought that six-month-old infants may develop mite sensitization, what this means in an infant can be discussed. In addition, it is equally difficult to explain the relationship between mite sensitization detected in a six-month-old baby and atopic dermatitis (fig. 2C).1 Father atopy is mentioned in the figure 2C as well but never discussed in the article text.1 Although these data are given as graphs/figures in the article, probably for this reason, they are not given as results and are not discussed.1

Minor concern, Fig. 2C is included in the data but seems to have been omitted from the main text without any mention. Fig. 2B is referenced in the text, but Fig. 2C data is given.1

In conclusion; on behalf of all readers, I would like to thank the authors for their seminal work on this controversial and interesting topic.
