Quantitative analysis of tritiated water using cavity ring-down spectroscopy
Authors: Ryohei Terabayashi, Erika Takayama, Hideki Tomita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cavity ring-down spectroscopy (CRDS) offers exceptional sensitivity for trace-gas detection, yet its application to quantitative tritium analysis has remained largely unexplored. In this study, we demonstrate mid-infrared CRDS targeting the fundamental \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu _{1}$$\end{document} vibrational band of tritiated water (HTO) near \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4.3}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m. By exploiting this strong fundamental absorption, the system achieves substantially improved detection sensitivity of HTO compared with previous optical approaches. For the first time, a proportional response to tritium activity was experimentally verified with high linearity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}^{2}$$\end{document} > 0.99) and excellent repeatability was demonstrated. Practical detection limits were rigorously assessed by accounting for sample introduction and utilization efficiency, enabling a realistic performance evaluation under operational conditions. The system enables quantitative measurements of microliter-scale samples and offers potential for rapid tritium assessment in fusion and accelerator facilities. These results establish a scalable spectroscopic framework for tritium analysis and provide a pathway toward future ultra-high-sensitivity implementations based on advanced CRDS architectures.

Introduction

Tritium (3H, T) is a radioactive isotope of hydrogen with a half-life of 12.3 year. Most tritium currently present in the environment originates from anthropogenic activities, particularly atmospheric nuclear weapons testing conducted in the mid-20th century. Although tritium is also produced naturally through cosmic-ray interactions, localized and intermittent releases associated with the operation, decommissioning, and accidental events of nuclear facilities have been reported1,2. In certain environments, especially in the vicinity of such facilities or immediately following discharge, the anthropogenic contribution can exceed the natural background level. Consequently, accurate and rapid determination of tritium concentrations in wastewater, waste materials, and exhaust gas is essential for environmental monitoring and regulatory compliance.

A large quantity of radionuclides was released into the environment during the 2011 accident at the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant (FDNPP). As these included tritium as well as 137Cs, investigations into their environmental impact are ongoing3. Furthermore, a large volume of water was used in the process of cooling the damaged reactors. This water was treated to remove most radioactive isotopes, although tritium was not removed, and was stored. Since August 2023, the treated water has been discharged into the ocean4. Japan imposes a concentration limit of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${60,000}\,\hbox {BqL}^{-1}$$\end{document} on effluent from nuclear facilities5, necessitating simple, robust, and highly sensitive analytical techniques capable of reliably assessing such levels. In addition, tritium monitoring is required in the operation of the accelerator facilities6,7 as well as nuclear fusion experimental devices or reactors. Larsen et al. discuss that environmental releases of fusion-produced tritium are challenging to fully eliminate, and that the widespread deployment of fusion energy may lead to a measurable increase in global background tritium levels8, highlighting the necessity for sensitive and long-term environmental tritium monitoring. Beyond nuclear and accelerator facilities, tritium is widely employed as a biological tracer in pharmacokinetic and related studies due to its chemical equivalence to hydrogen9,10.

The current standard method for tritium analysis is liquid scintillation counting (LSC)11,12, which detects the low-energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-ray (mean energy: 5.7 keV) emitted during radioactive decay. In recent years, high-sensitivity LSC measurements have been increasingly reported, driven by the growing analytical demand associated with the severe accident and subsequent decommissioning of FDNPP13. Alternative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-ray counting methods, such as those employing ionization chambers or proportional counters14–16, have also been developed. Although \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-ray spectrometry offers high sensitivity, accurate quantification of tritium without prior radiochemical separation is inherently challenging because its \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} emission exhibits a continuous energy spectrum at very low energies. In the presence of other \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}- or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-emitting radionuclides, spectral overlap and variations in detection efficiency can lead to significant interference, precluding unambiguous attribution of the observed signal to tritium alone. Consequently, chemical separation procedures to remove interfering nuclides are generally required prior to measurement17. Such preprocessing steps substantially increase analytical complexity and represent a major bottleneck for the rapid analysis of nuclear samples containing multiple radionuclides. As a result, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-ray counting techniques are poorly suited for quantitative, real-time monitoring of large-volume samples such as exhaust gas, forcing facilities to adopt operational constraints such as temporary gas storage7.

Tritium quantification via mass spectrometry is typically achieved either by direct 3H counting using accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS)18 or by measuring accumulated 3He, the stable daughter nuclide produced by 3H decay, through the ingrowth method19,20. Direct AMS enables highly sensitive tritium detection but requires complex, large-scale instrumentation. In contrast, the 3He ingrowth method achieves exceptionally low detection limits; however, it requires waiting for tritium to decay and produce a measurable amount of 3He, resulting in extended analysis times for low-concentration samples. Moreover, mass spectrometric techniques generally rely on batch-type sample preparation and measurement, making them inherently unsuitable for continuous or high-throughput monitoring. Accordingly, these mass-spectrometric approaches are primarily applied to specialized environmental studies and reference measurements rather than routine or rapid monitoring.

To address the growing demand for more rapid and simplified tritium analysis, optical spectroscopy targeting the infrared absorption of gaseous tritiated water (HTO) has emerged as a compelling alternative.This approach offers a key intrinsic advantage in that it requires no complex chemical pretreatment: HTO can be introduced directly into the sensing cell in the vapor phase. Among various optical techniques, cavity ring-down spectroscopy (CRDS)21,22 is particularly noteworthy for its exceptional sensitivity, achieved through an ultra-long effective optical path length within a high-finesse resonator. CRDS has already demonstrated the capability to detect 14C at sub-natural abundance levels23–27.

Recent developments in optical gas sensing have enabled simultaneous multi-species detection using advanced spectroscopic techniques, including QCL-based dual spectroscopy28, self-calibrated 2f/1f wavelength modulation spectroscopy29, quartz tuning fork–enhanced spectroscopy30, multi-resonant photoacoustic spectroscopy31, and direct frequency-comb-based CRDS32. These approaches represent significant technological advances toward versatile gas sensor platforms capable of addressing practical applications in environmental monitoring, industrial process control, and complex gas analysis. Compared with these broadband or multi-species sensing strategies, the present work focuses specifically on establishing a quantitative and isotopically selective CRDS framework for HTO in the mid-infrared fundamental band. Such a robust single-species foundation is an essential step toward future multi-component tritium monitoring systems based on advanced CRDS architectures.

Optical approaches targeting HTO have also been reported, including tunable diode laser absorption spectroscopy (TDLAS)33, CRDS in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${2.17}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m region by Brey et al.34 and cantilever-enhanced photoacoustic spectroscopy (CEPAS) by Karhu et al.35. However, these studies were largely limited to the observation of absorption spectra and, to date, a demonstration of quantitative tritium analysis using spectroscopic methods, including an assessment of quantitative linearity and accuracy across varying concentrations, has not yet been achieved.

In this study, we focus on the fundamental \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu _1$$\end{document} vibrational band of HTO, which exhibits strong absorption intensity in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4.3}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m region, and develop a CRDS spectrometer operating at this wavelength. Using this system, we clearly observe the infrared absorption spectrum of HTO and, for the first time, demonstrate quantitative linearity across samples with different concentrations, including an evaluation of isotopic concentration precision through repeated measurements of identical samples.

Nevertheless, HTO absorption spectroscopy remains challenged by cross-sensitivity and spectral interference arising from overlapping transitions of water isotopologues and other background species. In addition, instrumental effects such as etalon fringes36 can introduce baseline distortions that further complicate quantitative analysis. These effects collectively limit quantitative accuracy. Advanced CRDS architectures, including saturated CRDS23,37 and multi-color CRDS26,38, may offer enhanced selectivity and sensitivity. However, these techniques introduce additional experimental complexity. The present work therefore focuses on establishing a robust linear CRDS framework for quantitative HTO analysis as a necessary step toward more advanced implementations.

Methods

HTO transition simulation

Spectroscopic parameters of molecular transitions and absorption features are publicly available in databases such as HITRAN39; however, HTO has not yet been included in the current HITRAN compilation. Therefore, in this work, previously reported spectroscopic data were utilized, including Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) measurements of the HTO \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu _{1}$$\end{document} band absorption lines40 and computational predictions of line positions and transition strengths from the SPECTRA database41,42.

Simulations were performed using a methodology previously developed for a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${2.17}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m CRDS spectrometer for HTO detection43, here extended to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m region. Figure 1(a) shows the calculated transition intensities of relevant water isotopologues. Among the infrared absorption lines of HTO, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu _{1}$$\end{document} transitions located around 2300 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {cm}^{-1}$$\end{document} ( \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4.3}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m ) are well-separated from those of other isotopologues and exhibit relatively high line intensities. These characteristics motivated our focus on this specific spectral region.

Expected absorption spectra were calculated using a line-by-line approach based on the HITRAN Application Programming Interface (HAPI)44, supplemented with the additional HTO dataset. Spectral simulations were conducted over the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu _{1}$$\end{document} band range to identify candidate HTO absorption lines satisfying two primary criteria: (i) strong line intensity and (ii) high isotopic selectivity relative to stable water isotopologues. While several candidate transitions met these criteria, the selection was constrained by the tuning range of the diode laser source available for this study. Consequently, a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4.3}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m CRDS system was constructed targeting the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu _{1}$$\end{document} fundamental transition at 2311.2 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {cm}^{-1}$$\end{document}, corresponding to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1 0 0)\, 1_{01} \leftarrow (0 0 0)\,0_{00}$$\end{document} transition. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(v_1 v_2 v_3)$$\end{document} represent the vibrational quantum numbers for the symmetric stretch, bending, and asymmetric stretch modes, respectively, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_{K_a K_c}$$\end{document} denote the rotational quantum numbers in the asymmetric-top notation.

Cavity ringdown setup

The schematic of the experimental setup is shown in Figure 2, including a representative ringdown signal and its corresponding fitting residuals obtained under vacuum conditions. All optical components were mounted on a 60 cm \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 45 cm water-cooled optical breadboard.

To access the HTO absorption line in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4.3}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m region, a continuous-wave distributed-feedback quantum cascade laser (DFB-QCL; Hamamatsu, L12004-2310H-E) was employed as the probe light source and driven by a dedicated QCL controller (Hamamatsu, C16174-01). The laser output first passed through an optical isolator (Thorlabs, I4500W4, 30 dB isolation at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4.5}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m) to suppress optical feedback to the laser. The beam polarization was then adjusted using a half-wave plate before being directed into an acousto-optic modulator (AOM; Isomet, M1208-G80-4).

The first-order diffracted beam from the AOM was guided through a second optical isolator and a mode-matching telescope before coupling into the optical cavity. The second isolator plays a critical role in fully suppressing residual optical feedback from the high-reflectivity cavity mirrors, which is essential for stable operation of the QCL under high-finesse cavity conditions. The laser power was measured to be 30 mW at the QCL output and 1.5 mW at the cavity entrance.

Mode matching was performed using a telescope consisting of a plano-convex lens with focal length of 30 mm placed 300 mm from the QCL output, followed by a second plano-convex lens with 40 mm focal length positioned 73 mm downstream. The cavity center was located 430 mm after the second lens. The optical cavity was constructed on an ultra-low-thermal-expansion (ULE) glass plate (Ohara, CLEARCERAM-Z) and housed inside a square gas cell. Two-axis flexure mounts were fixed on the base plate to hold a pair of high-reflectivity silicon mirrors (LohnStar Optics, reflectivity of 99.994%, evaluated by the decay-time constant of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${16.18}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}sec, rear surface anti-reflection coated) with a radius of curvature of 1 m and a mirror separation of 270 mm. The resulting free spectral range (FSR) of the cavity is 555 MHz. This configuration yields an effective optical path length (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\mathrm{eff}} = c\tau$$\end{document}) of 4.85 km, providing the physical basis for the high-sensitivity measurement of trace absorption features. To suppress unwanted etalon effects, the input mirror was equipped with a wedged rear surface (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${0.5}^{\circ }$$\end{document}). A piezoelectric actuator was attached to the output mirror, allowing the cavity length to be scanned over one free spectral range (FSR). Sapphire windows were installed at both ends of the gas cell to transmit mid-infrared radiation. To reduce unnecessary dead volume inside the gas cell, aluminum-alloy spacers were introduced around the cavity region to occupy the remaining space, thereby minimizing the total internal volume without disturbing the cavity resonance.

Light transmitted through the cavity was detected using a liquid-nitrogen-cooled InSb photodetector (Teledyne Judson, J10D-M204-R500U-30) with an active area diameter of 0.50 mm, a peak responsivity of 3.0 A/W, and a specific detectivity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {D}^{*}$$\end{document} of 1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times ~10^{11}$$\end{document} cm \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {Hz}^{1/2}~\hbox {W}^{-1}$$\end{document} at 1 kHz and Noise-Equivalent Power (NEP) of 0.4 pW \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {Hz}^{-1/2}$$\end{document}. The detector output was amplified using a low-noise, gain-adjustable transimpedance amplifier (FEMTO, DLPCA-200), operated at a transimpedance gain of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}^{5}$$\end{document} V/A with a bandwidth of 400 kHz. When the transmitted intensity exceeded a predefined threshold set by a comparator circuit, the AOM rapidly switched off the first-order diffracted beam, initiating a cavity ring-down event. The resulting ring-down signals were digitized using a high-resolution waveform digitizer (National Instruments, PXI-5922) operated at a sampling rate of 5 MS/s with a voltage resolution of 20 bits. The 5 MS/s sampling rate was chosen to accurately resolve the baseline decay time of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${16.18}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}s. The ring-down decay curves were fitted with single-exponential functions in LabVIEW to extract the decay constants. An FPGA-based multifunction module (National Instruments, PXIe-7856R) was used to control the QCL current modulation for spectral scanning, generate digital timing signals for AOM switching, and monitor the gas cell pressure.

As a reference, using the incident power (1.5 mW), the mirror reflectivity (99.994%) and its manufacturer-specified transmission (30 ppm), and a typical transmitted fringe amplitude of 0.4 V, the coupled intracavity power can be calculated to be 40 mW. This value is primarily limited by the linewidth mismatch between the free-running DFB-QCL (<1 MHz) and the narrow cavity resonance (11 kHz), as well as the transient nature of the PZT scanning. While this efficiency is sufficient for generating the ring-down signals in the present study, further improvement could be achieved by implementing active frequency locking, such as the Pound–Drever–Hall technique 45, to maintain a steady-state resonant condition.

In the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4.3}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m spectral region, strong absorption by atmospheric \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CO}_{2}$$\end{document} poses a significant challenge for free-space optical measurements. Near the HTO absorption peak, approximately 5 % of the laser power would be attenuated over a free-space path length of only 10 cm in ambient air. Although cavity ring-down spectroscopy is nominally insensitive to absorption occurring outside the optical cavity, strong extra-cavity absorption can severely limit the optical power available for CRDS measurement. Consequently, excessive atmospheric \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CO}_{2}$$\end{document} absorption not only reduces the signal-to-noise ratio but also destabilizes cavity coupling and ring-down triggering, thereby introducing substantial interference into CRDS measurements.

To mitigate these effects, the entire optical setup was enclosed in a nitrogen-purged enclosure, implemented using a simplified glovebox configuration. To accommodate this enclosure, a compact optical layout was specifically designed to ensure that the entire system remained within the water-cooled optical breadboard. This arrangement enabled optical alignment and optimization under low-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CO}_{2}$$\end{document} conditions and effectively minimized measurement artifacts arising from atmospheric \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CO}_{2}$$\end{document} absorption. By suppressing extra-cavity absorption losses, sufficient optical power could be delivered to the cavity entrance, enabling stable cavity buildup and reliable ring-down acquisition. As a result, stable and reproducible CRDS measurements in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4.3}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m region were achieved.

For the handling of unsealed tritiated samples, the system was initially assembled and its fundamental performance characterized in a standard laser laboratory. It was subsequently relocated to the radiation-controlled area of the Radioisotope Research Center at Nagoya University, where all experiments involving HTO were conducted. The ability to restore stable operation and high-precision measurements immediately after relocation highlights the robustness and portability of the present CRDS system.

Measurement procedure

Prior to each measurement, the gas cell was purged with high-purity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {N}_2$$\end{document} gas to suppress memory effects from the previous sample. After each closed measurement, a cleaning sequence was immediately initiated, consisting of three purge cycles, each comprising 2 min of evacuation to below 1 Pa followed by 8 min of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {N}_2$$\end{document} flow at 0.2 L \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {min}^{-1}$$\end{document} (resulting in a cell pressure of 2 kPa). After the third \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {N}_2$$\end{document} purge, the system was evacuated again for 2 min prior to the subsequent measurement, giving a total cleaning duration of approximately 32 min. During this process, switching between evacuation and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {N}_2$$\end{document} purging was controlled solely by opening and closing valve V0 (shown in Fig. 2), while the injection port remained connected to the vacuum pump. The spectral region shown in Fig. 3 was continuously monitored from the beginning of the cleaning process, and the next measurement was initiated only after confirming that no residual light-water absorption peak was present.

After completion of the cleaning sequence, valves V1 and V2 were closed to temporarily seal the gas cell. The microsyringe containing the prepared sample was then inserted into the septum, and valve V1 was opened to introduce the sample vapor into the gas cell. Once the absorption spectrum stabilized, valve V1 was closed to fully seal the gas cell, and spectra were recorded for 10 min under this closed condition (hereafter referred to as “closed measurement”). The microsyringe was subsequently withdrawn after sealing the gas cell to minimize contamination from ambient air.

To obtain spectra with reduced pressure broadening and hence narrower linewidths, valve V2 was momentarily opened to partially discharge the gas inside the cell, thereby lowering the internal pressure. After the pressure was stabilized at the reduced level, again, spectra were recorded for 10 min. This reduced-pressure measurement was introduced solely as a temporary measure to improve spectral resolution and may not be essential for routine operation.

Excluding this optional reduced-pressure step, the total time required from liquid sample injection to spectral acquisition–including vapor introduction, stabilization, and data recording–was 1000 s per sample. In the present work, conservative waiting and acquisition times were adopted to ensure sufficient precision; however, both the stabilization time and spectral acquisition time can be substantially shortened in future implementations without compromising the required analytical accuracy, particularly for high-throughput or continuous monitoring applications. In practice, the spectra used for quantitative evaluation in this study were obtained by averaging continuously acquired data over 100 s to 200 s, indicating that the effective acquisition time required for reliable analysis can be substantially shorter than the conservative durations adopted in the present measurement sequence.

Furthermore, the cleaning procedure between successive measurements was intentionally performed under conservative conditions to reliably suppress memory effects. Systematic optimization of the purging protocol–including reduction of purge duration and cycle number–together with improved mitigation of water adsorption on internal surfaces (e.g., surface treatment, temperature management, or material selection), would further decrease the overall measurement time and enhance the suitability of the system for rapid or high-throughput analysis.

This measurement and cleaning sequence was repeated for samples with different tritium concentrations.

Cell pressure measurement

Cell pressure was monitored using two different gauges depending on the experimental requirements. For the cleaning procedure, a Pirani gauge (Pfeiffer Vacuum, TPR281) was installed at the pump inlet to confirm sufficient evacuation (below 1 Pa) following \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {N}_2$$\end{document} purging cycles to suppress memory effects.

For quantitative spectral measurements, a capacitance manometer (MKS Baratron, 626B12TB, accuracy ±0.25% of reading) was used during validation experiments employing a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}L light-water sample prior to tritiated water measurements. Accordingly, under identical gas-handling conditions, the pressures corresponding to the higher- and lower-pressure spectra shown in Fig. 5 were determined to be 2.40 kPa and 0.95 kPa, respectively. During tritiated water measurements, the Baratron gauge was not directly connected to the HOT experimental line due to radiological handling considerations. Instead, a high-precision piezoresistive sensor (Puron Japan, PC-302T, accuracy ±500 Pa) was employed. These pressures were independently verified by spectral fitting of the pressure-broadened lines. The agreement between the mechanical and spectroscopic pressures was within 2%, indicating consistency between the two methods, and the previously determined Baratron pressures were therefore used for the pressure assignments in Fig. 5.

Temperature stabilization

Stable sample vaporization and minimization of adsorption were ensured by controlling the temperatures of both the injection port and the gas cell. The injection port, where the sample is introduced under reduced pressure below 1 Pa, was maintained at 363 K using a ribbon heater combined with a K-type thermocouple and a digital fine servo (Hakko, DG2) operated in an ON/OFF regulation mode. Under these conditions, complete and rapid evaporation of microliter-scale water samples was reliably achieved.

In contrast, temperature fluctuations in the gas cell can significantly affect quantitative isotopic analysis. Spectral simulations indicate that a ±1 K variation around 323 K in the HTO gas temperature leads to a change in peak absorption of ±0.7%, which would directly contribute to uncertainty in isotopic ratio determination. To achieve high stability, the gas cell was heated to 323 K using a foil heater with an integrated 10 k\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document} NTC thermistor (Thorlabs, HT10K) attached to the cell bottom and controlled by a temperature controller (Thorlabs, TC300B). Although the controller specification lists a stability of ±0.1 K, the temperature measured at the thermistor position remained within 323.00 ± 0.01 K over two hours.

This stability was achieved by thermally decoupling the cell from the optical breadboard using glass-fiber pillar posts (Thorlabs, RS50G/M) and enclosing the cell in 10 mm thick high-performance insulation to suppress external heat flux. Based on the simulated temperature dependence (±0.7% per ±1 K at 323 K), the observed ±0.01 K variation corresponds to an estimated absorption peak variation of ±0.007%. Therefore, temperature-induced uncertainty in the present isotopic measurements is negligible compared to other experimental contributions.

Spectral acquisition, calibration, and analysis

During spectral acquisition, the QCL injection current was continuously scanned in a triangular waveform to probe absorption over the target wavenumber region. To preserve the effective spectral resolution along the horizontal axis, the current–wavenumber calibration was performed using spectra acquired under reduced-pressure conditions. Specifically, the QCL current values corresponding to the centers of residual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CO}_2$$\end{document} absorption lines observed in the gas cell were extracted, and these were correlated with the corresponding line positions listed in the HITRAN database to construct the calibration curve. Because hysteresis is inherent in the QCL current tuning, separate calibration curves were generated for the rising and falling current scans and subsequently combined after wavenumber calibration. Based on the residual structure, the current–wavenumber relationship was represented by a third-order polynomial, with calibration residuals confined within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 0.0005~\mathrm {cm^{-1}}$$\end{document}.

Spectral data acquired over integration times of 200 s were averaged by binning at each wavenumber point to produce the final absorption spectra. On average, each data point represents the mean of around 30 individual ring-down events. The standard deviation calculated from this bin-averaging process was adopted as the experimental error for each spectral point. Quantitative linearity

The quantitative performance of the system was evaluated by measuring mixed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {HTO/H}_2$$\end{document}O samples with activities ranging from 0 to 126.7 kBq at a fixed total injected volume of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}L. Figure 5 shows the resulting absorption spectra. The HTO absorption peak intensity increases monotonically with increasing tritium activity. To clearly visualize the contribution of varying HTO activities, in Fig. 5, the residual absorption coefficient, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha - \alpha _{\mathrm {CO_2}}$$\end{document}, was calculated for display purposes by subtracting the modeled \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _{\mathrm {CO_2}}$$\end{document} contribution.

At a total pressure of 2.40 kPa (Fig. 5(a)), unidentified absorption features appear on both sides of the HTO peak. These features are also clearly observed in the light-water spectrum (0 kBq) and show no dependence on HTO activity. Although these peaks do not correspond to any transitions listed in the HITRAN database, their origin remains unidentified. Under the 2.40 kPa condition, the unidentified features partially overlap with the HTO peak, making it difficult to independently and reliably estimate their contributions.

In contrast, at a reduced pressure of 0.95 kPa (Fig. 5(b)), the narrowed linewidth of the HTO absorption enables improved spectral discrimination from the background features. By incorporating two additional Voigt functions into the multi-peak fitting model, the flatness of the residuals is significantly improved. In particular, for the light-water spectrum, the residuals within the displayed spectral window are confined within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 5.0 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm {cm^{-1}}$$\end{document}. Although a slight discrepancy between the fitted spectra and experimental data remains at higher activities due to imperfect matching of the unidentified background peaks, this approach effectively decouples the HTO signal from the unidentified background absorption. While the pressure reduction improved spectral separation by narrowing the linewidths, it also reduced the HTO number density due to sample evacuation, consequently degrading the detection limit.

A comparison of the two pressure conditions shows that the systematic offset observed at 2.40 kPa is effectively mitigated at 0.95 kPa when the unidentified background absorption is explicitly included in the fitting model, while the slope remains nearly unchanged. This result indicates that, within the present measurement range, the contribution of the unidentified features to the HTO isotopic ratio estimation manifests primarily as an additive systematic error.

While these unidentified features are treated as an additive systematic error in the current estimation, their physical origin remains a subject of investigation. Given that these isolated peaks only appear upon sample introduction and lack the spectral periodicity typical of etalon fringes, they are tentatively attributed to trace molecular absorption from either unidentified impurities or unassigned transitions of known species not documented in current spectroscopic databases. Repeatability measurement

To evaluate the repeatability of the proposed HTO isotopic ratio analysis and to assess the overall measurement precision of the system, a repeatability experiment (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 5$$\end{document}) was performed by repeatedly injecting the same HTO water sample into the gas cell. Five independent injections of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}L aliquot of the HTO solution with an activity of 126.7 kBq were performed, and the corresponding absorption spectra were measured at a total pressure of 2.40 kPa.

Figure 7 shows the retrieved HTO isotopic ratio as a function of the injection number. The retrieved mean HTO isotopic ratio was \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5.067 \times 10^{-7}$$\end{document}, with a standard deviation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document} of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.2 \times 10^{-8}$$\end{document}, corresponding to a relative standard deviation of 2.4%. The standard error of the mean (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma /\sqrt{N}$$\end{document}) was evaluated to be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5.4 \times 10^{-9}$$\end{document}, or 1.1%.

These results support the linearity demonstrated in Fig 6 and indicate that the shot-to-shot variability, including uncertainties associated with liquid sample handling and injection, is sufficiently small. Crucially, the observed standard deviation across independent measurements is significantly smaller than the individual parameter estimation uncertainties derived from the spectral fitting at each data point. This indicates that the overall precision of the present CRDS-based HTO isotopic ratio analysis is superior to what might be inferred from the conservative fitting-based error bars. The repeatability measurement therefore reflects the overall precision of the present CRDS-based HTO isotopic ratio analysis under practical liquid-sample introduction conditions.

Noise analysis and sensitivity evaluation

The spectroscopic sensitivity of the present CRDS system, expressed as the limit of detection (LOD) or the minimum detectable absorption coefficient, was evaluated following the methodology described by Kassi and Campargue 46. This involved both an Allan–Werle analysis to characterize the long-term noise performance 47 and a root-mean-square (RMS) analysis of the spectral residuals to determine the effective sensitivity during scanning. Figure 8 shows the corresponding 600 s time series of the baseline ring-down decay constant under vacuum conditions (upper panel) together with the Allan–Werle plot obtained under fixed laser frequency conditions (lower panel). The analysis indicates a single-shot minimum detectable absorption coefficient (or noise equivalent absorption: NEA) of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.5 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm {cm^{-1} Hz^{-1/2}}$$\end{document}, and LOD of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4.7 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm {cm^{-1}}$$\end{document} with 15 s of signal averaging at a ring-down sampling rate of 100 samples/s.

The sensitivity achieved in this work represents a substantial improvement over previously reported optical spectroscopic methods for HTO detection. In particular, the minimum detectable absorption coefficient evaluated under fixed laser frequency conditions shows more than a twofold improvement compared to a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${2.17}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m CRDS system reported in Ref. 34, reflecting the low-noise performance of the present CRDS implementation.

In addition to this improvement in spectroscopic sensitivity, the use of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu _1$$\end{document} fundamental transition at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${4.3}\,{\upmu }$$\end{document}m enables a substantial enhancement in the corresponding radioactivity sensitivity. Owing to the 12-fold stronger absorption intensity compared to overtone transitions employed in earlier studies, the inferred gas-phase activity detection limits reach \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${14}\,\hbox {Bqcm}^{-3}$$\end{document} for single-shot measurements and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${0.43}\,\hbox {Bqcm}^{-3}$$\end{document} with 15 s of averaging. Furthermore, the scanning sensitivity is often constrained by frequency-dependent background fluctuations, such as residual optical fringes.

Discussion

The retrieved HTO isotopic ratios in this study exhibit high repeatability and linearity, reflecting the reproducibility of both spectral fitting and liquid sample handling. A systematic underestimation of the absolute HTO concentration was observed 33% relative to the expected value calculated from the independently verified activity of the stock solution. This bias is primarily attributed to uncertainties in the spectroscopic parameters. In particular, the line intensities used were theoretical, and all other spectroscopic parameters, except for the line energies, were assumed to be identical to those of the corresponding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {HD}^{16}$$\end{document}O transitions listed in HITRAN, neglecting potential isotopic substitution effects.

Given that reliable experimental spectroscopic data for HTO comparable to HITRAN for stable water isotopologues are currently lacking, these approximations are the most likely source of the observed scaling deviation. Importantly, the offset is consistent across different pressures (2.40 kPa and 0.95 kPa), indicating that it reflects a global parameter bias rather than fluctuations in sample handling or spectral fitting.With appropriate calibration against known standards or with updated spectroscopic parameters specific to HTO, the present system is expected to provide quantitatively accurate measurements, in addition to its demonstrated precision and linearity.

Within the current performance range, the system is already suitable for applications requiring rapid or time-resolved tritium measurements. In particular, the method can be directly applied to tritium analysis in accelerator-driven facilities and fusion-related systems, where moderate activity levels must be tracked with high temporal resolution. Moreover, for tracer-based applications such as pharmacokinetic and biological studies, where sample availability is inherently limited, the present technique enables quantitative measurements using microliter-scale sample volumes, demonstrating a clear practical advantage. On the other hand, achieving the regulatory limits for environmental effluent monitoring in Japan (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${0.06}\,\hbox {Bq}{\upmu }\hbox {L}^{-1}$$\end{document}) would require more than three orders of magnitude improvement in sensitivity. Potential technical pathways to enhance sensitivity start with incremental improvements, including suppression of background absorption from unidentified impurities and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CO}_{2}$$\end{document}, improved gas handling and cell cleaning, optimization of sample introduction, reduction of cavity volume to increase effective HTO number density, and enhancement of the mechanical and thermal stability of the CRDS platform. These measures can realistically yield significant gains in performance.

While linear CRDS provides a robust foundation for quantitative HTO analysis, further improvements in sensitivity and spectral selectivity may be achieved through advanced CRDS architectures. Beyond these incremental refinements, such approaches offer a promising pathway toward ultra-high sensitivity. Saturated CRDS 23,37 and Two-Color CRDS 26,38 have demonstrated sensitivity improvements of several orders of magnitude in 14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CO}_2$$\end{document} spectroscopy. The HTO-CRDS demonstration established in this work therefore provides a critical experimental basis for exploring next-generation CRDS architectures aimed at ultra-high-sensitivity HTO detection.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully implemented mid-infrared cavity ring-down spectroscopy (CRDS) targeting the fundamental \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu _1$$\end{document} vibrational region of HTO and evaluated its quantitative performance. For the first time, a proportional response of the retrieved absorption signal to tritium activity was experimentally verified with high linearity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 > 0.99$$\end{document}), accompanied by excellent repeatability and quantitative precision. The resulting signal-to-noise characteristics and absorption sensitivity represent a significant advancement over previous optical approaches for tritiated water detection, thereby establishing a robust and reliable spectroscopic foundation for HTO analysis. In addition, we presented a practical discussion of the analytical sensitivity that explicitly accounts for sample introduction and utilization efficiency, which is essential for realistic tritium analysis.

Overall, this work demonstrates that mid-infrared CRDS provides a robust and quantitative optical framework for tritium analysis, complementing existing radiometric methods and enabling reliable HTO measurements using microliter-scale samples.
