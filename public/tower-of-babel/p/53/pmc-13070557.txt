Affinity-optimized TROP2 antibodies support potent antitumor activity in antibody–drug conjugates
Authors: Aiko Yamaguchi, Junping Hong, Leike Li, Kiyotaka Kobayashi, Chisato M Yamazaki, Summer Y Y Ha, Yasuaki Anami, Wei Xiong, Junquan Liu, Zhiqiang An, Ningyan Zhang, Kyoji Tsuchikama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (TROP2) is frequently overexpressed in epithelial tumors and is associated with poor prognosis, making it an attractive therapeutic target. Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) directed against TROP2 show clinical benefit, but expression in normal tissues such as skin raises concerns about on-target, off-tumor toxicity. Strategies that improve antitumor efficacy without increasing toxicity are needed.MethodsUsing an in-house phage library, we identified and characterized a novel fully human monoclonal antibody recognizing a unique conformational epitope of TROP2 with reduced binding affinity. This antibody was engineered into homogeneous ADCs carrying auristatin and/or duocarmycin payloads. Comparative studies with a surrogate of sacituzumab govitecan were performed in TROP2-expressing tumor models.ResultsThe novel ADCs demonstrated remarkable antitumor activity in mouse xenograft and syngeneic tumor models. Despite lower binding affinity, the novel antibody exhibited potent efficacy, suggesting that epitope selection and affinity tuning can be leveraged to enhance therapeutic outcomes.ConclusionsNovel anti-TROP2 ADCs offer a promising approach for assuring efficacy while potentially mitigating toxicity. Optimization of antibody binding properties may enable the development of safer and more effective TROP2-targeted therapeutics.

Introduction

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are a rapidly expanding class of chemotherapeutics composed of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) linked to cytotoxic payloads through chemical linkers [1, 2]. By enabling pinpoint payload delivery to tumor sites, ADCs offer greater efficacy, a broader therapeutic window, and a longer tumor suppression duration than traditional chemotherapy. To date, at least 13 ADCs have been approved and are marketed globally, and a myriad of ADC candidates are in clinical trials.

Trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (TROP2) is a transmembrane glycoprotein with an extracellular domain overexpressed on many types of epithelial cancers [3, 4]. TROP2 expression correlates with poor prognosis, aggressive growth, and high metastasis rates across several cancer types. Although TROP2 is also expressed in a variety of normal tissues, including keratinocytes and oral mucosa, its expression levels in normal tissues are generally lower compared to tumor cells [3, 5]. Given its expression pattern and association with poor prognosis, TROP2 is a rational target for anticancer drug delivery.

Sacituzumab govitecan (SG) is an ADC composed of an anti-TROP2 IgG1 coupled to SN-38, the active metabolite of irinotecan and a topoisomerase I inhibitor [6, 7]. SG has been granted FDA approval for patients with unresectable locally advanced or metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) who have received two or more prior systemic therapies. However, TNBC frequently relapses even after continuous treatment for over a year [8], partly due to heterogeneous TROP2 expression [9–12] and multidrug resistance induced by SN-38 [13]. The most commonly observed adverse effects in patients treated with SG include dose-limiting neutropenia (All grades: 62%, Grade 3–4: 47%) and diarrhea (NCT02574455) [14]. Datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd), an anti-TROP2 mAb conjugated with the topoisomerase I inhibitor DXd via the GGFG cleavable linker, is a recently approved ADC. However, in the Phase 3 TROPION-Lung01 trial (NCT04656652) in non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), Dato-DXd showed only modest improvement in progression-free survival compared to docetaxel, with limited overall survival benefit and notable rates of stomatitis and interstitial lung disease [15]. Thus, improving ADC efficacy, broadening the target scope, and assuring safety are all key challenges in providing more valuable treatment options to patients with TROP2-expressing tumors.

Here, we report the discovery of a novel anti-TROP2 monoclonal antibody and its capacity as an ADC platform. Using an in-house phage display library, we discovered a novel fully human mAb with reduced affinity for a unique conformational epitope of TROP2. This antibody was engineered into homogeneous dual-payload ADCs, a validated conjugation format with enhanced efficacy compared to single-payload ADCs [17, 18]. Our novel-TROP2 antibody-based ADCs demonstrated significant antitumor activity in TROP2-positive tumor models despite the lower binding affinity of our antibody relative to Sacituzumab. Our findings suggest that combining antibody discovery with rational affinity and epitope selection is a promising strategy to improve the safety and efficacy of TROP2-targeting ADCs for treating refractory tumors.

Results

Characterization of TROP2-targeting antibodies

We picked two TROP2-targeting antibodies by panning a phage-displayed human single-chain variable fragment (scFv) library against the human TROP2 extracellular (ECD) protein (Fig. S1). The antibodies, TROP2–1 and TROP2–2, exhibited specific binding to TROP2 with EC50 values of 93.29 and 159.70 ng/mL, respectively, compared to that of Sacituzumab (EC50 = 71.85 ng/mL) as measured by ELISA (Fig. 1A). The binding affinities of the antibodies to the TROP2 ECD protein were 1.38 nM, 9.59 nM, and 25.6 nM for Sacituzumab, TROP2–1, and TROP2–2, respectively, as determined by biolayer interferometry (BLI) (Fig. 1B–D). We then confirmed binding of the antibodies to the native TROP2 expressed on HEK293T cells by flow cytometry (Fig. 1E). A competitive BLI experiment revealed TROP2–1 and TROP2–2 bind different epitopes, with TROP2–2 showing increased binding when TROP2–1 was loaded first, but not with TROP2–1 or control antibody (Fig. 1F).

Anti-TROP2 ADCs show target-selective cell killing potency comparable with Sacituzumab-based conjugates

Next, we set out to investigate the antigen-specific cell killing potency of ADCs prepared from the newly discovered TROP2 mAbs (TROP2–1 and TROP2–2). To this end, we constructed anti-TROP2 dual-payload ADCs using microbial transglutaminase (MTGase) and the branched spacers developed by our group [17, 18]. Our studies have shown that the monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE)/monomethyl auristatin F (MMAF) DAR 4 + 2 dual system can exert superior tumor suppression effects as well as acceptable physicochemical properties compared to DAR 6 MMAE single-drug variants in heterogeneous tumors [17, 18]. This conjugation platform enables site-specific and quantitative installation of two identical or distinct payload molecules onto a single antibody through two orthogonal cycloadditions with azide-bicyclo[6.1.0]nonyne (BCN) and methyltetrazine-trans-cyclooctene click chemistry pairs (Fig. 2A) The branched spacers were site-specifically installed onto the side chain of glutamine 295 (Q295) within N297A anti-TROP2 mAbs by MTGase-mediated transpeptidation. Subsequently, we installed payload modules containing BCN or TCO (as a handle for strain-promoted click reaction), polyethylene glycol (PEG), enzymatically cleavable glutamic acid–glycine–citrulline (EGCit) tripeptide, p-aminobenzyloxycarbonyl group (as a self-immolative spacer), and payload MMAE, MMAF, or Duocarmycin DM (DuoDM)]. We obtained homogeneous anti-TROP2 ADCs with a DAR of 4 (single payload) or 4 + 2 (dual payloads). The homogeneity of each conjugate was confirmed by ESI-MS analysis (Fig. 2B–C and Fig. S2). Although ADCs with high overall payload loading are often associated with increased hydrophobicity and aggregation, the DAR 4 + 2 dual-payload conjugates in this study remained highly monomeric, as demonstrated by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Fig. 2B).

We then evaluated these ADCs for in vitro cytotoxicity in TROP2-high (JIMT-1), TROP2-intermediate (HCC1806), and TROP2-low (MDA-MB-231) human breast cancer cell lines (Tables 1–3 and Fig. S3). As controls, we also prepared and tested Sacituzumab-based conjugates. TROP2–1 ADCs exhibited cell-killing potency in JIMT-1 cell lines comparable to that of Sacituzumab ADCs, while TROP2–2 ADCs showed diminished cell-killing potency. Although the cell killing potency of TROP2–1 ADC was lower than that of Sacituzumab ADC in the HCC1806 cell line, all TROP2–1 ADCs showed subnanomolar EC50 values under our assay conditions. The EC50 values of these ADCs were 0.070–0.084 nM in JIMT-1, 0.120–0.167 nM in HCC1806. No significant cell killing effect was observed in the TROP2-low MDA-MB-231 cell lines for either ADC. Although MDA-MB-231 expresses TROP2 to some extent, it has been reported that this cell line is generally non-responsive to anti-TROP2 ADCs in vitro [19]. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the new TROP2–1 antibody provides ADCs with optimal TROP2 binding affinity and specificity, ensuring in vitro ADC potency comparable to Sacituzumab-based ADCs.

New TROP2 ADCs exert significant efficacy in xenograft and syngeneic tumor models

We sought to evaluate TROP2–1 ADCs for treatment efficacy in an orthotopic breast tumor xenograft mouse model (Fig. 3A–C). In the HCC1806 tumor model, orthotopically xenografted nude mice were injected intravenously with a single dose of anti-TROP2 dual-payload ADCs, Sacituzumab MMAE/F dual drug ADC, SG surrogate (Fig. S2), or unconjugated N297A TROP2–1 mAb at 3 mg/kg. Both TROP2–1 and Sacituzumab MMAE/F 4 + 2 dual-payload ADC exhibited remarkable tumor growth inhibition (%TGI on Day 10: 110%, TROP2–1 MMAE/F ADC; 100%, Sacituzumab MMAE/F ADC) and survival benefits. All animals remained tumor-free for over 80 days (Fig. 3A and B). DuoDM/MMAE 4 + 2 dual-payload TROP2–1 ADC, showed tumor growth inhibition at an early time point (%TGI on Day 10: 105%) but tumors started to regrow two weeks after the treatment. SG biosimilar, at this dosing schedule, did not show either antitumor effect (%TGI on Day 10: 40.4%) or survival benefit. All animals treated with SG surrogate were found dead or killed at the pre-defined humane endpoint (>1000 mm3 tumor size or > 20% body weight loss) by day 14. We did not observe any significant acute toxicity associated with the administration of ADCs over the course of the study as evaluated by monitoring significant body weight loss and other clinical symptoms (Fig. 3C).

Next, we performed an exploratory study to assess the immune-activating potential of the TROP2 ADCs using an immunocompetent mice model of breast cancer. It has been reported that ADCs can activate antitumor immunity and help improve the therapeutic efficacy of cancer immunotherapy through PD-1/PD-L1 blockade [20–23]. We prepared a human TROP2-expressing murine cell line, EMT6-hTROP2 (Fig. 4A), and tested in vitro cell killing potency of our ADCs in this cell line. DuoDM/MMAE dual ADC and SG surrogate showed dose-dependent growth inhibition (Fig. 4B), though the EC50 values were higher than that observed in HCC1806 or JIMT-1, which have much higher TROP2 expression levels. MMAE/MMAF dual ADC did not show growth inhibition at the tested dose range, probably due to the expression of multidrug resistant protein (MDR1) in the EMT6-WT cell line [24]. Although the expression level of TROP2 is critical for antitumor efficacy of TROP2-based ADCs [25], an excessive amount of expression could be a tradeoff between concern for raising the anti-human TROP2 immunity of the host animal, which could potentially induce spontaneous tumor regression. Therefore, we used this EMT6-hTROP2 cell line with a moderate TROP2 expression level for subsequent in vivo studies. Orthotopic breast tumor implantation of the EMT6-hTROP2 cell line in the immunocompetent BALB/c mice resulted in rapid, aggressive growth of the tumor. The treatment with a single injection of MMAE/F 4 + 2 dual-payload ADC and DuoDM/MMAE 4 + 2 dual-payload ADC at 3 mg/kg suppressed tumor growth to some extent with a survival benefit (P < .05, vs. mAb group), while SG analog at the same dose schedule did not induce a robust antitumor effect (Fig. 4C). One out of five mice from each dual-payload ADC group (MMAE/F 4 + 2 and DuoDM/MMAE) reached tumor-free, while all mice treated with SG surrogate were found dead or euthanized at the pre-defined humane endpoint (>1000 mm3 tumor size or > 20% body weight loss) by day 30 (Fig. 4D–G). The mice that remained tumor-free after the treatment were rechallenged with EMT6-WT at day 51 after the initial treatment, and the tumor growth was monitored. Age-matched tumor-implantation to treatment-naïve mice was also performed. All the ADC-treated tumor-free mice successfully rejected subsequent tumor rechallenge and remained tumor-free over 70 days after reinoculation (Fig. 4H). Throughout the study, we did not observe any significant acute toxicity associated with the administration of ADCs as evaluated by monitoring significant body weight loss and other clinical symptoms (Fig. S4). Since EMT6-WT does not express human TROP2, our observation suggests that the antitumor immune memory induced by our ADC treatment was directed not against the transduced human TROP2, but rather against endogenous tumor-associated antigens. Although we have not yet performed immune cell profiling to evaluate the tumor immune microenvironment or additional rechallenging with irrelevant tumor cell lines, such analyses are currently underway in our lab to elucidate the immunomodulatory mechanisms of our ADC and antigen dependence of the observed immune memory.

Epitope mapping of TROP2–1

TROP2 ECD comprises three main domains: a cysteine-rich domain (CRD, aa 27–73), a thyroglobulin type-1 domain (TY-1, aa 74–146), and a cysteine-poor domain (CPD, aa 147–274) (Fig. 5A). To identify the binding sites of TROP2–1, we created four truncated versions of the human TROP2 ECD that correspond to its structural regions of ECD, CRD, TY-1, and CPD. As expected, TROP2–1 and Sacituzumab bound the complete TROP2 ECD (Fig. 5B). Of the three main domains of TROP2 ECD, the two antibodies bind exclusively to the CPD domain (aa 147–274) (Fig. 5B). Like Sacituzumab, TROP2–1 recognized cynomolgus monkey TROP2 but not mouse TROP2 (Fig. 5C). Additionally, different from Sacituzumab, TROP2–1 failed to bind to either human or cyno TROP2 under reducing conditions (Fig. 5D), indicating that TROP2–1 recognizes a conformational epitope, and in contrast, Sacituzumab binds to a linear epitope, aligning with prior reports [26].

Since TROP2–1 targets a conformational epitope and does not bind to the mouse TROP2, to map the binding epitope of TROP2–1, we created human TROP2 mutants by substituting mouse TROP2-specific residues within the CPD domain (Fig. 5E,F). These full-length mutants were transfected into HEK293T cells, and TROP2–1 binding to the mutants was tested using flow cytometry. TROP2–1 exhibited similar binding to WT and most mutants (Mut1, Mut2, Mut4, and Mut5), except Mut3, which completely abolished binding (Fig. 5G). Mut3 contains residues A174S and R181Q, likely within the TROP2–1 binding epitope. The A174–R181 segment, mapped onto the crystal structure of the human TROP2 ECD, resides within the α2 helix, which is positioned at the C-terminal region of CPD (Fig. 5H). In contrast, Sacituzumab binds the extended polypeptide (Q237-Q252) in parallel with the α2 helix, resembling the ridge on CPD (Fig. 5H) [26]. These results suggest that TROP2–1 recognizes a new conformational epitope in the CPD’s α2 helix (A174–R181), distinct from Sacituzumab’s binding epitope.

Discussion

TROP2-targeting ADCs have demonstrated clinical benefit in several cancers, including triple-negative breast cancer and non-small cell lung cancer. However, challenges remain in optimizing efficacy, selectivity, and safety, particularly due to the expression of TROP2 in tumor and normal tissues. TROP2 is highly expressed in a range of epithelial malignancies, including breast, pancreatic, cervical, ovarian, prostate, gastric, and non-small cell lung cancers. At the same time, it is also present in normal tissues such as keratinocytes and oral mucosa3,5. As a result, balancing therapeutic efficacy with the risk of both antigen-dependent and antigen-independent toxicities is a central challenge in developing TROP2-directed ADCs [27, 28].

One potential strategy to improve tumor-versus-normal tissue selectivity for broadly expressed antigens such as TROP2 is modulating antibody binding properties, including affinity, avidity, and binding epitopes. Sacituzumab contained the humanized monoclonal antibody, hRS7, which was derived from the murine RS7-3G11 antibody generated by immunization [29]. The high affinity of Sacituzumab may cause the strong binding to both tumor cells and normal cells expressing TROP2, leading to off-tumor toxicity, including gastrointestinal toxicity, renal toxicity, and pulmonary toxicity [30]. Supporting this rationale, it has been reported that the tolerability of CXCR4-targeting ADCs, which also face challenges due to broad normal tissue expression, is inversely correlated with binding affinity: an ADC with medium binding affinity was less well tolerated than one with low affinity [31]. Similarly, a low-affinity anti-EGFR ADC demonstrated minimal binding to normal epithelial cells while maintaining antitumor efficacy in models with medium to high EGFR expression [31, 32]. Beyond improving selectivity for tumors with high antigen density, using a moderate-affinity antibody may offer a second key advantage: enhanced solid tumor penetration. A well-documented challenge in ADC therapy is the 'binding-site barrier' effect, where very high-affinity antibodies bind and internalize irreversibly to the first antigens they encounter upon entering a tumor. This can trap the ADC near the blood vessels after extravasation or on the periphery of the tumor, preventing it from reaching and killing cells in the deeper, often more hypoxic and aggressive, core of the mass [33–36]. In contrast, the faster off-rate of a moderate-affinity antibody allows it to dissociate and diffuse further into the tumor parenchyma, leading to a more uniform distribution and potentially greater efficacy against poorly perfused regions [37].

In this study, we selected two fully human monoclonal antibodies with distinct monovalent binding affinities, TROP2–1 (KD 9.59 nM) and TROP2–2 (KD 25.6 nM), from our in-house antibody library. The TROP2–1-based ADC demonstrated comparable in vitro cytotoxicity despite its lower affinity than that of a Sacituzibumab-based ADC (KD: 1.38 nM). However, weaker binder TROP2–2-based ADC showed reduced cell killing effect, indicating that subtle binding affinity alteration can meaningfully influence ADC activity. In vivo, a single dose of our novel TROP2–1 dual-payload ADC bearing auristatin analogs (MMAE and MMAF) at 3 mg/kg showed a significant antitumor effect in an immunocompromised orthotopic breast cancer model expressing TROP2, which was comparable with the efficacy of the Sacituzumab-based variant. While the dual-payload ADC with duocarmycin payloads showed more modest efficacy in this model, it elicited a comparable tumor-suppressive effect in the EMT6-hTROP2 immunocompetent syngeneic model. Importantly, no acute toxicity was observed in any ADC-treated groups across these experiments. While we employed dual-payload ADCs as a validated platform to achieve high efficacy [17, 18, 38], a systematic comparison between single- and dual-payload ADCs constructed from the newly identified TROP2 mAb will be the subject of future studies.

These findings suggest that rational tuning of binding properties can potentially enable the development of TROP2-targeting ADCs with desirable safety profiles without compromising efficacy. However, there are limitations in this study that need further clarification to support this hypothesis. First, apparent binding behavior in tissues may be influenced not only by monovalent affinity but also by antigen density and avidity-driven bivalent engagement. As such, an antibody with moderate monovalent affinity can exhibit strong apparent binding to high-antigen-density tumor cells through avidity while showing reduced engagement of lower-density normal tissues where binding is monovalent. As we did not quantify apparent affinity across antigen densities in the current study (for example, by cell-based binding titration or ELISA under low versus high antigen density conditions), future investigation is warranted to clarify how TROP2 density-dependent avidity can contribute to tumor-normal discrimination. Second, as our TROP2–1 mAb and Sacituzumab recognize distinct epitopes, the target accessibility, internalization, and intracellular trafficking kinetics of these ADCs may be substantially different. Thus, additional testing is required to clarify whether and how these parameters can also impact efficacy and specificity. Third, our in vivo efficacy comparisons were performed at a single dose and schedule (3 mg/kg); dose-ranging studies, including testing at lower doses, will be essential to define relative potency and therapeutic index across constructs. Finally, it will be important to perform in vivo evaluation using low/heterogeneous TROP2-expressing tumor models and toxicity testing on primary keratinocytes. Additional refinements, including the use of alternative payload classes, fine-tuning of drug-to-antibody ratios, or switching to a single-drug format for improved tolerability, may further enhance the therapeutic index. These optimization efforts are actively ongoing in our laboratory, with the goal of advancing next-generation TROP2-ADCs that deliver more effective and safer treatment options for patients with TROP2-expressing tumors.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

EMT6-hTROP2 was generated by lentiviral transduction of the murine breast carcinoma EMT6-wildtype cell line (EMT6-WT; NCI DCTD Tumor Repository, RRID:CVCL1923) using Lentifect™ lentiviral particles harboring the Homo sapiens TACSTD2 gene and a puromycin-resistant gene (GeneCopoeia, LPP-G0457). Transduction was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. EMT6-hTROP2 and EMT6-WT were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)/F-12 (Corning) supplemented with 15% EquaFETAL® (Atlas Biologicals), GlutaMAX® (2 mM, Gibco), sodium pyruvate (1 mM, Corning), and penicillin–streptomycin (100 units/mL and 100 μg/mL, respectively, Gibco). JIMT-1 (AddexBio, RRID:CVCL_2077) and HCC1806 (ATCC, CVCL_1258) were cultured in RPMI1640 (Corning) supplemented with 10% EquaFETAL® (Atlas Biologicals), GlutaMAX® (2 mM, Gibco), sodium pyruvate (1 mM, Corning), and penicillin–streptomycin (penicillin: 100 units/mL; streptomycin: 100 μg/mL, Gibco). MDA-MB-231 (ATCC, RRID:CVCL_0062) was cultured in DMEM (Corning) supplemented with 10% EquaFETAL®, GlutaMAX® (2 mM), and penicillin–streptomycin (penicillin: 100 units/mL; streptomycin: 100 μg/mL). HEK293T cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Expi293 was cultured in Expi293 Expression Medium. All cells except Expi293 were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2 (8% CO2 for Expi293) and routinely tested using a Mycoalert Mycoplasma Contamination Kit (Lonza) to ensure no contamination in cell cultures. Cells were passaged before becoming fully confluent, up to 20 passages. All cell lines were obtained from vendors as described above from 2014 to 2025, characterized by DNA profiling by each vendor, and no further authentication was conducted by the authors. All cell lines were validated for antigen expression by cell-based ELISA or flow cytometry before use.

Plasmids

The coding sequences of TROP2 CRD (aa 27–73), TY-1 (aa 74–146), and CPD (aa 147–247) were amplified from full-length TROP2 cDNA and cloned into a digested phFc vector, which carried a human IgG1 Fc using In-Fusion HD cloning enzyme mix (Takara). For the TROP2 mutants, mutations were introduced using overlap extension PCR. Briefly, primers were designed to amplify two fragments for each TROP2 mutant with mutations in the overlap regions. Assembly PCR was conducted to combine two purified fragments, and another PCR was performed to amplify the sequences for cloning. The purified full-length PCR products were cloned into the pCMV3 vector using In-Fusion HD cloning enzyme mix (Takara).

Panning of phage displayed scFv antibody library and IgG conversion

Protocols for panning our phage displayed scFv libraries, and subsequent cloning and expression of the IgG antibodies were described previously [16]. Briefly, recombinant human TROP2 ECD protein (Sino Biological, 10,428-H08H) was coated on high-binding MaxiSorp tubes for capture (2 mL vial), followed by the addition of scFv antibodies displayed on phage. After three rounds of panning selection with increased washing stringency, antigen-enriched phages were rescued by infecting TG1 E. coli cells in culture. TROP2 ECD binding antibody hits displayed on phage were screened using ELISA. The heavy chain and light chain of unique scFvs were amplified and further subjected to conversion into the respective vectors.

Positive phage-displayed antibody screening by ELISA

The ELISA binding assay was performed using a previously reported protocol with modifications [16]. Human TROP2 ECD proteins were briefly coated at 2 μg/mL in PBS on 96-well microtiter plates (Corning) at 4 °C overnight. The plates were washed three times with PBS and then blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in PBS (blocking buffer) for 2 h. The supernatants containing scFvs displayed on phage were then added and incubated with the antigen-coated plates for 1.5 h. After incubation and washing, the mouse-anti-M13 antibody (Santa Cruz, sc-53,004) was added at a dilution of 1:2000 in blocking buffer for 1 h. After washing three times with PBS, the plates were incubated with 100 μL/well of the tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 3–5 min at room temperature. The reaction was stopped by 50 μL/well of 2 M H2SO4, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices).

Expression and purification of proteins and antibodies

Plasmids encoding human TROP2 CRD (aa 27–73), TY-1 (aa 74–146), CPD (aa 147–247), or antibody heavy and light chains (1:1) were transiently transfected into Expi293 cells using polyetherimide (PEI) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The supernatant was collected 5–7 days post-transfection and filtered through 0.22 μm filters. The human TROP2 truncated proteins and antibodies were further purified with Protein A resin (Genscript).

Elisa

To assess the binding ability of antibodies, 100 ng/well of human, cynomolgus and mouse TROP2 proteins (Sino Biological) were coated on 96-well high-binding ELISA plates (Corning) at room temperature for 2 h, respectively. The plates were washed with PBS once and blocked with blocking buffer at room temperature for 2 h. The serially diluted antibody was added to the wells and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. After washing three times with PBST, 100 μL of HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG F(ab’)2 (Jackson Immunoresearch) with a dilution of 1:5000 was added to each well and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. After washing three times with PBST, 100 μL of TMB substrate was added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. The reaction was stopped with a 2 M H2SO4 solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Antibody affinity measurement with BLI

The antibody (30 μg/mL) was loaded onto the Protein A biosensors for 5 min to measure the affinity. Following a short baseline in kinetic buffer, a serially diluted human TROP2 protein was loaded for 3 min, followed by soaking the biosensors in kinetic buffer for 3 min as the dissociation step. Background wavelength shifts were measured from reference biosensors only loaded with antibodies to correct for sensor drifting. Samples were kept at room temperature with 1000 rpm shaking during all incubation steps. ForteBio's data analysis software was used to fit the data into a 1:1 binding model to extract an association and dissociation rates. The KD was calculated using the ratio of koff/kon.

Epitope competition with BLI

A BLI-based sandwich epitope binning assay performed on an 8-channel Octet RED96 instrument analyzed the epitope competition data. First, TROP2–1 (30 μg/mL) was loaded onto Protein A biosensors for 10 min. The remaining Fc-binding sites on the biosensors were blocked with an irrelevant human IgG1 antibody (300 μg/mL) for 5 min. The biosensors were then exposed to human TROP2 protein (400 nM) for 5 min to saturate the binding site of the first antibody. Finally, various secondary antibodies (30 μg/mL) were loaded for 5 min to detect the binding. Protein A biosensors were regenerated for 30 seconds in 100 mM glycine (pH 2.6). Raw data were processed using ForteBio's data analysis software.

Flow cytometry analysis

To analyze the antibody binding to wild-type (WT) TROP2 and mutant TROP2, pCMV3 plasmids expressing full-length WT and mutant TROP2 were transfected into HEK293T using PEI according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transfected cells were trypsinized and resuspended in PBS containing 2% v/v FBS. Cells were stained with antibodies at 4 °C for 1 h. After three washes with PBS, HEK293T cells were incubated with goat anti-human IgG Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C for 1 h. Following three washes, stained cells were analyzed by flow cytometry on an iQue3 instrument (Sartorius), and the data were evaluated with FlowJo software X 10.0.7 (BD Biosciences).

Western blot

TROP2 protein samples were resolved on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels (Bio-Rad) and transferred to Immun-Blot PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in PBST, then probed with TROP2–1, Sacituzumab, or control antibodies, followed by anti-human IgG secondary antibody diluted in 5% non-fat milk/PBST. Immunoreactive bands were detected using West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher).

Preparation and characterization of ADCs

All antibody conjugates were prepared and characterized according to the method we reported previously [17, 18]. See Supplementary Information for details.

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded in a culture-treated 96-well clear plate (1000 cells per well for EMT6-hTROP2 and EMT6-WT cell lines or 5000 cells for all other cell lines per well in 50 μL culture medium) and incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 24 h. Serially diluted samples (50 μL) were added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 72 h. For duocarmycin-based ADCs, all the tested cell lines were incubated for 120 h. After the old medium was replaced with 100 μL fresh medium, 20 μL of a mixture of WST-8 (1.5 mg/mL, Cayman Chemical) and 1-methoxy-5-methylphenazinium methylsulfate (1-methoxy PMS, 100 μM, Cayman Chemical) was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 2–4 h. After gently agitating the plate, the absorbance at 460 nm was recorded using a BioTek Synergy HTX plate reader. EC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism 9 software. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Animal studies

All procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston and performed according to the institutional guidelines for animal care and use. All animals were housed under controlled conditions, namely 21–22 °C (± 0.5 °C), 30–75% (± 10%) relative humidity, and 12:12 light/dark cycle with lights on at 7:00 am. Food and water were available ad libitum for all animals.

Orthotopic xenograft mouse models of human breast cancer

HCC1806 cells (1 × 107 cells) suspended in 100 μL of PBS were orthotopically injected into the inguinal mammary fat pad of female NU/J mice (6–8 weeks old, purchased from The Jackson Laboratory, Stock number: 002019). When the average tumor volume reached approximately 250 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 5 for each group). A single dose of each ADC (3 mg/kg) or vehicle was administered intravenously to mice. Tumor volume (0.52 × L × S2 mm3, L: long diameter, S: short diameter) and body weight were monitored twice a week using a digital caliper. Mice were euthanized when the tumor volume exceeded 1000 mm3, the tumor size exceeded 2 cm in diameter, greater than 20% weight loss was observed, or mice showed signs of distress. Such events were counted as animal deaths.

Syngeneic orthotopic xenograft mouse model of human breast caner

EMT6-hTROP2 cells (2 × 106 cells) suspended in 100 μL of PBS were orthotopically injected into the inguinal mammary fat pad of female BALB/c mice (5–8 weeks old, purchased from The Jackson Laboratory, Stock number: 000651). When the average tumor volume reached 175–250 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 4–5 for each group). A single dose of each ADC (3 mg/kg) or vehicle was administered intravenously. Tumor volume, body weight, and animal deaths were recorded as described above.

Statistical analysis

Although no statistical analysis was performed before performing each experiment, the sample size was determined by following the methods for similar experiments in the field reported previously. Whenever possible, the investigators were blinded to allocation during experiments. A Welch’s t-test (two-tailed, unpaired, uneven variance) was used for xenograft tumor model studies. Kaplan–Meier survival curve statistics were analyzed with a log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. To control the family-wise error rate in multiple comparisons, crude P values were adjusted by the Holm–Bonferroni method. Differences with adjusted P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant in all analyses.
