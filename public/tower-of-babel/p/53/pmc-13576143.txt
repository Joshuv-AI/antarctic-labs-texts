A zinc-coordinated cascade-responsive therapeutic nanoassembly for remodeling the pathological microenvironment and restoring mitochondrial homeostasis in spinal cord injury
Authors: Chunyu Xiang, Xiaodong He, Fengshuo Guo, Haowen Luo, Liumin He, Wanguo Liu, Rui Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSecondary injury after spinal cord injury (SCI) is sustained by coupled oxidative stress and inflammation, which drives neuronal apoptosis and bioenergetic failure. Here, a cascade-responsive Zn2+-centered nanoassembly (Zn-PC/PA@Gel) is constructed through stepwise coordination among Zn2+, procyanidin (PC), and polyarginine (PA) to form a core-shell architecture with a Zn2+-procyanidin core (Zn-PC) and a Zn2+-polyarginine shell (Zn-PA). In a reactive oxygen species (ROS) rich injury microenvironment, oxidation of guanidino groups in the polyarginine shell enables in situ nitric oxide (NO) release and weakens Zn2+ coordination, triggering controlled shell disassembly for early modulation of local inflammation and tissue microenvironment. The subsequent release of PC and Zn2+ provides continuous antioxidant protection. Zn2+ further restores mitochondrial quality control by regulating the STAT3-FOXO3a-SOD2 axis, thus enhancing mitochondrial autophagy, enhancing endogenous antioxidant defense, and restoring mitochondrial homeostasis and energy metabolism. In a mouse spinal cord contusion model, Zn-PC/PA@Gel mitigated inflammation and oxidative stress, alleviated the burden of mitochondrial dysfunction, protected neurons, and promoted motor recovery, resulting in a Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) score of 7.0 on day 28. Overall, these results support Zn2+ coordinated cascade therapy nanoassembly, which combines microenvironmental regulation with mitochondrial homeostatic recovery to reduce secondary injury after SCI and promote locomotor improvement.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04760-0.Keywords: Zinc-coordinated nanoassembly, Procyanidin, Polyarginine, Mitochondrial homeostasis, Spinal cord injury

Introduction

Secondary injury following spinal cord injury (SCI) is characterized by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and persistent inflammation, which form a self-reinforcing cycle that ultimately converges on mitochondrial dysfunction and bioenergetic collapse at the cellular level [1–5]. This process eventually promotes neuronal apoptosis and limits nerve recovery [6, 7]. Current clinical interventions, including surgical decompression and high-dose steroids, can partially inhibit the expansion of lesions, but have limited accuracy in controlling the internal events of cells that determine the fate of neurons, especially the coupling interaction between oxidative stress, inflammatory signals and metabolic collapse [8–11]. Many candidate therapies mainly focus on scavenging ROS or inhibiting inflammatory mediators, so they act on the downstream of pathological cascade and have limited ability to interrupt the feedforward circulation caused by mitochondrial damage, thus further amplifying the production and inflammation of ROS [12–15]. Therefore, the achievement of multi-level and temporally coordinated interventions within the key window of secondary injury remains a core challenge and an urgent unmet need [16, 17].

To target the intertwined oxidative and inflammatory axes of the post-injury microenvironment, procyanidin (PC) and polyarginine (PA) offer complementary bioactive foundations [18–22]. PC is enriched with polyphenolic motifs that enable direct ROS scavenging and can further enhance endogenous antioxidant capacity by activating cellular defense pathways such as Nrf2, integrating exogenous neutralization with endogenous reinforcement [23–26]. PA is rich in guanidino groups and exhibits favorable biocompatibility and cellular uptake, while its structural features are potentially linked to the modulation of inflammation-associated signaling and immune responses at the lesion site [27–29]. However, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant interventions alone often fail to yield durable neuroprotection because restoration of mitochondrial quality control is a decisive step for neuronal survival [30–34]. Damaged mitochondria not only serve as a major source of ROS but also trigger mitochondria-dependent intrinsic apoptosis through loss of membrane potential and permeability transition, driving irreversible neuronal loss [35–38]. Therefore, advancing intervention to mitochondrial homeostasis and energy metabolism reconstruction is essential for closing the mechanistic loop of secondary injury [39, 40].

Here, Zn2+ is introduced as a regulatory node to establish a zinc-centered cascade therapeutic nanoassembly (Zn-PC/PA@Gel), which is delivered locally via an in situ photo-crosslinkable hydrogel to achieve lesion retention and sustained release. The resulting Zn-PC/PA@Gel adopts a core-shell organization composed of a Zn2+-procyanidin core (Zn-PC) and a Zn2+-polyarginine shell (Zn-PA), in which distinct microenvironmental cues orchestrate staged disassembly and function (Scheme 1A). In the ROS-rich milieu of acute SCI, oxidative conversion of guanidino functionalities within the Zn-PA shell supports localized nitric oxide (NO) generation and concurrently weakens Zn2+ coordination, leading to controlled shell destabilization and early modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment. As the cascade proceeds, the acidic conditions of injured tissue promote decoordination within the Zn-PC core and enable sequential release of procyanidin and Zn2+ to sustain redox control. Procyanidin supplies direct ROS scavenging and can further strengthen endogenous antioxidant defenses through activation of pathways such as Nrf2. Zn2+ cooperates with PC to reinforce antioxidant capacity. Moreover, Zn-PC/PA promotes mitophagy of damaged mitochondria in neurons via the STAT3-FOXO3a signaling axis, accompanied by increased SOD expression and more stable ATP production, together supporting mitochondrial functional recovery. In a mouse SCI contusion model, Zn-PC/PA@Gel improves key pathological features of secondary injury, including oxidative stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial damage, and is associated with improved locomotor outcomes (Scheme 1B, C). Overall, the constructed Zn-PC/PA@Gel provides a cascade-responsive nanotherapeutic strategy that associates lesion microenvironment sensing with mitochondrial protection after SCI, offering a potential approach for coordinated regulation at both the microenvironmental and organelle levels.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of Zn-PC/PA

As illustrated in Fig. 2A, Zn-PC/PA with a core-shell architecture was constructed via a stepwise coordination-driven self-assembly strategy. First, Zn2+ coordinates with the phenolic hydroxyl groups of the catechol moieties in PC, promoting rapid aggregation under mild conditions and yielding a well-defined Zn-PC core [38, 41]. Subsequently, the exposed Zn2+ sites on the Zn-PC surface further coordinate with guanidino groups on the side chains of PA, forming a shell layer and generating the final Zn-PC/PA assembly [42, 43]. SEM images show that Zn-PC exhibits a uniform hexagonal bipyramidal morphology with sharp particle boundaries, indicating that coordination between Zn2+ and PC drives the formation of a highly ordered assembly (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S1A). In contrast, Zn-PA presents as relatively loose aggregates and does not form a well-defined assembled morphology (Supplementary Fig. S1B). After the introduction of PA, the particle size of Zn-PC/PA increases slightly, while the surface evolves from relatively smooth to evidently rough and becomes covered by a continuous granular overlayer (Fig. 2C). TEM images more clearly revealed the core-shell features of Zn-PC/PA. The Zn-PC core was compact and homogeneous, whereas the outer region displayed a continuous boundary indicative of a stable shell layer (Fig. 2D-G). Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping and quantitative analysis indicated that Zn accounted for approximately 16.7% of the detected atoms, supporting its role as the coordinating metal during assembly (Fig. 2H, I). DLS measurements further indicated a hydrodynamic diameter of ~ 354.0 nm for Zn-PC, which increased to ~ 429.7 nm after PA coating, in line with the TEM observations (Fig. 2J). Moreover, the zeta potential shifted from − 20.2 mV to + 42.0 mV after assembly, suggesting successful surface modification and stable formation of Zn-PC/PA (Fig. 2K).

In order to further evaluate the batch repeatability of Zn-PC/PA synthesis, three independent batches of Zn-PC/PA were prepared under the same conditions, and three repeated measurements were carried out for each batch (Supplementary Fig. S2). The hydrodynamic diameter range of different batches is 416 to 455 nm, and the polydispersity index (PDI) value is between 0.12 and 0.24, indicating that the particle size distribution is moderately narrow and repeatable. The ζ potential is always positive, ranging from 40.6 to 45.5 mV. In addition, the analysis of inductively coupled plasma emission spectrum (ICP-OES) shows that the zinc content is maintained between 6.67 and 9.58 wt%. These results show that the gradual Zn2+-mediated colocation assembly strategy can achieve the repeatable preparation of Zn-PC/PA with stable particle size, surface charge and metal incorporation. The colloidal stability of Zn-PC/PA in biologically related media was further evaluated, including DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) and 0.9% sodium chloride solution (Supplementary Fig. S3). During the 7-day incubation period, the hydrodynamic diameter in all media was maintained in the range of about 420–455 nm, and the PDI value was maintained below 0.30, and there was no obvious aggregation. Although the ζ potential fluctuated moderately due to the effect of protein adsorption and ion shielding, Zn-PC/PA always maintained a positive surface charge during the whole observation period. These results show that Zn-PC/PA has good colloidal stability under physiological conditions.

Based on the above morphological characterization results, the metal coordination of Zn-PC/PA was further evaluated through multiple spectroscopic analyses. The TGA curve shows the thermal transformation characteristics of Zn-PC and Zn-PC/PA around 300 °C, verifying their good structural stability and orderliness (Fig. 2L). In the ultraviolet-visible spectrum (UV-vis), compared with PC and Zn-PC, Zn-PC/PA shows more obvious absorption redshift, which is consistent with the increase in frame stiffness and the expansion of conjugation after Zn2+ is coordinated with PC and PA (Fig. 2M). The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrum provides evidence of direct bonding, including C-O-C vibration band at 1035 cm− 1, Zn-O coordination at 550 cm− 1, and a narrow -OH stretching band, indicating that the hydrogen bond weakens when Zn2+-phenol is arranged (Fig. 2N). The XRD spectrograms of Zn-PC and Zn-PC/PA both show sharp diffraction peaks (Fig. 2O), confirming the highly ordered crystal structure, and a new Raman band appeared near 376 cm− 1 for Zn-PC and Zn-PC/PA, further supporting the coordination of Zn2+ and the ligand. (Fig. 2P). XPS analysis strengthens this conclusion by resolving the elemental composition, chemical states, and coordination environments. The survey spectrum confirms that Zn-PC/PA consists primarily of C, N, O, and Zn, and the emergence of an N 1s signal indicates PA incorporation (Fig. 2Q, R). Upon introducing PA, the O 1s spectrum shows changes in both the relative contribution and binding-energy position of coordinated oxygen species. Meanwhile, the Zn 2p spectra remain dominated by the Zn2+ signature but display noticeable shifts in binding energies and/or component distributions, consistent with electron-density redistribution and a reconfigured coordination field around Zn (Fig. 2S, T). Overall, these results show that PA participates in the Zn2+ coordination network through nitrogen-containing groups, resulting in the reconfiguration of the coordination environment, involving oxygen sites from PC and nitrogen sites related to PA. This reconfiguration contributes to shell stabilization while preserving the ordered Zn-PC core, resulting in a robust core-shell architecture.

Preparation and characterization of Zn-PC/PA@Gel

To ensure local retention and sustained release of Zn-PC/PA at the lesion site, Zn-PC/PA was encapsulated within an injectable, photo-crosslinkable GelMA hydrogel. The chemical structure of the synthesized GelMA was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 3A). Macroscopic gelation was assessed by an inversion test (Fig. 3B). Both pure GelMA and the Zn-PC/PA@Gel precursor solutions were flowable at room temperature and rapidly crosslinked into stable, non-flowing hydrogels upon blue-light irradiation (405 nm), demonstrating robust photocuring capability. SEM revealed that GelMA formed a smooth, interconnected porous network, whereas Zn-PC/PA@Gel retained a similar porous architecture but exhibited a rougher surface. High-magnification images showed Zn-PC/PA nanoparticles uniformly embedded within the pore walls, indicating effective encapsulation without disrupting network integrity (Fig. 3C). EDS elemental mapping of Zn-PC/PA@Gel showed a homogeneous distribution of C, N, O, and Zn throughout the composite, indicating uniform incorporation of the nanoassembly within the hydrogel matrix (Supplementary Fig. S4). Quantitative EDS analysis further revealed weight percentages of 52.87 wt% for C, 25.86 wt% for N, 19.32 wt% for O, and 1.95 wt% for Zn (Supplementary Fig. S5). For spinal cord injury applications, injectability and tissue-matched mechanics are essential. The precursor could be readily injected into a mold and photocured to a defined shape (Fig. 3D). Rheological measurements further supported appropriate viscoelasticity, with the storage modulus exceeding the loss modulus within the linear viscoelastic region for both GelMA and Zn-PC/PA@Gel (Fig. 3E, F), consistent with solid-like behavior after curing. In addition, the composite hydrogel exhibited pronounced shear-thinning behavior (Fig. 3G), facilitating injection through a needle while maintaining shape fidelity after placement, as illustrated by the ability to write “GEL” without spreading.

Mechanical compatibility was further evaluated by compression testing. Stress-strain curves showed that increasing the GelMA content (10% to 30%) enhanced mechanical strength (Fig. 3H), and incorporation of Zn-PC/PA reinforced the hydrogel network, yielding higher compressive stress at failure than pure GelMA (Fig. 3I). The modulus of the composite hydrogel remained stable over a broad strain range (Fig. 3J), indicating structural integrity under loading. Biodegradability was assessed using MMP-2-mediated enzymatic degradation. Both hydrogels degraded over time, and Zn-PC/PA@Gel displayed a slightly faster degradation profile than GelMA alone, which may be attributable to increased hydrophilicity or surface area after particle incorporation (Fig. 3L). In parallel, Zn2+ release from the composite hydrogel was monitored, reaching a cumulative release of 86.62% at 16 h (Fig. 3M). Safety and biocompatibility were then examined. Hemocompatibility assays showed that red blood cells incubated with Zn-PC/PA@Gel extracts maintained normal morphology comparable to the PBS control, and hemolysis remained well below the 5% safety threshold across all tested concentrations (Fig. 3N, O). Cytocompatibility was confirmed by Live/Dead staining and CCK-8 assays. Encapsulated cells remained largely viable with a healthy spreading morphology over 12, 24, and 48 h, and quantitative analyses indicated cell viabilities above 80% across concentrations (Fig. 3K, P). Proliferation measurements further suggested slightly enhanced cell growth in the Zn-PC/PA@Gel group by 48 h (Fig. 3Q), consistent with a supportive microenvironment provided by the functionalized scaffold. Overall, Zn-PC/PA@Gel exhibits injectability, rapid photocuring, mechanical integrity, biodegradability, and favorable biocompatibility, supporting its use for localized delivery in SCI.

In vitro stimuli-responsive release behavior and ROS-scavenging capacity of Zn-PC/PA

To verify the stepwise responsiveness and functional outputs of Zn-PC/PA under lesion-relevant microenvironmental cues, in vitro studies were conducted focusing on stimuli-triggered degradation, NO generation, and redox regulation. The 0.5 mM H2O2 and pH 5.5 conditions were used as simplified in vitro stimuli to model ROS-rich and acidic pathological features associated with SCI-related oxidative stress and acidic intracellular compartments, rather than to exactly reproduce the full in vivo SCI microenvironment. Specifically, H2O2 was used to evaluate ROS-triggered shell destabilization and NO generation, whereas pH 5.5 was selected as a stringent acidic condition relevant to intracellular acidic compartments after nanoparticle uptake. As shown in Fig. 4A, Zn-PC/PA is designed to undergo a two-stage degradation process, with an initial ROS-responsive event followed by an acidity-promoted core disassembly. Specifically, H2O2 oxidizes guanidino groups on the PA chains, disrupting the coordination framework and destabilizing the outer shell, whereas an acidic environment weakens Zn2+-PC coordination and drives gradual disassembly of the Zn-PC core, enabling sustained release of Zn2+ and PC. Morphological changes were first examined by incubating Zn-PC/PA in 0.5 mM H2O2 or in a combined condition of 0.5 mM H2O2 at pH 5.5 and imaging by SEM (Fig. 4B). Under H2O2 alone, evident disruption of the PA shell was observed, while the combined H2O2 and acidic condition led to pronounced overall degradation, consistent with sequential engagement of both triggers. Given that oxidative conversion of guanidino groups can generate NO, NO production was further evaluated using an NO-specific indicator strip. A clear color change to red occurred in the presence of 0.5 mM H2O2, indicating NO generation (Fig. 4C). Quantitative measurements showed sustained NO release under both H2O2 alone and H2O2 at pH 5.5, with comparable release kinetics and cumulative output, indicating that H2O2 triggers NO generation while acidity is dispensable (Fig. 4D). In order to further verify the generation of NO using more quantitative methods, the Griess test was carried out by measuring the accumulation of NO2−, and a standard curve was established to convert the absorbance value into NO2− concentration (Supplementary Fig. S6). The Griess assay results further confirmed the stimulus-dependent NO generation from Zn-PC/PA under H2O2-containing conditions. In parallel, PC release during degradation was slow and sustained, consistent with progressive core disassembly (Fig. 4E). Together, these data support a staged microenvironment-responsive behavior in which ROS initiates shell destabilization and NO generation, followed by acidity-promoted core disassembly enabling sustained release of PC and Zn2+.

After confirming the stimuli-responsive degradation and functional outputs, the ROS-scavenging and antioxidant capacity of Zn-PC/PA was further evaluated using complementary chemical assays. As shown in Fig. 4F and G, antioxidant activity was quantified by monitoring the absorbance changes of DPPH (517 nm) and ABTS (734 nm) radical probes, together with the TMB oxidation assay (654 nm). Zn-PC/PA exhibited strong radical-quenching activity against both DPPH and ABTS and effectively suppressed TMB oxidation, with an overall performance slightly lower than the positive control yet markedly superior to the other formulations. To clarify whether the antioxidant effect was concentration dependent, dose-response experiments were performed. Increasing Zn-PC/PA concentrations resulted in a pronounced rise in the scavenging efficiencies for DPPH and ABTS radicals as well as for oxTMB reduction, indicating a clear and reproducible concentration-dependent antioxidant behavior (Fig. 4H, I). This robust activity is primarily attributable to the intrinsic reducing power of PC, while the Zn-centered coordination environment is expected to provide cooperative redox-active sites that further enhance electron-transfer and radical-quenching processes, leading to an overall synergistic effect. Overall, Zn-PC/PA undergoes staged degradation with NO and PC release under ROS-rich and acidic conditions while providing sustained ROS scavenging and antioxidant protection, thereby offering mechanistic support for its application in oxidative stress-associated pathological microenvironments.

In vitro anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic effects of Zn-PC/PA@Gel

To further probe the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic potential of Zn-PC/PA at the cellular level, the investigation was initiated by focusing on its anti-inflammatory effects in M1 macrophages. Zn-PC/PA@Gel is expected to attenuate the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and promote a shift toward an M2-like reparative state, accompanied by reduced inflammatory activation and enhanced anti-inflammatory polarization (Fig. 5A). To clarify cellular uptake, Zn-PC/PA was labeled with the Cy3 fluorophore, and flow cytometry was used to quantify macrophage-associated fluorescence after incubation for different durations. As shown in Fig. 5B, cellular uptake increased progressively over time and approached saturation at approximately 2 h. Subcellular distribution was further visualized by confocal laser scanning microscopy, where the intracellular Cy3 signal intensified markedly with prolonged incubation (Fig. 5C, D). Quantitative analysis indicated efficient internalization within 1 h, followed by a slower uptake phase that reached a plateau around 2 h, consistent with the flow-cytometry kinetics, indicating that M1 macrophages actively and rapidly internalize Zn-PC/PA as a prerequisite for subsequent intracellular activity.

Anti-inflammatory efficacy was then evaluated in an H2O2-induced inflammatory model of M1-polarized macrophages by comparing six groups, including a blank control, an H2O2 model group, and treatments with GelMA, Zn-PC@Gel, Zn-PA@Gel, and Zn-PC/PA@Gel. Immunofluorescence staining of the M2 marker CD206 and the pro-inflammatory mediators iNOS, TNF-α, and IL-6 showed that Zn-PC/PA@Gel produced the strongest CD206 signal while markedly suppressing iNOS, TNF-α, and IL-6 (Fig. 5E). These trends were further supported by fluorescence quantification (Fig. 5I-L). In contrast, Zn-PC@Gel and Zn-PA@Gel showed only partial effects, suggesting that the complete Zn-PC/PA nanoassembly provides a stronger integrated anti-inflammatory effect than the partial coordination assemblies. Because free Zn2+, PC, or PA dispersed in GelMA cannot reproduce the same coordination network, core-shell architecture, ROS/acidity-responsive disassembly behavior, or sustained local release profile, the enhanced activity of Zn-PC/PA@Gel is interpreted in the context of the coordinated nanoassembly rather than as a simple free-component effect. This enhanced integrated effect may be attributed to ROS scavenging by PC, which dampens oxidative stress-driven inflammatory activation, together with NO generation from PA under ROS conditions, which can further modulate key inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and facilitate macrophage reprogramming toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype. Consistently, ELISA of culture supernatants revealed significantly reduced secretion of TNF-α and IL-6 after Zn-PC/PA@Gel treatment, in agreement with the immunofluorescence results and further supporting its anti-inflammatory efficacy at the level of cytokine release (Fig. 5F-H). To further quantitatively evaluate whether the combined components produced synergistic rather than merely additive effects, Chou-Talalay combination index (CI) analysis was performed. In the anti-inflammatory assay using M1-polarized macrophages, PC and PA jointly suppressed IL-6 secretion with a CI value of 0.6765, indicating a synergistic anti-inflammatory effect (Supplementary Fig. S7A).

To further clarify whether NO generation contributes to the anti-inflammatory activity of Zn-PC/PA@Gel, an NO-scavenging experiment was performed using carboxy-PTIO, a broad-spectrum NO scavenger. Under H2O2-induced inflammatory conditions, Zn-PC/PA@Gel markedly reduced TNF-α and IL-6 expression, whereas carboxy-PTIO partially weakened this inhibitory effect (Supplementary Fig. S8). These results indicate that NO generation participates in the anti-inflammatory effect of Zn-PC/PA@Gel. However, the reversal was incomplete, suggesting that the overall anti-inflammatory activity is likely mediated by multiple coordinated mechanisms, including ROS scavenging, Zn2+-associated regulation, and NO release.

Building on the macrophage results, Zn-PC/PA@Gel was next examined in a neuronal model to determine whether it could directly protect neurons from oxidative injury beyond immunomodulation. VSC4.1 motor neuron-like cells were therefore selected for subsequent studies (Fig. 6A). Cellular uptake kinetics were first quantified by flow cytometry using Cy3-labeled Zn-PC/PA. As shown in Fig. 6B, intracellular fluorescence increased progressively over 0–3 h, indicating a continuous rise in nanoparticle internalization. Consistent visualization was obtained by confocal microscopy, where the intracellular red fluorescence became markedly stronger with prolonged incubation and approached a steady level after 2–3 h (Fig. 6C, D). Together, these results demonstrate that VSC4.1 cells efficiently internalize Zn-PC/PA and reach an uptake equilibrium within several hours, providing a foundation for subsequent mechanistic studies of intracellular protection.

To delineate the molecular basis of cytoprotection, key proteins closely related to oxidative stress responses and apoptosis were examined. Nrf2 is a central transcription factor governing cellular antioxidant responses. Upon activation, it induces a broad set of antioxidant enzymes and phase II detoxification genes, thereby strengthening cellular resistance to oxidative injury [44, 45]. Caspase-3 is a pivotal protease in apoptosis, and its activated form, cleaved caspase-3, directly mediates the terminal apoptotic process [46]. Using an H2O2-induced oxidative injury model in VSC4.1 cells, the effects of different treatments on these proteins were evaluated. Immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess the expression and subcellular localization of Nrf2, caspase-3, and cleaved caspase-3, followed by quantitative analysis (Fig. 6E-H). Compared with other groups, Zn-PC/PA@Gel markedly enhanced Nrf2 expression and nuclear translocation, indicating effective activation of endogenous antioxidant defense pathways. Notably, Zn-PC@Gel also increased Nrf2 levels, and its effect was not significantly different from that of Zn-PA@Gel, supporting that Zn2+ contributes to Nrf2 pathway activation. In contrast, H2O2 stimulation substantially elevated caspase-3 and cleaved caspase-3 expression, consistent with activation of apoptotic progression. Treatment with Zn-PC@Gel, Zn-PA@Gel, or Zn-PC/PA@Gel significantly suppressed both caspase-3 and cleaved caspase-3, with Zn-PC/PA@Gel showing the most pronounced inhibition among the tested formulations. These results indicate that Zn-PC/PA effectively interrupts oxidative stress-triggered apoptotic signaling and that the combined formulation provides enhanced cytoprotection relative to the individual components. Consistently, Chou-Talalay CI analysis based on VSC4.1 cell viability showed that the combination of PC and Zn2+ produced a CI value of 0.8378 under H2O2-induced oxidative stress, supporting a synergistic neuroprotective effect (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Overall, these neuronal-cell results complement the macrophage findings and support that Zn-PC/PA@Gel not only modulates inflammation-related cellular responses but also strengthens neuronal survival under oxidative stress through activation of the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant response and suppression of the caspase-3-dependent apoptotic pathway. Together, the current results suggest that Zn-PC/PA@Gel may protect neurons through both direct neuronal uptake-mediated protection and indirect macrophage-mediated immunomodulation. However, neuron-macrophage co-culture or conditioned-medium experiments were not performed in this study and will be valuable in future work to further distinguish the relative contribution of these two effects.

Mitochondrial protection by Zn-PC/PA@Gel was next interrogated at the cellular level through analyses of mitochondrial membrane potential, redox balance, and Ca2+ homeostasis. JC-1 staining revealed intact mitochondrial polarization in the control group, whereas H2O2 exposure caused a typical depolarization characterized by diminished red aggregates and enhanced green monomers. GelMA alone provided limited protection, while Zn-PC@Gel and Zn-PA@Gel partially restored the red aggregate signal. Notably, Zn-PC/PA@Gel produced the most pronounced recovery, evidenced by an increased aggregate to monomer ratio (Fig. 7A, B), indicating effective blockade of oxidative stress-triggered mitochondrial depolarization. Consistently, DCFH-DA measurements showed robust cytosolic ROS accumulation after H2O2 stimulation, which was markedly reduced by Zn-PC/PA@Gel toward near baseline levels. The single component also decreased ROS but to a lesser extent (Fig. 7C, D). Given the pivotal role of ROS-Ca2+ coupling in amplifying mitochondrial injury and apoptosis, intracellular Ca2+ was further assessed using Fluo-4 AM. Oxidative stress induced aberrant Ca2+ elevation, whereas Zn-PC/PA@Gel significantly normalized Ca2+ levels, with PA containing treatments showing a more evident contribution to Ca2+ correction, suggesting a PA-related role in ion homeostasis regulation (Supplementary Fig. S9). Mitochondrial superoxide was then evaluated using MitoSOX. H2O2 markedly increased mitochondria-derived ROS, while Zn-PC/PA@Gel strongly suppressed this signal, indicating that the antioxidant effect extends to the mitochondrial compartment and interrupts the feed-forward loop of mitochondrial ROS escalation and functional collapse (Fig. 7E, F). Finally, mitophagy activation was examined by co-staining lysosomes and mitochondria with LysoTracker and MitoTracker. Compared with the other treatment groups, Zn-PC/PA@Gel markedly increased lysosomal signals and enhanced mitochondria-lysosome colocalization (Fig. 7G). Line-scan profiles showed more consistent peak overlap, indicating activated mitophagy (Fig. 7H). Taken together, these results support that Zn-PC/PA@Gel confers multi-layer mitochondrial protection by suppressing cytosolic and mitochondrial ROS, stabilizing Ca2+ homeostasis, preserving membrane potential, and promoting clearance of damaged mitochondria, thereby restoring mitochondrial quality control under oxidative stress.

Mechanistic investigation of Zn-PC/PA@Gel in modulating inflammatory signaling and restoring mitochondrial homeostasis

To clarify the mechanisms underlying Zn-PC/PA@Gel-mediated repair after SCI, transcriptomic profiling was performed on spinal cord tissues from SCI mice and Zn-PC/PA@Gel-treated animals. Principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering showed high within-group consistency and clear separation between groups (Fig. 8A and Supplementary Fig. S10), supporting the robustness of the sequencing dataset. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the thresholds of |log2(fold change)| > 1 and adjusted P value/FDR < 0.05. Based on these criteria, Zn-PC/PA@Gel broadly remodeled the post-injury transcriptional landscape, with 179 genes upregulated and 1197 genes downregulated relative to the SCI model (Fig. 8B).

(E) GO enrichment of downregulated DEGs. (F) GSEA showing coordinated suppression of JAK-STAT pathways. (G, H) FOXO3a expression and nuclear localization in H2O2 injured VSC4.1 cells, along with FOXO3a fluorescence quantification. Scale bars represent 50 μm. KEGG enrichment of the downregulated genes highlighted strong suppression of canonical inflammatory and immune-response pathways, including TNF, NF-κB, NOD-like receptor, Toll-like receptor, IL-17, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways (Fig. 8C). Notably, pathways closely linked to oxidative stress and secondary tissue damage, such as necroptosis, complement and coagulation cascades, and neutrophil extracellular trap formation, were also significantly downregulated. These signatures collectively indicate that Zn-PC/PA@Gel concurrently restrains neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, with JAK-STAT signaling emerging as a potentially important regulatory hub. Consistently, representative downregulated genes included pro-inflammatory cytokines (Il1β, Il6), chemokines (Ccl2, Ccl3, Cxcl2, Cxcl10), inflammation-associated enzymes (Nos2, Ptgs2), NADPH oxidase subunits (Ncf1, Ncf2), and the JAK-STAT transcription factors (Stat3, Stat1) (Fig. 8D), supporting a coordinated reduction in inflammatory amplification and ROS-producing machinery. GO enrichment further refined these alterations, showing significant representation of terms related to positive regulation of inflammation and ROS metabolic processes in the biological-process category, and downregulation of inflammation- and redox-associated structures such as the NADPH oxidase complex and inflammasome-related components in the cellular-component category (Fig. 8E). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) reinforced these conclusions at the pathway level, revealing coordinated suppression of the overall JAK-STAT gene set, together with consistent downshifts of TNF, NF-κB, NOD-like receptor, and Toll-like receptor signaling programs (Fig. 8F and Supplementary Fig. S11). In order to further verify the findings of transcriptomics, some representative genes related to inflammatory response, oxidative stress and mitochondrial regulation were detected through qPCR, including Stat3, Stat1, Il1β, Il6, Nos2, Ptgs2, Ccl2, Cxcl10, Ncf1, Ncf2, Sod2 and Foxo3a. The qPCR results are generally consistent with the trend of RNA-seq (Supplementary Tab. S1 and Fig. S12), which supports the reliability of transcriptome analysis.

Guided by these omics signatures, mechanistic validation was performed, emphasizing JAK-STAT signaling and downstream mitochondrial quality control. In an H2O2-induced injury model in VSC4.1 cells, the transcription factor FOXO3a, a key downstream effector associated with mitochondrial stress adaptation and mitophagy-related gene programs, was examined (Fig. 8G, H) [47, 48]. Immunofluorescence indicated that Zn-PC@Gel, Zn-PA@Gel, and Zn-PC/PA@Gel all enhanced FOXO3a expression and nuclear translocation, with the most pronounced effect observed for Zn-PC/PA@Gel. Given prior evidence that Zn2+ can modulate STAT3-related signaling, these results support a role for Zn2+ released from the formulation in tuning this pathway and strengthening FOXO3a-mediated adaptive responses. As a functionally relevant downstream component, the mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme SOD2 was markedly upregulated by Zn-PC/PA@Gel (Fig. 8M), suggesting reinforcement of mitochondrial antioxidant defense through a STAT3-FOXO3a-SOD2 -related pathway.

To further determine whether STAT3-related signaling contributes to the mitochondrial protective effect of Zn-PC/PA@Gel, a pathway-interference experiment was performed using Stattic, a STAT3 inhibitor. Under H2O2-induced oxidative stress, Zn-PC/PA@Gel improved SOD2 expression and partially restored intracellular ATP levels, whereas Stattic partially attenuated these effects (Supplementary Fig. S13). These results suggest that STAT3-related SOD2 regulation contributes to the mitochondrial protective activity of Zn-PC/PA@Gel. Functional readouts of cellular bioenergetics and redox balance further supported this mechanism. Zn-PC/PA@Gel significantly increased intracellular ATP levels and elevated the activity of antioxidant enzymes including HO-1 and SOD, while reducing the lipid peroxidation marker MDA (Fig. 8I-M). Recovery of ATP reflects improved mitochondrial energy production, whereas increased HO-1 and SOD activity together with reduced MDA indicates strengthened antioxidant capacity and attenuated oxidative damage. Collectively, transcriptomic analyses and targeted validation support that Zn-PC/PA@Gel is associated with multi-layered regulation of inflammatory, stress-response, and mitochondrial-protective pathways. The enhanced biological effects are closely related to the integrated Zn2+-coordinated nanoassembly, which combines ROS-responsive NO generation, sustained antioxidant release, and mitochondrial protective signaling. However, direct structural-disruption experiments, such as selective shell removal or deliberate destabilization of the core-shell architecture, will be valuable in future studies to further define the causal relationship between nanoassembly architecture and biological function.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of Zn-PC/PA@Gel in a mouse spinal cord contusion model

Secondary injury after SCI exhibits a clear therapeutic time-window. The acute phase is dominated by inflammation and oxidative stress that are driven by tissue disruption and cell death, followed by a chronic phase characterized by structural remodeling and locomotor improvement [49]. In line with this course, the in vivo therapeutic potential of Zn-PC/PA@Gel was systematically evaluated in a mouse spinal cord contusion model. Experimental groups included a sham group, an SCI group, and SCI mice implanted with blank GelMA, Zn-PC@Gel, Zn-PA@Gel, or Zn-PC/PA@Gel, following the timeline shown in Fig. 9A. Histological assessments were performed on day 3 and day 28 to distinguish early tissue protection from later structural repair. H&E staining on day 3 revealed typical injury-associated architectural disruption, edema, and inflammatory infiltration. Relative to the SCI group, all treatment groups mitigated tissue damage to varying extents and reduced cavitation. Notably, Zn-PC/PA@Gel preserved better tissue continuity in the acute phase and further exhibited a more intact morphology with smaller tissue defects by day 28 (Fig. 9B). Nissl staining provided consistent evidence for preservation of neuronal structural integrity. While all treatments promoted Nissl substance retention compared with the SCI group, Zn-PC/PA@Gel maintained a richer and more continuous Nissl distribution at day 28 (Fig. 9C). These findings suggest that the benefit extends beyond physical filling or mechanical support by the hydrogel and is more likely attributable to attenuation of acute secondary injury that creates a microenvironment favorable for subsequent remodeling. In comparison, the partial coordination formulations showed partial improvement but remained inferior to Zn-PC/PA@Gel, supporting the advantage of the integrated Zn2+-coordinated nanoassembly under complex post-injury conditions.

Because SCI is frequently accompanied by neurogenic bladder dysfunction, bladder tissues were also examined by H&E on postoperative day 7, and no obvious differences were observed among groups (Fig. 9D). This outcome likely reflects effective perioperative aseptic control and standardized bladder management, which can reduce infection and urinary retention-associated secondary bladder wall injury, making morphological differences less apparent within this window. The analysis therefore focused on neurons within the lesion, since neuronal survival is a prerequisite for locomotor improvement and the acute apoptotic cascade is a major driver of irreversible cell loss [49]. Immunofluorescence on day 3 showed a larger NeuN-positive area together with weaker cleaved caspase-3 signal in the Zn-PC/PA@Gel group, and quantitative analysis similarly indicated the lowest apoptosis-associated signal with the highest neuron-associated area (Fig. 9E-G). These data suggest that Zn-PC/PA@Gel effectively suppresses the apoptotic execution process during the peak secondary-injury period, thereby preserving neuronal reserves and providing a cellular basis for the improved tissue structure and Nissl retention observed at day 28. Overall, these findings provide consistent evidence across tissue architecture, neuronal integrity, and the balance between apoptosis and survival, supporting that Zn-PC/PA@Gel confers both acute neuroprotection and enhanced tissue remodeling after SCI and establishes a solid histological basis for subsequent functional improvement.

A central challenge after SCI is that secondary inflammation is self-amplifying, which perpetuates local tissue destruction and accelerates neuronal loss, such that early immune microenvironment remodeling often sets the upper limit for subsequent repair. Following the tissue collection and analysis workflow in Fig. 10A, the injured segment was harvested on day 3 after injury and serial sagittal sections were prepared for integrated molecular and in situ assessment across the lesion core and peri lesion regions. ELISA showed that TNF α, IL-6, and IL 1β were markedly elevated in the SCI model, whereas Zn-PC/PA@Gel produced the strongest suppression of all three cytokines and outperformed the single component treatments Zn-PC@Gel and Zn PA@Gel, indicating more effective disruption of early pro inflammatory amplification (Fig. 10B-D). Consistently, in situ immunofluorescence provided spatial and cell level evidence, as F4/80 labeled macrophage and microglia infiltration was prominent in the model and GelMA groups but was significantly reduced by Zn-PC/PA@Gel, suggesting restrained recruitment and accumulation of inflammatory cells around the lesion. In parallel, IL-6 and F4/80 colocalization was clearly weakened after Zn-PC/PA@Gel treatment, with quantification confirming a reduced IL-6 positive area, supporting that Zn-PC/PA@Gel not only lowers inflammatory cell burden but also dampens their pro-inflammatory output (Fig. 10E-G). In contrast, TGF-β signaling was enhanced in the Zn-PC/PA@Gel group and was more evident within regions associated with F4/80 positive cells, with quantification showing an increased TGF-β positive area, indicating a shift toward a repair associated immune milieu (Fig. 10H-J). Together, these results suggest that Zn-PC/PA@Gel does not merely suppress inflammation but remodels the immune microenvironment by limiting excessive infiltration while promoting repair-related signaling. The partial improvements observed with Zn-PC@Gel or Zn-PA@Gel alone further support a synergistic advantage of Zn-PC/PA@Gel, consistent with concurrent downregulation of pro-inflammatory cues and upregulation of reparative factors in situ.

Across the 28-day follow-up, Zn-PC/PA@Gel improved multiple behavioral endpoints and produced a coherent locomotor recovery trajectory in the mouse SCI model. Although ROS inhibition, inflammatory regulation and transcriptomic analysis were mainly carried out in the early stage after the injury, these early molecular and cellular events are closely related to subsequent tissue protection and repair. Early reduction of oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions can reduce neuronal apoptosis, maintain tissue integrity, and create a favorable microenvironment for later vascular remodeling, glial scar attenuation and axon protection. Consistently, the decrease in the level of early inflammatory factors and the reduction of immune cell infiltration are accompanied by the improvement of neuronal preservation on the third day and the optimization of histological reconstruction on the 28th day, thus establishing a mechanistic link between early microenvironmental regulation and long-term locomotor improvement. Footprint-based gait analysis showed more continuous hindlimb contact and smoother step trajectories in the Zn-PC/PA@Gel group, suggesting better weight bearing and left-right coordination (Fig. 11A and Supplementary Fig. S14). This was consistent with a larger maximal inclination angle in the inclined plane test and a longer latency to fall in the rotarod assay, indicating more stable gains in strength, endurance, and fine motor coordination (Fig. 11B, I). Longitudinal BMS scoring further revealed a steeper recovery slope and a higher plateau in the Zn-PC/PA@Gel group, supporting both accelerated early improvement and improved long-term locomotor performance (Fig. 11H). Faster body weight recovery further reflected a systemic benefit (Supplementary Fig. S15). In addition, H&E staining of major organs showed no evident histopathological abnormalities after Zn-PC/PA@Gel treatment, supporting favorable in vivo biocompatibility (Supplementary Fig. S16). Importantly, these quantitative behavioral improvements aligned with immunohistological findings at day 28. Immunofluorescence showed that Zn-PC/PA@Gel reduced the GFAP positive area while increasing Tuj-1 positivity, indicating suppression of excessive reactive astrocyte activation and attenuation of glial scar-associated inhibitory barriers, which may facilitate axonal preservation or regrowth (Fig. 11C-E). Meanwhile, CD31 positive microvessels were markedly increased in the Zn-PC/PA@Gel group, suggesting enhanced revascularization and improved local oxygen and nutrient delivery (Fig. 11F, G). This vascular support is expected to alleviate chronic hypoxia and metabolic stress, and may also create more favorable conditions for repair by promoting waste clearance and cell trafficking. Overall, the behavioral and histological outcomes were directionally concordant and support that Zn-PC/PA@Gel acts beyond transient local protection. The treatment mitigates oxidative and inflammatory burden during the acute phase to preserve tissue integrity and reduce neuronal loss, and it further limits scarring while strengthening vascular support during recovery, thereby providing a structural and metabolic basis for improved gait, coordination, and overall locomotor function.

Conclusions

In summary, this work presents a zinc-coordinated cascade therapeutic nanoassembly embedded in an injectable, photo-crosslinkable GelMA matrix for localized treatment of spinal cord contusion injury. The Zn-PC/PA core-shell structure responds to the ROS-rich and acidic injury milieu, enabling early NO-mediated microenvironment tuning together with sustained antioxidant protection, and further supports neuronal mitochondrial homeostasis through enhanced mitochondrial quality control. In vitro, Zn-PC/PA@Gel attenuated inflammatory activation in macrophages, promoted a reparative shift, protected motor neuron-like cells from oxidative injury, and suppressed apoptosis while improving mitochondrial membrane potential, Ca2+ homeostasis, and mitophagy associated coupling. Transcriptomic profiling and validation in injured spinal cord tissue revealed broad downregulation of inflammatory and stress networks, including prominent suppression of JAK-STAT associated signaling. In vivo, Zn-PC/PA@Gel reduced acute inflammatory cytokines and immune-cell-associated signals, preserved tissue architecture and neuronal integrity, limited glial reactivity, promoted revascularization, and translated these tissue level benefits into sustained locomotor improvement over 28 days. Overall, Zn-PC/PA@Gel provides a potential approach for coordinated microenvironmental and organelle-level regulation, offering a rational therapeutic strategy to mitigate secondary injury and support preclinical locomotor improvement after SCI.
