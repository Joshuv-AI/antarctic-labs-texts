Accurate Quantification of 45N2O with a Membrane Inlet Mass Spectrometer by Optimizing the Temperature of a Cold Trap
Authors: Chunyi Kuang, Xiaomei Shen, Zhiming Qu, Baoshan Zhang, Hui Zhao, Jiapeng Wu, Yiguo Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Membrane Inlet Mass Spectrometry (MIMS) is extensively applied in the study of nitrogen cycling processes. However, robust methods for the quantitative detection of N2O by this technique are still scarce. In this study, acidified azide was employed to reduce 15NO2 – to generate 45N2O, which was used to make a standard curve for qualifying 45N2O with MIMS. Key parameters affecting the azide reduction, including reagent concentration, reaction time, reaction mode, and salinity, were systematically evaluated and optimized. Then, the generated 45N2O dissolved in water was introduced into the MIMS via a cold trap maintained at −88 ± 2 °C. This specific temperature capitalizes on the difference in the freezing points of N2O (−91 °C) and CO2 (−78 °C), thereby effectively minimizing CO2 interference during N2O measurement. Under optimized conditions, the linear fitting between 45N2O ion current intensity and expected 45N2O concentration showed an excellent linearity in the range of 0.1–100 μmol/L (R 2 = 0.998), with a detection limit of 0.1 μmol/L. This method achieves accurate (relative error: 4.688%) and precise (relative standard deviation: 1.560%) detection of 45N2O and holds potential for future application in tracing microbial N2O source–sink processes across diverse habitats.

Introduction

Nitrous oxide (N2O) is a long-lived (116 ± 9 years) potent greenhouse gas and ozone-depleting substance, possessing a global warming potential 265–298 times more than that of CO2. , It is mostly released via microbial nitrification (from NH4 +) and denitrification (from NO3 – and NO2 –) activities. Its concentration has been escalating annually due to increased ambient nitrogen loading, , presenting a substantial threat to global climate change and ecosystem balance. , Therefore, accurate estimation of its fluxes and sources is of crucial importance. ,

15N stable isotope tracing is a commonly used technique for determining the sources of N2O, , relying on Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometry (IRMS) , and Isotope Dilution Mass Spectrometry (IDMS). However, IRMS requires high sample purity and minimal matrix interference, which results in cumbersome and time-consuming sample pretreatment (including chemical preprocessing steps such as purification and separation). IDMS incurs significant expenses due to the necessity of synthesizing particular labeled internal standards.. Recently, the Membrane Inlet Mass Spectrometer (MIMS) has been employed for the quantification of dissolved gases (N2 and NO) and the detection of microbial nitrogen cycling rates (nitrogen fixation, , denitrification, , nitrification, and anammox , ) in aquatic ecosystems due to its high precision, rapid sample processing, and cost-effectiveness (a comparison of MIMS, IRMS, and IDMS in terms of cost and time efficiency is shown in Table S1). Nonetheless, CO2 and N2O possess identical mass-to-charge ratio (m/z = 44), and the semipermeable membrane of MIMS cannot retain CO2. − The resulting peak overlap issue interferes with N2O detection. Despite prior research has endeavored to mitigate CO2 interference using a degasser and ascarite trap, drawbacks such as complex procedures and high detection limits still exist.

In this study, by leveraging the difference in freezing points between CO2 (−78 °C) and N2O (−91 °C), a cold trap device was used to freeze CO2 during the gas transmission process from the membrane injector to the mass spectrometer. This approach thereby diminishes interference and enhances the quantitative accuracy of 45N2O. Additionally, azide (HN3) was employed as the reducing agent to reduce 15NO2 – to 45N2O, following the reaction: 1 N15O2−+H++HN3→N452O+H2O+N2

Based on the reaction, the reaction conditions (azide concentration, reaction mode, reaction time, and salinity) were optimized. The accuracy and precision of 45N2O determination with MIMS were evaluated under optimal conditions. This study aimed to establish a method for the precise detection of 45N2O with MIMS.

Materials and Methods

General Procedure for the 45N2O Determination with MIMS

The reagents and solutions were prepared following the description in the Supporting Information and Methods section. The 45N2O determination with MIMS was accomplished by two primary procedures: preparation and detection of 45N2O (Figure ). 15NO2 – reacted with azide to generate 45N2O. A 1 mL plastic syringe was used to inject the reducing agent (acidified azide solution) into Labco bottles via a double-needle technique (long steel needle in, short needle out). The long needle delivered 0.2 mL of acidified azide solution to the vessel bottom, while the short needle penetrated the sealed cap to displace 0.2 mL of the 15NO2 – solution and maintain pressure equilibrium. Labco bottles containing the reaction solution reacted fully under specific conditions, and then 45N2O was detected with MIMS (HPR40, Hiden Analytical).

General procedure of the detection of 45N2O with MIMS. The cold trap (cylindrical Dewar flask, KGW, Germany) is made of borosilicate glass, with a capacity of 1 L, an inner diameter of 77 mm, a height of 235 mm, and a liquid nitrogen evaporation rate of 0.35 L/day.

Samples were introduced into the equipment at a flow rate of 3 mL/min via a peristaltic pump. A portion of the gas entered the cold trap through the membrane injector, while the waste liquid flowed into the waste collection tank. The semipermeable membrane was composed of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). The cold trap could mitigate the interference of water vapor and CO2 on the target gas. The gas for measurement enters the quadrupole mass analyzer, where it is initially ionized by the ionization device. Subsequently, ions with different m/z ratios enter the detector after being separated by an oscillating electric field. The dissolved gas concentrations measured with MIMS were calibrated using the ion current intensity.

Optimizing a Suitable Cold Trap Temperature for 45N2O Determination

To examine the influence of cold trap temperature on 45N2O detection, three cold trap temperatures were set: −50 ± 3 °C (above the freezing point of CO2, prepared by mixing n-butanol and liquid nitrogen in a volume ratio of 2:1), −88 ± 2 °C (between the freezing points of CO2 and N2O, made by mixing n-butanol and liquid nitrogen in a volume ratio of 1:2), and −196 ± 3 °C (below the freezing point of N2O, composed of liquid nitrogen). The concentrations of 44N2O and 45N2O produced from the reaction between 15NO2 – (0, 100, 200, and 300 nmol/L) and azide were measured to elucidate the influence of the m/z of 44 and 45 current intensities on the accurate determination of 44N2O and 45N2O at varying cold trap temperatures.

Effects of Different Factors on the 15NO2 – Reduction

To ensure a sufficient reduction of 15NO2 –, it was necessary to investigate the optimal azide concentration and reaction time. The acidified azide solution (containing 10 mmol/L azide solution and 10% acetic acid) was diluted by 2-, 10-, 20-, and 100-fold, respectively, and 0.2 mL of each diluted solution was introduced into a Labco bottle (with 12.5 mL of 10 μmol/L 15NO2 –). The final azide concentrations in the bottles were 320.0 (undiluted), 160.0, 32.0, 16.0, and 3.2 μmol/L, respectively. The 45N2O concentrations were measured at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h. The optimal azide concentration and reaction time for the 15NO2 – reduction were established according to the generated 45N2O concentration under different azide concentrations and reaction times.

To investigate the effects of reaction mode and reaction time on the 15NO2 – reduction, static and oscillating (120 rpm) reactions were conducted at identical time intervals (0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h). The optimal reaction mode and reaction time were determined by the 45N2O concentration generated under different reaction modes and reaction times for 15NO2 – reduction.

To explore the effect of salinity on the 15NO2 – reduction, salinity levels were set at 0, 5, 10, 20, and 30‰, respectively. The influence of salinity on the 15NO2 – reduction was clarified based on the 45N2O concentration generated by the reaction at varying artificial seawater concentrations.

Standard Calibration Curve Formulation and Method Evaluation

A standard calibration curve was constructed based on the optimal cold trap temperature and reaction conditions. The linear range for 45N2O detection was established by performing a linear regression between the measured 45N2O concentrations and the known 15NO2 – concentrations (0.1–0.5, 0.1–100 μmol/L). The detection limit was expressed using absolute error (AE) and relative error (RE) based on the expected and measured 45N2O concentrations. Additionally, the precision of 45N2O determination with MIMS was evaluated through standard deviation (SD) and relative standard deviation (RSD) with three replicates (n = 3).

Results and Discussion

Optimal Cold Trap Temperature for 45N2O Detection with MIMS

The ion current intensities of 44N2O and 45N2O (pA) generated from the reaction between 15NO2 – and azide under various cold trap temperatures are illustrated in Figure a,b. Both CO2 and N2O were solidified while traversing the cold trap at a temperature of −196 ± 3 °C, resulting in low detected intensities of 44N2O and 45N2O (25–30 pA and 0.33–0.37 pA, respectively). When the cold trap temperature was −50 ± 3 °C, CO2 could not be frozen. Consequently, the 44N2O and 45N2O detection interfered with CO2, resulting in excessively high and unstable current intensities. A linear correlation was identified between the intensities of 44N2O (Figure a, R 2 = 0.928, slope = 1.333) and 45N2O (Figure b, R 2 = 0.970, slope = 0.027) and the 15NO2 – concentrations (0–300 nmol/L) at the cold trap temperature of −88 ± 2 °C. The CO2 could be effectively trapped at this temperature, thereby reducing its interference on the mass spectrometer signals (m/z of 44 and 45). Regulating the entrance temperature of the cold trap mitigates the CO2 interference. Accurately controlling the temperature between the freezing points of the target gas (N2O) and the interfering gas (CO2) facilitates the efficient capture of CO2 while averting the freezing of N2O. This substantially diminishes the interference of CO2 on mass spectrometer signals, establishing a crucial basis for the precise quantification of N2O content in samples with MIMS. Thus, −88 ± 2 °C was determined as the optimal cold trap temperature for 45N2O detection with MIMS.

Optimization for 15NO2 – Reduction

The impacts of azide concentration and reaction time on the 15NO2 – reduction are illustrated in Figure a. When the azide concentration was 320 μmol/L, the reduction of 15NO2 – proceeded rapidly within a 0–2 h period. The 45N2O concentration generated reached a maximum (109.91 pA) at 4 h and remained stable from 4 to 8 h. When the azide concentration was 160 μmol/L, the reaction still proceeded slowly during the 4–8 h period. The 45N2O concentration produced at 8 h (109.89 pA) was close to that generated at 4 h with 320 μmol/L. The 45N2O concentration was increased within 0–8 h when the azide concentration was below 160 μmol/L, but all maximum (32 μmol/L: 91.30 pA; 16 μmol/L: 69.32 pA; 3.2 μmol/L: 26.58 pA) values were lower than 109.91 pA. Therefore, 320 μmol/L of azide and a 4 h reaction time were selected as the reaction conditions for subsequent experiments.

The effects of the reaction mode and reaction time on the 15NO2 – reduction are shown in Figure b. The concentration of 45N2O reached a maximum (109.91 pA) at 4 h and then stabilized under the oscillating reaction. In contrast, the concentration of 45N2O continued to increase during the 4–8 h reaction period (30.43–74.42 pA) in the static reaction. Overall, the concentration of 45N2O generated in the oscillating reaction (109.91 pA) was higher than that in the static reaction (74.42 pA). Consequently, subsequent experiments were performed with oscillation for 4 h.

Under salinity conditions ranging from 0 to 30‰, the concentrations of 45N2O showed no significant change (Figure S1). Salinity (0–30‰) had no significant effect on the reduction of 15NO2 –, indicating that this method is applicable to the detection of 45N2O under different salinity conditions (e.g., freshwater and seawater). Although this study confirms that salinity within the range of 0–30‰ has no significant impact, it does not cover extreme ionic strength conditions (e.g., >50‰). In the future, membrane materials with antiadsorption properties can be used to reduce the adsorption of organic matter on the membrane surface. Alternatively, exploring the use of ultrapure water for sample dilution (while correcting the dilution factor) and optimizing membrane operating parameters (such as appropriately increasing the membrane temperature to enhance mass transfer efficiency) can be conducted to mitigate the interference effects caused by high ionic strength.

Linear Range, Accuracy, and Detection Limit

Based on the optimal conditions (cold trap temperature of −88 ± 2 °C, azide concentration of 320 μmol/L, reaction time of 4 h, and oscillating reaction), the linear range, accuracy, and detection limit of 45N2O with MIMS were evaluated. Regressions between the ion current intensity of 45N2O (pA) and the 15NO2 – concentration were linear in the ranges of 0.1–100 μmol/L (R 2 = 0.998) and 0.1–0.5 μmol/L (R 2 = 0.996) (Figure ). The AE and RE of 0.1 μmol/L 15NO2 – concentration were 0.005% and 4.688%, respectively (Table ). The detection limit of 45N2O with MIMS was 0.1 μmol/L. The SD and RSD for the 15NO2 – concentration of 0.1 μmol/L were 0.002 and 1.560%, respectively. Therefore, through the optimization of cold trap temperature and 15NO2 – reduction conditions, a sensitive (0.1 μmol/L), accurate (RE: 4.688%), and precise (RSD: 1.560%) determination of 45N2O with MIMS was achieved.

Method Evaluations and Applications

By exploration of an appropriate cold trap temperature, the interference from CO2 was reduced. Compared with the use of a degasser and an ascarite trap, the application of a cold trap to reduce CO2 interference not only simplifies the experimental procedures but also lowers the detection limit from 0.38 μmol/L to 0.1 μmol/L. Regarding the highly toxic azide used in the experiment, this study reduced its dosage while ensuring the sufficient completion of the reaction. In comparison with the previous studies, , the working concentration of azide was reduced from over 0.1 mol/L to 320 μmol/L. Safety protection measures should be properly implemented, such as wearing gloves, masks, goggles, etc., operating in a fume hood, and uniformly collecting and treating the waste liquid after use. Hydroxylamine is an important precursor of N2O. It can be converted into N2O by sodium hypochlorite for analysis, and the conversion is nontoxic and rapid. Sulfamic acid can be converted into N2O under acidic conditions. Both sulfamic acid and hydroxylamine are nontoxic reagents and can be used in experiments in the future to improve experimental safety.

In addition to the accurate detection of 45N2O, this method is also expected to be extended to trace the N2O source–sink processes in different habitats as ocean, estuary, and lake. By combination of isotope tracing and incubation, this method enables the determination of N2O production rates. Subsequent research may enhance this strategy by analyzing environmental samples. Additionally, by measuring N2O production rates and the processes of nitrification and denitrification in complex substrates (e.g., agricultural wastes and estuarine sediments), the contributions of various habitats to N2O emissions can be explored, thereby deepening our understanding of the nitrogen cycle.

Conclusion

A method for the detection of 45N2O with MIMS was established by optimizing the cold trap temperature (−88 ± 2 °C) and reaction conditions (azide concentration of 320 μmol/L, oscillating reaction for 4 h). This method enables accurate (RE: 4.688%), precise (RSD: 1.560%), and sensitive (0.1 μmol/L) detection of 45N2O with MIMS. The method can be expanded to investigate the N2O source–sink processes in various habitats. In the future, by testing samples from complex substrates (e.g., agricultural wastes and estuarine sediments), the contributions of various habitats to N2O emissions can be explored, thereby deepening our understanding of the nitrogen cycle.
