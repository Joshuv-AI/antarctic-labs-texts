Integrative multi-omics, machine learning, and experimental validation reveal that TPM1 suppresses M2 macrophage polarization and enhances chemosensitivity in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Yue Li, Baosheng Wei, Liuping Hu, Yuzhen Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) remains a therapeutic challenge due to chemoresistance and immunosuppression. This study aimed to discover novel regulatory mechanisms in AML pathogenesis.MethodsIntegrative transcriptomic (GSE30029) and proteomic (PXD008378) analyses with machine learning (LASSO, SVM-RFE, random forest) were performed to identify core AML regulators. Functional validation was conducted in AML cell lines (HL-60, NB4), including cell proliferation and apoptosis assays, cytarabine (Ara-C) sensitivity testing, and a subcutaneous xenograft model. Macrophage polarization was evaluated in co-culture systems. Molecular mechanisms were explored via MeRIP, RIP, and mRNA stability assays.ResultsTropomyosin 1 (TPM1) was identified as a potential key factor in AML. TPM1 was downregulated in AML tissues and cells. TPM1 overexpression inhibited AML cell proliferation, promoted apoptosis and oxidative stress, and enhanced sensitivity to Ara-C. Furthermore, TPM1 upregulation attenuated M2 macrophage polarization. Mechanistically, the m6A eraser fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) destabilized TPM1 mRNA in an m6A-dependent manner. Downregulation of TPM1 reversed the effects of FTO depletion on AML cell proliferation, Ara-C sensitivity, and M2 macrophage polarization.ConclusionThis study unveils a novel FTO/m6A/TPM1 axis that coordinately governs AML cell growth, chemosensitivity, and macrophage polarization, highlighting it as a promising therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10565-026-10201-3.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is an aggressive hematological malignancy driven by the uncontrolled proliferation and impaired differentiation of myeloid progenitor cells in the bone marrow (DiNardo et al. 2023). It represents one of the most common acute leukemias in adults, with an incidence that increases significantly with age (Schlenk 2023). The pathogenesis of AML involves the accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations, such as mutations in FLT3, NPM1, TP53, and DNMT3A, which disrupt key cellular processes including proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis, ultimately leading to clonal expansion of immature blasts and bone marrow failure (Peroni et al. 2023; Sallman and Stahl 2025; Misir et al. 2024). Standard induction therapy with cytarabine (Ara-C) and anthracyclines achieves remission in many younger patients; however, relapse is frequent, and outcomes are particularly poor in older adults and those with adverse genetic profiles (Wang et al. 2022; Sema et al. 2024). Although targeted agents and stem cell transplantation have improved survival in select subgroups, intrinsic and acquired drug resistance continues to significantly limit long-term disease control (Stelmach and Trumpp 2023; Ganesan et al. 2022; Shi-Han et al. 2024). A more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms driving AML progression and resistance to chemotherapy may uncover novel vulnerabilities and support the development of more effective, personalized therapeutic strategies.

Macrophages exhibit functional plasticity, with M2 polarization representing an immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic state (Bai et al. 2025). In AML, leukemic cells actively promote M2-like polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in the bone marrow microenvironment, which in turn supports blast survival, angiogenesis, and immune evasion (Liu et al. 2022; Ning et al. 2023). Their presence is often correlated with poor prognosis and chemoresistance (Miari et al. 2021). Consequently, targeting M2 polarization has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy in AML.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation, as the most prevalent internal RNA modification in eukaryotes, is dynamically regulated by writers, erasers, and readers and plays a crucial role in governing mRNA stability (An and Duan 2022). Dysregulation of m6A has been implicated in AML, where aberrant m6A levels contribute to leukemogenesis by altering the expression of oncogenes or tumor suppressors (Fang et al. 2024). Among the key regulators, fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO), functioning primarily as an m6A eraser, has been identified as a critical oncogenic driver in AML (Li et al. 2017). Moreover, FTO is associated with chemotherapy resistance in AML (Zhang et al. 2023).

Emerging integrative multi-omics strategies, encompassing transcriptomics, proteomics, and epigenomics, have advanced the discovery of molecular drivers underlying AML pathogenesis and therapy resistance (Zhang et al. 2024; Zhang and Zhang 2025). Machine learning further enhances this process by enabling the systematic identification of critical regulatory genes and pathways from complex, high-dimensional datasets (Guarnera and Visconte 2024). In the present study, transcriptomic and proteomic data from AML were combined and analyzed using machine learning to pinpoint central regulators of leukemogenesis. Subsequent experimental validation in AML cell lines and xenograft models established the FTO/tropomyosin 1 (TPM1) axis as a pivotal mechanism in disease progression, demonstrating its role in modulating Ara‑C sensitivity in leukemic cells and regulating M2 macrophage polarization. This study reveals a previously unrecognized regulatory axis in AML and underscores its therapeutic relevance.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and differential expression analysis

Gene expression profiles in AML were analyzed using the GSE30029 dataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE30029), consisting of CD34 + mononuclear cells from 46 AML patients and 31 normal CD34 + bone marrow cells. Additionally, proteomic alterations in AML were examined using the PXD008378 dataset (https://proteomecentral.proteomexchange.org/cgi/GetDataset?ID=PXD008378-1), which included CD34 + CD123 + stem cells isolated from five AML patients and five healthy donors. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and proteins (DEPs) were selected based on the same criteria of |Log2Fold Change|> 1 and an adjusted P-value < 0.05. The overlap between DEGs and DEPs was subsequently analyzed through intersection using the online tool Draw Venn diagram (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/cgi-bin/liste/Venn/calculate_venn.htpl).

Machine learning

Three computational approaches: LASSO regression, support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), and random forest (RF), were employed to identify core factors from the overlapping set. Feature selection from overlapping factors was conducted via LASSO logistic regression (fivefold cross-validation) using the “glmnet v4.1.8” package (RRID: SCR_015505), with genes identified based on the minimum lambda (lambda.min) criterion. Through SVM-RFE implemented in the “e1071 v1.7.16” package (RRID: SCR_027751) (halve.above = 100, fivefold cross-validation), genes were ranked and prioritized based on optimal accuracy or minimal error rate for further analysis. The RF algorithm was implemented using the “randomForest v4.7.1.2” package (RRID: SCR_015718) under a fivefold cross-validation framework. Gene selection was based on model configurations achieving the lowest mean error rate across varying numbers of trees. The final consensus core genes were identified through intersection analysis of the three gene sets using Draw Venn diagram.

Human tissue specimens

Peripheral blood samples were collected from an independent clinical cohort comprising 53 AML patients and 48 healthy donors at Sixth People's Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were subsequently isolated from these samples using density gradient centrifugation with Ficoll-Paque Plus (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). For patients, inclusion criteria required a confirmed diagnosis of AML based on WHO diagnostic criteria, aged 18 or above, and having not undergone prior chemotherapy. Exclusion criteria included concurrent hematologic malignancies or active infectious diseases. Healthy volunteers were age- and sex-matched to the patient cohort, possessed no known history of hematologic disorders, and demonstrated normal complete blood count results at the time of enrollment. Ethical approval for the study was granted by Sixth People's Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine’s Ethics Committee, with informed consent secured from each participant.

Cell lines

This study employed four AML cell lines: HL-60 (Cat No. CL-0110, Procell, Wuhan, China; RRID: CVCL_0002), MOLM-13 (Cat No. CL-0681, Procell; RRID: CVCL_2119), NB4 (Cat No. CL-0676, Procell; RRID: CVCL_0005), and KG-1 (Cat No. SNL-180, Sunncell, Wuhan, China; RRID: CVCL_0374). As the control, the normal bone marrow stromal cell line HS-5 (Cat No. STM-CL-5539, Stem Recell, Shanghai, China; RRID: CVCL_3720) was utilized. Human monocytic THP-1 cells (Cat No. CL-0233, Procell; RRID: CVCL_0006) were used for macrophage polarization research. Culture media consisted of IMDM (Procell) for HL-60 and KG-1 cells, RPMI-1640 (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) for MOLM-13, NB4 and THP-1, and DMEM (HyClone) for HS-5 cells. The growth medium contained 10% FBS (Biosera, Boussens, France) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Procell). Culture conditions were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a Thermo Fisher Scientific incubator (Waltham, MA, USA).

Quantification of mRNA expression was performed by the 2−ΔΔCt method, using GAPDH for normalization. Analysis of diagnostic potential of the candidate targets

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of the candidate genes, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted on the GSE30029 dataset and RT-qPCR data from collected PBMCs using the R programming environment. The “pROC” package was employed to calculate the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and to plot the ROC curves. For individual genes, ROC curves were generated by fitting binary logistic regression models, and the optimal cutoff value was determined based on the Youden index. The integrated diagnostic model was constructed using a nomogram developed with the “rms” package, which combined the candidate targets via multivariate logistic regression. The predictive probability derived from the nomogram was then used as a composite score for ROC analysis, allowing comparison of the combined model’s performance against each gene individually.

Immune infiltration analysis

The CIBERSORT algorithm, applying the LM22 reference gene set, was utilized to analyze immune infiltration in the GSE30029 dataset. A total of 1,000 permutation tests were conducted to evaluate result significance, with subsequent multiple comparison correction performed by the Benjamini–Hochberg method (FDR threshold < 0.05). Statistically significant immune cell subsets (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < 0.05) were retained for further analytical evaluation. These subsets were analyzed for distributional differences between AML and control groups, followed by Spearman’s rank correlation analysis, adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, to assess associations with key gene expression.

Plasmids, shRNAs, and lentivirus particles

This study utilized the following constructs (Miaoling Plasmid, Wuhan, China): pLV3-CMV-TPM1(human)-CopGFP-Puro (Vector-TPM1), its corresponding control Vector, pLV3-U6-TPM1(human)-shRNA-CopGFP-Puro (shTPM1), pLV3-U6-FTO(human)-shRNA-CopGFP-Puro (shFTO), and a scrambled shRNA control (shNC). The TPM1‑overexpressing lentivirus particles and its control counterpart (Vector) were constructed through VectorBuilder (Guangzhou, China).

Transfection and transduction

Using RFect Plasmid Transfection Reagent (BaiDai Biotech, Changzhou, China), HL-60 and NB4 cells underwent transient transfection with specified plasmids and shRNAs (Vector, Vector-TPM1, shNC, shFTO, shFTO + shNC, and shFTO + shTPM1), and subsequent evaluations were performed 24–72 h later. TPM1‑overexpressing lentiviral particles were used to transduce HL-60 cells, generating a stable cell line for xenograft experiments. A two-week puromycin selection (2 µg/mL, Solarbio, Beijing, China) was applied post-transduction to establish stable cell populations.

Western blot

Protein extraction from PBMCs, cultured cells, and xenograft tissues was performed with ice-cold RIPA buffer (Beyotime) with added protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, Milano, Italy). Proteins were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels loaded with 15–25 μg of protein per lane and subsequently transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, Shanghai, China) using electroblotting. Primary antibodies applied in immunoblotting included: anti-TPM1 (Cat No. 28477–1-AP, 1:6000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China; RRID: AB_2881151), anti-BAX (Cat No. ab32503, 1:5000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; RRID: AB_725631), anti-BCL2 (Cat No. ab238041, 1:1000, Abcam), anti-ABCB1 (Cat No. ab170904, 1:3000, Abcam; RRID: AB_2687930), anti-ABCC1 (Cat No. 67228–1-Ig, 1:10,000, Proteintech; RRID: AB_2882516), anti-FTO (Cat No. 27226–1-AP, 1:2000, Proteintech; RRID: AB_2880809), and anti-GAPDH (Cat No. 60004–1-Ig, 1:50,000, Proteintech; RRID: AB_2107436). Enhanced chemiluminescence detection was performed on a LAS-4000 mini image analyzer (Fuji Photo Film, Minamiashigara, Japan), followed by densitometric quantification of the resulting bands using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Cell proliferation and apoptosis assays

Proliferation of transfected suspension cells (HL-60 and NB4) was evaluated using the BeyoClick™ EdU-AF488 system (Beyotime). Cells were EdU-labeled directly in culture medium, followed by centrifugation, washing, and fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde. After fixation, cells were stained with AF488 fluorescent dye and counterstained with DAPI (Solarbio) to visualize nuclei. Apoptosis of transfected cells was evaluated using a FITC-conjugated Annexin V/PI Detection Kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) as suggested by the supplier. Both EdU-positive cells and apoptotic rates were quantified on an Epics XL-MCL flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA).

Detection of GSH and ROS levels

In transfected HL-60 and NB4 cells, GSH levels were quantified using the GSH Assay Kit (Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), while ROS were detected with the DCFH-DA-based ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime). GSH quantification was performed via optical density measurement using a Tecan Infinite M200 system (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland), while ROS analysis was conducted using the Epics XL-MCL flow cytometer.

Ara-C sensitivity analysis

HL-60 and NB4 cells, following transfection, were exposed for 48 h to a range of Ara-C concentrations (0, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1600 nM) obtained from Selleck (Shanghai, China). Subsequently, CCK-8 reagent (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) was introduced into the culture medium, followed by a 2-h incubation period. The collected supernatant was plated in a 96-well plate, and the optical density was read at 450 nm.

Animal studies and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The in vivo animal studies were performed in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Sixth People's Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Eighteen female BALB/c nude mice (six-week-old, Sibeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were randomized into three groups of equal size (n = 6 per group), designated as Vector + PBS, TPM1 + PBS, and TPM1 + Ara-C. A Matrigel suspension of HL-60 cells (2 × 107 cells/mouse), transduced with Vector-TPM1 or control lentivirus, was subcutaneously implanted into the right flanks of nude mice to generate xenografts. Using calibrated calipers, tumor volume was assessed at seven-day intervals according to the calculation (length × width2)/2. Once tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, a daily regimen of either Ara-C (75 mg/kg) or PBS control was administered via intraperitoneal injection. Upon completion of the 28-day experiment, mice were euthanized and xenografts were harvested.

For immunohistochemical analysis, harvested xenografts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and then processed through dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at 4 µm. Consecutive tissue sections underwent IHC assay by incubation with primary antibodies including anti-TPM1 (Cat No. 28477–1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech; RRID: AB_2881151) and anti-Ki67 (Cat No. 27309–1-AP, 1:3000, Proteintech; RRID: AB_2756525). Color development for antigen detection was performed with a DAB substrate Kit (Beyotime). For nuclear contrast, hematoxylin counterstaining was applied to the DAB-stained sections, after which bright-field microscopy was used for imaging and evaluation.

Co-culture experiments

A 24-h treatment with 100 ng/mL PMA (Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to induce THP1 cell differentiation into macrophages (M0). In co-culture experiments, transfected HL-60 and NB4 cells were mixed with THP1-differentiated M0 macrophages at a ratio of three leukemic cells to one macrophage, with a density of 1 × 105 total cells/well in a 6-well culture plate. Macrophages were isolated following a 48-h co-culture period for downstream analytical procedures.

Analysis of the percentage of the CD206 positive cells

The percentage of CD206-expressing macrophages was determined by staining co-cultured macrophages with an anti-CD206 PC7 antibody (Cat No. 25–2069-42, Thermo Fisher Scientific; RRID: AB_2573426). Flow cytometric analysis using an Epics XL-MCL flow cytometer was employed to quantify the proportion of CD206 M2-like macrophages.

Prediction of the m6A sites on TPM1 mRNA and the binding m6A regulators

The putative m6A modification sites on TPM1 mRNA were predicted through bioinformatic analysis employing the online SRAMP database (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp). The identification of potential m6A-binding proteins for TPM1 mRNA was carried out using the RBPsuite platform (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/RBPsuite/reference_new.html).

MeRIP and RIP experiments

RIP experiments utilized an anti-FTO antibody (Proteintech, Cat No. 27226–1-AP; RRID: AB_2880809) along with a commercially available m6A MeRIP Assay Kit (Genelily, Shanghai, China) that included both anti-IgG and anti-m6A antibodies. RNA preparation and fragmentation were performed on untransfected, shNC-transfected, and shFTO-transfected HL-60 and NB4 cells. The fragmented RNA was incubated with PGM bead complexes pre-bound with anti-m6A, anti-IgG, or anti-FTO antibody. After harvesting the precipitates, RNA isolation was performed to measure TPM1 mRNA enrichment.

Analysis of TPM1 mRNA stability

Treatment with actinomycin D (Act D, 5 µg/mL, Selleck) was administered to shNC- and shFTO-transfected HL-60 and NB4 cells over time intervals of 0, 2, 4, and 8 h. Following treatment, the remaining levels of TPM1 mRNA were quantified by RT-qPCR.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The regulation of TPM1 m6A methylation by FTO was evaluated through the creation of TPM1 reporter constructs, wherein the pmirGlO vector (Promega, Vienna, Austria) was ligated with a TPM1 mRNA fragment spanning the predicted m6A modification site with the predicted highest score (GGACA, TPM1-WT) or its corresponding mutation (GGGCA, TPM1-MUT). Co-introduction of each reporter vector with shFTO or shNC was performed in both HL-60 and NB4 cell lines. At 48 h following transfection, the Dual-Luciferase Assay System (Promega) was employed to quantify luciferase activity.

Statistical analysis

All graphical data showed mean values with error bars representing the standard deviation. ANOVA (one- or two-way) was utilized for comparisons across multiple groups, and unpaired Student’s t-tests were employed for two-group analyses, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

To validate these findings in an independent clinical cohort, PBMCs were collected from peripheral blood samples of 53 AML patients and 48 healthy donors and subjected to expression analysis. The three‑gene nomogram again showed strong diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.964) (Fig. 3D). RT‑qPCR data confirmed the downregulation of TPM1 and NUDT11 and the upregulation of ACTA2 in AML PBMCs (Fig. 3E). Immune infiltration analysis in AML

Next, the immune cell infiltration landscape in AML was characterized by analyzing the GSE30029 dataset with the CIBERSORT package. Distinct differences in immune cell composition were observed between AML and control samples (Fig. 4A). Myeloid-derived immune cells, chiefly M2 macrophages, rose markedly in relative proportion, becoming the predominant immune component across most AML samples. In striking contrast to the relatively uniform immune profiles seen in controls, AML samples showed considerable interindividual heterogeneity. The balance between myeloid and lymphoid populations varied widely from patient to patient, highlighting the diverse immune architectures underlying the disease (Fig. 4A). Several immune cell subsets, such as M1 macrophages, M2 macrophages, and monocytes, were found to be significantly altered in AML samples compared with the control group (Fig. 4B). Correlation analysis further revealed that all three candidate targets were most significantly associated with M2 macrophages (Fig. 4C). Discussion

TPM1 is a key cytoskeletal protein that stabilizes actin filaments and maintains cellular architecture, and it has emerged as a context-dependent tumor suppressor across diverse malignancies. Its downregulation is frequently observed in endometrial, lung, colorectal, and gastric cancers, where it is associated with increased metastasis, epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT), and poor clinical outcomes (Liu et al. 2025a; Xu et al. 2025; Mlakar et al. 2009; Hu et al. 2020). Notably, TPM1’s role in chemotherapy response appears highly isoform- and cancer type-specific: while full-length TPM1 enhances sensitivity to docetaxel in castration-resistant prostate cancer depending on the AZGP1P2/UBA1/RBM15-m6A axis (Wang et al. 2023), specific low-molecular-weight isoforms (Tpm1.8/9) paradoxically confer resistance to taxanes and platinum agents in ovarian cancer by promoting EMT and stemness (Xu et al. 2024). This duality underscores the complexity of TPM1 function in tumor biology and therapy response. In the context of AML, however, the functional relevance of TPM1 remains incompletely defined. Our finding that TPM1 is downregulated in AML patient samples and cell lines, and that its restoration suppresses proliferation, elevates oxidative stress, and potentiates Ara-C sensitivity, positions TPM1 as a novel tumor-suppressive factor in AML. Importantly, this aligns with emerging evidence that cytoskeletal regulators can modulate redox homeostasis and drug response in leukemia (Park et al. 2025; Soh et al. 2017; Williams et al. 2015), suggesting that TPM1 may serve not only as a biomarker but also as a node for therapeutic intervention aimed at overcoming chemoresistance in AML. Despite the significant downregulation of TPM1 in AML, survival analysis using publicly available cohorts in GSCA database did not reveal a statistically significant association between TPM1 expression and patient outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 2). This finding suggests that TPM1 may primarily influence fundamental biological processes such as cytoskeletal organization and cell proliferation, which contribute to leukemogenesis but exert limited independent effects on disease prognosis. Thus, TPM1 is more relevant as a functional molecule involved in AML pathogenesis rather than as a standalone prognostic indicator.

Emerging evidence suggests that TPM1 plays a context-dependent role in cancer immunity, with prior studies reporting its positive association with an M2-like, pro-tumorigenic macrophage phenotype in solid tumors, such as bladder cancer, where it contributes to immune suppression and disease progression (Liu et al. 2023). These findings align with the broader notion that cytoskeletal regulators like myosin and ROCK1 can shape immune cell behavior within the tumor microenvironment (Tang et al. 2025; Rao et al. 2022). However, our data in AML reveal a strikingly opposite effect: TPM1 upregulation in AML cells significantly suppresses M2 polarization of co-cultured THP-1-derived macrophages. This discrepancy underscores the plasticity of TPM1’s immunomodulatory functions across cancer types. Notably, while solid tumors often exploit stromal and immune interactions to foster an M2-skewed milieu, the leukemic niche may uniquely rewire these signals-potentially through altered cytokine crosstalk or direct cell-contact mechanisms. In summary, our data highlight that TPM1’s function in macrophage polarization is not uniform but may vary critically depending on the disease microenvironment, revealing a potential unique immunomodulatory axis in AML worthy of further exploration.

FTO, an m6A demethylase, is a well-established oncogenic driver in diverse malignancies, such as colorectal, breast, and gastric cancers (Qiao et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025; He et al. 2025). In AML, FTO is overexpressed in specific subtypes (e.g., MLL-rearranged, FLT3-ITD) and promotes leukemogenesis by erasing m6A marks on key transcripts (e.g., ASB2, RARA), thereby blocking differentiation and enhancing proliferation (Li et al. 2017). FTO has also been implicated in chemoresistance in AML; its upregulation reduces global m6A levels and erases m6A marks on targets (e.g., FOXO3, IGFBP2) to stabilize oncogenic transcripts, leading to decreased sensitivity to Ara-C and other agents (Zhang et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2024). These findings have spurred therapeutic interest, with small-molecule FTO inhibitors demonstrating efficacy in restoring differentiation and chemosensitivity in preclinical AML models (Huang et al. 2019). Beyond cell-intrinsic effects, emerging literature reveals a context-dependent role for FTO in regulating M2 macrophage polarization within the tumor microenvironment, primarily through m6A-dependent mRNA stability control of key secreted factors. In thyroid cancer, FTO suppresses macrophage M2 polarization by destabilizing RASGRF1 mRNA (Li et al. 2025). Conversely, in triple-negative breast cancer, oncogenic metabolite D-2HG inhibits FTO, leading to increased m6A on and enhanced translation of ANGPTL4, which paracrinely promotes M2 polarization (Zhang et al. 2025). Similarly, in lung adenocarcinoma, FTO stabilizes QPCT mRNA, thereby enhancing the secretion of CCL2 to recruit and polarize macrophages toward an M2 phenotype (Liu et al. 2025b). This duality underscores that FTO’s immunomodulatory impact hinges on the specific downstream transcriptome it regulates in a given cellular milieu. Our findings identified that FTO destabilizes TPM1 mRNA in an m6A-dependent manner. FTO depletion suppresses AML growth, enhances Ara-C sensitivity, and inhibits M2 polarization, all of which are reversed upon TPM1 knockdown. In summary, our work elucidates a novel FTO/m6A/TPM1 regulatory axis central to AML pathophysiology. This axis coordinately regulates leukemic cell growth, chemosensitivity, and immunomodulation within the microenvironment. Targeting this pathway, particularly FTO's catalytic activity, presents a promising therapeutic strategy to simultaneously impair AML cell fitness and counteract immunosuppression.

Despite the compelling data, our study has several inherent limitations. First, although our cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) model demonstrated the impact of TPM1 on tumor growth, it did not fully recapitulate the systemic nature of AML, particularly the bone marrow homing and peripheral blood dissemination of leukemia cells. Therefore, establishing an intravenous transplantation model will be essential in future investigations to validate the therapeutic potential of TPM1 in vivo, including its effects on survival and leukemic cell engraftment in the bone marrow. Secondly, the precise molecular mechanisms by which TPM1, a cytoskeletal protein, regulates intracellular oxidative stress and communicates with macrophages to alter their polarization state remain to be fully elucidated. A primary direction is to dissect the exact signaling pathways downstream of TPM1 in future work. Third, our findings are primarily based on a subset of AML cell lines and patient samples; the clinical prevalence and prognostic significance of the FTO-high/TPM1-low signature across diverse AML genetic subtypes require validation in larger, well-annotated cohorts. Fourthly, while we identified m6A-dependent mRNA stability as a key regulatory mechanism, we cannot exclude potential additional layers of regulation, such as effects on TPM1 translation efficiency. Lastly, one limitation of the initial screening strategy is that the mass spectrometry-based proteomic dataset (PXD008378) inherently captures a relatively limited subset of the proteome, constrained by factors such as dynamic range and ionization efficiency. Consequently, the multi-omics intersection converged on a restricted number of overlapping candidates. Future studies employing deeper proteomic coverage, such as fractionation-coupled LC–MS/MS or targeted proteomic approaches, could help identify additional complementary regulators that function alongside TPM1 in AML pathogenesis.

In conclusion, our work delineates a novel FTO/m6A/TPM1 regulatory axis that concurrently governs AML cell intrinsic properties (growth, oxidative stress, chemosensitivity) and extrinsic immunomodulation (macrophage polarization) (Fig. 15).
