Clinical Implications of the Langer’s Axillary Arch Muscle in Breast Cancer Patients
Authors: Eunhye Kang, Ji-Jung Jung, Hong-Kyu Kim, Han-Byoel Lee, Wonshik Han, Jung Min Chang, Hyeong-Gon Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeLanger's axillary arch (LAA) is a common muscular variant that may affect the assessment of axillary lymph nodes in breast cancer. We investigated whether the presence of the LAA had a meaningful clinical impact in a large cohort of Korean patients with breast cancer.MethodsThe medical records and imaging of 2,904 patients (2,953 cases) who underwent axillary surgery for breast cancer were reviewed. The presence of LAA was determined using chest computed tomography scans. The number of retrieved lymph nodes, the accuracy of axillary ultrasonography, and survival outcomes were compared between the LAA and non-LAA groups.ResultsThe LAA muscle was identified in 301 patients (10.2%). Preoperative ultrasound evaluation showed a lower specificity in the LAA group (77.9% vs. 83.6%, p = 0.031). Additionally, invasive disease-free survival (iDFS) was significantly worse in the ‘LAA-positive patients’ after adjusting for stage, histologic grade, and treatment (adjusted hazard ratio, 2.00; 95% confidence interval, 1.00–4.01; p = 0.048), with regional axillary recurrence being notably more frequent in patients with the LAA muscle (p = 0.005).ConclusionThe presence of the LAA muscle was associated with reduced specificity on preoperative ultrasound. Patients with LAA muscle-associated tumors had worse iDFS, with regional axillary recurrence occurring more frequently. Recognition of the LAA muscle during axillary staging and surgery may improve nodal assessment accuracy and outcomes, especially in the era of axillary surgery de-escalation.Keywords: Axilla, Anatomic Variation, Breast Neoplasms, Lymph Node Excision

INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women in South Korea, with 29,729 new cases diagnosed in 2019, making it the most common cancer among Korean women [1]. Significant advancements have been made in breast cancer management, particularly in axillary staging. Following the ACOSOG Z0011 trial, sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) became the standard surgical procedure for axillary staging of early breast cancer [2]. In Korea, 73.2% of patients undergo SLNB alone [1]. The widespread adoption of SLNB underscores the critical importance of accurate identification and removal of sentinel nodes.

Langer's axillary arch (LAA), also known as the axillary arch muscle, is a relatively common muscular variant of the axillary region [3,4]. Generally, the LAA originates from the latissimus dorsi (LD) muscle, crosses the axilla, and inserts into the pectoralis major muscle [5]. The prevalence of LAA ranges from 0.8 to 26.1% [6]. The presence of the LAA may overlay the external axillary region, which contains numerous axillary lymph nodes, leading to potentially inadequate surgical exposure of the axillary space or inaccurate imaging assessment of the axillary nodal status [5,7,8,9].

Accordingly, studies have suggested that patients with LAA may be at risk of insufficient preoperative evaluation of axillary lymph nodes and incomplete removal of axillary lymph nodes during surgery. This issue is particularly important considering the recent efforts to minimize the extent of axillary surgical staging in patients with a negative axilla [10,11,12]. The present study aimed to address this issue by investigating the accuracy of imaging and surgical assessment of the axilla and its potential impact on survival in relation to the presence of the LAA muscle in a large cohort of Korean patients with breast cancer.

METHODS

Patients

This retrospective study was conducted by reviewing the medical records and imaging of patients with newly diagnosed breast cancer who underwent axillary surgery at Seoul National University Hospital between January 2017 and October 2022.

A total of 2,904 patients met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 49 patients underwent bilateral axillary surgery simultaneously, resulting in 2,953 axillary surgery cases being analyzed. The group in which the LAA muscle was present was defined as the ‘LAA group,’ while the group in which the LAA muscle was absent was defined as the ‘non-LAA group.’

For patient-based analyses, including survival analysis, the patients were categorized into two groups: ‘LAA-positive patients’ and ‘LAA-negative patients.’ Patients were classified as LAA-positive if the LAA muscle was present in at least one surgically treated axilla. This included patients who underwent unilateral axillary surgery with the LAA muscle present, or bilateral axillary surgery with the LAA muscle present in either one or both axillae. Patients were classified as LAA-negative if the LAA muscle was absent in all the surgically treated axillae. This classification ensures that survival analysis is conducted on a per-patient basis, reflecting the overall impact of LAA muscle presence in the treated axilla on patient outcomes.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. 2401-117-1505). The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: i) patients with primary breast cancer, ii) patients who had preoperative chest computed tomography (CT) images, and iii) in cases of bilateral surgery, the left and right sides were considered separate cases. The exclusion criteria were as follows: i) patients who did not undergo axillary surgery, ii) patients with a history of axillary surgery, iii) patients with a history of breast cancer, and iv) patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy or neoadjuvant endocrine therapy. Axillary surgery included SLNB and axillary lymph node dissection (ALND).

Imaging assessment of axilla by ultrasonography

Preoperative ultrasound examinations for axillary assessment were performed within two days prior to surgery in 98% of the cases. Sonographic axillary metastasis was defined as any of the following: cortical thickness > 3.5 mm in the absence of imaging or clinical features suggesting reactive or vaccination-related lymphadenopathy, cortical thickness ≤ 3.5 mm with additional sonographic features suspicious for metastasis such as loss of fatty hilum or eccentric cortical thickening, or pathologic confirmation of nodal metastasis on fine needle aspiration cytology or core needle biopsy. Cortical thickness was determined as the largest value measured among all visualized lymph nodes. Patients not meeting any of these criteria were classified as clinically node negative [13].

Identification of LAA muscle using CT images

The presence of the LAA muscle was determined based on the most recent chest CT (contrast or non-contrast) scan prior to surgery (Figure 1). According to a meta-analysis of LAA anatomy, 91.9% of LAA muscles originate from the LD muscle or LD tendons, whereas in less than 10% of cases, they originate from the long head of the triceps brachii or the serratus anterior muscle. Additionally, the pectoralis major muscle is the most common insertion site, followed by the coracoid process, biceps brachii head, and pectoralis minor muscle [6]. Statistics

This study aimed to compare the number of retrieved lymph nodes between groups with and without the LAA muscle and to investigate whether the presence of the LAA muscle influences the predictive utility of axillary ultrasound.

The utility of axillary ultrasonography was evaluated in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy in both groups. Accuracy was defined as the proportion of true-positive and true-negative results in the total population. Differences in sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy between groups were compared using Pearson's χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables, presented as median and interquartile range, were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables, presented as frequencies and percentages, were compared using Pearson's χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test.

Additionally, we analyzed invasive disease-free survival (iDFS) as a secondary endpoint. iDFS was defined as the time from surgery to the first breast cancer recurrence, specifically locoregional recurrence, contralateral breast cancer, or distant metastasis. Patients who did not experience any event were censored on the date of their last follow-up visit. Local recurrence was defined as a tumor in the ipsilateral breast or chest wall, whereas regional recurrence was defined as a tumor in the ipsilateral axillary, internal mammary, subclavicular, or supraclavicular nodes. iDFS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Patients

A total of 2,953 patients met the inclusion criteria during the study period. The LAA muscle was identified on the CT images of 301 patients (10.2%). The prevalence of LAA was comparable between the left and right axillae. There were no significant differences in body mass index, axillary surgery method, sonographic node staging, cortical thickness, pathological T stage, or pathological nodal metastasis between the LAA and non-LAA groups (Table 1).

Survival analysis

The median follow-up duration was 40.4 months (range, 1–91 months). We observed a small but statistically significant difference in the iDFS between the two groups. iDFS was worse in LAA-positive patients compared with LAA-negative patients, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.12 (95% CI, 1.06–4.22; log-rank p = 0.029). The 5-year iDFS rate was 95.9% (95% CI, 93.3–98.6) in LAA-positive patients and 97.9% (95% CI, 97.3–98.6) in LAA-negative patients (Figure 3). Within five years, regional recurrence was significantly more frequent in the LAA-positive group. Regional recurrence occurred in 11 of 2,608 (0.42%) LAA-negative patients and in 5 of 296 (1.69%) LAA-positive patients (p = 0.005, Supplementary Table 1).

In the multivariate analysis that included T stage, N stage, histologic grade, chemotherapy, and endocrine therapy, the presence of the LAA muscle remained an independent predictor of worse iDFS. Patients with the LAA muscle had a two-fold higher risk of recurrence or death compared with those without the LAA muscle (HR, 2.00; 95% CI, 1.00–4.01; p = 0.048), after adjustment for these prognostic factors.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the effect of the LAA muscle on preoperative ultrasound assessment, axillary surgery, and survival outcomes in patients with breast cancer. Our findings revealed significant associations between the presence of the LAA and several key clinical factors, including ultrasound specificity and iDFS. This study is the largest to date investigating the LAA muscle, and although various case studies suggest that the presence of the LAA muscle may impact axillary surgery and lead to missed lymph node dissection, no previous studies have confirmed this by correlating surgical and imaging results. Our study is significant because it addresses this gap in the literature.

Our results demonstrated that patients with LAA muscles undergoing SLNB had a similar median number of dissected lymph nodes (5 nodes in both groups), although the distribution showed a marginal trend toward fewer nodes in the LAA group (p = 0.070). This finding aligns with those of previous studies that reported difficulties in detecting and removing lymph nodes in the presence of the LAA [9,14]. In some cases, sentinel lymph nodes were found in unusual locations or hidden beneath the LAA muscle and were only discoverable after dissecting the boundaries of the muscle. A prospective study found that 90.9% of patients with LAA had at least two lymph nodes lateral to the arch. Furthermore, 13.04% of patients who underwent SLN tracing had SLNs located lateral to the LAA [15]. These findings suggest that without proper awareness and management of the LAA during surgery, there is a risk of incomplete lymph node removal and potentially missed metastases. Additionally, we observed lower specificity in ultrasound evaluation in the LAA group. This finding suggests that the presence of additional anatomical structures in the axillary region may interfere with accurate lymph node assessment during ultrasound examination.

Interestingly, our data demonstrated a significant difference in iDFS between patients with and without the LAA. Patients with LAA showed worse iDFS, and this difference was primarily driven by higher rates of regional axillary recurrence in the LAA group. The presence of the LAA may lead to incomplete lymph node removal during surgery owing to its anatomical complexity, potentially resulting in residual metastatic cancer cells in the axilla. This hypothesis is supported by a previous prospective study showing that approximately 17% of lymph nodes found lateral to the axillary arch are metastatic nodes [15]. The higher rate of regional axillary recurrence observed in the LAA group is consistent with this surgical sampling failure mechanism. Moreover, incomplete lymph node removal may lead to improper nodal staging and result in suboptimal treatment decisions.

This finding underscores the potential clinical significance of the nodes in this anatomical location and the importance of a thorough examination of this area during axillary surgery. Another possible explanation is that the presence of the LAA can influence lymphatic drainage patterns in patients with breast cancer. Lymphatic drainage patterns, including the anatomic locations of the tumor-draining lymph nodes, are well-known prognostic markers in lung cancer and gastric cancer [16]. Sang et al. [15] suggested that the presence of LAA can be associated with a unique lymphatic drainage pattern that does not correlate with conventional axillary lymphatic drainage. More detailed studies that collect information on lymphatic drainage patterns are required.

Although iDFS was significantly worse in patients with the LAA muscle, the absolute risk of recurrence remained low in our cohort. Because we excluded patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy to minimize confounding effects on axillary nodal status, the overall 5-year recurrence rate was below 5%. Therefore, the observed association between the LAA muscle and survival should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive evidence for clinical decision-making. Larger cohorts, including more diverse treatment populations, and prospective validation studies are required to confirm these findings.

Our study had several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single institution, which limited our ability to gather data from diverse groups of surgeons. This may affect the generalizability of our findings to different surgical practices and techniques. Second, to minimize confounding factors in axillary surgery, we excluded patients who had received neoadjuvant treatment or had undergone previous axillary surgeries. It is well established that sonographic predictive accuracy is relatively low in patients who have undergone neoadjuvant treatment [17]. Moreover, anatomical variations like the LAA muscle may have an even greater impact on determining the precise surgical field and achieving appropriate node biopsy and dissection in these complex cases. Given these considerations, future studies should investigate the effect of the LAA muscle on more diverse patient populations. In our study, we used chest CT to confirm the presence of the LAA muscle preoperatively; however, other studies used MRI and ultrasound [18,19,20]. Although it is likely that different imaging modalities can correctly identify the presence of the LAA, potential differences in sensitivity should be addressed in future studies.

Our study demonstrated that the presence of the LAA was significantly associated with several important clinical outcomes in patients with breast cancer. Recognizing and appropriately managing the presence of the LAA represents an important step toward more personalized and effective breast cancer treatment, potentially improving both the accuracy of staging and long-term patient outcomes.
