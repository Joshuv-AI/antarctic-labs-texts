Efficacy of continuous glucose monitoring in comparison to self-blood glucose monitoring in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus under insulin treatment: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jingjing Zhu, Lu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes Mellitus (DM) affects approximately 10.5% of the global population, necessitating effective glucose monitoring to mitigate complications. Self-monitored blood glucose (SMBG) lacks dynamic glycemic profiling, while continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) provides real-time/intermittent data. This review compares CGM and SMBG in insulin-treated type 2 diabetes (T2DM) patients.MethodA systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) from PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus was conducted until May 2025 and included RCTs compared CGM with SMBG in terms of glycemic indices in adults with T2DM on insulin therapy (± oral agents), excluding studies < 6 weeks or non-insulin treatments. Meta-analysis using a random-effect model was performed.ResultsThirteen RCTs (n = 1550) were included. CGM significantly reduced HbA1c (mean difference [MD] = − 2.78 mmol/mol, 95% CI: −4.68 to − 0.88) and TBR (MD = − 1.30%, 95% CI: −1.94 to − 0.65) versus SMBG. TAR reduction (MD = − 4.12%, 95% CI: −9.11 to 0.88) and TIR increase (MD = 4.04%, 95% CI: −0.09 to 8.17) were non-significant. Substantial heterogeneity in HbA1c (I²=89.66%) persisted despite subgroup analyses based on CGM type.DiscussionCGM improves long-term glucose control (HbA1c) and hypoglycemia risk (TBR) in insulin-treated T2DM, supporting its clinical utility. Non-significant TIR/TAR trends may reflect limited statistical power. Future studies are warranted to delineate the outcome disparities between CGM and SMBG.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02167-4.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Glycemic indices, CGM, SMBG

Introduction

Diabetes Mellitus (DM) is one of the major chronic health problems, with an approximate prevalence of 10.5% of the world population, equivalent to 537 million individuals, according to the international diabetes federation (IDF) [1]. DM is linked to the development of irreversible multi-organ complications within the cardiovascular, nervous, renal, and ophthalmologic systems [2]. These complications, closely related to uncontrolled blood glucose, also in the long term, impose a healthcare burden of $966 billion [3]. The preventive measurements, as simple as consistent blood glucose monitoring, would protect against the development of DM complications and thereby potentially reduce the healthcare burden.

The self–blood glucose monitoring (SMBG), performed by application of lancets and test strips, was one of the first methods for blood glucose control, helping many patients reach and maintain a normal range of blood glucose and hemoglobin A1C (HbA1C) [4]. However, SMBG does not provide information on the daily profile of blood glucose fluctuations and current direction, and consistent use is difficult within certain populations, such as patients with low health literacy or socioeconomic status, young children and older adults, and those with physical and mental disabilities [5–7]. Also, the SMBG method is not equipped with an alarm that notifies patients of hypoglycemic episodes. As such, another method emerged that relies on continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) technology, comprised of two types of real-time CGM (rtCGM) and intermittently scanned CGM (isCGM) (also known as flash glucose monitoring) [8]. In both methods, a disposable sensor is inserted subcutaneously and attached to a transmitter. The sensor measures the glucose level in the interstitial fluid (instead of capillary blood in SMBG) at a specific interval, which is then forwarded to the transmitter. Finally, a receiver or smartphone displays the results continuously (in rtCGM) or following the scanning of the sensor (in isCGM) [9]. The application of CGM has been much more thoroughly investigated in people with type 1 diabetes mellitus, consistently demonstrating notable improvements in glycemic control [10].

Currently, our knowledge of the comparison between the traditional SMBG method and CGM in insulin-treated patients is limited to a few studies. CGM provides a dynamic profile of glucose fluctuations, enabling the evaluation of critical parameters such as HbA1C, time in range (TIR), time below range (TBR), and time above range (TAR), metrics that are not captured by intermittent SMBG measurements [11]. Recent studies suggest that CGM systems provide non-negligible advantages over SMBG, particularly in improving glycemic indices, reducing the risks of hypo- or hyper-glycemia, and potentially enabling proactive insulin adjustments [12–14]. However, difficulties in access in resource-limited settings and disparities in adherence underscore the need for a personalized approach in clinical practice.

In this study, we conduct a systematic review of clinical trials comparing the effects of SMBG and CGM on glycemic parameters in patients with type 2 DM receiving insulin therapy, either as monotherapy or in combination with oral antidiabetic agents. The findings of this review will inform clinical decision-making by elucidating the optimal glucose monitoring strategy, thereby potentially mitigating the risk of diabetes-related complications in this patient population.

Methods

Data sources and search strategy

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis by searching the following databases from inception until May 2025: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Web of Science, and Scopus. The search strategy included the following keywords and MeSH terms: “Type 2 diabetes”, “Diabetes Mellitus, Type 2,” “T2DM,” “non-insulin-dependent diabetes”, “Continuous glucose monitoring”, “CGM,” “continuous glucose monitoring,” “real time continuous glucose monitor”, “rtCGM”, “intermittently scanned continuous glucose monitoring”, “isCGM,” “flash glucose monitoring”, “glycemic control”, “HbA1c,” “glycated hemoglobin,” “time in range,” “TIR,” “time below range,” “TBR,” “time above range,” “TAR”. These terms were adapted for each database to ensure comprehensive coverage of the literature. We restricted our search to articles published in English and focused on clinical trials. Additionally, we manually reviewed the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews to identify any additional eligible articles.

Study selection

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that investigated the use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), either real-time CGM (rtCGM) or intermittently scanned CGM (isCGM), compared to self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) in adults with type 2 diabetes who were using insulin as part of their treatment. Studies with an intervention duration of less than 6 weeks, those investigating patients with type 1 diabetes, or studies involving patients treated with only glucose-lowering therapies were excluded. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility using the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1). Full-text articles were assessed for inclusion, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. A completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in the supplementary material.

PRISMA diagram

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standardized form, including study characteristics (author, year, design, follow-up duration), participant demographics (age, sex, diabetes duration), type of treatment (insulin, oral glucose-lowering therapy), intervention details (type of CGM, comparator), and outcomes. The primary outcome was the change in HbA1c, and secondary outcomes included time in range (TIR), time below range (TBR), time above range (TAR), and glycemic variability.

Data synthesis and quality assessment

A meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model to account for potential heterogeneity across studies. Mean differences (MD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for continuous outcomes. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with values of 0–40%, 30–60%, 50–90%, and 75–100% indicating low, moderate, substantial, and considerable heterogeneity, respectively. The quality of included studies was assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool version 2 (RoB-2) to ensure adherence to systematic review reporting standards. This tool consists of five different domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported results. Funnel plots assessed the publication bias, confirmed by Egger’s test.

Results

Search results

The initial search in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science yielded 7,489 articles (Fig. 1). After removal of duplicates, 4599 articles were screened for title and abstract, of which 4547 articles were excluded. The most common reason for exclusion was incorrect study design (n = 2384). The full texts of 52 studies were assessed, and finally, 13 randomized clinical trials met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis.

Characteristics of included trials

The 13 RCTs were published between 2008 and 2025 and included a total of 1550 participants (Table 1). Among the study population, the glucometry method was SMBG in 706 and CGM in 844 patients. The sample size ranged from 25 to 224 participants and the duration of follow-up ranged from 10 to 52 weeks. Eight trials [15–21] compared rtCGM to SMBG, and five trials [14, 22–25] compared isCGM to SMBG. No studies compared rtCGM to isCGM. In one out of 13 studies, the patients were treated with intensive insulin therapy [14], while in others, a combination of insulin and oral therapy was administered. The intervention duration ranged from 10 to 52 weeks. The mean age of study participants in the control group ranged from 52.3 to 65.9 years, and in the intervention group, it ranged from 53.5 to 67.6 years. The baseline HbA1c ranged from a mean of 62.3 mmol/mol (7.85%) to 77.9 mmol/mol (9.27%). As the primary outcome, 8 trials had HbA1c, 4 trials had TIR, and another trial had treatment satisfaction.

Outcomes

The outcomes of meta-analysis are illustrated in Figs. 2, 3 and 4. Overall, the mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence interval in HbA1C between CGM and SMBG groups was − 2.78 mmol/mol [-4.68, -0.88] (I2 = 89.66%). The mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence interval in TAR between CGM and SMBG groups was − 4.12% [-9.11, 0.88] (I2 = 51.96%). The mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence interval in TBR between CGM and SMBG groups was − 1.30% [-1.94, -0.65] (I2 = 0.00%). The mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence interval in TIR between CGM and SMBG groups was 4.04% [-0.09, 8.17] (I2 = 28.64%) (Fig. 2).

To find other sources of heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analyses for each parameter based on the CGM method (Fig. 3).

For HbA1C, a subgroup analysis was conducted based on the CGM method. The MD for rtCGM subgroup was significant (-2.87mmol/mol [95% CI: -4.35, -1.39]), while for the isCGM was not significant (-1.56 mmol/mol [95%CI: -5.88, 2.75]).

Subgroup analysis of TAR by the CGM method demonstrated showed that the MD for rtCGM subgroup was significant (-8.10% [95% CI: -14.85, -1.35]), while for the isCGM was not significant (0.55% [95% CI: -4.10, 5.20]).

Subgroup analysis of TBR based on the CGM method demonstrated that MD for rtCGM subgroup was significant (-1.26% [95% CI: -1.96, -0.57]), while for the isCGM was not significant (-1.71% [95% CI: -4.83, 1.41]).

For TIR, the subgroup analysis based on the CGM method revealed that the MD for rtCGM subgroup was significant (8.13% [95% CI: 2.98, 13.27]), while for the isCGM was not significant (0.06% [95% CI: -4.22, 4.34]).

In conclusion, our analysis demonstrated that continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) significantly reduced HbA1c (by MD: -2.78 mmol/mol), and time below range (TBR) (by MD: -1.30%,) compared to self-monitored blood glucose (SMBG) in type 2 diabetes. While time above range (TAR) showed a non-significant reduction (MD: -4.12%), time in range (TIR) suggested a potential increase (MD: 4.04%) that did not reach statistical significance. Subgroup analyses by CGM method yielded identical point estimates to the primary analysis for all outcomes, indicating this factor did not explain the observed heterogeneity.

To better illustrate the effects of mean age of participants and the duration of the interventions, we subsequently conducted a meta-regression analysis (Table 2). In the meta-regression analysis, we found that mean age and duration of intervention were the most influential moderators for TBR, accounting for approximately 47.8% and 35% of the explained variance, respectively. Additionally, the duration of intervention accounted for about 25% of the variation observed in TAR.

Publication bias

The outcomes of the risk-of-bias assessment conducted on included studies are summarized in Table 3. Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Fig. 4) is illustrated, which showed a slight asymmetry, indicating the possibility of publication bias. Egger’s test was applied for evaluation of funnel plot asymmetry, yielding the following results: HbA1c (beta1 = − 4.20, p = 0.0618), TAR (beta1 = 0.20, p = 0.9712), TBR (beta1 = − 1.11, p = 0.4680), and TIR (beta1 = 1.00, p = 0.6546). These findings collectively show a non-significant publication bias. Nonetheless, the limited number of studies may suggest that the asymmetry could be attributed to the heterogeneity among included studies rather than systematic bias.

Discussion and conclusion

One of the important pillars in achieving glycemic control in patients with diabetes mellitus is glucose monitoring. Although the measurement of HbA1C provides a reliable estimate of average serum glucose levels over the preceding three months, it does not capture glycemic variability, including transient episodes of hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia [26]. Identifying these fluctuations is particularly critical in high-risk populations, including patients with diabetes mellitus who require direct insulin therapy as part of their treatment, as they typically exhibit moderate to advanced disease progression with an increased risk of developing microvascular and macrovascular complications, and also patients who suffer from multiple endocrine disorders [27]. As such, this study was performed to review the existing methods of glucose monitoring, CGM and SMBG, with the prospect of concluding the best approach for insulin-treated DM patients.

Upon the introduction of CGM, several studies, including but not limited to the GOLD trial, DIAMOND trial, and IN CONTROL trial [12, 28, 29] demonstrated its clinical efficacy among patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus. However, evidence regarding the utility of CGM in patients with type 2 diabetes on an insulin regimen remains comparatively limited. This review of existing evidence showed an improvement in the HbA1c level and other glycemic parameters in CGM users. Although not measured, this improvement is indirectly related to a decrease in the incidence of hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia.

This meta-analysis confirms that CGM provides clinically meaningful improvements in two critical glycemic domains for type 2 diabetes: long-term glucose control (HbA1c) and hypoglycemia risk (TBR). The significant HbA1c reduction (− 2.78%) exceeds established minimal clinically important differences, supporting CGM’s therapeutic value. However, the substantial unexplained heterogeneity (I²=89.66%) in HbA1c outcomes warrants caution, suggesting unmeasured factors, such as CGM adherence, diabetes duration, or technology generation, may significantly influence efficacy. Notably, subgroup analyses failed to resolve heterogeneity or modify effects, implying that neither CGM methodology (e.g., real-time vs. intermittent) nor treatment type (e.g., insulin vs. non-insulin) accounts for these variations. The non-significant TIR/TAR trends may reflect limited statistical power, though the consistent direction of effects across outcomes suggests CGM favors balanced glucose exposure. Future studies should investigate sources of heterogeneity and confirm TIR benefits in larger cohorts.

While this study showed superior outcomes in patients using CGM, there are some limitations with its implementation in a clinical setting. First, cost-effectiveness remains questionable for patients managed solely with oral glucose-lowering agents, given the equivocal therapeutic benefits in this population. Similar constraints apply to patients with significant comorbidities, where competing clinical priorities may diminish CGM’s relative advantage. Additionally, usability and adherence challenges are particularly pronounced among elderly patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, further complicated by potential deficits in healthcare providers’ capacity to interpret CGM-derived metrics. Furthermore, systemic barriers, including restricted insurance coverage and limited device accessibility, may disproportionately limit its widespread adoption in real-world settings.

In conclusion, CGM outperforms SMBG in reducing HbA1c and hypoglycemia risk in insulin-treated T2DM patients. Future studies should investigate heterogeneity sources and confirm TIR benefits. Despite advantages, economic, usability, and systemic barriers must be addressed for broader implementation.
