Relationship between baseline right ventricular systolic function and left ventricular recovery at six-months of follow up among women with peripartum cardiomyopathy in Uganda
Authors: Juliet Nabbaale, Emmy Okello, Annettee Nakimuli, Graham Chakafana, Karen Sliwa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLV recovery in Peripartum Cardiomyopathy (PPCM) depends on several baseline factors including left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), LV end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), duration of symptoms. The role baseline RV function in LV recovery remains poorly described. This study sought to determine the relationship between baseline RV systolic function and LV recovery at six-months follow up among women with PPCM in Uganda.MethodsProspective cohort study of 80 PPCM cases and 80 healthy matched controls observed over a 6-month period while on goal-directed medical therapy (GDMT). All enrolled participants had a 12-lead electrocardiography, echocardiography at baseline and at 6-months follow-up for assessment of LV systolic function using LV global longitudinal strain (LV GLS) and LV EF whereas RV systolic function was assessed using; fractional area change (FAC), tricuspid annular plane excursion (TAPSE), RV lateral wall S’ (RV S’), fractional wall strain (FWS) and RV global longitudinal strain (RV GLS).ResultsThe mean data of cases included age of 33.6 ± 6.6 years, LVEF 35.7 ± 11.0 %, LV GLS −11.9 ± 4.7 % and RV GLS −14.7 ± 10.9 %, RV FAC 32.9 ± 13.5 %, Lat S’ 10.6 ± 3.0 cm/s and RV FWS −17.1 ± 7.2 %. LV recovery occurred among 46.3 % cases. Factors which predicted LV recovery included heart rate and LVEDD.ConclusionUnder half of PPCM cases had LV recovery at six-months on GDMT and Bromocriptine. Baseline heart rate and LVEDD predicted LV recovery.Keywords: Peripartum cardiomyopathy, LV recovery, RV function, Africa

Introduction

Peripartum Cardiomyopathy (PPCM) emerges as a significant cause of heart failure (HF) during pregnancy that is characterized by a variable rapid clinical course particularly affecting previously healthy young women leading to substantial morbidity and mortality. A few cases have good probability for spontaneous recovery despite high rates of relapse with subsequent pregnancies [1], [2]. However, predictors of LV recovery remain poorly understood globally.

Over the past two decades, research findings to elucidate the pathophysiology of PPCM suggests a multi-hit model ranging from genetic predisposition, extremes of maternal age, multiple pregnancies, high parity and cleaved N-terminal 16-kDa prolactin fragment which is also a key predisposing factor and predictor of LV recovery [1], [3]. Treatment recommendations for PPCM patients primarily draw upon extrapolations from guidelines governing other types of systolic heart failure with an aim of controlling volume status, neutralizing maladaptive neurohormonal responses and preventing thromboembolic complications [4].

Retrospective studies of patients with PPCM in the United States have shown that mortality is as high as 18 %, LVEF returns to normal at 6 months to 1 year in only 40 % to 62 % of patients [5], [6], [7].

Contemporary studies from Nigeria and South Africa demonstrated variation in the frequencies of LV recovery with low frequencies of LV recovery associated with several predictors of poor outcomes while regular use of beta blockers correlated with late survival among the Nigerian cohort while data from the global EORP data showed regional variation in the clinical presentation and low recovery rates [8], [9].

Previous studies have identified numerous predictors of poor outcome among PPCM patients, and the most cited is the degree of LV systolic dysfunction and LV dilatation at diagnosis although none has focused on RV systolic function as a predictor of LV recovery [10].

This existing knowledge gap as evidenced by the recent publication under the PPCM prediction score [11] that did not include RV function parameters to predict LV recovery serves as an additional rationale for this study to explore the role of RV systolic function as a key prognostic indicator for LV recovery. However, RV function at diagnosis has been established as a predictor of outcomes in other acute cardiomyopathies [12] therefore it seems reasonable to expect that RV function at baseline will predict LV recovery among PPCM patients in Uganda.

Methods

This was a single center, prospective cohort study conducted from June 2022 to August 2023. Patient screening took place at the outpatient clinic of the Uganda Heart Institute (UHI), the antenatal and postnatal clinics of Kawempe Women’s National Referral Hospital. Recruitment and data collection took place at the Uganda Heart Institute. Participants were enrolled into the study after obtaining written informed consent where their identity remained anonymous. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the respective Ethical Research Committees of Makerere University (School of Medicine Research Ethics Committee with IRB number Mak-SOMREC-2022–287) as the governing ethical board of UHI and University of Cape Town (Higher Research Ethics Committee with approval number HREC 093/2022) where my study is registered as a PhD research project. The study conformed to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki on the principles for the medical research involving human subjects [13].

Study population

The inclusion criteria for cases comprised; (i) peripartum state; (ii) manifestation of signs and/or symptoms of heart failure; (iii) left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction ≤ 45 %; and (iv) exclusion of alternative causes of heart failure. The exclusion criteria for cases included (i) Peripartum woman with heart failure confirmed with an identifiable cause of heart failure.

Sample size estimation

A study by Blauwet et al showed that using RV FAC at presentation predicted LV recovery and clinical events in patients with PPCM [14]. They further estimated that the standard deviation of the baseline RV FAC was 11.2, therefore based on these estimates we determined that at 5 % level of significance and 80 % power would require 80 participants to detect a 5-unit difference in RV FAC that ranges between 5 and 10 units. This sample size was obtained using formula of hypothesis testing for two population means.

Study procedure

Echocardiography protocol and equipment

A Vivid E95® (GE Healthcare) transthoracic echocardiographic machine was used to acquire the cardiac images by two cardiologists with a 1.5–4.6 MHz transducer (M5Sc) according to either ASE or ESC echo guidelines. All images were stored and analyzed by a single observer. LVEF was assessed by 2D method from parasternal long axis view using Teicholz method to assess LVEF. MAPSE was measured by M−Mode at the apical 4-chamber view. Strain analysis with STE was achieved by using LV global and regional longitudinal systolic strain measurement through 3 different views (apical 3-, 4-, and 2-chamber views). Manual adjustment was attempted, in the case of unsatisfied endocardial tracking. All the regional longitudinal strains were demonstrated on a Bull’s eye diagram, while GLS was calculated as the mean of the regional longitudinal strains. Right ventricular systolic function assessment was performed using the following modalities; Right Ventricular end-diastolic area (RV EDA) and RV end-systolic area (RV ESA) were measured with manual tracing of digital images from the apical 4-chamber view (Fig. 4). RV fractional area change (RV FAC) was calculated as follows: RV FAC (%) = (RV EDA −RV ESA)/RV EDA X 100 [15].

RV longitudinal free wall and global wall strain were assessed with speckle-tracking echocardiographic analysis. Measurements were performed online with dedicated software (2D Cardiac Performance Analysis) peak longitudinal systolic strain was obtained for the basal, middle and apical segments of the RV free wall and interventricular septum from software generated curves and averaged for mean RV free wall and RV global strain values, respectively in Fig. 1.

Echocardiographic categorization of parameters at baseline

Using ASE guideline thresholds [16], the categorical distribution of the left ventricular systolic function parameters included; (i) Left ventricular ejection fraction categories with mild to moderate abnormal LVEF ranging between 45 % to 35 % and severely abnormal whereby LVEF was below 35 %. (ii) Global Longitudinal Strain (GLS) normal parameters > -18 % to −26 %, mildly reduced −15 % to −12.5 %, moderately reduced ranging −8.1 % to −12.5 % and severe < -8.0 %.

Categorical distribution of right ventricular systolic function parameters included (i) Tricuspid Annular Plane Systolic excursion (TAPSE) whereby the normal TAPSE was 17 mm to 25 mm, mildly reduced TAPSE was 16 mm to 15 mm, moderately reduced was 15 mm to 10 mm and severely reduced TAPSE was below 10 mm. (ii) Lateral S’ wave categorical distribution included mildly impaired with measurements ranging from 9 cm/s to 12 cm/s, moderately impaired ranging 10 cm/s to 15 cm/s and severely reduced Lat S’ ranging from 6 cm/s to 9 cm/s. (iii) Right ventricular GLS was categorized as follows; normal ranging from −35 % to −17 %, mildly reduced ranging from −16 % to −12 %, moderately reduced ranging from −11 % to −8% and severely reduced was defined as RV GLS < −7%. Categorization of left ventricular recovery parameters at six months

The definition of the left ventricular systolic function recovery was based on ASE guidelines using Left ventricular ejection fraction ≥ 50 % at six months follow up on heart failure GDMT.

Pharmacological treatment

The utilization of optimal GDMT for heart failure included; two thirds were on beta blockers and almost quarter of the cases (n = 22, 27.5 %) were prescribed angiotensin receptor blockers, a third (n = 33, 41.3 %) were prescribed spironolactone. 58 (72.5 %) were prescribed beta blockers. Digoxin was used in 41 (51.3 %) and a diuretic in 60 (75.0 %). A total of 68.7 % were prescribed bromocriptine at a dose of 2.5 mg twice daily for 14 days and 2.5 mg once daily for 6 weeks. At six-months follow up, 12.5 % participants were prescribed Warfarin due to its availability without cost implications compared to the none-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs).

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous data was summarized as mean and standard deviation (SD) while skewed data was summarized as median with corresponding interquartile range (IQR). Categorical data was summarized as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the χ2 test. For categorical variables with more than two categories, proportions were presented in tables and bar graphs. We determined the relationship between Right Ventricular systolic function parameters and LV recovery at bivariate analysis using a Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, a two-sample t-test or Wilcoxon rank sum for continuous variable. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were traced according to baseline RV function and compared with the log-rank test. RV function parameters were included in a Cox proportional hazards regression model to correct for the baseline variables. All statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS version 17.0 software.

The evaluated right and left ventricular functional parameters demonstrated limited discriminatory ability for the outcome in Table 6, with AUC values ranging from 0.35 to 0.55. Among these, LV GLS showed the highest AUC (0.55) with high specificity (84 %) but poor sensitivity (27 %), while RV EF (AUC 0.51) and RV FAC (AUC 0.50) performed at chance level with modest sensitivity but low specificity. Other indices, including RV GLS, TAPSE and RV Lat S’ which yielded AUC values below 0.5 which reflected weak classification power. Although LVEF (AUC 0.40, p = 0.038) and LVED (AUC 0.35, p < 0.001) were statistically significant, their poor predictive performance suggested that these associations may lack clinical utility. Overall, none of the tested parameters provided meaningful balance between sensitivity and specificity, highlighting their limited value as standalone predictors.

Discussion

The major findings of our study are as follows: (1) A substantial proportion of patients with PPCM recovered based on an absolute threshold of the LVEF ≥ 50 % at six-months of follow up which was accompanied with a reduction in LV end-systolic diameter. (2) There was improvement of RV systolic function parameters specifically RV TAPSE and RV GLS from baseline to six-months follow up however, none of the RV systolic function parameters predicted LV recovery. (3) The present study demonstrates heart rate and LVEDD predicted LV recovery in this cohort of PPCM cases. To the best of our knowledge, this prospective study represents one of the first studies in an African population to address the prognostic predictors of LV recovery and the value of RV systolic function parameters as independent predictors of LV recovery in women with PPCM specifically in the Ugandan context.

PPCM is distinct from other types of cardiomyopathies since it demonstrates a more favorable recovery rate ranging from 45 % to 78 % within six months post diagnosis although the symptoms are similar to those of dilated cardiomyopathy [17], [18] Oxidative stress, genetic susceptibility, autoimmunity and myocarditis have all been implicated in the pathogenesis of PPCM and other types of cardiomyopathy [19], [20].

Our findings indicate a higher burden of RV systolic dysfunction compared to most PPCM cohort studies [21]. Findings from the EuroObservational Research Programme registry demonstrated a less burden of RV systolic dysfunction which was reported among 47 % of women [22]. These differences may be attributed to factors such as difference in the study methodology, classification of RV size and function and potential regional phenotypical differences.

TAPSE is an easily obtainable parameter that is commonly used to assess RV function in clinical practice, however our study consistent with findings from Blauwet L et al revealed TAPSE is not a reliable predictor of PPCM recovery [14]. TAPSE assumes that the displacement of basal and adjacent segments of RV represents the entire RV function which in reality contributes 60–70 % of RV function therefore giving a limitation to assess the contractility of the interventricular septum. We however documented improvement of TAPSE from baseline to 6 months follow up. TAPSE has been found to be significantly lower in PPCM cases, compared to the level in those diagnosed with dilated nonischemic cardiomyopathies [23].

Other parameters such as RV lateral S’ is not a reliable RV function parameter as it relies on tissue doppler that is subject to accuracy in the angle of alignment and volume overload in the left ventricle. In other studies, FAC has been found to have a significant role in predicting LV recovery[14]. RV FAC has the ability to detect RV alterations especially those affecting the interventricular septum.

Previous findings from the Investigations of Pregnancy-Associated Cardiomyopathy (IPAC) study showed a high incidence of LV recovery among this cohort of PPCM participants. Furthermore, the study showed that RV function per echocardiographic fractional area change at presentation was correlated with both subsequent LV recovery and clinical outcomes emphasizing its prognostic significance [14].

Studies using strain methodology to assess RV function in PPCM remain limited, however we documented a significant change in the RV GLS from baseline to six months without statistical significance in predicting LV recovery.

Complete LV recovery was documented among 46.3 % of PPCM cases in the present study, other studies have reported complete recovery rates between 20 % and 60 % [24], [25]. In previous studies, factors that predicted LV recovery included baseline ejection fraction ≥ 30 % and LVEDD < 5.5 cm [26], [27]. All these studies were from single centers and involved fewer than 55 PPCM cases with limited follow up. A recent prospective study by McNamara et al followed up 100 patients in USA where they documented LV recovery in 65 % of women at 6 months and demonstrated baseline EF and race were the predictors for LV recovery [28].

A recent report of 176 South African patients with PPCM followed up for only six months concluded that older age and lower LV end-systolic diameter predicted LV recovery [25]. This variation in predictors of LV recovery highlight the difference in genetic and geographical findings in PPCM cohorts.

LV dilatation does not occur in all patients with PPCM. As a response to LV dysfunction caused by volume overload issues, the cardiac remodeling process may generate LV dilatation therefore, our results highlight the importance of integrating LVEDD and heart rate at diagnosis, providing valuable prognostic insights that could inform patient management strategies for PPCM both in Uganda and globally.

Further still, supporting findings from other studies that RV systolic dysfunction in acute nonischemic dilated cardiomyopathy including PPCM is an independent predictor of morbidity and mortality. The exact mechanisms of the underlying mechanisms of RV systolic dysfunction in these patients is unclear. However, it seems likely that RV function is linked to LV function in at least some patients and potential mechanisms by which LV systolic dysfunction leads to RV systolic dysfunction include involvement of RV by the same cardiomyopathic process involving the LV and secondary pulmonary hypertension resulting in increased RV afterload [29].

Similarly, the recently published ESC EORP predictive score variables [11], results from our study demonstrated LVEDD as a common predictor for LV recovery in PPCM patients in Uganda.

Since an echocardiogram is a noninvasive tool that is not available in most health care facilities in Uganda, clinicians can easily and quickly use heart rate to evaluate the prognosis of PPCM patients at first medical contact in low income countries.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. We had a small sample size of 80 cases given the rarity of PPCM which limits the generalizability of our study findings majorly to the interest of local readers. Additionally, the deaths of 6 participants further compromised the robustness of the outcome assessment and our conclusions therefore further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted.

Conclusion

Findings in this prospective study potentially contribute valuable insights with LVEDD and heart rate at presentation are strong independent prognostic predictors of LV recovery in patients with PPCM in Uganda and sub-Saharan Africa.
