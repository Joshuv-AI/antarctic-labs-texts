Characterizing dietary diversity among urban office workers in northern Ghana: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ahmed Alhassan, Kasim Abdulai, Yakubu Mubaric, Paul Armah Aryee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe nutrition transition in low- and middle-income countries is driving a surge in non-communicable diseases (NCDs), with urban populations being particularly affected. In Ghana, office workers represent a key demographic at risk due to sedentary lifestyles and changing food environments. We aimed to assess the dietary diversity and identify underlying dietary patterns of this group in the Tamale Metropolis.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study among 216 office workers. Dietary intake was assessed using a two-day 24-hour recall. Dietary diversity was evaluated using the 9-point Individual Dietary Diversity Score (IDDS). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used on the nine food groups to derive dietary patterns.ResultsThe mean IDDS was 4.89 (SD 1.31), indicating medium dietary diversity. Only 33.8% of participants achieved high dietary diversity. PCA identified three dietary patterns: a “Higher-Diversity, Plant-Rich” pattern, a traditional “Animal-Source and Staples” pattern, and an “Accessory Animal Products” pattern. The diet was dominated by starchy staples (77.0%) and meat/fish (60.0%), with low consumption of fruits (43.0%) and dark green leafy vegetables (39.0%).ConclusionThe diet of urban office workers in Northern Ghana is suboptimal, characterized by low diversity and specific dietary patterns that are high in energy-dense staples and low in protective foods. This dietary profile is a significant public health concern. Targeted interventions are urgently needed to improve dietary diversity and mitigate diet-related disease risk in this population.

Introduction

Diet stands as a central, modifiable pillar in the architecture of chronic disease prevention and management. The 21st century has been defined by a global nutrition transition, a complex process involving profound shifts in dietary structure, particularly within low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. This transition is characterized by a decisive move away from traditional, plant-centric diets—often rich in fiber and legumes—towards a “Westernized” pattern high in refined starches, added sugars, sodium, and ultra-processed foods, alongside a concurrent decline in the consumption of fruits, vegetables, and whole grains [2]. The health consequences of this shift are stark and paradoxical. Many nations now grapple with the “double burden of malnutrition,” where the persistence of undernutrition and micronutrient deficiencies coexists with a rapid rise in overweight, obesity, and diet-related non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Globally, dietary risks are now the leading cause of mortality, accounting for an estimated 11 million deaths annually, surpassing even tobacco smoking [3]. This epidemiological shift is driven by a confluence of factors including globalization, trade liberalization, foreign direct investment in food systems, and the pervasive marketing of energy-dense, nutrient-poor products [4–6].

This phenomenon is particularly acute in Sub-Saharan Africa, a region undergoing one of the fastest rates of urbanization in the world [7]. As populations move from rural to urban centers, food systems are fundamentally reshaped. Diets shift from being based on home production and local markets to a heavy reliance on purchased foods within complex urban food environments [8]. These environments are increasingly characterized by the proliferation of supermarkets and convenience stores, which displace traditional markets and alter food availability, accessibility, and affordability [9–11]. This rapid transformation is a primary driver of the escalating NCD epidemic across the continent, placing immense strain on health systems that are often ill-equipped to manage the complex, long-term care that chronic conditions require [12–14].

Ghana is deeply embedded in this nutritional shift. Over the past two decades, the nation has experienced significant economic growth and urbanization, which has transformed its food environment, especially in cities [15]. The proliferation of fast-food chains, supermarkets, and street food vendors offering convenient and affordable meals has fundamentally altered dietary habits [16]. This evolution is a primary engine driving the nation’s rising prevalence of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension. National data indicate an alarming trend; for instance, the prevalence of adult hypertension ranges from 19% to 48% in some communities [17], and overweight/obesity affects over 40% of the urban population [18]. These conditions, once considered diseases of affluence, now permeate all levels of Ghanaian society, creating a significant public health crisis [19–21].

Within this context, urban office workers represent a demographic at the vanguard of the nutrition transition and a crucial population for public health surveillance. Their integration into the formal, urban economy often provides them with higher disposable income, enabling access to a wider variety of foods, including expensive processed and imported items [22]. However, the demands of their occupation—long, sedentary hours, high-stress environments, and limited breaks—simultaneously create a strong demand for convenience [23]. This pushes them towards ready-to-eat meals, processed snacks, and sugar-sweetened beverages, which are readily available in the urban food environment [24]. This creates a nutritional paradox where increased economic access does not automatically translate to improved dietary diversity or quality [25]. Instead, this group may be among the most vulnerable to the negative health consequences of the nutrition transition, acting as a sentinel for future public health challenges in the wider population [26–28].

To provide a holistic assessment of diet, contemporary nutritional epidemiology has moved beyond single-nutrient analysis to focus on overall dietary patterns. The Individual Dietary Diversity Score (IDDS) is a validated, qualitative measure of the variety of food groups consumed and serves as a strong proxy for micronutrient adequacy and overall dietary health [29–31]. A higher IDDS is consistently associated with a greater likelihood of meeting micronutrient requirements and is inversely associated with the risk of several NCDs [32]. While several studies have examined specific food intakes in Ghana, there is limited recent research that uses a validated index like the IDDS to holistically evaluate the dietary diversity of a high-risk occupational group like urban office workers. This is particularly true for the rapidly developing context of Northern Ghana, which has a distinct food culture and is at a different stage of the nutrition transition compared to the more extensively studied southern coastal cities [33]. This study, therefore, aimed to fill this critical knowledge gap by comprehensively assessing the dietary diversity and identifying the underlying dietary patterns of office workers in the Tamale Metropolis.

Methods

Study design and setting

We employed a cross-sectional study design to provide a snapshot of dietary patterns among office workers. The study was situated in the Tamale Metropolis, the administrative and commercial capital of Ghana’s Northern Region. This setting is significant due to its rapid urbanization and evolving food environment, making it a key location for studying the nutrition transition.

Participants and sampling

The study population comprised public office workers aged 18 years and older. We calculated a required sample size of 216 participants, assuming a 16.9% prevalence of obesity from a previous study in the region, a 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error.

A multi-stage random sampling technique was employed. First, the metropolis was divided into its four official sub-districts. From each sub-district, one of two sub-clusters was randomly selected. Within each of the four selected sub-clusters, public office institutions were identified, and 54 participants who met the inclusion criteria (office worker, age ≥ 18, not pregnant) were randomly selected and invited to participate. A total of 230 individuals were invited, of whom 216 provided complete data, yielding a response rate of 93.9%.

Data collection

Data were gathered between June 2021 and June 2022 by trained research assistants using a pre-tested, structured questionnaire. This instrument collected comprehensive information on sociodemographic characteristics, including age, sex, marital status, and education level, as well as key lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption and smoking history.

Dietary assessment and diversity measurement

Dietary intake was assessed on two separate occasions using a standardized, multi-pass 24-hour dietary recall [34]. The recalls were administered for two non-consecutive days, covering one weekday and one weekend day to capture variations in eating patterns. To measure dietary diversity, we used the Individual Dietary Diversity Score (IDDS) based on Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) guidelines [35]. The IDDS was calculated by pooling the food groups reported across both recall days and then summing the number of unique food groups consumed out of nine pre-defined, nutritionally distinct groups consumed out of nine pre-defined, nutritionally distinct groups: (1) Starchy staples, (2) Dark green leafy vegetables, (3) Other vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables, (4) Other fruits and vegetables, (5) Organ meat, (6) Meat and fish, (7) Eggs, (8) Legumes, nuts, and seeds, and (9) Milk and milk products. The final score was categorized according to established cut-offs: Low Diversity (≤ 3 groups), Medium Diversity (4–5 groups), and High Diversity (≥ 6 groups).

Statistical analysis

All data were entered and analyzed using SPSS (Version 25.0). We utilized descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) to summarize participant characteristics, IDDS scores, and food group consumption. To identify underlying dietary patterns, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the binary (consumed/not consumed) data for the nine food groups. Components with eigenvalues > 1.0 were retained, and a varimax rotation was applied to improve interpretability. Food groups with a factor loading of > 0.40 on a component were considered to significantly contribute to that dietary pattern.

Dietary diversity (IDDS)

The mean IDDS for the sample was 4.89 (SD 1.31), falling into the “medium dietary diversity” category. A detailed breakdown revealed that 109 (50.5%) office workers had a medium-diversity diet needing improvement. Only 73 (33.8%) consumed a high-diversity diet (IDDS ≥ 6), while 34 (15.7%) had a poor, low-diversity diet (IDDS ≤ 3).

When disaggregated by sex, the distribution of dietary diversity scores showed slight variations (Table 2). The prevalence of low dietary diversity was marginally higher among males (17.6%) compared to females (14.5%). Conversely, a slightly higher proportion of females (34.4%) achieved a high-diversity diet compared to their male counterparts (32.9%). Further bivariate analyses comparing IDDS categories (Low, Medium, High) against marital status and ethnicity found no statistically significant associations (p > 0.05 for all).

Food group consumption patterns

Analysis of food group consumption showed a heavy reliance on a few categories (Fig. 1). Starchy staples (e.g., rice, maize, yam) were the most consumed group, eaten by 77.0% of participants in the preceding 24 h. This was followed by meat and fish (60.0%). Consumption of protective food groups was notably low. Fruits and vegetables (combining ‘Other vitamin A-rich,’ ‘Dark green leafy,’ and ‘Other fruits & veg’) were collectively under-consumed. Specifically, only 43.0% of workers had consumed any fruit, and only 39.0% had consumed dark green leafy vegetables. Consumption of legumes, nuts, and seeds (36.0%) and milk products (16.0%) was also infrequent.

Discussion

This study provides a granular assessment of dietary diversity among urban office workers in Northern Ghana, revealing a concerning pattern of suboptimal nutrition within an educated and employed population. The mean Individual Dietary Diversity Score (IDDS) of 4.89 indicates that, on average, diets are monotonous and lack the variety essential for long-term health and NCD prevention. The finding that two-thirds of participants failed to achieve a high-diversity diet is a stark indicator that healthy eating patterns are the exception rather than the rule, even among a relatively affluent segment of society. This aligns with broader surveys across West Africa which report a trend towards monotonous, staple-based diets, even as urbanization and rising incomes theoretically increase food availability and access [36–38]. This mediocrity in dietary diversity is a critical public health issue, as it is strongly associated not only with an increased risk of NCDs but also with “hidden hunger”—the micronutrient deficiencies that can persist even in the presence of adequate or excessive caloric intake [39–41].

The PCA further elucidates these findings by identifying three distinct dietary patterns. The “Animal-Source and Staples” pattern likely reflects a more traditional dietary framework, centered on an energy-dense staple with a protein source. In contrast, the “Higher-Diversity, Plant-Rich” pattern aligns more closely with global recommendations for a prudent diet. The emergence of these distinct patterns suggests that the population is not homogenous in its dietary transition; rather, different segments of the population may be adopting different eating styles.

The overwhelming dominance of the “Animal-Source and Staples” pattern, as evidenced by the high consumption frequency of these food groups, is a critical finding. Many of the staples common in the Ghanaian diet, such as polished white rice and highly processed maize and cassava products, have a high glycemic index [42]. A diet heavily reliant on these foods, particularly when coupled with the low levels of physical activity characteristic of office work, promotes postprandial hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia. Over time, this contributes directly to the pathophysiology of insulin resistance, visceral fat accumulation, and the eventual development of type 2 diabetes [43–45]. This heavy reliance on a single food group displaces the intake of more nutrient-dense foods, creating a diet that is high in energy but poor in essential vitamins, minerals, and protective phytochemicals [46–48].

Perhaps the most significant public health concern highlighted is the alarmingly low consumption of fruits and vegetables, which is evident in the low prevalence of the “Higher-Diversity, Plant-Rich” pattern. With fewer than half of the participants reporting consumption of fruit (43%) or dark green leafy vegetables (39%) in the preceding 24 h, this population is missing out on the powerful, dose-dependent protective effects of these food groups. An extensive body of evidence from large-scale meta-analyses has unequivocally linked higher fruit and vegetable intake—rich in potassium, magnesium, and antioxidants—to a lower risk of hypertension, coronary heart disease, stroke, and several cancers [49–51]. The World Health Organization’s “5-a-day” recommendation is a global public health standard, yet the consumption levels observed here suggest that the majority of these office workers are not consuming even one portion of fruit per day. This dietary deficit represents a massive, missed opportunity for primary disease prevention and is a key modifiable behavior that must be addressed [52–54]. Furthermore, our analysis revealed subtle differences in dietary diversity between sexes, with females showing a slightly higher propensity for a high-diversity diet. While these differences were not statistically significant in our bivariate analysis, they may point to underlying gendered patterns in food choice, purchasing habits, or health-seeking behaviors that warrant further investigation in larger cohort studies.

The underlying drivers of these suboptimal dietary choices are likely multifaceted and deeply embedded in the local food and work environment. The urban food environment of Tamale, like many cities in LMICs, can be characterized as “obesogenic” [55]. There is a high density and visibility of outlets selling cheap, convenient, and heavily marketed energy-dense processed foods and sugar-sweetened beverages, while access to affordable, high-quality fresh produce may be more limited [56, 57]. The workplace itself functions as a critical micro-environment that shapes dietary behavior. Long working hours, high-pressure deadlines, and short breaks create a “time poverty” that incentivizes the consumption of convenient, ready-to-eat foods over meals prepared at home [58]. The lack of healthy options in workplace canteens or nearby food vendors further constrains choice [59]. Furthermore, there may be a knowledge-practice gap, where individuals may be generally aware of healthy eating principles but lack the practical skills, motivation, or, most importantly, the enabling environment to translate that knowledge into consistent, healthy behaviors [60–62].

These findings have significant implications for public health policy and practice in Ghana. The data underscore the urgent need for multi-sectoral interventions that move beyond individual education to address the systemic drivers of poor dietary patterns. At the policy level, this could include implementing fiscal measures such as taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages, which have proven effective in other contexts, alongside subsidies to make fruits and vegetables more affordable [63]. Stronger regulations on the marketing of unhealthy foods, particularly to urban consumers, are also warranted [64]. At the community and workplace level, interventions should focus on creating healthier food environments. Workplace wellness programs that include nutrition education, subsidized healthy meals in canteens, and creating a supportive culture for healthy eating could be highly effective in this specific population [65]. For clinicians, these findings highlight the importance of routinely incorporating dietary assessment as a core component of health screening and NCD management.

A key strength of this study is its use of a validated dietary diversity index in an under-researched region. However, it has limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between diet and health outcomes. Furthermore, while the IDDS is a robust measure of variety and a strong proxy for micronutrient adequacy, it does not quantify intake nor fully capture all facets of diet quality; for instance, a high-diversity score could theoretically coexist with high intakes of sodium or added sugars. Additionally, the study population was highly homogenous in terms of educational attainment (99.5% tertiary education), a strong proxy for socio-economic status, which precluded any meaningful analysis of dietary diversity across different SES levels.

Conclusion

The dietary diversity among urban office workers in Tamale, Ghana, is characterized by a monotonous, low-diversity pattern centered on specific dietary patterns that are high in starchy staples and low in protective foods like fruits and vegetables. These dietary patterns are a major public health concern and are likely a key contributor to the region’s growing burden of obesity, diabetes, and hypertension. A robust response is urgently needed, including public health policies that address the systemic drivers of poor dietary patterns by reshaping the urban food environment, alongside targeted, workplace-based interventions designed to promote and enable the adoption of more diverse and healthier dietary patterns.
