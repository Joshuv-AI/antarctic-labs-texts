RRM1 competes with NEDD4 to stabilize USP19 by blocking K387 ubiquitination and suppresses autophagy-mediated chemoresistance in colorectal cancer
Authors: Lingxiao Wang, Weixing Zhang, Yifan Kang, Yingjia Wang, Shenghuai Hou, Fan Wang, Yuqiao Bai, Ruijun Ma, Jian Yang, Yaoping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResistance to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) remains a major clinical challenge in colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment. This study investigates the non-canonical role of ribonucleotide reductase subunit M1 (RRM1) in regulating autophagy-mediated chemoresistance.MethodsA comprehensive multi-omics strategy, including metabolomics, transcriptomics and proteomics analyses, was employed to study 5-fluorouracil-resistant colorectal cancer tissues from patients and matched drug-resistant cell models. Protein-protein interactions, ubiquitination events and functional consequences were validated via complementary assays including Co-IP, GST pull-down, in vivo ubiquitination, structure-guided molecular docking, site-directed mutagenesis, and subcutaneous xenograft mouse models. Structure-based virtual screening was conducted to identify candidate compounds targeting the RRM1-USP19 interaction, with in vivo anti-tumor activity verified in 5-FU-resistant CRC xenografts.ResultsRRM1 is significantly downregulated in 5-FU-resistant CRC tissues, and low RRM1 expression correlates with poor clinical prognosis. Mechanistically, RRM1 directly binds USP19 via conserved E647/R648 residues, competitively blocking NEDD4-mediated USP19 K387 ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. Loss of RRM1 destabilizes USP19, impairs autophagic substrate deubiquitination, hyperactivates autophagic flux and thereby induces 5-FU resistance. Restoration of RRM1 or expression of the ubiquitination-defective USP19-K387R mutant re-sensitizes resistant CRC cells to 5-FU. The peptide GAGGVGKSAL, identified via virtual screening, specifically disrupts the RRM1-USP19 interface, inhibits excessive autophagy and potently suppresses tumor progression in 5-FU-resistant CRC xenograft models.ConclusionsThis study identifies the novel RRM1-USP19-NEDD4 regulatory axis as a core mediator of autophagy-driven 5-FU resistance in CRC, uncovers a non-canonical, metabolism-independent role of RRM1 as a USP19 stabilizer via competitive inhibition of NEDD4-mediated ubiquitination, and validates GAGGVGKSAL as a promising lead compound targeting this axis to reverse 5-FU resistance. Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the second most common cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1], with a striking increase in incidence observed in younger age groups, highlighting the pressing need for innovative treatment options [2]. Despite the availability of multimodal therapies-including surgery, immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and radiation-their impact on advanced-stage disease remains suboptimal. A key component of current CRC treatment is 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)-based chemotherapy, widely used as first-line therapy; however, its clinical utility is greatly hindered by the high prevalence of drug resistance. Between 30% and 50% of patients develop either primary or acquired resistance after initial treatment, often leading to tumor recurrence or disease advancement [3, 4]. Some studies report resistance rates exceeding 40%, which substantially reduces therapeutic efficacy [5–7]. Importantly, resistant cancer cells frequently exhibit more aggressive behaviors, such as increased invasive capacity and higher metastatic propensity, further complicating clinical management [8]. These observations highlight the necessity of discovering new molecular targets for precision interventions and underscore the importance of elucidating the underlying mechanisms of 5-FU resistance to design effective approaches for reversing resistance and enhancing survival outcomes in CRC patients [9, 10].

Autophagy is a phylogenetically conserved cellular process involving lysosomal degradation that plays a complex and context-specific role in the development of CRC [11, 12]. In the early stages of tumorigenesis, it serves as a protective mechanism by removing dysfunctional organelles and maintaining genomic stability; however, in established tumors, autophagy supports cancer cell survival under stressful conditions such as nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and exposure to anticancer therapies, thereby facilitating chemoresistance and tumor recurrence [13, 14]. Growing research underscores the multifaceted regulation of autophagy in CRC. For instance, OTUB1-mediated deubiquitination of SRPX2 triggers autophagic activation and leads to chemotherapy resistance, while deficiency in miR−34a/b/c reduces autophagy yet unexpectedly increases resistance to 5-FU [15]. Furthermore, essential components of the autophagy machinery-such as lysosome-associated membrane proteins (LAMPs) and critical autophagy-related genes like ATG4B and ATG7-are involved in mediating drug resistance, and their suppression has been shown to enhance tumor responsiveness to treatment [16]. Non-coding RNAs also contribute to this regulatory landscape: for example, the long non-coding RNA FIRRE enhances pro-survival autophagy by stabilizing BECN1 mRNA, whereas SP0495, a protein encoded by KIAA0495, induces tumor-suppressive autophagy, highlighting the divergent roles autophagy can play in cancer progression [17]. Despite these advances, the full scope of molecular networks governing autophagy in CRC remains unclear, particularly regarding its crosstalk with key oncogenic signaling pathways. Ubiquitination and deubiquitination represent pivotal post-translational modifications that dynamically regulate autophagic activity. Dysregulation of this regulatory network profoundly alters autophagy flux and ultimately drives the acquisition of chemoresistance in colorectal cancer. Emerging evidence reveals that the protein stability of deubiquitinases is frequently controlled by ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation [18, 19]. Notably, USP19 itself is subject to ubiquitination modification, adding a new layer of regulation to autophagy and drug resistance.

RRM1 is a well-established regulator of pyrimidine metabolism and DNA repair, with its canonical roles extensively documented in cancer biology [20, 21]. However, accumulating evidence reveals that many classical metabolic enzymes play diverse non-metabolic roles in tumor progression, protein modification, and signal transduction, largely independent of their traditional catalytic activities [22–24]. In pancreatic cancer, elevated RRM1 expression has been identified as a biomarker of resistance to gemcitabine [25, 26], while in CRC, RRM1 and its homologs display divergent clinical implications: RRM2 overexpression is linked to adverse outcomes, whereas higher levels of RRM1 and RRM2B correlate with improved patient survival [27, 28]. Moreover, RRM1 enhances tumor cell radiosensitivity by upregulating RAD51AP1, a critical mediator of homologous recombination, through an E2F1-dependent mechanism [29]. Despite these advances, the role of RRM1 in modulating autophagy via the ubiquitin system in CRC remains poorly understood. Clinically, high RRM1 expression is correlated with aggressive histological phenotypes of colorectal cancer. Intriguingly, patients with elevated RRM1 tend to have a better overall prognosis, largely because RRM1 effectively restrains tumor chemoresistance and improves the response to 5-FU treatment. Here, we report a previously unidentified pathway in which RRM1 suppresses autophagic flux in drug-resistant colorectal cancer. We demonstrate that RRM1 directly interacts with USP19-a deubiquitinating enzyme localized to the endoplasmic reticulum and known for its involvement in ER-associated degradation (ERAD) and substrate stabilization-in a functionally significant manner. Mechanistically, RRM1 competitively disrupts the interaction between the E3 ubiquitin ligase NEDD4 and USP19 at lysine residue K387, thereby preventing NEDD4-mediated proteasomal degradation of USP19. This RRM1-USP19-NEDD4 axis is essential for maintaining USP19 protein stability and suppressing autophagy activation. Our findings not only uncover a novel, non-canonical function of RRM1 in autophagy regulation but also establish this signaling module as a promising therapeutic target to overcome treatment resistance in CRC.

Materials and methods

Patient samples and ethics authorization

Surgical specimens were collected from colorectal cancer patients diagnosed by histopathological and clinical evaluation at Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital. Fresh frozen tumor and matched adjacent non-cancerous tissues were obtained from each patient. One portion was immediately placed in RNA stabilization reagent and stored in cryovials for nanopore-based transcriptomic analysis. The other was labeled and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for non-targeted metabolomics. A total of 98 colorectal cancer patients were enrolled in this study, all of whom received standard FOLFOX chemotherapy. The collected clinical specimens were classified into two groups: 49 cases with treatment response and 49 cases with chemoresistance. Notably, the drug resistance observed here refers to acquired resistance developed during treatment along with tumor recurrence and progression, instead of primary resistance at baseline. Tissue microarrays containing tumor and adjacent tissues from 150 patients were constructed. Protein expression was assessed by immunohistochemistry and correlated with clinical follow-up data. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. 264(2022), Provincial Health Commission).

Proteomics, metabolomics, ONT transcriptomics analysis

Fresh colorectal tumor specimens and paired non-tumor tissues were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen upon collection to maintain biomolecular stability. Metabolite profiling was conducted using the Thermo Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer, whereas long-read RNA sequencing was implemented via the Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) platform. In parallel, cell pellets from 5-fluorouracil-resistant and 5-fluorouracil-sensitive HCT116 cell lines were cryopreserved under the same conditions and analyzed by 4D-data independent acquisition (4D-DIA) proteomics with the Thermo Vanquish chromatography system. This multi-omics strategy facilitated a systematic investigation of molecular changes linked to colorectal cancer development and therapeutic responsiveness.

Cell culture, transfection and lentivirus infections

Human colorectal cancer cell lines HCT116 and Lovo, and their 5-fluorouracil-resistant sublines (HCT116/5-FU and Lovo/5-FU) were obtained from Shanghai Jin Yuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All cell lines have been authenticated via short tandem repeat (STR) profiling to ensure cell identity and exclude cross-contamination. All cells were routinely tested and confirmed to be mycoplasma-negative throughout the experiments. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 or high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Resistant sublines were continuously grown in 1 µM 5-fluorouracil to maintain resistance. The IC50 values for all cell lines are provided in Supplementary Table 4. All plasmid vectors and siRNAs were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Transient transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For stable gene modulation, lentiviral constructs for RRM1 knockdown or overexpression were designed and validated by GenePharma. Briefly, cells were seeded at 5 × 10⁴ cells/mL. The next day, viral particles were added at an optimized MOI with HiTransG P enhancer. Stable populations were selected with puromycin (1 µg/mL) for 7–10 days until non-transduced cells died. The siRNA interference sequences and the cloning sites of the mutant plasmids are provided in Supplementary Tables 6, 8, and 11.

CCK-8 and colony-formation assay

Colorectal cancer cells from different experimental groups were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured in medium supplemented with 5-FU for 1 to 5 days. At each time point, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (HYCEZMBIO, Wuhan, China) was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to assess cell viability. For clonogenic assays, 500 cells per well were seeded in 24-well plates and maintained under standard culture conditions until visible colonies formed. Colonies were then fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and counted manually under a microscope.

Transmission electron microscope

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) sample preparation followed a standard protocol. Samples were initially fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), then post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide to enhance membrane contrast. Dehydration used a graded acetone series: 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%, with three changes at the final concentration to ensure complete water removal. The specimen was gradually infiltrated with Epon-812 embedding medium using acetone-resin mixtures at 3:1, 1:1, and 1:3 ratios to facilitate resin transition. After infiltration, samples were embedded in pure Epon-812 and polymerized at 60 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin Sects. (60–90 nm) were cut with an ultramicrotome and mounted on copper grids. Sections were doubly stained: first with uranyl acetate (10–15 min) to highlight nucleic acids and lipids, then with lead citrate (1–2 min, room temperature) to improve ultrastructural clarity. TEM imaging was performed under standard conditions to obtain high-resolution images.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and mass spectrometry

Total protein extracts from colorectal cancer cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were rotated at 4 °C for 1 h, then centrifuged at 12,000 RPM for 30 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were transferred to new tubes and incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation and primary antibodies: RRM1 (1:400, ProteinTech), USP19 (1:150, ProteinTech), or normal IgG (1:250, ProteinTech) as a negative control. After antibody binding, immune complexes were captured by adding Protein A/G Plus-Agarose beads and rotating for 5 h at 4 °C. Beads were pelleted by brief centrifugation and washed three times with ice-cold lysis buffer to remove nonspecific proteins. The immunocomplexes were resuspended in 1× SDS-PAGE loading buffer, boiled at 95 °C for 5 min, and analyzed by western blot. For identifying interacting proteins, Co-IP eluates were sent to APTBIO (Shanghai, China) for LC-MS/MS analysis. To assess in vivo ubiquitination, cells were pretreated with MG132 (10 µM) for 6 h before lysis, followed by Co-IP under denaturing conditions to preserve ubiquitin conjugates.

GST pull-down assay

The plasmid encoding the glutathione-S-transferase (GST)-USP19 fusion protein was transformed into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) cells, and recombinant protein expression was induced by the addition of isopropyl β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) at a final concentration of 0.5 mM for 4–6 h at 37 °C. Bacterial cells were harvested and lysed by sonication in ice-cold lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 RPM for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was incubated with glutathione-sepharose beads at 4 °C for 1 h to capture the GST-USP19 fusion protein. Following removal of unbound proteins through washing, the immobilized GST-USP19 was incubated with the target protein in binding buffer under gentle rotation overnight at 4 °C to facilitate interaction. Beads were subsequently washed thoroughly with cold wash buffer, and the protein complexes were eluted using reduced glutathione elution buffer. Eluted samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE and analyzed by western blotting to detect protein-protein interactions. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions for the Pierce GST Protein Interaction Pull-Down Kit, with minor optimizations to enhance yield and specificity.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, then washed twice with PBS (5 min each). Permeabilization was done with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature, followed by two PBS washes. Blocking was performed using 10% goat serum in PBS for 1 h. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies in blocking buffer for 1.5 h, washed three times with PBS, then incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Signal amplification was achieved with TSA650 for 20 min, followed by washing. A second round of staining for RRM1 included primary antibody incubation under the same conditions, then labeling with coralite488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG. TSA570 was used for additional amplification (20 min), followed by extensive washes. Nuclei were stained with DAPI in antifade mounting medium for 5 min. Samples were mounted with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant and imaged using a confocal microscope with appropriate filters.

Western blot and RT-qPCR

Total protein was extracted with RIPA buffer containing PMSF, phosphatase inhibitor, and protease inhibitor cocktail (100:1:1:1) to prevent degradation. Protein concentration was measured using a BCA kit, and 20–40 µg of protein was analyzed by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Proteins were detected using ECL reagents and a chemiluminescence imaging system. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus, reverse-transcribed into cDNA with 5× PrimeScript RT Master Mix, and analyzed by qRT-PCR using SYBR Green Master Mix. mRNA levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH or β-actin as reference genes. Primer sequences are listed in Table S7.

In vivo ubiquitination assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 10 cm culture dishes and incubated for 24 h under standard conditions (5% CO₂, 37 °C). Following this pre-culture period, cells were treated with 10 µM MG132, a proteasome inhibitor, for 8 h to block ubiquitin-proteasome-mediated degradation and accumulate ubiquitinated proteins. After treatment, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed in denaturing lysis buffer containing 1% SDS supplemented with protease and deubiquitinase inhibitors. The cell lysates were then diluted to reduce the SDS concentration after heat denaturation and sonication. Immunoprecipitation was performed using an anti-USP19 antibody conjugated to protein A/G magnetic beads to enrich USP19-associated complexes. The immunoprecipitated samples were then subjected to SDS-PAGE and analyzed by Western blotting with an anti-ubiquitin (anti-Ub) antibody to specifically detect ubiquitinated USP19. This experimental design ensures enhanced detection of post-translational modifications by stabilizing ubiquitinated species and specifically isolating the target protein complex.

Molecular docking analysis

AlphaFold3 enables high-accuracy structural prediction of biomolecular complexes, including those composed of proteins, nucleic acids, small molecules, ions, post-translationally modified residues, and antibody-antigen pairs. To investigate the interaction between small molecules and the protein encoded by the gene of interest, the FASTA sequence of the target protein was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The FASTA sequences for the specific proteins are provided in Supplementary Table 9. Structural modeling and molecular docking simulations were subsequently performed using AlphaFold3 (accessible at https://alphafoldserver.com/) to predict binding conformations. The resulting complex structures were visualized, analyzed, and refined using PyMOL (Schrödinger, LLC), with particular attention to ligand-binding interfaces and interaction geometries.

Experimental animals

Female BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, SPF grade) were obtained from the Animal Experiment Center of Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital. They were housed at 23–25 °C with 55% humidity and a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with free access to water and food. After one week of acclimatization, 5 × 10⁶ cells were injected subcutaneously into the flank to establish tumors. Once tumor growth was confirmed, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized. Tumors were excised; one part was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h for histology, and the rest was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for molecular analysis.

Immunohistochemical staining

Systematic pretreatment procedures were conducted on paraffin-embedded tumor sections, encompassing dewaxing, dehydration, antigen retrieval, blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity, and incubation with primary antibodies. In the immunohistochemistry (IHC) experiments, the primary antibodies utilized were RRM1, USP19 and Ki67. Subsequently, an enzyme-labeled goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG polymer was employed as the secondary antibody. The sections were then subjected to gradient alcohol dehydration and xylene clarification. Finally, the sections were sealed using neutral gum. The clinical baseline characteristics and survival time of patients in the immunohistochemistry group are detailed in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Virtual screening and drug discovery

Structure-based virtual screening was conducted using Schrödinger Suite (version 5.5). Prior to molecular docking, both protein and ligand structures were systematically prepared. Following the retrieval of the initial protein structure, preprocessing was performed using the Protein Preparation Wizard to optimize the target structure. This included removing non-essential co-crystallized chains, eliminating irrelevant water molecules, reconstructing missing heavy atoms, assigning formal charges, adjusting bond orders, and adding hydrogen atoms with appropriate partial charges derived from the OPLS4 force field. Energy minimization of the receptor was carried out until convergence was achieved or the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of atomic positions reached a maximum threshold of 0.30 Å. The refined protein structure was then used for grid generation. The centroid of the grid box (20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å) was defined based on key DNA-binding residues within the Receptor Grid Generation panel. Approximately 1.6 million compounds were processed through LigPrep for ligand preparation under the following conditions: (i) application of the OPLS4 force field; (ii) generation of ionization states at a target pH of 7.0 ± 2.0 using the Ionizer module; (iii) removal of salts; (iv) enumeration of tautomers; and (v) generation of up to 32 conformations per compound while preserving specified chiral centers and allowing inversion at other stereocenters. The prepared ligands were subsequently subjected to a hierarchical virtual screening cascade comprising Glide HTVS (High-Throughput Virtual Screening), Glide SP (Standard Precision docking), and Glide XP (Extra Precision docking), with a 90% similarity cutoff applied at each stage to reduce redundancy and enhance screening efficiency. The specific sites for virtual screening are outlined in Supplementary Table 10.

Reagents, antibodies

The reagents and antibodies used in this study were listed in Supplemental Table 5.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data in this study were obtained from independent biological replicates, and the exact replicate numbers (n) for each experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. SPSS 26.0 statistical software was used for statistical analysis. Measurement data were presented as mean ± standard deviation ( ± s). All measurement data were tested for normality, data with non-normal distribution were logarithmically converted, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied for comparisons between two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons involving three or more groups. All count data were expressed as rate (%) using χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical data were collected by using Graph Pad Prism 9.5 software and made into histogram. The results are expressed as Means ± SEM. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Integrated multi-omics analysis identifies RRM1 as a core differentially expressed gene linked to fluorouracil resistance

To systematically characterize the metabolic and transcriptomic alterations associated with fluorouracil resistance in colorectal cancer, we performed an integrated non-targeted metabolomics and ONT transcriptome sequencing analysis on clinical tissue samples from 98 patients. Comparative abundance profiling identified approximately 18,000 differentially abundant metabolites, a subset of which is visualized in Fig. 1A. Pathway enrichment analysis based on differential abundance scores revealed significant dysregulation of the pyrimidine metabolism pathway in resistant versus sensitive tissues (Fig. 1B), implicating this pathway in the molecular mechanisms of drug resistance. Five key metabolites within this pathway were identified as putative regulatory mediators (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Integration with ONT transcriptomic data facilitated the identification of multiple genes linked to pyrimidine metabolism, nine of which displayed expression patterns potentially modulated by these metabolites (Supplementary Table 1). Notably, multi-omics integration using O2PLS analysis identified RRM1 as the most significantly differentially expressed gene between resistant and sensitive groups, highlighting its central role in the transcriptional signature of fluorouracil resistance (Fig. 1C Supplementary Fig. 1B, C).

Low expression of RRM1 contributes to 5-FU resistance and correlates with poor prognosis

To explore the potential role of RRM1 in fluorouracil resistance, we first evaluated its expression levels in clinical colorectal cancer tissue microarrays and pathological specimens. As shown in Fig. 1D and E, the protein level of RRM1 in tumor tissues was significantly higher than that in adjacent normal tissues. Further clinical correlation analysis revealed that high RRM1 expression was significantly positively correlated with increased lymph node metastasis and worse histological grade, but there was no significant association with patient age or gender (Tables 1 and 2). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated that patients with high RRM1 expression had a relatively better prognosis (Fig. 1F). Further examination of fluorouracil resistance and sensitive tumor tissues in clinical samples revealed that the expression level of RRM1 in resistant tissues was lower (Fig. 1G, H). We next validated this finding in well-established 5-FU resistant cell lines. Consistent with the clinical observations, Western blot analysis revealed a significant reduction in RRM1 protein expression in resistant HCT116 cells relative to their parental HCT116 counterparts (Figs. 2A and Supplement Fig. 2A). Combining multi-omics data, we hypothesized that RRM1 might affect the survival outcome of patients by regulating the mechanisms related to fluorouracil resistance. Cells were cultured with sustained 5-FU exposure throughout the experiments (1.25 µM for HCT116, 400 µM for Lovo). In 5-FU-resistant cell lines, proliferation experiments showed that RRM1 expression was significantly downregulated, and the cell proliferation ability was significantly enhanced compared to 5-FU-sensitive cells (Fig. 2B and Supplement Fig. 2B). When RRM1 was overexpressed in resistant cells, cell proliferation was inhibited; the clone formation experiment further confirmed that RRM1 deficiency could enhance the clonogenic ability of colorectal cancer cells (Fig. 2C, D and Supplement Fig. 2C). In the xenograft mouse model treated with continuous 5-FU administration, the tumor volume and weight derived from resistant HCT116 cells were significantly greater than those from sensitive cells; however, restoring RRM1 expression could partially inhibit tumor growth, and both tumor volume and weight were significantly reduced compared to the resistant control group (Fig. 2F-H). Immunohistochemical Ki67 analysis of transplanted tumor tissues further showed that the proportion of Ki67-positive cells was significantly lower in the high RRM1 expression group (Fig. 2E). In conclusion, these results indicate that RRM1 is not only associated with a better prognosis but may also be a key molecule regulating the response of colorectal cancer cells to 5-FU treatment.

RRM1 reverses fluorouracil resistance through suppression of the autophagy pathway

Proteomic profiling of fluorouracil-resistant HCT116 cells and their RRM1-overexpressing derivatives identified significant enrichment of differentially expressed proteins in the autophagy signaling pathway (Fig. 3A), prompting us to investigate whether RRM1 modulates chemoresistance via autophagy regulation. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed abundant autophagolysosomal structures in resistant cells-characterized by single-membrane vesicles containing degraded cytoplasmic components-consistent with enhanced autophagic activity (Fig. 3B). Notably, RRM1 overexpression markedly reduced the number of these autophagic vesicles, indicating suppressed autophagy. To directly assess autophagic flux, we employed a tandem GFP-RFP-LC3 reporter assay: resistant cells exhibited robust GFP-LC3 (autophagosome) and RFP-LC3 (autolysosome) punctate signals, reflecting elevated autophagic flux, which was significantly blunted by RRM1 overexpression (Fig. 3C-E). To assess the impact of RRM1 overexpression on autophagy in fluorouracil-resistant CRC cells, we analyzed the expression of autophagy markers p62 and LC3 in HCT116 and Lovo resistant cells. Western blot results demonstrated that RRM1 overexpression induced p62 accumulation and reduced LC3-II/I ratios in HCT116 resistant cells, indicating impaired autophagic flux. Treatment with the lysosomal inhibitor CQ further increased p62 and LC3-II levels in both control and oeRRM1 cells, with an additive effect observed in oeRRM1 cells (Fig. 3F, G). These data suggest that RRM1 overexpression suppresses autophagic flux in chemoresistant CRC cells, which can be enhanced by CQ treatment.

RRM1 modulates USP19 protein stability through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway

These findings prompted us to investigate the molecular mechanism underlying RRM1-mediated fluorouracil resistance. Through immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) analysis of RRM1-overexpressing colorectal cancer resistant cells, we identified USP19 as a novel interacting partner of RRM1. Proteomic data further revealed that USP19 protein levels were significantly reduced in resistant cells with downregulated RRM1 expression, exhibiting a strong positive correlation (Fig. 4A). Notably, previous studies [30] (Cui J et al.) have demonstrated that USP19 regulates autophagy-mediated selective degradation of p62, a function highly consistent with the enhanced autophagic flux and drug-resistant phenotype observed in our resistant cell models. To validate the IP-MS results, endogenous co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays confirmed a direct and specific physical interaction between RRM1 and USP19 (Fig. 4D and Supplement Fig. 2D). Immunofluorescence co-localization analysis revealed prominent co-localization signals for RRM1 and USP19 in the cytoplasm and plasma membrane of HCT116 resistant cells (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, exogenous Co-IP and GST pull-down experiments provided convergent evidence supporting a stable interaction between the two proteins (Fig. 4E, G and Supplement Fig. 2E).

To elucidate the role of RRM1 in regulating USP19 stability, we overexpressed RRM1 in resistant cells. While RRM1 overexpression did not alter USP19 mRNA levels (Fig. 4C and Supplement Fig. 2G), it markedly increased USP19 protein abundance (Fig. 4B and Supplement Fig. 2F), indicating post-transcriptional regulation. To assess the impact of RRM1 on USP19 protein stability, cycloheximide(CHX) chase experiments were performed. The results showed that RRM1 overexpression significantly slowed the degradation rate of USP19 and extended its half-life (Fig. 4H, I and Supplement Fig. 2H). Moreover, treatment with proteasome inhibitors effectively blocked USP19 degradation (Fig. 4J and Supplement Fig. 2I), confirming that its stability is governed by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Subsequent ubiquitination assays using HA-tagged ubiquitin (HA-Ub) demonstrated that RRM1 overexpression substantially reduced USP19 ubiquitination compared to parental resistant cells (Fig. 4K and Supplement Fig. 2J). Collectively, these findings indicate that in the context of fluorouracil resistance, loss of RRM1 leads to increased ubiquitination of USP19 and accelerates its degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system.

To precisely define the interaction interface between RRM1 and USP19, we generated five RRM1 point mutants based on molecular docking predictions (Fig. 4L) and expressed them in drug-resistant cells. Co-IP assays demonstrated that the E647A/R648I mutant completely abolished USP19 binding (Fig. 4M and Supplement Fig. 2K). Consistently, in cells expressing this mutant, USP19 failed to be stably maintained and exhibited increased ubiquitination levels, a shortened protein half-life, and markedly reduced stability (Fig. 4N-P and Supplement Fig. 2L, M). These findings conclusively demonstrate that RRM1 directly interacts with USP19 through specific residues (E647/R648), thereby suppressing its ubiquitination and preserving protein stability.

NEDD4 is identified as the key E3 ligase responsible for USP19 K387 ubiquitination and degradation

Given that RRM1 is not an E3 ubiquitin ligase, the identity of the specific E3 ligase responsible for mediating RRM1-dependent regulation of USP19 remained elusive. To address this, we used UbiBrowser to predict potential E3 ligases that interact with USP19 and identified the top five candidates likely to form a functional complex with both RRM1 and USP19 (Fig. 5A): NEDD4, BMI1, MARCH1, MARCH8, and SYVN1. Systematic knockout screening revealed that depletion of NEDD4-but not the other four candidates-effectively preserved USP19 protein expression in 5-FU-resistant colorectal cancer cells (Fig. 5B and Supplement Fig. 3A), indicating a selective role for NEDD4 in regulating USP19 stability under these conditions. Co-IP assays confirmed a direct physical interaction between USP19 and NEDD4 (Fig. 5C and Supplement Fig. 3B). Furthermore, protein half-life and CHX chase experiments demonstrated that NEDD4 knockout markedly stabilized USP19, significantly prolonging its half-life (Fig. 5D, E, G and Supplement Fig. 3C-E). In vivo ubiquitination assays validated that NEDD4 directly catalyzes USP19 ubiquitination (Fig. 5F and Supplement Fig. 3F). Molecular docking analysis predicted a potential interaction between the K560 residue of NEDD4 and the 387–389 region of USP19 (Fig. 5H). Functional validation using the NEDD4 K560R mutant showed that this substitution substantially reduced USP19 ubiquitination, leading to increased protein stability and accumulation (Fig. 5I-K and Supplement Fig. 3G-I).

USP19 interacts with NEDD4 and undergoes K48-linked ubiquitination at Lys387

To identify the ubiquitination sites on USP19, we mapped the structural determinants governing its interaction with the E3 ubiquitin ligase NEDD4. Guided by USP19’s domain architecture (Fig. 6A), we generated a series of truncation mutants. GST pull-down assays revealed that the Δ(1–540) mutant failed to bind NEDD4, completely disrupting this interaction (Fig. 6B). These data establish that the N-terminal CS1/CS2 domains as indispensable for NEDD4 binding, whereas the central UBL/ZnF region and C-terminal TM region are dispensable. Furthermore, site-specific ubiquitination mapping combined with systematic alanine or arginine substitution mutagenesis confirmed that the K387R mutation reduced USP19 ubiquitination levels, while K393R and K856R showed no appreciable effect (Figs. 6C, D and Supplement Fig. 3L). To determine the topology of the ubiquitin chain, we performed Co-IP experiments using lysine-restricted ubiquitin mutants. Compared to wild-type ubiquitin, the K48R mutant significantly reduced USP19 ubiquitination, whereas the K63R mutant had no such effect, indicating that USP19 undergoes primarily K48-linked ubiquitination (Fig. 6E). Notably, siRNA-mediated knockdown of NEDD4 significantly decreased USP19 ubiquitination levels, suggesting that NEDD4 acts as the E3 ligase mediating K63-linked ubiquitination of USP19 at lysine 387. In summary, USP19 directly interacts with NEDD4 via its N-terminal CS1/CS2 domains and is subject to NEDD4-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination at lysine 387 in fluorouracil-resistant colorectal cancer cells.

Competitive binding of RRM1 and NEDD4 at USP19-K387 controls USP19 stability

To elucidate the regulatory interplay among RRM1, USP19, and NEDD4, we systematically assessed their interaction dynamics through Co-IP assays. Overexpression of RRM1 markedly suppressed the association between USP19 and NEDD4 (Fig. 7A), whereas expression of the RRM1-E647A/R648I mutant, which disrupts RRM1’s functional integrity, significantly enhanced USP19-NEDD4 binding (Fig. 7B). In contrast, the K387A mutation in USP19 impaired its interaction with both NEDD4 and RRM1 (Fig. 7C), indicating that lysine 387 constitutes a critical molecular interface for competitive binding by these two proteins. At the protein level, both RRM1 overexpression and NEDD4 knockdown led to a significant increase in USP19 abundance (Fig. 7D). Functionally, restoring RRM1 expression while introducing the USP19-K387 mutation in drug-resistant cells synergistically suppressed autophagy (Fig. 7E). Similarly, in 5-FU-sensitive colorectal cancer cells, RRM1 knockdown enhanced autophagic flux, which was partially rescued by USP19 overexpression, but not by the USP19 K387R mutant. Treatment with the autophagy inhibitor CQ further confirmed that RRM1-mediated USP19 stabilization suppresses autophagic activity in a K387-dependent manner (Fig. 7F-H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that RRM1 competitively binds to USP19 at the K387 domain via its E647/R648 residues, thereby blocking NEDD4-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of USP19.

Virtual screening identifies GAGGVGKSAL as a lead compound targeting the RRM1-USP19 axis to suppress tumor growth in vivo

To develop novel therapeutic strategies targeting the RRM1-USP19 signaling axis, this study integrated high-throughput virtual screening with both standard and ultra-precise molecular docking approaches to systematically assess the binding potential of compounds in a large-scale library toward this pathway. By comprehensively analyzing docking scores, binding modes, and drug-likeness profiles, five high-affinity candidate molecules were selected from an initial pool of 70 hits, and their binding stability was further validated through molecular docking simulations (Fig. 8A-F). In a nude mouse xenograft tumor model, the top three candidates were administered via intraperitoneal injection. Notably, the peptide GAGGVGKSAL exhibited robust anti-tumor activity, significantly suppressing tumor growth and enhancing the tumor inhibition rate (Fig. 8G, I). Immunohistochemical analysis further demonstrated that GAGGVGKSAL effectively restored USP19 expression levels in tumor tissues and markedly suppressed autophagic activity (Fig. 8H).

To dissect the underlying molecular mechanism, we performed Western blot analyses in both HCT116-resistant and Lovo-resistant cells (Fig. 8J). Consistent with our in vivo observations, treatment with GAGGVGKSAL recapitulated the effects of USP19 K387R mutation or NEDD4 knockdown, decreased the protein level of the autophagy substrate P62 and increased the ratio of the autophagosome-associated form LC3-II, indicating enhanced autophagic flux. Importantly, these regulatory effects were dependent on the integrity of the RRM1-USP19 axis. While overexpression of wild-type RRM1 partially reversed the modulation of P62 and LC3 induced by GAGGVGKSAL, the RRM1 E547A/R648A mutant—unable to interact with USP19—failed to rescue this phenotype. These findings confirm that the anti-tumor efficacy of GAGGVGKSAL likely stems from its specific modulation of the RRM1-USP19 interaction, thereby preserving the functional integrity of this signaling cascade. Owing to its potent biological activity and well-defined mechanism of action, GAGGVGKSAL exhibits strong druggability and represents a highly promising lead compound for targeted cancer therapy against the RRM1-USP19 axis.

Discussion

5-Fluorouracil and its prodrugs, such as capecitabine, have long served as the cornerstone of systemic chemotherapy for CRC [31]. However, the clinical efficacy of 5-FU is consistently limited by the development of acquired resistance [4]. Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated that nearly all patients who initially respond to treatment eventually experience disease progression during therapy [32]. This widespread emergence of resistance directly contributes to treatment failure and tumor recurrence, representing a major obstacle to improving patient outcomes in CRC. The development of 5-FU resistance is a multifactorial and multilayered biological process. Beyond the classical mechanisms involving overexpression or altered activity of thymidylate synthase (TS), resistance is also driven by dysregulated drug metabolism, evasion of apoptosis, enhanced DNA damage repair, and-more recently recognized-tumor microenvironment remodeling and activation of protective autophagy [9, 33]. This intricate regulatory network renders single-target interventions largely ineffective, underscoring the urgent need to systematically identify core resistance pathways and develop novel strategies to re-sensitize tumors.

Multi-omics integration analysis has emerged as a powerful approach for uncovering key molecular drivers in cancer [34]. In this study, non-targeted metabolomics revealed significant enrichment of the pyrimidine metabolism pathway in 5-FU-resistant tissues. Cross-validation with ONT transcriptomic data identified RRM1 as a central differentially expressed gene. While prior evidence suggests RRM1 functions as an oncogene in several cancers [22, 35], our findings in CRC reveal a paradoxical role: high RRM1 expression correlates with lymph node metastasis and poor histological differentiation, yet it is associated with improved survival outcomes. This apparent contradiction implies that RRM1’s function in CRC may be context-dependent, with its tumor-promoting effects potentially outweighed by its critical role in maintaining chemosensitivity.

The pivotal discovery of this study is the identification of RRM1 as a novel regulator of USP19 protein stability. The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) plays a central role in protein homeostasis and profoundly influences tumorigenesis, metastasis, and drug resistance [36, 37]. E3 ubiquitin ligases, which confer substrate specificity in ubiquitination, are particularly crucial in this process [38, 39]. Contrary to previous reports [40, 41], we demonstrate for the first time that USP19 itself can serve as a substrate for ubiquitination, revealing a previously unappreciated layer of complexity in ubiquitin-mediated regulation. Given that RRM1 lacks intrinsic E3 ligase activity, the identity of the E3 ligase responsible for targeting USP19 remained unknown. We provide experimental evidence that RRM1 directly interacts with USP19 through residues E647 and R648. This interaction does not alter USP19 transcription but instead exerts a steric blockade that competitively inhibits the E3 ligase NEDD4 from recognizing and ubiquitinating USP19 at lysine 387. NEDD4 is well-documented in mediating the degradation of multiple oncoproteins [42, 43], and recent work by Sushma Anand et al. links NEDD4 to 5-FU resistance via JNK signaling modulation [44], further corroborating our mechanistic model. Notably, this represents the first report that the stability of a deubiquitinating enzyme-USP19-is itself regulated by post-translational ubiquitination. Upon RRM1 downregulation, as observed in 5-FU-resistant cells, NEDD4 gains access to USP19, catalyzes its K387-linked polyubiquitination, and promotes proteasomal degradation. This finding significantly advances our understanding of the dynamic regulation within protein stability networks.

Functionally, USP19 degradation is tightly linked to autophagy activation. Previous studies indicate that USP19 modulates autophagic flux by stabilizing key regulators such as p62 [30]. Our results align with this: in RRM1-low resistant cells, reduced USP19 levels coincide with increased LC3-II/I ratio and p62 degradation, indicative of enhanced autophagic flux. As autophagy serves as a cytoprotective mechanism under metabolic and therapeutic stress, its hyperactivation is widely implicated in chemoresistance [45]. While Wu et al. reported RRM1’s involvement in SRSF1-mediated mitophagy and cisplatin resistance in bladder cancer [46], our study is the first to establish RRM1 as a direct regulator of canonical autophagy in colorectal cancer, highlighting its potential as a therapeutically targetable node. Thus, the RRM1-USP19 axis acts as a critical molecular switch governing cellular response to 5-FU by fine-tuning autophagic activity.

Leveraging this mechanistic insight, we performed structure-guided virtual screening and identified GAGGVGKSAL as a lead compound that specifically disrupts the RRM1-USP19 interaction interface. In vivo experiments demonstrated that GAGGVGKSAL significantly suppresses the growth of 5-FU-resistant tumors, validating the therapeutic feasibility of targeting this axis. These results offer a promising new strategy to overcome 5-FU resistance, aligning with the growing trend of developing small-molecule inhibitors against specific protein-protein interactions [47].

In summary, by integrating metabolomics and ONT-based transcriptomics, this study uncovers a central role for RRM1 in mediating 5-FU resistance in CRC. We show that RRM1 downregulation disrupts its protective interaction with the deubiquitinase USP19, enabling NEDD4-mediated K387 ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of USP19. This leads to unchecked autophagy activation and ultimately confers chemoresistance. The elucidation of the RRM1-USP19-NEDD4 regulatory axis provides a novel framework for understanding resistance mechanisms in CRC. Furthermore, our findings expand the functional repertoire of RRM1 beyond its canonical role in nucleotide metabolism, positioning it as a potential autophagy inhibitor and a compelling therapeutic target in colorectal cancer progression.

Nonetheless, this study has limitations. First, the downstream effectors through which the RRM1-USP19 complex regulates autophagy remain to be fully characterized. Second, the pharmacokinetic profile and potential toxicity of GAGGVGKSAL require comprehensive evaluation in advanced preclinical models. Finally, this study primarily focused on resistance to 5-FU and did not investigate the potential regulatory role of RRM1 in other commonly used chemotherapeutic agents within FOLFOX or FOLFIRI regimens, such as oxaliplatin and irinotecan. Since different chemotherapy drugs activate distinct cell death and resistance signaling pathways, whether RRM1 plays a universal role in mediating multidrug resistance remains to be further clarified. Future studies will broaden the range of drugs examined and conduct stratified clinical analyses to more comprehensively explore the broader regulatory functions of RRM1 in tumor chemoresistance.

Conclusion

Based on comprehensive multi-omics analyses and functional validation, this study demonstrates that RRM1 serves as a critical suppressor of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) resistance in colorectal cancer. Mechanistically, RRM1 directly interacts with USP19 via its E647 and R648 residues, competitively inhibiting the E3 ubiquitin ligase NEDD4 from recognizing and ubiquitinating USP19 at lysine 387. This competitive binding prevents USP19 polyubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation, thereby stabilizing USP19 protein levels. In 5-FU-resistant cells, downregulation of RRM1 disrupts this protective interaction, resulting in accelerated USP19 degradation, activation of autophagy, and ultimately chemoresistance. Furthermore, the lead compound GAGGVGKSAL, identified through virtual screening, effectively targets the RRM1-USP19 interface and significantly suppresses tumor growth in vivo, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent to overcome 5-FU resistance. These findings establish the RRM1-USP19 axis as a functionally significant pathway in CRC progression and provide a robust mechanistic foundation and translational framework for developing novel targeted therapies.
