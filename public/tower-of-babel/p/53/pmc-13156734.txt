External Validation and Recalibration of a Risk Calculator for Major Bleeding After Diagnostic Kidney Biopsy
Authors: Jordan Thorne, Victoria Lebedeva, Aran Thanamayooran, Tulayla Katmeh, Isaac Bai, Bright Huo, Geraint Berger, Leah E Cahill, Keigan More, David Clark, Amanda Vinson, Matthew A Weir, Andrew A House, Karthik Tennankore, Pavel S Roshanov

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveBleeding is a serious complication of diagnostic kidney biopsy. Kidney biopsy is an essential tool in the diagnosis and management of kidney disease.1,2 Although frequently performed, it is not without adverse effects. Bleeding remains the most common adverse effect; however, significant variability in reported bleeding rates is present in the literature, with most analyses reporting bleeding rates <10%.1,3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Although most bleeding events are minor, some kidney biopsies result in blood transfusion, surgical intervention, and/or death.3 As health care centers move toward performing outpatient kidney biopsies, it is useful to accurately identify those at highest risk for bleeding.

A risk assessment model (https://perioperativerisk.com/kbrc/) developed using data from 617 patients who underwent a kidney biopsy from 2012 to 2017 relies on 6 simple variables to predict major bleeding: age, preprocedural serum hemoglobin and platelets, body mass index (BMI), kidney length, and allograft or native kidney status.11 The model performed well in internal validation (C-statistic, 0.83) and later in temporal validation in 400 patients biopsied at the same center between 2018 and 2022 (C-statistic, 0.90).12 Thus far, external validation of this model has not been conducted. Therefore, our primary objective was to validate the existing risk model externally. Our secondary objective was to recalibrate the model on a combined dataset of all 3 cohorts and extend it to incorporate kidney function, if this improved predictive performance.

Methods

Cohort Generation

Our study included 3 separate cohorts. The derivation cohort11 included a total of 617 patients who underwent biopsy of a native or allograft kidney between 2012 and 2017 at the London Health Science Centre University Hospital, a 415-bed tertiary care center in Southwestern Ontario. This cohort was used for original model development and examined patient characteristics including age, platelet count, hemoglobin concentration, kidney size, BMI, severity of kidney dysfunction, coagulation status, the type of kidney (native or allograft), comorbid conditions, number of passes with the biopsy needle, and the indications for biopsy before establishing the risk prediction model using the 6 most clinically significant variables. The temporal validation cohort12 included 400 patients who underwent biopsy of a native or allograft kidney at the same center from 2018 to 2022 and was used for temporal internal validation of the prediction model.

The external validation cohort consisted of all patients who underwent inpatient or outpatient kidney biopsy from 2012 to 2019 at the QEII Health Sciences Centre, a 950-bed inpatient quaternary care center located in Halifax, NS, with a catchment population of approximately 750,000 individuals. Halifax is the sole transplant site for Atlantic Canada (population of approximately 2.6 million people); therefore, patients who were admitted for transplant from other provinces and required biopsy during their initial hospitalization were also included. Patients were identified using the Discharge Abstract Database for the QEII Health Sciences Centre, which includes diagnostic codes for outpatient and inpatient kidney biopsy procedures. Both diagnostic native kidney biopsies and allograft kidney biopsies were included; intraoperative allograft implantation biopsies and diagnostic biopsies for kidney masses were excluded from the analysis.

Patient characteristics were collected for each patient included in the validation cohort. Variables used in the derivation of the bleeding risk prediction model included age, weight, height, platelet count before biopsy, hemoglobin before biopsy, size of kidney on ultrasound in greatest dimension, and native or allograft kidney status. Laboratory results from ≤30 days before the procedure were included. Kidney size was collected from reports of ultrasounds performed before biopsy using radiologist-confirmed measurements. Medical records of patients in the validation cohort were reviewed to identify complications occurring up to 1 month after the biopsy. Bleeding events were defined in a similar fashion as in previous analyses.11,12 Specifically, major bleeding was defined as bleeding requiring transfusion, surgical intervention, embolization of the bleeding vessel, or death. All bleeding events were reviewed to exclude patients who required transfusion for non–biopsy-related bleeding events.

Clinical Practice

Kidney biopsy practices varied between sites. Patients included in the previous London analyses underwent biopsies performed by nephrologists under ultrasound-guidance. Preprocedural medication management included holding warfarin for 5 days and antiplatelets for 7 days. Neither desmopressin nor conjugated estrogen was routinely used. In all cases, the BARD Monopty 18-gauge, 16 cm disposable core biopsy instrument was used (C. R. Bard Inc). Operators and ultrasound technicians scanned the kidney after each pass to detect bleeding. Immediate post-biopsy hematomas were identified as a perinephric echogenic fluid collection. For patients with a post-biopsy hematoma, pressure was applied for 10 minutes followed by immediate repeat scanning. Those with ongoing bleeding underwent repeat ultrasound or computed tomographic angiogram after consultation with interventional radiology. Patients without any evidence of bleeding remained supine for 4 hours post procedure and were monitored for 5 hours, after which outpatients were discharged home. Inpatients were monitored on the ward, kept supine for 5 hours post procedure, and had a repeat complete blood count checked after the 4 hours. A hemoglobin decrease of greater than 10 g/L, symptomatic hypotension, or persistent pain would prompt an urgent repeat ultrasound. Gross hematuria in isolation did not instigate further investigation.

Patients in Halifax all underwent biopsies performed in an interventional radiology suite by an experienced interventional radiologist as per local QEII Health Sciences Centre procedure. Preprocedural administration of desmopressin was up to the discretion of the referring nephrologist. Preprocedural management of patient medications potentially impacting bleeding events (antiplatelets, antihypertensives, anticoagulation) was in conjunction with the referring nephrologist and consulting radiologist. Aspirin was discontinued 7 days before biopsy when possible, alternative antiplatelet agents were held 5 days before, and direct oral anticoagulants 3 to 4 days before biopsy based on the patient’s kidney function. Medications withheld before the procedure were resumed 24 hours after the procedure if there were no bleeding complications. Outpatients were monitored clinically in the interventional radiology suite. Standard monitoring consisted of 4 hours bed rest, followed by discharge if the patient was hemodynamically stable with no bleeding at puncture site. Inpatients were also mandated to 4 hours of bedrest; however, timing of repeat bloodwork and ultrasonographic imaging was at the discretion of the referring nephrologist. All biopsies in London, ON were performed with a 18-gauge needle. In Halifax, 16-gauge needles were used more commonly; however, needle gauge varied based on the proceduralist and was documented at time of use.

Statistical Analysis

External validation was performed using the original risk prediction model to estimate the probability of major bleeding for each patient in the validation cohort. Using the predicted probabilities, model performance was assessed for discrimination using the C-statistic and its 95% confidence interval (CI),13 calibration using a calibration slope and intercept with 95% CIs along with a calibration curve for visual assessment,14 and net benefit using decision curve analysis across a range of threshold probabilities from 0.01 to 0.15.15

All continuous predictors (age, kidney length, preprocedural hemoglobin, platelet count, and BMI) were modeled as continuous variables using restricted cubic splines with 3 knots (Table S1). Target kidney status was modeled as a binary variable (native vs allograft, reference = allograft). No variables were dichotomized or categorized, and no arbitrary cutoffs were applied.

Model recalibration was performed in a combined cohort formed from the derivation and validation cohorts. Model coefficients were refit using logistic regression on major bleeding. Recalibrated model discrimination, calibration, and net benefit were assessed. Internal validation was performed to estimate bias-corrected discrimination (C-statistic) and calibration (slope, intercept, and calibration curve) using 1,000 bootstrap samples drawn with replacement from the combined cohort dataset. For each sample, the recalibrated and extended models were refit using logistic regression on the outcome of major bleeding in the bootstrap sample. The model was then recalibrated using a bootstrap-based shrinkage factor to adjust the model coefficients as a safeguard against extreme predictions in new patients. The model extension used restricted cubic splines with 3 knots to model the association between serum creatinine and major bleeding risk.

Using the pmsampsize package16 in R, we calculated a required sample size of 1,682 patients. This estimate was based on the following parameters: expected C-statistic of 0.83 (as estimated in the original development study), 13 predictor parameters in the model, an expected shrinkage factor of 0.86, and a major bleeding event proportion of 0.028.

To account for patients with missing predictor data for model recalibration and extension, single stochastic conditional imputation of missing predictor data was performed using predictive mean matching in the combined dataset. This imputation model included major bleeding and all predictor variables.

Nova Scotia Health Research Ethics Board approval was received before validation cohort data collection. The London cohorts were generated as a quality improvement project, thereby meeting criteria for a waiver of review by the Research Ethics Board at Western University, London, ON, Canada.

External Validation and Model Recalibration

In the external validation cohort, the model showed good performance with an estimated C-statistic of 0.78 (95% CI, 0.69-0.85), calibration slope 0.72 (95% CI, 0.41-1.02), and calibration intercept 0.41 (95% CI, 0.02-0.80). The external validation calibration plot is shown in Fig 1. Decision curve analysis (Fig 2) suggested the model would be most useful for decision making in scenarios where the threshold of risk that would alter the decision to proceed to biopsy or how patients are monitored after biopsy is 5%-15%.

Model Extension

Model extension was performed with the addition of serum creatinine and did not show improved performance (estimated C-statistic, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.79-0.88]; calibration slope, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.47-1.17]; and calibration intercept, −0.47 [95% CI, −1.40 to 0.45]).

Subgroup Analysis

As antiplatelet agents were withheld prior to biopsy at both cohort sites in a similar manner, we also examined the impact of antiplatelet use on bleeding outcomes. Antiplatelet exposure was available for the development and external validation cohorts and was known for 1,328 patients (Table S2); however, it varied significantly across cohorts with available data (χ2 = 30.1, P < 0.001). Overall, major bleeding occurred in 14 of 374 (3.7%) patients receiving antiplatelet therapy and 26 of 954 (2.7%) patients not receiving antiplatelet therapy (Fisher exact test P = 0.372; OR, 1.39; 95% CI, 0.66-2.79). Model performance was assessed within both antiplatelet subgroups (Figs S1 and S2). A comparison of model performance showed a difference of −0.031 (95% CI, −0.17 to 0.09; P = 0.619), indicating no difference in discrimination between the subgroups.

Given differences in bleeding rates and needle bore size among the cohort sites, model performance was also assessed accounting for needle size. Table S2 demonstrates rates of needle bore use among cohorts (noting that 1 patient had a biopsy with a 20-gauge needle who was excluded from subgroup analysis). Overall, major bleeding occurred in 23 of 613 (3.8%) patients exposed to 16-gauge needles and 25 of 1,118 (2.2%) exposed to 18-gauge (Fisher exact test P = 0.091; OR, 1.70; 95% CI, 0.92-3.16). Model performance is shown in Figs S3 and S4; a comparison of model performance showed a difference of 0.005 (95% CI, −0.09 to 0.10; P = 0.915), indicating no difference in discrimination between the subgroups.

Discussion

In this study, we performed an external validation of a pre-existing risk prediction model for major bleeding after kidney biopsy. Model performance was good in external validation and improved with recalibration in the combined cohort. Our analysis did not show improved model performance with serum creatinine modeled as a continuous variable with restricted cubic splines. Furthermore, the addition of antiplatelet use before biopsy and the use of differing needle bore sizes did not further improve model performance. Although the use of desmopressin varied per site and was not consistently captured, the consistent performance of the original model through external calibration leads us to believe its inclusion in the model would not further improve performance. This is further re-emphasized by the fact it was used more commonly in the external validation cohort, which also had a relatively higher number of major bleeding events.

To our knowledge, this is the first externally validated kidney biopsy-associated risk model. Alternative risk scores have been developed for kidney biopsy bleeding risk prediction. A nationwide cohort study in France17 including administrative data from over 28,000 patients undergoing allograft biopsy found female sex, kidney function, heart failure, anemia, and acute kidney injury to be risk factors for major bleeding. The analysis generated a risk score ranging from 0 to 5 for major bleeding events and, although developed from allograft biopsies only, performed well on retrospective review of native kidney biopsies. High-risk patients with a score of 5 were found to have an estimated major bleeding rate of 19.2%. A separate analysis from New Mexico18 developed a risk prediction score using patient characteristics including hemoglobin <110 g/L (11 g/dL), platelets <150 × 109/L, urea >17.85 mmol/L (50 mg/dL), and chronic kidney disease morphology on ultrasound. This analysis included native kidney biopsies from 1,205 patients and resulted in a point-based scoring system to categorize patients into low-, moderate-, and high-risk groups, where expected major bleeding rate for high-risk patients was 26.1%. Both scores were generated and internally validated in cohorts with similar rates of major bleeding as those in our combined cohort; however, neither have undergone external validation.

This bleeding risk model can be used to inform postprocedural monitoring. A previous analysis12 used this model to assess the safety of shorter postprocedural monitoring time and has since resulted in a reduction from 4 hours to 2 hours of routine postprocedural monitoring for outpatient biopsies. Applying this model to typical patient characteristics in our combined cohort can give an example of common fluctuations in risk prediction between patients. For example, using the median age, hemoglobin, platelets, BMI, and kidney size from our combined cohort (Table 1), a ‘typical patient’ presenting for native kidney biopsy would have an assigned risk of major bleeding of 2.5%. That same patient, if presenting for allograft kidney biopsy, would have a 1% risk of major bleeding. As an additional example of a lower-risk patient compared with a higher-risk patient using the reference points in the forest plot (Fig 4), a 65-year-old patient requiring native kidney biopsy with the following parameters (10 cm kidney length, preprocedural hemoglobin 100 g/L, platelets 150 × 109/L, and BMI 24 kg/m2) has a major bleeding risk of 7.4%. In contrast, a 45-year-old patient presenting for allograft kidney biopsy with 12 cm kidney length, preprocedural hemoglobin 110 g/L, platelets 300 × 109/L, and BMI 30 kg/m2 has a major bleeding risk of 0.4%.

We included decision curve analyses (Figs 2 and 4) to demonstrate the potential utility of this model in clinical practice. After recalibration, the model appears to be of most use when the threshold for risk is <25%. This higher threshold means it can be applied for most circumstances when discussing risks and benefits with patients, taking into account their own preferences and values in addition to potential benefit from pursuing biopsy.

This study has several strengths including the large cohort size of allograft and native kidney biopsies and being the first to externally validate an existing model of post-biopsy bleeding risk. In addition, we assessed model performance under extension with serum creatinine, further demonstrating the reliability of the original model.

However, our work has limitations. Patient comorbid conditions were collected solely from clinical documentation, potentially limiting reliability. Although model extension was performed with a relatively large patient cohort, the precision of performance remained limited by the small number of major bleeding events. There remained some mild miscalibration in the probability range exceeding 25% risk where the model begins to overpredict risk, but this is likely of minimal clinical significance because patients carrying this degree of risk are typically not sent for biopsy.

Conclusions

A model to predict major bleeding associated with percutaneous kidney biopsies performed well in external validation and was improved further with minor recalibration. These results support the clinical use of this bleeding risk model to inform risk and benefit discussions with patients.
