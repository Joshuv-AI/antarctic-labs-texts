Research trends in readiness for hospital discharge between 2002 and 2021: A bibliometric analysis
Authors: Rong Zhang, Dongmei Wang, Ling Zhu, Yongdong He, Ling Cheng, Jifen Ma, Ting Zhang, Jun Zhang, Longti Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aimed to undertake a bibliometric analysis to provide comprehensive information in demonstrating the current status and outline the overall trends in the area of readiness for hospital discharge (RHD).DesignA bibliometric and visual analysis of RHD literature was undertaken.MethodsArticles were retrieved from the Web of Science network from 2002 to 2021. VOSviewer was used to identify the co‐authorship network of countries/institutions, co‐authorship and co‐citation analysis of authors, and co‐citation analysis of references and citation analysis of documents. CiteSpace was used to identify the keyword co‐occurrence network and perform cluster analysis, detecting the keywords with citation bursts and speculated frontiers in this research field.ResultsA total of 512 articles were included in the final analysis. Key findings are: (1) There has been a continuous but somewhat fluctuating rise in the number of publications. (2) 56.05% of publications come from the USA with Marquette University making the highest contribution. (3) Most publications (17, 3.32%) in RHD research were from the Journal of Clinical Nursing, and Anaesthesia and Analgesia had the highest number of citations (584 citations). (4) Weiss ME (25, 4.88%) was the most productive author whose articles have been the most highly cited (646 citations). (5) Cited references from Weiss ME (2007) also made the largest contribution to co‐citations, and the most cited reference was from Jack BW (2009) (1022 citations). (6) The 20 most frequent keywords and keywords with the strongest citation bursts were retrieved. There were seven research hotspots, and three emerging research frontiers were explored.ConclusionsThe bibliometric analysis of material published in the last 20 years indicates that there have been statistically significant gains in comprehensive information on RHD, including the knowledge mapping of the countries, institutions, authors, references and keywords. The hotspots and frontiers, which have been explored can give guidance to researchers as to new angles and directions to take.Implications for Nursing Management The effects of the implementation of ERAS on RHD‐related risk for adverse post‐discharge outcomes in surgical patients will be of increasing concern for healthcare professionals.It is important for patients and their relatives to be confident that on discharge they are sufficiently ready for hospital discharge to enable them to safely and smoothly make the transition to home.Improving the level of RHD in discharged patients can help nurse managers and researchers measure the effectiveness of discharge planning services.

INTRODUCTION

Readiness is defined by the Merriam‐Webster dictionary as ‘the quality or state of being ready: such as a state of preparation or prompt willingness’ (Dictionary, 2023). Readiness for hospital discharge (RHD): conceived of as both a state and a process for imminent discharge, forms a key part of hospital discharge planning and is an issue with international relevance in health care (Galvin et al., 2017). The concept of RHD was initially defined by Fenwick (1979), as ‘the feeling of being prepared to face reality perceived by the patient who were leaving the hospital, returning to family and society, and further recovering’ (Fenwick, 1979). Currently, the concept is well developed and continuously improving. According to Weiss and other colleagues, RHD refers to the judgement of patients before leaving hospital about their immediate state and abilities and their self‐perception about their own capabilities for self‐care management in the home setting (Weiss & Piacentine, 2006). According to the concept analysis of Galvin et al. (2017), there are four dimensions to RHD, these are (a) functional ability: being able to care for themselves at home, (b) the Having suitable support in place to help them deal with the many tasks needed after leaving the hospital, (c) being psychologically competent to manage the change process and (d) know and understand how to manage their own care and deal with minor problems that arise once they had left hospital (Galvin et al., 2017).

For patients to manage the switch from hospital to home it is essential that they are ready for discharge (Meng et al., 2020; Weiss & Lokken, 2009). Only 47.8% of patients thought that they had adequate preparation for discharge according to previous studies (Mabire et al., 2019), and making the switch from hospital to home posed problems for 20% of caregivers (Auger et al., 2014). Patients and family care givers describe things that prevented an effective discharge process. These included poor communication and lack of engagement, disjointed care, failure of supporting services to provide adequate healthcare and less than adequate support from community services (Piccenna et al., 2016). The transition process can lead to patient vulnerability and many people may suffer from a number of negative events and adverse emotions such as functional loss, pain, anxiety, hopelessness, stress, discouragement or loneliness. Poor discharge preparation in the multimorbid older adult may bring about problems with medication adherence, readmission or other problems after discharge (Forster et al., 2004; Jack et al., 2009; Pellet et al., 2020).

Shorter hospital stays and early discharge is a global phenomenon driven by economic considerations, Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) has been widely implemented all over the world with the resulting shorter hospital stays meaning that there is less discharge preparation time (Ljungqvist et al., 2017; Smith Jr. et al., 2020; Yin et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2014). A rushed discharge process can result in patients feeling that they are unready or improperly prepared for discharge. So rushing the discharge process can result in patients feeling unready or insufficiently ready to cope when they are leaving the hospital, which is likely to be associated with adverse events such as readmission (Weiss et al., 2010, 2007, 2017; Weiss & Piacentine, 2006), disruption in infant development, maternal stress and negative interactions between parents and infants (Hynan et al., 2013). Approximately 19% to 28% of patients have been identified as having experienced preventable adverse events after discharge according to recent studies (Forster et al., 2003, 2004; Rodakowski et al., 2017), and this has been associated with an increase in readmission risk of 6% to 9% (Weiss et al., 2014, 2020).

Bibliometrics uses statistical methods to analyse published academic literature enabling researchers to follow developments in specific research areas over an identified time span. It identifies how different publications individuals and institutions have affected, the identified research field and looks at the contributions of different countries and the patterns of authorship. This enables readers to ascertain the main areas needing research in the future within a specific field (Ma et al., 2020). In recent years, an increasing number of studies have focused on RHD. However, the overall status and research trends in RHD are unclear. Therefore, bibliometric analysis is needed to explore the RHD research field by clearly describing the state of research, showing hot spots and frontiers in this area. Therefore, we used the Web of Science (WoS) Core Collection database to extract relevant scientific publications from the past 20 years (from 2002 to 2021) and to conducted a bibliometric and visual analysis using VOSviewer and CiteSpace to provide comprehensive information in this research field.

METHODS

Data source and search strategy

All data published between 2002–2021 were retrieved from the WoS on January 28, 2022. The search terms were obtained from the MeSH database via PubMed and relevant references. We, therefore, searched the database with the following search strategy: TS = (‘readiness for hospital discharge’, OR ‘readiness for discharge’, OR ‘discharge readiness’, OR ‘discharge preparedness’, OR ‘discharge preparation’, OR ‘preparedness for hospital discharge’, OR ‘preparedness for discharge’). The inclusion document types were articles and review articles, while exclusion criteria were: papers reporting on the procedures of meetings abstracts, letters, editorial comments, corrections and news items. Additionally, unrelated articles were also excluded.

Data collection

Two authors independently searched and identified eligible publications. The total records were downloaded from the WoS and Microsoft Excel was then used to extract relevant material from the records. The following information was extracted from eligible articles: article title, names and nationalities of authors along with, their affiliations, year of publication, name of publishing journal, research area, keywords and abstract. Data extracting and screening was independently performed by 2 reviewers. Assistance from a third author was sought to discuss any discrepancies and resolve disagreements.

Data analysis

The CiteSpace and VOSviewer were used in the bibliometric analysis, where CiteSpace is a Java application that enables the visualization of bibliographic databases retrieved in the search and is considered a valid econometric analysis tool (Chen, 2014). CiteSpace (Version 5.8. R3, Drexel University, Chaomei Chen) was used to generate a keyword co‐occurrence network and to perform cluster analysis, detecting the citation bursts associated with different keywords and speculating on frontiers in this research field. The parameters of CiteSpace were as follows: time span (2002–2021), year of slice (1), selection criteria (Top 50), link retaining factor (LRF = 3), look back years (LBY = 5), e for top N (e = 1), pruning (Pathfinder). The statistically significant keywords with the strongest citation bursts are used to pick out the hotspots in specific fields and to suggest possible next steps and directions for research in the near future. VOSviewer (Version 1.6.17, Leiden University, van EckNJ) was used to explore the research networks in this study using co‐authorship analysis to compare different countries, institutions and authors, co‐citation analysis of authors, co‐citation analysis of references and citation analysis of documents.

DISCUSSION

This study was statistically significant because it provides a comprehensive overview of relevant research conducted in the area of RHD. Critical information identified from this bibliometric analysis provides a perspective on scientific progress in the field over the last 20 years. The hotspots and frontiers which have been explored can give guidance to researchers as to new angles and directions to take.

General trends in the RHD research field from 2002 to 2021

In recent times, the RHD has received increasing attention. The increasing number of publications is predicted to continue to grow over the next decade, suggesting that further research in this area is likely to be fruitful. The USA, Canada and Australia have been collaborating with various countries in the field. Marquette University was ranked the first in the co‐authorship analysis, suggesting its close cooperation with other institutions. From co‐authorship analysis of country and institution it is evident that international cooperation is becoming a popular mode of operation in this area.

Co‐citation analysis has identified the influential highly cited studies in this field. The Journal of Anaesthesia and Analgesia has the largest number of citations. This is unsurprising, because RHD was initially used to describe patients who had recovered sufficiently to enable them to be safely discharged after anaesthesia and ambulatory surgery (Korttila, 1991). Additionally, the results of these publications reflect the research area. RHD has received somewhat more attention in the nursing field compared to other areas.

Analysis of co‐authorship and co‐citation by authors was able to reveal influential authors in this field and possible areas for collaboration. Weiss ME, Yakusheva O, Bobay KL, Lerret SM, Sessler DI, Costa L, Pecorelli N, Ilfeld BM are identified as being among the top 10 most active and cited authors. Yakusheva O, Bobay KL and Costa L have always had a very close cooperative relationship with Weiss ME. These authors are active and influential in the field of discharge service. Weiss ME has conducted extensive research on discharge readiness assessment and the determination of its related influencing factors and outcomes, which makes a statistically significant contribution to guiding the application of theory of discharge services to practice (Bobay et al., 2018; Weiss et al., 2004, 2007, 2008, 2010, 2011, 2014, 2015, 2017, 2019, 2020; Weiss & Lokken, 2009; Weiss & Piacentine, 2006). Yakusheva O from the School of Nursing, University of Michigan has conducted in‐depth research into healthcare sciences & services. Yakusheva and team have found a relationship between the level of individual nurses engagement in discharge preparation and those patient's hospital readmission rates within 30 days (Yakusheva et al., 2019). This provides a strong evidence base for discharge service practice.

References are an important component of high‐quality papers, that not only provide a strong argument basis for the author's findings but also expand the information chain and reflect the scientific value of the research. Particularly, the co‐cited references analysed by the bibliometric method are a statistically significant indicator (Chen, 2014), which also reflects the scientific basis of a paper. It is noteworthy that Weiss's team have published the top four co‐cited references by verifying the psychometric properties of the Readiness for Hospital Discharge Scale (RHDS). This has provided the strongest predictor of adult medical‐surgical patients' views of their RHD and offered pre‐discharge scientific evidence for identifying patients at risk of readmission (Weiss et al., 2007, 2010, 2014; Weiss & Piacentine, 2006). The RHDS is the sole instrument measuring an individual's self‐perception of their ability to cope with discharge before leaving hospital, consisting of 23 items in four dimensions (personal status, knowledge, coping ability and expected support), and its excellent psychometric properties have been demonstrated in a combined sample of adult medical‐surgical patients, postpartum mothers and parents of hospitalized children. Highly cited articles show the areas of greatest interest in the field with the number of citations showing their significance. RHD has also been used as a secondary outcome, Jack BW et al. whose work has far more citations than others, has evaluated the effectiveness of a selection of discharge services aimed at reducing hospital readmission within 30 days of discharge (Jack et al., 2009). Similarly, as an outcome measure in clinical trials, the time to readiness for discharge has been used to test the effectiveness of care after colonic surgery, colorectal surgery, shoulder arthroplasty and abdominal surgery (Brueckmann et al., 2015; Carli et al., 2002; Challand et al., 2012; Ilfeld et al., 2006).

From analysis of the results, the presented trends deserve further attention. The most notably results we found were in some linkage, such as the most contributed countries/institutions, active authors, top co‐cited references and research areas. Weiss ME from Marquette University College of Nursing in USA is an outstanding scientist and Registered Nurse and a pioneer in this topic, with the highest number of publications and citations. Most of the top ten active authors and cited authors were from the United States, which explains, to some extent, why the majority of work has been done there. The top four co‐cited references we found published from 2007 to 2014, which were cited more frequently were all from Weiss's team. Authors such as Yakusheva O who have always had a very close cooperative relationship with her have contributed to the field of nursing. What is more, discharge planning service has a clear focus in nursing work, and patients' RHD should be given more attention by researchers and healthcare professionals. Therefore, it is not difficult to understand why in the result of this study the top well‐represented research area was Nursing.

Research hot spots and frontiers in the future

Research hot spots

Commonly used keywords, have often been utilized during the analysis process to identify hot issues in a research field. This study classified co‐occurring keywords into 9 clusters (small clusters were automatically filtered) (as shown in Figure 8), 7 of which revealed major hot spots in the field of RHD as follows.

Discharge criteria

Keywords ‘length of stay’, ‘criteria’, ‘surgery’ and the cluster ‘# 0 discharge criteria’ emphasized this research trend. There were many different professional members of the healthcare team engaged in discharge preparation and many different methods and assessment criteria for RHD were employed (Galvin et al., 2017). Members of the different professional groups focused on their own areas of responsibility with those professionals who majored on helping patients regain their functional capacity understandably seeing this as the most important issue in discharge preparation. Discharge criteria were set to improve patients' discharge readiness. 70 different sets of criteria to indicate RHD of patients after colorectal surgery have been described in a 156 paper systematic review (Fiore, Browning, et al., 2012). Masoom's team performed a retrospective cohort study which identified discharge criteria to determine children's hospital discharge readiness after adenotonsillectomies, including stable vital signs, fully oriented, alert, tolerant of sufficient oral intake, pain control and a minimum 4‐hour stay before discharge (Masoom et al., 2012). Patients' discharge readiness may be influenced by standards which aren't universally applied for discharge by doctors or other issues which in turn lead to adverse health outcomes. Additionally, as an outcome measure, the time to readiness for discharge enriched discharge criteria. Certain reports have noted that the time to readiness for discharge was used to validate the effectiveness of surgical therapy to help patients be safely discharge from the hospital (Brueckmann et al., 2015; Carli et al., 2002; Challand et al., 2012; Ilfeld et al., 2006).

Influencing factors

The summary of this hotspot comes from the cluster ‘# 2 influencing factors’. RHD and factors influencing this has generated an increasing number of papers over recent years. This has involved patients from a variety of different groups and many have been shown to be unready for discharge (Lau et al., 2016; Liu & Jiang, 2017; Weiss & Lokken, 2009; Weiss et al., 2017). The factors influencing of RHD included age, sex, marital and educational status (Kaya, Sain Guven, Aydan, et al., 2018; Lau et al., 2016; Xiong et al., 2021), length of stay (Kaya, Sain Guven, Aydan, et al., 2018; Lau et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2021; Xiong et al., 2021), depression (Lau et al., 2016; Xiong et al., 2021), quality of discharge teaching (Brent & Coffey, 2013; Mabire et al., 2019; Pellet et al., 2020; Seyedfarajollah et al., 2018; Weiss et al., 2007; Xiong et al., 2021), cognitive impairment (mild vs none) (Lau et al., 2016), comorbidity or persistent symptoms or disability (Cyrille & Patel, 2017; Lau et al., 2016; Metra et al., 2010; Schmocker et al., 2015), low satisfaction with healthcare services (Lau et al., 2016) and socioeconomic status (Schmocker et al., 2015). From the view of clinical professionals, Mabire C et al. and Yakusheva O et al. found that more experienced nurses and nurse productivity were associated with higher patient RHD (Mabire et al., 2019; Yakusheva et al., 2019). These findings provided meaningful implications for managers, indicating that human resource decisions need to consider the need for sufficient experienced nurses to be involved in discharge preparation.

Anaesthesia and ambulatory surgery

The cluster ‘# 4 postoperative pain management’ has a strong association with ‘# 3 anaesthesia and ambulatory surgery’. Common keywords ‘postoperative’, ‘anaesthesia’, ‘analgesia’ and ‘management’ were included in both these clusters. With the introduction of early discharge for ambulatory surgery the concept of RHD was used to assess when patients were sufficiently recovered from anaesthesia and ambulatory surgery to safely make the transition from hospital to home. Discharging patients who have had anaesthesia promptly and safely is crucial for the successful completion of ambulatory surgery (Korttila, 1991). The use of shorter acting anaesthetic agents plays an important part in achieving this and so plays a major part in the outcomes of the surgery and in RHD (Schwenk & Johnson, 2020). A review with high citations in this study found that continuous peripheral nerve blocks could improve both the surgical patient's RHD and the actual discharge procedure (Ilfeld, 2011). This has also been demonstrated by other studies (Edokpolo et al., 2019; Friedberg, 1993; Lacasse et al., 2011; Singh et al., 2020; YaDeau et al., 2018). The effectiveness of pain management for these patients after anaesthesia and ambulatory surgery also affected their discharge readiness to varying degrees.

RHD‐related risk for adverse post‐discharge outcomes

A small but important cluster is ‘# 5 predictor of risk for adverse post‐discharge outcomes’. Keywords ‘risk’, ‘outcome’ and ‘readmission’ also emphasized this research trend. RHD has been used to measure the outcomes of the hospital discharge procedure and to predict the likelihood of complications leading to readmission or ED visit (Weiss et al., 2011). Readmission to the hospital not only increased the healthcare costs, but also led to emotional and financial burdens for patients and their families, along with negative patient and caregiver experiences. Research estimated that 27% of readmissions were preventable (Rodakowski et al., 2017). Several studies have revealed that insufficient discharge readiness may lead to unnecessary patient outcomes such as readmission (Greysen et al., 2017; Meng et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2017; Weiss et al., 2007, 2010, 2017; Weiss & Piacentine, 2006), mortality (Halm et al., 2002; Lau et al., 2016) and emergency department visits (Bobay et al., 2010; Kaya, Sain Guven, Aydan, et al., 2018; Meng et al., 2020).

Populations with high risk for RHD issues and assessment

The cluster ‘# 6 assessment and target population’ shows the population characteristics of this research hotspot. Keywords ‘validity’, ‘scale’, ‘infant’, ‘mother’ and ‘older adult’ were helpful for emphasizing this research trend. The populations with high risk for RHD issues have gained extensive attention from researchers in the field of our study including parents of premature and low birth weight infants, puerperal patients, patients with chronic conditions (especially the elders), post‐surgical patients, people with cancer and their caregivers. Because some patients leaving hospital will have residual symptoms or disability of varying degrees insufficient discharge readiness may lead to unnecessary patient outcomes as mentioned above, the assessment of RHD is particularly important during transition from hospital to home recovery and care for patients' safety, satisfaction and positive outcomes (Weiss et al., 2007). An interactive patient centred discharge toolkit (PDTK) developed and designed by Fuller et al.(year)which is incorporated into the digital health record tools forming the Electronic Health records system (HER) enables patients to self‐assess and indicate to the team caring for them when they are ready for discharge (Fuller et al., 2020). This tool could identify at‐risk patients and help administer accurate interventions and is more clinically meaningful in understaffed units with insufficient nurses. A recent JAMA study recommended that structured assessment of patient RHD should be added to standards for discharge preparation to allow for patient specific interventions to reduce the risks of adverse outcomes (Weiss et al., 2019). Weiss et al. (Weiss & Piacentine, 2006), have developed the RHDS which is a general scale with different language versions (Kaya, Sain Guven, Teleş, et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2014; Salmani et al., 2020). Also, other specific more prevalent scales developed by researchers include the post‐anaesthetic discharge scoring score (Chung et al., 1995), Post Total Hip Replacement Discharge Scoring Scale (Wong & Wong, 1997), Perceived Readiness for Discharge after Birth Scale (Weiss et al., 2006), Post Colon Surgery Discharge Scoring Scale (Fiore, Bialocerkowski, et al., 2012) and the Neonatal Discharge Assessment Tool (Aykanat Girgin & Cimete, 2016).

Transitional care

The largest cluster ‘# 7 transitional care’ has the most extensive overlap and contains most of cluster 1 ‘# 1 readiness for hospital discharge’. Keywords ‘scare’, ‘transition care’, ‘hospital discharge’, ‘readiness’ and ‘quality of life’ contribute to this hot spot. As mentioned above, successful transition from hospital to home depends on patients actually being ready for discharge. Transitional care is described as ‘a set of actions designed to ensure the coordination and continuity of care as patients transfer between different locations or different levels of care within the same location’ (Boockvar & Vladeck, 2004; Spencer & Singh Punia, 2021). This includes discharge readiness assessment, patient education, discharge planning, ensuring patients have the necessary medication and booking the necessary appointments before discharge. One‐fifth of caregivers have reported that the transition from hospital to home has resulted in some problems (Auger et al., 2014) and during that period, patients' RHD has previously been noted as the most important factor affecting their ability to function followed by the quality of patient‐centred transitional care and the provision of healthcare services (Howard‐Anderson et al., 2014; Hu et al., 2020). The transition process puts patients in a very vulnerable state leading to the risk of adverse events and emotions. The discharging nurses are the people patients relate to immediately before they are formally discharged, and they need considerable knowledge and skills so that patients and caregivers can be assured of more possibilities to enable them to transition safely and smoothly.

Discharge planning and teaching

Keywords: ‘discharge planning’, ‘quality of teaching’, ‘education’ and ‘follow‐up’ were included in this cluster ‘# 8 discharge planning and teaching’. The intervention of discharge planning is an individualized ongoing, care and support program for a patient who is leaving hospital, with the aim of meeting the continuing care needs of patients and improving their health outcomes. Effective discharge planning which may prevent or ameliorate adverse events can be measured by RHD (Xiong et al., 2021). The IDEAL model of discharge planning developed by Luther B et al. provides a guide to assist in developing structure and planning steps including the process of assessing RHD (Luther et al., 2019). Studies focusing on discharge teaching provide evidence for the link between quality of pre‐discharge teaching and post‐discharge outcomes as such teaching boosts the level of RHD (Hua et al., 2020; Pellet et al., 2020; Seyedfarajollah et al., 2018). This is especially important for parents of premature or newborn infants and the elders. Nurses giving excellent discharge teaching make an important contribution to the smooth transition from hospital for these groups, with bedside teaching being rated as one of the most effective methods (Larsson et al., 2017; Smith et al., 2013; Stelfox et al., 2013). Recently, Pellet J and team explored the relationship between the context, hidden mechanisms and outcomes of discharge teaching by nurses (Pellet et al., 2020), which can be helpful for nurse managers and researchers who are seeking to measure the results of discharge teaching.

Frontiers in the future

The strongest citation burst keywords are identified as indicating emerging trends. The latest keywords may predict the frontiers of research as follows:

Individualized discharge teaching and communication

The latest keywords ‘discharge teaching’ (2017–2019), ‘communication’ (2017–2021), ‘health literacy’ (2019–2021) suggest this research trend. Readiness for discharge from hospital depends on good discharge teaching and sufficient communication is vital for this. There is no doubt that high‐quality discharge teaching conducted by nurses provides patients and caregivers with the skills and health literacy for optimally engaging in home care. The challenge here is that patients all have different characteristics, such as educational level, socioeconomic status and accompanying symptoms, so patient's discharge needs are very individualized and complicated. For example family and/or caregivers of patients in rural/regional locations will have difficulties in utilizing formal support services. Discharge teaching for some patients such as those having had a stroke needs to avoid information overload, even though this information is crucial for recovery. More attention needs to be given to tailoring it to meet the patient's needs.

RHD in early discharge patients

Keywords ‘system’ (2017–2021), ‘safety’ (2018–2021), ‘management’ (2019–2021) indicate this future trend. With the wide implementation of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS), made possible by developments in medical care technology the average hospital stay has been reduced leading to less discharge preparation time resulting in insufficient discharge readiness and more complex healthcare issues for patients and caregivers to deal with at home. The COVID‐19 pandemic has highlighted the issue of hospital acquired infection and as a result the practice of discharging patients on the day of surgery rather than admitting them and thus avoiding an infection risk is an increasingly common phenomenon. The RHD level is one of the most effective indicators of early hospital readmission (Braet et al., 2016; Weiss et al., 2014). The safety management of ERAS pathways in surgical patients has always given researchers statistically significant concern (Dalgatov et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2021).

In the future, study on the RHD of the patients discharged after surgery using the ERAS pathway will be of great significance in the reduction of adverse events caused by early discharge and the development of ERAS generally.

Infant and parent

The top keywords by the end of 2021 including ‘perception’ (2018–2021), ‘parent’ (2019–2021), ‘infant’ (2019–2021) may predict this frontier. The focus population has changed focus from older adult to preterm infant/infant and parent. Keywords with the strongest citation bursts have indicated that, the most popular subjects of RHD‐related articles and reviews from 2006 to 2007 mainly concentrate on older adults. From 2016 to 2021, however, there are also a statistically significant number of studies focusing on preterm infants/infants and parents. Research in recent years has focused on assessing RHD and exploring post‐discharge outcomes for this population (Salmani et al., 2020; Weiss et al., 2020). Hua W et al. demonstrated how quality of discharge teaching affects parental RHD which in turn affects parental self‐efficacy in parents of preterm infants (Hua et al., 2020).

The chief issues in a paper are expressed as Keywords, which can then be used to analyse the topic area. We have explored hot spots in this field by using the results of co‐occurring keywords and cluster analysis and have forecasted frontiers using the keywords with the highest citation bursts before 2021. We have identified that the top well‐represented area in research was nursing which means that RHD has been receiving more attention in this field. RHD which forms the central component of discharge planning is a relevant issue in health care worldwide, and RHD assessment is also an important part of transitional care. With the growing demand from medical services and the wide implementation of ERAS, the safety outcomes of discharged and post‐discharged post‐surgical patients, other patients discharged early or other populations with high risk of RHD issues, such as preterm infants, are the focus of healthcare work including follow‐up. Our findings clearly visualized the strong association and extensive overlap among the clusters such as cluster ‘# 7 transitional care’, ‘# 1 readiness for hospital discharge’, ‘# 8 discharge planning and teaching’ and ‘# 5 predictor of risk for adverse post‐discharge outcomes’. Thus, it is not difficult to see that the hotspots and frontiers detected in this study are consistent with the research coverage in the field of RHD.

Strengths and limitations

As far as we know, this study is the first to perform a bibliometric analysis and visual display of the RHD studies using comprehensive information which has provided proof of the body of knowledge including the co‐authorship network, co‐citation analysis and keywords with citation bursts. The software used for this review enabled analysis of the contributions to this field made by different countries, institutions, journals, references and authors and detection of hot topics. By its prediction of future trends this will be of interest to researchers over the coming years. Nevertheless, the study limitations should be noted. First, because of the limitations of the software, we only analysed articles and reviews in English from WoS. If we had been able to combine papers listed in other databases, for example PubMed and Scopus this could have strengthened our results. Second, the included papers published in recent years have been cited less frequently, so the co‐occurrence analysis relying on high‐frequency keywords may have a certain risk of bias.

CONCLUSION

Bibliometric tools were used to analyse articles focused on RHD found in WOSCC database from 2002 to 2021. Researchers may find this study gives useful information on potential collaborations among researchers and institutions, key references, research hotpots and frontiers and other important research content. Investigators may be able to use these findings to assess the current state of research in this area and to find new directions for future studies. Seven hot spots were explored in this field using the results of co‐occurring keywords and cluster analysis. Recent keyword bursts indicate that ‘individualized discharge teaching and communication’, ‘RHD in early discharge patients’, ‘infant and parent’ may be the latest research frontiers. These hotspots and frontiers can also help optimize discharge services‐related research. In the future, researchers and managers need to pay attention to the management element of their discharge service, so that discharge patients with specialized RHD needs get as much attention as possible. Moreover, multidisciplinary collaboration to improve the level of patient's RHD and high‐quality evidence‐based interventions to help discharged patients rehabilitate at home should be further explored.

IMPLICATIONS FOR NURSING MANAGEMENT

Our study provides a comprehensive overview of relevant research conducted in the area of RHD and contributes important information for demonstrating the current status and outlining the overall trends. Understanding the important role of RHD will be of value to healthcare professionals involved in discharging of patients from hospital and can play an important role in their education and transition. It is important for discharge patients and their relatives to be confident that on release they are sufficiently ready for hospital discharge to enable them to safely and smoothly make the transition to home. With the wide implementation of ERAS, the RHD‐related risk for adverse post‐discharge outcomes in surgical patients will give healthcare professionals more concern. Additionally, improving the degree of RHD in discharge patients can help nurse managers and researchers measure the effectiveness of discharge planning services.
