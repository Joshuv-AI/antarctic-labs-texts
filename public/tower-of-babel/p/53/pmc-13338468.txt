Five-Year Outcomes of Paclitaxel-Coated Balloons in Patients with Diabetes from the BIOLUX P-III Single-Arm Study
Authors: James Dodd, Gabrielle Stratford, Marianne Brodmann, Matej Moscovic, John Chaw Chian Wang, Giovanni Nano, Thomas Zeller, Johnny Kent Christensen, Gunnar Tepe, Bibombe Patrice Mwipatayi, On behalf of the BIOLUX P-III Global Registry, Don Robertson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeLong-term outcomes of paclitaxel-coated drug-coated balloons (DCBs) in patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) and infrainguinal peripheral arterial disease (PAD) are incompletely defined. In the current study, we evaluated 5-year outcomes in the diabetic cohort of BIOLUX P-III.Patients and MethodsBIOLUX P-III was a prospective, international, multicenter, post-market, single-arm study evaluating the Passeo-18 Lux DCB in routine practice. Between October 2014 and January 2017, 877 patients with infrainguinal lesions were treated at 47 centers, of whom 418 (47.7%) had DM. The primary clinical endpoint was 6-month freedom from major adverse events (MAEs), defined as freedom from procedure- or device-related death through 30 days, major target-limb amputation, or clinically driven target-lesion revascularization (CD-TLR). The primary performance endpoint was 12-month freedom from CD-TLR. Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods and the Log rank test.ResultsAmong 877 treated patients, 418 had DM. Follow-up completion was 81.6%, 86.4%, 82.3%, and 31.3% at 6, 12, 24, and 60 months, respectively. At 5 years, freedom from MAE was lower in patients with DM than in those without DM (71.2% vs 74.7%; P = 0.0359), whereas freedom from CD-TLR was similar between groups (79.8% vs 79.6%; P = 0.535). Patients with DM also had lower overall survival (56.3% vs 73.8%; P < 0.0001), lower freedom from major target-limb amputation (92.4% vs 98.8%; P < 0.0001), and lower amputation-free survival (53.0% vs 73.6%; P < 0.0001).ConclusionIn this real-world registry, the Passeo-18 Lux DCB was associated with durable freedom from reintervention through 5 years, with similar CD-TLR rates in patients with and without DM. Patients with DM had poorer long-term survival and limb outcomes. However, these findings should be interpreted in the context of baseline between-group differences and limited 60-month follow-up (ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT02276313).

Introduction

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is a common manifestation of systemic atherosclerosis and an increasingly important global cause of disability, limb loss, and cardiovascular mortality.1,2 Its burden continues to rise worldwide, driven by population aging and the increasing prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM), chronic kidney disease (CKD), hypertension, and other cardiometabolic risk factors.1,2 Beyond its direct effects on walking capacity, wound healing, and quality of life, PAD also reflects diffuse vascular disease and is associated with a substantially increased risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and death.1,2 Hence, treatment strategies for infrainguinal PAD should be considered not only in terms of lesion durability but also in relation to long-term patient outcomes.

Endovascular therapy is now widely used for infrainguinal PAD; however, the optimal revascularization strategy remains unclear, particularly in clinically high-risk subgroups. Drug-coated balloons (DCBs) have emerged as an attractive “leave-nothing-behind” approach, as they deliver an antiproliferative agent locally without requiring a permanent metallic scaffold. By reducing neointimal hyperplasia and restenosis, DCBs have been associated with lower rates of recurrent stenosis, target-lesion revascularization, and loss of patency than with conventional balloon angioplasty across a range of femoropopliteal settings.3 This approach is particularly appealing when stent implantation is undesirable because of lesion length, vessel mobility, fracture risk, future treatment options, or the likelihood of repeat intervention.

Despite these advantages, the long-term safety of paclitaxel-coated devices remains a subject of debate. Katsanos and colleagues first raised concerns about a possible late mortality signal in a meta-analysis of randomized trials,4 prompting intense scrutiny from clinicians, regulators, and professional societies. Subsequent patient-level meta-analyses and more recent systematic reviews have yielded conflicting results,5,6 and the issue remain unresolved. More recently, the SWEDEPAD randomized trials reported increased 5-year mortality in patients with intermittent claudication treated with paclitaxel-coated devices. In contrast, no such signal was observed in patients with chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI).7,8 These findings highlight the need for cautious interpretation of long-term outcomes and emphasize the importance of disease severity, patient selection, and clinical context when evaluating paclitaxel-based technologies.

DM is particularly important in this context. It is a major risk factor for PAD and is associated with more diffuse, calcified, and distal arterial disease, more frequent infrapopliteal involvement, impaired endothelial and microvascular function, delayed wound healing, and a markedly higher risk of limb loss and cardiovascular events.9,10 Patients with DM often present later in the disease course, frequently with tissue loss or CLTI, and their outcomes after revascularization are influenced not only by lesion-level treatment efficacy but also by the broader burden of systemic comorbidity.9,10 In diabetic PAD, durable reductions in restenosis or repeat revascularization may therefore not translate into equivalent gains in limb preservation or survival. This distinction between lesion-level efficacy and patient-level outcome is central to interpreting long-term studies in this population.

DCB therapy may offer advantages in patients with DM, in whom complex anatomy and small-vessel disease often make permanent implants less desirable. Meanwhile, concerns have been raised that paclitaxel-based technology could adversely affect wound healing or limb outcomes in this high-risk group, particularly in those with advanced tissue loss or distal disease.11 Although previous studies suggest that DCB treatment in diabetic PAD can be safe and effective, the available evidence is limited by relatively short follow-up, selected populations, or post hoc subgroup analyses.11–13 Long-term real-world data are thus needed to evaluate both durability and clinical outcomes in patients with DM treated with paclitaxel-coated balloons.

BIOLUX P-III was established as a large, prospective, international, post-market registry to assess the safety and effectiveness of the Passeo-18 Lux DCB in routine clinical practice. Previous analyses from BIOLUX P-III reported favorable shorter-term outcomes in patients with DM,12 but longer-term data remain limited. Other large studies have also reported 5-year outcomes in selected diabetic or CLTI cohorts treated with paclitaxel-coated devices,13 yet additional real-world evidence is needed to better define the long-term clinical profile of this strategy in diabetic PAD. Accordingly, the current study reports the 5-year outcomes of the diabetic cohort within BIOLUX P-III, with particular emphasis on reintervention, limb outcomes, and survival.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

BIOLUX P-III was a prospective, international, multicenter, post-market, all-comers registry evaluating the clinical performance of the Passeo-18 Lux DCB in the treatment of infrainguinal atherosclerotic disease. Between October 2014 and January 2017, 882 patients were enrolled at 47 centers worldwide; 877 underwent treatment with the Passeo-18 Lux DCB. Follow-up assessments were scheduled at 6, 12, 24, and 60 months after the index procedure.

The inclusion and exclusion criteria have been reported previously.12 All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice guidelines, and applicable regulatory requirements, and was approved by the independent ethics committee or institutional review board at each participating center. Endpoint data were monitored in at least 25% of the patients. All major adverse events (MAEs), target-lesion revascularizations (TLRs), and deaths were adjudicated by an independent Clinical Events Committee. The study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02276313) prior to initiation. For this exploratory analysis, patients were stratified by investigator-reported diabetes status (DM vs non-DM).

Study Device

The Passeo-18 Lux DCB (Biotronik AG, Bülach, Switzerland) is a percutaneous transluminal angioplasty balloon catheter available in diameters from 2.0 to 7.0 mm and lengths from 40 to 200 mm. Its balloon surface is coated with paclitaxel at 3 μg/mm2 and incorporates the hydrophobic excipient butyryl-tri-hexyl citrate to facilitate drug transfer, enhance tissue uptake, and stabilize paclitaxel delivery to the target lesion.

The device has a protective sheath to minimize drug loss during handling and optimize transfer of the active agent to the arterial wall. It was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions and at the operator’s discretion, with no limit on the number of lesions treated per patient.

End Points and Definitions

The primary clinical endpoint was freedom from MAE at 6 months, defined as the absence of procedure- or device-related death within 30 days, major target-limb amputation (above the ankle), or clinically driven TLR (CD-TLR) within 6 months. The primary performance endpoint was freedom from CD-TLR at 12 months, defined as repeat intervention for at least 50% diameter stenosis of the target lesion, assessed visually, in the presence of recurrent clinical symptoms. All primary endpoints were independently adjudicated.

Secondary endpoints included overall survival; freedom from CD-TLR and clinically driven target-vessel revascularization at 6, 12, 24, and 60 months; and primary patency at 12 and 24 months. Primary patency was defined as freedom from more than 50% restenosis in the target lesion, determined using duplex ultrasonography (peak systolic velocity ratio >2.5) or angiography, without clinically driven repeat intervention. Additional secondary outcomes were freedom from MAEs at 12, 24, and 60 months; changes in ankle–brachial index (ABI) or, when ABI was unreliable, thigh–brachial or toe–brachial index; cause of death; improvement in Rutherford classification; amputation-free survival; and patient-reported outcomes assessed using a standardized pain scale and the Walking Impairment Questionnaire.

Coronary artery disease was defined as documented physician-diagnosed coronary artery disease in the medical record, including previous myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization, or established obstructive coronary disease. Consistent with the registry design, coronary artery disease status was determined by baseline clinical documentation and investigator-reported comorbidity data rather than by protocol-mandated cardiac testing.14,15

Hypertension was defined as documented physician-diagnosed hypertension, office systolic blood pressure of at least 140 mm Hg, office diastolic blood pressure of at least 90 mm Hg, or current use of antihypertensive medication, in accordance with established criteria.16,17 DM was defined as documented physician-diagnosed DM, fasting plasma glucose of at least 7.0 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin level of ≥ 6.5%, 2-h plasma glucose level of ≥ 11.1 mmol/L during oral glucose-tolerance testing, or current use of glucose-lowering therapy.18,19 In this registry, diabetes status was determined from baseline medical-record review and treatment status rather than protocol-driven biochemical reassessment.18,19

Renal insufficiency was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, indicating at least moderately reduced kidney function and aligning with KDIGO definitions for kidney disease classification.20 Given that this was an international multicenter registry, the specific creatinine-based equation used to calculate eGFR was not uniformly captured across sites. Hence, renal impairment was defined as eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 per the registry threshold. Under current practice, eGFR would typically be estimated using the 2021 race-free CKD-EPI creatinine equation described by Inker and colleagues and recommended in National Kidney Foundation–American Society of Nephrology Task Force guidance.20,21

Vascular calcification was assessed from angiographic appearance at the index procedure and recorded qualitatively by the treating investigator. Hence, as in routine registry practice, calcification severity was based on site-reported lesion assessment rather than core-laboratory adjudication or a validated calcium-scoring system; this limitation is acknowledged in the manuscript.22,23

Statistical Analysis

BIOLUX P-III was a prospective, real-world, post-market registry, and no formal sample-size calculation was performed for this subgroup analysis. Analyses were performed in both the intent-to-treat and as-treated populations; unless otherwise specified, this report is based on the intent-to-treat cohort.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts (percentages). Distributional assumptions were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test, as appropriate. Paired longitudinal comparisons were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Time-to-event outcomes, including freedom from CD-TLR, MAEs and their individual components, overall survival, freedom from major target-limb amputation, and amputation-free survival, were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the Log rank test. Standard errors for Kaplan–Meier estimates were calculated using Greenwood’s formula, and proportional-hazards assumptions were assessed graphically.

Missing data were handled according to the analytic context. In selected cross-sectional analyses, pairwise deletion was used so that each comparison included all available observations for the variables of interest. No formal multiple-imputation approach was applied, as most missingness at later time points reflected incomplete follow-up rather than isolated missing covariates, and the registry dataset lacked sufficient auxiliary information to support a robust prespecified imputation model. For time-to-event outcomes, patients without a recorded event were censored at their last known event-free follow-up, consistent with standard Kaplan–Meier methodology. Follow-up completion declined substantially by 60 months; therefore, the longest-term event estimates should be interpreted cautiously. This is particularly relevant because Kaplan–Meier methods assume non-informative censoring, an assumption that may not be fully satisfied in a registry setting with substantial late attrition. Accordingly, informative censoring cannot be excluded.

No multivariable adjustment, propensity-based analysis, or competing-risk modeling was performed. Comparisons between patients with and without DM should thus be considered descriptive and exploratory. Given the marked baseline differences between groups, these analyses do not support causal inference about the independent effect of DM on long-term outcomes.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.1.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Compared with patients without DM, those with DM exhibited a more complex disease phenotype. They had a significantly higher prevalence of infrapopliteal (crural) disease (22.8% vs 11.8%; P = 0.0007) and heavy calcification (17.9% vs 13.2%) and were more likely to have prior ipsilateral minor amputation (11.6% vs 1.1%) or active ulceration (37.6% vs 17.8%; both P < 0.0001). Lesion length and diameter stenosis were comparable between groups.

Procedural characteristics reflected consistently high technical success (Table 3). The Passeo-18 Lux device was successfully delivered, inflated, deflated, and retrieved in nearly all cases (DM: 99.7%, non-DM: 99.9%). Patients with DM were treated with smaller-diameter balloons (mean 4.5 ± 1.2 mm vs 4.8 ± 1.0 mm; P < 0.0001) and therefore received a lower cumulative paclitaxel dose (7.1 ± 5.5 mg vs 7.7 ± 5.7 mg; P = 0.0007). Mean cumulative inflation time was also shorter in the DM group (139 ± 60s vs 151 ± 63s; P < 0.001). Clinical Outcomes

Major Adverse Events

At 5 years, freedom from MAEs remained high in both cohorts but was significantly lower among patients with DM. Kaplan–Meier estimates of MAE-free survival in the DM group were 85.0% (95% CI: 81.0–88.2) at 12 months, 80.0% (95% CI: 75.6–83.8) at 24 months, and 71.2% (95% CI: 64.2–77.1) at 60 months, compared with 91.0% (95% CI: 87.8–93.3), 86.0% (95% CI: 82.3–89.0), and 74.7% (95% CI: 68.3–80.0) in those without DM (P = 0.0359; Figure 2, Supplementary Table 1). When stratified by lesion location, CD-TLR–free survival followed similar trajectories in femoropopliteal and below-the-knee (BTK) lesions across both cohorts. At 60 months, freedom from CD-TLR was 80.0% (95% CI: 75.4–83.8) for femoropopliteal lesions and 70.5% (95% CI: 57.5–80.1) for BTK lesions. Within each anatomical subgroup, outcomes were comparable between patients with and without DM, suggesting no effect modification by diabetes status despite higher absolute reintervention rates in BTK disease.

Overall Survival

Overall survival diverged between cohorts over time (Supplementary Figure 1, Supplementary Table 3). At 60 months, survival was 56.3% (95% CI: 48.4–63.5) in patients with DM versus 73.8% (95% CI: 67.1–79.3) in those without DM (P < 0.0001). Among patients with DM, cardiac causes accounted for 32.7% of deaths and non-cardiovascular causes for 30.7%; however, the cause of death was undocumented in nearly one-third of cases (Supplementary Table 4). This pattern was consistent with the greater systemic disease burden in the DM subgroup.

Limb Outcomes

Limb preservation remained high overall but was significantly lower in patients with DM (Figure 4, Supplementary Table 5). Freedom from major target-limb amputation was 94.6% (95% CI: 91.9–96.4) at 12 months, 94.0% (95% CI: 91.1–95.9) at 24 months, and 92.4% (95% CI: 88.3–95.1) at 60 months in the DM group, compared with 99.1% (95% CI: 97.6–99.7), 98.8% (95% CI: 97.2–99.5), and 98.8% (95% CI: 97.2–99.5), respectively, in the non-DM group (P < 0.0001).

Amputation-free survival at 5 years was also markedly lower in patients with DM (53.0%; 95% CI: 45.4–60.1) than in those without DM (73.6%; 95% CI: 67.0–79.1; P < 0.0001; Figure 5, Supplementary Table 6). Most amputations occurred within the first 6 months following the index procedure, underscoring the need for early post-procedure surveillance, wound optimization, and multidisciplinary follow-up in this high-risk group. Discussion

This analysis extends the existing BIOLUX P-III reports by providing 5-year outcomes in patients with DM treated with the Passeo-18 Lux DCB in routine practice. In this real-world cohort, treatment was associated with durable lesion-level efficacy over 5 years, with similar CD-TLR rates in patients with and without DM. In contrast, patients with DM had poorer long-term survival and limb-related outcomes, including lower freedom from MAEs, major target-limb amputation, and amputation-free survival. These findings are clinically relevant but should be interpreted as observational rather than causal, given the single-arm registry design, lack of adjusted analyses, and substantial baseline differences between groups.

Patients with DM represent a particularly high-risk subgroup within the broader population with PAD. They often present with more diffuse, multilevel, and calcified disease, including infrapopliteal involvement, impaired runoff, endothelial dysfunction, heightened inflammatory and thrombotic activity, and delayed wound healing.24–26 They also have a greater burden of CLTI, renal dysfunction, coronary artery disease, and frailty, all of which adversely influence limb prognosis and survival.24–26 Although surgical bypass remains preferred for selected patients with CLTI who have a suitable conduit and an acceptable operative risk, many patients with DM are not ideal surgical candidates.24,26 In that context, an endovascular-first approach remains common, and the present data suggest that the Passeo-18 Lux DCB was associated with durable lesion-level performance even in a clinically complex diabetic cohort.

Freedom from CD-TLR remained high and broadly similar in patients with and without DM throughout follow-up. This finding is consistent with prior studies demonstrating durable lesion-level efficacy of DCB treatment in PAD,3,12 and should also be considered in the context of the previously published 24-month outcomes of the full BIOLUX P-III Global Registry and the final 5-year results of the IN.PACT Global Study, both of which reported sustained lesion-level efficacy after paclitaxel-coated balloon angioplasty in real-world PAD populations.27,28 Taken together, these data support the interpretation that the preserved freedom from repeat target-lesion revascularisation observed in the present analysis is compatible with a durable local treatment effect, despite the higher-risk clinical profile and greater anatomical complexity of the diabetic cohort. From a clinical perspective, sustained suppression of repeat revascularisation is particularly relevant in patients with DM, in whom systemic comorbidity may complicate recovery and limit future treatment options.29 However, because these analyses are unadjusted and the diabetic and non-diabetic cohorts differed substantially at baseline, the similar CD-TLR rates observed should not be construed as evidence of equivalent treatment effect across groups.

The poorer long-term survival and limb outcomes in patients with DM are more appropriately explained by their greater baseline burden of CLTI, tissue loss, renal insufficiency, coronary artery disease, ulceration, and prior amputation than by a device-specific effect.30 The pattern observed in this study is notable: lesion-level outcomes were similar, whereas patient-level survival and limb outcomes were substantially worse in the DM cohort. This suggests that adverse long-term outcomes were not driven solely by failure of the treated lesion, but more likely reflect the broader consequences of advanced atherosclerotic disease, microvascular dysfunction, impaired wound healing, infection risk, and competing cardiovascular and renal comorbidity. Nevertheless, these interpretations remain inferential, and this registry cannot establish causality.

The clustering of major amputations within the first 6 months after the index procedure identifies a particularly vulnerable period in the diabetic population. This is clinically plausible in a cohort enriched for CLTI, ulceration, prior amputation, and renal impairment. It underscores the importance of early post-procedural surveillance, meticulous wound care, prompt treatment of infections, optimization of glycemic control, and close multidisciplinary follow-up involving vascular specialists, diabetologists, podiatrists, wound-care teams, and primary care clinicians.31,32 In this setting, durable treatment of the index lesion alone may be insufficient if the broader determinants of tissue healing and cardiometabolic risk are not addressed simultaneously.

The relationship between paclitaxel-coated devices and long-term mortality remains controversial and should be interpreted cautiously in the current study. Concerns have been raised that paclitaxel-based technologies may contribute to distal embolization or other adverse biological effects that could influence limb or survival outcomes.33–37 These mechanisms are biologically plausible, particularly in a diabetic population with advanced distal and microvascular disease, but they remain hypotheses and cannot be tested directly in this registry. Patients with DM received a lower mean cumulative paclitaxel dose than those without DM; however, this should not be interpreted as disproving a potential device-related effect. Rather, it suggests that the observed differences in limb outcomes cannot be explained simply by greater drug exposure in the diabetic cohort.

All-cause mortality was higher among patients with DM, and this divergence widened over 5 years. This pattern is consistent with the well-recognized excess burden of cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic risk in DM.38 Meanwhile, BIOLUX P-III was not designed to assess the long-term safety of paclitaxel-coated devices and lacked a non-paclitaxel comparator arm. Accordingly, the results of the current study neither confirm nor exclude a device-related contribution to late mortality. Comparisons with SWEDEPAD and related studies are therefore only contextual, as the study designs, patient populations, lesion profiles, and treatment strategies differ substantially.7,8 Cardiac causes constituted the largest documented category of death in the diabetic cohort, but cause-of-death data were incomplete, limiting confidence in any cause-specific interpretation.

These findings carry important clinical implications. In high-risk diabetic PAD, lesion-level efficacy and patient outcomes should not be conflated. A device may provide durable freedom from reintervention while overall survival and limb preservation remain strongly influenced by baseline disease severity, infection risk, renal dysfunction, and cardiovascular comorbidity. Hence, the results of this study support the integration of endovascular revascularization with aggressive cardiovascular risk reduction, optimized glycemic management, structured wound surveillance, infection control, and coordinated limb-preservation pathways. This approach may be particularly important during the early post-procedural period, when amputation risk appears greatest.

Certain limitations of this study merit acknowledgement. As a registry-based analysis, BIOLUX P-III is inherently vulnerable to selection bias and residual confounding. The absence of multivariable adjustment, propensity-based analyses, and competing-risk modelling limits the strength of inference for comparisons between diabetic and non-diabetic cohorts. Follow-up completion declined substantially by 60 months, raising the possibility of informative censoring and limiting confidence in the most prolonged estimates. Cause-of-death data were incomplete, thereby restricting interpretation of cause-specific mortality. The study also lacked a non-paclitaxel comparator and therefore cannot conclusively address paclitaxel safety. In addition, important diabetes-specific variables, including glycemic control, duration of diabetes, and treatment modality, were not captured, although each is likely to influence vascular prognosis and long-term limb outcomes.31,32 Their absence limits characterization of diabetes severity and increases the possibility of residual confounding. The creatinine-based equation used to derive estimated glomerular filtration rate was not applied uniformly across centers; accordingly, renal insufficiency was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Vascular calcification was assessed qualitatively by treating investigators on angiographic appearance and was neither standardized by a validated scoring system nor adjudicated by an independent core laboratory. Finally, the absence of independent core-laboratory review of angiographic or duplex imaging may have introduced additional variability in lesion assessment and outcome classification.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable long-term real-world data in an understudied, clinically high-risk population. The findings suggest that treatment with the Passeo-18 Lux DCB was associated with durable lesion-level efficacy through 5 years while also underscoring the less favorable long-term clinical trajectory of patients with DM. Together, these observations indicate that although lesion-level outcomes appeared durable, patient-level survival and limb outcomes remained strongly influenced by baseline comorbidity burden and disease severity, highlighting the need for adjusted comparative analyses, more complete follow-up, and integrated strategies to improve both limb and survival outcomes.

Conclusions

In this prospective real-world registry, treatment with the Passeo-18 Lux DCB was associated with durable lesion-level efficacy through 5 years, with similar CD-TLR rates in patients with and without DM. Patients with DM had poorer long-term survival and limb outcomes; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given greater baseline disease severity, the absence of adjusted analyses, and limited 60-month follow-up.
