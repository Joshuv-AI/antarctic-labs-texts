DNA Circles as Vehicles for Genes to Evade Chromosomal Discipline
Authors: Monica Rojas-Triana, Jacobus J Boomsma, Birgitte Regenberg, Angela Oliverio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this Perspective article, we follow the journey of a gene that breaks free from its stringent chromosomal transmission dynamics to become an independently multiplying agent on so-called extrachromosomal circular DNA (ecDNA/eccDNA). We discuss how the release of a gene from its chromosomal anchor can affect its dosage, regulatory context, and potential evolutionary trajectory before examining the phenotypic implications for unicellular and multicellular eukaryotes that transmit genes on circular DNA. We also briefly explore the fundamental difference between circularized genes in flowering plants (angiosperms) and mammals (primarily cancer) concerning somatic and germline inheritance.Keywords: circularized gene, double minute, extrachromosomal DNA, genome evolution, herbicide resistance, innate system

For the past century, the prevailing paradigm has been that eukaryotic genes are inherited only on chromosomes, which ensure preservation of gene dosage and enforce regulatory control. We challenge this view by arguing that genes can also escape onto circular DNA, providing an alternative pathway for gene inheritance that bypasses the chromosomal constraints. We list evidence that the extrachromosomal inheritance of genes will occasionally enable large phenotypic effects and drive rapid evolutionary changes, which may have large effects for our understanding of evolution.

Introduction

The relationship between a focal gene and its chromosomal embedding is a fundamental aspect of prokaryotic and eukaryotic cellular life, determining how the gene is maintained across generations, how it interacts with other genes and the nucleoplasm, and how its evolutionary change will affect the phenotype. The chromosomal organization determines a gene's position along the DNA molecule and its placement within the chromatin environment, which includes euchromatin (active DNA), heterochromatin (silenced DNA), and specialized structures such as centromeres and telomeres. This organization both enables and constrains a gene's expression, its replication during mitotic cell division, and, in eukaryotes, its intergenerational transmission via chromosomal segregation after meiosis.

However, the same features that structurally determine a gene's position on a chromosome also constrain its amplification potential. The well-known selfish gene metaphor (Dawkins 1982) made us aware that any gene is fundamentally self-preserving, so that (multi)cellular life can only be stable by imposing stringent chromosomal transmission dynamics. This discipline is necessary to secure faithful co-transmission during meiosis and suppress outlaw genes that bias their transmission by the so-called “parliament of genes” (Leigh 1977; Ågren and Clark 2018). It is this superb replicational discipline that allowed organizational complex phenotypes to evolve, be maintained in a highly competitive world, and adaptively diversify into many lineages. Among the evolved constraint mechanisms enabling such transmissional robustness, we outline three principal categories: (i) the enforcement of gene dosage stability through fixed degrees of ploidy; (ii) the regulatory environment imposed by chromatin and nuclear organization; and (iii) the long-term positional effects, ie all aspects relating to synteny (gene order), that limit the extent to which genes can pursue independent evolutionary trajectories. We use this context to explore the transmission dynamics of genes that become excised from their chromosomes to form extrachromosomal circular DNA molecules (Table 1). Such acquisition of independent existence in the nucleoplasm releases circularized genes (Table 1) from these fundamental constraints.

Until just a decade ago, this form of gene independence was essentially unknown or considered to be a mere fringe phenomenon. However, we now have consistent evidence that circular DNA (Table 1) can be found in animals, fungi, and plants (Møller et al. 2015, 2018a, 2020; Wang et al. 2021; Ni et al. 2025), spanning sizes from only a few dozen base pairs to several megabases, with many circles large enough to carry full genes and their cis-regulatory elements (Shibata et al. 2012; Koo et al. 2018; Kim et al. 2020; Koche et al. 2020). Current evidence suggests that such circles originate when chromosomal DNA breaks are occasionally mis-repaired by homologous- and nonhomologous DNA repair pathways, as reviewed in detail by ourselves and others (Wu et al. 2022; Eugen-Olsen et al. 2025). DNA damage from endogenous cellular processes, such as high transcription, or from exposure to exogenous agents, has been consistently linked to increased DNA circularization (Møller et al. 2018b; Hull et al. 2019; Paulsen et al. 2021; Zhuang et al. 2024; Liang et al. 2025). In addition, circular DNA is now known to be widespread and abundant across all tested eukaryotes, yet the frequency with which new circles form remains largely unexplored. Both direct and indirect measurements in yeast and human germline cells indicate that formation can occur at frequencies comparable to, and sometimes exceeding, those of other mutations (Falcón and Aris 2003; Turner et al. 2008; Prada-Luengo et al. 2020; Axelsson et al. 2024). Most circular DNA lacks a centromere and is therefore subject to mis-segregation during cell division, a process that induces large fluctuations in the number of specific circles per cell. This is important because intact genes found on circular DNA can thereby undergo dosage changes with phenotypic consequences that are subject to selection.

Some well-documented cases have made clear that selection can change the frequency of these “free-riding” genes in ways that are not possible when they are still embedded in their original chromosomal setting (Gresham et al. 2010; Turner et al. 2017; Koo et al. 2018; Hull et al. 2019; Prada-Luengo et al. 2020). Such forms of differential amplification often occur in somatic cells, contributing to increased nutrient transport in yeast, herbicide resistance in plants, and oncogenic activity in malignant animal tumors, all by rapidly increasing gene dosage under positive selection (Gresham et al. 2010; Turner et al. 2017; Koo et al. 2018; Hull et al. 2019; Prada-Luengo et al. 2020). However, these somatic amplifications are not always evolutionary dead ends, as genes on DNA circles have been shown to pass through meiosis into gametes and be sexually transmitted to progeny in plants (Koo et al. 2018, 2023a). Additionally, cases of reintegration into the chromosomal germline have been reported in animals (Durkin et al. 2012; Holt et al. 2023), revealing that circularization allows genes to permanently change their position in genomes, potentially also capitalizing on a permanent change in gene copy number.

Gene Dosage Control and its Disruption by Chromosomal Escape of Circularized Genes

A gene's dosage (Table 1) is largely determined by the cell's ploidy, which is set in a (normally diploid) zygote or a haploid founding cell and can subsequently be modified depending on an organism's mode of reproduction (e.g., haplodiploidy). Once ploidy is established, the chromosomal organization maintains and reinforces gene dosage through cellular mechanisms that protect the physical integrity of DNA and ensure the faithful transmission of genetic material to daughter cells during mitosis. Positioned on a chromosome, a gene is replicated once per cell cycle in coordination with all other nuclear genes. The resulting copies are distributed equally to mitotic daughter cells via centromere-mediated segregation of sister chromatids during spindle formation. However, once the gene becomes extrachromosomal on a DNA circle, it is physically separated from the centromere, and its honest segregation coercion, so its stable transmission is no longer ensured, and its dosage is no longer fixed. Instead, transmission now depends on: (i) whether the gene resides on a circular DNA molecule that can replicate autonomously; and (ii) on how the resulting copies of the gene segregate during cell division (Arrey et al. 2022). Imbalances of this kind can thus introduce dosage variation, and when this variation affects the phenotype, selection can potentially maintain fitness-enhancing DNA circles (Arrey et al. 2022).

A circularized gene appears to be physically protected by the covalently closed DNA circle. Therefore, its maintenance across cell cycles depends on its ability to replicate and segregate. Circular DNAs that persist must replicate or risk being progressively diluted across cell divisions. For example, in Saccharomyces cerevisiae (budding yeast), long-term transmission is restricted to circular DNAs encoding well-defined replication origins that enable the initiation of autonomous replication (Sinclair and Guarente 1997; Gresham et al. 2010; Hull et al. 2019; Prada-Luengo et al. 2020). In cancer cells, early studies demonstrated that certain gene-bearing circular DNAs replicate once per cell cycle during S-phase, in proportion with the chromosomal genome, as part of mechanisms supporting circular DNA maintenance in cancer cell populations (Barker et al. 1980; Von Hoff et al. 1988; Ruiz et al. 1989). Despite these well-documented cases, the capacity for self-replication among circularized genes is difficult to generalize in plants and animals, where the absence of sequence-defined replication origins limits predictability. Unlike yeast, which relies on conserved DNA elements (e.g., autonomously replicating sequences; ARS) to specify replication initiation, multicellular eukaryotes lack such sequence definition and instead employ flexible, context-dependent origin activity (Méchali 2010; Costas et al. 2011). Even so, more recent observations of inheritance patterns and the persistence of circularized genes in cancer cells and plants suggest underlying replication regimes consistent with those demonstrated in early studies (Lundberg et al. 2008; Turner et al. 2017; Koo et al. 2018; Yi et al. 2022). Throughout this Perspective, we limit our scope to full-length genes on DNA circles that replicate once per cell cycle.

Physically separated from the centromere, circular DNA is highly likely to segregate unevenly between daughter cells so that some circularized genes can potentially increase more rapidly in copy number across cell divisions than they could do via chromosomal duplications (Fig. 1). For example, in budding yeast, more than 90% of rRNA gene circles are retained in the mother cell after binding to nuclear pore complexes (Denoth-Lippuner et al. 2014), while other DNA circles that diffuse passively are excluded from daughter nuclei due to spatial and temporal constraints associated with asymmetric division (Gehlen et al. 2011; Prada-Luengo et al. 2020). These pronounced segregation biases toward the mother lineage enable the progressive accumulation of gene copies on DNA circles. As this lineage serves as a circular DNA reservoir that steadily increases in copy number over successive divisions, even the smaller fraction inherited by daughters can represent an increasing absolute number of gene copies, ultimately elevating dosage in both mother and daughter lineages beyond levels achievable through chromosomal duplication. In plants and mammalian cancer cells, the uneven segregation of circular DNA instead occurs randomly during cell division, and DNA circles are often found tethered to chromosomes (Levan et al. 1977; Kanda et al. 2001; Koo et al. 2018, 2023a). This has been measured directly in some cancer cell lines (Lundberg et al. 2008; Turner et al. 2017; Yi et al. 2022) and has been inferred to happen in plants from observed segregation patterns and somatic mosaicism (Koo et al. 2018, 2023a). Such variability in the segregation of circular DNA enables certain cell lineages to progressively accumulate higher copy numbers over successive divisions (Fig. 1). In all three types of model organisms, uneven segregation of circularized genes has led to rapid increases in gene dosage over time, in some instances reaching several hundred copies.

Selective Consequences of Dosage Variation Caused by Gene Circularization

Any gene that expresses a functional product may have phenotypic implications for its host if a new variation in dosage exceeds the buffering capacity of regulatory mechanisms and leads to the alteration of a cellular process (Birchler and Veitia 2012). We reason that this principle also applies to circularized genes since the encoded function is independent of the carrier DNA molecule—whether a chromosome or a DNA circle. We suggest that, as with other natural variants that alter dosage, the fate of circularized genes within a population ultimately depends on the fitness effects of dosage change. For example, circular DNA with the transporter gene for the amino acid permease GAP1 in budding yeast increases in copy number under nitrogen limitation. The resulting dosage effects are thought to enhance amino acid uptake and confer a condition-dependent fitness advantage, enabling genotypes with a higher number of circularized genes to spread and become dominant in the population (Gresham et al. 2010).

In agricultural weeds, gene amplifications on circular DNA confer resistance to the herbicides glyphosate and glufosinate. Glyphosate-resistant Amaranthus palmeri, Amaranthus spinosus, and Lolium perenne accumulate circularized EPSPS to high copy numbers, while glufosinate-resistant A. palmeri also accumulate circularized GS2—both genes encoding the metabolic enzymes targeted by these herbicides (Koo et al. 2018, 2023a, 2023b; Carvalho-Moore et al. 2025). The resulting dosage increase likely restores amino acid biosynthesis and nitrogen assimilation, thereby allowing resistance to be obtained despite herbicide inhibition. Identical circular elements have been detected in geographically distant populations of A. palmeri across the United States, suggesting a common origin and strong selective advantage of the circularized EPSPS and GS2 genes (Camposano et al. 2022).

In cancer genomes, circularized oncogenes have been shown to accumulate to hundreds of copies, suggesting strong selection for expression and high oncogene dosage (Turner et al. 2017). Such genes include genes encoding transcription factors involved in early development like MYC, cell-cycle regulators like AKT1, and growth-factor receptors like EGFR, whose increased dosage via circular DNA deregulates cellular growth and cell-cycle checkpoints, thereby obliterating somatic homeostasis through uncontrolled cell proliferation. Together, the yeast, plant, and cancer cases show that strong directional selection can favor phenotypic extremes resulting from enhanced single-gene dosage mediated by circular DNA (Fig. 2).

Escape from Regulatory and Linkage Constraints

When a gene escapes its chromosomal constraints, many potential but normally silenced activities can become unleashed.

Regulatory Consequences of Positional Escape

In the interphase nucleus of eukaryotes, transcription is influenced by a gene's physical position within the nuclear architecture, such as proximity to different chromatin states, cis- and trans-regulatory sequences, and nuclear landmarks. Genes near constitutive heterochromatin or epigenetically silenced alleles may experience reduced expression due to restricted chromatin accessibility or interference with transcriptional machinery. A circularized gene may thus acquire increased transcriptional activity. This is observed in cancer, where circular DNA is packaged in nucleosomes lacking higher-order chromatin organization, resulting in greater chromatin accessibility than the corresponding chromosomal sequence (Wu et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2021). This directly contributes to increased transcriptional activity of genes on circular DNA, as supported by evidence of reduced promoter methylation and active promoter usage when genes become circularized (Kim et al. 2020; Hung et al. 2021).

Circularization also alters the syntenic cis-regulatory context (Helmsauer et al. 2020; Zhou and Parsons 2023), which enables interactions between genes and regulatory sequences that are otherwise suppressed by chromosomal topology. Oncogenes have been observed on circular DNA alongside distal or insulated noncoding elements, including enhancers—a form of enhancer hijacking—that increase transcription in cancer cells (Morton et al. 2019; Helmsauer et al. 2020; Hung et al. 2021). In plants, circularized stress-response genes in glyphosate-resistant A. palmeri are selectively transcribed in response to herbicide exposure, indicating that the DNA circles contain cis-regulatory sequences sufficient to support coordinated expression. However, whether these interactions are newly formed or ancestrally conserved remains unknown (Molin et al. 2020).

Independence from its chromosome can also reshape a gene's trans-regulatory interactions. Once circularized, it can move more freely through the nucleus. Live-cell imaging and chromatin interaction assays have shown that circularized genes explore larger nuclear spaces and associate with more transcriptionally active chromatin (Zhu et al. 2021; Yi et al. 2022). In cancer cells, circularized genes can also relocate from the nuclear periphery (Itoh and Shimizu 1998; Shimizu et al. 2001) to centralized positions where they can join transcriptionally active hubs that promote cooperative enhancer-promoter interactions across distinct DNA circles (Hung et al. 2021; Zhu et al. 2021; Yi et al. 2022). They can thus become engaged in regulatory networks that are inaccessible to chromosomal loci (Hung et al. 2021; Zhu et al. 2021; Yi et al. 2022). Plant genes on circular DNA can even move between distant plant tissues across graft junctions and persist across clonal generations, underlining their functional stability under changing conditions (Zhang et al. 2024). Because our insight into the biology of circularized genes is still limited, the overall prevalence of these phenomena across different tissues and species remains unknown.

Escape from Genetic Linkage

When two loci are close neighbors on a chromosome, crossover events are rare, especially in low-recombination regions such as constitutive heterochromatin, which causes linkage disequilibrium between phenotypically advantageous and deleterious alleles and prevents selection from being effective (Slatkin 2008). Circularization enables a gene to leave these positional constraints behind while gaining transcriptional and possibly also evolutionary independence. Such a circularized gene may thus become selectively favored based solely on its contribution to organismal fitness, free from any linkage constraints normally imposed by neighboring genes. Conversely, when originally linked to regions under strong selection, circularization can prevent a gene from being purged along with deleterious alleles. Particularly under directional selection, circularized genes may increase in frequency faster when they confer a selective advantage (Fig. 1).

Somatic and Heritable Adaptation Mediated by Circularized Genes

Multiple studies have now confirmed that once decoupled from their chromosomal organization constraints, circularized genes can drive rapid adaptation in response to novel selective pressures. Such adaptations usually remain somatic but can also become heritable.

Somatic Adaptation May Serve the Organism or Lead to Deleterious Conflict

In unicellular eukaryotic organisms such as budding yeast, gene amplification on circular DNA can be beneficial, deleterious, or potentially neutral (Sinclair and Guarente 1997; Gresham et al. 2010; Møller et al. 2015; Prada-Luengo et al. 2020). Since there is only a single (cellular) level of organization, the phenotypic effect at the cellular and the organismal level is the same. However, in obligate multicellular organisms like mammals and angiosperm plants, evidence supports two contrasting scenarios: one where circularized genes enhance cellular fitness at the expense of the host organism (Yan et al. 2024) and one where fitness gains remain aligned across the lower and higher levels of the biological organization (Koo et al. 2018; Carvalho-Moore et al. 2025) (Fig. 2). When lower-level and higher-level selection work in opposite directions, gene amplification on circular DNA generates an obvious conflict between somatic and organismal fitness (Yan et al. 2024). When the directions are aligned, favoring similar outcomes at both the cellular and organismal levels, circularized genes can support coordinated adaptation (Koo et al. 2018; Carvalho-Moore et al. 2025).

In cancer, somatic driver mutations corrupt the balanced selection regime that maintains multicellular homeostasis (Bozic et al. 2010), favoring any metabolic and proliferative advantages conferred by circular oncogene amplification (Yan et al. 2024). These mutations and structural driver events such as circularized oncogenes often synergistically support the clonal expansion of cancerous cell lineages, enabling them to outcompete neighboring cell lineages within the organism. This conceptualization of circular DNA-driven adaptation at the cellular level is consistent with the view that cancer is a Darwinian evolutionary process (Greaves and Maley 2012). In this view, somatic cells acquire traits that confer a selective advantage in opposition to the interest of the unitary mammalian body that they belong to, which is served by homeostatic development, error-free mitotic cell divisions, and constant gene dosage throughout the lifespan.

On the other hand, when all cells in a multicellular organism are subject to the same environmental stress, circularized genes may confer a fitness benefit not only at the lower cellular level but also at the higher organismal level. This situation is more likely to occur in modular multicellular organisms. It has been demonstrated to apply in crop weeds, where herbicide resistance is acquired through the proliferation of circularized genes that spread through somatic tissues. The ensuing adaptation to environmentally induced stress can persist in reproductive tissues because the circularized genes become part of pollen grains, making the stress response heritable, albeit not by returning to their original disciplined chromosomal embedding (Koo et al. 2018, 2023a, 2023b; Carvalho-Moore et al. 2025).

Heritable Adaptations via Circularized Genes May Depend on Germline Determination

To become heritable, positively selected circularized genes need to be transferred from the soma back to the germline. This transmission is easiest in unicellular eukaryotes, such as budding yeast, where a single cell serves as both soma and germline; therefore, circularized genes can increase to high copy numbers when positively selected, as in the previously mentioned adaptive response to nutrient stress (Gresham et al. 2010). However, complex multicellular organisms have segregated germlines and soma, and it is here that the difference between unitary and modular growth is likely to make a crucial difference.

In angiosperms and other modularly multicellular lineages, germlines are multiple, peripheral, and emerge from somatic cell lineages later in development (Walbot and Evans 2003). This not only implies that beneficial somatic mutations can increase reproductive success without being heritable but, in light of the current evidence, they can also potentially reach the germline to confer heritable adaptation, as demonstrated in glyphosate-resistant weeds. In these plants, circularized genes have been observed in male meiotic cells (Koo et al. 2018, 2023b), and their pollen-mediated transfer has been documented in both experimental and natural hybrids (Koo et al. 2018, 2023a). These circularized plant genes are thus transmitted through sexual reproduction while conferring herbicide resistance to subsequent generations (Koo et al. 2018, 2023a).

As proposed by Koo et al. (2018), the mechanisms of gene amplification driven by circular DNA appear to exemplify one of Barbara McClintock's postulated innate systems. In these systems, genomic responses to stress in the sporophyte generate heritable variation, thereby linking the somatic experience to evolutionary change. Interestingly, acquired resistance to glyphosate can be maintained and transmitted to the next generation, even in the absence of herbicide stress under experimental conditions. However, it remains unknown whether this circular DNA-mediated adaptation can persist in the long term in the absence of specific selective pressure. It is also unclear whether such persistence would represent a novel form of heritable change confined to the circular DNA component of the genome.

While the acquired resistance to herbicides in agricultural weeds is currently the clearest example of heritable adaptation driven by circular DNA, it is also the only documented case to date, and all known instances involve exposure to herbicides, ie a single environmental stress factor. This rarity may reflect a research bias since herbicide resistance is both economically important and shaped by unusually strong selection. Other undetected cases of heritable circular DNA-mediated adaptation might thus be found in future plant studies.

In contrast, in bilaterian animals, such as mammals, with a unitary germline and matching somatic growth, early germline segregation isolates the heritable genomes from the somatic variants (Spiller et al. 2017). Therefore, one can argue that it is difficult to imagine that somatic mutations, both in general and those mediated by circularized genes, can be transferred from the soma to the germline and have adaptive consequences. The odds of finding adaptations mediated by circularized genes in mammals thus seem more severe than in flowering plants and may require DNA circles to become reinserted into a chromosome. For example, a dominant pigmentation phenotype in cattle is known to result from circularization and subsequent chromosomal reintegration of a segment containing the KIT gene. This case seems remarkable because a circle-mediated rearrangement became part of the heritable genome and the associated coat-color trait subsequently increased in frequency under artificial selection by cattle breeders (Durkin et al. 2012).

In humans, circular DNA has been detected in sperm (Henriksen et al. 2022). Additionally, traces of formerly circularized genes that have reintegrated and altered their position in ancestral human genomes have been identified, suggesting that circularized genes can occasionally leave permanent marks in the genome despite the early segregation of mammalian germline and soma (Holt et al. 2023). These isolated mammalian examples are intriguing, but they are too sparse to draw general conclusions yet. However, they go some way toward providing proof of concept for the idea that circularized genes can at least sometimes become re-engaged in germlines and leave heritable genomic marks.

Conclusions

This Perspective has examined how circularization enables genes to escape chromosomal constraints, facilitating rapid, stress-driven adaptation across diverse eukaryotic systems. Although circular DNA has long been recognized, interest in the evolutionary significance of circularized genes has only recently taken off. While major research efforts in this direction have focused on cancer, our understanding of other animal systems, fungi, and plants remains in its infancy. Our survey highlights notable parallels in how budding yeast, crop weeds, and cancer cells use circularized genes to boost dosage under selection, even as differences in cellularity, growth form, and inheritance routes shape these dynamics. By drawing attention to these shared strategies and their biological constraints, we encourage more in-depth comparative studies and modeling efforts to better understand the evolutionary roles of circularized genes across the eukaryotic branch of the tree of life.
