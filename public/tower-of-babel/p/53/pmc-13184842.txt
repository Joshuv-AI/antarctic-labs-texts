Successful Management of Chest Pain Through Cardiac Autonomic Rehabilitation
Authors: Jun Yang, Riwang Wang, Rongjing Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSome patients experience persistent chest pain after complete coronary revascularization without evidence of ischemia. Dysautonomia may be the underlying cause.Case SummaryA 43-year-old man with recurrent chest pain after multivessel revascularization showed poor response to standard cardiac rehabilitation. Evaluation revealed significant autonomic dysfunction (sympathetic overactivation), not ischemia. After implementing an integrated autonomic-regulation protocol (low-intensity aerobic exercise combined with abdominal breathing, standing meditation, and mindful walking), his symptoms and functional capacity improved markedly.DiscussionAutonomic imbalance is a key pathophysiology. Success required a paradigm shift from the standardized cardiac rehabilitation exercise outlined in current international guidelines to a neural regulation–centered approach.Take-Home MessagesWhen developing cardiac rehabilitation plans, full account should be taken of the impact of the cardiac autonomic nervous system on symptoms, including palpitations, chest tightness, and chest pain. The targeted integration of neural regulation training leads to concurrent improvement in both clinical symptoms and functional status.Key words: exercise, lifestyle, myocardial infarction

History of Presentation

A 43-year-old man presented to the cardiac rehabilitation clinic with a 1-year history of recurrent chest tightness, pain, and throat constriction after coronary stent implantation. The patient had undergone percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty of the right coronary artery in 2022 and stent implantation in the left anterior descending artery in 2024 for acute coronary events. Despite successful revascularization, he experienced frequent recurrence of chest tightness, chest pain, and throat constriction, particularly triggered by emotional stress or mild physical exertion. Physical examination demonstrated stable vital signs and unremarkable cardiopulmonary auscultation findings.

•Autonomic nervous system dysfunction should be highly suspected as a core mechanism in patients with persistent symptoms after coronary intervention that are dissociated from conventional evidence of ischemia.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of hypertension (grade 2, very high risk), type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypokalemia, and mild sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome. There was a family history of premature coronary artery disease.

Differential Diagnosis

For this patient with persistent chest tightness, pain, and throat constriction after revascularization, the differential diagnosis should encompass both cardiac and noncardiac etiologies. Cardiac considerations must include coronary microvascular disease, in-stent restenosis, or new-onset acute coronary syndrome. Noncardiac etiologies should incorporate gastroesophageal reflux disease and anxiety disorder with somatic symptoms. These potential diagnoses require systematic investigation for confirmation or exclusion.

Investigations

Baseline cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) conducted in December 2024 revealed a peak Vo2 of 21.7 mL/min/kg (69% predicted), with test termination owing to chest tightness after 4 minutes 7 seconds of exercise, and a Borg score of 15 points. No ischemic ST-T changes were observed. Heart rate at anaerobic threshold was 118 beats/min, and heart rate recovery at 1 minute was 17 beats/min. Baseline heart rate variability (HRV) assessment in February 2025 indicated a low-frequency (LF) power of 423 ms2, a high-frequency (HF) power of 81 ms2, and an LF/HF ratio of 5.251. A 24-hour ambulatory electrocardiogram showed occasional premature beats and nonspecific ST-T changes. The results of serial CPET and autonomic function assessments are summarized in Tables 1 and 2.

Management

In strict accordance with the FITT-VP (frequency, intensity, time, type, volume, and progression) principles of the international standard cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription, we established a target heart rate of 113 to 123 beats/min based on the anaerobic threshold heart rate measured by CPET. Despite good patient compliance, after 6 months of intervention, the patient's chest tightness symptoms showed no improvement, leading to his return to the clinic, where the cardiac rehabilitation physician performed another CPET. The repeat CPET revealed no improvement in functional status, with even a slight decrease in peak Vo2 and further deterioration in heart rate recovery at 1 minute, confirming the ineffectiveness of the initial approach. The initial HRV assessment demonstrated an LF/HF ratio of 5.251 (normal range: 0.50-2.00), indicating significant sympathetic dominance.1 After 6 months of home-based rehabilitation according to the standard international guideline protocol, this ratio further increased to 7.389, demonstrating progressively worsening sympathetic overactivation.

Based on these findings, we modified the exercise prescription, reducing the aerobic exercise intensity to a target heart rate of 90 to 110 beats/min and systematically integrating diaphragmatic breathing, standing meditation, mindful walking, and heel-raise exercises. This integrated strategy aims to enhance parasympathetic tone, reduce sympathetic activity, and improve overall regulatory stability.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After 5 months of integrated cardiac autonomic rehabilitation, the patient demonstrated substantial clinical improvement, manifested by significantly reduced frequency of chest pain episodes and enhanced exercise tolerance. Objective measures confirmed this progress: peak Vo2 increased to 25.2 mL/min/kg, and the HRV LF/HF ratio significantly decreased to 2.09, indicating a trend toward autonomic balance. Extended follow-up revealed sustained therapeutic effects, with continued improvement in the patient's quality of life.

Discussion

This case offers a profound insight by dialectically supplementing the cornerstone concept of exercise prescription in cardiac rehabilitation: The prescription must be individualized based on the underlying pathophysiological state2; otherwise, a potentially beneficial intervention may prove ineffective or even detrimental.

Traditional cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescriptions, employing moderate- to high-intensity aerobic exercise (target heart rate: 113-123 beats/min), apply a controlled load to the cardiovascular system intended to enhance cardiorespiratory endurance and improve coronary blood flow and metabolism. This approach is indeed highly effective for patients whose core issue is myocardial ischemia.3

In the present case, although each CPET was terminated owing to chest tightness, none demonstrated ischemic ST-T changes during exercise. Conversely, the initial HRV monitoring clearly indicated sympathetic overactivation. This revealed that the patient's fundamental pathology was no longer myocardial ischemia but rather a dysregulation and imbalance of the autonomic regulatory system. HRV serves as a noninvasive and reproducible measure of autonomic nervous system function4 and is a valid biomarker linking negative affective states with physiological dysfunction.5 The abnormal HRV data provided the breakthrough for identifying the appropriate treatment target.

The exercise-induced chest tightness and pain in this setting can be explained by several neurocardiac mechanisms, as elaborated in the pathophysiological model proposed by Cattaneo et al.6 First, in a state of sympathetic overactivation, exercise fails to elicit a balanced autonomic response. Instead of appropriate parasympathetic withdrawal and coronary vasodilation, the excessive sympathetic outflow may trigger paradoxical coronary microvascular constriction and increase myocardial oxygen demand disproportionately, leading to a sensation of chest tightness and pain in the absence of epicardial coronary ischemia. Second, the heightened sympathetic tone lowers the threshold for visceral pain perception, a phenomenon known as central sensitization. Normal mechanical and metabolic stimuli from the heart during exercise (eg, increased contractility, heart rate) are thereby amplified and perceived as painful symptoms. This mechanism is particularly relevant given the patient's high Patient Health Questionnaire–15 item score (Table 4), indicating a predisposition to somatization.7 Consequently, the patient's exercise tolerance was limited not by true ischemic threshold, but by a maladaptive neurocardiac reflex arc and altered central pain processing.

For this patient, the moderate- to high-intensity exercise in the traditional prescription acted not as a remedy, but as a potent sympathetic agonist. The continuous sympathetic input exacerbated his preexisting neural imbalance, leading to a vicious cycle. This explains the fundamental reason why his symptoms failed to improve and objective parameters worsened initially, despite strict adherence to the exercise regimen.

Given that heart rate variability serves as an independent predictor of cardiovascular prognosis,8 our initial HRV findings provided a compelling rationale for fundamentally rethinking the treatment strategy. Our therapeutic success does not refute the inherent value of the standardized cardiac rehabilitation exercise prescription itself, but rather lies in its targeted modification. While maintaining low-intensity aerobic exercise to preserve basic physical capacity, we systematically introduced a series of autonomic nervous system–regulating exercises, including diaphragmatic breathing, standing meditation, mindful walking, and heel-raise training. Postintervention results aligned with findings from Santos-Hiss et al9: HFnu increased, LFnu decreased, and the LF/HF ratio decreased. These practices, characterized by mind-body integration and a combination of dynamic and static elements at moderate intensity, safely and effectively activate the parasympathetic nervous system, providing an ideal intervention for modulating autonomic function.10 The significant increase in the patient's peak Vo2 further confirmed the effectiveness of this modified approach.

Therefore, the patient's recovery stemmed from our identification of the crucial lead via HRV results, enabling a shift from a singular focus on moderate- to high-intensity aerobic exercise to an integrated rehabilitation model combining low-intensity aerobic activity with neural regulation. This constituted not merely a dosage adjustment, but a fundamental transformation in the mode of exercise. The low-intensity aerobic component provided a safe foundation, while the neural regulation training actively repaired the dysregulated control system.

Conclusions

For patients with persistent symptoms such as chest tightness and pain after revascularization, integration of cardiac autonomic nervous system assessment—particularly HRV monitoring—is crucial in clinical evaluation. When standardized cardiac rehabilitation protocols demonstrate insufficient efficacy, implementing an individualized comprehensive rehabilitation strategy centered on cardiac autonomic regulation represents a paradigm shift in therapeutic management that is essential for achieving breakthrough treatment outcomes.
