Ischemic Ventricular Septal Defect Presenting With Progressive Exertional Dyspnea
Authors: Satyen Hargovan, Shaaheen Laher, Oliver Hayes, Nathan Hopkins, Dale Murdoch, Christopher Raffel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe present a case of postinfarct ventricular septal defect (VSD) presenting with exertional breathlessness, progressing to an index emergency presentation of biventricular heart failure and pulmonary embolism without shock.Case summaryMultimodality cardiac imaging demonstrated a postinfarct muscular VSD in the midinferior septum measuring 9 mm, with a pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio of 2.5. Percutaneous transjugular VSD closure was successfully undertaken using a 16-mm Amplatzer postinfarct VSD occluder, with robust clinical outcomes to date.DiscussionThis case highlights the need to consider exertional breathlessness as an angina equivalent, the utility of multimodality cardiac imaging in preprocedural planning, and the safety, efficacy, and durability of transcatheter closure for chronic ischemic VSD.Take-Home MessageThis case adds to the literature on the growing role of transcatheter closure as a reasonable alternative to surgical closure in this population.

History of Presentation

An independent 70 year-old Caucasian woman was referred to the emergency department from her home after experiencing 1 month of new and progressive exertional dyspnea associated with orthopnea and pedal edema. Observations revealed type 1 respiratory failure, with oxygen saturation of 90% on room air, raised diastolic blood pressure of 129/101 mm Hg, and atrial fibrillation at 140 beats/min. Examination was significant for respiratory distress, a raised jugular venous pressure, bibasal lung crepitations to midzones, and pedal edema. Past Medical History

The patient had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with a 50 pack-year smoking history, dyslipidaemia on rosuvastatin 20 mg daily, hypertension on ramipril 2.5 mg daily, and surgical repairs of her tricuspid valve (28-mm MC3 annuloplasty ring) and mitral valve (34-mm physio ring) in 2019.

Differential Diagnosis

Broad respiratory (pulmonary embolus, pneumonia, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation) and cardiac (congestive cardiac failure, acute coronary syndrome, arrhythmia, dissection, and tamponade) differentials were considered. An ischemic cardiomyopathy was deemed most likely.

Investigations

Electrocardiogram (ECG) showed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation with intermittent reversion to sinus, with a pathological Q-wave in lead V2 and premature ventricular ectopics (Figure 1). Full blood count, electrolytes, and renal and hepatic indices were unremarkable. B-type natriuretic peptide was elevated (2100 pmol/L), as were high-sensitivity cardiac troponin I (40 ng/L, normal: <10 ng/L) and D-dimer (2.23 mg/L, normal: <0.49 mL/L). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated severe left ventricular (LV) dilatation, with an ejection fraction of 57% and midseptal akinesis. There was moderate RV dilatation with severely impaired function. The RV systolic pressure was estimated at 36 mm Hg, consistent with mild pulmonary hypertension. The left atrium was severely dilated (indexed at 79 mL/m2). Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and gadolinium-enhanced cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (GE-cMRI) were undertaken to better define the defect. TEE confirmed the findings shown on TTE, and GE-cMRI revealed a 9-mm postinfarct VSD in the mid inferoseptal segment, with a large left-to-right shunt (Qp:Qs of 2.5) causing RV dilatation and severely impaired function (RV ejection fraction: 18%). LV dysfunction was noted (LV ejection fraction: 38%) with apical septal akinesis, and the inferoseptal region adjacent to the VSD showed full-thickness delayed enhancement consistent with infarction (Figure 4). Coronary angiography confirmed VSD, quantifying its shunt with a Qp:Qs of 2.93. We confirmed the shunt on right heart catheterization using a “shunt run.” The coronary arteries were codominant, showing mild diffuse coronary artery atherosclerosis without high-grade stenosis. Management

These findings were consistent with a full-thickness inferoseptal infarct causing a 9- to 11-mm muscular VSD in the midinferior septum, shunting left-to-right with a Qp:Qs of 2.5 and subsequent biventricular dilatation and failure.

The PE and atrial fibrillation were treated with low–molecular weight heparin and digoxin, respectively. The congestive cardiac failure improved with intravenous frusemide and supplemental oxygen, however definitive management required VSD closure. Given the moderate size, high symptom burden, and chronicity allowing adequate tissue stabilization and lesion demarcation, transcatheter closure was deemed reasonable to attempt.

Using an 8-F right internal jugular vein (RIJ) approach, and with right femoral vein and left femoral artery access, a 16-mm Amplatzer muscular postinfarct VSD occluder (St Jude Medical) (Figure 7) was introduced via the LV using a 6-F JR4 catheter and exchange glidewire.Figure 7Amplatzer Muscular Postinfarct Ventricular Septal Defect Occluder

A 6-F Swan-Ganz catheter was placed in the pulmonary artery via the RIJ and exchanged for a 35-mm gooseneck snare catheter, which captured the glidewire in the pulmonary artery and was exteriorized to the RIJ, creating an arteriovenous rail. An Amplatzer TorqVue 45° torque sheath (St Jude Medical) was then passed across the VSD and into the LV. The JR4 and glidewire were removed from the arterial end and replaced with a 5-F pigtail catheter. The 16-mm postinfarct VSD occluder device was placed in the VSD, taking the main superior 10/4 direction channel (Figure 8). Intraoperative TEE showed no residual shunting, and the device was deployed. A final left ventriculogram showed shunt obliteration.Figure 8Intraoperative Transesophageal Echocardiography Imaging Demonstrating 16-mm Postinfarct VSD Occluder Device Placed in the VSD Taking the Main Superior 10/4 Direction ChannelVSD = ventricular septal defect.

Postoperative day 1 TTE showed a superiorly directed mild left-to-right shunt, gradient measuring 57 mm Hg, originating from the apical margin of the LV side of the device. Repeat TTE 6 weeks later showed trivial shunt, with gradient 80 mm Hg. At 5 months, TTE showed shunt obliteration.

Discussion

Ischemic VSDs occur from infarct-related tissue necrosis. Although rare, arising in 0.2% of all postinfarct patients, they have a 30-day mortality rate of up to 94% without intervention, making their recognition and management critical.1 Advanced age, female sex, initial infarct, and delayed reperfusion all contribute to ischemic VSD formation and were manifested in our case.2

Exertional breathlessness, an angina equivalent correlating to coronary artery disease in meta-analyses, may have been the infarct index presentation.3 Ischemic LV dysfunction and pathological VSD shunting, with Qp:Qs of >2 suggesting hemodynamic significance, necessitates management to prevent worsening heart failure and mortality.4 Subsequent severe left atrial dilatation provides substrate for atrial fibrillation and high thromboembolic risk, estimated at 7.2% per year using the CHA2DS2-VASc score, although in our patient there was no spontaneous contrast, thrombus, or sludge in the left atrial appendage on TEE.

In our patient, the presence of new exertional breathlessness, apical septal hypokinesis on TTE/TEE, and full-thickness delayed enhancement in a coronary territory on GE-cMRI make historical ST-segment acute myocardial infarction likely. However, coronary angiography showed no occlusive disease. We theorize that a thromboembolic infarct of the distal inferoseptal branches of the right coronary artery occurred, and was treated with the anticoagulation commenced for PE before angiography. Other differentials included atheroembolic phenomena from plaque rupture or coronary cameral fistula (not present on angiography in 2019) causing ischemic VSD.

Surgical or transcatheter VSD closure was indicated given hemodynamically significant large shunt (Qp:Qs >2) and heart failure. During the 30 year history of transcatheter VSD closure, optimal timing and type of VSD closure has been debated. Transcatheter interventions promise less invasiveness, with quicker recovery, shorter length of stay, and reduced health care expenditure. Yet both American and European Cardiology Society guidelines advocate for delayed surgical closure, with transcatheter closure favored only if surgical risk is prohibitive.5 Most studies quoted in these guidelines, however, include early presentations with acute hemodynamic instability and cardiogenic shock and therefore are not generalizable to all patients with ischemic VSD.

No clinical trials have compared surgical versus transcatheter closure for chronic ischemic VSD. Data are observational or retrospective and comprise mostly acute unstable ischemic VSD closure. For example, the largest surgical closure study reported a mortality rate of 42.9% (n = 2,876),6 while in transcatheter closure a systematic review of smaller and more heterogenous studies showed a mortality rate of 32% (n = 273).7 Another study comparing surgical versus transcatheter closure in a British registry of 362 patients found no difference in mortality after 5 years.8 Only 1 systematic review describes outcomes for chronic ischemic VSD closure, with a mortality rate of 14% (n = 68),9 however data were only collected to 30-days postinfarct, and 40% of patients still had cardiogenic shock, which is correlated with poorer outcomes. Although promising, more outcomes data are needed before transcatheter approaches can be adopted into clinical practice and guidelines.

Apart from appropriate patient selection and a skilled operator, diligent preprocedural defect characterization via multimodality imaging is a prerequisite for successful transcatheter closure.10 The choice of occluder devices is limited to the few available on the market, of which only the Amplatzer postinfarct VSD occluder is specifically designed for use postinfarction, and no head-to-head comparisons exist. Challenges to transcatheter closure include inferior defects due to lack of a circumferential septal rim, basal defects due to proximity to the tricuspid valvular apparatus, and serpiginous defects due to complicated morphology.

Outcomes/Follow-Up

At 30 months postprocedure, the patient remained independent at home, reporting NYHA functional class II symptoms and good quality of life. She remained on maximally tolerated heart failure therapy and completed cardiac rehabilitation. ECG showed normal sinus rhythm at 60 beats/min, and TTE demonstrated recovered LV size and function, (LV ejection fraction: 60%). The Amplatzer occluder was well seated, with no residual shunting.

Conclusions

New exertional breathlessness is an important angina equivalent, and it should prompt consideration of acute coronary syndrome. Mechanical complications of acute coronary syndrome confer high morbidity and mortality, and as such, must be carefully excluded through history, examination, and investigations. We demonstrate a case of safe, effective transcatheter closure of a chronic ischemic VSD with durable results at 30 months. This case highlights the growing role of transcatheter closure as an alternative to surgical closure in this population.
