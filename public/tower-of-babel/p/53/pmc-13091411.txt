Longitudinal evolution of rheumatoid factor-expressing lymphoma precursors in a patient with Sjögren's disease
Authors: Adrian YS Lee, Jing Jing Wang, David A Brown, Joanne H Reed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePatients with Sjögren's disease (SjD) have an increased risk of developing B cell lymphomas, which may express rheumatoid factors (RhF). However, it remains unclear whether RhF-secreting cells undergo malignant transformation. To address this question, we longitudinally tracked the evolution of a RhF clone in a rare SjD case before and after lymphoma diagnosis.MethodsPeripheral blood B cells, serum and malignant lymph node biopsy were collected from a 68-year-old male with SjD who developed diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL). Clonal RhF sequences were identified in serum by mass spectrometry and in B cells by mRNA sequencing. Recombinant IgM were generated from clonal sequences and assessed for RhF activity and cryoaggregation.ResultsA monoclonal RhF was detected in serum and circulating B cells six months before DLBCL diagnosis. The same clone was expressed by the lymphoma. Clonal analysis demonstrated divergence into two branches: one with seven antibody mutations expressed by blood B cells, and a second with twelve antibody mutations in the serum RhF and lymphoma. Recombinant IgM representing the blood and serum RhF demonstrated RhF activity, whereas the lymphoma clone acquired three additional mutations that impaired RhF binding.ConclusionsOur prospective and longitudinal analyses provide direct evidence for a RhF secreting clone evolving into a lymphoma in a SjD patient. This work expands on previous cross-sectional studies of RhF expressing lymphomas in SjD and provides a proof-of-concept case, which potentially supports early therapeutic targeting of expanded RhF clones.Keywords: Autoantibody, Cryoglobulin, Lymphoma, Rheumatoid factor, Sjögren's disease

Introduction

Sjögren's disease (SjD) is an autoimmune disease characterised by sicca, B cell activation and autoantibodies [[1], [2], [25]]. Patients with SjD have a 20-fold increased risk of developing B cell lymphoma [2]. Clinical features associated with chronic B cell activation indicate a higher risk of lymphoma [3,4]. Among these risk factors are rheumatoid factor (RhF) autoantibodies, which are present in approximately 50% of SjD patients [4], most commonly of the IgM isotype, and directed towards the Fc portion of IgG antibodies. RhF can contribute to organ damage by forming immune complexes with IgG that precipitate at temperatures lower than 37 °C, termed cryoglobulins. Mixed cryoglobulins are a prominent feature of SjD, associating with serological markers of disease and representing a state of monoclonal B cell activity [5]. Their presence indicate a subset of SjD patients at higher risk of progression to lymphoma [3,6], making cryoglobulins important clinical prognostic markers.

Analyses of malignant B cells and their B cell receptors (BCR) from patients with SjD and B cell lymphoma revealed remarkable heavy chain complementarity-determining region 3 (HCDR3) homology with known RhF and their expression as recombinant IgM confirmed specificity for IgG Fc [[7], [8], [9]]. Based on these findings, it was postulated that RhF BCRs contributed to lymphomagenesis by providing chronic stimulation to promote B cell survival and proliferation [10]. Moreover, we have demonstrated that expanded B cells producing cryoprecipitating RhF carry somatic mutations in lymphoma-driver genes [11,12]. These findings lead to the hypothesis that the same RhF B cells that contribute to autoimmune pathology are pre-malignant and poised to transition into lymphomas. However, these cryoprecipitating RhF clones were detected in SjD patients over several years without transitioning into lymphomas [11], which raises the question: do the same B cell clones that secrete RhF and contribute to autoimmune pathology become lymphomas?

Given the rarity of identifying and sampling SjD patients pre- and post-lymphoma diagnosis, studies to date have largely been cross-sectional in nature, leaving a significant knowledge gap in the longitudinal clonal evolution of RhF clones into malignancy. Moreover, studies that have identified lymphomas with RhF activity have not identified the clonal serum RhF making it difficult to confirm whether the pathogenic RhF B cells become malignant or a separate, unrelated RhF clone. To address these knowledge gaps, we describe a single patient with SjD and cryoprecipitating RhF that developed a high-grade B cell lymphoma. Tracking the clonal RhF in serum, peripheral blood B cells and the malignant lymph node (LN) demonstrated that the B cell lymphoma and secreted cryoprecipating RhF derived from the same clone in this patient. These findings provide a plausible explanation for the increased lymphoma risk in patients with cryoglobulinaemia.

Patients and methods

Patient samples

Blood was obtained via venepuncture into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid and serum-separating tubes. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were separated from whole blood using the Ficoll density gradient centrifugation method (Cytiva) and stored in liquid nitrogen with 10% dimethyl sulfoxide. An excised LN was collected in RPMI media and single cell suspension made. Ethics approval was granted from the Western Sydney Local Health District Research Office (ETH01030). The patient gave written, informed consent for participation and publication.

Flow cytometry and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)

Thawed PBMCs or a single-cell suspension of LN were incubated with Fc receptor block (BD Biosciences) and the following mouse anti-human fluorophore-labelled antibodies obtained from BD Biosciences or Biolegend: CD3 APC-H7 (clone SK7), CD14 APC-H7 (MφP9), CD19 BV421 (HIB19), CD27 APC (O323), IgD BB700 (IA6-2), IgM BV650 (G20-127), and Igκ BV510 (TB28-2). CD19+IgM+Igκ+ cells were single cell sorted into 96-well plates containing 5 μL of 10 μM Tris-hydrochloride with 2.5 units Promega RNasin Ribonuclease Inhibitor. Plates were snap frozen on dry ice and stored at −80°C for paired heavy and light chain BCR sequencing.

Sequencing of antibody and B cell receptors

Mass spectrometry was used to sequence RhF protein isolated from patient serum heated to 37 °C as described [13]. For transcriptomic analysis of the BCR, we performed Sanger sequencing on paired heavy and light chain amplicons from sorted single IgMκ B cells [14], and massively parallel sequencing of heavy and light chain mRNA amplified from 5 million PBMCs using the MiSeq platform [11].

Synthesising recombinant IgM rheumatoid factors

Complementary DNA encoding IgM heavy, light and J chains were synthesised by GenScript and cloned into pcDNA3.1(+) vectors as described [11]. Vectors were transfected into Expi293 cells (Gibco) to produce pentameric IgM as per manufacturer's instructions. The unmutated common ancestor (UCA) sequence was derived from the International ImMunoGeneTics Information System (IMGT). All numbering of residues uses IMGT numbering.

Rheumatoid factor and cryoaggregation assays

RhF enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) were performed as previously described using serial dilutions of recombinant IgM antibody starting at 100 μg/mL in technical duplicates [11]. A heat aggregated gamma globulin in-solution RhF assay was performed as previously described using 10 μg of recombinant antibody [13]. The cryoprecipitating properties of recombinant IgM were evaluated as described [11], incubating 0.3 mg/mL with a 13-fold molar excess of γ-globulins (Sigma-Aldrich).

Results

Patient details

SS2 is a 68-year-old male with SjD manifesting with sicca symptoms and intermittent cutaneous cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis. He had serum anti-SS-A/Ro52 IgG autoantibodies, an unstimulated whole salivary flow rate of <0.1 mL/min, and therefore fulfilled the American College of Rheumatology and European League Against Rheumatism 2016 classification criteria for SjD [15]. He had a history of biopsy-proven autoimmune hepatitis, which was treated with azathioprine. He was positive for IgM RhF, low C4 complement (Fig. 1A) and detectable cryoglobulin with cryocrit <1% (type II, IgMκ). Over several months, he reported an increasing left cervical LN which was positron emission tomography (PET) scan-avid (June 2022, Fig. 1B) and constitutional symptoms. A biopsy of the LN revealed a clonal population of CD19+CD20+CD5−CD10±Igκ+ B cells (4% of lymphocytes) on flow cytometry. On histology, there was a confluent infiltrate of atypical CD10+ B cells with a Ki-67 index of 95%, consistent with diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL).

SS2 received 6 cycles of chemotherapy of rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine and prednisolone to clinical remission (commenced July 2022; completed January 2023). His cutaneous vasculitis and hepatitis regressed, accompanied by an increase in C4 complement and decrease in RhF, that remained low but detectable (Fig. 1A). Approximately 12 months later, his autoimmune hepatitis returned necessitating the re-introduction of azathioprine. He remained in clinical remission from his lymphoma and cutaneous vasculitis.

Sequencing rheumatoid factors in anatomical compartments

Flow cytometry analysis of SS2's PBMCs six months prior to the diagnosis of DLBCL, identified an IgMκ population comprising 55% of CD19+ B cells (Fig. 1C). Two-thirds of the circulating IgMκ population were CD27+CD38hi consistent with a plasmablast or activated memory phenotype. In contrast, 93% of the B cells in the LN were IgMκ with heterogenous CD27 CD38 expression (Fig. 1C). Single cell BCR sequencing of the peripheral blood IgMκ B cells revealed a clonally expanded stereotypic RhF “Bla” idiotype (IGHV4-59/IGKV3-20) [16], which comprised 37% (70/190) of sequenced IgMκ B cells. The clonal sequences contained 6 and 4 amino acid substitutions in the heavy and light chains, respectively (termed “Blood”, Fig. 1D). A variant with a c.337C > G substitution causing an amino acid change, Leu117Val in the HCDR3 was also identified (termed “L117V”, Fig. 1D). The kappa light chains were identical in Blood and L117V subclones (Fig. 1D). A similar clonal sequence was identified in the IgMκ B cells from the diagnostic LN biopsy (termed “DLBCL”, Fig. 1D), sharing three amino acid changes with the Blood clone (2 in the heavy chain, 1 in the light chain). However, the DLBCL sequence was more heavily mutated containing 11 distinct mutations in the heavy chain and 4 in the light chain compared to the Blood clone (Fig. 1D). Among these additional mutations were 2 HCDR3 (Gly111.1Ala and Ser112.3Asn) and 1 LCDR3 (Ser114Thr) mutations. For both heavy and light chains, the LN sample had lower replacement to silent mutation (R/S) ratios (Fig. 1D).

While previous studies have demonstrated that B cell lymphomas in SjD express stereotypic RhF idiotypes [7,9], no study to date has determined whether these malignant clones arise from the same clones responsible for autoimmune pathology by secreting RhF-cryoglobulins. Therefore, we performed mass spectrometry peptide sequencing of RhF IgM isolated from serum six months pre-lymphoma diagnosis. The serum RhF had the same clonal BCR sequence as the peripheral blood and LN IgMκ B cells (termed “Serum”, Fig. 1D). Intriguingly, the Serum RhF shared more mutations with the DLBCL than the Blood subclone, but lacked the additional CDR3 mutations observed in the DLBCL (Fig. 1D). Analysis of the amino acid substituting (nonsynonymous) mutations across the Blood, DLBCL and Serum identified a shared common ancestor containing two heavy and one light chain mutations before the subclones diverge. The Serum and DLBCL subclones shared twelve mutations suggesting a distinct trajectory from the peripheral B cell subclones (Blood and L117V), which contained seven distinct nonsynonymous mutations (Fig. 1E).

To assess the stability of the peripheral blood subclones (Blood or L117V) over time and determine whether the subclones move from the LN to blood, we performed deep IGHM sequencing on 5 million PBMCs per timepoint over 3 years (Fig. 1F). At the peak of the serum RhF titre (December 2021, six months pre-DLBCL diagnosis), approximately 3% of peripheral blood IGHM mRNA sequencing reads were the RhF clonal sequence. Remarkably, just three months after commencement of chemotherapy (October 2022), nearly three-quarters of IGHM reads were RhF, indicating possible persistence of the RhF clone, which reduced in the following years, consistent with immune system reconstitution (Fig. 1F). We next evaluated the frequency of reads for each RhF subclone circulating at these timepoints. The Blood (Fig. 1D) subclone dominated during early time-points, representing nearly two-thirds of the clonal sequence and persisted three months after chemotherapy. However, eleven months post-chemotherapy, the L117V subclone dominated the sequencing reads. The L117V variant persisted for at least the next year with the original Blood subclone sequence re-emerging to represent 10-15% of RhF reads more than fifteen months after chemotherapy (Fig. 1G). The DLBCL HCDR3 was not identified in the blood at any timepoint. Massively parallel sequencing of the malignant LN revealed 99.6% of IGHM sequencing reads contained the clonal RhF sequence. Among these, 96.7% had the DLBCL HCDR3 amino acid substitutions G113A and S115N, while 1.34% and 0.02% of sequencing reads were the Blood and L117V subclones, respectively. The Serum RhF sequence (Fig. 1D) was not detected among the peripheral blood and LN IGHM reads, indicating the daughter cells responsible for secreting RhF were localised to another compartment or present at a very low frequency beyond detection by massively parallel sequencing.

Functional testing rheumatoid factor clonal variants

Somatic hypermutation of the BCR has been shown to increase RhF affinity to self IgG and drive cryoprecipitation [11]. To investigate the function of BCR mutations in the clonal variants identified in distinct compartments, we synthesised recombinant IgM representing the subclones from peripheral B cells, serum and LN (Fig. 1D) and tested RhF activity by ELISA and heat-aggregated IgG pull-down assay. The blood derived variants, Blood and L117V, showed strong dose-dependent binding to IgG Fc by ELISA. In contrast, the UCA, with all mutations reverted to germline sequence, demonstrated weak but consistently detectable binding, particularly compared to the DLBCL subclone, where no binding was detected. Surprisingly, the serum RhF subclone also had no detectable binding by solid-phase IgG Fc ELISA (Fig. 2A). However, RhF activity was detected in the serum subclone using the in-solution IgG pull-down assay. The DLBCL was the only subclone to lack RhF activity on both assays (Fig. 2B). Despite displaying weak RhF activity, the UCA was the only recombinant IgM that cryoprecipitated when mixed with IgG (Fig. 2C).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that a B cell clone responsible for contributing to autoimmune pathology via the secretion of RhF IgM underwent malignant transformation in a patient with SjD. In fact, the serum RhF more closely resembled the DLBCL than the circulating RhF cells. This contrasts with our previous findings in SjD patients without lymphoma where the peripheral blood RhF clone was identical to the serum RhF [11]. The absence of circulating cells that carried the mutations observed in the serum and LN, suggests that the daughter cells that produced the serum RhF and DLBCL remained in the tissue or were present at levels below detection in the peripheral blood. The higher frequency of BCR mutations in the tissue-localised clones (Serum and DLBCL) supports the hypothesis that the tissue provides an ideal niche for mutation and contributes to the acquisition of lymphoma-driver mutations and malignant transformation.

Expression of the RhF subclones as recombinant IgM demonstrated that the DLBCL lost detectable reactivity to IgG Fc, most likely due to the acquisition of mutations in both the heavy and light chain CDR3. While this finding raises questions regarding antigen-dependent and antigen-independent signalling for the DLBCL [17], it is important to note the limitations of solid phase ELISA for determining RhF activity. A recent study measuring RhF activity using a cell line expressing an ancestral unmutated RhF clone showed binding to multimerised IgG yet the same clone did not bind to IgG by ELISA [12]. Therefore, it is plausible that the DLBCL may still exhibit low affinity binding for IgG Fc that cannot be detected by ELISA. Nevertheless, the DLBCL reactivity to IgG was reduced compared to the Serum and Blood subclones. This implies that the DLBCL may be less dependent on chronic BCR stimulation via antigen, possibly due to the acquisition of lymphoma driver mutations. Moreover, the lower replacement-silent mutation ratios in the DLBCL subclone (Fig. 1D) suggests that there is less antigen-driven somatic hypermutation in these cells reinforcing the idea of a reduced requirement for antigen stimulation [18,19]. Previously we and others demonstrated that BCR somatic hypermutation increased RhF activity and cryoprecipitation [11,12]. However, we also demonstrated other RhF subclones that had acquired additional BCR mutations that reduced RhF activity and corrected solubility problems. Similarly, in this case, the UCA showed the highest propensity to cryoaggregate. Together these findings imply that BCR mutations result in various protein folding and solubility outcomes and may be reflected by fluctuating RhF and/or cryoglobulin levels in the clinic and potentially intermittent cryovasculitis in the patient.

The finding that a RhF B cell clone transformed into a lymphoma in this SjD patient raises the question of whether treatment approaches to eradicate non-malignant RhF clonal expansions could be considered to prevent lymphoma. To answer this question, we would need to establish the generalisability of our findings, particularly how often expanded RhF clones become lymphomas, which would require large patient cohorts examined over decades. Moreover, whether it is possible to eradicate these clones with current treatments is another important question. Here, massively parallel sequencing demonstrated that the circulating (non-malignant) RhF clone re-emerged in the blood more than 12 months following the completion of chemotherapy. Intriguingly, a shift in clonal variants was observed with the L117V subclone dominating post-treatment. It is plausible that these circulating RhF clones carry lymphoma-driver mutations that may account for their persistence and re-emergence post-treatment [11]. An additional explanation is that memory B cells and antibody-secreting cells in anatomical niches may be resistant to the peripheral effects of therapy. LN biopsies from rheumatoid arthritis patients obtained 4-weeks post-rituximab revealed an overall decrease in B cells, but persistence of IgD−CD27+ switched memory and IgD−CD27− double negative B cells [20]. In the current study, BCR sequencing indicated a low frequency of sequences matching the blood RhF subclones present in the LN pre-treatment, suggesting that these clones in the LN may survive and recirculate post-treatment. It is unclear whether the malignant clone persisted post-treatment, as a LN biopsy post-treatment was not performed.

This study has some important limitations. Firstly, the investigation is based on a single patient due to the rarity of identifying and collecting longitudinal samples in multiple anatomical locations during malignant transformation, which can take years. In particular, our case report describes the DLBCL subtype of lymphoma in SjD which is rarer compared to the more common mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma [21]. However, MALT lymphomas in SjD also express stereotyped sequences recognising RhF [7,9] make our findings more generalisable. Furthermore, a recent study that identified clonally expanded B cells expressing RhF in the salivary glands of a SjD patient that transformed into a DLBCL two years later [22] is highly consistent with our findings. Our study adds to these findings by linking the malignant clone with the secreted RhF autoantibody and thereby confirming the pre-malignant status of expanded RhF-secreting B cells in a case of SjD.

Secondly, we are limited by the clinical samples available. It would be ideal to examine the bone marrow and salivary glands, as these are potential sites that may harbour the daughter cells responsible for secreting RhF, which were not detected in the blood or LN. Plasma cells, which typically reside in the bone marrow and may be an important source of autoantibodies, may persist despite B cell depleting therapy [23]. Our patient also never underwent a salivary gland biopsy for his diagnosis given that he satisfied classification criteria through other means [15]. Yet, a previous study found that RhF clones were present, at low levels, in inflamed salivary glands of SjD patients [24]. Finally, our study was limited to evaluating mutations in the BCR. However, a comparison of the broader mutational landscape of RhF B cells in the LN and blood would determine whether accumulation of lymphoma-driver gene mutations occur during the transformation to malignancy and if these mutations influence DLBCL antigen dependency and subclone dominance observed following treatment.

In summary, our study tracking the molecular evolution of a RhF clone in different anatomical niches over time provides proof-of-concept that autoimmunity and malignancy can arise from the same clone. We anticipate that the precise longitudinal tracking of RhF clones in patient populations has the potential to develop better monitoring and treatment strategies to slow or halt the progression to lymphoma in RhF-positive SjD patients.
