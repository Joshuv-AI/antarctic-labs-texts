Body sizes of species determining the success of biological control in a three-level food chain
Authors: William Campillay-Llanos, Samuel Ortega-Farías, Gonzalo A Díaz, Marlon M López-Flores
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiological control in plant–insect systems represents a fundamental challenge in theoretical ecology, particularly within agricultural systems. This challenge is amplified by climate change, which, through increasing temperatures, has induced variations in insect body size, altering their ecological interactions and, consequently, their abundance. Although allometric relationships provide a static description of the relationship between body size, metabolism, and population density, dynamic models are needed to adequately simulate agroecological systems. In this context, incorporating body size as a dynamic parameter in trophic models offers an analytic approach to linking climate-induced morphological changes with the effectiveness of biological control and the indirect effects on plants. The main objective of this study is to develop a mathematical model based on a three-level food chain (plant–pest-biological control), where body size is incorporated as a key parameter in the dynamics of the plant–pest biological control system. Specifically, the goals are to: 1) Identify the relationships between the body sizes of species that limit population densities and prevent coexistence. 2) Determine the body size relationships that promote the success of biological control. 3) Model the trophic cascade effect as a function of body size and analyze its indirect impact on plants. Through theoretical analysis, indicators based on body sizes are proposed to evaluate the effectiveness of biological control strategies. The results suggest that allometric relationships between body sizes can modify the qualitative behavior of the system, offering a potential tool for evaluating biological control strategies based on these indicators. This study offers a tool for evaluating biological control strategies based on key indicators, complementing experimental designs and advancing integrated pest management through an interdisciplinary framework in which biomathematical models serve as a foundation for digital agriculture.Subject terms: Ecology, Plant sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Rising temperatures are driving changes in body size across diverse species, suggesting that this could represent a third universal response to global warming1. Since body size influences an organism’s ability to dissipate or retain heat, increasing temperatures can affect both thermoregulation and energy efficiency. In this context, a reduction in body size could constitute an adaptive response driven by global warming. Several studies have suggested that the adaptive value of body size may be linked to its influence on fecundity or as a response to altered developmental patterns resulting from increased mortality under warmer conditions2–5. Additionally, body size can act as a limiting factor for oxygen availability, exerting selective pressure that favors a reduction in body size as temperatures rise6,7. Although these changes may be subtle, their repercussions could extend to larger scales, affecting agricultural production and crop sustainability. Eventually, this could impact food availability and compromise food security8. Insects are particularly affected, as their thermal limits are altered, impacting their metabolism and, consequently, their body size, making shifts in abundances likely5,9,10. However, it remains unclear how these body size changes will influence ecosystem dynamics or pest control strategies4.

A connection has been documented between body size and key biological attributes such as basal metabolism, reproductive rate, mortality, and population abundance, known as allometric relationships or scaling laws11–14. Mathematically, this relationship can be expressed as: , where Y represents one of these biological attributes, is a species-specific factor, m denotes body size, and is the allometric exponent15–18. Scaling relationships that describe variations in population density within ecological communities, such as the thinning law in plant ecology19, can be explained by observing how individuals of different sizes utilize available resources20. Data on xylem transport rates relative to stem diameter show that resource consumption rates in individual plants follow an approximate scale of relative to body mass, similar to metabolic rates in animals21. These relationships enable the integration of biological aspects into models that represent trophic chain dynamics, simulated through ordinary differential equations22–25. This approach is particularly relevant to plant–insect interactions, given the complexities of these systems26–29. To achieve a better understanding of emerging complexities, the patterns proposed in the Metabolic Theory of Ecology11 and mathematical modeling help establish relationships between body sizes, providing a clearer perspective on system structure. In a tritrophic system, three distinct techniques are available for measuring body size. In this study, to standardize these measurements, plant size is associated with leaf area30, while the body size of the pest and its control organism can be expressed in grams or length using the allometric cube law31.

In agricultural systems, it is essential to have reliable indicators derived from experimental and/or theoretical designs that facilitate the development of effective strategies for pest control through the introduction of natural biological control agents32,33. This approach aims to eradicate pests while minimizing the use of chemical products, thus promoting sustainable resource management. From a practical perspective, biological control strategies often involve experimental processes that can be costly and time-consuming; however, these can be complemented and optimized through theoretical research based on the analysis of mathematical models that describe the dynamics between plants, pests, and biological control agents34,35. A key indicator of pest control success is the body size of the species. Although the relationship between body size, plant growth rate, carrying capacity, predation rates (functional responses), and abundance has been extensively studied using systems of ordinary differential equations22–25,36,37, there remains limited research on mathematical relationships with ecological interpretation between body sizes for effective biological control and cascade effect modeling, which are crucial for the development of management strategies38. Furthermore, while body size relationships between two species (plant–herbivore; prey–predator)14 have been explored, the emergence of ecological patterns linking the body sizes of three species simultaneously remains an underexplored area.

In this study, we present a tritrophic model represented by a system of differential equations, whose parameters depend on species’ body sizes. Our research aims to address three fundamental questions: Which relationships between species’ body sizes limit population densities and prevent coexistence? What body size relationships ensure the success of biological control? How can the trophic cascade effect be modeled as a function of body size, and how can it impact indirectly plants? These questions aim to deepen our understanding of the role of body size in shaping ecological interactions and the effectiveness of biological control strategies. To address the first question, we explore the conditions under which the model generates bounded density trajectories, determine equilibrium points, and analyze system stability. In this context, stability is understood as the ability of the plant–pest-biological control system to withstand disturbances and maintain its structure and function over time, either by returning to an equilibrium state (resilience) or remaining within certain dynamic limits (persistence). To answer the second question, we derive a biological indicator from the modeled system, denoted as , which primarily depends on body sizes. This parameter is analogous to the basic reproductive number in epidemiology and, in the context of plant–pest biological control, represents the predator’s ability to produce offspring throughout its lifetime after being introduced into a stable prey population. If , both the biological control agent and the pest population will eventually disappear; conversely, if , both will persist in the system39. Finally, to evaluate the impact of biological control on plants, we consider previous theoretical ecology studies suggesting that the indirect effects of predators on basal resources may depend on body size40. We model the trophic cascade effect to identify which body sizes exert greater pressure on plants and how these effects influence system dynamics.

This analytical approach complements the broader understanding gained through simulation modelling41 and provides a structured and transparent framework for incorporating body size into ecological models. Framed within ecological theory, it provides a scientific basis for predicting potential behaviours of pest control systems and for designing strategies in agroecological contexts affected by body size modifications induced by rising temperatures. By connecting morphological traits with ecosystem-level outcomes, this modelling framework supports multidisciplinary collaboration with entomologists and other specialists. It can also help the design of experiments aimed at determining optimal body size configurations for effective biological control, and promote clear documentation, validation, and communication of modelling assumptions and results, as recommended in practical ecological modelling approaches42. In this way, mathematical modelling emerges as an accessible and conceptually robust tool with the potential to support applied research, foster a deeper understanding of natural systems, and contribute to more informed decision-making in the context of digital agriculture.

Discussion

Insect pest outbreaks affecting agricultural production represent a critical threat to food security, and management strategies can become increasingly complex due to adaptive responses, particularly changes in body size driven by rising temperatures7,53–55. These temperature-induced shifts can alter insect body size, potentially impacting their interactions with natural resources. Developing management strategies based on easily measurable indicators is essential to address this challenge. This study proposes body size-dependent indicators within a three-level food chain (plant–pest–biological control). These indicators assess abundance, persistence, potential extinctions within biological control, and trophic cascade effects, providing a framework to evaluate and optimize pest management in dynamic ecological contexts. The model from which these indicators are derived is based on a system of differential equations parameterized according to body size, in accordance with the Metabolic Theory of Ecology11,23–25. Recent research has studied the dynamic structure of the model considering a Holling Type I functional response, in order to connect coexistence theory25. Additionally, this type of model has been analyzed in detail by Kuznetsov et al.48, who provided an in-depth view of the possible bifurcations described in Eqs. (11) and (12). In our proposal, we present an applied approach that offers a better understanding of how changes in body size impact ecological mechanisms and the structure of the tritrophic dynamic system represented56. Equations (9) and (10) (which describe population densities without biological control), along with Eqs. (13)–(16) (representing the coexistence of the three species), illustrate the dependence of coexistence on body size, providing a valuable perspective for studying strong and weak trophic interactions in ecological communities57,58.

Environmental changes influence body size, which in turn affects predator behavior, altering trophic interactions and the physiology of herbivores54, yet there are no biological indicators that account for these factors. This study presents a theoretical proposal for an indicator based on Eq. (5) to establish a limit on species abundance. Experimentally, it could support the design of studies similar to those conducted in the United States, such as the case of the spruce budworm (Choristoneura fumiferana), which has migrated northward with reduced populations at lower latitudes, and the hemlock woolly adelgid (Adelges tsugae), which has similarly expanded its distribution northward while declining in the south due to heat intolerance in young nymphs56. In conjunction with this, the relationships between body sizes in Eqs. (6), (7), and (8) could be used to indicate potential extinction conditions, anticipate critical experimental situations, and contribute to integrated pest management from a multidisciplinary perspective59.

To address one of the central questions of this research What body size relationships ensure the success of biological control? we proposed an indicator that determines pest extinction and resource persistence. This indicator, defined in Eqs. (17) and (19)39,60, establishes quantitative relationships between body sizes and has the potential to be evaluated in field experiments. Figure 4 illustrates two contrasting scenarios. When , the system exhibits initial oscillations reflecting the pest’s impact on plant abundance. In contrast, if , biological control is immediate, allowing plant abundance to reach its carrying capacity in the short term. Similarly, Fig. 6 shows that when , biological control is effective, and plant abundance stabilises at its carrying capacity. Conversely, if , population densities tend to zero, which could eventually lead to resource loss. These results highlight the practical significance of this study by providing quantitative relationships that could be used in pest control programmes. For instance, the global costs associated with managing the diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella) range between 4 and 5 billion USD annually61. Furthermore, since pest responses to global warming vary across latitudes, body-size-based indicators could facilitate local assessments for entomologists and farmers. The integration of these indicators into experimental designs has the potential to optimise management strategies and reduce operational costs54. More broadly, the proposed model enables a quantitative assessment applicable to diverse trophic networks, supporting an abstract yet cost-effective approach to pest control in agricultural and forestry systems56,62.

To investigate top-down mechanisms, we introduce a new indicator that models the trophic cascade effect, as defined in Eq. (19) and illustrated in its three-dimensional representation in Fig. 7. Classic studies on trophic cascades, such as McCann et al.57, have emphasized the importance of weak trophic interactions in stabilizing ecological networks, while Yodzis and Innes22 showed that body size is a key determinant of consumption rates and consumer–resource dynamics. In this work, we build on these foundations by introducing an explicit indicator that models the trophic cascade effect as a function of the relative body sizes of the plant () and the biological control agent (), defined in Eq. (19) and illustrated in three dimensions in Fig. 7. For the parameter values considered in that figure, we observe that small plant body sizes () combined with larger sizes of the control agent () lead to more pronounced cascade effects on plant dynamics, thereby integrating trophic structure and morphological variability into a dynamic framework with potential empirical applicability. This trophic cascade model is consistent with empirical observations from a tritrophic system in the Sierra Nevada, where parasitoids regulate the population of a weevil that preys on the seeds of Hormathophylla spinosa, indirectly benefiting the plant63. Similarly, studies in soybean crops have shown that an increase in spider populations reduces foliar damage by controlling herbivorous insects, whereas their removal leads to greater damage in certain years. These findings confirm the existence of a trophic cascade, where predators indirectly benefit plants by regulating herbivore populations64. The applicability of this model is not necessarily limited to simplified habitats, where herbivore control relies on a single parasitoid species. Instead, it can be extended to agroecological systems, where multiple generalist predators contribute to herbivore regulation. This distinction suggests that trophic cascades in biological control may be stronger but less stable than those found in natural food webs, where regulation is distributed among multiple natural enemies65. From this perspective, we can explore the “enemy hypothesis”, which is widely supported in agricultural systems and posits that greater plant diversity increases the abundance of natural enemies, thereby enhancing herbivore control33,66,67. A key challenge for modeling will be to incorporate trophic cascades that depend on multiple body-size relationships, better reflecting the complexity.

One of the main limitations of this model is the lack of incorporation of evolutionary elements, such as adaptation to body size, which could provide a more integrated view of ecological and evolutionary processes from a tritrophic perspective33. Another parameter that could be incorporated is temperature, to explore its ecological consequences and analyze the relationship between temperature and body size11,68,69. Although chaotic dynamics are beyond the scope of this study, our approach suggests that relationships between body sizes, as presented in Eqs. (11) and (12), could complicate integrated pest management. The mathematical structure of the model has also been explored in the works of Bo Deng70–72, who characterized the chaotic behavior of the system. This has significant implications for pest management, as predator biomass can peak at the onset of a chaotic state70,73, potentially providing valuable insight into understanding these complexities74. On the other hand, recent advances in remote sensing and spatial modeling have significantly improved our understanding of plant diversity and characteristics75–80. In this study, we identify as the leaf area, which could facilitate the integration of crop information with the proposed indicator , fostering interdisciplinary collaboration between ecological aspects of tritrophic chains and technological tools. Finally, the modelling proposal presented here is a theoretical exploration, with a strong focus on practical applications aimed at supporting integrated pest management and advancing digital agriculture through data-driven decision-making.
