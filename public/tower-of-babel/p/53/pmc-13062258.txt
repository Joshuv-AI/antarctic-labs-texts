Feasibility and Outcomes of Clazosentan in Ruptured Vertebrobasilar Dissecting Aneurysms: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Takuma Maeda, Tatsuki Kimura, Hiroki Kobayashi, Akio Teranishi, Yushiro Take, Tomomichi Kayahara, Kaima Suzuki, Masataka Yoshimura, Shinya Kohyama, Hiroki Kurita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSubarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) caused by ruptured vertebrobasilar dissecting aneurysms (VBDAs) is associated with high morbidity and mortality rates. Although clazosentan is effective in preventing cerebral vasospasm after SAH due to saccular aneurysms, its efficacy and safety have not yet been established in patients with ruptured VBDAs. We aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of clazosentan in patients with SAH due to ruptured VBDAs.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients treated for SAH due to ruptured VBDAs at a single institution between January 2017 and March 2025. The patients were divided into the clazosentan (n = 10) and control (n = 28) groups. The baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes were compared between the groups.ResultsA total of 38 patients were included in the study. The severity of SAH did not differ significantly between the two groups (World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grades IV–V: 60.0% vs. 64.3%, p > 0.99). No significant differences in the incidences of symptomatic vasospasm (10.0% vs. 14.3%, p > 0.99) or delayed cerebral ischemia (10.0% vs. 10.7%, p > 0.99) were observed between the clazosentan and control groups. Pulmonary edema occurred more frequently in the clazosentan group than in the control group (30.0% vs. 10.7%, p = 0.31). However, unfavorable outcomes at discharge were comparable between the clazosentan and control groups (40.0% vs. 60.7%, p = 0.29).ConclusionClazosentan use in patients with SAH due to VBDAs appeared to be feasible and generally well tolerated without apparent excess harm. Larger prospective studies with adequate sample size are warranted to clarify the effect on clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Vertebrobasilar dissecting intracranial aneurysms (VBDAs) are a distinct cause of stroke, particularly in younger individuals, and they present clinical challenges [1]. Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) caused by VBDA is characterized by high severity at onset and a correspondingly high mortality rate [2] as well as a significant risk of rebleeding, which necessitates urgent and definitive treatment to prevent catastrophic outcomes [3, 4].

Similar to saccular aneurysms, cerebral vasospasm (CVS) remains the primary cause of delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) and subsequent poor neurological outcomes in patients with ruptured VBDAs [5]. Clazosentan, a selective endothelin A receptor antagonist, reduced CVS‐related events in randomized trials for aneurysmal SAH [6]. However, because VBDAs exhibit distinct vessel wall pathologies [7], including intramural hematoma, intimal flap formation, and disruption of endothelial integrity, the pathophysiology of CVS and the response to clazosentan may differ from those of saccular aneurysms. The efficacy and safety of clazosentan for VBDAs have not yet been established. Therefore, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of clazosentan in patients with SAH caused by ruptured VBDAs.

Methods

The authors confirm that the data supporting the findings of this study are available within the article.

Patient Selection and Ethical Considerations

This retrospective study included patients who underwent surgical procedures for SAH caused by ruptured VBDAs at our institution between January 2017 and March 2025. From an initial cohort of 544 patients treated for aneurysmal SAH, 478 were excluded with saccular cerebral aneurysms. To improve cohort homogeneity and address baseline imbalance, particularly aneurysm location, we restricted the analytic cohort to VBDAs treated with endovascular procedures. We excluded anterior circulation cases, peripheral branch‐related dissections (e.g., posterior inferior cerebellar artery, anterior inferior cerebellar artery, and superior cerebellar artery), and cases treated with surgical trapping. In addition, we excluded one patient with a mycotic intracranial aneurysm, patients who were not treated within 72 h, and those with regrowth after clipping. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Saitama Medical University International Medical Center (Nos. 2024‐035 and 2025‐049), which waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Data Extraction and Clinical Endpoints

We extracted several categories of data from the patients' medical records. The baseline characteristics included age, sex, and comorbidities. We also collected information on the initial clinical and radiological findings, including the Fisher group and World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) grade, as well as aneurysm location. SAH severity was categorized into two groups based on WFNS grades: WFNS grades I–III as mild SAH and WFNS grades IV–V as severe SAH. The primary outcome was the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score at discharge, which was categorized as favorable (0–3) or unfavorable (4–6).

Definitions, Treatment Protocols, and Management

Key clinical events were defined according to established criteria. Angiographic CVS was defined as a narrowing of ≥ 34% in the internal carotid artery or its major branches, as visualized on digital subtraction angiography, three‐dimensional computed tomography angiography (CTA), or magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), which is consistent with the findings of a previous study [6]. Given that this study focused on posterior circulation lesions, this definition was also applied to the vertebral and basilar arteries and their major branches. Symptomatic CVS was identified by the development of new focal neurological deficits, a decline in consciousness, or cognitive dysfunction attributable to CVS occurring 3–14 days after SAH, after excluding other causes. Symptomatic CVS was considered present only when there was sustained neurological worsening, such as a ≥ 2‐point drop in the Glasgow Coma Scale or a ≥ 2‐point rise on the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale. In line with expert consensus, DCI was defined as new neurological deterioration or a new cerebral infarction not present on imaging up to 48 h after aneurysm occlusion and occurring within 6 weeks of SAH, and angiographic confirmation of CVS was not required [8, 9, 10].

Shunt‐dependent hydrocephalus was defined as hydrocephalus for which a permanent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) diversion procedure—ventriculoperitoneal or lumboperitoneal shunt placement—was performed. Indications for shunt placement were determined based on the following criteria: (1) failure to wean from temporary CSF diversion (external ventricular drainage or spinal/lumbar drainage), and/or (2) ventriculomegaly after day 14, defined as an Evans index (EI) greater than on admission or an EI > 0.30. Clinical improvement following a CSF tap test was also considered as supportive evidence when available.

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia. Systemic heparinization was initiated to achieve an activated clotting time > 250 s. Until April 2022, the following conventional protocol was used in all patients. Patients received extracellular fluids (at least 2000 mL/day) to maintain mild hypervolemia. Patients were generally administered 90 mg/day of intravenous fasudil hydrochloride during the CVS period. As an adjunct agent, intravenous ozagrel (80 mg/day) was also administered during the CVS period. Cilostazol (200 mg) and atorvastatin (10–40 mg) were given orally or in a gastric tube until at least day 14. Except for fasudil, the use of these adjunct agents was at the treating physician's discretion.

Since May 2022, clazosentan has been introduced as part of a simplified management approach. The infusion rate of extracellular fluids was limited to 1 mL/kg/hr and adjusted based on the 24‐h water balance to maintain normovolemia. Patients with a positive water balance of 1000 mL/day or more received 5–10 mg intravenous furosemide. Clazosentan was administered for up to 14 days after SAH onset and was discontinued if fluid retention became difficult to control. The use of other adjunct medications for CVS was managed at the treating physician's discretion.

MRI/MRA or CTA was performed four times after the procedure, on postoperative day 2 (±1 day), day 7 (±1 day), day 10 (±1 day), and day 13 (±1 day). Additional imaging was obtained as needed when new neurological symptoms developed.

When symptomatic CVS occurred, rescue therapy with intra‐arterial fasudil infusion, percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA), or a combination of these treatments was considered.

Statistical Analyses

This study adhered to the STrengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational research. Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between the groups. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test. The normality of the distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For non‐normally distributed data, groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, and the data are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). All tests were two‐tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using the JMP Pro 16 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

The median age of patients was 53 years (IQR, 45–65), and 16 (42.1%) were female. Fourteen patients (36.8%) had a medical history of hypertension, 4 (10.5%) had diabetes, and 14 (36.8%) had hyperlipidemia. Fifteen patients (39.5%) presented with headaches, and 23 patients (60.5%) presented with disturbance of consciousness. Severe SAH was observed in 24 patients (63.2%). Most patients were classified into Fisher group 3 (34/38, 89.5%). The vertebral artery was the most common aneurysm site (37/38, 97.4%), followed by the basilar artery (1/38, 2.6%).

All 38 patients underwent endovascular trapping or coiling, and an occipital artery–posterior inferior cerebellar artery bypass was performed as an adjunct in one (2.6%) patient. Overall, surgical complications occurred in 8 patients (21.1%), including ischemic complications in 6 (15.8%) and hemorrhagic complications in 2 (5.3%). Recanalization was observed in 5 patients (13.2%), and 4 patients (10.5%) required retreatment for recanalization. Clazosentan was administered to 10 patients (26.3%) for a median duration of 13 days (IQR, 13–14) and was discontinued due to fluid retention in 2 patients (20.0%). CVS occurred in 8 patients (21.1%), with symptomatic CVS in 5 patients (13.2%) and angiographic CVS in 3 patients (7.9%). Symptomatic CVS occurred a median of 8 days (IQR, 8–10) after SAH onset. Among them, 3 patients (7.9%) underwent rescue therapy (PTA, n = 1; and intra‐arterial fasudil infusion combined with PTA, n = 2). Four patients (10.5%) developed DCI. Eight patients (21.1%) showed shunt‐dependent hydrocephalus. Pulmonary edema was observed in 6 patients (15.8%), and pleural effusion was observed in 10 patients (26.3%). The median length of stay was 33 days (IQR, 24–54). An unfavorable outcome (mRS ≥ 4) at discharge was observed in 21 patients (55.3%, Supplemental Table 1).

Comparisons of the characteristics, treatments, and outcomes of patients treated with and without clazosentan are shown in Table 2 and supplemental Table 2. The median age of the clazosentan group was 52 years (IQR, 45–57) compared with 55 years (IQR, 46–65) in the control group (p = 0.38). Female sex was more common in the clazosentan group than in the control group (50.0% vs. 39.3%, p = 0.83). Hypertension (30.0% vs. 39.3%, p = 0.72), diabetes mellitus (0% vs. 14.3%, p = 0.56), and hyperlipidemia (20.0% vs. 42.9%, p = 0.27) did not differ significantly between the groups. Headache (60.0% vs. 32.1%) and consciousness disturbances (40.0% vs. 67.9%) were similar between the groups (p = 0.15). The severity of SAH was not significantly different between the two groups (p > 0.99). The thick SAH (Fisher group 3) was comparable (100% vs. 85.7%, p = 0.56). The incidence of surgical complications (20.0% vs. 21.4%, p > 0.99), recanalization (30.0% vs. 7.1%, p = 0.11), symptomatic CVS (10.0% vs. 14.3%, p > 0.99), DCI (10.0% vs. 10.7%, p > 0.99), shunt‐dependent hydrocephalus (30.0% vs. 17.9%, p = 0.41), and pleural effusion (30.0% vs 25.0%, p > 0.99) were not significantly different between the groups. Pulmonary edema occurred more frequently in the clazosentan group than in the control group, but the difference was not statistically significant (30.0% vs 10.7%, p = 0.31). The median length of stay was 41 days (IQR, 26–53) for the clazosentan group and 31 days (IQR, 24–53) for the control group (p = 0.42). Unfavorable outcomes (mRS ≥ 4) were comparable between the clazosentan and control groups (40.0% vs. 60.7%, p = 0.29). The distribution of mRS scores at discharge is shown in Supplemental Table 1.

Discussion

In this single‐center cohort of patients with SAH due to VBDAs, clazosentan use appeared to be feasible without apparent excess harm compared with conventional management. There is no definitive evidence to support the use of clazosentan for VBDAs, and its effect on CVS prevention remains uncertain. Our findings suggest that clazosentan use for VBDA, similar to its use in saccular aneurysmal SAH, may predispose patients to fluid retention, while its impact on clinical outcomes appears to be limited.

Compared with conventional treatments, such as fasudil hydrochloride, clazosentan has demonstrated increased efficacy in preventing DCI and reducing CVS‐related morbidity and mortality in patients with SAH [11, 12, 13]. Similarly, when compared with cilostazol, a phosphodiesterase 3 inhibitor known for its antiplatelet and vasodilatory effects, clazosentan ranks among the effective prophylactic therapies for reducing morbidity and mortality after aneurysmal SAH, although nimodipine and cilostazol may be broadly effective according to network meta‐analyses [14]. However, there are no comprehensive reports on the use of clazosentan for VBDAs, and only a few case reports have been published. Yoshida et al. reported a case of a VBDA in which the therapeutic effect of clazosentan appeared to be limited [15]. Previous randomized trials and meta‐analyses of clazosentan have also focused on saccular aneurysmal SAH, and evidence regarding its use in dissecting aneurysms remains extremely limited [16].

Clazosentan, a selective endothelin A receptor antagonist, inhibits endothelin‐1–mediated vasoconstriction and has been shown to reduce angiographic vasospasm after aneurysmal SAH [16, 17, 18]. However, its mechanistic effects in VBDAs remain unclear. Given the lack of physiological measurements in this study, including cerebral blood flow or related biomarkers, any potential effects on microcirculation or systemic perfusion remain speculative [19]. In addition, the distinct pathological features of VBDAs, such as intramural hematoma and endothelial disruption, may influence the response to clazosentan [15].

Clazosentan is also associated with fluid retention due to increased vascular permeability, which may lead to complications such as pleural effusion or pulmonary edema [6, 20, 21, 22, 23]. In our cohort, pulmonary edema was more frequently observed in the clazosentan group, although the difference was not statistically significant. Despite a tendency toward greater fluid retention, clinical outcomes were comparable between groups. This may be partly related to the relatively younger age of patients in our cohort, as younger patients generally have a more favorable tolerance to complications such as pleural effusion and pulmonary edema [24].

Limitations

This single‐center retrospective study was vulnerable to unmeasured confounding factors, with strong time‐dependent confounding as a dominant limitation. Clazosentan was introduced in May 2022 concurrently with a revision of our institutional fluid management protocol, such that our results may reflect a care bundle (clazosentan plus fluid strategy and other secular era effects) rather than clazosentan alone.

The sample size limited precision and statistical power, particularly for safety and DCI endpoints. Therefore, the confidence intervals should be interpreted with caution. In the revision phase, we added cases from April 2024 to March 2025, a period that falls entirely within the clazosentan era, potentially leading to differential enrichment in the clazosentan group.

We used mRS at discharge as the primary functional outcome measure, which limits assessment of longer‐term recovery. Discharge mRS may also be influenced by non‐neurological factors (e.g., medical complications, rehabilitation intensity, and discharge disposition), which is particularly relevant given the discordance between similar CVS/DCI rates and the observed difference in discharge mRS.

Recanalization was observed only in patients with favorable outcomes, resulting in a strong apparent association. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as it likely reflects reverse causation and survivorship bias. Patients with better neurological status are more likely to survive long enough to undergo follow‐up imaging, thereby increasing the likelihood of detecting recanalization. Therefore, recanalization should not be interpreted as a protective factor but rather as a marker of patients with a more favorable clinical course.

Hydrocephalus detection and management may have varied across patients and over time, introducing residual confounding and ascertainment bias. In this retrospective study, latent or underdiagnosed hydrocephalus cannot be fully excluded and may have affected discharge outcomes.

Finally, this study lacked physiological and biomarker data (e.g., cerebral blood flow, transcranial Doppler, or related markers), limiting mechanistic interpretation. Therefore, any potential effects beyond clinically observable variables should be considered speculative.

Conclusions

In this hypothesis‐generating study, clazosentan use in patients with SAH due to VBDAs was associated with a tendency toward fluid retention but appeared to be feasible and generally well tolerated without apparent excess harm. Larger prospective studies with adequate sample size are warranted to clarify the impact of clazosentan on clinical outcomes.
