Association of hospital profit status with operative rates for acute appendicitis
Authors: Konmal Ali, Sona Mahrokhi, Amulya Vadlakonda, Troy Coaston, Melissa Justo, Deep Mehta, Edward Livingston, Peyman Benharash
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough appendicitis can now be treated nonoperatively, the uptake of nonoperative management of appendicitis (NOM) has been slow. We explored patient and hospital factors that might explain variation in appendectomy and NOM rates.MethodsAll adult hospitalizations for appendicitis were tabulated from the 2019–2022 Nationwide Readmissions Database. Initial, exploratory analyses revealed that appendectomy rates were lower in not-for-profit (NFP) relative to for-profit (FP) hospitals. Bayesian logistic regression and Fairlie decomposition were employed to explain the bases for these differences.ResultsThere were 4394 NFP and 818 FP hospitals. When compared with FP facilities, NFP hospitals had a smaller proportion of appendicitis cases treated by appendectomy (82.9 ± 11.8 v. 89.7 ± 8.0) (mean ± SD) (Cohen's d: −0.70) and less than one-half the amount of charges $44,713 [30,217, 65,442] vs. $93,078 [64,171, 129,301] (median ± IQR) (Cohen's d > −0.7, medium effect size). When estimated from regression equations, the gap between NFP and FP hospital appendectomy rates was 7.5 %. Total charges explained almost all of the gap between NFP and FP hospital appendectomy rates (0.068/0.090 = 75 %) of the gap. The presence of complicated appendicitis and EGS volume reduced the gap by −0.013 (−0.013/0.090 = 14 %).ConclusionAppendectomy rates for the treatment of appendicitis were greater in FP hospitals with almost all the difference in the rates explained by much higher charges observed in FP hospitals. The potential for greater profit from appendectomy as compared with NOM of appendicitis may explain why appendectomy is more common in FP hospitals.Keywords: Appendicitis, Nonoperative management, Center-level, Nationwide Readmissions Database

Introduction

Several randomized trials have shown that NOM of appendicitis yields similar outcomes to appendectomy [[1], [2], [3]]. Patient factors including advancing age, female sex, and perforation are associated with increased likelihood of the pursuit of NOM [4]. While is it well established that appendicitis can be managed nonoperatively, it is not known how non-operative management (NOM) of appendicitis is accepted by patients and surgeons.

The proportion of patients treated surgically for appendicitis is known to vary across facilities [5,6]. Within a consortium of pediatric hospitals, Rangel et al. found considerable center-level variation in NOM for uncomplicated acute appendicitis after adjusting for patient factors [5]. Similarly, several multicenter studies have shown that hospital type, geographic location, and institutional protocols influence the use of NOM [7]. Urban teaching hospitals favor operative management in children with perforated appendicitis, compared with their rural counterparts [5,8].

Although patient factors have been cited as potential sources of variation in appendectomy v NOM rates, little is known about the influence of hospital characteristics on these rates [9,10]. Conceivably, appendectomy is profitable at some centers. If true, some hospitals might incentivize or facilitate operative management of appendicitis [11]. We hypothesized that hospital profit status might influence appendectomy v NOM rates.

Methods

All adult (≥18 years) non-elective primary admissions of acute appendicitis were tabulated from the 2019–2022 Nationwide Readmissions Database using previously validated International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes [12]. As the largest all-payer national database capturing readmissions, the NRD uses validated survey-weighting methods to provide accurate estimates for ∼60 % of all U.S. hospitalizations. Readmissions are tracked across hospitals within each calendar year using unique patient linkage numbers. Records representing inter-hospital transfers or missing key data on age, sex, cost, income or payer status, were not considered (2.6 %). To enhance reliability of the findings, only centers performing at least 15 annual appendectomy cases were included, while hospitals with 100 % non-operative or operative management, were excluded (Supplementary Fig. 1). Due to the deidentified nature of NRD data, this study was exempted from full review by the Institutional Review Board at the University of California, Los Angeles.

Patient and hospital characteristics including age, sex, insurance coverage, comorbidities, hospital teaching status and for-profit (FP), and not-for-profit (NFP) ownership were defined in accordance with the NRD Data Dictionary [13]. The burden of chronic illness was quantified using the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, a validated composite of 30 comorbidities. Individual comorbidities and postoperative complications were classified according to previously published work [14]. Costs were calculated via the application of center-specific cost-to charge-ratios to overall hospitalization charges followed by inflation adjustment to the 2022 Personal Health Care Price Index [15]. Separately, the annual caseload of emergency general surgery (EGS) operations at each center was tabulated using validated ICD codes. EGS were defined as non-elective admissions for large bowel resection, small bowel resection, repair of a perforated ulcer, lysis of peritoneal adhesions, laparotomy, appendectomy and cholecystectomy. Centers were categorized into low-, medium-, and high-volume tertiles based on the annual number of EGS hospitalizations.

To account for disease severity, complicated appendicitis included acute cases with generalized peritonitis, localized peritonitis, perforation, and abscess. Uncomplicated appendicitis was defined as acute appendicitis and acute appendicitis without perforation or gangrene as previously described. Patients were grouped into Complicated and Uncomplicated cohorts based on the above classification. Multilevel, mixed-effects logistic regressions were developed to estimate in-hospital mortality and postoperative complications. The first level incorporated patient factors such as age, sex, and comorbidities (congestive heart failure, arrhythmia, liver disease, coagulopathies, peripheral vascular disease, smoker status, hypertension, obesity) while hospital identifiers comprised the second level. Bayesian logistic regression methodology was used to estimate the random intercept (log odds of the baseline event rate), which is the risk-adjusted preference for operative management at the center level. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (University of Auckland, New Zealand). Binary and categorical variables were converted to factors, and the appendectomy outcome was encoded as an integer (0/1). Continuous variables (age, Elixhauser comorbidity score) were standardized to have a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1. Observations with missing values for appendectomy, FP status, or discharge weight (weights used for estimating population counts), or with non-positive discharge weight values, were excluded to ensure valid weighting and model fitting. The final dataset included 84,710 discharges after filtering.

Statistical analysis

We employed a Bayesian mixed-effects logistic regression model to account for the survey design and the hierarchical structure of hospital admissions across years. The model included fixed effects for all predictors and a random intercept for hospital admission year to capture year-specific variation. The outcome (appendectomy) was modeled using a Bernoulli distribution with a logit link function. Due to limitations in the brms package (version 2.22.0) preventing the direct use of survey weights (discharge weight) in Bernoulli models with random effects, we implemented a resampling approach. The discharge weight weights were normalized to sum to 1, and the dataset was resampled with replacement proportional to these weights, maintaining the original sample size. This resampling, performed with a fixed seed for reproducibility, effectively weighted observations according to the survey design.

Weakly informative priors were set as normal (0,2) for fixed effects and intercept, and normal (0,1) for the random effect standard deviation, with Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) control parameters adjusted (adapt_delta = 0.99, max_treedepth = 15) to eliminate divergent transitions. The model was fitted using four MCMC chains, each with 2000 iterations (1000 burn-in). Convergence was assessed via R-hat values (target <1.1) and effective sample sizes.

To evaluate the effect of hospital ownership on appendectomy probability, we generated posterior predictive distributions. A prediction dataset was created with FP status varying (0 = not-FP, 1 = FP), while holding other predictors at reference levels (e.g., sex = male, income = lowest quartile, payer = Medicare, all clinical factors = 0) and standardized age and Elixhauser score at their means. The hospital admission year was set to a randomly selected year. Posterior predictions were obtained from the fitted model, yielding probability distributions for appendectomy under each FP status scenario. These distributions were visualized using kernel density plots, with separate densities for not-FP (blue) and FP (red) hospitals.

Software and reproducibility

Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1) using the dplyr (version 1.1.4), brms (version 2.22.0), survey (version 4.2-1), and ggplot2 (version 3.5.1) packages. The resampling step used a fixed seed (123) for reproducibility. Code is available upon request.

Fairlie decomposition

Hospital discharge data was examined for differences in appendectomy rates between FP and NFP by Fairlie decomposition analysis. The analysis was restricted to records with complete data on all variables of interest, resulting in a final sample of 37,157 observations after excluding 35 records with missing values. Each record was weighted by a discharge weight (discharge weight) to account for the sampling design and ensure representativeness of the hospital population.

Variables

The primary outcome was appendectomy, a binary variable (1 = appendectomy performed, 0 = not performed). The grouping variable was for profit, a binary indicator (1 = FP hospital, 0 = non-profit hospital). Covariates included Continuous Variables: Appendicitis volume (appendicitisvol), emergency general surgery volume (egsvol), hospital bed size (hosp_bedsize), and total charges (totchg), all standardized to a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1 using weighted means and standard deviations based on discharge weight, Categorical Variables: Teaching status (teaching, binary), presence of complicated appendicitis (complicated, binary), perforated appendicitis (perfappy, binary), and patient income quartile (income, 4-level categorical variable) and a weighting variable: Discharge weight (discharge weight), a positive numeric variable reflecting the sampling weight of each record.

Statistical analysis

A two-stage approach was used to investigate the appendectomy rate gap between FP and NFP: weighted logistic regression and weighted Fairlie decomposition. Weighted Logistic Regression: Separate logistic regression models were fitted for FP and NFP to estimate the association between covariates and the probability of appendectomy.

Income was included as a set of three dummy variables (reference: lowest quartile). Continuous variables were standardized using weighted means and standard deviations to facilitate comparison. Models were weighted by discharge weight to account for the sampling design, implemented using the glm function in R with the weights argument. Coefficients were interpreted as the change in log-odds for a one-standard-deviation increase in continuous predictors or a category change for categorical predictors. Average marginal effects (AMEs) were computed using the margins package to estimate the change in probability of appendectomy for a one-unit increase in raw annual appendicitis case volume, scaled by its weighted standard deviation.

Weighted Fairlie decomposition

To decompose the difference in appendectomy rates between FP and NFP, we applied a weighted Fairlie decomposition, an extension of the Oaxaca-Blinder method suitable for binary outcomes [16]. The Fairlie decomposition partitions the gap in weighted mean appendectomy rates into and explained component that is attributable to differences in covariate distributions and an unexplained component that is attributable to differences in coefficients. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1), with results visualized using ggplot2 and formatted into HTML using stargazer and base64enc.

Categorical variables are reported as percentages (%) while continuous variables are shown as means with standard deviation or medians with interquartile range (IQR), if not normally distributed. Bivariate comparisons were performed using the Pearson χ2 and Mann-Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Standardized mean differences (SMD) were used to demonstrate effect size, with significance defined as >0.10. Covariate selection for all models was guided by Elastic net regularization, an automated technique which optimizes model parsimony while reducing collinearity among factors [17]. Linear and logistic model estimates are reported as beta-coefficients (β) or adjusted odds ratios (AOR), respectively, both with 95 % confidence intervals (CI).

Effect sizes were considered as small if Cohen's d ≥ 0.2, medium if ≥ = 0.5 and large if ≥0.8. Effect sizes expressed as odds ratios were considered as small if the OR ≥ 1.42 (or ≤0.70), medium if ≥2.39 (or ≤0.42) and large if ≥4.04 (or ≤0.25) based on the following relationships:

Charge/cost analysis

The CCR was calculated as the ratio of total charges to costs (totchg/cost). Discharges with missing values, non-positive costs, non-positive survey weights, or infinite/undefined CCR were excluded. Age and Elixhauser scores were standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1) to improve model convergence. Payer type and complicated status were treated as categorical variables, with payer categorized as Medicaid, Medicare, Private Insurance, or Other Payer, and complicated status as a binary indicator (0 = uncomplicated, 1 = complicated). Hospital profit status was a binary factor (0 = non-profit, 1 = for-profit). After filtering, the analytic dataset comprised 238,719 discharges.

Bayesian regression was performed to estimate the posterior probability distributions of CCR, stratified by hospital profit status and adjusted for age, Elixhauser score, payer type, and complicated status. The model assumed a log-normal distribution for CCR, given its right-skewed nature (range: 0.8489–17.5439, median: 4.0323). To account for the survey design, we normalized survey weights (discharge weight) to sum to the sample size and used weighted subsampling to select 10,000 discharges, with replacement, proportional to the normalized weights (discharge weight_normalized). This approach approximated the survey-weighted likelihood while reducing computational burden.

Results

Hospital characteristics

Of an estimated 414,342 appendicitis records, 170,709 comprised the Uncomplicated cohort. There were 4394 NFP and 818 FP hospitals (Table 1). When compared with FP facilities, NFP hospitals had a smaller proportion of appendicitis cases treated by appendectomy (82.9 ± 11.8 v. 89.7 ± 8.0, Cohen's d: −0.70) and less than one-half the value of charges $44,713 [30,217–65,442] vs. $93,078 [64171–129,301] (Cohen's d > −0.7). There was no difference in the cost per patient in NFP facilities ($11,724 (8693–$16,583) v. FP hospitals ($10,473 (8188–$13,828, Cohen's d = 0.18, Table 1, Table 2). Although median costs for appendectomy admissions were about $2000 less in FP hospitals $12,192 [9291, −16,988] v. $10,686 [8487–13,942], the effect size for these differences was not significant (Cohen's d = 0.18). There was a substantial difference in charge to cost ratios for appendectomy admissions between NFP hospitals (3.9) and FP (9.0) hospitals (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Patterns for differences in charges, costs and charge to cost ratios between NFP and FP hospitals were similar between appendectomy and NOM hospital admissions (Table 2). Costs and charges for appendectomy were consistently 50 % to 70 % higher than for NOM of appendicitis in both NFP and FP hospitals. The major difference between NFP and FP hospitals were substantially higher charge to cost ratios for both appendectomy and NOM care (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Hospitals of NFP status had more appendicitis cases (131.0 [49.4,212.6] v. 115 [63,166]; Cohen's d = 0.30) and emergency general surgery case volume 353.5 [157.0,550.1] v. 284 [148,420], Cohen's d = 0.34) than did FP hospitals. More NFP were teaching institutions (75.8 % v. 61.8 %, OR 2.03) and had more beds (2.4 ± 0.8 v. 2.1 ± 0.7, Cohen's d = 0.39).

Patient factors

Patients at NFP centers more commonly presented with complicated appendicitis (61.6 % v. 43.8 %, OR = 1.97) and perforated appendicitis (46.8 % v. 36.5 %, OR = 1.46). There were fewer patients in the lowest income quartile in NFP hospitals (22.5 % v. 31.2 %, OR = 0.64) and a greater number of patients in the highest income quartile (26.3 % v. 15.8 %, OR = 1.87). More patients at NFP had peripheral vascular disease at NFP hospitals (2.2 % v. 1.4 %, OR = 1.53).

There were no differences between NFP and FP hospitals for patient age, sex, sum of Elixhauser scores, Medicaid, Medicare or private insurance payer status, CHF, arrythmia, chronic lung disease, coagulopathy, smoking status, and hypertension.

A one-standard-deviation increase in the volume of appendicitis cases in the NFP model was associated with a −0.12 (p = 0.007) decrease in the log odds of performing an appendectomy whereas it was associated with a 0.29 (P = 0.061) increase for FP facilities. These correspond to ORs of 0.883 (0.871, 0.895) for NFP hospitals and 1.340 (1.189, 1.511) for FP facilities.

Complicated appendicitis was associated with a greater likelihood of treating appendicitis with appendectomy with a stronger effect in FP hospitals. Increases in perforation rates were associated with more NOM in both types of hospitals. The income quartile effects varied, with higher income quartiles generally associated with increased appendectomy likelihood in FP hospitals with the opposite effect observed in the NFP hospitals although the effects sizes for these observations were not significant.

Weighted Fairlie decomposition

Weighted Fairlie decomposition (Fig. 2a) explained the 0.075 (7.5 %) gap in appendectomy rates. The explained component accounted for 0.090 (e.g., 9 %), driven by differences in covariate distributions. The discrepancies in reported gap values results from differences in the appendectomy rates being estimated from weighted case counts (gap = 6.8 %), regression equations (9.0 %) or MCMC sampling procedures (7.5 %). Fig. 2b shows the contribution of each individual variable to the gap between appendectomy rates at NFP and FP hospitals. This is displayed graphically in Fig. 2c. Total charges was the largest contributor to greater appendectomy rates observed in FP hospitals, explaining almost all (0.068/0.090 = 75 %) of the gap. Complicated appendicitis and EGS volume reduced the gap by −0.013 (−0.013/0.090 = 14 %). The income variable was 0 suggesting that income did not explain any of the gap in appendectomy rates between NFP and FP hospitals.

Charge/cost analysis

The median CCR for NFP hospitals was 3.6 and 7.8 for FPs. Bayes regression of CCR data revealed that FP hospital status was associated with a significantly greater log-CCR (posterior mean = 0.80, 95 % CI 0.78–0.82), corresponding to an approximate 2.2-fold increase in CCR on the original scale (exp (0.80) = 2.2). Supplemental Fig. 2 shows the posterior probability distributions of CCR for NFP (blue) and FP (red) hospitals, derived from 50 posterior draws on a weighted subsample (n = 1000). There is a clear rightward shift in CCR for FP hospitals consistent with generally larger CCR values compared with NFP hospitals. The NFP distribution peaked around a CCR of 3.9 (mean value), while the FP distribution showed a broader spread and greater density at elevated CCR values, peaking at 8.1 (mean value), also consistent with the model's finding of a 2.2-fold increase in FP CCR.

Discussion

The landmark Appendicitis Acuta (APPAC) trial was the first to demonstrate successful NOM treatment of appendicitis [18,19]. Although avoiding surgery seems appealing, when patients or their representatives were asked to choose between appendectomy or NOM, 2/3 selected appendectomy [[20], [21], [22]]. Factors other than a desire to avoid surgery appear to motivate the decision to pursue appendectomy instead of NOM. We explored several factors that might influence this decision-making finding that hospital profit status was significantly associated with a greater appendectomy rate as compared with NFP hospitals and that the amount of charges per admission explained nearly all of the difference between NFP and FP hospital appendectomy rates.

In the present work, costs and associated charges of providing appendectomy care was about 50 %–70 % greater than NOM. An important difference between NFP and FP hospitals was in charge to cost ratios: for NFP the CCR was 3.5 for NOM and 3.9 for appendectomy. For FP hospitals, the CCR was 8.1 for NOM and 9.0 for appendectomy. These very large charge to cost ratios translated to approximately double the charges for care in FP hospitals. If these hospitals have contracts that pay a percent of charges, or high insurance deductibles that patients have to pay out of pocket, or cash paying patients, FP hospital revenue could be substantially enhanced by setting very high charge rates.

The range of charges for appendicitis care at NFP hospitals ranged from $30,217 to 65,442) and ($64,171 to 129,301) at FP centers. Charges can result from delivering expensive, complex care but can also be inflated related to actual costs as is evidenced by the high charge to cost ratio for FP hospitals. In this study, it seemed unlikely that FP hospitals were delivering more complex care given that the rate at which they treated complicated disease was nearly 20 % less than for NFP hospitals.

If done efficiently, appendectomy can be very profitable for FP hospitals. Conceivably, FP hospitals might facilitate the performance of appendectomy by making OR time available, establishing hospital care pathways that make it easy for surgeons to manage these patients in the hospital system etc. Furthermore, given that case volume was controlled for within our models, the rate of non-complicated appendicitis alone at FP does not explain the increased rate of appendectomy selection at these centers.

Our study has several important limitations. Due to the retrospective nature of the NRD, the reliance on ICD coding can lead to variations in reporting practices between clinicians, hospitals, and regions. Similarly, patient preferences and operative decision-making could not be ascertained. Data on antibiotic duration, route, and coverage were unavailable. Additionally, we could not account for surgeon-specific factors that might influence treatment decisions independently of hospital characteristics. Despite these limitations, we used a large, all-payer database and robust statistical methods to reduce the risk of bias and increase the generalizability of our findings at the national level.

In conclusion, we noted hospital profit status to be significantly associated with appendectomy rates for the treatment of appendicitis, with for-profit hospitals demonstrating substantially higher operative management rates compared to their not-for-profit counterparts. Further, we found total charges to explain nearly three-quarters of the observed gap in appendectomy rates between hospital types, with substantially elevated charge-to-cost ratios in for-profit hospitals, suggesting emphasis on financial incentives may be a key driver of this disparity. These findings raise important concerns about how hospital ownership affects clinical decision-making when equally effective therapeutic alternatives exist, highlighting the need to ensure that treatment decisions prioritize patient outcomes over financial considerations.
