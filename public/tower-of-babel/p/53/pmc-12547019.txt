Epidemiology and clinicodemographic features of microbiologically confirmed and presumptive extrapulmonary tuberculosis in Ethiopia: a multicenter study
Authors: Hilina Mollalign, Shewki Moga, Dawit Chala, Muluwok Getahun, Habteyes Hailu Tola, Dereje Beyene
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The prevalence of microbiologically confirmed extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) in Ethiopia is high.•The mean distribution of hematological values poorly distinguishes presumptive and confirmed EPTB.•Previous TB treatment history and comorbidity are predictors of EPTB.•Alcohol use and frequent hospitalization are associated with presumptive EPTB.

Background

Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) was responsible for an estimated 10.8 million tuberculosis (TB) cases and 1.25 million fatalities in 2023 [1]. About a quarter of the global population is infected with TB, while a fraction of these progress into active pulmonary TB (PTB) or extrapulmonary TB (EPTB), depending on an interplay between the host, pathogen, and environment [2]. EPTB refers to clinically diagnosed or microbiologically confirmed forms of TB infecting any organ other than the lung, and it is usually associated with health disparities. It occurs most frequently in women and individuals with impaired immune systems [3]. Worldwide, EPTB constitutes 16% of the global TB burden [1]. The pooled prevalence of EPTB is 26% in Africa, where the prevalence is relatively low (16%) in Western Africa and high in the East African region (32%) [4].

TB lymphadenitis, the predominant form of EPTB in Ethiopia, constituted 80% of all EPTB forms and has remained a public health challenge over the past decade, accounting for more than a quarter of the total TB incidence [5]. The trend of notified EPTB remains relatively stable, ranging between 28% and 31%, while diverse host and environmental risk factors such as adulthood, female sex, contact with active PTB, and contact with livestock are related to EPTB [1,6]. In addition to the risk factors contributing to EPTB, the clinical presentation of EPTB is often nonspecific, complicating its definitive diagnosis. Thus, microbiologic evidence remains crucial for efficient clinical management. However, the invasiveness of the procedure to collect appropriate clinical specimens, combined with the low sensitivity of available diagnostic tools, often leads to reliance on clinical judgment to guide treatment [7].

Therefore, understanding the differences in the clinical features and sociodemographic characteristics between presumptive and definitive EPTB helps as a proxy indicator for appropriate diagnosis. However, there is limited information on the determinants and characteristics that distinguish presumptive from definitive EPTB where bacteriologic confirmation is not applicable. In this study, we aimed to determine the epidemiology of EPTB in Ethiopia. We further examined the differences in the clinical and sociodemographic characteristics between presumptive and microbiologically confirmed EPTB. We hypothesized that the magnitude of EPTB would remain high, while there would be clinical and sociodemographic features that can distinguish persons with definitive EPTB from those presumptive for EPTB.

Materials and methods

Study design and study settings

A cross-sectional study was conducted between August 2022 and October 2023 to enroll 542 eligible persons in whom EPTB was decided to be a diagnostic consideration (presumptive EPTB) from eleven selected health facilities across six regions of Ethiopia (Table 1). Study site selection was made based on annual statistics of TB patient flow to the facility. Study participants and sample collection

A consecutive sampling method was used to enroll all patients who presented at the selected health facility during the study period and were identified as presumptive EPTB. All participants classified as presumptive EPTB by the clinician were consented to participate in the study. Clinical and sociodemographic information was collected by a trained data collector using a structured, pretested questionnaire. Details of the collected sample types are given in Table 1. The samples were triple-packed with ice and transported to the EPHI NTRL for further analysis.

Laboratory test

Isolation and detection of the MTB complex (MTBC) were performed using the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra and TB culture. TB culture was performed using the Mycobacterium Growth Indicator Tube (MGIT) and Lowenstein-Jensen (LJ) media. TB identification testing was performed by Ziehl-Neelsen smear microscopy and the MPT64 immunochromatographic assay. All positive tubes underwent a contamination check by subculturing into a brain heart infusion (BHI) agar plate. Bacteriologic confirmation was defined as a composite result of TB culture and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra for newly diagnosed persons, whereas positivity by the gold standard (TB culture) was used for treatment follow-up persons.

For hematological and lipid parameter testing, 5 mL of venous blood was collected aseptically and aliquoted into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid and serum-separating tubes. The whole blood in the ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tube was used to measure white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), hemoglobin, platelets (PLT), neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, basophils, and eosinophils (EO) using a DxH 800 hematology analyzer (Beckman Coulter). The blood in the serum-separating tubes was allowed to clot for about 30 minutes, and the serum was separated by centrifugation at 5000 x g for 10 minutes. The Cobas 6000 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics) was used for the measurement of total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein. The quality control provided by the manufacturer was run before running samples for both assays.

Statistical analysis

Data was double-entered into EpiData version 4.6.0.6 and cleaned. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). The clinical and sociodemographic data were compared using descriptive statistics. Variables with a P-value of <0.25 in univariable analysis were included in a multivariable model. The mean and median distribution of continuous clinical data were compared using Student’s t-test and the Mann-Whitney U-test, respectively, and a P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Furthermore, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed to compare the diagnostic values of clinical laboratory results.

Results

Participants' characteristics

The majority (78.4%) of the study participants were adults in the age range of 18–50 years, while more than half (51.1%) were female. Most of the participants were enrolled from the North (35.6%) and Central (29.5%) areas. Of the total enrolled presumptive EPTB cases, 34.9% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 30.9-39.0) and 43.5% (95% CI: 39.3-47.8) were microbiologically confirmed EPTB using the TB culture and GeneXpert Ultra test, respectively. More than half of the samples collected were lymph node aspirates (58.7%), followed by pleural fluid (20.8%) (Table 1).

Sociodemographic characteristics of presumptive and microbiologically confirmed EPTB

Participants in the age range between 18 and 50 years were higher in both groups (P = 0.033), with no differences in the proportions of males and females (P = 0.546). Participants with a previous history of TB treatment were significantly higher among the microbiologically confirmed EPTB group (P <0.001). Recurrent hospitalization (P <0.001), any treatment nonadherence history (P <0.001), alcohol use (P = 0.007), and chewing khat (P = 0.043) were significantly different between presumptive and microbiologically confirmed EPTB participants (Supplementary Table S1).

Comparison of clinical characteristics between presumptive and microbiologically confirmed EPTB

There was no significant difference in the mean body mass index distribution between the two groups (P >0.05). However, a significant difference was observed in the mean hematological indices of WBC, RBC, and PLT between male and female participants across the groups. A significantly higher number of presumptive EPTB participants showed an increased mean WBC compared to bacteriologically confirmed groups (P <0.001). Comparison of the mean WBC distribution between male and female participants in the two groups showed that the distribution was marginally similar between the groups for female participants (P = 0.059), while it was significantly different for male participants (P = 0.003). Total RBC was significantly lower among presumptive EPTB participants, and the difference was significant among female participants (P <0.001). The distribution of mean platelet count, on the other hand, was different between the groups and significantly higher among bacteriologically confirmed males (P = 0.042) and females (P = 0.005). No differences were observed between the two groups for the distribution of mean hemoglobin measurement and lipid profiles, except for HDL, which was significantly lower among bacteriologically confirmed male (P <0.001) and female (P = 0.003) patients. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was also lower among confirmed EPTB patients, and the difference was significant among female patients (P = 0.033). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis of hematological and lipid profiles to distinguish presumptive and confirmed EPTB

Correlation and logistic regression analysis of clinical factors have revealed variations and overlap of hematological indices and lipid profiles between presumptive and microbiologically confirmed EPTB. We then evaluated whether these parameters could accurately distinguish between the two groups. The area under the curve (AUC) of all the hematological and lipid profile indices poorly distinguishes between the two groups (AUC = 0.533-0.645). There was a modest discriminatory AUC value for WBC (AUC = 0.594, 95% CI, 0.525-0.663, P = 0.008) and HDL (AUC = 0.627, 95% CI, 0.556-0.697, P <0.001) between the groups. Discussion

In this study, the majority (78.4%) of presumptive and bacteriologically confirmed EPTB participants were in the age range of 18-50 years, and more than half (51.1%) were females. Furthermore, we did not identify an association of either age or sex with EPTB. A review of EPTB risk factors in Ethiopia reported that male patients were mostly affected [6], whereas others reported the odds of EPTB among females with cervical TB lymphadenitis (TBLN) [8]. Studies from both high and low-EPTB-burden countries reported a high EPTB burden among females and elderly patients [9,10]. The variability of age and sex as a risk for EPTB in different settings might be due to variations in healthcare-seeking behaviors, geographic locations, socioeconomic status, and study design.

The overall TB culture positivity rate of our study was 34.9%, without differences in the proportions of male to female participants. Due to the invasiveness of the specimen collection procedure and the paucibacillarity of EPTB clinical specimens, cytological examination complemented with computed tomography scans is the most widely used EPTB diagnostic. A review of the pooled prevalence of bacteriologically confirmed EPTB showed a varied prevalence, where the detection rate by TB culture was higher compared to smear microscopy and GeneXpert [6]. A high rate of microbiologic confirmation of EPTB and therapeutic success, mainly among male patients, has been reported in Cameron [11]. Bacteriologic confirmation of EPTB has been associated with successful treatment outcomes [12]. In the current study, both TB culture positivity and GeneXpert positivity rates were high, where the number of GeneXpert positives exceeded that of culture positives among treatment follow-up EPTB cases. This implies that TB culture remains the gold standard for the accurate detection of actively replicating bacterial populations in the affected organs, ensuring antibiotic stewardship.

Previous TB treatment history and HIV coinfection were associated with bacteriologically confirmed EPTB, whereas recurrent hospitalization, history of medication (any) nonadherence, alcohol use, and chewing khat were associated with presumptive EPTB. Different clinical and sociodemographic factors have been identified as the predictors and/or risk factors for EPTB. In agreement with our findings, HIV coinfection, a previous history of TB treatment, and contact history with active PTB have frequently been reported as risk factors for EPTB [6,8,13]. A compromised immune system, mainly depletion of the CD4+ cells, weakens the immune system, which is crucial to controlling the TB infection and its spread to peripheral sites [14]. Furthermore, previous exposure to TB and anti-TB treatment weakens the immune system and increases the host susceptibility and the risk of dissemination of the bacilli [15]. The strong evidence that supports previous TB treatment as a risk for EPTB warrants the need for targeted screening of previously TB-treated patients for EPTB.

Alcohol abuse, chewing khat, a history of treatment nonadherence, and recurrent hospitalization were significantly associated with presumptive EPTB cases. This finding was supported by a previous report [16]. Alcohol consumption often correlates with social and environmental determinants such as social mixing and nutritional deficiencies, which have direct consequences for TB transmission [17]. On the other hand, presumptive EPTB patients may present with more acute conditions and severe symptoms that may require hospital admission and intensive management compared to patients with definitive EPTB diagnoses. The association between the lack of early definitive diagnosis and hospital admission has been reported [18]. This implies that the nonspecific clinical presentations between presumptive and confirmed EPTB could result in misdiagnosis unless closely supervised. Furthermore, behavioral factors such as alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, and khat chewing were significantly associated with medication nonadherence [19]. Thus, the negative correlation between EPTB and these behavioral factors highlights that accurate detection and appropriate treatment plans for EPTB independently could have a successful treatment outcome.

Pulmonary tuberculosis is known for its chronic inflammatory responses and depletion of host lipids [20]. In our study, a slight but significant difference in the hematological indices of WBC, RBC, and PLT was observed between the groups. However, neither the lipid profile nor the hematological indices distinguished between presumptive and confirmed EPTB. These parameters can be used as supplementary for treatment monitoring and predicting treatment outcomes [21]. The variations of these parameters are significantly higher among PTB patients with other comorbidities [22]. In our investigation, there was a relatively higher mean distribution of total RBC and PLT, and a decrease in the lipid profiles among bacteriologically confirmed EPTB compared to the presumptive groups. A higher total RBC count was reported among EPTB patients compared to PTB [23], whereas the total WBC and PLT counts were reported within a normal range in most EPTB cases [24]. The identified leukocytosis among presumptive EPTB groups in our study might be due to lymphadenopathies caused by infections other than TB. The hematological parameters remain variable in different populations despite the anatomical localizations of the disease [25]. However, none of these differences independently distinguished between presumptive and confirmed EPTB in the present study. This finding agreed with a previous report, which revealed that despite a prognostic significance between the pretreatment and post-treatment mean cell indices, none distinguished between disease and control [26].

An inverse association between lipid profile and risk of TB has also been established [27]. Even though TB is associated with hypolipidemia, the predicting factors for whether a preceding hypolipidemia predicts TB or TB reduces the serum lipid concentration remain controversial [27,28]. Lower systemic inflammation has also been marked by an increase in serum cholesterol levels during TB treatment [29]. This might imply a direct association between TB cure and a reversion to a normal lipid profile in cases of PTB. A slight but nonsignificant difference in lipid profiles between our study groups might be due to different factors. First, lipid metabolism might be variable based on the localization of TB bacteria to different organs. Second, the inflammatory response to EPTB might be less intense compared to PTB [30]. Third, detailed investigation of lipid species and their isomers over direct serum lipid concentration measurement might be as predictive and more precise. The nonsignificant difference in body mass index between our study groups, which implies a less pronounced nutritional deficiency in EPTB patients, and the lower bacterial load in EPTB clinical specimens might not affect direct serum lipid concentration. Thus, we conclude that even though there are alterations in the hematological and lipid profiles of an EPTB patient, the variation does not distinguish bacteriologically confirmed patients from patients who are triaged for EPTB diagnosis.

This study was not without limitations. First, the hematological indices and lipid profile were compared at baseline visits, and no follow-up samples were included. Second, further data on the clinical workups for TB-negative participants were not collected, hindering us from drawing a definitive conclusion.

In conclusion, the prevalence of EPTB, mostly in northern and central Ethiopia, remained high. Most clinical features of presumptive and microbiologically confirmed EPTB overlap, while there are slight differences in sociodemographic characteristics. Thus, the findings of the present study could provide a clue for a very similar clinical presentation of presumptive and definitive EPTB. Further studies on the evaluation of the clinical characteristics and biomarkers of EPTB, along with the healthy controls, are needed.
