Bridging communities of practice: lessons from the Humanitarian Health Research Forum on climate, crisis, and collaboration
Authors: Gillian McKay, Siautu Alefaio, Blythe Beecroft, Lauren D’Mello-Guyett, Alice Karanja, Christopher LeBoa, Carrie Ngongo, V Bhargavi Rao, Ruwan Ratnayake, Calistus Wilunda, Beatrice Kiage
Published: 2025

In 2025, an estimated 300 million people will require urgent humanitarian assistance.1 Such humanitarian crises disproportionately affect populations in southern and eastern Africa and the Middle East and north Africa—regions that also face disproportionate risks from climate impacts. For example, in 2021, countries considered highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change accounted for 95% of all internal conflict-related displacements.2

Climate change acts as both a driver and a force multiplier of existing humanitarian crises. Severe weather events such as floods, cyclones, droughts, extreme heat, and heavy rainfall exacerbate hunger, increase morbidity and mortality, fuel gender-based violence, trigger displacement, and cause economic devastation.1,3–5 Several crisis-affected populations face overlapping challenges—conflict, displacement, poverty, and heightened risks from communicable and noncommunicable diseases, further undermining their resilience to environmental shocks driven by climate change. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s sixth assessment report states that “climate change is contributing to humanitarian crises where climate hazards interact with high vulnerability”, highlighting its role in sustaining conflict, driving mass displacement, and contributing to longer, more severe humanitarian crises globally.6 While the evidence linking climate and health continues to grow, research on the compound effects of climate change on populations affected by humanitarian crises remains sparse.7

Understanding how to build resilience and adapt to current and emerging health threats among crisis-affected populations, while also implementing strategies to mitigate climate change and support long-term health, is essential. Recognising the impacts of climate change on individuals, communities, and health systems in fragile and conflict-affected settings, Elrha and the African Population and Health Research Centre (APHRC) co-convened the Humanitarian Health Research Forum in May, 2025. The event brought together 150 global experts, including researchers, practitioners, policy makers, and climate specialists, to examine research gaps and needs at the intersection of climate and humanitarian health—two domains that often remain disconnected.

While several participants hesitated to label themselves explicitly as climate researchers, there was broad consensus that climate hazards, including extreme weather events, are increasingly affecting all areas of health. New collaborations are therefore needed to better integrate climate into humanitarian health research, both through standalone projects and by mainstreaming climate perspectives. However, one panellist cautioned:

“Integrating climate into all health research is essential, but without clear ownership, climate change risks becoming everyone’s responsibility and also no one’s responsibility.”

Dr Lauren D’Mello-Guyett (London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine)

Integration approaches could include mapping the intersection of humanitarian needs and climate hazards, followed by formative, operational, and implementation research to test locally relevant solutions. In parallel, health systems and policy research are needed to understand how governments and responding agencies can finance and lead adaptive programme planning, supported by climate-responsive health funding mechanisms.

Equally essential is the engagement with culturally and linguistically rooted, community-based knowledge systems to better understand the impacts of climate on health in crisis settings. Indigenous and local knowledge systems are not only sources of insight but also key drivers of resilience.8 As noted by a panellist:

“Research that overlooks the way climate is experienced and articulated locally will be ineffective.”

Dr Siautu Alefaio (University of Otago)

Examples from the Pacific and Kenya highlighted the importance of collective action, based on kinship ties and community empowerment, to not only document and advocate for contextually relevant climate action but also identify and scale mitigation and adaptation solutions. While climate impacts, particularly in already stressed humanitarian settings, can exacerbate mental health challenges, there are also strong foundations for hope and resilience. As observed by a panellist:

“Hope is not abstract but lives in the collective action of community.”

Dr Alice Karanja (African Population and Health Research Centre)

Amidst shrinking global health budgets, several panellists shared examples of using secondary and routine data for tracking the impacts of climate change on population health, such as birth outcomes9 and seasonal malaria trends.10 Such data sources allowed researchers to examine changes over time, showing how climate-related factors influenced the health indicators of interest. There is significant scope for increased use of such datasets in linking climate and humanitarian health outcomes of interest, provided challenges related to data access, governance, and quality can be mitigated.

Building on the insights and priorities identified at the forum, future research at the intersection of climate and humanitarian health must be conceptualised and implemented in ways that elevate Indigenous and local knowledge, leverage existing data, and decolonise research by supporting leaders from crisis-affected communities. While all humanitarian health researchers have a role in integrating climate into their work, building a robust evidence base requires equitable partnerships and multidisciplinary collaboration. Such approaches are essential for understanding climate impacts and ensuring that interventions remain evidence-based and grounded in local strengths.

As we reflect on these conversations, we recognise the distinct roles played by different actors. Funders such as Elrha are embedding a climate lens in future research calls to ensure funding is both responsive and climate sensitive. Implementers such as APHRC are generating evidence and working with policy makers and communities to translate findings into action, positioning climate and humanitarian health research as a driver of real change. This collective commitment is essential for addressing urgent needs and strengthening resilience in the face of a changing climate.
