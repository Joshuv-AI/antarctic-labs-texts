The French registry of sudden unexpected death in infancy (SUDI): a 7-year review of available data
Authors: Mathilde Ducloyer, Sophie de Visme, Bérengère Jarry, Léa Ferrand, Pauline Scherdel, Karine Levieux, David Lair, Christèle Gras-Le Guen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The French “OMIN registry” was established in 2015 to collect nationwide standardised data concerning biological, clinical, environmental and social characteristics of sudden unexpected death in infancy (SUDI) and unexpected death in children aged 1–2 years. A biobank has existed since July 2020 to store biological samples for each case. This article aimed to detail (1) a brief history and the objectives of the registry; (2) a description of the methodology used; (3) the first results of the registry, i.e. the main characteristics of the cases included so far; (4) the process for accessing the data for research projects; and (5) issues regarding weakness and improvement and perspectives offered by the registry. On 31 May 2024, 1975 cases were included in the OMIN registry; on 31 December 2022, 4606 biological samples from 176 cases were collected. For each deceased child, different types of data are registered on an electronic case report form: socio-demographic data, personal and familial medical background, environment and feeding data, clinical data, and biological and imaging results. A strict and continuous quality control process is used to ensure the reliability of the data, in parallel with specific actions to improve the exhaustiveness of the registry. The OMIN registry database is one of the largest and the most complete databases on SUDI, especially in Europe, and the first in the world to associate a standardised biological sample collection with it. Perspectives of research provided by our registry are numerous and could be supported by national and international scientific collaborations.Conclusion: This article details the objectives and methods of the French registry of SUDI. It provides initial results relating to the population included in the register and the procedure for accessing the data. What is Known:• In Western Europe, France is one of the countries with the highest SUDI rate, making it the first cause of death of infants between 28 and 364 days.• The development of epidemiological tools on a national and international scale is essential to advance research into the determinants and risk factors of unexpected death in children under 2 years of age.What is New:• The OMIN registry was created in France in 2015 to collect nationwide standardised social, environmental, clinical, and paraclinical data for cases of unexpected death in children aged 0 to 2 years.• To date, the OMIN registry has included 680 data from almost 2000 children unexpectedly deceased, completed by a biocollection since 2020.• Data from the OMIN registry, unique in its field, are freely available for scientific research teams, after acceptation by the scientific committee of the registry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00431-024-05727-9.

Introduction

Sudden unexpected death in infancy and childhood (SUDIC) is defined as the sudden death of a child before 18 years, which would not have reasonably expected to occur 24 h previously and in whom no pre-existing medical cause of death is apparent [1–3]. It covers sudden unexpected death in infancy (SUDI) before the age of 1; this definition is extended to children up to the age of 2 in France. When the death remains unexplained after thorough post-mortem examinations, SUDI can be classified as sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) and SUDIC as sudden unexplained death in childhood (SUDC) [4]. In Western Europe, France is one of the countries with the highest SUDI rate, making it the first cause of death of infants between 28 and 364 days [5]. In 2015, the SUDI rate was calculated at 36.8/100,000 live births [5]. From 2015 to 2021, the French database on causes of death (CépiDC, https://www.cepidc.inserm.fr/) estimated that the average number of infants who died of SUDI (International Cause of Disease codes R00–R99) was 370 per year based on death certificate. However, since death certificates do not provide information on the circumstances of death and the final diagnosis after post-mortem investigations, they are not sufficient tools for epidemiological surveillance of SUDI and SIDS research [6]. In addition to the trauma experienced by the families, the social burden of this event is tangible in terms of the years of potential life lost with these premature deaths [7].

Until 2015, collecting reliable data on the extent of the phenomenon constituted a huge challenge, first, because of territorial disparities regarding the SUDI definition, the post-mortem investigations, which led to a high variability of the number and characteristics of the SUDI cases concerned [8, 9]. Second, the lack of dedicated epidemiological monitoring tools was a real impediment to collecting data for epidemiological surveillance and research on SUDI and SIDS. The creation of a national registry was the best answer to these challenges. The French registry of SUDI, called the Observatoire national des Morts Inattendues du Nourrisson (OMIN) registry, was established in 2015 [10]. It includes all SUDI (between 0 and 1 year) and SUDIC (between 1 and 2 years of age) occurring in France and managed by a referral centre. For greater readability, we use the single abbreviation SUDI to describe all the cases included in the registry, from 0 to 2 years of age. At 7 years after the registry’s creation, we aim to detail (1) a brief history and the objectives of the registry; (2) a description of the methodology used; (3) the first results of the registry, i.e. the main characteristics of the SUDI cases included so far; (4) the process for accessing the data for research projects; and (5) issues regarding weakness and improvement and perspectives offered by the registry regarding international collaborations and collaborative networks.

History and objectives

History of the registry

The history of the registry is detailed in Supplemental file 1.

Objective of the OMIN registry

The main objective of the OMIN registry is to create a national database gathering standardised social, environmental, clinical, and paraclinical data for cases of SUDI [10]. This objective has two viewpoints: public health and research. First, the OMIN registry was inspired by The Global Action and Prioritization of Sudden infant death (GAPS) [11]. It aims to institute an epidemiological monitoring of SUDI and sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) by analysing and comparing the geographic and time-related variations in incidence in the French territory and studying the disparities of practices regarding the post-mortem investigations among the centres. Thus, it may help in developing innovative approaches for health education and prevention campaigns, especially for vulnerable and underprivileged populations. Second, the registry aims to manage epidemiological research on the frequency and reasons for non-compliance with safe infant sleep recommendations to evaluate the impact of prevention campaigns at local, regional or national levels. It will also be a strong support to initiate or participate in national and international studies on SUDI and SIDS to identify potential new risk factors by comparison with living controls from the French national system of health data. The objective of the unique BioMIN collection created in July 2020 is to preserve and make available to researchers biological samples to conduct fundamental research.

Method: who is in the registry, what are the data collected and how are they collected? [10]

OMIN inclusion process

Children are included by SUDI referral centres which have agreed to participate and have been including data for children gradually since 2015, guaranteeing exhaustive national coverage. The inclusion criteria follow the international definition of SUDI, with an extension for children up to 2 years old justified by the decision of the HAS [1, 2]. Thus, the registry includes SIDS, explained deaths and those that were undetermined according to the terms recommended by an international consensus [9]. The notification of a case of SUDI is transmitted to the referral centre mostly by the mobile emergency care units but also by resuscitation and intensive care units (in the event of transient child survival) or forensic institutes (when death is suspected to be of criminal origin, at the death scene or during post-mortem investigations). The notification of the case is transmitted by the referral centre to the OMIN registry by e-mail within 48 h after the child’s admission to hospital. After registration, the child is finally included after parental consent, which can be given when the child is admitted to hospital just after death, at the medical appointment scheduled around 10 days after death, or when all the results are handed in, between 3 and 6 months after death, if consent has not been obtained beforehand. OMIN data collection

The type of data collected was determined according to national and international guidelines [3, 12]. The complete list of data collected is detailed in supplemental Table 1 and precisions on death scene management in supplemental file 1.

Data on socio-demographic characteristics of the SUDI case and the parents, medical history at birth and until death, medical history of the family, environmental and feeding habits of the child, environmental and feeding the day of the death are gathered during the interview with the parents when the child is taken into care at the SUDI referral centre. During this care, the referral centre also collects data on the date, hour and location of the death and the results of all examinations performed: clinical examination; biological, biochemical, microbiological, virological, and toxicological tests; imaging reports; fundus photographs and autopsy report. Finally, the referral centre collects data related to any identified disease, diagnosis and classification of the cause of death. This data collection is supported by an electronic case report form (eCRF), implemented by the referral paediatricians of each centre, with the help of the whole medical team performing the investigations (emergency physicians, paediatricians, nurses, pathologists, radiologists, forensic pathologists).

The BioMIN collection holds biological samples of different natures: hair strands, whole blood on blotting paper, whole blood in PAXgene blood RNA, EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood, serum, plasma, peripheral blood mononuclear cells, faeces, cerebrospinal fluid, nasopharyngeal swabs and urine (Table 1). Data associated with the biological samples are also collected in the eCRF.

The OMIN coordination unit then enriches the data collection by performing certain calculations: z-scores for height, weight, and head circumference based on the Intergrowth-21st standards and tools [13], specific indices such as the French social deprivation index [14], and small for gestational age, based on the French intrauterine growth reference curves for birth size and weight [15]. Once a year, data on healthcare and medication consumption are collected for the SUDI cases by using a probabilistic matching process with the French health data system (SNDS). This matching is performed with a 95% success rate. OMIN data quality, exhaustiveness and security

The quality of data collected is guaranteed by several checkpoints. Every 6 months, data are extracted by the data manager of the OMIN coordination unit to check the consistency of the data and missing data. Data in each file are continuously monitored, and in December 2022, about 93% of the 1595 files were audited. This systematic control involves a study monitor verifying all the data in the eCRF with data from the native medical file.

The exhaustive census of every SUDI is a major issue of the registry, which is difficult because of the multiplicity of the actors potentially involved. Several actions are carried out to ensure the completeness of the registry. Continuous training is regularly delivered in each referral centre to explain the goals of the OMIN registry and the practicalities of the eCRF. A match between the OMIN registry and the French national database of causes of death (CépiDC) is also ongoing to identify any potential SUDI cases not recorded in the OMIN registry.

Regarding security, the registry has been approved by the French commission for data protection (Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés; authorization DR-2016–004). The consent of parents is systematically solicited; then, the child is anonymised by a six-character code. The OMIN registry database is supported and hosted by the software package VOOZANOO, edited by Epiconcept (Paris, France; https://www.epiconcept.fr/en/) which has been certified as a health data host in accordance with French regulations. The database is supplied by the eCRF, accessible with an online application, via a secure Https flow. Data are extracted by the OMIN registry coordination cell, from VOOZANOO to a secure server of Nantes University Hospital (also certified as a health data host), with restricted access.

Results: coverage

Number of children included

As of 31 May 2024, a total of 1975 children who died of SUDI were identified in the OMIN registry (Fig. 2). These cases were managed by a SUDI referral centre (n = 1643; 83%), or a forensic institute (n = 193, 10%). A small number (n = 139; 7%) were outside the management protocol because the parents did not want post-mortem investigations to be performed on their child. These children are included in the database, but few data are available. After the withdrawal of parental refusals to participate, 1886 (95%) were included in the OMIN registry. The distribution of the SUDI cases on the French territory is depicted in Fig. 1.

General description of the SUDI cases included

The following results relate to SUDI cases included between May 2015 and December 2022, as they have undergone quality control to verify and validate the data collected. The classification of SUDI cases is subject to significant variability at local, national and international levels [16]. As we do not have a standardised classification in the registry and a large number of referral centres (more than 30), we decided to present these general results for all the SUDI cases and not just for SIDS. All causes combined (SIDS, explained and undetermined death), the median age of the SUDI cases was 3.8 months (113 days, 95% confidence interval 55–203); 75% were under age 6.8 months (Fig. 3). Regarding the known risk factors for SIDS, 57% were boys, with a 1.3 boy/girl ratio; many (47%) were socially disadvantaged; 7% were from twin or multiple pregnancies; 32% were at the ≥ 3rd place in the birth order; 20% were premature and 23% had low birth weight (Table 2). In total, 50% and 48% of cases exhibited exposure to tobacco in utero or after birth (Table 2). Regarding protective factors, 27% were exclusively breastfed. Sleep environment of the SUDI cases included

For 59% of the SUDI cases, the sleep environment involved room-sharing practices (without sharing the sleep surface), and 30% shared the sleep surface with another person (Table 3). The sleep surface was suboptimal for 59%, according to recommendations of the American Academy of Pediatrics [17]. A suboptimal sleeping position (everything but supine) was usual, at bedtime and at discovery for 30%, 35% and 58% of SUDI cases. For most of them (74%), the environment included the presence of soft bedding such as a pillow, duvet, sheet, cushion, bumper pads or another object. It should be noted that, to date, these sleep risk factors are only relevant for infants under 12 months, who represent the major part of the children included in the registry. BioMIN collection

Up to 31 December 2022, 21 centres associated with 20 biobanks have actively participated in the BioMIN collection. A total of 176 SUDI cases are included in both the OMIN registry and the BioMIN collection, with 4606 samples available for research teams, described in Table 1. Since November 2021, biological samples have been sent once a year to the Nantes biobank, thus providing a complete and growing set of samples, rapidly available for research projects.

Data resource use and access

Data and biological samples are available for any public research team, after acceptance of the research project by the scientific committee. The project is submitted to the scientific committee by completing the research project form (supplemental file 2), which requires information on the main issues of the research project, the required data and the retention period. Only data from the OMIN data catalogue can be provided. Specific conventions are necessary for access to data by research teams outside the OMIN registry consortium. Access requests should be sent to the unique e-mail address bp-omin@chu-nantes.fr. The scientific committee will study the relevance of the project, eventually by soliciting specialists in the field, and the respect of the regulatory authorizations. If necessary, the requesting team is in charge of regulatory authorizations for a specific project that may directly or indirectly identify data. The data request can concern data already collected (analysis projects) or can request that new data be collected (complementary projects). There is no charge for access to the data. Fees will be applied according to a pre-defined cost table for the transfer of the biological samples. Regarding publications containing OMIN data, authorship rules must be respected before journal submission and must detail the origin of the data; the sample access charter is presented in supplemental file 3.

Discussion

Comparison with other national datasets

Currently, there are very few national databases dedicated to epidemiological surveillance and research into SUDI in Europe. The National Child Mortality Database (NCMD), launched in England in 2019, collects information on all deaths of children aged 0–18 years from all causes [18]. There are a number of similarities with the OMIN registry: the aim to better understand the determinants of child mortality and to adapt prevention programmes to reduce avoidable child deaths; the method of enrolling children, based on 58 regional Child Death Overview Panels (CDOPs), which report each child death using pre-defined forms on a web platform; and the possibility of sharing standardised data on specific research topics. The wide range of data collected makes it possible to carry out various analyses, on different causes and contexts of death (including SIDS), socio-economic factors or the COVID period [19, 20]. An article published in 2020 highlighted changes in SIDS risk factors between 1993 and 2020, showing an increase in the impact of prematurity and/or low birth weight, and the persistence of the impact of sleeping conditions, particularly co-sleeping with an adult [21]. The main difference is the population studied, which is not limited to children who died before the age of 2, but includes the entire paediatric population. As a result, the NCMD is undoubtedly one of the most ambitious registries for studying the determinants of child mortality. It could serve as a model for setting up national databases in European countries that do not have them and as a basis for pooling data to create a European registry.

Weaknesses and improvement

One of the major issues of the registry is the exhaustiveness of the census of every SUDI case. Missing data can be secondary to different biases: when the infant is not admitted to a referral centre, the difficulty in asking for parental consent and information in this context, or local technical difficulties. We collect more than 680 variables per SUDI case in the registry. To date, the number of missing data is estimated at 18%. Missing data are mainly data on parental socio-demographic characteristics and tobacco or drug use. Different actions are carried out to manage these points. In general, nationwide trips to each centre for training and communication with local investigators, the development of specific media to help investigators collect data or technical support for the biological collection. After the first declaration of the SUDI case, close contact is kept with the referral centre, to invite them to complete all the available data. Once the centre has validated the SUDI case file, a first level of quality control is carried out during the monitoring visit by a clinical research associate (CRA) in the centre. During this visit, the CRA checks the validity of all the data by reading the medical file and completes any missing data. These initial stages take up to 1 year. Twice a year, a second level of quality control is carried out by the clinical data manager to check the consistency of the data and validate it. Another main issue is an unavoidable delay between the first notification and the completeness of the case reports because some post-mortem investigations need some weeks or months to be completed (i.e. pathological examination). Hence, attention is essential to ensure that all data are provided for each case.

The second major issue is the sustainability of the funding for the OMIN registry. The registry is currently funded by specific research projects and sponsorship, so the OMIN coordination unit is constantly searching for funding.

Finally, living control infants are needed to conduct case–control studies to identify potential new risk factors. Because of the variability of candidate exposures to potential risk factors, envisaging a single cohort of living control infants is difficult, both in terms of technical feasibility and funding. However, data on deceased control infants can be obtained from the OMIN registry for SIDS cases; these are SUDI cases with an explained cause of death after complete investigations, particularly deaths with a traumatic origin. Furthermore, in case–control studies, deceased controls have the advantage of being a more similar population to cases than living controls [22].

Perspectives

The prospects offered by this national register are numerous.
