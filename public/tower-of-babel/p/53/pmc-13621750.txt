CSF1R inhibition following antibody mediated demyelination has limited impact on remyelination
Authors: Lisa C. Golden, Graham C. Peet, Rebecca E. Rodriguez, Nicholas J. Jahahn, Gregory P. Owens, Jeffrey L. Bennett, Wendy B. Macklin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, immune-mediated, demyelinating disease of the central nervous system. B cell depleting treatments are effective MS therapies, and while antibodies may serve as a biomarker, little is known about how they participate in MS pathology. Using a proteolipid protein 1 complex-specific (PLP1c) recombinant antibody cloned from cerebrospinal fluid plasmablasts of MS patients, we studied the development and resolution of antibody-mediated demyelinating lesions in vivo in mice. Demyelination was complement-dependent and resolved over four weeks. Because of the previously described impact of microglia on remyelination, we targeted microglia depletion with CSF1R inhibition to test their role in this model. Despite a significant reduction in total microglia following CSF1R inhibition, microglia or macrophage density was high in recovering lesions. Premyelinating oligodendrocyte populations were altered without impacting gross lesion recovery. Future work will determine if PLP1c-binding antibodies are representative of other MS-derived antibodies, and how pathogenic MS autoantibodies may contribute to the variability of MS lesion remyelination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40478-026-02370-3.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an inflammatory demyelinating disease of the central nervous system (CNS). In MS, autoimmune attack on the myelin sheath produces brain lesions that do not fully recover, leading to long-term disability [46]. MS affects up to two million people in the United States and presents a high morbidity burden through a long disease course that ultimately shortens the lifespan [45]. Both T cells and B cells are implicated in MS pathogenesis, but the process remains poorly understood. MS patient cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) typically contains oligoclonal bands of immunoglobulin produced by intrathecal B cells [16, 31]. B cell depleting monoclonal antibody therapies reduce the density of B cells in the CSF and slow disease progression in certain MS populations [7]. While antibodies are also present in certain types of MS lesions, their pathogenic role is not well understood [36].

Our studies have established that a myelin membrane surface complex containing proteolipid protein 1 (PLP1c), a ubiquitous and highly expressed myelin structural protein, is a target of intrathecal immunoglobulin G (IgG) in over half of MS patients [15, 32]. Recombinant antibodies (rAbs) cloned from MS patient CSF plasmablasts that target PLP1c can initiate transient demyelination in both ex vivo cerebellar slices and in vivo in the striatum in a complement dependent process, indicating a potential pathogenic role [18, 32]. PLP1c autoantibody-mediated demyelination in these models is complement-dependent and requires normal human serum as a source of complement component proteins [18]. Beyond complement dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), myelin binding antibodies may induce other forms of pathology through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) or antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), particularly in the more complex in vivo environment containing infiltrating immune cells.

CNS-resident and infiltrating professional immune cells respond rapidly to complement-opsonized myelin and can be found expressing Fc receptors in demyelinated lesions in humans [34, 37]. Among these, microglia are known to have diverse roles in demyelinating lesions, including myelin debris clearance, cytokine secretion, and oligodendrocyte progenitor cell (OPC) recruitment [1, 6]. Microglia may shift phenotypes during myelin injury and repair, and the timing of these transitions affects the mechanisms of injury [25, 27, 39]. Microglia rely on signaling via CSF1R for survival, and in recent years small molecule CSF1R inhibitors have created opportunities to dissect microglial contributions to disease via cellular depletion studies [11]. In addition to myeloid cells, OPCs and astrocytes express Fc receptors that allow them to respond to autoantibodies in developing or recovering lesions, setting up a complex local response during the evolution of MS lesions [17].

The details of this complex local response to MS autoantibodies have not been extensively investigated. Here, we investigate the implications of PLP1c-targeting IgG in vivo by inducing focal IgG-mediated demyelinating lesions in the mouse corpus callosum. PLP1c-targeting antibody-mediated demyelination in vivo was transient, with debris clearance and remyelination occurring over the course of multiple weeks. Interestingly, depletion of microglia via CSF1R inhibition was high in unaffected parenchyma but limited within the acute lesion environment. Effects on oligodendrocyte differentiation were observed during lesion recovery; however, mature oligodendrocyte densities were not affected at later timepoints.

Materials and methods

Animals

All animal experiments were performed with approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Colorado, Anschutz Medical Campus. Animals were housed in accordance with IACUC policy with chow and water ad libitum in a temperature, humidity, and light regulated environment. All mice used were C57BL/6J strain. At indicated time points, mice were perfused for immunohistochemistry. Both male and female mice were used for all experiments in equal numbers.

Antibody production and purification

Immunoglobulin sequences were cloned from patient intrathecal B cell repertoires and expressed as full length human IgG1 as previously described [31, 47]. Briefly, bivalent human IgG1 antibodies were expressed in EXPI293 cells (ThermoFisher) and purified with protein A-Sepharose (Sigma-Aldrich). The PLP1c binding clone MS04-2#30 was used for all studies [32] along with indicated isotype controls.

Immunoglobulin depletion of normal human serum

Normal human serum (NHS, Complement Technology, Lot 44E) was depleted of antibody heavy and light chains using Pierce Protein A/G and Protein L magnetic beads following the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Scientific, Cat #88802, Lot #XI365857 and Cat #88849, Lot #YC364276). The NHS was diluted 1:1 in saline. NHS was first treated with Protein A/G beads for 4 h rotating at 4 °C, followed by Protein L beads for 3 h rotating at 4 °C. The resulting antibody-free NHS (NHS-Ig) was used for stereotactic injections.

Stereotactic injections

Adult male and female C57BL/6J mice (15–20 weeks old) were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane and injected into the right hemisphere corpus callosum at stereotactic coordinates X = 1.00 mm, Y = 1.00 mm, Z=-1.92 mm in a location similar to several studies of lysolecithin-mediated demyelination [21, 43]. Injections (1 µL) contained 2 µg rAb and 20% Ig-depleted NHS in saline, unless indicated otherwise. Solutions were injected through a 33-gauge needle (Hamilton) at a rate of 0.2 µL/minute. All antibodies are human IgG1. The isotype control antibody (clone 2B4) was cloned from the CSF of a patient with neuro-infectious measles virus.

Microglia depletion

Adult mice were fed chow (Research Diets) containing 1200 ppm PLX5622 (Medchem), a selective CSF1R inhibitor, ad libitum to deplete microglia with limited off-target effects on PDGFRα [19] starting 10, 17, or 21 days post-injection until 25 days post-injection when all animals were euthanized [4]. When not receiving PLX5622 chow, mice received control chow of the same formulation.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were given an intraperitoneal injection of Fatal Plus (Vortech Pharmaceuticals). Mice were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were removed and post-fixed overnight with 4% PFA followed by cryoprotection in 30% sucrose with 0.1% sodium azide at 4 °C, then frozen in Tissue Freezing Medium (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Cat. #72592) and stored at -80 °C. Serial coronal sections (25 μm) were cut on a Leica CM 1950 cryostat and placed in PBS with 0.1% sodium azide. The first of every ten sections was used to estimate total lesion volume. Free-floating sections were washed three times with PBS then permeabilized and blocked with 0.3% Triton X-100 with 5% normal donkey serum (NDS, Jackson Immunoresearch) in PBS for 30 min shaking at room temperature. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies with 2% NDS in PBS overnight shaking at room temperature. Primary antibodies used and dilution: Chicken anti-MBP (Neuromics CH22112, 1:1000); Rabbit anti-TCF7L2 (Cell Signaling 2569, 1:1000); Rat anti-IBA1 (Abcam ab283346, 1:1000); Rabbit anti-degraded myelin (QD9, Millipore AB5864, 1:1000); Mouse IgG2a anti-OLIG2 (Millipore MABN50, 1:500); Goat anti-PDGFRα (R&D Systems AF1062, 1:1000); Mouse IgG1 anti-BCAS1 (Santa Cruz sc-136342, 1:1000); Rabbit anti-GSTπ (ENZO ADI-MSA-102, 1:1000); C5b-9 neoantigen (Complement Tech A227 1:500). Sections were washed three times before incubating with secondary antibodies with 2% NDS in PBS for 1.5 h shaking at room temperature. Sections were washed three times with PBS then mounted with Fluoromount G (Invitrogen).

Image acquisition and analysis

Fluorescent images were taken on a Leica DMi8 Stellaris 5 confocal microscope using a 20X water immersion objective. Images were analyzed with ImageJ software (NIH). Spectral confocal imaging (SCoRe) was performed as previously described [8]. All microscopy settings were kept constant to maintain consistent and comparable images for analysis. For all oligodendrocyte marker quantifications, lesions were imaged with consistent image dimensions that encompassed as much of the demyelinated lesion as possible for each section. SCoRe imaging was used to determine the lesion boundary. All cells within the SCoRe lesion area were counted. OLIG2 and TCF7L2 counts were performed using consistent intensity thresholds and size filtered particle counting [12]. For other cell counts, investigators were blinded to experimental conditions during analysis and images were quantified manually. Intensities of each channel were kept constant during manual counting to maintain consistent identification of positive cells. Cell counts are presented as counts per lesion area in mm2. For quantification of IBA1+ microglia depletion by CSF1R inhibition, images with consistent dimensions were taken of similar regions of the cortex and corpus callosum for both the ipsilateral and contralateral hemispheres of each section. Percent depletion was quantified by counting total IBA1+ cells per field of view for the contralateral hemisphere images for each condition and comparing those to the control animals. Percent depletion is normalized to untreated controls. IBA1 signal intensity was quantified within the lesion area region of interest on the ipsilateral side.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in Graphpad Prism. Ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for analysis unless it was determined that the data were not normally distributed, in which case a Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA was used. Multiple comparisons, where appropriate, were made to the control or peak lesion condition using a Dunnett’s or Dunn’s post-hoc test.

Results

PLP1c binding antibody induces transient demyelinating lesions in the corpus callosum

PLP1c-binding antibodies cause complement-dependent transient demyelination in cultured murine cerebellar slices, and oligodendrocyte death in vivo in the striatum [18, 32]. We hypothesized that PLP1c-binding antibodies injected with human complement into the mouse corpus callosum would provide an anatomically-relevant model to study the effect of PLP1c-autoantibodies on myelin injury and repair. Adult mice were injected in the right corpus callosum near the cingulum with a mixture of 2 µg recombinant human IgG1 antibody along with 20% immunoglobulin depleted normal human serum (NHS) or with the indicated control formulation (Fig. 1A). Depletion of human Igs from the NHS was critical to reducing background demyelination and Ig depleted serum co-injected with PLP1c binding antibody produced a large area of C5b-9 deposition (Sup. Figure 1A, B, C). We measured lesion volume by absence of myelin basic protein (MBP) in skip serial sections at seven days post injection (DPI) and found significant demyelination from PLP1c-binding rAb plus NHS (Fig. 1B, Sup. Figure 1D). Injection of NHS alone, PLP1c-binding rAb alone (clone MS04-2#30), isotype rAb (clone 2B4), or isotype rAb+ NHS did not result in significant demyelination. These findings indicated that, consistent with our prior work, demyelination was PLP1c antigen specific and complement dependent [18]. Consistent with other focal demyelination models in the corpus callosum, we did not observe any gross motor deficits or behavioral alterations in demyelinated mice [21, 43]. We next measured the evolution of lesion volumes by skip serial section reconstruction at 3, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35 DPI (Fig. 1C, Sup. Figure 2). Lesion boundaries were determined based on spectral confocal reflectance (SCoRe) imaging, which visualizes compact myelin sheaths. The area of myelin loss measured by SCoRe reached a maximum volume at 14 DPI before reducing to about 20% of the maximum volume at 35 DPI. A similar pattern of lesion development and recovery was seen using IBA1 as a marker of microgliosis (Fig. 1D, Sup. Figure 2). No sex differences in SCoRe or IBA1 lesion volumes were noted. Human antibody signal remained in the lesion up to 14 DPI (Fig. 1E, Sup. Figure 2). In addition to persistent human recombinant antibody, degraded myelin was apparent in the lesion to 21 DPI (Fig. 1F, Sup. Figure 2) [23]. These findings indicate that a PLP1c-binding IgG1 causes complement-dependent transient demyelination and recovery in the mouse corpus callosum on a time frame consistent with other focal demyelination models [33].

Oligodendrocyte differentiation correlates with lesion resolution

To determine how oligodendrocyte numbers and differentiation states change during antibody-mediated demyelination and subsequent remyelination, oligodendrocyte counts and frequencies were quantified across the same serial timepoints as above. We hypothesized that once antibody and degraded myelin were cleared from the lesion, differentiation of new oligodendrocytes would proceed. The density of OLIG2+ oligodendrocyte lineage cells within the growing lesion remained at the same low level during lesion development to 14 DPI but returned to uninjured levels between 14 DPI and 28 DPI (Fig. 2A, Sup. Figure 3). The density and fraction of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) increased during lesion development until 14 DPI, then decreased during lesion recovery (Fig. 2B, Sup. Figure 3). Numbers of premyelinating cells increased early in recovery, with TCF7L2+ and BCAS1+ premyelinating oligodendrocyte numbers peaking at 21 DPI and 28 DPI respectively (Fig. 2C, D, Sup. Figure 3) [9, 12]. BCAS1+ premyelinating oligodendrocyte numbers remained high from 21 DPI onwards, consistent with the fact the BCAS1 expression continues into the myelinating oligodendrocyte stage [9], while TCF7L2, a transient premyelinating marker, began to decrease after 21 DPI. The fraction of BCAS1+ oligodendrocytes remained relatively constant over the time course, with the numbers of BCAS+ cells increasing proportionally to the increases in the total number of OLIG2+ cells. Increases in the number of mature oligodendrocytes were apparent later in the time course, with the number of GSTπ+ cells increasing from 14 to 35 DPI but never fully recovering to uninjured levels (Fig. 2E, Sup. Figure 3) [3, 42]. All oligodendrocyte markers were colocalized to OLIG2+ cells to ensure counts represented definitive oligodendrocyte lineage cells. These data show oligodendrocyte loss and recovery in this setting that is similar in timing to other focal demyelination models.

CSF1R inhibition has limited depletive effects in the lesion microenvironment and does not impact lesion resolution

Microglia depletion via CSF1R inhibition has been reported to alter lesion development and resolution in cuprizone, lysolecithin, and virus-mediated demyelination contexts [22, 25, 39]. We hypothesized that CSF1R inhibition during lesion recovery would deplete microglia from the lesion and slow the recovery of lesion size. We targeted microglial depletion at three time points during recovery: either continuously from before the peak of OPC density in the lesion through recovery, continuously starting when the increase in premyelinating cell density starts, or only at the late stage of recovery starting at the time of the increase in mature cell density. Mice were fed chow containing PLX5622, a selective CSF1R inhibitor [4], from days 10 to 25, 17 to 25, or 21 to 25 after PLP1c-binding antibody-mediated demyelination (Fig. 3A). At 25 DPI, regardless of the timing of administration, animals receiving PLX5622 showed approximately an 80% reduction in the number of IBA1+ cells in the white matter of the contralateral hemisphere compared to animals receiving control chow (Fig. 3B, C). IBA1 signal within the lesioned tissue of PLX5622-treated animals, however, was not significantly altered relative to controls (Fig. 3B, D). Analysis of SCoRe images revealed no changes in lesion size attributable to CSF1R inhibition at 25 DPI for any treatment window (Fig. 3E, I). The persistence of human antibody in the corpus callosum lesion also showed no quantitative changes between treated and control animals (Fig. 3F, J). Finally, analysis of degraded MBP in the lesion, as measured by QD9 staining (thresholded area or fluorescence intensity), showed no treatment-related effects (Fig. 3G, H) [23]. These data together indicate limited or no change in phagocyte density, antibody clearance, or myelin debris clearance in response to CSF1R inhibition, irrespective of the timing of CSF1R inhibition during recovery.

CSF1R inhibition moderately promotes oligodendrocyte differentiation but not maturation

Previous research has reported that microglia aid remyelination by promoting debris clearance and recruitment of remyelinating oligodendrocytes [25, 39]. Hence, we hypothesized that CSF1R inhibition during the recovery phase of antibody-mediated demyelination would reduce the number of differentiating and new oligodendrocytes in recovering lesions. In contrast, we observed limited changes in oligodendrocyte lineage populations in animals fed PLX5622 chow at any timepoint after antibody-mediated demyelination (Fig. 3). At the 25 DPI endpoint, we observed no change in total densities of OLIG2+ oligodendrocyte lineage cells within the residual lesion volumes after CSF1R inhibition (Fig. 4A). Similarly, there were no changes in the density or fraction of PDGFRα+ OLIG2+ OPCs in lesions (Fig. 4B). Although we observed increases in the density and fraction of BCAS1+ OLIG2+ premyelinating cells in all PLX5622 treated conditions, they were not statistically significant (Fig. 4C). The number of TCF7L2+ OLIG2+ premyelinating cells was significantly increased in animals receiving PLX5622 chow from 10 to 25 and 21–25 DPI, while the fraction remained the same in all treatment conditions (Fig. 4D). These increases in premyelinating cells, however, did not impact the later stages of differentiation, as the density and fraction of mature GSTπ+ OLIG2+ cells was not altered (Fig. 4E). These data point towards a small effect of CSF1R inhibition in initial differentiation of OPCs in the remyelinating environment that ultimately does not alter the number of mature oligodendrocytes.

Discussion

We present and characterize a demyelination model that is closely tied to a pathogenic autoantibody recovered from MS patients. Consistent with our previous work, demyelination mediated by PLP1c-binding antibody in the corpus callosum was dependent on co-injection of human serum containing complement components [18, 32]. The current study defined the time frame of demyelination and remyelination in this model: maximum demyelination was measured at 14 DPI, which is later than the 3 to 7 days peak observed in lysolecithin mediated focal demyelination, and suggests a more extended damage phase [33, 43]. Lesions were largely remyelinated by 35 DPI, although some residual damage was still apparent and counts of mature oligodendrocytes remained lower relative to uninjured controls. It is unclear if full recovery is achievable, and whether it would require months or rapidly resolve along a linear trajectory. This is an important question as repair in MS patients or remyelination in other animal models, such as cuprizone-induced cortical demyelination, do not result in complete remyelination [24].

Recovery from PLP1c-autoantibody demyelination was not impacted by microglial depletion by CSF1R inhibition. Given the well-established concept that oligodendrocyte progenitor cells maintain their density in homeostasis, it was not surprising that the number of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells was not impacted by the length of CSF1R inhibition [5]. However, CSF1R inhibition impacted oligodendrocyte differentiation in the recovering lesion. Regardless of the timing of CSF1R inhibition during recovery, there was a small but significant increase in the number of TCF7L2+ premyelinating oligodendrocyte lineage cells within the lesion area at 25 DPI when compared to control. BCAS1+ premyelinating oligodendrocyte lineage cells showed a trend in the same direction but did not achieve significance, possibly because after turning on at a premyelinating stage, BCAS1 continues to mark a wider range of myelinating cells on the time scale of weeks [9]. These findings are similar to recent effects reported in response to microglial depletion in a cuprizone demyelination model [20] suggesting that microglial depletion may enhance OPC differentiation to premyelinating cells. The changes in the number of premyelinating cells did not ultimately impact the maturation to myelinating oligodendrocytes as there were no significant changes in the number or proportion of mature oligodendrocytes.

The ability of MS patient-derived antibodies to cause rapid demyelination in the mouse corpus callosum suggests that antibody-mediated pathology may play a direct role in CNS damage analogous to antibody-mediated pathology in other inflammatory neurological conditions [35, 38]. Although SCoRe imaging is able to resolve individual myelin sheaths along their length, it does not allow us to estimate the fraction of remyelinated axons or the thickness of new myelin. Similarly, although neurofilament staining revealed largely intact axons at the 25 DPI timepoint, we observed some axonal blebbing indicative of axonal pathology (unpublished data). Future work will be required to characterize off-target damage to axons and the quality of remyelination in this model.

Unexpectedly, and regardless of timing, treatment with a CSF1R inhibitor had a very limited effect in the remyelinating environment. Studies in various models of demyelination have documented that the microglial response during remyelination is complex and dependent on the inflammatory state of the cells and the stage of recovery [14, 25, 39]. Clearance of myelin debris in the demyelinated lesion by microglia and macrophages is an essential step in the remyelination process [2]. Depleting microglia during early debris clearance results in impaired oligodendrocyte differentiation and diminished remyelination of the damaged tissue [14]. Given our focus on the impact of microglia in the recovery phase, we started the depletion near the end of the demyelination phase. Although we observed peak loss of myelin at 14 DPI, it is possible that key debris clearance and signaling events happen prior to our earliest depletion window. Alternatively, complement-dependent demyelination may recruit CSF1R-independent myeloid cells into the CNS that compensate for the innate myelin clearance capacity of microglia. Additionally, the microenvironment established by complement-dependent cytotoxicity may provide an alternative survival signal to microglia that is absent in other injuries. Activated microglia such as those observed in Alzheimer’s disease plaques survive independent of CSF1R signaling [10, 40]. Very dense IBA1 staining was seen in the demyelinated lesions, regardless of CSF1R inhibitor treatment (Fig. 3), despite the surrounding tissue having significant loss of IBA1-positive cells. These findings underline the need to measure functional changes in microglia and peripheral macrophage populations both in the general brain parenchyma as well as the affected microenvironment.

Multiple cell types in the demyelinating environment may detect and respond to antibodies. Fc receptors on microglia and macrophages may stimulate the secretion of signaling molecules that affect oligodendrocyte survival and maturation such as IGF1, IL1𝛽, and IFN𝛽 [13, 44]. When microglia/macrophages are depleted experimentally, the loss of these cytokines and growth factors may alter the recruitment of OPCs and the differentiation of premyelinating oligodendrocytes independent of myelin debris clearance [14]. Although we observed changes in the premyelinating oligodendrocyte population, we did not define a mechanistic connection between microglial depletion and these oligodendrocyte changes. Our earliest microglia depletion window likely did not deplete microglia until OPCs had reached their peak density in the lesion. It should be noted that outside the context of acute demyelinating injury studied here, the interaction between microglia and myelin binding antibodies could produce distinct responses of importance to MS pathology. Regardless, recent works show that OPC differentiation rates are high in many environments and the events leading to successful terminal maturation may be more critical than differentiation attempts to successful remyelination [26]. Finally, the changes we observed in premyelinating oligodendrocyte density could represent a delay in the timing of differentiation rather than an increase in the rate of differentiation of oligodendrocytes.

While reports of complement mediated demyelination in MS lesions are decades old, the importance of this process to pathogenesis and clinical practice is now becoming evident [28, 29, 41]. Nevertheless, peak lesion volume in this model, using multiple metrics, is not reached until 14 DPI, which suggests alternative pathways of injury or delayed oligodendrocyte death. Persistent CDC is unlikely due to limiting amounts of human complement proteins; therefore, ADCC or ADCP could continue as levels of human antibody bound to myelin and myelin debris remain high in the lesion. Incomplete and poorly characterized cross-reaction between human IgG and mouse Fc receptors, however, makes the contribution of ADCC and ADCP difficult to interpret without further work using PLP1c recombinant antibodies with modified Fc backbones [30, 38].

Conclusions

In this study, we show that PLP1c-binding autoantibodies injected into the corpus callosum cause focal demyelinating lesions that are dependent on complement proteins, peak at 14 days, and subsequently resolve as measured by myelin reflectance, immunoglobulin clearance, and oligodendrocyte differentiation after 5 weeks. CSF1R inhibition, initiated at 10 DPI or later, has limited impact on lesion resolution. Despite robust depletion of microglia in non-lesioned brain tissue, CSF1R inhibition did not deplete myeloid cells in the recovering lesion. CSF1R inhibition increased the density of premyelinating oligodendrocyte lineage cells but not the extent of remyelination. These findings underline the potential of MS patient-derived CSF autoantibodies to directly cause demyelination and impact lesion recovery. The limited effect of CSF1R inhibition on microglial survival in the recovering lesion illustrates that the mechanism of injury and its effect on local microglial differentiation and myeloid infiltration likely plays an impactful role in remyelination.
