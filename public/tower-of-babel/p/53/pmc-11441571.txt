Heritable gene editing in tomato through viral delivery of isopentenyl transferase and single-guide RNAs to latent axillary meristematic cells
Authors: Degao Liu, Evan E. Ellison, Erik A. Myers, Lilee I. Donahue, Shuya Xuan, Ryan Swanson, Songyan Qi, Lynn E. Prichard, Colby G. Starker, Daniel F. Voytas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificancePlant gene editing often relies on tissue culture, which requires considerable time and effort. Viral delivery of gene editing reagents through infection is an emerging approach to create germline-edited plants. Many plant species, however, exclude viruses from the meristem, which is a barrier in using viruses as vectors for gene editing. Here, we overcome this barrier by inducing gene-edited shoots de novo through viral delivery of both gene editing reagents and a cytokinin biosynthesis gene to latent axillary meristematic cells. Cytokinin induces multiple gene-edited shoots from infected cells. Our method is likely applicable to diverse plant species and will help realize the promise of gene editing at scale in model and crop plants.

Genome editing is a powerful tool for both accelerating biological discovery and crop improvement (1–3). The potential of gene editing, however, has not been fully realized in many plant species due to the dependence on tissue culture for creating gene-edited plants (4). Regeneration of plants through tissue culture works for limited species and genotypes and requires considerable time and effort (5). Recently, developmental regulators, such as the cytokinin biosynthesis gene, isopentenyl transferase (ipt), have been used to avoid tissue culture for in planta gene editing (6). In this approach, ipt and gene editing reagents are delivered to cells on soil-grown plants, and their expression induces formation of gene-edited shoots that produce flowers and transmit the targeted gene modification to the next generation. Constitutive expression of developmental regulators, however, often results in developmental abnormalities. For example, expression of ipt in sink tissue in Solanum lycopersicum (tomato) leads to abnormal morphological changes, such as smaller leaves and swollen stems (7). Another method to bypass tissue culture utilizes RNA viral vectors to deliver sgRNAs directly to plant stem cells to create heritable gene edits (virus-induced gene editing, VIGE). This approach has been used to create targeted, heritable genetic modifications in the dicotyledonous model plants Nicotiana benthamiana and Arabidopsis thaliana using Tobacco Rattle Virus (TRV) vectors (8–11). The approach has also worked in monocotyledonous crop species, namely wheat and barley, using vectors derived from Barley Stripe Mosaic Virus (BSMV) (12–15). However, heritable gene editing in dicotyledonous crop species using RNA viral vectors has not yet been achieved.

TRV is a desirable vector for delivering gene editing reagents due to its high infectivity, minimal host symptoms, broad host range, and lack of seed transmission (16). TRV can invade the N. benthamiana germline; however, examples of meristem invasion in other species are limited (17). Many plants exclude viruses from meristems to protect their gametic cells from infection. This limits the use of VIGE, as the virally delivered sgRNAs cannot access the germline to create heritable genetic modifications. Adding RNA motifs to sgRNAs that promote cell-to-cell movement can increase frequencies of heritable mutagenesis (9); however, additional methods are needed to overcome virus meristem exclusion to enable more robust VIGE in crop species.

In this study, we implement three approaches to overcome viral meristem exclusion in tomato to enable heritable gene editing. These approaches involve either de novo induction of gene-edited meristems by trimming to induce shoots from edited somatic cells or viral infection of latent axillary meristematic cells. In a third approach, de novo meristem induction is improved by inclusion of ipt on viral vectors to promote shoot formation. Phenotypically normal, gene-edited shoots are efficiently induced on infected plants, and the edits are transmitted to progeny. Further, we demonstrate delivery of multiple sgRNAs for multiplexed gene editing.

Results

Viral Delivery of sgRNAs for Tomato Genome Editing.

To monitor virus-induced mutagenesis of somatic cells in tomato, we engineered TRV vectors that express a sgRNA targeting PHYTOENE DESATURASE (PDS); loss of PDS results in photobleaching due to the lack of photoprotective carotenoids (9). The PDS sgRNA included the mobility motif from Arabidopsis FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT), which presumably promotes cell-to-cell movement of the sgRNA, resulting in increased frequencies of somatic and germline mutagenesis in Cas9-expressing N. benthamiana (Fig. 1A) (9). The mobile PDS sgRNA was driven by a Pea Early Browning Virus subgenomic promoter, enabling its expression via the virus’ RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. The TRV vectors were delivered to Cas9-expressing transgenic tomato lines, cultivar Robura, via the Agrobacterium tumefaciens leaf infiltration method. After 3 wk at 22 °C, we observed white sectors characteristic of loss of PDS function in systemically infected leaves (Fig. 1B). Mutagenesis frequencies at PDS in mature leaves displaying a photobleached phenotype averaged 68% ± 12.4% (n = 8), indicating that the photobleached phenotype was likely induced by mutations in the PDS gene. As the infected tomato plants matured, typically no sectoring was observed in the later developing leaves. This differs from analogous experiments performed in N. benthamiana, wherein white sectoring often becomes more extensive as infected plants mature (9). Our observations are consistent with reports of Virus-Induced Gene Silencing (VIGS) in tomato, wherein silencing is less prevalent in older tissues, presumably because virus infection is mitigated by plant immunity (18). Lower temperatures can counteract systemic silencing of viruses and promote VIGS in older tissues and fruit (18). Indeed, reducing the temperature to 18 °C after launching the viral infection resulted in plants with pds phenotypes in later developing leaves. This was observed when the TRV vectors expressed sgRNAs fused to mobility motifs from either FT or a tRNA isoleucine (tRNAIleu). Despite the more pronounced and late persistence of the pds phenotype, fully bleached shoots, as seen in N. benthamiana (9), were not observed, and no heritable mutations were recovered (SI Appendix, Table S1).

De Novo Induction of Gene-Edited Meristems through Trimming.

Because we effectively edited somatic cells, we next sought to induce de novo meristems from these edited cells. We infected plants with our viral vectors and after 8 to 12 wk, plants were trimmed to induce new shoots (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1) (6). Remarkably, among 10 trimmed plants, 6 fully photobleached shoots and 1 pale green shoot emerged (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Four of these shoots produced fruit (Fig. 1E), but only the pale green shoot produced fruit with seeds (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 E–G). We believe that the pale green shoot was a chimera, and the green tissues offset the defect due to loss of PDS function. Nine seeds were obtained from fruit produced by the pale green shoot, and all produced seedlings that were completely photobleached with targeted, inactivating indel mutations in PDS (Fig. 1 F and G). We concluded that heritable gene edits can be recovered in tomato through viral delivery of sgRNAs; however, lower temperatures are required to promote infection, and trimming is necessary to induce shoots from what are likely edited somatic cells. Our success was enabled by the highly visible pds phenotype, allowing us to identify and focus on edited shoots.

De Novo Induction of Gene-Edited Shoots through Viral Infection of Latent Axillary Meristematic Cells.

Our ability to induce gene-edited shoots from trimmed plants suggested that it may be advantageous to directly deliver our viral vectors to latent axillary meristematic cells (Fig. 2A). These cells reside in the axil of the leaf, and as shown in tomato and Arabidopsis (19, 20), undergo only a limited number of cell divisions. Furthermore, they do not express WUS, which is involved in protecting stem cells from viruses (21), and de novo apical meristems can be generated from latent axillary meristematic cells by inducing WUS expression with a cytokinin signaling pulse (20, 22, 23). Directly delivering our TRV vectors in proximity to the latent axillary meristematic tissues contrasts with the experiments described above, wherein the viral vectors were delivered to seedling cotyledons, and required waiting 8 to 12 wk for systemic spread to edit the cells that gave rise to shoots after trimming.

To test delivery to latent meristematic cells, we cloned into our TRV vectors a sgRNA targeting PDS with tRNAIleu-derived mobility sequences. Also, the PDS sgRNA, driven by a U6 promoter, was cloned into a conventional transfer DNA (T-DNA) vector to yield pU6::PDS sgRNA. The TRV-PDS sgRNA and pU6::PDS sgRNA vectors were transferred into Agrobacterium strain GV3101, cultures were grown and diluted to an OD600 of either 0.25 or 0.50. Transgenic Cas9 tomato seedlings at the three or four true leaf stage were pruned to remove upper shoots (keeping two true leaves and two cotyledons). Agrobacterium strains with the TRV-PDS sgRNA or pU6::PDS sgRNA vectors were injected into the axillary tissues of both the leaves and cotyledons using a syringe. Following vector delivery, typically 0 to 3 shoots were induced per injected plant. There were no observed pds knock-out phenotypes and no PDS editing in all the 13 shoots induced through pU6::PDS sgRNA treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). The new shoots created through viral infection of axillary tissues showed a more pronounced pds knock-out phenotype compared to that created by viral inoculation of leaves (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Instead of genotyping the mature leaves of the newly formed shoots, we genotyped a top leaf (first leaf counting from top with a length of >1.8 cm). Low gene editing frequencies (≤3%) were observed in the top leaves of all five plants infected through leaf infiltration (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E), suggesting that virus-induced editing occurs during leaf maturation. Genotyping of the 11 shoots created by viral infection of axillary tissues (treatment at an OD600 of 0.25) found that four shoots had a total indel frequency ≥58%. Furthermore, the viral infection of axillary tissues generated a photobleached shoot bud which emerged from the injection site ~39 d postinjection (DPI) (Fig. 2B). The bud developed into a fully photobleached shoot. Sanger sequencing revealed that this shoot had −2 bp/−1 bp biallelic mutations (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Unfortunately, the shoot did not set fruit and eventually died. Since the frequency of unique sequence modifications in all other shoots was ≤24.0% (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), we reasoned that the mutations were not likely fixed, and so we did not check for heritability. Collectively, we achieved de novo induction of gene-edited meristems by TRV infection to latent axillary meristematic cells. A biallelic mutant shoot emerged around 1 mo postinjection, suggesting that removing the shoots that appear in the first month can increase the frequency of germline-edited shoots, hence, all the shoots that appeared in the first month in subsequent experiments were removed and discarded.

To further demonstrate utility of the axillary meristematic cell infection method, we next targeted four other tomato genes: I SUBUNIT OF MAGNESIUM-CHELATASE (CHLI), HAIRLESS, HAIRPLUS, and ANTHOCYANIN 2 (AN2) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). CHLI is involved in chlorophyll biosynthesis in Arabidopsis (24). The spontaneous tomato hairless mutant is defective in trichome development; its trichomes are severely bent and shortened, resulting in a granular appearance to the epidermal surfaces (25). Knock-out of HAIRPLUS increases trichome density (26), and VIGS of AN2 strongly decreased anthocyanin accumulation in tomato leaves and stems (27). sgRNAs targeting CHLI and HAIRLESS were designed and cloned individually into TRV2. An AN2 sgRNA was designed and cloned into TRV2 along with the HAIRPLUS sgRNA to generate a duplex editing vector. The TRV vectors were injected into the latent axillary meristematic tissues, and all shoots that appeared in the first month were removed and discarded. Shoots appearing after the first month were genotyped. Yellow-white shoots and hairless shoots formed at the injection sites of TRV-CHLI and TRV-HAIRLESS sgRNAs, respectively (Fig. 2 C and D). ICE CRISPR Analysis Tool (28) or Deconvolution of Complex DNA Repair (DECODR) (29) were used to quantify indel frequencies. The TRV-CHLI and TRV-HAIRLESS sgRNAs created gene-edited shoots (total indels > 15%) at a frequency of 4/4 and 9/10, respectively; The shoots with at least one sequence modification ≥35% were created at a frequency of 3/4 and 7/10 (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Table S2). A fully green shoot formed at the injection site of TRV-HAIRPLUS/AN2 sgRNAs, suggesting anthocyanin production was impaired (Fig. 2E). The TRV-HAIRPLUS/AN2 sgRNAs created AN2-edited shoots at a frequency of 7/12; the number of shoots carrying at least one sequence modification ≥35% in AN2 was 4/12. We observed low editing frequencies from the HAIRPLUS sgRNA (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Table S3).

To test whether the gene edits observed in the edited shoots were heritable, seeds from two shoots carrying a −1 bp (48%) and −2 bp (56%)/−6 bp (34%) mutations at HAIRLESS were germinated and genotyped (Fig. 3 A–C). Among the 10 progeny from the shoot carrying the −1 bp (48%) mutation, 1 homozygous mutant (−1 bp/−1 bp) and 8 monoallelic mutants (−1 bp/WT) were identified (Fig. 3C). We genotyped seven progeny seedlings from the parental shoot carrying −2 bp (56%)/−6 bp (34%) and recovered one homozygous mutant (−2 bp/−2 bp), four biallelic mutants (−2 bp/−6 bp), and two homozygous mutants (−6 bp/−6 bp). The homozygous mutant carrying the −1 bp/−1 bp mutations or carrying −2 bp/−2 bp mutations exhibited the hairless phenotype on hypocotyls, stems, leaves, and floral organs (Fig. 3 A and B). The two homozygous mutants carrying −6 bp/−6 bp mutations did not exhibit the hairless phenotype, indicating that the mutated HAIRLESS gene remains functional. For the heritability analysis of AN2-edited shoots, seeds collected from two shoots carrying −5 bp (49%)/−1 bp (45%) and −8 bp (50%), respectively, were germinated and genotyped (Fig. 3 D–F). Among the three seedlings from the parental shoot carrying −5 bp (49%)/−1 bp (45%), two biallelic mutants (−5 bp/−1 bp) and one homozygous mutant (−5 bp/−5 bp) were identified (Fig. 3F). We also genotyped nine progeny seedlings from the parental shoot carrying −8 bp (50%) and recovered two homozygous mutants (−8 bp/−8 bp) and four monoallelic mutants (−8 bp/WT). The monoallelic mutants and WT exhibited purple coloration in stems and young petioles when grown under a light intensity of 400 µmol m–2 s–1; only green coloration was observed in the homozygous knock-out mutants and biallelic knock-out mutants (Fig. 3 D and E). Unfortunately, the CHLI-edited shoot did not set fruit and eventually died. We extended this method to another genotype: we injected latent axillary meristematic tissue of a Cas9-expressing line of tomato cultivar, M82, with a TRV vector delivering a sgRNA targeting CYCLOPS (Solyc08g075760) and the FT-derived mobility motif (30). After 1 mo, sequence modifications were identified in 5/6 emerged shoots with 2/6 containing at least one sequence modification ≥35% (SI Appendix, Table S4). Five seedlings from one of these two highly edited shoots were genotyped and all five were revealed to be homozygous (+2 bp/+2 bp or −3 bp/−3 bp) or biallelic (+2 bp/−3 bp) mutants (SI Appendix, Table S5). In conclusion, we efficiently induced gene-edited shoots through viral infection of latent axillary meristematic cells in two tomato cultivars, and the gene edits were transmitted to progeny.

De Novo Induction of Gene-Edited Meristems through Viral Delivery of ipt and sgRNAs.

Since only a few shoots were induced at injection sites, recovering a variety of mutation types or multiplexed editing likely requires injection of multiple plants. To promote induction of multiple shoots, we transiently delivered ipt together with sgRNAs into axillary tissues using TRV. Expression of ipt produces cytokinin, promoting shoot growth and meristem proliferation (6). ipt was cloned into TRV along with a mobile sgRNA targeting PDS, generating VirIPT-PDS sgRNA (Fig. 4A). The concentrations of TRV1 and TRV2 were adjusted to OD600 = 0.25 or 0.5 and injected into the axillary tissues as described above. Plants were grown at 18 °C to increase TRV accumulation. Abundant shoots emerged from the injected plants at 39 DPI, but these shoots showed developmental abnormalities (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). As described above, viral silencing in plants is temperature-dependent, and the accumulation of RNA viruses such as TRV decreases with rising temperature (31, 32). We reasoned that the developmental abnormalities of VirIPT-induced shoots might be relieved if higher temperature decreased RNA virus accumulation. Indeed, the plants growing at 25 °C showed less developmental abnormalities (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). We then cut the shoots, dipped them into rooting hormone, and grew the shoots in soil at 28 °C (Fig. 4D); the developing plants appeared phenotypically normal and no longer showed the effects of ectopically expressed cytokinin. A subset of the induced plants was photobleached or had bleached sectors, suggesting inactivation of PDS (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). The treatments of OD600 = 0.25 and 0.5 produced PDS-edited shoots at frequencies of 45.5 to 58.8% and 48.1 to 100%, respectively; Shoots carrying editing in at least one allele were recovered at a frequency of 5.3 to 22.2% and 18.5 to 94.1%, respectively (Fig. 4E). We observed a series of tomato genotypes with different fruit colors, including red, yellow, white, red with white stripes and red with yellow stripes (Fig. 4 F and G and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). To test whether the PDS-edits observed in the parental shoots were heritable, we genotyped progeny and recovered homozygous mutants from all five parental shoots (Fig. 4 H–J and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Table S6). Bleached progeny with inactivating mutations in both PDS alleles were also recovered. There is no morphological difference between green progeny carrying monoallelic mutations and WT plants (Fig. 4I). Thus, the transient VirIPT method enables induction of abundant gene-edited shoots, eliminating the phenotypic defects of constitutively expressed developmental regulators, and enables recovery of heritable edits from these shoots.

Multiplexed Gene Editing through Viral Delivery of ipt and sgRNAs.

To test whether the VirIPT method enables multiplexed gene editing, we cloned ipt, and two mobile sgRNAs targeting UGT75C1 (Solyc09g092500) and HAIRLESS into a TRV2 vector, generating VirIPT-UGT75C1/HAIRLESS sgRNAs (Fig. 5A). The two sgRNAs were separated by a 24 bp spacer (tcgcggccgggtacgtgttcgagc). UGT75C1 encodes UDP-glucose: anthocyanin 5-O-glucosyltransferase (5GT), which is an important enzyme for modifying anthocyanins and making them more stable (33, 34). We injected VirIPT-UGT75C1/HAIRLESS sgRNAs into the axillary tissues as described above and the plants were grown at 18 °C. Shoots formed during the first month postinfection were removed; abundant new shoots emerged from the injected plants during the second month postinfection (Fig. 5B). We then moved the plants to 28 °C where the shoots showed less developmental abnormalities (Fig. 5C). The shoots were cut, dipped in rooting hormone and grown in soil (Fig. 5D). A subset of shoots showed the hairless phenotype, suggesting inactivation of HAIRLESS (Fig. 5E). Shoots induced from two infected plants were genotyped. Efficient targeted mutagenesis at UGT75C1 or HAIRLESS was detected (Fig. 5F). The two infected plants produced shoots carrying duplexed editing of both UGT75C1 and HAIRLESS at a frequency of 6/13 and 7/12, respectively (Fig. 5G). Shoots carrying at least one sequence modification ≥35% in both UGT75C1 and HAIRLESS were recovered at a frequency of 2/13 and 2/12, respectively. The shoots set fruit normally, and the hairless phenotype was also observed at the reproductive stage (Fig. 5 H–J). We genotyped progeny from two parental shoots; six of eight progeny from one shoot and eight of eleven progeny from the other shoot carried mutations at both UGT75C1 and HAIRLESS (Fig. 5K and SI Appendix, Table S7). The hairless phenotype was observed in the progeny carrying mutations in both HAIRLESS alleles (Fig. 5 L–N). Thus, the VirIPT method also enables duplexed heritable gene editing in tomato.

Discussion

Tissue culture-free gene editing promises to greatly accelerate crop improvement. VIGE is emerging as an effective in planta gene editing approach. However, the exclusion of viruses from the meristem is a barrier for the widespread use of VIGE in crop gene editing. In this study, we first developed a method to avert meristem exclusion by inducing de novo meristems from pruned plants that were infected through cotyledon and leaf infiltration. To increase the frequency of creating germline gene edits, we delivered the TRV vectors expressing mobile sgRNAs directly to axillary meristematic tissues. Germline-edited shoots carrying monoallelic or biallelic mutations were efficiently induced in two cultivars, and the mutations were transmitted to progeny. Thus, our methods avert exclusion of viruses from meristems—a bottleneck of virus-induced gene editing in plants—and achieve creation of heritable gene edits in tomato.

Though the viral infection of latent axillary meristematic cells efficiently created germline-edited shoots, typically only few shoots were induced at each injection site. We previously found that the expression of ipt, which was delivered on DNA replicons, induced multiple shoots per plant, but constitutive expression of ipt often results in developmental abnormalities (6). In this study, we transiently expressed ipt together with sgRNAs using RNA viral vectors. However, many of these shoots were phenotypically abnormal due to persistent expression of ipt from our viral vectors. Temperature is known to dramatically affect RNA silencing-based antiviral defense in plants, and the accumulation of RNA viruses such as TRV decreases with rising temperature (31, 32). We previously found that growing infected Arabidopsis plants at low temperatures (18 °C) greatly enhanced TRV-mediated base-editing (10). In the current study, we found that low temperatures (18 °C) increased somatic editing in the TRV-infected tomato plants. We also took advantage of the effects of temperature on TRV accumulation to regulate ipt expression, i.e., we increased ipt expression under low temperatures (18 °C) to induce meristems and inhibited ipt expression under high temperatures (28 °C) to relieve the developmental abnormalities in the shoots. Our work found a simple and efficient strategy to control TRV-mediated gene editing and the expression of developmental regulators through temperature manipulation.

RNA viruses are constrained by their limited cargo capacity, typically around 1 kb, which prevents their use for delivering larger gene editing reagents such as Cas9 from Streptococcus pyogenes (4.1 kb). Thus, a limitation of the TRV-mediated sgRNA delivery system for heritable gene editing is the requirement for a Cas9-expressing plant. Recent work has demonstrated the potential for gene editing in plants using hypercompact Cas systems (35). Future studies could leverage TRV vectors to deliver hypercompact Cas systems into WT plants, thereby eliminating the need for a Cas-expressing plant.

In conclusion, we developed three methods to efficiently bypass tissue culture and achieve heritable gene editing in tomato through induction of shoots from edited somatic cells, viral infection of latent axillary meristematic cells, and viral delivery of ipt and sgRNAs. Only a single transgenic plant expressing Cas9 needs to be generated; thereafter, it can be used repeatedly to generate nearly unlimited genetic diversity. Generating gene-edited plantlets using tissue culture typically takes at least 4 mo (36), whereas gene-edited shoots can be generated using our axillary meristem injection method in 2 mo. Because stimulating the growth of axillary buds by cytokinin and pruning shoot apical meristems is a common plant propagation technique, and TRV can infect a wide range of plants, our method is likely to efficiently create heritable gene edits in diverse agriculturally relevant species, promising to enable gene editing at scale for biological discovery and to accelerate the genetic improvement of crops.

Materials and Methods

Vector Construction.

Our Golden Gate cloning platform was used for vector construction (37). Briefly, each vector expressed from TRV2 a sgRNA from the Pea Early Browning Virus (PEBV) subgenomic promoter. The vectors used in Fig. 1 express sgRNAs with mobility motifs from either FT (pEE485) or tRNAIleu (pEE771). Some vectors tested included sequences from a WUS gene fragment (pEE834); endogenous WUS gene expression has been shown to be involved in virus entry into the meristem in Arabidopsis (21). The vectors used in Figs. 2–5 expressed sgRNAs with an enhanced sgRNA architecture and a tRNAIleu (10). The sgRNAs targeted PDS (pDL518), CHL1 (pDL562), or HAIRLESS (pDL649). Multiplexing vectors expressed either two sgRNAs from the PEBV promoter (targeting HAIRPLUS and AN2, pDL639); alternatively, they expressed ipt and a sgRNA targeting PDS (pDL560) or ipt and two sgRNAs targeting UGT75C1 and HAIRLESS (pDL691). Also, the PDS sgRNA, driven by a U6 promoter, was cloned into a conventional transfer DNA (T-DNA) vector to yield pU6::PDS sgRNA (pDL655). All vectors were transferred into Agrobacterium strain GV3101. The vectors and their complete DNA sequences will be available at Addgene.

TRV Infection through Leaf Infiltration.

A Cas9-expressing line of tomato, cultivar Robura, was generated using a T-DNA with Cas9 expressed from a 35S promoter (pTC232) (38). Sterile seeds were germinated on petri dishes with solid ½ Murashige and Skoog medium and 50 ng µL−1 kanamycin; plates were maintained at 25 °C with a 16 h/8 h day/night cycle. When the first true leaves emerged (after approximately 2 wk), seedlings were moved to soil and kept for 5 d in a growth chamber at 22 °C with a 16 h/8 h day/night light cycle, a light intensity of 100 μmol m–2 s–1, and 30% humidity. Agrobacterium cultures were initiated 2 d prior to infection. Strains with T-DNA plasmids harboring either TRV1 or TRV2 were grown separately in lysogeny broth (LB) with 50 ng µL−1 kanamycin and 50 ng µL−1 gentamycin for 2 d at 28 °C. Cells were pelleted in a centrifuge for 10 min at 2,500 relative centrifugal force (RCF). The supernatant was discarded, and four mL of infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2 and 10 mM 2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid, pH5.6) was used to resuspend the cells. Cells were again pelleted for 10 min at 2,500 RCF, and the supernatant was discarded. Infiltration buffer was used to resuspend the cells to OD600 = 1.5. Acetosyringone was added to 200 µM, and after approximately 3 to 4 h, strains with TRV1 (carrying pYL192) and TRV2 were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and infiltrated into the cotyledons and first true leaves of the tomato seedlings.

De Novo Induction of Gene-Edited Meristems through Trimming.

For the trimming protocol, infected plants were kept in a growth chamber at 18 °C for 2 to 3 mo or until 3 to 5 axillary shoots emerged (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The apical meristem was then removed, and a razor blade was used to excise the axillary meristems (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Plants were then moved from 8 cm × 8 cm × 8 cm pots to 15 cm × 15 cm ×12 cm pots and placed in a growth chamber at 22 °C, a 16 h/8 h day/night light cycle, a light intensity of 100 μmol m–2 s–1, and 30% humidity. To quantify frequencies of somatic cell gene editing, DNA was prepared from two different 8 mm leaf punches. Leaf punches were taken from all shoots that emerged after trimming, regardless of phenotype. If editing was detected, the shoot was maintained until fruit matured so that seed could be collected. If editing was not observed, then the shoot was removed at its base. Plants were trimmed a total of four times.

De Novo Induction of Gene-Edited Meristems By Infecting Latent Axillary Meristematic Cells.

Transgenic Cas9-expressing tomato seedlings with three to four true leaves were pruned to remove the upper shoot, keeping two true leaves and two cotyledons. The leaf axils were not excised. The culture and preparation of Agrobacterium strain GV3101 cells were performed as described above with some modifications. The Agrobacterium cells were resuspended in buffer (10 mM MgCl2 and 10 mM 2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid (MES), pH 5.6) with 0.2 mM acetosyringone and were incubated at room temperature for 3 h. For the experiments related to the TRV2 vectors without ipt, the concentrations of TRV1 and TRV2 were adjusted to OD600 = 0.25 or 0.5 for the TRV2-PDS sgRNA experiment and adjusted to OD600 = 0.25 for other target sites. TRV1 and TRV2 were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and were injected into the axillary tissues of the two true leaves and two cotyledons using syringes with 31G needles. Each site was injected ~5 times, and the Agrobacterium solution oozed from adjacent tissues after each injection. The injected tomato plants were grown at 25 °C for 2 d and then moved to 18 °C with a 16 h/8 h day/night cycle, a light intensity of 100 μmol m−2 s−1, and 50% humidity. For the PDS knock-out experiment, all the shoots emerging from TRV injection sites were genotyped. For other target sites, the shoots that appeared in the first month were removed and discarded, and all subsequent shoots were genotyped. Shoots carrying a single unique sequence modification ≥35% or two sequence modifications ≥35% were kept and moved to 28 °C/22 °C day/night growth conditions with a 16 h/8 h day/night light cycle, a light intensity of 400 µmol m–2 s–1, and 50% humidity. Otherwise, the shoots were removed to encourage new shoot formation.

For the VirIPT experiments, the concentrations of TRV1 and TRV2 were adjusted to OD600 = 0.25 or 0.5 for knocking out PDS and adjusted to OD600 = 0.5 for duplexed editing of UGT75C1 and HAIRLESS. Injection of axillary tissues was performed as described above. The injected tomato plants were grown at 25 °C for 2 d and then moved to 18 °C for meristem induction. For the VirIPT-PDS sgRNA experiment, all shoots emerging from TRV injection sites were kept. For the VirIPT-UGT75C1 sgRNA-HAIRLESS sgRNA experiment, the shoots that appeared in the first month were removed and discarded, and all subsequent shoots were kept. Plants were then moved to 25 °C or 28 °C/22 °C day/night (16 h/8 h day/night light cycle) to relieve the developmental abnormalities. The shoots were cut and dipped into rooting hormone and planted in soil. The light periods, light intensity, and humidity were the same as described above.

Genotyping Analysis.

Genomic DNA was extracted using a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol (39), or the DNeasy Plant Pro Kit (QIAGEN, Cat. No.: 69206). Targeted regions were amplified by PCR using Q5 DNA polymerase or the PHIRE Plant Direct PCR Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and gene-specific primers (SI Appendix, Table S8). PCR products were purified using either ExoSAP-IT (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN) and sent for Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics LLC). ICE CRISPR Analysis Tool (28) or Deconvolution of Complex DNA Repair (DECODR) (29) were used to quantify indel frequencies.

For the analysis of heritable editing efficiencies, the following definitions of genotype were used: total indel frequency between 0% and 15%, WT; a single unique sequence modification between 35% and 65% (with nonmodified sequence between 35% and 65%, and with sum of the single unique sequence modification and nonmodified sequence between 85% and 100%), monoallelic mutant; a single unique sequence modification between 85% and 100%, homozygous; two sequence modifications between 35% and 65% (with sum of the two sequence modifications between 85% and 100%), biallelic mutant. All seedlings assayed matched one of the above definitions.
