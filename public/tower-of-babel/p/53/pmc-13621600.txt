Characteristic flow of different right ventricular outflow tract morphology in repaired tetralogy of fallot patients studied by 4D-flow cardiac magnetic resonance images
Authors: Auttapol Srivichean, Paweena Chungsomprasong, Yonthakan Sanwong, Thanyarat Phoprom, Chodchanok Vijarnsorn, Karnkawin Patharateeranart, Watcharachai Kangvanskol, Prakul Chanthong, Thita Pacharapakornpong, Ploy Thammasate, Kritvikrom Durongpisitkul, Jarupim Soongswang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRight ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) morphology may influence hemodynamics and suitability for transcatheter pulmonic valve implantation (PPVI) in patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot (rTOF).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 120 rTOF patients who underwent cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging with 4D-flow analysis. RVOT morphology was classified as tubular, pyramidal, or trapezoid, with inverted pyramidal and concave morphologies absent. Due to small numbers in minor groups, analysis focused on tubular (n = 86) and pyramidal (n = 22) RVOTs. Conventional CMR parameters, including ventricular volumes, function, pulmonic valve (PV) annulus size, and pulmonary regurgitation (PR), as well as 4D-flow hemodynamic parameters, were compared between groups.ResultsThe tubular and pyramidal RVOT groups showed no significant differences in patient demographics, transannular patch history, long-term clinical events (arrhythmia, pulmonic valve replacement necessity), or conventional CMR measures of LV/RV volumes, function, or PR severity. However, the 4D-flow analysis revealed distinct hemodynamic profiles. The tubular RVOT group generally demonstrated a higher hemodynamic load than the pyramidal group, specifically showing higher peak velocity at the RVOT (but lower at the RPA), higher maximum magnitude wall shear stress (WSS) at the RVOT, PV, and MPA (but lower at the RPA), higher average magnitude WSS at the PV and MPA and greater average energy loss at PV.ConclusionsDespite similar conventional CMR findings and clinical outcomes, the tubular RVOT morphology is associated with a significantly higher local hemodynamic profile across the PV and MPA compared to the pyramidal morphology. This suggests that advanced 4D-flow parameters are more sensitive to morphological differences and may be important for risk stratification and understanding the long-term integrity of the different RVOT morphologies.

One of the major problems in patients with repaired Tetralogy of Fallot (rTOF) is pulmonary regurgitation (PR), resulting in right ventricular (RV) dysfunction, RV dilatation, and ventricular arrhythmias. In patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot who develop significant pulmonary valve dysfunction, percutaneous pulmonary valve implantation (PPVI) has become the preferred treatment when anatomical conditions are suitable, as recommended by current guidelines. However, PPVI, both balloon-expandable and self-expandable valves, has limited valve sizes, and it may not be suitable in some RVOT morphologies. Progressive RVOT dilatation in repaired TOF patients correlates with larger RV volumes and increased PR severity. In addition, RVOT morphology and the dimensions of the pulmonary artery branches have been linked to RV remodeling and exercise capacity in patients who underwent non-conduit repair [1, 2].

The four-dimensional (4D) flow cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) studies in repaired TOF patients have revealed multiple abnormal flow patterns, such as helical or vortical flow in the MPA. It can accurately quantify flow-related parameters such as wall shear stress (WSS), viscous energy loss (EL), relative pressure, peak velocity, and pulse wave velocity. These parameters provide a valuable complement to conventional CMR, enhancing visualization of complex congenital heart anomalies [3–5] Hemodynamic findings based on 4D flow CMR studies may serve as early indicators of pathological flow, preceding the onset of overt RV dilatation or dysfunction [4, 5].

The purpose of this study is to describe the characteristic flow profiles associated with each RVOT morphology in patients with rTOF using 4D-flow MRI.

Method

This retrospective study was conducted at Siriraj Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand. The medical records, conventional CMR, and 4D-flow imaging were reviewed. We included patients who underwent CMR with 4D-flow studies between 2017 and 2023. Patients were excluded if they had more than moderate pulmonary stenosis, incomplete imaging, insufficient follow-up data, or associated myocardial diseases. An arrhythmia event was defined as the documented occurrence of atrial arrhythmia—including atrial tachycardia, atrial flutter, atrial fibrillation—or ventricular tachycardia, as recorded on previous electrocardiograms, Holter monitoring, or medical records. The indication for pulmonary valve replacement was classified according to 2020 ESC Guidelines for the management of adult congenital heart disease [6].

This study was approved by the institutional review board, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

CMR and 4D-Flow MRI Protocol

All CMR studies were performed using a 3-T whole-body scanner (Ingenia or Intera Achieva; Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands) following a standardized protocol. Cine images in short-axis, two-chamber, three-chamber, and four-chamber views were acquired during end-expiratory breath-holds to evaluate ventricular function. On the short-axis cine images, biventricular end-diastolic volume (EDV), end-systolic volume (ESV), and ejection fraction (EF) were measured using IntelliSpace Portal version 9 (Philips Healthcare). All 4D-flow acquisitions were performed after contrast administration as part of the clinical CMR protocol.

The phase contrast images using a semi-automatic vessel edge detection algorithm with operator correction were used to determine 2D-pulmonary flow and velocity. Regurgitant fraction was calculated as the percent of backward flow over forward flow.

Acquisition of CMRA sequences was three-dimensional and obtained in the coronal plane covering the chest, the neck, and the upper abdomen. Either a standard three-dimensional (3D) CMRA sequence or the mDixon sequence with respiratory and ECG-gating for steady-state CMRA was performed in every patient. The MRA used standard and mDixon sequences to classify the RVOT morphology. RVOT can be classified into five types: pyramidal with a wide proximal and narrow distal shape, inverted pyramid with narrow proximally and wide distally, tubular with a uniform diameter, trapezoid with a wider distal than proximal diameter, and concave with narrow centrally and wide proximal and distal [7, 8].

Per recommendations on 4D Flow cardiovascular magnetic resonance consensus statement, the 4D-flow sequences with echo-planar imaging were performed with the following settings: TE/TR 2.2 ms/3.7 ms, flip angle 6°, reconstructed spatial resolution 3 × 3 × 3 mm3, VENC 150 cm/s, 30 frames/cycle, and retrospective ECG gating [9, 10]. A flip angle of 6° was used according to the institutional protocol at the time of acquisition. The commercial CMR software (cvi42; Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc.) was used for the post-processing analysis. The pulmonary vasculature was segmented into the main pulmonary artery (MPA), right pulmonary artery (RPA), and left pulmonary artery (LPA). Six analysis planes were placed along the pulmonary arteries at the following levels: pre-pulmonary valve, pulmonary valve, mid-pulmonary artery, pre-bifurcation, and 1 cm into both the RPA and LPA (Fig. 1). The following hemodynamic parameters were obtained at each plane: pulse wave velocity (m/s); wall shear stress (WSS), including maximum and average values for axial, circumferential, and magnitude components relative pressure (mmHg), using the RVOT as a reference point; and energy loss (mW), also referenced to the RVOT (Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). To compare 4D-flow parameters among the three RVOT morphology groups, Kruskal-Wallis tests were performed. Pairwise comparisons were conducted for individual group differences. Group comparisons for normally distributed data were conducted using Student’s t test, and for comparisons involving more than two groups, the one-way ANOVA test was employed. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to evaluate the relationship between the variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

We included 120 patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) who underwent cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging with 4D-flow analysis. Right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) morphology was classified into tubular and pyramidal types based on multiplanar CMR images. Of the 120 patients, 86 (71.7%) had a tubular RVOT, 22 (18.3%) had a pyramidal RVOT, and 12 (10.0%) had a trapezoid RVOT. After excluding 6 patients with moderate to severe pulmonary stenosis, 80 patients in the tubular group were included in the final analysis; no patients exhibited inverted pyramidal or concave morphologies (Fig. 3). Because the trapezoid, inverted pyramidal, and concave groups contained only a small number of patients, our analysis focused on the two predominant RVOT morphologies: tubular and pyramidal. There were no significant differences in patient demographics, including age at repair, age at CMR, or history of transannular patch placement (Table 1). Likewise, the incidence of arrhythmia and the need for pulmonic valve replacement did not differ between the two groups.

According to conventional CMR measures, there were no differences in LV volumes or systolic function between the tubular and pyramidal RVOT groups. Similarly, the PV annulus size, RV volumes, and PR severity were not significantly higher in the pyramidal group.

The 4D-flow hemodynamic parameters, including average and maximal magnitude as well as average and maximal axial and circumferential WSS, energy loss (EL), relative pressure, peak velocity, and pulse wave velocity in each RVOT morphology group, are demonstrated in Table 2.

Overall, the tubular RVOT group demonstrated a higher hemodynamic profile, characterized by increased peak velocity at the RVOT level, higher wall shear stress at the pulmonic valve and main pulmonary artery, and greater energy loss at the pulmonic valve. In contrast, the pyramidal group demonstrated relatively higher flow velocity and axial wall shear stress in the right pulmonary artery, suggesting differences in downstream flow distribution. Furthermore, the tubular group had a greater average EL at the PV level. No significant correlation was observed between the average EL and RV or LV volumes (EDV or ESV).

Discussion

Consistent with previous studies, the tubular-shaped RVOT was the most common morphology in our population. In contrast, we found pyramidal shaped RVOT more frequent than the trapezoid-shaped RVOT [2]. Although several studies have examined RVOT morphology in patients with TOF who underwent complete repair using either valve-sparing or transannular patch techniques, none have explained the contributing factors behind the variation in RVOT morphology among patients. For surgical pulmonic valve replacement (SPVR), surgeons can implant a new pulmonic valve regardless of RVOT size or morphology. However, RVOT morphology becomes an important consideration when patients require percutaneous pulmonary valve implantation (PPVI), as PPVI remains limited by RVOT size and shape. Assessment of RVOT anatomy using either computed tomography angiography (CTA) or CMR should be used to determine patient eligibility for PPVI [7]. The landing zone in a pyramidal-shaped RVOT is typically shorter than in a tubular-shaped RVOT, which makes PPVI with balloon-expandable valves more challenging. However, the tubular-shaped RVOT may also be unsuitable if it becomes too large. Therefore, when patients meet the indication for pulmonic valve replacement (PVR) and prefer PPVI, the procedure should be performed before the RVOT becomes excessively dilated. Previous studies have reported that rTOF patients with a tubular RVOT are the most common subgroup and are generally suitable candidates for PPVI [7, 11]. However, in our population, the proportion of patients with a tubular RVOT who underwent PPVI did not differ significantly from those with a pyramidal RVOT. While RVOT morphology may influence procedural planning for PPVI, device selection depends on detailed anatomical assessment and cannot be determined solely from morphology classification.

For a better understanding of hemodynamics in different RVOT morphologies, we are the first to report the impact of RVOT morphology on 4D-flow CMR hemodynamic parameters in this population. Our study focused on the two most common RVOT morphologies in patients with rTOF. The study revealed that RVOT morphology significantly influences various aspects of pulmonary flow dynamics, particularly WSS, peak velocities, and energy loss across the pulmonic valve and pulmonary arteries. In our report, patients with tubular RVOT morphology exhibited higher peak velocity in both the RVOT and MPA, along with elevated maximum WSS, indicating more streamlined and forceful flow through a narrower outflow tract. As already known, increased WSS has been implicated in endothelial injury, vascular remodeling, aneurysm development, and altered pulmonary artery hemodynamics [11]. On the other hand, the pyramidal group demonstrated lower WSS and energy loss at the pulmonic valve, suggesting a more distributed flow pattern that may be less hemodynamically burdensome.

Lee et al. reported dilated aorta with secondarily decreased WSS in patients with rTOF [12]. To correlate with the clinical outcomes, the abnormal aortic flow patterns have been studied in patients with a bicuspid aortic valve, showing that higher WSS and WSS angle were related to more rapid aortic growth [13, 14]. RVOT size in rTOF patients has been found to increase over time based on conventional and serial CMR assessments. Factors associated with the rate of RVOT enlargement included a larger RV and more severe PR [1]. However, there is no evidence indicating whether these factors are a cause or a consequence of RVOT enlargement. Based on the association of WSS and aortic growth, we postulated that high WSS in tubular-shaped RVOTs may lead to more rapid RVOT dilatation. However, no studies have examined the impact of WSS on the rate of dilatation in the RVOT. While increased wall shear stress has been associated with vascular remodeling in other cardiovascular conditions, such as bicuspid aortic valve disease, the implications of these findings in repaired tetralogy of Fallot remain uncertain. In the present study, the observed differences in wall shear stress and energy loss should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require validation in longitudinal studies.

Interestingly, we found differences in RPA flow among RVOT morphologies. The pyramidal group showed higher peak velocity and axial WSS in the RPA. These findings suggest that RVOT morphology may influence flow dynamics not only at the level of the RVOT but also in the downstream pulmonary artery branches. The pyramidal shape may alter the angulation of the pulmonary arteries, particularly affecting the RPA. The observed differences in 4D-flow parameters, especially between the tubular and pyramidal groups, highlight the potential role of RVOT anatomical remodeling in long-term postoperative hemodynamics.

Previous studies have demonstrated associations between RVOT dilatation and abnormal flow patterns with adverse clinical outcomes such as RV dysfunction and arrhythmia [6, 15]. Elsayed et al. reviewed the potential and clinical applications of 4D-CMR in rTOF assessment [16]. Compared to the healthy volunteers, patients with rTOF had greater SVC/IVC and higher vortical flow patterns in the RA and in the RV in the diastolic phase, and increased vortical flow features in the pulmonary arteries [17]. This study contributed to a better understanding of the interactions among valvular regurgitation, including pulmonic and tricuspid valves, pulmonary artery stiffening, and right heart function. The Fredriksson el at. demonstrated that total RV total kinetic energy loss was the strongest potential predictor for risk stratification of RV dilatation and regurgitant fraction in adults with rTOF [18].

Importantly, the absence of differences in conventional CMR parameters—including ventricular volumes, systolic function, and pulmonary regurgitation severity—despite significant differences in 4D-flow-derived metrics highlights a key finding of this study. These results suggest that advanced flow parameters may detect subclinical hemodynamic alterations that are not captured by standard imaging.

RVOT geometry, including segmental diameters and length, is likely to influence flow dynamics. However, these measurements were not consistently available in this retrospective cohort and were therefore not included in the analysis. Future studies should integrate detailed geometric assessment of the RVOT with 4D-flow parameters to better characterize the interaction between anatomy and hemodynamics.

Similarly, comprehensive measurements of pulmonary artery dimensions were not uniformly available. Given the observed differences in downstream flow patterns, particularly in the right pulmonary artery, future studies incorporating pulmonary artery size and geometry are warranted.

This dissociation supports the concept that morphology-dependent flow abnormalities may precede overt structural remodeling. Therefore, 4D-flow metrics such as wall shear stress and energy loss may represent early physiological markers rather than direct indicators of established disease severity. The present findings should be interpreted as mechanistic and hypothesis-generating rather than directly predictive of clinical outcomes.

Although our study did not evaluate direct correlations between 4D-flow parameters and clinical endpoints, the detailed hemodynamic profiling provided here may serve as a foundation for future prognostic studies.

Unfortunately, due to the small sample size and high intra-group variability, there were no statistically significant differences compared to the trapezoid and concave morphologies in our study.

Limitations

Given the small number of patients with trapezoid-, inverted pyramidal-, and concave- RVOT morphologies, we were unable to analyze detailed hemodynamic differences in these groups. This study did not include a healthy control group or post-pulmonary valve replacement cohort. Comparative analysis with these populations would help contextualize the observed hemodynamic patterns and determine their clinical relevance. As this was a retrospective study, follow-up data were not available to assess changes in 4D-flow CMR parameters over time. The prospective and follow-up assessment using 4D flow CMR in rTOF patients may aid better understanding of disease mechanisms.

Conclusion

RVOT morphology is associated with distinct differences in pulmonary flow dynamics as assessed by 4D-flow CMR. These differences occur despite similar conventional CMR parameters and clinical characteristics, suggesting that advanced flow metrics may reveal subclinical physiological alterations. The clinical implications of these findings remain to be determined and require validation in prospective studies incorporating functional and outcome-based measures.
