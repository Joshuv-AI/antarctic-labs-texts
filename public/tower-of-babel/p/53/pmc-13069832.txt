Uncovering the relationship between the human ocular surface microbiome and gut microbiome
Authors: Nina Eldridge, Livia Spörri, Marco Kreuzer, Gianna Haldimann, Martin S Zinkernagel, Denise C Zysset-Burri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe human body harbors diverse microbial communities that play essential roles in health and disease. While the gut microbiome (GM) has been extensively studied and linked to numerous systemic conditions, the ocular surface microbiome (OSM) remains less well understood due to its low microbial biomass and technical challenges in sequencing. Previous work has suggested possible connections between gut dysbiosis and ocular disease, but whether the OSM and GM are directly related remains unclear.ResultsThis study investigated the relationship between the ocular surface microbiome (OSM) and the gut microbiome (GM) by analysing matched samples from both niches in the same individuals. Using high-throughput sequencing, we characterised microbial composition and diversity at each niche, followed by statistical analysis to show common taxa. A Mantel test was applied to assess potential correlations between the two microbial communities. Our results revealed no significant correlation between the composition of the OSM and GM, neither in structure nor in diversity.ConclusionThis study suggests that the two niches are shaped independently, likely due to their distinct environmental conditions and microbial densities. These findings highlight the uniqueness of each microbiome and underscore the need for niche specific approaches when investigating host microbiome interactions.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, TRN: NCT02438111, Registration date: 28 April 2015, and TRN: NCT 04658238, Registration date: 01 December 2020.Keywords: Ocular surface microbiome, Gut microbiome, Whole metagenome shotgun sequencing

Background

The ocular surface microbiome (OSM) refers to the community of microorganisms and their genomes that inhabit the surface structures of the eye, including the lid, cornea and conjunctiva [1–6]. Although initially considered a sterile environment due to its constant exposure to antimicrobial components in tears and blinking, recent advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have revealed a resident microbiome even in this low biomass site [7–11]. The OSM contributes to ocular surface health and disease, playing roles in local immune regulation, competitive inhibition of pathogens and maintenance of epithelial barrier integrity [12–16]. Studies suggest that the composition of the OSM is influenced by both host and environmental factors [17–20]. Disruptions in this microbial balance (dysbiosis) have been associated with ocular diseases [17–19]. However, OSM research remains in its early stages in terms of stability, interindividual variability and potential for therapeutic treatment [1, 11, 21–23].

In contrast, the gut microbiome (GM) is one of the most densely populated and metabolically active microbial ecosystems in the human body, consisting of up to 100 trillion microorganisms [16, 24, 25]. This complex and dynamic community plays a central role in host physiology, including digestion, energy metabolism, immune system development and the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis [16, 26]. Characterised primarily through 16S rRNA gene sequencing and whole metagenome shotgun sequencing, the GM shows interindividual variation and is shaped by factors such as diet, age, genetics, medication and environment [24, 27, 28]. Alterations in gut microbial composition have been linked to a wide range of diseases, including metabolic disorders, inflammatory bowel disease, neurodegenerative conditions and cardiovascular disease [20, 29, 30]. More recently, the gut eye axis has gained attention, as studies have suggested that dysbiosis in the gut may influence autoimmune response in the eye [31–38].

To date, studies of the OSM and the GM have been conducted independently. In this study, samples from both the ocular surface and the gut were collected from each participant, providing a unique opportunity to investigate potential correlations between these two distinct microbial niches. Although this approach allows to better understand how different microbiome niches interact with each other, the analysis poses significant challenges. Since the gut is a high-biomass, anaerobic environment with a densely populated and metabolically rich microbial community, current sequencing technologies may be applied to characterise its microbiome [11, 16, 39–41]. In contrast, the ocular surface represents a low biomass niche with constant exposure to air, light and antimicrobial tear components, which results in sparse microbial colonisation and makes sequencing more technically demanding due to the risk of contamination and low DNA yields [11, 42–44]. These fundamental differences in microbial density and environmental conditions must be considered in both, the interpretation of results and the optimisation of analytical methods.

The aim of this retrospective study was to investigate whether there is a correlation between the microbial communities of the ocular surface and the gut on a community level and on an individual basis. By comparing the composition and diversity of microbiomes from both niches, we determined whether patterns or shared features exist despite the differences in their environments. Identifying such correlations could reveal common factors shaping microbiome composition across body sites. Ultimately, these findings may contribute to a better understanding of microbiome interactions and open new possibilities for diagnostics or therapeutic strategies targeting microbiome related diseases.

Methods

Study design and recruitment

This research involved a retrospective analysis of microbiome samples from a cohort of 51 participants whose samples were originally collected as part of Spörri et al. [45] and another unpublished study of our Department. The studies were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Canton of Bern (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02438111 and NCT04658238). Secondary use of the samples for this analysis was also approved under the same ethical protocol. Participants were recruited from the Department of Ophthalmology of the University Hospital Bern (Inselspital), Switzerland. Study procedures adhered to the CONSORT guidelines as well as to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects, as established by the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after they were provided with both oral and written explanations of the study. Individuals were excluded if they had undergone ocular surgery, taken regular medication, had a systemic disease or had received systemic or topical antibiotics within the preceding three months. To be included in the study, participants had to meet the following criteria: willingness and ability to provide written informed consent, age of 18 years or older, being non-smoker, not wearing contact lenses and no history of ocular surgery in the preceding three months.

Sample collection

After written informed consent was given and no exclusion criteria were met, a total of 51 participants were included in the study (M: 22 (43.1%), F: 29 (56.9%); Mean age 73.04, s.d. ±9.45). To avoid temporal variation in samples, sampling was conducted at the same time of the day and during the same season. After applying a local anaesthetic (Tetracaine 1%, Théa, Schaffhausen, Switzerland), conjunctival swabs using flocked nylon swabs (FLOQSwabs #518CS01, Copan, Brescia, Italy) were collected.

Stool samples were collected by the study participants, immediately cooled, and transported to the research facility within 16 h after defecation. Upon arrival, samples were promptly frozen at − 20 °C until further analysis.

DNA isolation and sequencing

The ocular surface swabs were processed, including host-DNA depletion, and DNA was isolated on the same day using the QIAamp DNA Microbiome Kit (51704) from QIAGEN (Hilden, Germany). Until further processed, DNA samples were stored at -20 °C. Library preparation was carried out using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (SKU E7645L). Dual-indexed libraries were subsequently sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system using 150 bp paired-end reads. Whole metagenome shotgun sequencing was conducted at the Next-Generation Sequencing Platform, University of Bern, Switzerland as described in Spörri et al. [45].

Stool samples were processed and DNA isolated using the PSP® Spin Stool DNA Plus kit (Stratec Biomedical AG, Beringen, Switzerland). Until further processed, DNA samples were stored at -20 °C. The TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Library Preparation kit was used for library preparation and whole metagenome shotgun sequencing was done on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system by the Next-Generation Sequencing Platform of the University of Bern, Switzerland, as described in Morandi et al. [32].

Taxonomic annotation

DNA sequences were generated containing Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMIs; [46]) for OSM samples and not containing UMIs for GM samples. UMIs are specifically applied to mitigate PCR amplification bias and improve quantification accuracy in low-biomass microbiome samples. These UMIs were initially extracted using UMI-tools (v.1.1.4.). Following extraction, read quality filtering was performed with fastp (v.0.20.1; [47]). To remove host-derived sequences, the filtered reads were aligned to the human genome reference GRCh38. Reads that did not map to the host genome were isolated using SAMtools (v.1.10). The non-host reads were subsequently aligned to the ChocoPhlAn reference database (version mpa_vOct22_CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202212) using bowtie2 (v. 2.3.4.1; [48]). The resulting SAM alignment files were converted into BAM format, sorted, and indexed using SAMtools (v. 1.10; [49]). These sorted BAM files of OSM samples were then deduplicated based on both UMI and alignment position using UMI-tools (v. 1.1.4). The GM BAM files were not deduplicated. The deduplicated BAM file of the OSM and the sorted BAM file of the GM served as input for the MetaPhlAn4 pipeline (v. 4.0.4; [50, 51]) for taxonomic profiling.

Statistical analysis

The bioinformatic pipeline used for this project is summarised in Fig. 1. All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (v. 4.2.2). All analyses were done on the phylum and genus level. To visualise shared microbial taxa between the two sampled niches, a Venn diagram was generated using the ggplot2 package (v. 3.5.0; Fig. 2A-B). The distribution of shared taxa between the two niches and across participants was further illustrated using an UpSet plot (Fig. 2C-D). In addition, a barplot was created to quantify the number of shared taxa for each individual (Fig. 3A). To assess within sample microbial diversity, the Shannon diversity index was calculated using the vegan R package (v. 2.6.4) and visualised as boxplots for the two niches with ggplot2 (Fig. 3B).

Results

Read statistics

Sequencing libraries from negative controls did not pass quality and concentration requirements, suggesting that contaminating DNA, if present, was below detection limit. All samples from both microbiomes passed standard quality control checks using FastQC.

In total, 6.38 billion 150 bp paired-end reads were generated from the ocular surface samples with a median read depth of 132.8 million per sample. Following adapter trimming and host read removal, approximately 653 million high-quality (10.24%), non-human reads remained for the ocular surface samples, averaging 12.79 ± 7.92 (s.d.) million reads per sample. As reported in previous studies as well as expected due to low microbial biomass, the majority of reads originated from the host [42]. Subsequent deduplication using (UMIs) resulted in a total of 1.23 million unique reads available for downstream analysis, with a mean of 24’000 ± 34’000 (s.d.) reads per sample.

A total of 3.69 billion paired-end reads were generated from the gut samples with a median read depth of 66.1 million per sample. After adapter trimming and host removal, 3.56 billion high-quality (96.48%), non-human reads remained for the gut samples, with an average of 67.23 ± 24.86 (s.d.) million reads per sample, for downstream analysis.

Common taxa

A total of 19 phyla and 939 genera were identified across all samples. Among them, 5 phyla and 43 genera were shared between the two niches, 4 phyla and 54 genera were unique to the ocular surface and 10 phyla and 842 genera to the gut (Fig. 2A). The UpSet plot of the phyla across both niches (Fig. 2C) showed that Actinobacteria was the most prevalent phylum, detected in 49 out of 51 individuals. Firmicutes and Proteobacteria were present in 40 and 39 individuals, respectively. The UpSet plot reveals that the combination of the three most prevalent phyla was found in 25 individuals, almost half of the cohort.

The UpSet plot with the most prevalent genera (Fig. 2D), showed that Escherichia was present in 12 and Pseudomonas in 11 out of 51 individuals. Furthermore, the combination of the two was found in 6 individuals. The results showed that there was no single shared taxa found in all individuals.

A bar plot illustrating the number of taxa found in both niches per individual (Fig. 3A) showed that on phylum level, most participants had three taxa in common between the two niches, while only one shared none. Notably, no individual had all five shared phyla, showing a limited overlap between the two niches. A strong shift in shared taxa was seen on genus level, with 31 individuals having no common genera between the two niches. Only 3 individuals had 3 common genera and only one had 4 common genera.

To assess differences in microbial diversity between the two niches, the Shannon diversity index was calculated for each sample. Boxplots (Fig. 3B) showed that the GM exhibited a higher median Shannon index compared to the OSM on both, phylum and genus level. A paired t-test confirmed this difference to be statistically significant for both, phylum (p < 0.001) and genus (p < 0.00001).

Non-metric multidimensional scaling

The NMDS of the microbial communities from both niches revealed distinct clustering patterns for the two niches (Fig. 4). Samples from the gut clustered more homogenously than samples from the ocular surface. The analysis yielded a stress value of 0.09.

Bray-curtis dissimilarity matrix and mantel test

The Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix generated for the gut samples showed a homogenous distribution of values (Fig. 5A). The ocular surface matrix revealed a broad range of dissimilarity values, indicating that while some samples were highly similar, others were very different (Fig. 5B). The high dissimilarity in most of the samples reflect a high inter-individual variability. The ocular surface matrix displayed a rather scattered pattern with limited clustering. When comparing the dissimilarity patterns between the two niches, no consistent overlap in clustering was observed. The Mantel test resulted in a correlation coefficient r of − 0.12 and a p-value of 0.97.

Ocular- and enterotypes

PAM clustering based on the Jensen–Shannon distance revealed that the optimal number of clusters was k = 3 for both OSM and GM samples (Fig. 6A1, A2), as determined by the Calinski–Harabasz index. The BCA plots illustrated separated clusters in the gut dataset (enterotypes), with cluster 1 comprising 31 individuals, cluster 2 seven individuals, and cluster 3 thirteen individuals (Fig. 6B1). In contrast, the ocular surface clusters appeared less distinct, with cluster 1 containing thirteen individuals, cluster 2 containing 32 and cluster 3 six individuals (Fig. 6B2). Silhouette scores were calculated for each point and averaged per cluster. Scores for the gut clusters were 0.23 for cluster 1, 0.44 for cluster 2 and 0.11 for cluster 3. Slightly higher scores were obtained for the ocular surface clusters with 0.54 for cluster 1, 0.26 for cluster 2 and 0.49 for cluster 3.

Taxonomic profiling revealed dominant genus within each cluster: Alistipes in gut cluster 1, Prevotella in cluster 2 and Bacteroides in cluster 3 (Fig. 6C1-3). In the ocular surface, cluster 1 showed a higher abundance of Cutibacterium, cluster 2 Limosilactobacillus and cluster 3 Corynebacterium (Fig. 6D1-3), which we refer to here as oculartypes, by analogy to gut enterotypes. However, no consistent pattern of sample clustering between the two niches was observed, indicating that clustering of individuals in one niche did not correspond to clustering in the other.

Discussion

This study highlights a big difference in microbial abundance between the OSM and GM. As expected, the gut exhibited a far greater microbial richness and diversity [10], while only a limited number of taxa were found to be shared between the two niches. This discrepancy became even more pronounced at genus level. This was further emphasised by individual level comparisons with bar plots (Fig. 3A), which revealed that over half of the participants had no genera in common between their gut and ocular samples. The UpSet plot (Fig. 2C) indicated that the phylum Actinobacteria was present in nearly all individuals, which is in accordance with previous studies, which have found Actinobacteria to be pivotal in the maintenance of gut homeostasis [6, 53, 54]. However, this apparent overlap largely diminished if assessed at higher taxonomic resolution. At genus level, the most prevalent shared genus Escherichia was found in only a quarter of the participants (Fig. 2D), underscoring the limited microbial overlap across these anatomically and functionally distinct niches.

The limited overlap between the ocular surface and gut microbiomes can be further understood in the context of ocular surface biology. The tear film contains antimicrobial peptides, lysozyme, and immunoglobulins that actively suppress microbial colonisation. Physical factors, such as blinking, tear flow, and the small surface area of the eye, further limit bacterial colonisation and persistence [5]. In addition, the ocular surface represents a largely aerobic environment, whereas the gut is predominantly anaerobic, which constrains the types of microbes that can survive in each niche and further contributes to their limited overlap. Together, these biological features create a selective and constrained environment, which likely contributes to the distinct composition and low diversity of the OSM relative to the gut.

The comparison of alpha diversity between the OSM and GM revealed significant differences at both, phylum and genus levels, with more pronounced differences at genus level (Fig. 3B). This highlights not only the distinct microbial ecosystems that characterise each niche but also the considerable variability in microbial richness and evenness. Given the limited number of taxa shared between the two niches (Fig. 2A), especially at higher taxonomic resolution, a direct comparison or attempt to correlate the two microbiomes is challenging. Previous studies have tried to define and determine a stable microbial core, but neither for the OSM nor the GM has one been consistently identified [55]. The lack thereof further complicates efforts to explore inter niche microbial relationships.

The NMDS plot (Fig. 4) revealed a clustering of samples according to their niche, emphasising the substantial differences in microbial community composition between the two niches. With a stress value of 0.09, the NMDS provided a reliable two-dimensional representation of the high-dimensional data, reinforcing the assumption that the two microbial environments have minimal overlap.

The Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices provided further insight into intra niche variability (Fig. 5). For the ocular surface, we observed a broad range of dissimilarity values, suggesting that while some samples are relatively similar, others are highly divergent. A few samples showed slight clustering, indicating high similarity within these samples. In contrast, the gut samples exhibited consistently high dissimilarity values, highlighting the pronounced inter-individual variability of the GM [6]. No apparent clustering was observed among gut samples. Given the absence of clustering in the gut, it was not feasible to directly compare cluster structures between the two niches. This was further assessed by the Mantel test, which yielded a correlation coefficient (r) of -0.12 and a p-value of 0.9, indicating no detectable correlation between the dissimilarity matrices of the OSM and GM with the current methodology. Previous studies on the GM have identified distinct clusters of individuals characterised by driving genera, commonly referred to as enterotypes [52]. To the best of our knowledge, this classification has not yet been applied to the OSM so far. In this study, we conducted a clustering analysis separately for each niche (Fig. 6), as the minimal overlap between their microbial communities made a combined approach unfeasible. Using the Calinski-Harabasz index, we determined that three clusters represented the optimal grouping for both the OSM and GM. The driving genera of the gut clusters—Alistipes, Prevotella and Bacteroides—are consistent with previously described enterotypes. In contrast, for the OSM, this is the first indication that three distinct oculartypes may exist, characterised by Cutibacterium, Limosilactobacillus and Corynebacterium. While the silhouette scores were moderate, they support the presence of meaningful clustering in both niches. It should be noted that in high-dimensional microbiome data, silhouette scores are generally lower due to the inherent sparsity and complexity of community composition. No association in metadata such as sex or age was found between the cluster distributions of matched samples across the two niches, further supporting their independent microbial profiles. However, the entero- and oculartyping should be considered exploratory, as they are sensitive to sequencing depth, distance metrics, and clustering algorithms.

Given the low microbial biomass of the ocular surface, the absence of negative controls represents a methodological limitation of this study. In addition, different library preparation strategies were applied to the two niches, including the use of Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMIs) for ocular surface samples but not for gut samples. This reflects the substantially lower biomass and higher susceptibility to amplification bias in the ocular surface microbiome. However, as our primary objective was to investigate relative community patterns and distance relationships rather than absolute taxonomic composition, the consistent niche-specific separation observed across multiple analytical approaches suggests that the main findings are unlikely to be driven by technical artefacts. Taken together, these methodological considerations should be taken into account when interpreting the results but do not invalidate the central conclusion of niche-specific and largely independent microbial profiles.

Conclusions

This study presents a comprehensive comparison of the OSM and GM within the same individuals, offering a novel view into cross-niche microbial relationships. Our analyses showed that the two niches are compositionally distinct, with minimal taxonomic overlap and no detectable correlation in microbial structure. While three potential microbial clusters were identified in each niche, their distributions were independent, underscoring the uniqueness of each microbial environment. The low microbial biomass of the ocular surface poses significant technical challenges for sequencing, potentially limiting the detection of rare taxa and complicating direct comparisons with the highly abundant and diverse GM. These methodological constraints may partly account for the missing correlation. Overall, our findings emphasise that host microbiome interactions are likely niche specific and highlight the need for optimised approaches tailored to low biomass environments like the ocular surface.
