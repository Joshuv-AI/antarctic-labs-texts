Decreased PU.1 expression in mature B cells induces lymphomagenesis
Authors: Shinya Endo, Nao Nishimura, Kosuke Toyoda, Yoshihiro Komohara, Joaquim Carreras, Hiromichi Yuki, Takafumi Shichijo, Shikiko Ueno, Niina Ueno, Shinya Hirata, Yawara Kawano, Kisato Nosaka, Masashi Miyaoka, Naoya Nakamura, Ai Sato, Kiyoshi Ando, Hiroaki Mitsuya, Koichi Akashi, Daniel G. Tenen, Jun‐ichirou Yasunaga, Masao Matsuoka, Yutaka Okuno, Hiro Tatetsu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common subtype of lymphoma, accounting for 30% of non‐Hodgkin lymphomas. Although comprehensive analysis of genetic abnormalities has led to the classification of lymphomas, the exact mechanism of lymphomagenesis remains elusive. The Ets family transcription factor, PU.1, encoded by Spi1, is essential for the development of myeloid and lymphoid cells. Our previous research illustrated the tumor suppressor function of PU.1 in classical Hodgkin lymphoma and myeloma cells. In the current study, we found that patients with DLBCL exhibited notably reduced PU.1 expression in their lymphoma cells, particularly in the non‐germinal center B‐cell‐like (GCB) subtype. This observation suggests that downregulation of PU.1 may be implicated in DLBCL tumor growth. To further assess PU.1's role in mature B cells in vivo, we generated conditional Spi1 knockout mice using Cγ1‐Cre mice. Remarkably, 13 of the 23 knockout mice (56%) showed splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, or masses, with some having histologically confirmed B‐cell lymphomas. In contrast, no wild‐type mice developed B‐cell lymphoma. In addition, RNA‐seq analysis of lymphoma cells from Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice showed high frequency of each monoclonal CDR3 sequence, indicating that these lymphoma cells were monoclonal tumor cells. When these B lymphoma cells were transplanted into immunodeficient recipient mice, all mice died within 3 weeks. Lentiviral‐transduced Spi1 rescued 60% of the recipient mice, suggesting that PU.1 has a tumor suppressor function in vivo. Collectively, PU.1 is a tumor suppressor in mature B cells, and decreased PU.1 results in mature B‐cell lymphoma development.

INTRODUCTION

Diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common subtype of lymphoma, accounting for 30% of non‐Hodgkin's lymphoma. 1 Although the anti‐CD20 antibody rituximab dramatically improves the prognosis of patients with DLBCL treated with a combination of chemotherapeutic agents, a subset of patients may benefit from more effective novel molecular‐targeted therapies to achieve a cure. 2 , 3

Although DLBCL is heterogeneous, it can be classified into two distinct molecular subtypes based on the cell of origin (COO): the germinal center B‐cell‐like (GCB) subtype and the activated B‐cell‐like (ABC) subtype. The remaining 10%–15% of cases are unclassifiable. 4 , 5 Patients with the ABC subtype of DLBCL generally have worse prognosis than those with the GCB subtype. 1 , 6 , 7 Owing to the challenges associated with using gene expression profiling in clinical practice, immunohistochemistry‐based algorithms, such as the Hans algorithm, are utilized to categorize cases as either GCB or non‐GCB. 8 , 9 Recent comprehensive studies on molecular aberrations, including gene mutations and copy number alterations, have led to the proposal of new subtype‐specific gene clusters for DLBCL. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Despite these advances, the mechanisms underlying lymphomagenesis remain elusive.

PU.1, encoded by Spi1, is an ETS transcription factor essential for the development of myeloid and lymphoid cells. 16 The conventional knockout of Spi1 results in defects in granulocytes, monocytes/macrophages, and B cells, leading to embryonic lethality or death soon after birth in mice. 17 , 18 In myeloid cells, PU.1 activates genes, such as M‐csf, M‐csfr, Gm‐csfr, and Cd11b, whereas in lymphoid cells, it transactivates Il‐7r, immunoglobulin, and Cd19. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 The PU.1 expression requires an upper regulatory element (URE) located at −17 kb in humans and at −14 kb in mice of the transcription start site (TSS) of Spi1 in addition to the Spi1 promoter. 26 , 27 Conditional knockout of URE in mice results in decreased Spi1 expression in 20% of wild‐type mice, leading to the development of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and B lymphocytic leukemia. 28 , 29 URE also contains a suppressor element of Spi1 expression in T cells, and conditional knockout mice develop T‐cell lymphoma. 29 Spi1 was originally identified as an oncogene when it was ectopically expressed in erythroid cells by spleen focus‐forming virus (SFFV), leading to erythroleukemia. 30 , 31 Recently, in humans, translocations involving the Spi1 locus, which result in PU.1 upregulation in T‐cell progenitors, were found in 3.9% of pediatric T‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) cases. 32 Deletion of both Spi1 and SpiB induces pre‐B cell ALL before 30 weeks of age. 33 These data clearly suggest that PU.1 must be expressed in appropriate cell lineages with proper differentiation timing, and failures in such appropriate PU.1 expression result in hematological malignancies.

We previously demonstrated that Spi1 is downregulated in most myeloma cell lines by methylation of its promoter and −17 kb URE. 34 PU.1 is downregulated in primary myeloma cells from patients with multiple myelomas, whereas normal plasma cells express relatively high levels of PU.1. Conditionally expressed PU.1 induces complete growth arrest and apoptosis in myeloma cell lines, suggesting that Spi1 may be a tumor suppressor in myeloma cells in vitro. 35 , 36 Spi1 is also downregulated in classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHD) cell lines by methylation of its promoter and URE, and conditional expression of PU.1 in cHD cells results in G1/G0 cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. 37 These findings suggest that Spi1 may act as a tumor suppressor in mature B‐cell neoplasms, including DLBCL.

To address these questions, we first confirmed that PU.1 expression is notably downregulated in human DLBCL samples, especially in the non‐GCB type. To further validate the significance of decreased PU.1 expression in B cell lymphoma, we generated the conditional knockout of PU.1 in mature B cells under control of Cγ1‐Cre in mice (Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F ). These mice exhibited clonal expansion of mature B cells and demonstrated lymphomagenesis. On transplanting these B lymphoma cells into immunodeficient recipient mice, all recipients succumbed within 3 weeks, suggesting that PU.1 has a tumor suppressor function in vivo. Collectively, PU.1 functions as a tumor suppressor in mature B‐cells, and its reduced expression leads to the development of B‐cell lymphoma.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study approval

The human studies were approved by the Ethics Committee of Kumamoto University (approval genome No. 499 and No. 1174) and Tokai University Hospital (14R‐080). Paraffin blocks of patients with DLBCL were obtained from the Departments of Hematology, Rheumatology, and Infectious Disease, Kumamoto University Hospital and the Department of Hematology‐Oncology, Tokai University School of Medicine. We obtained written informed consent or used an opt‐out option based on the decision of our ethics committee because of anonymized data. All animal procedures were conducted in compliance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kumamoto University (approval A27‐039).

Cell lines

The OCI‐Ly3 and SUDHL4 DLBCL cell lines were kindly provided by Dr. Kazuaki Teshima (Akita University).

Animal model

The Cγ1‐Cre mice (Sv129 background) and Spi1 F/F mice (C57BL/6N background) were kindly provided by Dr. Daniel G. Tenen (Harvard Stem Cell Institute, Harvard Medical School) and Dr. Klaus Rajewsky (Immune Regulation and Cancer, Max‐Delbruck‐Center for Molecular Medicine). Cγ1‐Cre and Spi1 F/F mice were crossbred to produce Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice (mixed background: Sv129 and C57BL/6N). 38 , 39 , 40 All mice were treated in accordance with the Standards for Human Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Kumamoto University School of Medicine and the Guidelines for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments set by the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science, and Technology of Japan. Basically, Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice, aged 8–12 weeks, were immunized to facilitate Cre‐mediated recombination through intradermal injection of NP‐CGG (Chicken Gamma Globulin) combined with Freund's Complete Adjuvant (Sigma‐Aldrich). Subsequently, they were re‐immunized in the same manner at 4 and 8 weeks after the first immunization. With respect to organ evaluation in mice, splenomegaly was defined as 20 mm or greater and was not observed in control mice.

Complete blood counts

EDTA‐anticoagulated peripheral blood samples from the mice were analyzed using an Advia 2120i Hematology System (Siemens). Complete blood count results were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Lentivirus production, transduction, and transplantation (Rag2−/−Jak3 −/− BALB/c mice)

The CSII‐EF‐MCS‐IRES‐Venus lentiviral vector and packaging constructs VSV‐G Rev and CAG‐HIVgp were purchased from the RIKEN Bio Resource Center. Murine Spi1 cDNA was subcloned into the NotI site of a CSII‐EF‐MCS‐IRES‐Venus lentiviral vector. Viruses were produced in 293 T cells, and splenocytes or tumor cells from Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice were infected. PU.1 was transduced into cells using a lentiviral vector for 2 days, and the transduced cells were sorted based on Venus expression. The sorted Venus+ cells were injected to immunodeficient recipient mice after 9 Gy irradiation. One million murine splenocytes or tumor cells were intravenously transplanted into 8–12 week‐old Rag2 −/− Jak3 −/− BALB/c mice. 41

Real‐time PCR

Genomic DNA and RNA were extracted from the purified cells using a MasterPure Complete DNA and RNA Purification Kit (Epicentre Biotechnologies). Quantitative TaqMan PCR assays were performed using commercially available assay‐on‐demand probe primer sets for human SPI1 and ACTB/Actb, as described previously. 34

RNA‐sequencing and expression analysis

B220+ cells were purified using B220 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec) from tumor in Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice or splenocytes in Spi1 F/F mice (control) without lymphoma. RNAs were extracted from B220+ cells using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA sequencing was performed by NovoGene. After the FASTQ files were cleaned with Trim Galore, the obtained reads were aligned to the mm10 genome using HISAT2 42 with the following options: ‐‐dta‐cufflinks, ‐‐no‐discordant, ‐‐no‐mixed, and ‐‐rna‐strandness FR. The aligned data were analyzed by samtools. 43 After counting reads in features with htseq‐count of HTSeq, 44 differentially expressed genes were identified if the absolute fold change was greater than 2 and the adjusted P value (padj) was less than 1 × 10e−6 in the DESeq2 analysis. 45 The results were then subjected to over representation analysis (ORA) 46 using clusterProfiler. 47 To identify B‐cell receptor (BCR) clonality, we utilized ImReP, 48 which enables the extraction of BCR reads from RNA‐Seq data and detection of clones with identical complementarity‐determining region 3 (CDR3) amino acid sequences.

Immunohistochemistry

Sections of paraffin blocks of patients with DLBCL were prepared, incubated with antibodies, washed in PBS, and then incubated with a horseradish peroxidase‐labeled secondary antibody (Nichirei). The sections were visualized using a diaminobenzidine substrate system (Nichirei). PU.1 (Clone C3; Santa Cruz) expression was examined by two investigators, and PU.1 positive cases were defined as those in which more than 50% of CD20 positive B cells expressed PU.1. Conventional immunohistochemical analysis using anti‐CD10 (Clone 56C6; Nichirei), anti‐BCL6 (Clone PG‐B6p; Dako), and anti‐MUM1 (Clone MUM1p; Dako) antibodies distinguished GCB‐type DLBCL from non‐GCB‐type DLBCL. 8 Anti‐Iba‐1 antibody (Wako) was used to detect macrophages.

For immunohistochemistry of murine samples, anti‐PU.1 antibody (#230336; Abcam) and anti‐CD45R (clone B220; BD Pharmingen) antibodies were used. For double immunofluorescence, anti‐rat immunoglobulins labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 and rabbit immunoglobulins labeled with Alexa Fluor 546 (Invitrogen) were used as secondary antibodies. For double immunohistochemistry, an anti‐CD19 antibody (Clone LE‐CD19; Dako) was used to detect lymphoma cells and visualized using HistoGreen (Linaris).

Flow cytometry

To assess PU.1 expression levels in human DLBCL cells, mononuclear cells were extracted from the lymph nodes of patients with DLBCL. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from healthy donors. Extraction was performed using Ficoll Paque Plus (GE Healthcare). Cells were fixed and permeabilized using a Transcription Factor Fixation/Permeabilization Buffer Set (BioLegend). Subsequently, the cells were stained with anti‐human CD19 (Clone 7C6B05; BioLegend) and anti‐SPI1 (PU.1) antibodies (Clone 7C6B05; BioLegend).

Microarray data analysis

The GSE42203 database, which is publicly accessible, contains gene expression profiles derived from lymphoma patient samples and cell lines. Gene expression profiles were produced using Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array. Matrix files in series were procured from the Gene Expression Omnibus.

TCGA database analysis

For the analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data, the GEPIA2 web‐based tool was used, as referenced in PMID: 31114875. We compared Spi1 expression in patients by incorporating normal samples from TCGA and Genotype‐Tissue Expression (GTEx). A summary of the tumor‐type abbreviations from TCGA can be found on the following website: https://gdc.cancer.gov/resources‐tcga‐users/tcga‐code‐tables/tcga‐study‐abbreviations.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Excel, EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), and Prism 8 (GraphPad Software). EZR provides a graphical user interface for R, a programming language for statistical computing, and specifically enhances the R commander with additional biostatistics functions. 49

Data deposition

The RNA‐seq data associated with this study can be found in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE117631.

RESULTS

PU.1 is downregulated in the majority of patients with non‐GCB type DLBCL

First, we assessed Spi1 expression in TCGA database via the GEPIA2 (PMID: 31114875). We observed that Spi1 was considerably downregulated in several cancer types, including adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and thymoma (THYM) cancer types (Figures 1A and S1; P < 0.01). Notably, SPI1 expression in DLBCL tissues was significantly lower than that in normal tissues (Figure 1B; P < 0.01). We evaluated Spi1 expression in lymphoma cell lines OCI‐Ly3 (ABC‐type DLBCL) and SUDHL4 (GCB‐type DLBCL). We found that Spi1 expression was lower in DLBCL cell lines than in CD19+ B cells in PBMC from healthy donors (Figure 1C). Flow cytometry was then used to ascertain whether PU.1 protein expression was downregulated. PU.1 expression of OCI‐Ly3 and SUDHL4 was lower than that of U937 (myeloid leukemia cell line: positive control) or CD19 + B cells in PBMC from a healthy donor (Figure 1D,E). Furthermore, we examined the expression level of Spi1 in GCB‐ or ABC‐type DLBCL cell lines using publicly available data to determine which cell line is primarily downregulated (Figures 1F and S2; P < 0.01). Spi1 expression was considerably lower in ABC cells than in GCB cells (GSE42203).

To confirm the expression of PU.1 at the protein level in patient samples, we subsequently conducted immunostaining of lymph node tissues from patients with DLBCL to evaluate PU.1 expression at the protein level. Patients with DLBCL were assigned to GCB‐ or non‐GCB‐type groups based on immunostaining with anti‐CD10, anti‐BCL6, and anti‐MUM1 antibodies. Although the expression of PU.1 in DLBCLs was reduced compared to that in B cells in normal tonsils, PU.1 expression in GCB‐type or non‐GCB‐type tumors varied. Therefore, we classified patients into PU.1 high and low DLBCL groups (Figure 2A). Of 134 patients with DLBCL, 87 and 47 were identified as PU.1‐low/negative and PU.1‐positive, respectively (Figures 2B and S3). Twenty‐one (47.4%) patients with GCB‐type DLBCL were PU.1‐low/negative, while 66 (73.3%) patients with non‐GCB‐type DLBCL were PU.1‐low/‐negative. These results suggested that a significantly higher number of patients with non‐GCB DLBCL exhibited decreased PU.1 expression compared to patients with GCB type DLBCL (P < 0.01). Additionally, using double staining for PU.1 and Iba‐1, we identified tumor‐associated macrophages surrounding the DLBCL cells, indicating that these background macrophages may robustly express PU.1, even in PU.1‐negative samples. This potentially influenced the overall mRNA expression analysis (Figure 2C). We validated PU.1 protein expression levels in sorted primary DLBCL cells using flow cytometry (Figure 2D,E). Although PU.1 protein expression was relatively elevated in CD19+ B cells in PBMC from the three healthy donors, its expression was notably downregulated in sorted non‐GCB‐type DLBCL cells (P = 0.0001). These findings indicated that PU.1 expression is reduced in DLBCL, especially in the non‐GCB type.

Mice with conditionally deleted Spi1 allele in mature B cells suffered from B cell lymphoma

To evaluate whether the downregulation of Spi1 in mature B cells results in B cell lymphoma in vivo, we generated Spi1 F/F mice and Cγ1‐Cre mice (Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F ). 38 In Cγ1‐Cre mice, the expression of Cre recombinase is driven by the Cγ1 enhancer locus after class switch recombination from μ to the γ1 locus (Figure 3A). 39 , 40 We confirmed the conditional deletion of the Spi1 gene in the bone marrow and spleen (Figure 3B). All Cγ1‐Cre mice Spi1 F/F mice showed no signs of morbidity at 18 months. In addition, survival of the Cγ1‐Cre mice Spi1 F/F mice (n = 23) was similar to that of Cγ1‐Cre control mice (n = 26). The overall median survival was 107 weeks versus 98 weeks respectively (Figure 3C; P = 0.83). A complete blood count showed no abnormal white blood cell, lymphocyte, platelet, or hemoglobin levels between 18‐ and 24‐month‐old Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F and Cγ1‐Cre mice (Figure 3D). One of the Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice was sacrificed. Although no splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, or mass formation was observed (Figure 3E), pathological examinations indicated immature mononuclear B cell infiltration in various organs, including the spleen, liver, lung, kidney, and salivary glands, suggesting that loss of PU.1 expression facilitates the emergence of lymphoma (Figure 3F).

We therefore sacrificed 23 of the 18–24‐month‐old Cγ1‐Cre F/F Spi1 F/F and Cγ1‐Cre F/wt Spi1 F/F mice and analyzed their phenotypes. Nine (39%) mice suffered from splenomegaly, seven (30%) from tumors, and 13 (56%) from either splenomegaly or tumors (Figures 4A,B and S4). Additionally, we sacrificed 18–24‐month‐old Spi1 F/F mice and Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 WT/WT mice as controls. Neither the 18–24‐month‐old Spi1 F/F nor Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 WT/WT mice suffered from splenomegaly or tumors. Among them, eight Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice were dissected. Pathologically, the spleen tissue was massively infiltrated with immature mononuclear cells positive for B220, suggesting that these mice had B‐cell lymphoma (Figures 4C and S5). In mice with lymphoma, immature large cells proliferated and were positive for B220; 40% of these cells were Ki67‐positive, suggesting an aggressive phenotype of B‐cell lymphoma, akin to DLBCL in humans (Figure 4D). Immature B220+ cells in Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice were PU.1 negative, suggesting that these tumorigenic cells were derived from Spi1‐deficient mature B cells (Figure 4E).

Gene expression profiling revealed clonal expansion of B cells in Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 knockout mice

To investigate the clonality and gene expression of lymphomas that developed in Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice, we performed RNA‐seq analysis of lymphoma cells from Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice. First, we analyzed immunoglobulin heavy chain sequences to verify monoclonal expansion. The representative tumor in two Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice had high frequency of each monoclonal CDR3 sequence, while no clonality of CDR3 was observed in the splenocytes in two control mice and two Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice without splenomegaly, tumors, or lymphadenopathy. This indicated that these tumor cells were monoclonal B‐cell tumors (Figures 5A and S6), considering a notable majority of Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 knockout mice could possess B cell proliferative disorders.

We subsequently examined the differences in gene expression between B cell lymphoma cells from Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice and splenic B cells from Spi1 F/F mice. Among the genes upregulated in the former group, Ezh2, Aurka, Aurkb, Ccnb1, Chek1, Cdk6, and Mef2b were identified (Figure 5B). We conducted pathway analysis of these upregulated genes using ORA. Notably, genes related to malignant lymphoma were extracted, including “Mantle cell lymphoma,” “Malignant lymphoma, lymphocytic, intermediate differentiation, diffuse” and “High Grade Lymphoma (neoplasm)” (Figure 5C). These findings suggest that the lymphoma cells in Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice were monoclonal aggressive tumor cells.

Spi1 acts as a tumor suppressor for B lymphoma cells in Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice

To confirm the tumorigenicity of the expanded tumor cells in Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice, we performed transplantation of the splenocytes or tumor cells from Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice into immunodeficient mice (Rag2 −/− Jak3 −/− BALB/c) 41 (Figure 6A). All recipient mice died within 1 month and suffered severe hepatosplenomegaly (Figure 6B). Moreover, we observed the infiltration of lymphoma cells into the liver and spleen of these mice (Figure 6C). When serial transplantations were conducted with 1 × 106 splenocytes from P1 mice (Figure 3E,F), recipient mice died within 1 month, indicating that serial transplantation accelerated the disease progression of B cell lymphoma. The mice also showed severe invasion of lymphoma cells into the spleen, liver, and lungs (Figure 6D). A high frequency of each monoclonal CDR3 sequence was confirmed in splenocytes (Figure 5A). These data demonstrate that the lymphoma cells were transplantable malignant cells.

Next, to determine if Spi1 acts as a tumor suppressor in the B lymphoma cells of Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice, we transduced murine Spi1 into B lymphoma cells derived from Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 F/F mice using a lentiviral system and then performed transplantation assays (Figure 6E). 37 , 50 , 51 All recipient mice transplanted with vector‐transduced B lymphoma cells died within 24 days, whereas three of the five mice (60%) transplanted with Spi1‐transduced B lymphoma cells survived for more than 60 days (Figure 6F; P < 0.01). These data show that Spi1 exhibits tumor suppressor activity in B lymphoma cells in vivo.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrated that PU.1 is downregulated in lymphoma cells from a subset of patients with DLBCL, particularly of the non‐GCB type. Conditional knockout of Spi1 in mature B cells induces B cell lymphoma in mice. The survival of immunocompromised mice transplanted with Spi1‐transduced lymphoma cells was longer than that of mice without Spi1 transduction, suggesting that Spi1 acts as a tumor suppressor in mature B cells (Figure 6G). PU.1 is a critical factor in B cell development and is constitutively expressed in BM‐developing B cells and splenic naïve B cells. 38 , 52 However, deletion of PU.1 alone using CD19‐Cre–mediated or Mb1‐Cre–mediated gene excision resulted in only minimal phenotypes. 53 , 54 , 55 Both CD19‐Cre and Mb1‐Cre are specifically active in early B cells, while Cγ1‐Cre is active in GC B cells. This study suggests that downregulation of PU.1 in GC B cells by Cγ1‐Cre is possibly critical in the development of B‐cell lymphomas.

The minimal phenotype in CD19‐Cre and mb1‐Cre mice was likely due to redundancy with the closely related family member Spi‐B. This is evident as all PU.1‐ and Spi‐B‐deficient mice develop pre‐B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia by 30 weeks of age. 56 Similarly, various double knockout mice, including Spi1 mice, exhibit symptoms. Deletion of Spi1 and Interferon regulatory factor 8 (IRF8) via Mb1‐Cre, which is highly expressed in all B cell subpopulations except plasma cells, 57 led to severe defects in follicular and germinal center B cells. This suggests that IRF8 and PU.1 are vital for late‐stage B cell development. 58 IRF4, a potential genome‐wide partner of SPI‐B, PU.1, and BTF, plays a crucial role in the transcriptional network of ABC‐DLBCL. 59 Approximately 50% of all PU.1/IRF4‐deficient and PU.1/IRF8 double‐deficient mice develop pre‐B‐ALL because of the reduced expression of the B‐cell tumor suppressor genes, Ikaros and Spi‐B, associated with pre‐B‐ALL. 33 , 55 These findings suggest a close interplay between PU.1, Spi‐B, IRF4, and IRF8 in the regulation of B cell development and prevention of B cell malignancies. Further studies using double knockouts are essential to identify the factors associated with PU.1, which induced lymphoma in our model.

In the current study, Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 knockout mice required 18–24 months to develop B cell lymphoma, which corresponds to an advanced age in mice. Although B‐cell lymphomas in Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 knockout mice exhibit the DLBCL phenotype, the survival time of the mice was similar to that of Spi1 F/F control mice. Cγ1‐Cre Spi1 knockout mice survival may be too short for B cell lymphoma progression to affect survival. In contrast, serial transplantation of lymphoma cells into immunocompromised animals accelerates disease progression, suggesting that they are also involved in tumor growth. These data suggest that decreased PU.1 levels confer oncogenic properties in B cells but are not enough to promote B cell lymphoma development in younger mice and that decreased immune surveillance may induce the B cell lymphoma phenotype in elderly mice.

In conclusion, our research highlights that PU.1 acts as a tumor suppressor of B cell lymphoma in mice in vivo and that PU.1 is decreased in DLBCL in humans, suggesting that decreased PU.1 is also involved in the development of DLBCL and maintenance of tumor growth in humans. Further studies are essential to elucidate the exact mechanism(s) responsible for the downregulation of PU.1 in lymphoma cells.
