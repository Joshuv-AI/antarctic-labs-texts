SLIRP differentially modulates RNA binding of pathogenic LRPPRC variants
Authors: Louise Lambert, Urška Rovšnik, Amandine Moretton, Katarina Johansson, Mãdãlina Sãcultanu, Mathilde Chéron, Adèle Porcan, Bertil Macao, Siet van den Wildenberg, Xuefeng Zhu, Maria Falkenberg, Géraldine Farge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe LRPPRC/SLIRP complex is a key post-transcriptional regulator of mitochondrial gene expression, stabilizing mitochondrial mRNAs and promoting their polyadenylation and translation. Mutations in LRPPRC cause mitochondrial disorders, including Leigh syndrome French-Canadian type (LSFC), primarily affecting oxidative phosphorylation. Here, we examined the RNA-binding properties of wild-type LRPPRC and three pathogenic variants (A354V, K909del, and R1276_K1300del) using electrophoretic mobility shift assays, acoustic force spectroscopy, and AlphaFold 3 modeling. All three mutations reduced intrinsic RNA binding, with R1276_K1300del showing no detectable interaction in the absence of SLIRP. Remarkably, SLIRP restored RNA binding of this mutant to near wild-type levels, likely through conformational stabilization, as supported by single-molecule and structural analyses. These findings highlight SLIRP’s critical role in modulating LRPPRC function and suggest that enhancing SLIRP activity represents a potential therapeutic strategy for LRPPRC-related mitochondrial disorders.

Introduction

Mitochondria are essential eukaryotic organelles that contain their own circular genome, the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Mitochondrial gene expression is tightly regulated and relies on a dedicated transcriptional machinery distinct from that of the nucleus [1]. The mtDNA is transcribed into polycistronic precursors from promoters on both the heavy and light strands, which are subsequently processed into individual mitochondrial messenger RNAs (mt-mRNAs), transfer RNAs (mt-tRNAs), and ribosomal RNAs (mt-rRNAs) [2]. With the exception of ND6, all mt-mRNAs are polyadenylated by the mitochondrial poly(A) polymerase (mtPAP), which generates relatively short and heterogeneous poly(A) tails of ∼45–50 nucleotides that contribute to stop codon completion and the regulation of mt-mRNA stability and translation [3].

Another factor contributing to the stabilization and post-transcriptional regulation of mt-mRNAs is LRPPRC, a leucine-rich pentatricopeptide repeat-containing protein from the PPR (pentatricopeptide repeat) family. PPR proteins are conserved regulators of mitochondrial gene expression across eukaryotes [4, 5]. LRPPRC contains multiple predicted PPR motifs and a C-terminal RNA-binding domain that includes the final two PPR motifs [6]. While current predictions estimate ∼22 PPR motifs, the limited precision of existing motif-detection algorithms indicates that the actual number may be greater, possibly approaching 30 to 35 [7]. Compared to plant PPR proteins, which are sequence-specific RNA-binding factors that follow a modular one-repeat–one-nucleotide recognition code in which each PPR motif specifies one RNA base via defined amino acid positions [8], LRPPRC shows broader RNA-binding specificity and primarily associates with the mitochondrial transcriptome, with a preference for mt-mRNA [9]. LRPPRC forms a stable complex with the small protein SLIRP (SRA stem-loop-interacting RNA-binding protein) through interactions between LRPPRC’s PPR motifs and the RNA-binding domain of SLIRP [7]. Within this complex, the two proteins are mutually stabilized, and RNA binding is predominantly mediated by LRPPRC, consistent with its high density of PPR motifs. The LRPPRC/SLIRP complex stabilizes mt-mRNAs, as evidenced by its depletion in mice leading to a reduction of all mt-mRNAs except ND6 [10], and enhances their polyadenylation by stimulating mtPAP activity [11]. It also delivers mt-mRNAs to the translating mitoribosome [12].

Mutations in LRPPRC cause a range of mitochondrial disorders, primarily affecting the OXPHOS system. The most well-known disease related to LRPPRC is the French-Canadian type of Leigh Syndrome (LSFC), an autosomal neurodegenerative disease characterized by an early-onset COX (cytochrome c oxidase) deficiency, developmental delay, acidosis, hypotonia, and ataxia [13, 14]. The cause of LSFC was identified as a C-to-T point mutation at nucleotide position 1119 of the LRPPRC gene [13], changing the alanine at position 354 to valine (A354V). As a consequence, both the cellular level of mutant LRPPRC and the steady-state levels of mt-mRNAs are significantly reduced [15, 16]. Numerous additional pathogenic variants have since been identified, including missense mutations and deletions associated with a broad spectrum of mitochondrial and neurodevelopmental phenotypes [17, 18]. Notably, LSFC-like phenotypes have been reported in patients outside the French-Canadian population carrying distinct LRPPRC variants, including the K909del and R1276_K1300del mutations, which similarly cause reduced Complex I and IV levels and abnormal Complex IV assembly [17]. Despite the expanding catalogue of pathogenic variants, how these mutations impair LRPPRC function at the molecular level, and whether SLIRP can compensate for their effects, remain poorly understood.

In this study, we investigated the RNA-binding properties of wild-type LRPPRC and three pathogenic variants located in distinct regions of the protein. These include the classical LSFC founder mutation A354V, together with K909del and R1276_K1300del, identified in patients with LSFC-like phenotypes outside French-Canada. By combining electrophoretic mobility shift assays, single-molecule acoustic force spectroscopy (AFS), and AlphaFold 3-based structural modeling in the presence or absence of SLIRP, we provide mechanistic insight into how these mutations affect LRPPRC function and reveal a differential role for SLIRP in modulating RNA binding.

Materials and methods

Cloning

An N-terminal 6 × His-tagged version of WT LRPPRC codon-optimized for expression in Escherichia coli was used as a template for site-directed mutagenesis. Mutagenesis was performed using the QuikChange II site-directed mutagenesis kit (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All introduced mutations were verified by Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics).

Protein expression and purification

Bacteria were transformed with the recombinant plasmids and grown at 37°C to an OD600 of 0.8 in LB medium. Protein expression was induced by the addition of 0.5 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Cultures were then incubated either at 16°C overnight with shaking or at 30°C for 3 h with shaking. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min and washed with 1× PBS. Bacterial pellets were lysed in lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 1× protease inhibitor and centrifuged at 10 000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was applied to a nickel affinity column (His-Select Ni²⁺ resin, Sigma–Aldrich). Binding was performed in buffer containing 10 mM imidazole, 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 400 mM NaCl, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 1× protease inhibitor. Elution was carried out using 250 mM imidazole in the same buffer composition. Further purification was performed using a Heparin HP column (HiTrap HP, Cytiva), followed by either anion-exchange chromatography on a MonoQ column using an NaCl gradient from 0.2 to 1.2 M (25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.8, 0.5 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 1 mM DTT, and 1× protease inhibitor) for LRPPRC mutant A354V or size-exclusion chromatography on a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 75 pg column (Cytiva) using the same buffer containing 0.2 M NaCl for all other proteins. The purity of the proteins was estimated by SDS–PAGE using TGX Stain-Free gels (Bio-Rad) to be >95%.

Analysis of protein complex formation by size-exclusion chromatography

Prior to chromatographic analysis, proteins were mixed at an equimolar concentration of 16 µM and dialyzed using 10 kDa MWCO Slide-A-Lyzer™ MINI Dialysis Devices (Thermo Scientific™) for 1 h in buffer [20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, and 10% (v/v) glycerol] at 4°C, to allow complex formation, before being concentrated using 10 kDa MWCO Vivaspin™ 500 Centrifugal Concentrators (Sartorius). The mixture was then subjected to size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (24 ml, Cytiva) using the same buffer as for dialysis, at a flow rate of 0.3 ml/min. Eluted fractions (0.5 ml each) were collected across the entire 28 ml elution volume. Collected fractions were analyzed by SDS–PAGE on 4%–20% Mini-PROTEAN® TGX™ Stain-Free precast gels (Bio-Rad) run for 22 min at 300 V. Protein bands were visualized by UV-induced fluorescence following a 5-min UV exposure using the stain-free imaging system.

Electrophoresis mobility shift assay

Electrophoresis mobility shift assays (EMSAs) were performed using 5′-Cyanine 5 (Cy5)-labeled RNA or 5′-Alexa Fluor 647 (AF647)-labeled DNA probes (3 nM final concentration per reaction). All oligonucleotides were synthesized by Eurofins Genomics. The probes used were: mATP6 RNA, 5′-CCACCCAACAAUGACUAAUCAAACUAAC-3′; mCOXI RNA, 5′-CACCACCUUCUUCGACCCCGCCGGAGGA-3′; poly(A) RNA, 5′-AAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAA-3′; poly(U) RNA, 5′-UUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUUU-3′; COX DNA 5′-CTATCCGGAATGCCCCGACGTT-3′; COX comp DNA 5′-AACGTCGGGGCATTCCGGATAG-3′. The double-stranded DNA probe was prepared by mixing equimolar amounts of labeled COX DNA and COX comp DNA in a buffer containing 20 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 50 mM NaCl, and 10 mM MgCl2, heating the mixture to 95°C for 1 min, and then gradually cooling it to 25°C over 30 min. Proteins, at the concentrations indicated in the figure legends, were mixed with the probes in binding buffer containing 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 20 mM DTT, 10% glycerol, 60 mM NaCl, and 0.1 mg/ml BSA, in a final volume of 15 µl. Reactions were incubated for 10 min at 30°C, resolved on 8% native polyacrylamide gels (PAGE), and visualized using a Vilber Fusion FX imaging system with a 647 nm filter (6-min exposure). Gels were quantified by measuring RNA band intensities using ImageJ. For each protein concentration, the fraction of bound RNA was calculated as bound probe/(bound probe + free probe). Protein structure prediction

AlphaFold 3 [19] was used to predict protein–RNA complexes, using the public web server. The input included the FASTA sequences for LRPPRC (WT, K909del, and R1276_K1300del), SLIRP, mATP6, and poly(A). The sequences used for protein prediction correspond to those used in functional experiments in this paper. AlphaFold 3 operated in non-template mode to predict the different LRPPRC–SLIRP–RNA complexes, as well as LRPPRC–RNA only complexes. Five candidate structures for each combination of proteins and RNA were generated. Models were evaluated using confidence metrics including per-atom confidence estimate (pLDDT) (Supplementary Fig. S7), predicted template modeling (pTM), interfacial ipTM, chain pTM and ipTM, chain pair predicted aligned error (PAE) (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2), overall ranking score, as well as visual inspection with ChimeraX (version 1.10.1) [20]. Only the best candidate was used for further evaluation and visualization. Molecular visualization, electrostatic surface representations, and rendering were performed using UCSF ChimeraX (version 1.10.1) [20]. Protein structures and RNA were represented as cartoons. Molecular surfaces were generated and colored by electrostatic potential using the Coulombic Surface Coloring tool, and the structural alignments were performed using the Matchmaker command. Final figures were rendered with ambient occlusion lighting and silhouette edges to enhance 3D depth, and images were exported at high resolution.

Acoustic force spectroscopy

The RNA substrate was obtained by in vitro transcription of three PCR fragments, one of 7 kb fragment and two of 150 nt-length, amplified from human mtDNA with the forward primers containing the T7 promoter sequence (PCR Long Amplification Taq PCR kit, NEB; primer sequences: 5′Handle_Fwd, 5′-TAATACGACTCACTATAGCGAGTATTGCAACTTACTGAGGGC-3′, 5′Handle_Rev, 5′-GGTGAACATACAAAACCCACCCCA-3′, 3′Handle_Fwd, 5′-TAATACGACTCACTATAGCGTGGGGAAGAGACTGATAATAAAGG-3′, 3′Handle_Rev, 5′-CCATGCACACTACTATAACCACCC-3′, 7kbRNA_Fwd, 5′-CGTAATACGACTCACTATAGGTGAACATACAAAACCCACCCCATTCCTC-3′, 7kbRNA_Rev, 5′-GTGGGGAAGAGACTGATAATAAAGGTGG-3′). The 150 nt RNA molecules were complementary to the 5′- or the 3′-end of the 7 kb RNA molecule. Transcription of the 7 kb fragment was performed with unlabeled nucleotides, while transcription of the 150 nt fragments was performed in the presence of labeled UTP in a ratio labeled:unlabeled of 1:3, according to the instructions of the HiScribe® T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB) with an overnight incubation at room temperature. The labeled UTP used for the 150 nt RNA fragment complementary to the 5′- and the 3′-end of the 7 kb RNA molecule were respectively Biotin-16-UTP (NU-821-BIO16, Jena Bioscience) and DIG-11-UTP (NU-821-DIGX, Jena Bioscience). Purification of the transcribed RNAs was performed with RNA Clean & Concentrator (Zymo). Annealing of the three purified RNA molecules was performed in 0.5× SSC with a 4-fold molar excess of the 150 nt RNA fragments in a thermocycler with the following program: the temperature was kept constant at 65°C for 1 h and was gradually reduced until a final temperature of 25°C with incremental steps of 1.2°C maintained for 5 min. Annealed RNA is then purified with RNA Clean & Concentrator (Zymo), following the alternative protocol to remove the excess of 150 nt RNA fragments. RNA concentrations are determined using the Quantifluor RNA System (Promega).

For the AFS experiments, RNA tethers were made by attaching the 7 kb RNA molecules labelled on one end with digoxigenins and on the other end with biotins through the annealed 150 nt RNA handles between a glass surface and 4.38 μm streptavidin polystyrene beads (Spherotech). A detailed description of the AFS instrumentation (LUMICKS B.V., Amsterdam, the Netherlands) can be found in [21]. Flow-cell functionalization was performed with anti-digoxigenin (50 μg/ml, Roche) for 20 min and passivation with protein buffer (10 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 60 mM NaCl, 20 mM DTT, 0.1% BSA, and RNasin) for at least 30 min. Beads in protein buffer were mixed with the RNA substrate diluted at 0.1 ng/μl in protein buffer for 3 min at room temperature before injection in the flow-cell and incubation without force for 5 min for tethering. Unattached beads were washed first with protein buffer under low force. After selecting the regions of interest, making the look-up tables, and performing the force calibration, force–extension curves of the bare RNA tethers were acquired. These steps with bare RNA constituted the control and were done for each assay. Proteins of interest were then flushed into the flow-cell at low force (<3 pN) at a flow rate of 4 μl/min for 3 min, incubated for 1 min, and rinsed with protein buffer for 3 min, also at low force, before force–extension curves were generated. Data acquisition was performed using AFS Tracking G2 and analysis with AFS Analysis G2 software (LUMICKS B.V., Amsterdam, the Netherlands). Results

WT LRPPRC binds to a wide range of nucleic acid substrates

LRPPRC and SLIRP were heterologously expressed in E. coli and purified to near homogeneity using affinity and chromatography-based purification strategies (Supplementary Fig. S1). They were analyzed for their ability to bind different nucleic acid substrates, including ssRNA (single-stranded RNA), poly(A) RNA, poly(U) RNA, ssDNA (single-stranded DNA), and dsDNA (double-stranded DNA) (Supplementary Fig. S2). LRPPRC displayed broad nucleic acid-binding activity, interacting with all tested substrates. Notably, LRPPRC showed strong binding to RNA substrates, including poly(A), poly(U), and fragments of mATP6 and mCOXI mRNAs, while slightly weaker binding was observed with DNA substrates, including dsDNA and ssDNA. In contrast, SLIRP displayed minimal nucleic acid-binding capacity. No detectable binding was observed for most tested substrates, with only weak interaction detected for the mATP6 and mCOXI ssRNA fragments and negligible binding to poly(A) and poly(U). These findings indicate that LRPPRC acts as a broad-spectrum nucleic acid-binding protein, whereas SLIRP exhibits very limited intrinsic binding, with detectable interaction only toward ssRNA.

A354V, K909del, and R1276_K1300del LRPPRC variants show reduced RNA affinity

To investigate the molecular basis of congenital lactic acidosis, we focused on disease-causing mutations in LRPPRC. We selected three distinct variants: the founding mutation A354V, representing the classical form [13], and two additional mutations, K909del and R1276_K1300del, identified in patients with clinical features resembling the LSFC but arising independently of that region [17] (Fig. 1A and B). LRPPRC mutants were heterologously expressed in E. coli and purified to near homogeneity using the same purification strategy as the wild-type protein (Supplementary Fig. S1).

We next investigated the RNA-binding properties of the pathogenic mutants and compared them with those of the WT protein. EMSAs were performed using a fluorescent RNA probe corresponding to a portion of the mitochondrial ATP6 transcript (mATP6 probe), selected based on previous studies demonstrating a strong affinity of LRPPRC for this sequence [7]. EMSAs were performed with increasing concentrations of LRPPRC in the absence of SLIRP (Fig. 1C). As previously observed, WT LRPPRC exhibits strong affinity for the mATP6 probe, with binding becoming nearly complete at 90 nM. The A354V mutant shows a markedly reduced binding capacity, with no complete binding even at 360 nM. The K909del mutant also displays reduced affinity compared to WT, requiring 180 nM to reach near-complete binding. In contrast, the large deletion mutant R1276_K1300del shows no detectable interaction with the mATP6 probe at the tested concentrations. We next assessed the effect of SLIRP on this interaction by repeating the EMSA experiments in the presence of SLIRP added in excess (10-fold over LRPPRC), while maintaining the same LRPPRC concentration range (Fig. 1D). Under these conditions, SLIRP enhances the binding of WT LRPPRC, with near-complete binding observed at lower concentrations (9–18 nM). In contrast, SLIRP does not significantly improve binding of the A354V mutant; instead, a band corresponding to SLIRP alone bound to RNA is detectable, suggesting that the A354V LRPPRC/SLIRP complex may not form efficiently. Similarly, no improvement is observed in the presence of SLIRP for the K909del mutant. Finally, SLIRP markedly improves binding of the R1276_K1300del mutant to the mATP6 probe, with near-complete binding observed at 90 nM, whereas no interaction is detected in the absence of SLIRP.

EMSA results were quantified to determine dissociation constants (Kd) by fitting the curves generated from plotting the fraction of bound RNA against protein concentration (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. S3). Kd values were calculated only only when complete was observed. The results confirm that SLIRP increases the binding affinity of WT LRPPRC for mATP6 (Kd = 16 ± 3 nM without SLIRP versus 5 ± 1 nM with SLIRP, P-value < 0.001). For the A354V mutant, binding is too weak to determine a Kd. For K909del, SLIRP slightly enhances the affinity, reducing the Kd from 48 nM ± 5 to 42 ± 3 nM, although this difference is not statistically significant. Both values, however, remain significantly higher than the WT value (P-value < 0.001). For the R1276_K1300del mutant, no Kd can be measured in the absence of SLIRP; however, its presence markedly improved binding, yielding a Kd of 54 ± 6 nM for mATP6, still higher than that of the WT (P-value < 0.001).

We next evaluated the interaction of LRPPRC with a poly(A) probe, mimicking the polyadenylated tails of mt-mRNAs, first in the absence of SLIRP (Fig. 2A). WT LRPPRC binds to the poly(A) probe with strong affinity, although weaker than that observed for mATP6, with complete binding observed only at 180 nM. For A354V, protein–RNA complexes are barely detectable under the tested conditions, while K909del also exhibits reduced binding compared to WT and fails to achieve complete binding. The R1276_K1300del mutant shows no detectable interaction with the poly(A) probe, similar to its behavior with mATP6. Finally, we assessed the effect of SLIRP on binding to the poly(A) probe by performing EMSAs in its presence (Fig. 2B). SLIRP enhances binding of WT LRPPRC, leading to improved affinity compared to conditions without SLIRP. For the K909del mutant, SLIRP also improves binding, allowing near-complete binding at high concentrations (360 nM), which is not achieved in its absence. In contrast, SLIRP does not significantly rescue the binding of the A354V mutant. For the R1276_K1300del mutant, no complete binding is observed even in the presence of SLIRP, although weak interactions become detectable. We also quantified binding for the poly(A) probe (Fig. 2C). SLIRP appears to increase the affinity of WT LRPPRC for poly(A) (Kd = 38 ± 9 nM without SLIRP versus 25 ± 9 nM with SLIRP) but our quantifications show no statistical significance between poly(A) and mATP6. For all mutants, Kd values could not be reliably determined. However, the binding curves indicate improved binding in the presence of SLIRP, particularly for K909del and R1276_K1300del. Overall, these results indicate that LRPPRC mutations impair RNA binding to varying extents and that SLIRP differentially enhances RNA binding depending on both the mutation and the RNA sequence.

Sequence-dependent RNA binding by LRPPRC–SLIRP

To gain structural insight into the sequence-dependent RNA-binding behavior observed in EMSA experiments, we used AlphaFold 3 [19] to generate structural models visualizing WT LRPPRC in complex with SLIRP bound to the two RNA sequences tested experimentally. The resulting models of the WT LRPPRC/SLIRP–mATP6 complex and the WT LRPPRC/SLIRP-poly(A) complex are shown in Fig. 3A and B. The predicted LRPPRC conformations differ between the two models, suggesting that the RNA sequence may influence the predicted protein fold (Fig. 3E). RNA itself is negatively charged due to the phosphate groups and would thus primarily interact with positive residues of the protein (Fig. 3A and C). The electrostatic surface representation of LRPPRC (with SLIRP removed from the image for better visualization) in complex with mATP6 (Fig. 3B) and poly(A) (Fig. 3D) displays the positive and negative surface charges of the protein and the potential for various protein–RNA interactions (Fig. 3A–D). It is worth noting that the AlphaFold 3 confidence parameters, which are based on per-residue confidence scores and error estimates, diverge substantially for the prediction of the RNA relative to the LRPPRC and SLIRP, as well as, for the prediction of LRPPRC and SLIRP relative to one another. This means that the position of the RNA itself could be quite different in a structure solved by a conventional structural biology method such as X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). In this prediction, mATP6 appears to be positioned closer to the pockets of positively charged residues, whereas poly(A) adopts a different position relative to LRPPRC.

Excess SLIRP is needed to restore R1276_K1300del LRPPRC mutant RNA binding capacity on mATP6 probe

The strong, although not complete compared to WT, SLIRP-dependent rescue of the R1276_K1300del mutant prompted us to investigate this variant further, to elucidate the mechanisms underlying its RNA binding rescue. We performed EMSAs using the mATP6 RNA probe with fixed amounts of WT LRPPRC or R1276_K1300del (90 nM) and increasing amounts of SLIRP (ranging from 0.1- to 10-fold molar excess) (Fig. 4A). Quantitative analysis of the binding curves (Fig. 4B) revealed that the R1276_K1300del mutant required a substantially higher SLIRP ratio to achieve binding levels comparable to WT. Curve fitting indicated that approximately 200–300 nM SLIRP (ratio: 2.5) is required to restore RNA binding to 50% of the maximal WT level. This finding suggests that higher SLIRP concentrations are required to achieve interaction with the mutant LRPPRC in solution, consistent with reduced binding efficiency or an altered mode of interaction.

(B) Graphical representation of RNA binding as a function of the [SLIRP]/[LRPPRC] ratio. Data are presented as mean ± SEM from three independent experiments.

To determine whether this altered requirement reflects impaired complex formation in solution, we next assessed the interaction between the R1276_K1300del mutant and SLIRP by size-exclusion chromatography (Supplementary Fig. S4). The two proteins were pre-incubated prior to gel filtration analysis. The elution profile showed that, similar to the WT protein, the mutant still forms a complex with SLIRP in solution in the absence of RNA, as evidenced by the co-elution of SLIRP at the molecular weight corresponding to LRPPRC. These results indicate that, despite its reduced functional efficiency in RNA binding compared to WT, the mutant retains the ability to physically interact with SLIRP in solution.

The R1276_K1300del LRPPRC mutant exhibits a WT-like RNA-binding behavior in the presence of excess SLIRP

Having investigated the RNA binding capacities of WT and R1276_K1300del LRPPRC alone and in complex with SLIRP using EMSAs, we further explored the binding modes of these proteins using AFS (Fig. 5A). We performed force distance extension (RNA stretching under an increasing force ramp) and retraction (RNA relaxation under a decreasing force ramp) experiments on bare 7 kb ssRNA and on LRPPRC/SLIRP-coated ssRNA. We fitted the force–distance retraction curves of bare ssRNA with the extensible Freely Jointed Chain (eFJC) model, commonly used to model the relationship between applied tension and single-strand nucleic acids extension, and obtained a contour length (Lc) of 3.48 μm ± 0.24 and a persistence length (Lp) of 1.40 nm ± 0.16 (median ± SD) (Supplementary Fig. S5A). These values are consistent with the literature for a 7 kb-long ssRNA molecule [24, 25]. We then assessed the impact of protein-binding on the RNA elasticity. To this end, the ssRNA template was first attached between the bead and the surface, then the protein was flushed in the flow-cell and incubated with the ssRNA and finally unbound/weakly bound proteins are washed away with protein buffer. The force–distance retraction curve of the ssRNA template incubated with SLIRP fully superimposes with the curve of bare ssRNA, indicating that SLIRP alone does not bind to the RNA under the experimental conditions used for AFS (Supplementary Fig. S5B). For LRPPRC, we observed that the force–distance curves for both WT and R1276_K1300del LRPPRC-coated ssRNA significantly deviated from the curves corresponding to bare ssRNA directly after incubation of the protein with the RNA template (Supplementary Fig. S5C and D). However, after rinsing of the LRPPRC-coated ssRNA with protein buffer, only the ssRNA incubated with the WT protein continued to show a deviation compared to bare ssRNA. This indicates that although R1276_K1300del binds ssRNA in this AFS experiment, the interaction is only transient, in line with the absence of binding observed in the EMSA experiments (Figs 1 and 2). We hypothesize that there is an equilibrium between binding and unbinding of R1276_K1300del, made possible by the reservoir of proteins present around the RNA molecule, which is lost after washing out excess protein. In contrast, binding of the WT protein is strong even in the absence of SLIRP, as already shown by the EMSAs. Comparison of the force–distance curves of ssRNA in the absence or presence of LRPPRC suggests that LRPPRC binding stiffens the ssRNA, as LRPPRC-coated ssRNA is more stretched at low force than bare ssRNA.

As SLIRP is functional in patients bearing the R1276_K1300del mutation, we then focused on the RNA mechanical properties after binding of the LRPPRC/SLIRP complex. To this end, we pre-mixed LRPPRC/SLIRP (50 nM/500 nM) in protein buffer before adding it to the flow-cell containing ssRNA molecules attached between the surface and the beads. We observed similar profiles for the force–distance curves of ssRNA coated with WT or mutant LRPPRC in complex with SLIRP after rinsing of unbound/weakly bound proteins (Fig. 5B). These curves were also similar to the curve of ssRNA coated with WT LRPPRC alone (Supplementary Fig. S5C and D), suggesting that the binding mode of LRPPRC to ssRNA is not modified when in complex with SLIRP. To further quantify the effect of LRPPRC binding on the ssRNA elasticity, we calculated the area between the force–distance curve of bare ssRNA and protein-coated ssRNA before the crossing point of these two curves (Fig. 5C), as well as the crossing constant k = Fcross/(Lcross/Lc), where Fcross and Lcross are the force and the distance, respectively, at the crossing point, and Lc is the contour length obtained for the bare ssRNA (Fig. 5D). We found an area and a crossing constant of 1.19 ± 0.16 pN.nm and 5.18 ± 0.77 pN for WT + SLIRP, and 1.00 ± 0.25 pN.nm and 5.48 ± 0.70 pN for R1276_K1300del + SLIRP, respectively, with both differences being non-significant (median ± SD). This indicates that under these experimental conditions, SLIRP restores the ability of R1276_K1300del LRPPRC to bind ssRNA, also at the molecular level.

Our AFS results reveal that WT LRPPRC protein modifies RNA elasticity. Furthermore, we observed that the R1276_K1300del mutant is capable of binding to an ssRNA substrate even in the absence of SLIRP, but this binding is highly unstable, explaining why it is not detected in EMSA experiments. In contrast, in the presence of SLIRP, this mutant binds RNA and modifies its elasticity in the same way as the WT protein. This indicates that the influence of LRPPRC on RNA elasticity is conserved in the mutant when associated with SLIRP, without any detectable molecular difference.

Structural prediction suggests that complex formation with SLIRP restores folding of the R1276_K1300del LRPPRC mutant

To visualize how the protein fold changes in the presence and absence of SLIRP and to determine whether these conformational changes are sequence-dependent, we generated AlphaFold 3 models for WT and each of the mutants (A354V, K909del, R1276-K1300del) with either an mATP6 or poly(A) RNA. Each of the predictions was first evaluated based on a set of parameters such as pTM, ipTM, overall score, and plDDT (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). The pTM is a measure of how well a multimer is predicted, with acceptable values above 0.5 indicating a confident prediction likely to resemble the true structure, a criterion that is met by almost all models in this study. ipTM measures the accuracy of the relative positions of the subunits, with values above 0.6 being acceptable [26, 27]. The overall ipTM values indicate that predictions generated without SLIRP fall below the threshold generally considered acceptable for reliable multimeric structure prediction (Supplementary Table S1). To determine whether the lower overall ipTM scores observed in models containing SLIRP were primarily attributable to the RNA component, we examined the chain-pair ipTM values (Supplementary Table S2). These analyses revealed that the RNA chains, with no significant difference between mATP6 and poly(A), are the main contributors to the reduced overall structural confidence. This conclusion is further supported by the pLDDT scores, which range from 0 to 100 and were used to assess the quality of each predicted model. Scores above 90 indicate high confidence, whereas scores below 50 suggest that the corresponding region is likely to be inaccurately predicted (Supplementary Fig. S7). Notably, the pLDDT scores for LRPPRC and SLIRP are consistently higher than those of the RNA chains, whereas the RNA chains show values well below 50, indicating low confidence in the predicted RNA conformations (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Removing SLIRP from the complex, in theory, creates space and exposes the RNA to the solvent, which appears to change the fold of the LRPPRC (Supplementary Fig. S6B). This new fold appears to be less favorable for RNA binding to the WT (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S6B) and even more so for R1276_K1300del mutant (Figs 1B and 6C). When SLIRP is present in the complex with R1276_K1300del, the modeled structure resembles that of WT, despite the large deletion in the mutant protein (Fig. 6D and E). This deletion removes an entire short α-helix of the PPR domain (Fig. 6A), which is then replaced by the next α-helix in the sequence to preserve the structural fold. It is possible that this swap only slightly changes the environment and remains favorable for RNA binding. It does, however, alter the surface charge distribution at the interface with the RNA (Fig. 6F). The resulting charge imbalance is compensated by SLIRP, which is highly positively charged on its LRPPRC-facing side (Fig. 6F). The model predicts that mATP6 RNA adopts a different position relative to the LRPPRC protein and interacts with different regions of the protein. In the WT model, the RNA binds closer to the charged regions of LRPPRC (Fig. 6E, left), while in the R1276_K1300del mutant it appears to be positioned closer to SLIRP while the deletion region is unoccupied (Fig. 6E, right). Interestingly, SLIRP-dependent rescue is observed only for the R1276_K1300del mutant and not for A354V or K909del. While AlphaFold 3 predicts the overall LRPPRC/SLIRP complex with relatively high confidence, the predicted RNA conformations have substantially lower confidence. Nevertheless, the predicted position of the mATP6 RNA differs between the mutant models (Supplementary Fig. S8). Although these observations suggest that the mutations may alter RNA binding in different ways, the low confidence of the RNA predictions precludes firm mechanistic conclusion.

Discussion

We observed that LRPPRC exhibits broad nucleic acid binding capacity, albeit with a weaker affinity for poly(A) RNA. This is consistent with previous in vivo and in vitro studies showing that LRPPRC broadly associates with mitochondrial RNAs and their precursors, with slightly reduced and more variable interactions with poly(A) [7, 9, 15, 28]. The molecular basis of this differential affinity has remained unclear. Our structural modelling suggests that RNA identity itself could be a key determinant. Even though the confidence of the RNA predictions is lower than that of the protein complex, it is consistent and suggests that LRPPRC adopts distinct conformations when bound to structured mATP6 versus homopolymeric poly(A). While electrostatic complementarity between the negatively charged RNA backbone and the lysine-, arginine- and histidine-rich surfaces of LRPPRC provides the basis for association, these interactions are largely non-specific. The preferential positioning of mATP6 near these basic patches points to additional structural or sequence complementarity, supporting a model in which LRPPRC recognizes mitochondrial transcripts through broader features rather than a strict PPR recognition code [7, 29]. Structured RNAs such as mATP6 likely present defined binding epitopes and promote cooperative interactions, whereas poly(A), being compositionally uniform and largely unstructured, engages LRPPRC more transiently. The altered LRPPRC fold observed in the poly(A)-bound prediction may reflect this less constrained binding mode, in which the protein samples alternative conformations in the absence of stabilizing RNA features. Such plasticity is a hallmark of many RNA-binding proteins [30] and may be functionally relevant for coordinating the diverse steps of mitochondrial RNA metabolism, including stabilization, processing, and translation.

Our AFS results align with current knowledge on ATP-independent RNA chaperones. The LRPPRC/SLIRP complex has been proposed to function as a mitochondrial RNA chaperone by directly interacting with RNAs and reorganizing their local secondary structures without ATP [9]. LRPPRC-binding sites are predominantly found on mitochondrial mRNAs and are associated with alterations in local RNA secondary structures, suggesting that relaxation by the complex promotes both mRNA stability and coordinated translation, including via polyadenylation. Here, we show, using AFS that LRPPRC/SLIRP modifies the biophysical properties of ssRNA, indicating potential local RNA stiffening at interaction sites. The force–extension curves of RNA coated with WT LRPPRC or R1276_K1300del show a characteristic shape, with the crossover between the protein-coated and naked RNA curves suggesting that protein binding alters both the structural organization and the elastic properties of the RNA. At low forces, the protein-coated RNA requires less force than naked RNA to reach a given extension, consistent with protein-induced disruption of secondary structure, as previously suggested [9], and/or base stacking thereby increasing the contour length accessible prior to mechanical stretching. At higher forces, however, the protein-coated RNA requires greater force than naked RNA to achieve comparable extensions, indicating an increased effective persistence length. This qualitative behavior resembles that observed for T4 gp32-coated ssDNA [31], where protein binding suppresses compact conformations and increases the effective stiffness of the nucleoprotein filament, giving rise to a crossover in the force–extension curves. Our results thus complement previous studies describing the complex’s role in remodeling RNA secondary structures [9]. Rather than inducing global destabilization, our findings suggest that the complex may locally constrain RNA dynamics, favoring more ordered, less flexible conformations. Such local stiffening could support LRPPRC’s chaperone function by stabilizing functional RNA conformations and limiting structural fluctuations that might impair recognition by other factors or reduce translation and maturation efficiency.

The A354V mutation, the most common in LSFC patients [13], is associated with decreased COX mRNA levels and enzymatic activity in tissues typically affected in Leigh syndrome, including the brain, heart, skeletal muscle, liver, and kidney [32, 33]. It also leads to defects in respiration, mitochondrial function, calcium homeostasis and metabolism [34, 35]. CLIP-seq analyses has previously revealed that LRPPRC preferentially binds mitochondrial transcripts, notably COI, COIII, and ATP6/8, with the strongest enrichment observed for mATP6 [9], which guided our focus on this transcript. We observed a marked reduction in RNA binding on mATP6 by the A354V mutant compared to WT, indicating that the mutation directly impairs RNA–protein interactions, which likely contribute to the pathological phenotype. The A354V substitution is located near the putative SLIRP interaction site (arginine 438 and asparagine 479 of LRPPRC). Our data show that SLIRP has almost no effect on RNA binding of the mutant, consistent with recent findings showing that A354V fails to directly interact with SLIRP in vitro [36]. These results thus suggest that the pathogenic effect of the A354V mutation stems mainly from direct disruption of LRPPRC’s RNA-binding capability rather than from altered SLIRP-mediated stabilization.

The effects of the K909del and R1276_K1300del mutations on patient phenotypes remain poorly documented. To date, only a single study provides detailed clinical data for patients carrying these mutations [17]. Muscle analyses of individuals with the R1276_K1300del deletion revealed COX deficiencies similar to those observed in LSFC patients with the A354V mutation. These analyses also showed reduced COX activity and a global decrease in mitochondrial mRNA levels, except for ND6, along with reduced polyadenylation. In addition, a reduction in LRPPRC protein levels was observed in patient fibroblasts and muscle accompanied by a concomitant decrease in SLIRP protein levels [17]. Overall, despite limited data, patients exhibit clinical features resembling those associated with the A354V mutation. The K909del mutation alters the protein binding affinity for both tested RNAs. While K909del shows higher RNA affinity than A354V on mATP6 (Kd = 48 ± 5 nM, while not measurable for A354V), its affinity is only slightly enhanced by SLIRP, and only for poly(A) RNA. This suggests that K909del may cause interaction defects with SLIRP similar to those observed for A354V. In striking contrast, the large-deletion mutant R1276_K1300del shows a fundamentally different behavior. It does not bind RNA in the absence of SLIRP, regardless of the RNA tested; however, binding is restored in the presence of SLIRP to levels approaching those of WT. This indicates that SLIRP may act as a stabilizing factor, allowing the mutant protein to adopt a conformation closer to that of WT, as supported by our in silico analyses. Moreover, previous work on the interaction between SLIRP and WT LRPPRC indicates that one molecule of SLIRP binds to one molecule of WT LRPPRC [7]. Our findings suggest that R1276_K1300del requires higher SLIRP concentration in solution than the WT to restore RNA-binding capacity. Nevertheless, the SLIRP-restored conformation is not completely identical to WT, potentially explaining residual decreases in binding affinity. Structural predictions suggest that the electrostatic properties of the WT LRPPRC–SLIRP–RNA complex differ from those of the R1276_K1300del–SLIRP–RNA complex, with the mutant displaying more acidic residues at the RNA-binding interface, partially offset by the electropositivity of SLIRP.

One limitation of our study is that our structural models are based on AlphaFold 3 predictions, which have lower confidence for RNA–protein interfaces than for protein cores; experimental structural validation (e.g. by cryo-EM) will be needed to confirm the predicted binding modes. Furthermore, testing more RNA substrates, for example native mitochondrial transcripts with complex secondary structures, may provide further insight in the behavior of LRPPRC. Finally, our biochemical experiments are performed in vitro and may not fully recapitulate the cellular context of LRPPRC/SLIRP function. Therefore, additional validation in cellular models could complement our results.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that while all three LRPPRC mutations markedly impair intrinsic RNA binding, SLIRP can partially restore the function of R1276_K1300del through protein stabilization and conformational modulation. While SLIRP-based strategies are unlikely to benefit patients with mutations that disrupt the SLIRP–LRPPRC interface (such as A354V), they may offer therapeutic potential for variants in which LRPPRC stability rather than SLIRP binding is impaired, such as R1276_K1300del. This highlights SLIRP as an important modulator of LRPPRC activity and suggests that therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing SLIRP function or mimicking its stabilizing effect may offer a promising avenue for the treatment of LRPPRC-related mitochondrial disorders.
