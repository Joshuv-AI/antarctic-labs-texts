Snare-assisted flapless closed-loop scleral refixation of dislocated Akreos Adapt intraocular lenses
Authors: Qing Liu, Xinlei Zhu, Lingxiao Lun, Cong Qin, Lei Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo describe a flapless, single-pass, snare-assisted technique for four-point scleral refixation of dislocated Akreos Adapt intraocular lenses (IOLs) using an 8 − 0 polypropylene suture in a closed-loop configuration.MethodsAn 8 − 0 polypropylene suture was passed through the four fenestrated haptics of the IOL using a non-sliding snare constructed from a 30-gauge thin-walled needle mounted on a 1 mL syringe. The extraocular portions of the suture were routed within the intrascleral plane between two fixation points on the same side (nasal or temporal), which are 2 mm away from the limbus, with a distance of 7 mm between the two fixation points, without conjunctival dissection or scleral flap creation. The external suture knot was buried within the scleral stroma.ResultsThis retrospective case series included five eyes with Akreos Adapt IOLs that were completely dislocated into the vitreous cavity. All lenses were successfully fixated without intraoperative complications. At a mean follow-up of 8.4 months, all IOLs remained well-centered and stable. Best-corrected visual acuity improved in all cases. The preoperative and postoperative spherical equivalent values ranged from − 5.00 D to 11.00 D preoperatively, and from − 2.50 D to -0.50 D postoperatively. No hypotony, suture erosion, knot exposure, or IOL redislocation were observed.ConclusionThis technique offers a minimally invasive and reproducible method for four-point scleral fixation of dislocated Akreos Adapt IOLs, eliminating the need for conjunctival or scleral dissection and requiring no specialized instrumentation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04418-9.Keywords: Flapless scleral fixation, Snare-assisted technique, Closed-loop configuration, Polypropylene suture

Introduction

Dislocation of posterior chamber intraocular lenses (IOLs) is a well-recognized long-term complication following cataract surgery, particularly in eyes with zonular weakness, high myopia, or a history of pars plana vitrectomy [1]. Four-point scleral fixation has become a widely accepted approach for secondary IOL implantation or repositioning without adequate capsular support, offering improved centration and rotational stability [2–7].

The Akreos Adapt IOL (Bausch & Lomb, Laval, Canada) is a foldable, hydrophilic acrylic lens featuring four fenestrated haptics designed to facilitate transscleral suture fixation through preformed eyelets. Its favorable anatomical stability and reduced risk of postoperative iris capture have made it a preferred choice for secondary fixation. However, refixation of dislocated Akreos Adapt IOLs remains technically demanding and is associated with a substantial learning curve. Although several modifications of scleral fixation techniques have been proposed, most still involve conjunctival peritomy, the creation of scleral flaps or tunnels, or the simultaneous passage of two needles through the eye [5, 6, 8–10]. These maneuvers increase procedural complexity and are particularly challenging in eyes with conjunctival scarring or a history of glaucoma filtration surgery.

In this study, we describe a flapless, closed-loop scleral refixation technique using an 8 − 0 polypropylene suture and a snare-assisted, single-needle transscleral approach for four-point fixation of dislocated Akreos Adapt IOLs. This method eliminates the need for conjunctival dissection, scleral flap formation, or specialized instruments, providing a simplified, minimally invasive, and reproducible alternative for IOL refixation.

Methods

Ethics committee approval was obtained from Qingdao Eye Hospital, Shandong First Medical University, for this retrospective case series. All procedures adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. We reviewed the medical records of all patients with dislocated Akreos Adapt IOLs (Bausch & Lomb, Laval, Quebec, Canada) who underwent four-point scleral refixation using flapless closed-loop 8 − 0 polypropylene suturing was assisted by a non-sliding suture snare at our institution between June and September 2024. The inclusion criterion was eyes with Akreos Adapt IOLs that were completely dislocated into the vitreous cavity. Although the Akreos Adapt IOL is a foldable hydrophilic acrylic lens with four-holed loops designed to facilitate four-point scleral suture fixation, its use constitutes an off-label application.

Written informed consent outlining potential risks was obtained from all patients. Demographic data, including age and sex, were recorded. Comprehensive ophthalmic examinations were performed both preoperatively and postoperatively. Collected clinical parameters included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP), refractive error, corneal endothelial cell density (measured using the Konan Noncon Robo; Konan Medical, Tokyo, Japan), and effective lens position as assessed by swept-source anterior segment optical coherence tomography (CASIA2; Tomey Corporation, Nagoya, Japan). Postoperatively, antibiotics and topical corticosteroids are routinely administered. Typically, gatifloxacin eye drops (four times daily for one week) and prednisolone acetate eye drops (for 3–4 weeks) are used. The minimum follow-up duration was 6 months.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (Version 25.0; ICM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Pre- and postoperative BCVA, IOP, and corneal endothelial cell counts were compared using the Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by a single surgeon (Lei Wan) under peribulbar anesthesia, as demonstrated in the Supplemental Digital Content (see Video). In cases where the dislocated intraocular lens (IOL) had dropped into the vitreous cavity, a 25-gauge pars plana vitrectomy was performed using the Constellation Vision System (Alcon Laboratories Inc., Duluth, GA, USA). A schematic illustration of the surgical steps (left eye) is provided in Fig. 1.

The main surgical procedure diagram.A. Gentian violet marks four scleral fixation points, and the Akreos Adapt IOL is placed in the anterior chamber.B. The 8 − 0 polypropy lene suture is inserted into a bent insulin needle and then passed through the inferonasal haptic of the IOL into the posterior chamber. C. A non-sliding suture snare was passed through another eyelet 180° away on the inferotemporal haptic of the IOL and retrieved the suture from the globe using a single puncture at the scleral fixation site. D. The suture is introduced through another IOL eyelet and two additional superior scleral fixation points. E. The suture has passed through the eye and four IOL eyelet. F. The suture is drawn out to bury the second knot, and the externalized end is trimmed flush with the sclera

The horizontal corneal meridian was marked with gentian violet, centered on the visual axis. Four transscleral fixation sites were identified 3.5 mm above and below this line and 2 mm posterior to the limbus (Fig. 1A). Two 1.0-mm clear corneal incisions were created at the 10 and 2 o’clock positions. After filling the anterior chamber with an ophthalmic viscosurgical device, the dislocated Akreos Adapt IOL was elevated above the iris plane using intraocular forceps. An 8 − 0 polypropylene suture (Ethicon, Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ) was loaded into a bent 30-gauge thin-walled needle and introduced into the posterior chamber. The needle was advanced through the eyelet on the inferonasal haptic of the IOL and exited at the corresponding inferonasal scleral fixation site (Fig. 1B), leaving the suture within the eye after passing through the inferotemporal haptic. A non-sliding snare, fashioned from a 30-gauge thin-walled needle mounted on a 1 mL syringe, was introduced through the horizontally aligned eyelet on the same haptic. A 25-gauge intraocular forcep inserted via the superonasal corneal incision was used to guide the previously introduced suture into the snare loop. The snare was then withdrawn, externalizing the suture through the inferotemporal scleral site (Fig. 1C). This maneuver completed suture passage through both inferior haptic eyelets. A bent 30-gauge needle was then used to create a transscleral tunnel from the inferotemporal to the superotemporal fixation site parallel to the limbus, allowing externalization of the suture at the superotemporal site. Following the same technique, the suture was sequentially passed through the remaining superior haptic eyelets and externalized, thereby completing the four-point closed-loop configuration (Fig. 1D–E). The suture was directed from the superonasal fixation point through an intrascleral tunnel across the nasal sclera and exited again at the original inferonasal site. After confirming IOL centration, suture tension was adjusted, and an overhand knot was tied at the inferonasal fixation site. A second overhand knot was placed approximately 4 mm distal to the first. One end of the suture was trimmed, leaving a 2–3 mm tail. The longer end was reinserted into a bent 30-gauge thin-walled needle to create a 4–5 mm scleral tunnel parallel to the limbus. The second knot was buried within the tunnel, and the suture was trimmed flush with the scleral surface (Fig. 1F). No conjunctival sutures were required.

Result

Five eyes from five male patients were included (Table 1 lists the patient characteristics in this study). The mean age was 66.8 years (54–82 years). The average follow-up period was 8.4 ± 1.1 months (7–10 months). The average interval between initial IOL implantation and refixation was 10 years. The causes of IOL dislocation in this cohort were as follows: one eye was associated with vitrectomy, one eye resulted from trauma, one eye was associated with retinitis pigmentosa, and two eyes had no identifiable cause. Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes are summarized in Table 1. No intraoperative complications occurred. At the final follow-up, all IOLs remained well-centered and stable. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) improved in all patients, with mean logMAR BCVA improving from 0.54 ± 0.30 preoperatively to 0.16 ± 0.05 postoperatively (p<0.05). The preoperative and postoperative spherical equivalent values ranged from − 5.00 D to 11.00 D preoperatively, and from − 2.50 D to −0.50 D postoperatively, reflecting varying degrees of refractive change following surgery.

No suture-related complications were observed, such as erosion, exposure, loosening, or granuloma. No hypotony, scleral thinning, cystoid macular edema, iris capture, retinal tear, or retinal detachment occurred. Discussion

Dislocation of posterior chamber intraocular lenses (IOLs) is a common complication following cataract surgery, especially in patients with compromised zonular integrity, previous vitrectomy, or ocular trauma [1]. Contemporary clinical practice favors refixing the original IOL whenever feasible, aiming to reduce surgical trauma and procedural complexity associated with lens exchange [11]. Nevertheless, repositioning dislocated IOLs remains technically challenging, typically demanding considerable surgical skill, prolonged operating time, and increased resource utilization [12]. Continuous refinement and innovation of surgical techniques are thus critically important. The Akreos Adapt IOL, characterized by its unique four-point fenestrated haptic design, provides enhanced anatomical stability and has gained widespread use in scleral fixation procedures. However, refixation of dislocated Akreos Adapt IOLs presents notable technical challenges, notably when adequate capsular support is lacking. Here, we describe a modified flapless, snare-assisted closed-loop scleral fixation technique utilizing an 8 − 0 polypropylene suture via a single-pass approach, offering distinct advantages, including minimal invasiveness, simplicity, excellent reproducibility, and effective clinical outcomes.

Compared to traditional methods requiring extensive scleral flap creation, large conjunctival dissections, or complex intraocular manipulations, our technique substantially reduces intraocular manipulation and avoids extensive tissue disruption. All intraocular procedures involving the IOL are performed at the iris plane, significantly minimizing potential retinal injury from vitreous cavity manipulations [5]. Furthermore, the exclusive use of 30-gauge needle punctures for scleral fixation eliminates wound leakage risks and the necessity for additional suture closure, making it especially suitable for patients with thin sclera, marked conjunctival scarring, or prior glaucoma filtration surgery. Additionally, this procedure requires only two 1-mm clear corneal limbal incisions, effectively reducing the risk of surgically induced astigmatism.

Various techniques have been described previously for the refixation of fenestrated-haptic IOLs. Zhang et al. reported a flapless technique using partial conjunctival dissection with 10 − 0 polypropylene sutures but raised concerns regarding potential long-term suture degradation and breakage [4]. Chen et al. described a handshake technique with 8 − 0 polypropylene sutures require complicated intraocular needle exchanges, considerable surgical expertise, partial conjunctival incisions, and specialized instrumentation [8]. Hu et al. presented a flapless method that avoided conjunctival manipulation but involved intricate intraocular threading steps, resulting in a steep learning curve [6]. Fan et al. reported a technique requiring scleral flaps, increasing surgical complexity and tissue trauma, and potentially prolonging operative time [5]. Icoz et al. published a modified double-flanged technique that allowed the placement of 6 − 0 polypropylene sutures using a 26-gauge needle, achieving excellent results in cases of subluxated foldable single-piece IOLs [13, 14]. More recently, a curved 27-gauge needle–guided suture snare technique was proposed, allowing minimally invasive scleral fixation using 8 − 0 polypropylene sutures with favorable anatomical and visual outcomes and no suture-related complications [15]. While promising, this approach still utilizes a larger needle and anterior limbal entry, whereas our method employs a 30-gauge thin-walled needle, enabling even less traumatic scleral entry and reduced manipulation. Besides of these approaches, the sutureless flanging technique described by Canabrava has also gained attention [16]. Although this method avoids suture-related complications, it is mainly indicated for secondary IOL implantation in aphakic eyes and requires a relatively large corneal incision. By contrast, our study focused on the refixation of dislocated four-haptic Akreos Adapt IOLs, which can be achieved through two 1-mm limbal incisions, consistent with minimally invasive principles.

Our snare-assisted closed-loop method avoids the need for scleral flaps and conjunctival dissection. All suture manipulations are performed externally, significantly simplifying the procedure. The even distribution of suture tension achieved by our method ensures superior postoperative IOL centration and stability. To the best of our knowledge this is the first report specifically focusing on a snare-assisted four-point scleral refixation of dislocated Akreos Adapt intraocular lenses.

Selection of suture material profoundly impacts the long-term surgical outcomes of scleral fixation. Traditionally, 10 − 0 polypropylene sutures have been widely used but are associated with high risks of long-term suture breakage, reportedly reaching as high as 26.2%, particularly in younger, more active patients [17]. Gore-Tex sutures offer excellent tensile strength and have gained popularity in scleral fixation procedures [18]; however, they remain off-label in ophthalmology and may be associated with complications such as chronic exposure or infection when not adequately buried. A recent report documented a case of endophthalmitis following delayed rotation of chronically exposed Gore-Tex knots, with infection resolution only after complete hardware removal, indicating possible colonization of the suture material [19]. These risks, combined with procurement limitations, surgical complexity, potential for tissue reactivity, and relatively higher cost compared to standard ophthalmic sutures, may limit their routine clinical use despite their favorable mechanical properties. We selected 8 − 0 polypropylene sutures, with appropriate caliber, tensile strength, and durability, has not been associated with significant long-term suture breakage in the existing literature, indicating better potential durability [3, 8, 20].

Regarding external knot management, our approach adopted the scleral knot-burial technique described by Jin et al. [21, 22]. By burying both the suture and the knot within the scleral layers, the risk of exposure and late breakage can be minimized. Furthermore, the continuous-thread configuration distributes forces evenly across the fixation points, thereby avoiding localized stress concentration and contributing to stable long-term IOL positioning. The current method utilizes standard vitreoretinal surgical instruments without requiring additional specialized equipment, making it accessible and cost-effective. The snare-assisted technique is straightforward to learn and adapt, particularly suitable for surgeons experienced in vitreoretinal procedures [23].

This study has several limitations. First, the tilt or decentralization of the IOL is a potential long-term complication of this technique. Although excellent IOL centration and stability were achieved in all cases, larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are needed to fully validate the long-term safety and stability of the technique. Second, a key limitation is the potential for IOL opacification, which could complicate future retinal procedures. Surgeons must understand and discuss this risk with patients during preoperative counseling. On the other hand, it is important to emphasize that the technique is corneal endothelium- and wound-friendly, as it avoids IOL explantation and can be performed through two 1-mm limbal incisions, largely protecting the corneal endothelial cells. While specifically applied to Akreos Adapt IOLs, the underlying principles may also apply to other fenestrated-haptic IOL designs.

In conclusion, our flapless, snare-assisted closed-loop scleral fixation technique provides significant advantages in terms of minimal invasiveness, ease of implementation, surgical efficiency, and safety, particularly beneficial in complex cases with compromised scleral conditions, extensive surgical histories, conjunctival scarring, or restricted intraocular anatomy. The available clinical evidence strongly supports this method’s safety and clinical value, positioning it as an optimal choice for scleral IOL refixation.
