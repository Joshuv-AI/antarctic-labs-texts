Identifying bio-behavioural signatures of persistent opioid use risk in trauma injury patients: a protocol for a prospective cohort study
Authors: Joy M. Schmitz, Jin Ho Yoon, Bruno Kluwe-Schiavon, John A Harvin, Preethi H Gunaratne, Darrion Mouton, Kandice Motley, Erin E Fox, Jessica Vincent, Megan Tarbet, Consuelo Walss-Bass
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionExposure to prescription opioids following traumatic injury can increase the risk of developing tolerance, persistent opioid use and opioid use disorder. The mechanisms underlying opioid tolerance or dependence are not well understood, and no biomarkers predict risk. Opioid exposure causes epigenetic modifications, including alterations in microRNA (miRNA) expression. Several miRNAs, which regulate synaptic plasticity, are hypothesised to underlie substance use disorders and influence µ-opioid receptor levels, modulating opioid tolerance. This project aims to develop a bio-behavioural signature to predict persistent opioid use and chronic pain up to 6 months post-discharge.Methods and analysisThe study will use a prospective cohort design, enrolling 180 adult patients at a Level I Trauma Center who are prescribed opioids at discharge. Prospective data will be collected in the hospital and at 7 days and 1, 3 and 6 months post-discharge. Biological data (genotyping and miRNA levels) and clinical measures of opioid use, pain, pain sensitivity (EEG) and psychosocial functioning will be collected at each time point. Bayesian regression methods will be used to identify baseline clinical, genetic, epigenetic and psychosocial predictors of opioid use and pain outcomes at 6 months post-discharge. Growth mixture modelling will identify distinct subgroups with varying trajectories, followed by Bayesian hierarchical modelling to predict trajectory classification based on predictor variables.Ethics and dissemination planEthics approval for this study was obtained from the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects (HSC-MS-24–0314). Findings will be disseminated in peer-reviewed scientific journals and at national and international conferences.

Introduction

Although prescription opioids are often a necessary strategy for treating acute pain, long-term use can lead to dangerous consequences. Individuals with traumatic injuries are especially vulnerable, with over 50% being prescribed an opioid at discharge for pain management1 and as many as one-third continuing to use opioids longer than the time recommended for acute pain management.24 As a result, trauma patients represent about 10.6% of the population of persistent opioid users5 and are 1.4 times more likely to develop opioid use disorder (OUD) compared with individuals without injuries.2 6

Long-term or persistent opioid use for chronic pain is associated with opioid-induced hyperalgesia (OIH), leading to higher and more frequent dosing to reduce pain, opioid tolerance and risk for OUD.7 8 While the mechanisms underlying tolerance and OIH are not fully understood, both reduced postsynaptic potassium conductance and voltage-gated calcium and potassium channels activity appear to be involved.9 10 These processes contribute to increased neuroinflammatory responses in the brain and spinal cord, particularly those mediated by toll-like receptor-4 and the NLRP inflammasome.10 11

Given the significant clinical challenges with using opioids for pain management, along with recently updated guidelines from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) aimed at reducing opioid use and dosage,12 the national opioid prescribing rate has declined substantially from 81.3 prescriptions per 100 persons in 2012 to 43.3 prescriptions per 100 persons in 2020.13 14 As the amount and conditions for prescribing opioids become more restricted, the focus has shifted to determining who should receive them. This type of precision medicine requires identifying the risk profiles of trauma injury patients, particularly concerning the development of chronic pain that may lead to persistent opioid use and using this classification to select pain management regimens that maximise benefits while minimising risks.

Assessments to identify risks have primarily focused on psychosocial factors, including history of anxiety, depression, illicit drug use and pre-injury opioid use,15 along with longer hospital stays and the presence of pre-existing chronic pain disorders.16 Screening tools, such as the Opioid Risk Tool (ORT),17 18 have been developed to guide pain management prescribing based on risk stratification; however, their predictive validity and actual utilisation by practitioners remain limited. More recent efforts to identify risk factors for OUD have focused on genetic variants and polygenic risk scores (PRS) associated with the condition.19 20 In 2023, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved a DNA genotyping test (AvertD) to assess the risk of developing OUD. The test analyses 15 common genetic variants based on their presumed effect on neural reward systems. However, in a case-control study of over 450 000 opioid-exposed individuals, the results provided no evidence supporting the clinical utility of this test, which was associated with high rates of false positives and false negatives.21 Clearly, alternatives to genetic testing alone are needed.

Opioid exposure leads to epigenetic modifications, including changes in microRNA (miRNA) expression, which may serve as valuable biomarkers for assessing persistent opioid use and OUD risk. Several miRNAs involved in regulation of synaptic plasticity are hypothesised to underlie drug addiction and miRNAs have been shown to regulate µ-opioid receptor levels and modulate opioid tolerance.22 Furthermore, miRNAs have been proposed as promising biomarkers for prediction of acute brain injury and analgesic efficacy outcomes.23 24 Specifically, miR-451a has been associated with analgesic efficacy,25 and Let7b has been shown to modulate pain through both neuronal and glial signalling.26 Building on the growing appreciation that miRNAs can cross the blood–brain barrier and thus serve as blood-based biomarkers that reflect brain function, we carried out miRNA profiling in same-subject postmortem samples from brain and blood tissues of patients with OUD compared with controls.27 We identified differentially expressed miRNAs in OUD, including Let7f that has been implicated in mechanisms of opioid tolerance,22 and the miRNA target genes and corresponding enriched pathways overlapped strongly in brain and blood tissues. These findings suggest that blood miRNAs may serve as biomarkers for predicting OUD risk.

Objectives

The overarching aim of this study is to identify bio-behavioural profiles associated with opioid use and pain outcomes following traumatic injury, by integrating clinical, genetic, epigenetic and psychosocial data. Specifically, we will identify early predictors that are longitudinally associated with persistent opioid use and chronic pain by (1) using pain and opioid-related indicators to define subgroups with distinct risk trajectories over time and (2) examining how discharge and early post-discharge factors predict membership in these risk trajectories. This approach will enhance our understanding of the complex factors contributing to long-term opioid-related problems and inform the development of targeted, personalised interventions for trauma patients at greatest risk.

Methods

Study design, setting, population

This prospective cohort study will enrol adult patients aged 18 years and older admitted to the inpatient Level 1 trauma service at the Memorial Hermann – Texas Medical Center. Hospital-based measures, including demographic information, clinical profile and blood samples, will be collected at the time of written consent, prior to discharge. Follow-up study visits will take place on day 7, and at 1, 3 and 6 months post-discharge. These visits will be conducted individually, either in-person at the outpatient clinic of the Center for Neurobehavioral Research on Addiction at the Texas Medical Center or at the participant’s home, if preferred.

Consenting patients will complete baseline measures at discharge, with the first follow-up visit occurring on day 7, allowing participants enough time to experience their opioid medication. All follow-up visits will be conducted in person to collect critical biological measures. Self-report measures will be administered through through Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant database used for collecting and storing data in clinical research.28

Hospital variables

Relevant patient demographic and clinical information, including age, sex, race, length of hospital stay and medications prescribed, will be extracted from the electronic medical record at the time of discharge. Injury severity will be rated according to the adapted Clavien-Dindo in trauma scale.29 Injury type will be classified based on the mechanism (eg, blunt, penetrating, burn) and location (eg, face, head, neck, abdomen).

Biological variables

Blood samples for miRNA sequencing, DNA genotyping and bioinformatics analysis will be collected at each time point. RNA will be extracted from plasma using miRNeasy micro kit by Qiagen. As a post-extraction quality control, the RNA will be quantified using Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher). Small RNA libraries will be generated using the Illumina small RNA protocol, sequenced on the Illumina Genome Analyzer NextSeq 2000, generating ~10–20 million 75 base pair reads per sample, and analysed using our published bioinformatics pipeline. Briefly, we will construct RNA-Seq libraries using the Takara SMARTer Universal Low Input RNA Kit designed to handle 2–100 ng of total RNA and retain strand-specific information. We will trim Illumina small RNA adapter sequences from the reads and discard reads of length below 10 nt or ending in homopolymers of length nine nucleotides or above. Total usable number of reads for each sample will be calculated and mapped to the miRbase using BLAST; the abundance of each expressed miRNA will be quantified as a fraction of the usable reads and expressed as parts per million. Polygenic Risk Scores (PRS) will be generated from all individuals after genotyping using the Infinium Global Screening Array-24 V.2.0 Kit (Illumina), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Raw genotyping data will be pre-processed in PLINK V.1.9 to exclude samples with high rates of genotype missingness (>10%) and filter out single nucleotide polymorphisms that are missing in a large proportion of subjects (locus missingness >10%), that have a minor allele frequency lower than 0.01 and that deviate from the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (p<1×10-−6). We will use BCFtools to fix strand orientation prior to imputation using the TOPMed Imputation Server and TOPMed reference panel (Version R2, 194 512 haplotypes). PRS for OUD will be calculated from the summary statistics of the largest discovery Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) for opioid dependence available at the time using a high-dimensional Bayesian regression framework,30 which is robust to varying genetic architectures and enables multivariate modelling of local linkage disequilibrium patterns.

Opioid-related variables

Total morphine milligram equivalents (MME) per day of hospital stay will be calculated based on the best available evidence converting opioids to oral MME.31 This measure will capture total inpatient opioid exposure, including intraoperative administration for patients undergoing surgery or non-surgical procedures. The original ORT17 18 is a 10-item self-administered questionnaire designed to assess predictors of opioid-related aberrant behaviour. Items, either checked ‘Yes’ or ‘No’, address areas related to family and personal history of an alcohol, prescription or illicit drug use disorder, age (between 16 and 45), history of preadolescent sexual abuse and history of psychological disorders or depression. A recent study revalidating the ORT found improved predictive validity when eliminating the history of preadolescent sexual abuse item and gender-specific weightings on each item.32 The current study will use this revised 9-item version of the ORT, with patients receiving scores of 0–2 (low risk) and ≥3 (high risk). Patients who endorse a history of opioid or other substance use on the ORT, prior to hospitalisation, will not be excluded from the study. Opioid use will be confirmed biologically through urinary drug tests via a standard National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) 5-Panel DrugTest Kit, which tests for opioid metabolites as well as cocaine, amphetamine, methamphetamine and tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) (Arham International, Inc., Greenville, South Carolina). The Timeline Follow-Back (TLFB) will be used to assess self-reported recent opioid use as well as other commonly used drugs of abuse. The Current Opioid Misuse Measure (COMM)33 is a valid and reliable self-report measure used to monitor aberrant opioid-related behaviour in chronic pain patients. The 17-item scale asks patients to report their behaviour over the past 30 days using a 5-point Likert-type rating scale (0=never, 4=very often). A clinical cut-off score of 9 or higher is generally considered suggestive of potential opioid misuse.

Pain variables

Subjective measures of pain will include the Defense and Veterans Pain Rating Scale,34 Brief Pain Inventory (BPI),35 Pain Vigilance and Awareness Questionnaire (PVAQ)36 and Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS).37 We will also measure pain detection and thresholds in a quantitative manner using the TSA2 Air Sensitivity Test by Medoc, an FDA-approved portable quantitative sensory testing device capable of delivering thermal stimulation. The TSA2 Air is associated with normative data from a multitude of populations, allowing for objective comparison of pain thresholds with the current target population. We will follow standardised procedures to assess thermal pain tolerance and thresholds using a 3×3 cm thermode that will deliver heat starting from 32°C up to 50°C in 1°C increments or cold starting from 20°C down to 0°C. Participants will be informed to press a mouse button immediately once sensing an uncomfortable sensation (eg, pinching or burning) for the threshold measurements or when the heat or cold is too painful to continue for the tolerance measurements. Resting-state EEG will be collected at all follow-up visits using the ANT neuro eego 24 to assess the association between changes in alpha frequency and prolonged pain sensitivity.38

Psychological variables

Health-related quality of life will be measured using the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System-29.39 The need for additional support and changes in health state will be assessed using the WHO Quality of Life-Brief.40 The PCL-541 will be used to screen for post-traumatic stress disorder based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria and monitor symptom changes throughout the study. Changes in sleep quality and patterns will be evaluated using the ISI.42 Suicidality risk will be assessed using the CSSR.43 In cases where responses indicate suicidal ideation, trained research therapists will assess risk and refer participants to appropriate medical support as needed.

Behavioural economic variables

Opioid demand is a behavioural economic measure of reward valuation, which will be measured using a hypothetical purchasing task assessing opioid pain pill purchasing and consumption as a function of increasing price. Drug demand assessed using hypothetical purchasing tasks has shown robust and reliable associations with clinically relevant aspects of drug use including drug use severity, frequency and treatment response.4448 Previous research from our group assessing opioid demand among trauma injury patients within a month after discharge was significantly associated with (1) hospital MME received, opioid use and refills, and self-report measures associated with pain49 and (2) less change in self-reported and opioid use 1 year post-discharge.50 Delay discounting is a behavioural economic measure assessing sensitivity to delayed consequences and is associated with drug use severity, treatment outcomes and various health-related behaviours.48 51 52 Additionally, greater delay discounting has been observed to predict overall pain severity among individuals with chronic pain53 and greater pain catastrophising contributing to poorer quality of life among individuals in SUD recovery reporting pain.54 In the current study, we will use the 5-Trial Delay Discounting Task.55

Compensation

Participants will receive $100, $150, $175 and $200 for completing the day 7, 1-month, 3-month and 6-month follow-up visits, respectively. In addition, at the 6-month visit, participants will receive a $50 completion bonus along with $25 for each of the previous follow-up visits completed (up to $75). Participants can earn up to $750 in total. Compensation for parking, bus fare or transportation will also be provided on an individual basis, averaging $32.50 per participant for each in-person follow-up visit.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

To identify subgroups of patients with varying patterns of pain and opioid use over time, we will apply Growth Mixture Modelling (GMM) using data collected at 1, 3 and 6 months post-discharge. This modelling will use both opioid-related measures (eg, urine drug tests, TLFB and COMM) and pain-related measures (specifically, BPI, PVAQ, PCS and TSA2 Air) as longitudinal indicators. In this stage, the pain measures are not treated as outcomes in the traditional sense, but rather as observed predictors that contribute to defining underlying latent subgroups based on co-evolving pain and opioid use profiles. Models with increasing numbers of classes (eg, 2, 3 or more) will be evaluated, and the optimal solution will be selected based on entropy and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

Once the subgroups are defined, they will be clinically interpreted and labelled based on key patterns (eg, persistent pain and opioid use vs low or resolving symptoms). In the next stage of analysis, the latent trajectory group membership, derived from GMM, becomes the primary outcome variable. To identify early predictors of membership in these trajectory-defined groups, we will use Bayesian Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression. Two models will be constructed: one using predictors collected at hospital discharge and another using predictors collected at 7 days post-discharge. These predictor sets may include clinical, genetic, epigenetic and psychosocial variables.

Together, this two-step analytic strategy—first using GMM to classify patient trajectories and then applying Bayesian LASSO to identify early predictors of those classifications—provides a robust and interpretable framework for modelling complex, multi-domain influences on persistent opioid use risk and chronic pain. Bayesian LASSO offers several advantages. By applying an L1 penalty, it shrinks less informative coefficients towards zero, thereby reducing overfitting and improving model interpretability. It effectively handles multicollinearity by selectively shrinking correlated predictors and enables probabilistic inference through the Bayesian framework. This includes the use of credible intervals, posterior distributions and posterior predictive checks to ensure that model predictions are consistent with the observed data and robust to overconfidence, particularly important in the presence of class imbalance. Moreover, the Bayesian framework allows for prior specification on the intercept, which is especially useful in our case given existing evidence suggesting that approximately 10% of patients who receive opioid prescriptions post-surgery will continue using them after 6 months. In our sample, this translates to roughly 18 individuals. By adjusting the prior accordingly, the model can account for the expected low prevalence, improving the accuracy and robustness of predictions. Model fit and performance will be evaluated using several metrics, including the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve, Precision-Recall Curve, F1 Score (harmonic mean of precision and recall) and Balanced Accuracy, which adjusts for class imbalance.

Sample size

Traditional power analyses are not directly applicable to Bayesian approaches due to their reliance on prior distributions and probabilistic interpretations. However, it is still important to justify the sample size with respect to the complexity of the models and the goals of the analysis. Our final sample comprises approximately 180 participants, which is adequate to support the planned two-step analytic strategy. For the GMM, simulation studies suggest that samples above 150 are generally sufficient to detect latent trajectory classes, particularly when using three to four time points and when entropy and model fit indices (eg, BIC) guide class selection. For the Bayesian LASSO regression, while there are no strict power thresholds, our use of shrinkage priors and regularisation techniques is specifically designed to address high-dimensional predictor spaces and small-to-moderate sample sizes. In line with recent recommendations for Bayesian model evaluation, we will begin with neutral priors and explore weakly informative priors (~Normal (μ=0, σ=1000)) to reflect initial uncertainty while allowing the data to drive posterior estimates. Decision making will be guided by a posterior probability threshold of ≥75%, approximating a Bayes factor of 3.0 in favour of the alternative hypothesis. Importantly, based on existing literature, approximately 10% of surgical patients receiving opioids are expected to continue use at 6 months, translating to about 18 participants in our sample. We incorporate this base rate into the prior specification of the intercept to improve model calibration, especially given potential class.

Discussion

Pain management after trauma is complex due to the diverse nature of the injuries and the heterogeneity of the patients’ and their clinical conditions, with opioids being a longstanding mainstay in managing trauma-related acute pain. However, the current landscape is shifting towards newer opioid prescribing practices aimed at reducing the risk of persistent opioid use and the development of OUD. This includes promoting multimodal pain regimens that incorporate low-dose and non-opioid analgesics;56 however, guidance is lacking on how to individually tailor post-trauma injury pain treatment based on specific biomarkers. This proposal represents a new step forward in developing a biological marker with sufficient sensitivity and specificity to predict risk levels in a highly vulnerable patient population. Its success is expected to lead to a subsequent biomarker-guided implementation study aimed at developing precision management for the treatment of post-trauma injury pain.

Several design decisions were considered in developing this protocol, starting with the range of measures to be collected. We opted for a comprehensive bio-behavioural-psychological approach, aiming to fully characterise the vulnerable phenotype and, through repeated longitudinal measurement, investigate how purported epigenetic patterns change over time in relation to opioid use and pain outcomes. This decision was weighed against potential measurement burden in a population of recently hospitalised patients experiencing acute pain. We anticipate that offering adequate compensation with escalating payments for completing all follow-up visits, along with transportation assistance and the option of home visits, will increase compliance rates. At the same time, we will closely monitor data collection fidelity to mitigate the risk of participants enrolling solely for compensation. The second decision was to target trauma-injury patients specifically, based on robust evidence, including our prior research,57 58 documenting the historically frequent use of opioids to control pain after injury, often serving as the initial exposure that leads to subsequent OUD.59 The extent to which our findings generalise to patient populations with pain aetiologies other than trauma will be important to establish in future research. A third decision related to follow-up duration, with several factors supporting a 6-month period rather than a longer duration. Definitions of persistent opioid use vary considerably, with no single, clinically meaningful definition agreed upon. A recent systematic literature search of definitions of persistent postoperative opioid use identified 29 unique definitions across 39 studies.60 The most stringent definitions involved a prescription for opioids filled 90 to 180 days after surgery. Furthermore, a 2024 retrospective cohort study on the likelihood of opioid continuation at 365 days following the initial opioid prescription showed that long-term opioid use for the overall sample (N=578 403) was 5.05% at 365 days,59 suggesting that fewer individuals are transitioning to long-term opioid use and that probabilities level off at 6 months (180 days). Moreover, a 6-month follow-up duration is expected to capture the transition from acute to chronic pain, which is defined as persistent pain lasting more than 3 months.61

Finally, we acknowledge that higher-than-expected dropout rates and/or a relatively small number of participants with persistent opioid use at 6 months may present significant challenge for our statistical analysis. We will implement robust Bayesian models that are well suited for handling small sample sizes and class imbalances. Additionally, including both subjective and objective pain measures as primary outcomes will introduce greater variability into the dataset, as these are numeric and continuous outcomes rather than categorical variables. This approach will provide richer information for both the predictive modelling in Aim 1 and the profiling of patient trajectories in Aims 2 and 3, ultimately enhancing the robustness of our analyses. By leveraging prior distributions and probabilistic interpretations, these models will enhance the reliability of our findings despite these limitations.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to characterise opioid-induced miRNA expression dynamics in trauma patients prescribed opioids at discharge. Based on the results, we will develop a high-throughput screening assay to predict the risk of persistent long-term opioid use, which we expect will be more effective than current tools. We anticipate that miRNAs will form the basis for precision medicine in the treatment of post-trauma injury and pain.
