How do young adults deal with suicidal ideation? A qualitative study of the experiences of stakeholders
Authors: Vasundharaa S. Nair, Miriyam Joseph, Neslin M. Ealias, Sharanya Mohan, Priya Sreedaran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Persistent suicidal ideation, self-harm behaviors, and suicide attempts constitute suicidality and are consistent predictors for future suicides. Research on what protects individuals from harming themselves is equivocal. While factors like impulsivity could increase risk of suicide, research is needed on what stops people from acting out on self-harm thoughts/suicidal ideation.Aim:To explore the help-seeking behaviors and other protective factors during periods of suicidality in individuals at risk of suicide.Method:This study aimed to explore help-seeking behavior and other protective factors that prevent or delay self-harm and suicide attempts during suicidality in individuals at risk. We used a qualitative study design and interviewed 15 participants comprising at-risk individuals, caregivers, and mental health professionals (MHPs). We used thematic analysis for deriving themes.Results:Themes in ‘at-risk individuals’ include mental state during suicidality, handling of oneself during suicidality, and seeking help from support systems. Themes from caregivers included handling of crises and the role of informal and formal support systems. Themes from MHPs included phenomenology of suicidality, handling of suicidality, help-seeking, and support systems.Conclusion:At-risk individuals handle suicidality by either managing themselves on their own or seeking help from informal sources and formal health systems. Caregivers appear to be crucial stakeholders in help-seeking during self-harm. The involvement of caregivers – in discussion with the at-risk individual – should be a necessary component of suicide risk management. Suicide prevention policies at institutional, regional, and national levels should include strategies to prevent burnout and other occupational health issues in MHPs.

INTRODUCTION

Suicidality refers to a state of persistent suicidal ideation and/or self-harm behaviors and suicide attempts.[1] Suicidality is a risk factor for future deaths due to suicide.[2] A significant proportion of individuals with suicidality do not seek or receive treatment for suicidality.[3] A significant proportion of these at-risk individuals believed that seeking help from formal health services would not be of use and preferred to handle the crises themselves.[3]

Prior to a medically serious suicide attempt, most individuals sought help from the family and friends as compared to healthcare providers.[4] Similarly, a systematic review showed that young people sought help from informal networks during times of suicidality.[5] Young adults preferred to handle suicidal thoughts by themselves.[6] Positive life events, good social support, spirituality, and ability to regulate emotions were protective for suicide.[7]

Risk factors in suicide prevention are categorized as static and dynamic. Static risk factors are historical and demographic factors like gender, religion, and comorbid psychiatric disorders. Static risk factors predict lifetime suicide occurrence. Dynamic risk factors are those that change over a short period of time. Other researchers have postulated that targeting dynamic risk factors could prevent suicide.[8] Suicide prevention is a stated priority in India. India’s National Suicide Prevention Strategy hopes for a 10% reduction in suicides by 2030.[9]

Findings on help-seeking and other protective factors during suicidality could assist in developing approaches for this. Integrating and embedding accounts of lived experiences of at-risk individuals and their caregivers could further refine these approaches.[10] Understanding helping-seeking behavior and factors that protect against suicidal behavior can bring insight into issues of relevance to young people and help focus on these aspects.[11] Qualitative approaches are suited for eliciting such view points as they allow for a thorough examination of people’s “lived experience”. In this background, we describe findings from a qualitative study that explored help-seeking behaviors and other protective factors that prevented or delayed self-harm and suicide attempts in individuals at risk.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

The study aim was to explore the help-seeking behaviors during periods of suicidality in individuals at risk of suicide.

The primary objectives were:

To explore the methods of delaying self-harm and/or suicide attempt in individuals at risk during active, intense suicidal ideation.To explore the methods of help seeking used in individuals at risk during active, intense suicidal ideation.

To explore the methods of delaying self-harm and/or suicide attempt in individuals at risk during active, intense suicidal ideation.

Setting

We conducted the study in a general hospital psychiatry setting of a private medical college in a metropolitan city. We approached individuals at risk, their caregivers, and mental health professionals (MHPs) for the study. We adopted a phenomenological approach (qualitative study design) and used inductive thematic analysis.[12] We recruited participants till we attained thematic saturation. Interview guides were developed on the basis of clinical experience of first, second, and last authors and also from literature review, which were pilot-tested (one sample from each group). The questions in the topic guide were according to the participants’ type and comprised of questions to identify and explore predisposing, precipitating, promotive, and protective factors pertaining to study objectives [Figures 1 and 2].

Theme

Model of care

We conducted this study after permission from the institutional ethics review board in line with principles of Declaration of Helsinki. The study followed the Braun and Clark guidelines for qualitative study.[12] We performed in-depth interviews which lasted from 30 minutes to 1 hour with all participants after receiving written informed consent. Data collection was done from April 2023 to June 2023. We conducted these audio-recorded interviews in out-patient and in-patient settings as per the participant’s convenience. The four authors interviewed the participants and prepared field notes, and all interviews were conducted with no other person present other than the author and participant. Data collected are stored on a cloud storage which is password-protected and encrypted and accessible only to the first author. The data shall be preserved for 5 years. All the interviews were transcribed manually and coded, and themes were generated inductively.

Author information

At the time of the study, the first, second, and last authors had around 20 combined years of clinical experience in dealing with persons with self-harm and suicide risk. The third and fourth authors were pursuing their postgraduate residency in psychiatry and were managing high-risk persons. The first, second, and last authors designed the study. All authors were acquainted with the participants to some extent before the interviews.

RESULTS

We approached 20 participants (seven individuals at risk, seven caregivers, and six MHPs) for the study. The duration of interviews ranged from 30 to 60 minutes. The first author transcribed the interviews verbatim and assigned codes in vivo after identifying units of meaning from direct words or expressions in the interview. After completing one interview in each category of participant, the first, second, and last authors together studied these preliminary codes and gathered similar codes into a theme after consensus. Subsequently, the first author continued to interview and analyze. There were periodic discussions between first, second, and last authors pertaining to themes. We stopped interviewing after achieving thematic saturation. We used an inductive approach for coding. As we reached thematic saturation at the fifth participant of each group, we stopped recruitment at 15 participants [Table 2, Table S1 and S2]. We categorized the themes according to the type of participant [Figure 1]. We have reported the study findings according to recommendations of SRQR and COREQ checklist.[13]

Person L, 27 years, female, homemaker

DISCUSSION

A substantial number of suicide attempts are linked to impulsivity and other dynamic factors.[14] Tackling these could help stop the progress from self-harm thoughts/suicidal ideation to death. Our study has described ways in which at-risk individuals handle their suicidal ideation. We propose that these findings could help develop strategies to face suicidality.

A key theme was the way at-risk individuals handled themselves during crises. Some preferred to manage on their own using methods like distraction, reflection, or reading religious texts. This is like another study which showed that most individuals used self-management strategies to handle the crisis.[15] Such approaches gave a sense of autonomy to those affected.[15]

Some at-risk individuals sought help from others during crises. In this regard, family was a prominent theme. When individuals disclosed their self-harm thoughts to family members, the former encountered validating as well as invalidating responses. Investigators have noted that some individuals perceived family responses to suicidality as worsening the suicidal ideation.[16] Family violence is a risk for suicidality.[17] The NCRB 2022 report documented that family problems were the most stated reasons for suicide.[18] Faulty family interactions, family-related emotional distress, and adverse childhood experiences with the lack of family support were the major reasons why at-risk individuals refused to count on family during suicidality.[19]

We noted that family was the first point of help seeking, similar to another review.[20] At-risk individuals disclosed suicidal ideation to the family first, who would persuade them to seek help.[20] We infer that family is a source of support as well as conflict during crises. Other investigators have endorsed this theme too.[21] We recommend the inclusion of families in suicide risk treatments, considering the comfort of at-risk individuals and their safety and ethical aspects. Researchers have described how to include and engage families in safety planning and bring hope to help at-risk individuals.[22]

We saw that some at-risk individuals preferred reaching out to friends. Other researchers have argued that models of the family should change to include friends and others from personal communities.[23] This is in line with a review that combined family and friends as facilitators who could be targeted for identification of emergent suicidality.[20]

In our study, some caregivers sought help from religion. A review found that while religion was protective for suicide, there were aspects that could increase the risk.[24] Other researchers have discussed incorporating this in treatments while keeping in mind the cultural sensitivities and inclinations.[25]

Participants had mixed experiences with MHPs. Some perceived MHPs as supportive, while others did not. A study showed that persons with severe mental illnesses were more likely to disclose suicidal ideation to their mental health providers as compared to the family.[26] Another study concluded that health care providers were well intentioned but ill-equipped during suicidality, with responses to at-risk individuals ranging from dismissal to over-the-top reactions.[27] Caregivers found the help seeking process from MHPs difficult due to unavailability of care and lack for support for their efforts.[28]

The MHPs agreed with the experiences of the other participants. MHPs mentioned how overwhelming it was to deal with acute suicidality. Researchers have concurred with these perspectives and opined that MHPs, especially those in suicide prevention, are suicide survivors similar to family and friends.[29]

We posit that due to the nature of their work, MHPs need help to cope with grief. Expert guidelines have recommended organization level strategies to improve work culture and individual level interventions to address burnout in medical professionals.[30] We recommend similar guidelines for MHPs.

All participant categories acknowledged the role of MHPs and formal mental/medical healthcare systems. There were also suggestions for increased helplines. Reviews have shown that helplines might assist at-risk individuals and their psychosocial stressors.[31] Individuals at risk for suicide experience double stigma due to their psychiatric disorders and suicidality.[27] This stigma might prevent them from seeking help and restrict recovery.[27] Gatekeeper training and improving general awareness are potential approaches for this, but their usefulness needs evaluation.[20]

MHPs asked for greater structural changes in suicide prevention. The National Mental Health Programme has committed to improving the availability of mental health resources.[32] The National Suicide Prevention Strategy 2022 reiterates this with clear implementation strategies.[9] We recommend that future suicide prevention policies take MHPs’ health also into consideration. We need an integrated system of intervention including awareness programs, crisis management, and gate keeper training strategies at individual, family, and community levels, with continued surveillance of suicidal behavior and attempts.[33] Hence, we are proposing this model for early intervention in suicide care [Figure 2].

Strengths and Limitations

We interviewed three different types of stakeholders for this study adding to study rigor, especially pertaining to triangulation of sources. Limitations include single centers which could bias the findings.

CONCLUSIONS

Self-management strategies and seeking help from others are the two main themes in how at-risk individuals deal with suicidality. Individuals seek help from informal and formal systems. The involvement of caregivers in discussion with the at-risk individual should be a necessary component of suicide risk management. Caring for MHPs should also be prioritized as part of suicide prevention.
