In-hospital cardiac arrest – A three-year Utstein- style report from a tertiary care centre in Poland
Authors: Tomasz Lonc, Mateusz Putowski, Grzegorz Niemczyk, Andrzej Morajda, Justyna Palmowska, Anna Kwinta, Tomasz Drygalski, Michał Terlecki, Paweł Krawczyk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

In-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) is a critical event resulting in high mortality, with incidence of 1.5 to 1.8 per 1000 hospital admissions and survival to discharge ranging from 15 % to 30 %, as reported by studies from various countries.1, 2, 3

Until now, there has been a paucity of systematically collected data on IHCA in Poland. Published information focuses on out-of-hospital cardiac arrests4 or administrative datasets. A retrospective analysis of the Polish General Hospital Morbidity Study in 2012 reported IHCA incidence of 2.9 per 1000 admissions and an overall mortality of 74 %.5 However, the study reported on ICD-10 coding only and lacked clinical data related to cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and outcomes. To the best of our knowledge, no study in Poland has systematically collected IHCA data using the Utstein resuscitation registry template − the standardized reporting framework that facilitates detailed analysis and international comparison.6

Rapid Response Teams (RRTs) represent a key component in the development of in-hospital care systems to reduce preventable IHCAs.7 In Poland, the widespread implementation of RRTs remains limited.8 Establishing RRT services offers a unique opportunity not only to improve individual patient care but also to enable systematic study of IHCAs in the context of an active Rapid Response System (RRS).9

Methods

Study design and settings

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of IHCAs at the University Hospital in Krakow (Poland), covering a 3-year period from January 1, 2022, through December 31, 2024. The University Hospital in Krakow is one of the largest hospitals in Poland, including 25 inpatient wards and providing tertiary referral services. The hospital’s RRT was established in 2020. The RRT consists of a four-person team: two physicians − a specialist and resident in anaesthesiology and intensive care and two paramedics. The team is activated by an emergency number (‘2222′) and is available 24 h a day. The standardized resuscitation carts with defibrillators, advanced life support medications and equipment are available on every ward throughout the hospital. The hospital staff undergo regular Immediate Life Support (ILS) initial and refresher trainings, delivered by RRT paramedics. The RRT responds to both medical emergencies and cardiac arrests, taking over leadership of resuscitation efforts upon arrival.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included all adult patients with IHCA in hospital wards as well as other non-monitored areas of the hospital (e.g. corridors, waiting rooms), for whom the RRT was activated, and CPR was initiated. Cardiac arrest was defined as cessation of cardiac mechanical activity with absent palpable pulse, requiring chest compressions and/or defibrillation in a hospital patient. Only the first cardiac arrest event for each patient was analysed. Cardiac arrests occurring in intensive care unit (ICU), operating room (OR) and Emergency Department (ED) were excluded from the analysis due their distinct clinical context and the direct presence of specialized teams in these areas. Patients who arrived at the hospital already in cardiac arrest were also excluded from the study.

Data collection

Data on each IHCA case were collected directly after the event by the RRT, using a standardized form based on the Utstein template for IHCA.6 These forms and supplemental information from the electronic medical records (Asseco Medical Management Solutions System) were reviewed retrospectively for this study. Recorded variables included patient demographics (age, sex, CPC at admission), event details (location and time of arrest, RRT activation and arrival, whether arrest was witnessed, initial cardiac arrest rhythm, chest compressions and airway management prior to RRT arrival, defibrillation and mechanical chest compression device use, adrenaline administered during CPR) and outcomes (ROSC, duration of resuscitation, survival at 30 days or hospital discharge, whichever occurred first). Neurological outcome at hospital discharge was assessed using CPC scores (1–2 considered as favourable, 3–4 unfavourable). If a survivor’s CPC or neurological examination was not documented, we recorded it as missing.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Jagiellonian University Medical College Ethics Committee (approval No: 118.6120.155.2023). Given the retrospective observational design and use of anonymized data prior to analysis, informed consent was waived.

Statistical analysis

Data were compiled in a research database (Excel 2021, Microsoft Corp) and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for macOS (v29, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test or by inspecting skewness with histograms. Descriptive results are presented as medians with first and third quartiles (Q1-Q3) for non-parametric continuous variables. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages.

Comparisons between groups (ROSC vs no ROSC) were performed using the Chi-square test for categorical variables and the Mann- Whitney U test for non-parametric continuous variables. We used logistic regression to explore factors associated with survival. The goodness-of-fit of the logistic model was evaluated with the Hosmer- Lemeshow test (>0.05 indicating adequate fit). The model included clinically relevant predictors and kept those that improved performance (Nagelkerke R2). The final multivariable model included sex, age, pre-arrest CPC (1–2 vs 3–4), initial rhythm (shockable vs non-shockable), CPR duration time and the use of mechanical chest compression. Results of the regression are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95 % confidence intervals. A two- tailed p- value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Missing data were managed using variable-specific denominators for descriptive statistics, and a complete-case approach for regression.

Results

Incidence and patient characteristics

Over the 3-year study period, a total of 460 IHCAs met inclusion criteria. This yields an approximate IHCA incidence of 2.5 per 1000 hospital admissions in our institution (181,644 admissions in 2022–2024). The median age of patients was 73 years (Q1-Q3; 64–81) and 269 (58.5 %) were male. Most patients (80.9 %) were CPC 1 or 2 on admission, prior to arrest. Most patients with IHCA (81.3 %) had emergency hospital admission via emergency medical service (58.9 %) or self- referral to ED (22.4 %). Only 86 (18.7 %) were scheduled admissions. The median hospitalization length before the IHCA event was 2 days (0–8 days). 51.3 % of arrests (236 cases) happened within the first 48 h of admission. Timing and location of IHCA

IHCA events were relatively evenly distributed over the 24-hour period with 56.7 % (n = 261) occurring during daytime hours (07:00–18:59) and 43.3 % (n = 199) during nighttime (19:00–06:59). ROSC was significantly more frequent during daytime IHCAs (62.5 % vs 51.3 %; p = 0.015). No statistically significant difference was found in response time between daytime and nighttime hours (respectively 3 vs 4 min, p = 0.37).

336 IHCAs (73 %) occurred on medical wards, whereas 117 (25.4 %) took place on surgical wards. Additionally, 7 events (1.5 %) took place in non– ward locations such as hospital corridors or waiting areas. The cardiac catheterization laboratory and cardiology unit were among those with the highest numbers of IHCA, together accounting for nearly one- third of all cases (31.9 %).

Management of IHCA and RRT performance

The median RRT response time (from ‘2222′ call to the team’s arrival at the patient) was 4 min (Q1-Q3; 3–4 min). In 13.3 % of IHCA (n = 61), RRT was already present and witnessed IHCA. Chest compressions were initiated before the arrival of the RRT in 95.2 % of cases (n = 380). In 394 cases (85.6 %), some form of airway management was provided: bag-valve-mask ventilation in 42.4 % (n = 195), a supraglottic airway device (SAD) had been placed in 23 % (n = 106) or intubation in 16.1 % (74). In 4.1 % (n = 19) of cases, the patient was already intubated (or had a tracheostomy) at the time of arrest. 14.4 % of IHCA (n = 66) had no ventilation provided before the RRT arrived, typically where the arrest was recognized just before or at the RRT arrival. Notably, patients who achieved ROSC were significantly more likely to have had an advanced airway (SAD) placed before RRT arrival compared to those without ROSC (28.1 % vs 18.2 %; p = 0.049).

A mechanical chest compression device (LUCAS TM 3, Stryker) was deployed in 51.5 % of resuscitations (n = 237). Its use was significantly less frequent among patients with ROSC (45.5 % vs 57.2 %; p = 0.012). Defibrillation was performed at some point in 25.2 % of the arrests (n = 116). Regarding medications, nearly every patient (96.5 %, n = 444) received intravenous adrenaline during resuscitation. The median dose of adrenaline was not recorded, but most cases received multiple 1 mg doses according to ERC guidelines.10

The overall median duration of CPR was 20 min (Q1-Q3; 10–27 min) from onset of chest compressions to either ROSC or termination of resuscitation. In patients with ROSC, the median CPR duration was shorter −11 min (vs 24 min in no-ROSC patients; p < 0.001).

Non– shockable rhythms were predominant among the initial rhythms recorded during IHCA. As shown in Table 2, 407 of 460 patients (88.5 %) had a non– shockable initial rhythm. Pulseless electrical activity (PEA) was the most common (55 %), followed by asystole (33.5 %). Only 53 patients (11.5 %) presented with a shockable rhythm (ventricular fibrillation in 10.2 % and pulseless ventricular tachycardia in 1.3 %). Outcomes

Out of 460 IHCA events, 224 patients had ROSC, yielding an initial resuscitation success rate of 48.7 %. The 30-day survival rate was 29.9 % of ROSC patients, which corresponds to 14.6 % of the entire IHCA cohort (67 out of 460). Of the 67 patients who survived to 30-day or were discharged from hospital, 40 (66.7 %) had a good neurological outcome (CPC 1 or 2) and 20 (33.3 %) had a poor neurological outcome (CPC 3–4). For 7 survivors, the precise CPC could not be determined due to missing data (inconsistent neurological status documentation). Factors associated with survival

Increasing age (OR 0.97, 95 % CI 0.96–0.99, p = 0.003) and longer duration of CPR (OR 0.9, 95 % CI 0.87–0.93; p < 0.001) were significantly associated with decreased odds of survival. The presence of shockable initial rhythm emerged as a strong positive predictor (OR 2.9, 95 % CI 1.32–6.18; p = 0.008). Neither sex, pre-arrest CPC 3–4, nor the use of mechanical chest compression were significantly associated with survival in this model. Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive overview of IHCA outcomes from Poland using standardized Utstein definitions and forms. The incidence of IHCA was approximately 2.5 per 1000 admissions. While this rate appears higher than those reported in other European studies (1.5–1.8 per 1000 admissions), direct comparison is challenging due to varying proportions of reported IHCAs occurring in ED, which range from 4 % to 14 % in published data1, 2, 3. Our hospital includes the largest ED in the region, which may contribute to these differences. Patients in our cohort were elderly (median 73 years), consistent with previous studies reporting median ages between 72 and 77 years1, 2, 3, 9. We observed that over half of arrests occurred up to 48 h after admission. This finding aligns with reports showing peak incidence on day 1 in Italy,3 a median time to IHCA of 2 days in the UK1 and 3 days in settings with well- established RRS.9

Our hospital’s ROSC rate of 48.7 % is comparable to other international reports, being lower than the rates observed in Finland, Australia and Denmark2, 9, but notably higher than the 35.7 % reported in the Italian multicentre study.3 In settings with mature RRS, higher ROSC rates may reflect better response, early identification and trigger for patient deterioration. Conversely, the lower ROSC rate in our study may be partially explained by healthcare consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic (not present in the referenced studies conducted between 2011–2013,1 2012–2014,3 2017 to 20182 and limited use of formal do-not-attempt resuscitation (DNAR) orders, which may be related with resuscitation attempts in patients with already present poor prognosis and limited chances of recovery.

In our study, patients who received a supraglottic airway device prior to RRT arrival were significantly more likely to achieve ROSC. One possible explanation is that early insertion of an SAD allows for more effective ventilation and oxygenation during the initial critical phase of resuscitation. In contrast, bag-mask ventilation, especially in acute settings, may be technically challenging for inexperienced providers and often suboptimal, potentially leading to insufficient oxygen delivery.

The median CPR duration in our cohort (24 min; IQR 20–30) aligns with findings from the registry observational study,11 reporting improved survival at hospitals with longer resuscitation efforts (median 25 min compared to 16 min). Okubo et al.12 recently demonstrated that survival and favourable neurological outcomes decline sharply beyond 32–39 min of CPR. Our durations remained below these futility thresholds, which may have positively influenced outcomes without exceeding clinically meaningful limits.

Survival to 30 days or hospital discharge (14.3 %) matches closely with Italy’s 14.8 %,3 however is lower than the rates observed in national registries from the UK (18.4 %)1 or Denmark (27.8 %).2 While our findings are within the overall range reported in literature, they appear slightly below the average survival rates. One of the key contributing factors may be the distribution of initial shockable rhythms. In our cohort, non-shockable rhythms accounted for almost 90 %, compared to 70–80 % in other studies.1, 2, 3 This is clinically relevant, as these rhythms typically reflect underlying either non-cardiac or irreversible conditions, consistently associated with poorer outcomes.

One area of concern highlighted by our data is the neurological outcome of survivors. Only approximately 66 % of our survivors had a good neurological outcome (CPC 1–2) at 30- day or discharge, which is considerably lower than the approximately 90 % reported in the Italian IHCA registry.3 Similar results are presented in a study of hospitals with mature RRTs in Finland and Australia, where 95 % of patients were discharged with CPC 1–2.9

It is important to note that a substantial group of patients (n = 67, 14.6 %) already had significant neurological impairment (CPC 3–4) prior to the cardiac arrest. Among them, 8 patients survived to hospital discharge or day 30. If this subgroup were excluded from the analysis, the proportion of survivors with a favourable neurological outcome would increase from 66 % to 77 %. Notably, patients with CPC 3–4 before IHCA were significantly less likely to achieve ROSC (10.4 % vs 19.8 %), further underscoring the poor prognosis in this population.

The outcomes of IHCA cannot be discussed in isolation from the ethical and legal context, particularly regarding end-of-life decisions and the limitation of futile therapy. While in many Western countries such decisions are often supported by established laws13 and public acceptance, the situation in Poland is more nuanced. Despite the existence of medical society guidelines14, 15 and even recent amendments to the Polish Code of Medical Ethics16 that clearly outline the approach to withholding or withdrawing therapy in cases of irreversible disease processes, this remains a highly sensitive and challenging area in clinical practice. Public understanding and acceptance of limiting futile therapy are still evolving and societal resistance can sometimes make these decisions particularly difficult for healthcare providers. Further progress is needed, particularly in the form of public education and awareness campaigns to promote the concept of dignified end-of-life care and better align societal expectations with medical realities.

Implications for practice

The high incidence of IHCA within 48 h of admission and the presence of warning signs in many cases (the RRT had been activated prior to 13.3 % of arrests) highlight the critical importance of improving early recognition of patient deterioration. Additionally, our findings indicate that arrests occurring during daytime hours (07:00–18:59) were significantly more likely to result in ROSC compared to those at night (53.6 % vs 42.2 %) likely reflecting more robust staffing and monitoring during regular working hours. Implementing track-and-trigger systems may help prevent some arrests, especially those occurring in the early phase of hospitalization.

Another important implication is the clear value of a structured and matured RRS, which goes beyond leading the resuscitation efforts. In our institution, the RRT is responsible not only for providing CPR but also for coordinating a broad set of systemic interventions. These include a universal RRT trigger, standardized clinical response algorithms in case of patient deterioration and a structured, simulation-based training program in resuscitation skills for healthcare providers in our hospital.

Limitations

This retrospective study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Data were collected after each in-hospital resuscitation managed by the RRT, excluding cases in the ICU, OR, and ED, due to their distinct contexts and specialist teams often managing IHCA without RRT. We did not collect data on patients’ comorbidities. Missing data were minimal, mainly involving CPC scores. As a large single-centre study, generalizability may be limited, particularly to smaller hospitals without established RRTs, which may influence outcomes.

Strengths

Despite limitations, this is the first Polish Utstein-based report on IHCA, not only providing high-quality data on IHCA management and outcome but also allowing for extensive comparison. We provide a large sample and robust data set of IHCA related to the introduction of the RRS. Moreover, highlighting the IHCA outcomes and ethical context of end-of-life decisions provides important evidence highly relevant for clinicians and policymakers in Poland.

Conclusions

The first Utstein-style report of IHCAs in Poland demonstrates that incidence and survival rates at a large tertiary care centre are comparable to those reported internationally. The presence of a dedicated RRT and standardized reporting allowed us to identify important factors influencing outcomes, which can inform quality improvement efforts. Many survivors had poor neurological outcomes, reflecting challenges in ethical decision-making and post-resuscitation care. Our findings highlight an urgent need for structured national approaches to establish IHCA registries for continuous data collection and audit. By sharing these results, we aim to foster better preparedness for IHCA and ultimately improve outcomes with good quality of life for the survivors.
