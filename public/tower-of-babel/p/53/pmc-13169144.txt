Addressing Food Insecurity in Pediatric Primary Care: Implementation of a Fresh Produce Delivery Pilot
Authors: Michelle C. Gorecki, Esineya N. Chigaga, Kayla Hall, Jenny Ciolino, Rachel Uhrig, Cynthia White, Christina Brown, Michael Truitt, Melissa D. Klein, Adrienne W. Henize, Kristen A. Copeland, Mary Carol Burkhardt, Chidiogo U. Anyigbo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Food insecurity negatively impacts young children’s health and development. Our pediatric primary care practice partnered with a local food bank to connect families experiencing food insecurity to a fresh produce home delivery program, using quality improvement (QI) methodology.Methods:Our multidisciplinary team developed a referral pathway from the pediatric practice to the food bank’s produce delivery program. The medical team and the food bank completed a QI training course. Our key driver diagram informed interventions, including electronic health record referrals, clinical staff educational initiatives, office signage promoting the program, and refinement of the produce delivery program by the food bank. Our primary outcome measure was referring more than 3% of all patients aged 5 years or younger seen in primary care to the produce delivery program, with a goal of 200 families within 6 months. Our process measure (the food bank’s primary outcome measure) was the number of days from referral to first delivery.Results:Over 6 months, we referred 192 families to the produce delivery program. Our initial outcome measure referral rate was 5% of all patients seen in primary care each week, which later adjusted to a sustainability rate of 3.5%. The food bank improved its delivery approach, reducing the mean days to first delivery from a peak of 74 days to 21 days.Conclusions:Addressing food insecurity in outpatient pediatrics requires a multifaceted approach, including connecting families to community-based resources. A shared commitment to QI by the primary care practice and the partner food bank ensured the success of this fresh produce home delivery pilot program.

INTRODUCTION

Food insecurity (FI) affected nearly 1 in 5 children in the United States in 2023.1 Children experiencing FI have negative health and developmental outcomes.2–7 Children facing FI consume less produce.8 This nutrition insecurity, meaning inadequate access to health-promoting foods,9 has further negative health consequences. Socially and/or economically marginalized communities experience significant disparities in the prevalence of FI.1 These disparities are exacerbated by historical and current disinvestment, such as racist practices that contributed to the creation of food deserts.10,11

The American Academy of Pediatrics and the Academic Pediatric Association recommend addressing FI during healthcare visits.12,13 Options to address FI include in-office food pantries,14,15 connection to federal nutrition benefits,16 produce prescriptions,17,18 and food delivery.19–22 One study found that caregivers prefer home delivery over other methods of food distribution.23 Prior pilot studies found that produce home delivery with healthcare-based referral is feasible, reduces FI, and may improve diet quality, although sustainability remains a concern.20,21 Using a long-standing clinical-community partnership with our local food bank, we aimed to connect families with young children to our partner food bank’s fresh produce home delivery program to address FI and increase the availability of fresh produce for families. Our practice has a high need for this program: the neighborhood surrounding our practice is an urban food desert,10 with 78% of children living in poverty and 31% of families reporting FI.24,25 We used quality improvement (QI) methodology to pilot and refine our referral pathway.26 (See figure 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays a detailed process map of the produce delivery referral pathway, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A708.) The pediatric practice and partner food bank created separate, and aligned, QI interventions. Following capacity discussions with the food bank, we identified our primary aim: connect 200 families with children aged 5 years or younger who endorsed FI and transportation barriers to the produce delivery program from August 2023 to February 2024. Referring more than 3% of all families with children aged 5 years or younger seen in primary care to the program could achieve this goal.

METHODS

Setting

Our pediatric primary care office, located in a mid-sized city in the Midwestern United States, serves as the medical home for 2,300 patients aged 5 years or younger. The majority (84%) of our patients are publicly insured; 82% self-identify as Black, 13% White, and 4% Hispanic. Approximately 5% speak a language other than English. The surrounding neighborhood is an urban food desert,10,24 created by disinvestment.

Our primary care practice has collaborated with our local food bank for over a decade on several community-based clinical partnerships. Our partnership began with the distribution of infant formula (2010), and later evolved to include shelf-stable food distributed via an on-site food pantry (2017).14 The food bank began produce home deliveries in 2020 to increase access to fresh produce for homebound seniors aged 65 years and older during the Coronavirus Disease 2019 pandemic. Each produce box contains approximately 20 pounds of donated fresh produce. A typical box contains a mix of seasonal fresh produce, along with potatoes, onions, apples/oranges, and a leafy green. In early 2023, the food bank began seeking additional referral sources, specifically those serving children, as all previous referrals had been from adult healthcare settings. Our practice staff were enthusiastic about the opportunity to connect families to an additional community resource. The Cincinnati Children’s institutional review board deemed this project exempt from institutional review board oversight for human subjects research.

Planning and Workflow Design

To plan the referral pathway, we assembled a multidisciplinary team with the practice’s medical director, a nurse, a medical assistant, and the food bank’s produce delivery program manager. The multidisciplinary team completed a QI training course together.26 The course teaches foundational QI skills, and teams complete a narrow-scope project. First, the team created a detailed process map27 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays a detailed process map of the produce delivery referral pathway, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A708), ensuring that both partners fully understood the other’s workflow and that any planned interventions would be feasible. In brief, primary care staff screened patients for eligibility, which included: (1) child aged 5 years or younger, (2) FI, (3) reported transportation barrier to accessing fresh produce, and (4) stable address in the delivery zone (our local county). We elected to limit the referral to children 5 years or younger because: (1) this is a critical developmental period, (2) frequent pediatric visits promote access to referral, (3) limited access to school-based nutrition programs for this age group, and (4) the food bank’s capacity to address referrals promptly.

FI was identified either through routine electronic health-related social needs (HRSN) screening with the Hunger Vital Sign,28,29 or during conversations with families during clinical visits. Families who agreed to the referral completed a release of information. Primary care staff then placed a referral order in the electronic health record (EHR). Clinical staff emailed the referral to the food bank, who confirmed receipt. Food bank volunteers packed boxes daily. A corporate delivery service delivered boxes to households. Families received a text message when their box was en route and upon delivery. Families received boxes every 2 weeks, unless a family opted out or moved outside the delivery zone. The food bank monitored disenrollment, keeping notes on any families removed from the delivery list. During the planning phase, the team created a simplified failure mode and effects analysis that informed the key driver diagram (Fig. 1) and interventions.27

Interventions and Iterative QI

The team planned a series of Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) ramp cycles27 and created separate PDSA ramps for the primary care practice and the food bank. Initial primary care staff PDSA ramps started in August 2023 and included a small series of test referrals sent by 1 and then 2 physicians, totaling 11 referrals during 4 weeks. Once the food bank confirmed receipt and enrollment of the initial referrals, the primary care office began an education series. The multidisciplinary QI team planned PDSA cycles to educate primary care staff at daily huddles, educating on how the produce delivery program works, its referral criteria, and the steps to complete a referral. The QI team kept a list of staff who learned of the program, ensuring all staff members were aware of the program. To increase family buy-in, we displayed patient-facing signs in examination rooms advertising a free fresh produce home delivery program for families with children 5 years or younger, encouraging families to ask if they qualify.

We created a Pareto chart 3 months after the pilot began to evaluate our primary failure modes and identify which interventions to prioritize. (See figure 2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the Pareto chart showing failure modes of the produce delivery program, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A709.) We found 2 predominant issues: (1) incorrect or incomplete addresses, which led to several conversations between the food bank and primary care staff around each family with an inaccurate address (15.6% of failure modes), and (2) a growing waitlist leading to delayed first box delivery (80.5% of failure modes). To resolve delivery address problems, we simplified the EHR order only to include the primary address (rather than pulling in all addresses into the order), which subsequently reduced address errors.

The second issue, the rising number of days to the first produce box delivery, began when referrals exceeded the weekly box building capacity, and the food bank added any newly referred families to the waitlist. The food bank planned PDSAs focused on testing interventions to increase their capacity to pack and deliver boxes. The food bank tested the expansion of volunteer produce box packing shifts, adding afternoon timeslots to pack boxes the day before delivery. The food bank deployed new volunteer recruitment strategies, including asking current volunteers to recruit friends and announcing opportunities through our medical center’s volunteer services. To maximize capacity, the food bank restructured its produce delivery schedule to occur every other week for all locations. This change affected referrals placed by other healthcare settings that previously had weekly deliveries.

Measures

Our primary outcome measure was referring more than 3% of all patients aged 5 years or younger seen in primary care to the produce delivery program, with the goal of enrolling 200 families from August 2023 to February 2024. We measured the weekly number of EHR referrals divided by all patient visits 5 years or younger. We chose all patients 5 years or younger as the primary outcome denominator, given we could not track verbally disclosed FI. As a process measure (the primary outcome measure for the food bank), we monitored the number of days from referral to the first produce box delivery, with a goal of 21 days. Twenty-one days was selected as a reasonable target after completing the detailed process map during the planning phase. Clinical staff used the food bank’s weekly delivery list to confirm that patients had started receiving deliveries and to calculate the number of days between referral and first delivery. Days to first delivery was the mean number of days between referral and the first produce box delivery among a week of referrals. As a balancing measure, we tracked the number of families of patients of all ages using the practice’s on-site food pantry monthly to assess if a new option to address FI changed the use of existing resources. We extended the balancing measure tracking beyond the QI study period (February 2024) because we saw early trends toward significance.

As an additional process measure, we tracked the percentage of patients 5 years or younger who disclosed FI on our practice’s HRSN screen,28,29 as an imperfect measure of potentially eligible families.

Analysis

We plotted statistical process control charts for process, outcome, and balancing measures to distinguish between common-cause and special-cause variation over time. Common-cause variation is the expected variation that is inherent to a process, whereas special-cause variation is due to a specific intervention and not part of a process.27 We used established rules to determine centerline shifts.27

RESULTS

From August 2023 through February 2024, the pediatric primary care practice referred 192 families to the produce delivery program, close our goal of 200. Our primary outcome measure increased rapidly following the launch of primary care staff education (Fig. 2), with an associated centerline shift to 5% of patients referred, equivalent to a mean of 9 new referrals per week. During the sustainability phase, which emerged shortly after the completion of our QI training course in December 2023, we observed a plateau in the mean percentage to 3.5%, exceeding our 3% goal.

Outcome measure: percent of all patients 5 years or younger referred to produce delivery program.

Our process measure (the primary outcome measure for the food bank) of days to first delivery initially rose from 30 to 74 days with the creation of a waitlist (Fig. 3). With the program changes and updated delivery schedule, all families moved off the waitlist within 2 weeks, 5 months into the QI project. Subsequently, the process measure A decreased significantly from a mean of 74 days to the target mean of 21 days. To date, there is no waitlist.

Process measure: days from referral to first produce box delivery.

As a balancing measure, we observed an increase in monthly primary care on-site food pantry usage following the first produce delivery referrals (Fig. 4). Before the produce delivery pilot, a mean of 35 families used the pantry each month, and this increased to a mean of 68 families the month we introduced the produce delivery referral, representing an more than 90% increase. This increase sustained after the QI training course ended. Notably, in April 2023, before the produce delivery program pilot, we observed a trend of increasing numbers of families using the on-site pantry per month, coinciding with the end of the expanded Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program benefits in March 2023.

Balancing measure: number of families served by primary care on-site food pantry. SNAP, Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program.

For the additional process measures: before the produce delivery pilot, a mean of 14% of patients aged 5 years or younger seen in our practice disclosed FI on the electronic screen (Fig. 5). Three weeks after we displayed signs advertising referral in patient rooms, there was a centerline shift to a mean of 19% of families disclosing FI, indicating special-cause variation. We also monitored program disenrollment. During the study period, 2 families disenrolled: one due to housing instability and another due to dissatisfaction with the program.

DISCUSSION

Through collaboration and careful planning, our clinical-community partnership successfully connected approximately 200 families to a fresh produce home delivery program, representing approximately 8% of all patients 5 years or younger we serve. We quickly reached our outcome goal, referring 5% of patients and then sustaining a rate of 3.5% of all patients 5 years or younger seen to the program, demonstrating our commitment to the work. This innovative pilot involved the simultaneous application of QI methodology by 2 partners, creating separate and aligned QI projects for each partner. The produce delivery program referral facilitators included primary care staff engagement and development of a simple and quick referral process. Additional keys to success included having primary care and food bank staff complete QI training together and the creation of a detailed process map for both organizations, which ensured that planned interventions were feasible. Training together ensured the partners had a shared language of QI, preventing a common source of communication breakdown when working across sectors. These methods highlight another approach for organizations to provide services collaboratively to a shared population.30

The improvement in the process measure, days to first produce a box delivery, highlights the importance of the food bank having its own QI goals, emphasizing the depth of this clinical-community partnership. The pediatric primary care practice provided data and analytic support as we tracked data and reported back to our partner food bank. Due to our collaboration, the food bank made substantive improvements to the operation of the entire program. By adjusting the delivery schedule and reorganizing volunteer shifts, the food bank successfully increased program capacity, reduced the time to first delivery, and eliminated the waiting list. The standardization of the delivery approach, which occurred every 2 weeks, led to the most substantial improvements. The sum of the program changes allowed us to sustain the goal of 21 days to first delivery, ensuring families have timely access to nutritious produce. The QI team planned not to exceed our partner food bank’s capacity; however, there was an initial surge of referrals. When the QI team noticed a growing waitlist, we continued to make referrals and modified the program, rather than pausing them. We encourage others planning similar work to continuously monitor and evaluate program capacity.31

We monitored primary care on-site food pantry usage as a balancing measure to understand if staff used existing resources differently after introducing a new program to address FI. Although we observed some increase in on-site pantry usage following the end of expanded Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program benefits in March 2023, we nonetheless found a substantial centerline shift with increased usage after the produce delivery referral program began. This result suggests that addressing FI through offering produce delivery encourages primary care staff and families to engage in more meaningful conversations around FI and use all available resources. It is also possible that increased disclosure of FI through electronic screening contributed to the increased use of on-site food pantries (as we documented with other HRSN in prior work).29 We hypothesize that patient-facing signs may reduce stigma, encouraging families to disclose FI and inquire about the produce delivery program, as families may believe their needs will be addressed rather than fearing negative repercussions.32,33 Furthermore, the signs advertising the program may have sparked conversations between families and staff, while also serving as reminders for staff to address FI and offer the program. Given that a prior study of an anonymous survey of our patients found an FI rate of 31%, the current FI rates likely represent an underreporting.25 In summary, establishing a new program to address FI in our pediatric primary care practice resulted in increased identification of FI and increased use of our existing on-site food pantry.

Our referral program enabled innovative problem-solving to mitigate barriers, as solutions required collaboration between partners. Resolving the challenges of a growing waitlist and delivery address errors was only possible through close communication between partners and consistent monitoring of process measures, especially the days-to-first-delivery measure. Additionally, the primary care practice developed a system that includes education about the referral process as part of staff onboarding, enabling the sustainability of the program. We hypothesize that the sustainability rate of 3.5% is lower than the initial 5% launch rate in part due to repeat patients/siblings who were previously referred.

This study has several limitations. First, our outcome measure is the percentage of patients referred, which does not reflect patient health outcomes, family perspective, or produce use. We observed that a small portion of families referred to the produce delivery program denied FI on the HRSN screen, despite endorsing FI through conversations during the clinical visit. No HRSN screening tool is 100% sensitive,34 which highlights the importance of clinicians discussing HRSN with all families, independent of their responses to screening, because some will endorse FI only through conversation. Second, given that retailers donated the produce, the food bank cannot control the quality of the produce, and it is possible that families may not be able to use all the produce before it spoils. Third, we are unable to account for repeat patients/families in the outcome measure’s denominator or for the weekly variation in the number of families experiencing FI or verbally identified FI. Fourth, the intervention took place at 1 site with an established relationship with the food bank, and for families with children aged 5 years or younger; therefore, some findings may not be generalizable. Also, families experiencing housing instability without a stable delivery address, a common HRSN, were unable to participate. Finally, although delivery is an important intervention given that transportation is a common barrier to food access,35 we rely on the food bank’s delivery list as a proxy for confirmation of receipt of the produce boxes, rather than confirmation from families.

Home produce delivery is a novel approach to addressing FI, with few pediatric-focused produce delivery programs described in the literature.17,20 More rigorous studies on child health outcomes among those enrolled in produce delivery programs are needed. Next steps include increasing referrals by including patients older than 5 years and monitoring the capacity of our food bank partner. We are currently conducting semistructured interviews to formally explore family experiences with the program. Anecdotally, families have provided positive feedback on the program.

CONCLUSIONS

Addressing FI in early childhood is critical in pediatrics. One feasible part of a multifaceted approach to addressing food and nutrition insecurity can include a home delivery program for fresh produce. Through the partnership of our pediatric practice and local food bank, we connected approximately 200 families experiencing FI to the produce delivery program within 6 months, using QI methodology. We observed increased disclosure of FI in our practice, accompanied by increased use of our on-site food pantry, signaling a positive spillover effect. The produce delivery program is unique in its promotion of both food and nutrition security, as well as the use of QI methods by 2 partners working collaboratively.
