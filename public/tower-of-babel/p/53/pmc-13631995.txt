Chronic disease comorbidity and its impact on healthcare utilization among older adults in China: a cross-sectional and longitudinal analysis
Authors: Houyu Chen, Xinru Huang, Yu Xiang, Ru Meng, Muwei Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe comorbidity rate of chronic diseases among the elderly in China is rising steadily, significantly influencing their healthcare utilization. This study aims to identify latent trajectories of chronic disease accumulation in the elderly and assess the impact of comorbidity and changes in the number of chronic diseases on outpatient and inpatient service use, providing clues for precise intervention and optimization of medical resource allocation.MethodsData from five waves (2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, 2020) of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) were utilized. Cross-sectional analysis (2020, n = 8,316, aged ≥ 60) employed multivariable logistic regression and zero-inflated negative binomial regression to assess comorbidity’s impact. Longitudinal analysis (n = 1,178, followed from 2011 to 2020, aged ≥ 60) used latent class growth model (LCGM) to identify chronic disease number trajectories. Multivariable logistic regression evaluated the dynamic impact of change in the number of diseases on service use. Binary and ordered logistic regressions analyzed baseline disease count’s effect on future increases.ResultsThe comorbidity rate among Chinese elderly (≥ 60) was 66.7%. Comorbidity and increasing disease numbers significantly raised the risk and frequency of outpatient and inpatient service utilization. Latent class growth model identified four distinct chronic disease development trajectories: relative stabilized trajectory, low-increment trajectory, medium-increment trajectory, and high-increment trajectory, all showing varying degrees of upward trend. Individuals in higher increment trajectories faced significantly greater risks of increased healthcare utilization. Higher baseline disease counts also predicted greater risk of accumulating additional diseases.ConclusionsChronic disease comorbidity is highly prevalent and increasing among Chinese elderly. Both the number of chronic diseases and their progression significantly drive higher healthcare utilization and demand. There are also significant differences in the development of the number of chronic diseases among the population. The findings stress dynamic risk prevention, identifying high-risk populations for coordinated, proactive health management and precise resource allocation to relieve system strain from comorbidities.Keywords: Chronic disease, Comorbidity, Elderly, Healthcare utilization

Background

Chronic comorbidity refers to a person suffering from two or more chronic diseases simultaneously [1]. It is not only an important influencing factor on the physical health and quality of life of the elderly, but also significantly increases the utilization of medical services and medical costs [2, 3], posing severe challenges to improving medical quality and cost control work [4].Chronic comorbidities have become one of the biggest challenges in the current and future decades of global health services [1, 5], and their high prevalence, difficult to control development trends, and heavy health and economic burdens need to be taken seriously.

In recent years, the world is facing an increasingly serious problem of population aging, and the change in age structure has made the issue of comorbidities among the elderly more prominent. As a result, the incidence of chronic diseases has significantly increased, and comorbidities are becoming increasingly common among the elderly population [6, 7]. And a higher number of illnesses and specific combination patterns often accelerate the deterioration of bodily functions [8, 9]. The impact of comorbidities on individual health status exceeds the simple superposition of the effects of a single disease, and this impact may further deteriorate due to the persistent imbalance between the burden of disease treatment and patients’ coping abilities, as well as limited medical resources, forming a vicious cycle of deteriorating patient health status [6, 10].The high incidence of illness and deteriorating health conditions have brought additional health service demands to the elderly. Multiple studies have shown that an increase in the number of chronic diseases can lead to a significant increase in the utilization of health services [4, 7, 11–13].

Although there have been numerous studies on the utilization of health services by comorbid populations and the relationship between comorbidities and health service utilization, there are still significant research gaps that need to be addressed. Existing research has mostly focused on the prevalence of specific diseases or the coexistence of multiple diseases [14, 15], while some studies have delved into the combination patterns of comorbidities [16].However, there is a lack of relevant research on the dynamic development of the number of chronic diseases, especially on the identification of the development trajectory of different disease types and their impact on the utilization of health services. In addition, within the Chinese context, previous studies have mainly focused on cross-sectional studies. It is therefore necessary to incorporate longitudinal studies to enhance the explanatory power of the findings and more effectively examine the correlation between these variables.

Therefore, this study aims to use representative national data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study from 2011 to 2020 to sort out the current status and development of chronic disease comorbidities among the elderly, explore the relationship between the number and development of chronic diseases and the utilization of health services (outpatient and inpatient), and identify and divide the trajectory of chronic disease development. This study can not only further enrich the research results in related fields, but also provide theoretical basis for the government and relevant departments to dynamically monitor, classify and manage chronic diseases in the elderly, and accurately intervene, and to reasonably tilt and optimize the allocation of medical resources.

Methods

Data sources

The data for this study comes from cross-sectional and longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). This project is a high-quality representative sampling survey targeting households and individuals aged 45 and above in China, covering 28 provinces. Its aim is to collect extensive data on the health status and use of medical resources of middle-aged and elderly residents [17, 18].

This study consists of two parts: cross-sectional study and longitudinal dynamic study. The cross-sectional study included respondents aged 60 and above in 2020. After excluding observations with missing data for the independent and dependent variables [19, 20], a total of 8,316 complete cases were retained for analysis. For the longitudinal analysis, we included subjects aged 60 and above who participated in all five survey waves (2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020). Those with missing data on any dependent or independent variable were excluded, a panel dataset was created, comprising a total of 1,178 observations.

Dependent variable

Our analysis placed primary emphasis on outpatient and inpatient services, given their status as the most direct and quantifiable forms of care for managing chronic conditions within the scope of our study. The exclusion of emergency, home-based, and other services was a strategic decision to minimize complexity and keep the analysis sharply focused on the core services relevant to multimorbidity management.

The dependent variable in the cross-sectional study section is the utilization of health services, including: whether outpatient services have been utilized in the past month, the number of outpatient services utilized in the past month, whether inpatient services have been utilized in the past year, and the number of inpatient services utilized in the past year, for a total of four indicators.

Two new indicators have been added to the longitudinal study, namely: whether the utilization of outpatient services has increased in the past month and whether the utilization of inpatient services has increased in the past year. In addition, to verify additional findings during the research process, two indicators were included: whether the number of cases increased compared to the baseline number of cases, and the increase in the number of cases.

Independent variable

The independent variable is the number of chronic diseases in the elderly. This variable is operationalized by summing the total number of chronic diseases reported by each respondent (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, ≥ 5) [7, 21]. The analysis in this study encompasses the complete progression from no disease, through single disease, to multimorbidity, in order to evaluate the risk gradient resulting from the accumulation of disease counts. The term “comorbidity” is specifically and strictly used to refer to the condition of having two or more concurrent chronic diseases. The definition and types of chronic diseases are derived from the original CHARLS questionnaire [22], The specific question is: “Have you been diagnosed by a doctor as having the following chronic diseases?” If participants answer “yes”, they are classified as having that disease. The final analysis included 14 chronic disease: hypertension, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, heart disease, stroke, emotional and mental problems, arthritis or rheumatism, abnormal blood lipids, liver diseases, kidney diseases, stomach or other digestive system diseases, asthma, memory related diseases [23, 24].

Include the trajectory of chronic disease development and baseline disease incidence in the longitudinal study section. The development trajectory is fitted using a latent class growth model, including four groups: relative stabilized trajectory, low-increment trajectory, medium-increment trajectory, and high-increment trajectory. The baseline number of illnesses was assessed based on self-reported data from respondents in 2011.

Control variables

The control variables included in this study include gender, age, marital status, education level, residence (urban and rural), geographic region, socio-economic status, and health insurance status (with health insurance and without health insurance). The socio-economic status (SES) is measured using the “annual per capita household expenditure” indicator [25] and evaluated using quartiles, divided into four groups: Q1 (< 7165 yuan), Q2 (7165–12395 yuan), Q3 (12395–21365 yuan), and Q4 (> 21365 yuan).

Statistical methods

First, descriptive analysis was performed to characterize participants’ basic information, comorbidity status, and development trends of multimorbidity. Statistical differences between groups were tested using chi-square tests.

Secondly, binary logistic regression analysis and zero-inflation negative binomial regression were used to explore the impact of the number of chronic diseases on the risk of outpatient and inpatient service utilization, as well as the frequency of outpatient and inpatient service utilization. Among the 8,316 data, 6485 and 6351 variables, namely outpatient service and inpatient service utilization frequency, respectively, had values of 0 (accounting for 77.98% and 76.37%, respectively). The mean (variance) was 0.498 (2.244) for outpatient visits and 0.415 (1.254) for inpatient visits. In both cases, the variance was substantially higher than the mean, demonstrating over-dispersion in the data. The data exhibited both zero-inflation and over-dispersion, meeting the typical conditions for applying a zero-inflated negative binomial regression model [26]. Therefore, a zero-inflation negative binomial regression model was employed to examine the impact of comorbidities on the frequency of both outpatient and inpatient service utilization.

In longitudinal studies, the latent class growth model (LCGM) is used to identify the trajectory of disease occurrence. The number of latent categories is determined by considering indicators such as Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), Adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC), Bootstrap based likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and Entropy, which reflect the goodness or badness of the model fitting. Statistical significance of differences across trajectory classes was assessed using chi-square tests and Kruskal-Wallis H tests.

Then, binary logistic regression and ordered logistic regression were selected to explore whether the baseline number of diseases affects the risk and magnitude of the increase in the number of chronic diseases, in order to verify an additional finding in the study: the “dynamic accumulation” effect of chronic diseases.

Finally, binary logistic regression was utilized to examine the differences in health service utilization among different development trajectory groups, revealing the longitudinal impact of chronic diseases on outpatient and inpatient service utilization.

Statistical analyses, including chi-square tests, Kruskal-Wallis H tests, binary logistic regression, and ordered logistic regression, were performed using SPSS 25.0. Zero-inflated negative binomial regression and latent class growth modeling were implemented in Stata 17.0 and Mplus 8.0, respectively. Figures 1 and 2 were produced with the R software, and Fig. 3 was completed using Origin, respectively. The statistical significance level was set at α = 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Basic characteristics of respondents

The mean age of all participants included in the cross-sectional study was 69.85 years. Among them, 1,118 older adults had no chronic diseases, accounting for 13.4%, while 7,197 had chronic diseases, accounting for 86.6%. Those with comorbidities constituted 66.7% of the participants. The age structure is relatively young, with 56.0% of the population under the age of 70. In terms of gender distribution, females slightly outnumbered males. The overall education level is generally low, mostly concentrated in junior high school and below. The distribution of these basic characteristics is basically consistent with the survey data of the National Bureau of Statistics of China, reflecting that the respondents included in this study have good representativeness [17]. Most families have robust family structure support, reflected in 78.7% of respondents being married. In terms of residence and region, rural residents account for a higher proportion than urban residents. The vast majority of respondents come from the eastern, central, and western regions of China, with a relatively minimal proportion in the northeast. The coverage rate of basic medical insurance and other medical insurance has reached 94.9%, approaching the level of universal coverage.

Table 2 provides the basic information and comorbidities of the respondents. The results showed that women, older adults, rural residents, non-eastern regions, high socio-economic status, and those with medical insurance were more likely to suffer from chronic comorbidities (P < 0.05). However, no significant differences were observed in marital status and cultural level.

The impact of chronic comorbidities on the utilization of health services

To thoroughly examine the impact of chronic diseases on healthcare utilization, this study divided the effects into two components: the risk of outpatient and inpatient service utilization and the frequency of service utilization. These were analyzed separately using two statistical models: a binary logistic regression model (Model 1) and a zero-inflated negative binomial regression model (Model 2). The full model incorporated demographic characteristics and family social factors as control variables, including gender, age, marital status, educational level, type of residence, geographic region, SES, and health insurance coverage.

Table 4 reports the results of all models. Model 1 shows that the risk of using outpatient services for patients with 1, 2, 3, 4, or ≥ 5 chronic diseases is 1.594, 2.340, 3.291, 3.617, and 5.979 times higher than that for those without chronic diseases; The risk of using inpatient services is 1.580, 2.255, 3.100, 4.267, and 6.687 times higher than that of no chronic patients. Model 2 shows that as the number of chronic diseases increases, the frequency of elderly people using outpatient and inpatient services also increases (Coef > 0, P < 0.05, IRR > 1 and continues to rise). This indicates that the number of chronic diseases in the elderly has a significant positive impact on their utilization of outpatient and inpatient services. In short, with the increase in the number of illnesses, the risk and frequency of elderly people using health services have significantly increased.

Development trends of chronic diseases in the respondents

The longitudinal cohort study segment utilized panel data from 1,178 persistently surveyed participants to analyze the progression of chronic disease burden. As shown in Table 5, both the prevalence and growth of chronic diseases among the surveyed individuals in different periods. Compared with baseline, the proportion of elderly people without chronic diseases decreased by 17.9%, while the proportion of comorbidities increased by 26.3%. The proportion of elderly people with ≥ 5 types of diseases increased significantly, from 3.7% to 20.7%. In addition, from the perspective of the respondents, the number of chronic diseases in 400 elderly people (34.0%) remained stable or slightly decreased, while 778 elderly people (66.0%) showed varying degrees of increase. More profoundly, 324 elderly people (27.5%) increased by 1 disease, 186 elderly people (15.8%) increased by 2 diseases, 144 elderly people (12.2%) increased by 3 diseases, and 124 elderly people (10.5%) increased by 4 or more diseases. The overall development of chronic diseases has always shown a trend of becoming more numerous and complex.

Dynamic development of chronic comorbidities and their longitudinal impact on health service utilization

LCGM was employed to capture trajectories of morbidity accumulation in older adults. Models estimating 2 to 5 distinct trajectories were sequentially fitted, with optimal class enumeration determined by interpretability and objective statistical indices.

Table 6 presents the fit indices of the LCGM. While the 5-class solution yielded the lowest AIC, BIC, and aBIC values, indicating a good statistical fit, it produced a class with a very small sample size (< 5%), which may suggest overfitting and poses challenges for meaningful theoretical interpretation. Therefore, the 5-class model was not retained. Although the 3-class model demonstrated a higher entropy value (0.969), reflecting better class separation, its AIC, BIC, and aBIC values were all higher than those of the 4-class model, indicating a poorer overall fit to the data. Previous research has shown that the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) demonstrates superior performance in comparing LCGM. Consequently, the model with the smallest BIC value is typically selected as the final model [27, 28]. More importantly, compared to the 4-class solution, the 3-class model likely obscures important heterogeneity within the data. In summary, after comprehensively considering statistical fit indices and interpretability, we concluded that the 4-class model represents the optimal balance between statistical evidence and substantive meaning.

Figure 3 shows the trajectory of the number of chronic diseases in each category, and selects the best four trajectory of quantity development. Based on the characteristics of these trajectory curves, all four trajectories show varying degrees of upward trend. However, class 1 is relative stabilized with increments mostly within one, and is named the “relative stabilized trajectory” (51.7%). Class 2 has increments mostly between 1 and 2, and is named the “low-increment trajectory” (24.3%). Classes 3 and 4 have increments mostly around 2–3 and 4, respectively, and are named the “medium-increment trajectory” (19.0%) and “high-increment trajectory” (5.0%). To ensure the rationality of the grouping, a chi-square test was conducted on the comorbidity situation of each trajectory population. The results showed that there was no significant difference in the risk of comorbidity increase among the trajectory populations (P > 0.05), while the difference in the increase was statistically significant (P < 0.05). This is consistent with the feature that classes 1 to 4 have all increased but the increment is getting larger, and the rationality of this grouping has been verified.

In addition, as the increase in illness progresses from low to high, the starting points of the four trajectories are also higher. Therefore, it is speculated that having more chronic diseases will increase the risk of developing other diseases in the future. We construct binary logistic regression (Model 3) and ordered logistic regression (Model 4) models here, with the baseline number of illnesses and whether the number of illnesses has increased, as well as the increase in the number of illnesses, as the core variables for 1178 respondents, to verify this additional finding.

Table 4 presents the running results of Model 3 and Model 4, which show a significant positive correlation (P < 0.05) between the number of cases and the risk and quantity of newly added diseases. Compared with those without chronic diseases, those with 1, 2, and 3 chronic diseases have an increased risk of new diseases by 22.7%, 55.0%, and 88.1%, respectively. When the number of diseases reaches 4, this risk will increase to more than double. As the number of diseases increases, the number of new diseases will also significantly increase, reaching a maximum growth rate of 2.069 times when the number of diseases is 3, and gradually stabilizing at 1.813 times when the number of diseases is ≥ 5. The number of chronic diseases does indeed have an impact on the risk of new diseases in the future, showing a “dynamic accumulation” effect.

To further investigate the correlation between the developmental changes in the number of chronic conditions and health service utilization, this study used the four developmental trajectories identified by the LCGM and the trend of increasing multimorbidity as the independent and dependent variables, respectively. By incorporating various control variables, a new binary logistic regression model (Model 5) was ultimately constructed.

Model 5 of Table 4 identifies a significant association between an increasing number of comorbidities and a higher risk of health service utilization from 2011 to 2020 (P < 0.05). Compared with the respondents in the relative stabilized trajectory group, those in the low-increment trajectory group had a 49.8% increased risk of increased utilization of outpatient services. The risk of increased outpatient service utilization among respondents in the medium-increment trajectory group increased by 1.064 times, and the risk of increased inpatient service utilization increased by 71.4%. The risk of increased utilization of outpatient and inpatient services in the high-increment trajectory group increased by 1.182 times and 1.210 times, respectively.

Discussion

Chronic comorbidities are an important cause of harm to the health of elderly people in China, which will increase their demand for health services. At present, there is relatively little longitudinal trend research on the development of chronic disease comorbidities among elderly people in China. This article is based on CHARLS data from 2011 to 2020, and uses both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies to deeply reveal the comorbidity and development trends of chronic diseases in the elderly, as well as their impact on the utilization of health services. The research results show that 86.6% of elderly people aged 60 and above in China suffer from at least one chronic disease, with a comorbidity rate of 66.7%. A study conducted in the United States indicates that 93.0% of older adults have at least one chronic disease, with a multimorbidity rate of 78.8% [29]. Another study from the United Kingdom reports a corresponding multimorbidity rate of 44.5% [30]. These differences likely result from the interplay of multiple factors, including variations in study design [31, 32], differences in population age structure (with China being in a phase of accelerated population aging) [31], disparities in the spectrum of predominant chronic diseases [33], as well as differences in the organization of healthcare systems, screening intensity, and diagnostic thresholds [32, 34]. Among the elderly people who continue to be surveyed, the comorbidity rate was 42.0% in 2011, which increased to 47.8% in 2013, reached 60.6% in 2015, further climbed to 67.6% and 68.4% in 2018 and 2020, and gradually stabilized. There is at least one additional chronic condition accumulated in 66% of the elderly population. Compared with those without chronic diseases, patients with a chronic disease or comorbidities have significantly higher risks and frequency of using health services. On this basis, with the passage of time, the possibility of further increase in the number of illnesses and the risk of health service utilization also significantly increases. Highlighting the significant impact of chronic disease comorbidities among the elderly on the utilization of health services, this study draws several important conclusions based on this.

Currently, research on multimorbidity and healthcare utilization among older adults primarily relies on cross-sectional studies, which aim to explore the relationship between chronic diseases and healthcare use [19, 35, 36]. Multiple studies have focused on specific patient populations, such as a Canadian study of cancer patients and a UK study of Alzheimer’s patients; these investigations examined comorbidities within these groups and analyzed their association with healthcare utilization [37, 38]. Additionally, a Chinese study analyzed the relationship between physical multimorbidity and healthcare utilization across different socioeconomic groups [20]. To some extent, these studies reflect the pervasiveness of multimorbidity and its broad impact on the healthcare domain. Furthermore, some research has systematically classified patterns and developmental trajectories of chronic conditions. For instance, a multinational study covering Finland, Poland, Spain, China, and Russia categorized 12 chronic disease combinations into “cardiopulmonary,” “metabolic,” “cardio-arthritic,” and “respiratory” patterns, while also proposing an “other specific patterns” category for country-specific combinations [16]. Other scholars have delved into developmental trajectory models of chronic diseases, finding that arthritis often marks the initial stage, digestive system diseases and hypertension typically play dominant roles, while memory-related diseases and diabetes frequently manifest as secondary conditions [39], however, this study focused on causal relationships between diseases. A key limitation of most existing studies is their failure to capture the dynamic process of individual disease accumulation and subsequently examine differences in health service utilization among populations with distinct morbidity trajectories. Notably, international studies utilizing longitudinal data have revealed the long-term impact of multimorbidity on health service utilization. For example, a multi-center cohort study in Switzerland confirmed that both the number of comorbidities and specific combinations are strong risk factors predicting patient readmission and prolonged hospital stays [12]. Another study of older adults in Ontario, Canada, further indicated that the number of comorbidities is a key driver of health service utilization; patients with ≥ 10 chronic conditions had a 2.16 times higher risk of potentially avoidable readmission compared to those with only 2 conditions [7]. These studies established a longitudinal link between the “number” of comorbidities and resource utilization. Going a step further, some research has explored the heterogeneity of multimorbidity. For instance, a study of acute inpatients in Denmark identified eight distinct comorbidity patterns and found that patterns like “complex multimorbidity” were significantly associated with higher rates of general practitioner visits and mortality risk [40]. Building upon this foundation, the present study integrates both the “quantity” and “heterogeneity” dimensions within a nationally representative sample of Chinese older adults. It applies a Latent Class Growth Model to fit “developmental trajectories” of the number of chronic conditions. Consequently, it focuses on examining differences in health service utilization among populations with different patterns of disease accumulation, thereby providing a new perspective for understanding the dynamic impact of multimorbidity.

In addition, the overall number of diseases among different individuals continues to rise, and there are four different development trajectories among populations, reflecting heterogeneity in the growth of chronic diseases among different populations. However, there is no downward trend in all trajectories, and they all show varying degrees of upward trend. At the same time, the results of univariate analysis also indicate that there is no significant difference in the risk of disease growth among different trajectory populations, fully reflecting the difficult to cure characteristics of chronic diseases. Although different trajectories have similar growth risks, populations with higher incremental trajectories have higher disease increments compared to relative stabilized trajectories. If further in-depth research is conducted on various trajectories, especially for the “relative stabilized trajectory” or “low-increment trajectory” population, it may provide theoretical basis for precise prevention and control of chronic diseases. The study also showed that individuals with a higher number of chronic diseases at the 2011 baseline had a greater risk of developing additional conditions, exhibiting a “cumulative effect,” meaning that the more patients with chronic diseases, the higher the risk of developing new chronic diseases in the future. This phenomenon has also been confirmed in other studies, such as individuals with a high number of chronic diseases being more likely to further develop diseases such as dementia and cognitive impairment [41, 42], which may be due to the interaction of multiple chronic diseases [39], further increasing the risk of adverse health outcomes and the possibility of new health problems occurring in the future. Therefore, as people age, the health burden caused by chronic diseases on the elderly will become increasingly severe.

There are significant differences in the risk of further increase in healthcare service utilization among populations with different growth trajectories, especially among those with high growth trajectories, where the risk of increased outpatient and inpatient visits is 1.182 and 1.210 times higher compared to the relative stabilized group. On the basis of cross-sectional studies, the relationship between chronic disease comorbidities and the utilization rate and frequency of outpatient and inpatient services was further confirmed, that is, an increase in the number of diseases will lead to an increase in the demand for health services. This is mainly attributed to several reasons: firstly, the increase in the number of chronic diseases will lead to the continuous deterioration of the health status and quality of life of the elderly, such as the decline in activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) abilities [8], resulting in more rigid needs, and even the need to maintain basic survival through frequent health services. Meanwhile, the coexistence of multiple diseases often leads to the complexity of treatment plans, requiring not only more diverse and intensive care [2, 43], but also poor medication adherence, multiple use of drugs, drug interactions [2], and more prominent issues of complications. In addition, departments in hospitals or other medical facilities are usually designed around a single disease, and medical professionals specialize in their work, resulting in multiple chronic disease patients needing to go to different departments for treatment [44]. Therefore, compared to a single disease, the accumulation of comorbidities in chronic diseases will result in more and additional consumption of medical resources, exacerbating the burden on the healthcare system.

The study also found that gender, age, marital status, education level, socio-economic status (SES), and region also have varying degrees of impact on the utilization of health services, which has been fully confirmed in other studies [45–47]. It is worth noting that in previous studies, medical insurance was an important influencing factor on the utilization of health services, but in this study, the presence or absence of medical insurance did not have a significant impact on the utilization risk and frequency of health services. The reasons for this phenomenon may be twofold: on the one hand, there were statistical biases in the research. However, among the elderly people included in the study, 94.1% had medical insurance, which is higher than the 87.33% shown in a study on the coverage rate of the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme among rural elderly migrant population [48]. Another study shows that the social medical insurance coverage rate for middle-aged and elderly residents in Chinese communities is 95.29% (with an uninsured rate of 4.71%) [49]. Therefore, the medical insurance coverage obtained by this study is within a reasonable range. On the other hand, the rigid demand for health services caused by deteriorating health conditions forces elderly people to bear higher medical expenses to maintain their basic survival needs. In addition, SES has a significant impact on outpatient and inpatient services, indicating that although medical insurance is not an influencing factor on the medical behavior of the elderly in this study, economic issues still have an important impact on their medical choices [50, 51].

The medical insurance system is a crucial component of China’s social security strategy [52]. Numerous studies have confirmed its remarkable achievements in expanding coverage, reducing out-of-pocket expenses, and providing financial risk protection [53–55]. However, other research indicates its limited effectiveness in curbing the incidence of chronic diseases [22, 56, 57], a finding supported by our study which shows health insurance’s restricted role in mediating healthcare-seeking behavior among the elderly. This suggests that despite continuous development and improvement of China’s social health insurance, limitations such as insufficient funding reserves and regional disparities in insurance pooling persist [58, 59]. These constraints stem from multiple factors beyond economic considerations, including governance structures, policy design complexities, and the profound pressures of rapid population aging [54, 60]. Addressing these issues requires systematic reforms focusing on institutional design improvement, strengthened regulation, and enhanced pooling levels [54, 60]. While our study does not identify specific subpopulations that would benefit most from differentiated coverage (such as those defined by region, insurance type, or socio-economic status), it demonstrates that health risks are not static but can be dynamically stratified based on the accumulation of disease counts. This finding suggests that identifying key populations for protection should consider these dynamic progression characteristics rather than relying solely on static attributes. Future policies could build on this approach by prioritizing groups exhibiting rapid deterioration in multimorbidity and designing corresponding tailored protection mechanisms, thereby enhancing the precision and equity of insurance coverage within existing fiscal constraints.

To address the above issues, this paper proposes several targeted suggestions: First, healthcare delivery strategies should incorporate dynamic trajectories as a dimension for risk stratification. This study found that the higher increasing comorbidity trajectory group exhibited significantly elevated risks of hospital service utilization. This finding provides a rationale for integrating the dynamic characteristics of disease accumulation into health assessment systems, enabling more precise identification of individuals who should be prioritized for case management and coordinated healthcare resources. Building on this, we recommend establishing coordinated care mechanisms for high-risk trajectory groups. Furthermore, our results demonstrate a significant association between specific comorbidity progression trajectories and high-intensity hospital service use. This suggests that for populations experiencing rapid disease accumulation, there is a need to design more coordinated management protocols. By enhancing follow-up continuity and proactive health management, we can ensure that the existing service system can more effectively address their complex needs. Additionally, there is a clear need to conduct more targeted, large-scale longitudinal studies. These studies should be specifically designed to understand the dynamic progression of multimorbidity and its long-term impact on healthcare utilization. The resulting empirical evidence can shed further light on the complex linkages between dynamic disease accumulation, health outcomes, and system demands, thereby providing a robust scientific foundation for optimizing medical resource allocation and refining multimorbidity management strategies.

Finally, there are several limitations to this study that need to be clarified: firstly, chronic diseases and healthcare service utilization are measured based on self-reported data from respondents, which may lead to recall bias in the results [56]. Secondly, this study defines comorbidities as simple counts of chronic diseases, without considering differences in disease combination patterns and severity, which may result in significant individual differences within the group. Future research can provide a more detailed classification of the severity or combination patterns of diseases and analyze their development trends, in order to better explore the development of chronic disease comorbidities and their impact on the utilization of health services. However, this study included longitudinal analysis, and the large sample size and panel data to some extent reduced random error [22], and control unmeasured time-invariant confounding, effectively confirming the association between comorbidities and health service utilization. Furthermore, sufficient variables were included in the study and strict statistical analysis was followed to maximize the reliability of the research results.

Conclusions

This study, utilizing nationally representative longitudinal data, reveals that multimorbidity of chronic diseases is not only highly prevalent among the elderly population in China, but also exhibits heterogeneous accumulation trajectories rather than following a uniform pattern. Moreover, individuals with a greater number of existing chronic conditions face a higher risk of developing new chronic diseases and a greater increase in disease count in the future. Multimorbidity significantly drives the utilization of healthcare services, as an increase in the number of chronic diseases raises both the likelihood and frequency of outpatient and inpatient service use among older adults. Notably, individuals following “medium-increment” and “high-increment” trajectories face a substantially higher risk of increased healthcare utilization compared to those in the “relative stabilized trajectory” group. This highlights the urgency and necessity of conducting early identification and implementing dynamic management to pinpoint high-risk groups.
