Post-cardiac arrest care during transport from referral centers to a tertiary children’s hospital
Authors: Sughra Ali, Maysaa Assaf, Michael R. Miller, Dhruv Gupte, Janice A. Tijssen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (POHCA) is rare but is associated with poor survival and unfavorable neurological outcomes. There is limited literature on post-cardiac arrest care during inter-facility transport of patients from referral centers to tertiary pediatric hospitals. This study aims to investigate if PCAC best practices are prioritized during inter-facility transport of POHCA patients and to describe PCAC prioritization and complications by survival status.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on POHCA patients transported to Children’s Hospital – London Health Sciences Center (CH-LHSC) by a critical care transport team between January 2006 and June 2022. PCAC prioritization was defined based on post-resuscitation guidelines and CH-LHSC transport team protocols.ResultsAmong the 46 POHCA patients included in descriptive analysis, 12 patients (26.1%) survived to hospital discharge with 10 survivors (83.3%) demonstrating favourable neurological outcomes at discharge. Hypotension was the most common complication during transport (n = 16, 34.8%). Of these, 15 POHCA patients (93.8%) were non-survivors, while only one patient (6.3%) survived to discharge.DiscussionThe study identified that PCAC in the hemodynamic and temperature management categories was not optimally prioritized during transport. Hypotension was the most common complication and more frequently observed in patients who did not survive to discharge. The small sample size and few survivors limited the ability to perform formal statistical comparisons. Further studies should combine data with other centers to improve the sample size, which will allow exploration of barriers to PCAC prioritization to pave way for areas of quality improvement in pediatric out-of-hospital post cardiac arrest care.

Introduction

Pediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (POHCA) is associated with extremely poor outcomes with survival rates of 2–24% and unfavorable neurological outcomes.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Once return-of-spontaneous circulation is achieved, post cardiac arrest care (PCAC) is not only paramount to minimize secondary brain injury from ongoing clinical instability, but also harmful cellular processes that occur following brain ischemia. Often, POHCA patients initially present to community hospitals where they require critical care transportation to tertiary centers for higher level of PCAC after initial stabilization.6 Studies show that mortality rates are higher among critically ill patients transported from a referring institution, likely due to the added complexities of transport.7, 8 As such, adherence to best practices during inter-facility transport is essential to prevent worsening of disease state and adverse events.

The Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) guidelines by American Heart Association (AHA) and Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada (HSFC) do not provide specific guidelines for PCAC in the transport setting.9 An AHA scientific statement on PCAC recommends that POHCA patients should receive the same care during transport as in-hospital PCAC, including monitoring requirements and treatment targets.9 Adhering to the same guidelines as the hospital setting may be difficult during transport due to the limitations in the transport environment resulting in deviations from the best practice guidelines. McKenzie et al. and Gupte et al. previously identified deviations from PCAC guidelines in the pre-hospital and in-hospital management of POHCA patients in Ontario’s Middlesex region, respectively, but the studies were underpowered to establish an association with outcomes.10, 11 However, there is no available published literature that investigates PCAC during inter-facility transport.

The aim for this study was to investigate post-cardiac arrest care for POHCA patients during transport from referral centers to Children’s Hospital-London Health Sciences Center (CH-LHSC). We aimed to (1) describe the prioritization of PCAC best practices and complications during inter-facility transport of POHCA patients, and (2) summarize PCAC prioritization and complications by survival status. The goals of the study were to provide insight into inter-facility PCAC in POHCA patients and to guide quality improvement initiatives for PCAC during inter-facility transport.

Methods

We performed a retrospective cohort study that included all atraumatic POHCA patients who were transferred to CH-LHSC from referral centers by a critical care transport team between January 1, 2006, and June 30, 2022. CH-LHSC serves a large geographic area in Ontario across both Southwestern and Northwestern Ontario, receiving referrals from 66 centers. The study was approved by Western University Health Science Research Ethics Board (REB# 121372).

Patient population

The study included all pediatric patients aged 1 day to less than 18 years, who had a POHCA event and were transferred to CH-LHSC from a referral center by a critical care transport team. Critical care transport teams included were the ORNGE Transport Team and the CH-LHSC Critical Care Transport Team. ORNGE is a ground and air ambulance system in Ontario that supports inter-facility transport for critically ill patients when hospital-based teams are unavailable. Patients were excluded if they were missing transport records, transported by EMS, or missing the primary outcome of survival to hospital discharge. The secondary outcome was favourable neurological outcome at discharge defined as Pediatric Cerebral Performance Category (PCPC) scores of 1–3. PCPC scores were collected at baseline, during interfacility-transport and at hospital discharge by chart review as a measure of neurological status.

Data collection

The CH-LHSC Pediatric Critical Care Unit database was used to identify the study population. Patient demographics, cardiac arrest characteristics, and patient outcomes were collected using paper based and electronic medical records from CH-LHSC, including the patients’ transport records. For patients transported by the ORNGE Transport Team, ORNGE transport records were accessed. The Utstein template for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) core variables was utilized to collect cardiac arrest data.12 Pediatric level of care available at referral centers was defined using the Provincial Council for Maternal and Child Health Summary Report.13 All data extraction was completed by S.A.

Process of defining PCAC prioritization and complications

Currently, there are no evidence-based PCAC guidelines specifically designed for the transport environment. As such, PCAC prioritizations were defined using a combination of post-resuscitation PALS AHA and HFSC guidelines, physiologic targets, as well as best practices as per the CH-LHSC Critical Care Transport Team. PCAC category definitions were adapted and modified from Gupte et al.10 Three categories of prioritizations were developed: (1) oxygenation and ventilation management, (2) hemodynamic management, and (3) temperature management. Each of the prioritization categories were subdivided into specific actions (see Table 1). Each action was then given a score with the following possible data entries: Yes/No/Unknown (see Appendix A1 for detailed criteria). Prioritization of PCAC within a category was defined as completing one or more actions within each prioritization category. PCAC prioritization definitions.

Complications during transport were defined using the most recent post-resuscitation guidelines by PALS AHA and a national consensus process using Delphi methodology which defined significant events during pediatric transport.14, 15, 16 Hypercarbia was defined as pCO2 > 55 mmHg, fever was defined as temperature ≥ 38.0℃, hypoxemia was defined as SpO2 < 94%, and hypotension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) < 60 mmHg for <1 m, <70 mmHg for 1 m–1 y, (<70 + 2x age) mmHg for >1–10 y, and <90 mmHg for >10 y. Delays during transport were also recorded during PCAC (see Appendix A2 for definitions). Delays were characterized as patient attributable or system attributable delays. Patient attributable delays were defined as delays due patient factors, including medical instability, endotracheal tube issues, delays starting fluids/inotropes, or other factors. System attributable delays were defined as delays arising from logistical, communication, equipment, or transportation issues, including waiting for transport, weather delays, or transport equipment malfunctions.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed on patient demographics, cardiac arrest characteristics, transport characteristics, PCAC prioritizations, and outcomes. Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and proportions. All analyses were conducted using SPSS v29 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Three POHCA patients were excluded from analysis as they were missing transport records, making the sample analyzed n = 46. PCAC prioritization

All POHCA events met at least one PCAC priority with a median of 2 (IQR 2–3). PCAC priorities were most frequently met in the oxygenation/ventilation management category (n = 46, 100%). Hemodynamic management was the category with the next most frequent PCAC prioritization (n = 38, 82.6%), followed by temperature management (n = 22, 47.8%). Ten patients (21.7%) had an arterial line during transport. Five patients (10.9%) were missing temperature management data, but they were retained in analysis and classified as not meeting temperature monitoring criteria. Complications

Twenty-three POHCA patients (49.9%) experienced one or more complications during inter-facility transport with a median of 1 (IQR 0–2). The most frequent complication during inter-facility transport was hypotension (n = 16, 34.8%). The next most frequent complication was desaturation (n = 14, 30.4%), followed by fever (n = 5, 10.9%), and hypercarbia (n = 3, 6.5%). Patients with hypotension were less likely to survive to hospital discharge (n = 1, 6.3%) compared to patients without hypotension (n = 11, 36.7%). Total complications were lower in patients who experienced system attributable delays compared to those without system delays.

Discussion

Our study investigated prioritization of PCAC best practices and complications during inter-facility transport. The main findings from our study were (1) Hypotension was the most common complication during inter-facility transport and was more frequently observed in patients who did not survive to hospital discharge, and (2) PCAC was most frequently prioritized in the oxygenation/ventilation management category while temperature management was the least frequently prioritized category.

The Post-Cardiac Arrest Syndrome is a critical period of illness with four key components: post-cardiac arrest brain injury, post-cardiac arrest myocardial dysfunction, systemic ischemia/reperfusion response and persisting precipitating pathophysiology. These pathophysiologic changes can cause hemodynamic changes, including hypotension. Our study showed that hypotension was more frequently observed in patients who did not survive to hospital discharge. We were unable to determine association with statistical analysis due to our small sample size. However, our findings support previous pediatric and adult cardiac arrest studies, which showed an association between hypotension in hospital and in-hospital mortality.17, 18, 19, 20 As such, in the post-cardiac arrest period, anticipating and assessing evolving systemic and organ dysfunction is paramount to prevent worsening. However, resources are often limited in the transport setting, including availability of healthcare professionals. For example, the AHA recommends that, if possible, an arterial catheter should be placed for continuous intra-arterial monitoring.9 However, our study found that only 10 patients (21.7%) had an arterial line during transport. Contributing factors may include a shortage of skilled professionals to establish arterial lines in community hospitals and transport settings, insufficient preparation time, or challenges in maintaining arterial line functionality during transport.

Hypotension was the most common complication POHCA patients experienced during inter-facility transport (n = 16, 34.8%); this finding was in keeping with previous studies, including a multi-center retrospective cohort study which found that hypotension was documented in 56% of pediatric PCAC patients in the first 6 hours post-resuscitation.17 Interestingly, the rate of hypotension events was lower in our study (34.8%) compared to studies that looked at hypotension in the hospital setting (47–65%).20, 21 However, this is likely because PCAC guidelines recommend POHCA patients are stabilized at the referral center prior to inter-facility transport to tertiary centers. Furthermore, many previous studies have been limited to the early post-resuscitation period as this is the period when hypotension is common in the Post-Cardiac Arrest Syndrome. However, POHCA patients during inter-facility transport are often past the early post-resuscitation period before they are transported to tertiary centers.

Hypotension and its sequelae can be avoided in the transport setting by prioritizing hemodynamic monitoring goals, such as allowing for a drop in blood pressure that does not result in hypotension (i.e., using ‘buffer’ targets). In addition, establishing management goals, for example running a minimum low-dose inotrope infusion on all patients, ensures that any hypotension is expeditiously and appropriately managed. Furthermore, creating transport specific post-cardiac arrest care guidelines is paramount to ensure recommendations for PCAC take transport resources and limitations into consideration. Doing so will increase adherence to the PCAC guidelines and may potentially decrease rates of complications during inter-facility transport.

Our study identified that oxygenation/ventilation management was the most frequently prioritized PCAC category (n = 46, 100%). Within the oxygenation/ventilation category, continuous oxygen monitoring was the most frequently completed action, likely due to easy access and management of monitoring equipment. Temperature monitoring was the least frequently prioritized PCAC category (n = 22, 47.8%). This was also the category with the most missing data. Temperature monitoring after pediatric cardiac arrest is essential due to poor neurological outcomes associated with fever.22, 23 The lack of prioritization may be multi-factorial, including focusing on respiratory and cardiovascular stability prior to temperature management. Furthermore, limited resources and personnel may result in focusing attention on essential life-support functions. Further research should be conducted to identify reasons for lack of prioritization of temperature monitoring and to address those areas as future quality improvement initiatives.

A major limitation of the study was the small sample size, limiting the ability to run statistical analysis and determine associations with survival to hospital discharge. The study should be reproduced with a larger sample size and at multiple centers to allow for the identification of the most impactful opportunities for improvement in patient care. Another limitation of this study is that it does not include data from the initial EMS resuscitation, care at the referring site, or management at the tertiary center after transport. These phases of care likely influence patient stability during transport and subsequent outcomes. Future research should capture information across all phases of post-cardiac arrest care to better understand their combined impact on outcomes.

A significant gap in the PCAC literature identified during this study was the lack of clear guidelines for post cardiac arrest care in the transport environment. Due to lack of PCAC guidelines during transport, this study defined prioritizations of PCAC using a combination of existing PCAC guidelines and best practices employed by our local transport team. Future quality improvement initiatives should focus on developing standardized guidelines to guide PCAC during transport that account for limitations and resources available in the transport environment. Standardizing guidelines could help address disparities in practice, enhance consistency across institutions and ultimately enhance patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This retrospective cohort study was the first to examine PCAC among POHCA patients during inter-facility transport. We identified that PCAC in the hemodynamic and temperature management categories was not optimally prioritized. Hypotension was the most common complication during inter-facility transport and was more frequently observed in patients who did not survive to hospital discharge. This study should be reproduced with a larger sample size, combining data from multiple centers, which will allow exploration of barriers to PCAC prioritization to pave way for areas of quality improvement in pediatric out-of-hospital post cardiac arrest care.
