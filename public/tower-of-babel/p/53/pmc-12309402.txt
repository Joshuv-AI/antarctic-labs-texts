A soft robotic, modular laparoscopic grasper for atraumatic retraction of the small intestine
Authors: Lorenzo Kinnicutt, Leah T. Gaeta, Jacob Rogatinsky, Jungjae Lee, Amy Cameron, Amartya J. Naik, Donald T. Hess, Tommaso Ranzani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYIn order to address the risk of iatrogenic trauma and retraction challenges associated with minimally invasive surgery, we propose a novel laparoscopic grasper equipped with a suction unit and elastomeric actuators that are inherently soft and compliant, as well as sensors to monitor tissue interaction forces. The device complies with laparoscopic size constraints by entering the abdominal cavity in a closed configuration, then expanding upon entry to gently grasp and retract even severely dilated intestinal segments. In order to minimize the usage of surgical access ports and personnel, the end effector of the proposed grasper is designed to detach and be anchored to the abdominal wall to serve as a passive retraction mechanism. Testing has demonstrated the ability of the proposed grasper to hold and retract in vitro and ex vivo intestinal segments in various contexts, including intestines that have been dilated with air and water to represent small bowel distention.

INTRODUCTION

Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) has been widely shown to deliver improved patient outcomes and shorter recovery times.1,2 Laparoscopic procedures have been effective in treating injuries of the small intestine3 as well as obstructions,4–6 the latter of which are responsible for hundreds of thousands of hospitalizations each year in the United States alone.7,8 However, the minimally invasive nature of laparoscopy deprives surgeons of tactile feedback afforded by open surgery, reducing surgeons’ ability to gauge tissue interaction forces.9 Combined with the fact that traditional rigid laparoscopic graspers impose high stress concentrations on tissues,10 the tactile disconnect present in MIS can lead to the infliction of unintentional iatrogenic trauma, including bowel damage and perforations11–15 and other injuries to abdominal organs,16 which can be life threatening and often result in emergency conversion to open surgery.

Despite the efficacy of laparoscopy in treating small bowel obstructions,4–6 many surgeons remain hesitant to adopt it due to the challenges associated with manipulating dilated intestines5,17 with diameters exceeding 2.5–3 cm.18,19 Such tissues are more sensitive to damage20 and may be much larger than the grasper’s maximum expansion, increasing the likelihood that the surgeon will apply excessive force during manipulation.21 Due to the high morbidity and mortality rates associated with small bowel trauma,16 there is a need to develop laparoscopic graspers capable of safely manipulating it.

Approaches for designing safer abdominal organ graspers include the development of instruments with larger contact areas21 and integrated sensors to monitor tissue interaction forces. The development of such devices is complicated by the size limitations of laparoscopic surgery.22 A non-sensorized, rigid instrument that envelops bowel segments rather than pinching them is described in Frank and Cuschieri,23 while designs similar to existing laparoscopic graspers but with integrated contact sensing are described in Kim et al.,24 Choi et al.,25 and Tholey and Desai.26 Devices that combine increased contact area with integrated sensing are presented in Liu et al.,27 Mirbagheri and Farahmand,28 and Vakili et al.29 Although the aforementioned devices aim to address tactile inadequacies in laparoscopic surgery using alternative grasping approaches and force sensors, the components of these instruments that make contact with tissue are still rigid. The substantial compliance mismatch between the rigid grasper materials and soft tissues can create high stress concentrations and lead to infliction of trauma due to clinician error.10,30,31 Additionally, none of the aforementioned devices has been designed to be compatible with pathologically dilated intestines. Hence, there is still an unmet need for graspers that are compliant, miniaturized, and sensorized to conform to the constraints of laparoscopic MIS.

Although grasping intestinal segments may be necessary during procedures that pertain to the bowels themselves, establishing a hold on the intestines and moving them is also important for accessing underlying tissues, a process known as retraction.32 Intestinal retraction is often performed during laparoscopic procedures to obtain an improved field of view.33 However, commercial retraction devices, such as those reviewed in Ladwa et al.,32 are rigid and require a trained assistant to actively grasp and move the intestine and hold it in position for the duration of the procedure. This process occupies potentially limited personnel and resources, in addition to requiring a dedicated trocar port for the duration of the procedure.34,35 Thus, various approaches have been proposed for passive retraction of the small intestine with the intention of freeing surgeons, assistants, and trocars for other tasks. In general, passive retraction can be achieved through anchoring to the abdominal wall in a way that does not require an assistant to maintain the organ’s retraction. For example, magnetic anchoring36–43 has been proposed, but this approach is limited by the distance (and thus the force of attraction) between magnets on either side of the abdominal wall. The distance can vary between patients44 and even based on the location of the anchor on the abdominal wall.45

To minimize damage to abdominal organs at the tissue-retractor interface, various atraumatic mechanisms have been researched in the literature. Suction-based mechanisms have been proposed in Vonck et al.46,47 and Horie et al.48 However, they require continual use of laparoscopic trocars for vacuum lines, which is not conducive to passive retraction. Soft robotic approaches have been proposed to mitigate iatrogenic trauma associated with grasping and retraction. For example, a retractor with pneumatic actuators has been proposed in O’Shea et al.,49 which can fit through a 5 mm trocar and laterally move the intestines. The work presented in O’Shea et al.49 introduces a simple and effective approach to achieve atraumatic bowel retraction to enable lateral bowel movement.

Cable-driven50 and origami-based51 designs with integrated sensing have also been explored. These devices all propose atraumatic approaches to retraction, but they still occupy laparoscopic trocars, require active work to maintain retraction, have not been designed for manipulating dilated intestine, and may lack the ability to move dilated intestines for a sufficient surgical field of view. Therefore, there is an unmet need for retractors that leverage the safety of soft robots with the ability to passively anchor to the abdominal wall, all while prioritizing effective grasping. To address shortcomings in the current approaches to grasping and retraction while abiding by the size constraints of MIS, we introduce an expandable mechanism that passes through a laparoscopic entry port (18 mm trocar) in a collapsed state and deploys once inside the abdominal cavity. The device is designed to enable retraction of the intestine, even when pathologically dilated, during a laparoscopic procedure. The proposed device, called the MAIR (modular apparatus for intestinal retraction), is designed to first establish a preliminary hold on a target intestinal segment to isolate it from the surrounding tissues using a suction unit located on its distal tip (Figure 1A). Subsequently, an expandable mechanism deploys two soft pneumatic actuators positioned 180° apart (Figure 1B), which inflate and envelop the target intestinal segment to grasp it (Figure 1C). The soft pneumatic actuators are equipped with soft sensors to monitor tissue interaction forces. These sensors are integrated as an additional precaution to prevent iatrogenic trauma, especially when manipulating dilated intestinal tissue.

The MAIR can be used as an atraumatic, trans-abdominal intestinal grasping instrument, but if the surgeon so chooses, it can also be converted into a passive retractor. This is accomplished by detaching the grasper end effector from the instrument body while maintaining the inflation of the pneumatic actuators. Then, the end effector is secured to the internal abdominal wall (Figure 1D) via a hybrid magnetic-needlescopic anchoring mechanism. Needlescopic instruments are differentiated from laparoscopic instruments by their sub-3 mm diameter, which is similar to the size of a hypodermic needle.52,53 Due to their small diameters, needlescopic tools are associated with lower rates of wound infection, shorter hospitalization, lower postoperative pain, and better cosmesis with respect to traditional laparoscopic incisions.54–56 Thus, this device is capable of not only safely grasping intestinal segments via a combination of a deployable mechanism, compliant materials, and integrated sensors but also passively retracting the intestine without additional laparoscopic ports or personnel.

In this work, we present the design and fabrication of each component, as well as preclinical validations in in vitro simulators and with ex vivo tissues.

RESULTS

The MAIR was designed to minimize compliance mismatch and maximize contact area when retracting healthy or pathologically dilated intestinal tissues during laparoscopic surgery, thus mitigating the likelihood of inflicting unintentional trauma on the patient. The atraumatic nature of this device is achieved using compliant pneumatic actuators equipped with elastomeric force sensors, which are deployed via an expandable mechanism that can fit through an 18 mm opening. This aggregation of features allows surgeons to circumferentially envelop intestinal segments rather than “pinching” them, thereby affording operators a reliable hold on abdominal tissues with a reduced risk of damage. The MAIR’s design consists of an end effector connected to the main body, which contains the handle, user interface, motors, and force transmission for actuating the end effector (Figure 2). The detachable end effector is designed for single use, while the rest of the MAIR can be reused.

Clinical workflow

The envisaged workflow for using the proposed grasper is depicted in Figure 1. Upon entry of the MAIR into the abdominal cavity through an 18 mm access port, the target intestinal segment is visualized by the surgeon via a standard laparoscope (Figure 1). The surgeon activates the suction functionality and makes contact with the surface of the intestinal segment. After a suction grip has been established on the intestinal segment, the surgeon proceeds to isolate the segment from the surrounding tissues (Figure 1A). The surgeon then deploys a pair of fiber-reinforced soft actuators (FRSAs) via an expandable mechanism, allowing the user to control both the horizontal and vertical positions of the FRSAs to adjust grasp orientation (Figure 1B). Once the surgeon has achieved the desired FRSA positioning, the FRSAs are inflated (Figure 1C). In the event that a traumatic force threshold is reached quickly and the FRSAs do not inflate sufficiently to maintain a strong and compliant grasp, the surgeon can deflate and reorient the FRSAs before once again attempting a safe, robust grip. Once such a grip has been established, the surgeon can turn off the vacuum used in the initial suction grip and retract the intestinal segment as needed so that underlying tissues can be accessed.

The surgeon also has the option of detaching the end effector from the body of the MAIR for use as a passive retractor and safely securing it to the abdominal wall using a hybrid magnetic-needlescopic anchoring mechanism (Figure 1D). In doing so, the surgeon frees up the laparoscopic port for other instruments, all while maintaining retraction and visualization of the surgical site through the needlescopic anchoring. This passive retraction method frees the surgeon and assistants to focus on different parts of the procedure rather than requiring personnel to actively maintain retraction. Each of these functionalities and the involved components are described in more detail in the subsequent sections of this paper.

Auxiliary suction functionality

Upon entry into the abdominal cavity, the proposed grasper establishes an initial hold on a target intestinal segment by pulling a vacuum through the suction-cup-shaped opening on its distal tip (Figure 2B). The vacuum lines supplying the suction force are routed through the body of the instrument. This vacuum functionality is controlled by the surgeon and allows them to safely isolate the target intestinal segment from surrounding bundles without the need for auxiliary instruments. The surgeon can toggle the suction functionality via a green latching push button located on the user handle (Figure 2D). Despite the utility of suction in surgical procedures, such a mechanism cannot be converted to a passive retraction mechanism since it is dependent upon external vacuum lines, which would require passage through a trocar. Additionally, retraction must be able to withstand almost the entire length of a surgical procedure (which can be several hours), and prolonged vacuum use could potentially lead to tissue damage. By using suction to initially engage with and isolate the intestines, vacuum is only used for a brief period of time. Once a preliminary hold has been established on the desired tissue using the vacuum functionality, the proposed grasper establishes a more robust hold on the intestine using pneumatic FRSAs (Figure 2B). Further information on the atraumatic nature of the initial suction grasp in ex vivo testing is presented in the supplemental information.

FRSAs

The proposed grasper is equipped with two pneumatically actuated FRSAs positioned 180° apart (Figure 2). FRSAs were selected for their strength and programmability and to provide curling motions to establish a circumferential grip on intestinal segments.57 This grasping approach distributes applied forces over a large surface area (i.e., 6.5 cm2 for each actuator) rather than concentrating it in a small region like traditional graspers.10 Additionally, they are made with Ecoflex 0030 silicone elastomer, which has a 100% modulus of ≈70 kPa,58 while the average modulus of intestinal tissue is between 0.9 and 2 MPa,59 thus addressing issues related to the compliance mismatch between the instrument and the tissue.

The FRSAs are 70 mm in length with a semicircular cross-section of radius 4.7 mm. Fiber wrappings and a strain-limiting layer cause them to curl rather than twist or elongate, enabling them to establish a circumferential grip on the intestine. Details on the fabrication of the actuators are reported in the supplemental information.

Characterizations of the motion of the FRSA when pressurized show that they follow a constant tip trajectory curvature of ≈25 mm, and their continuous deformation allows them to establish a circumferential grip on even severely dilated intestinal segments (radius ≈ 20 mm).18 Blocked force tests were performed on the both bare FRSAs and sensor-equipped FRSAs up to inflation pressures of 250 kPa. The maximum measured force was 6.62 N at an FRSA inflation pressure of 250 kPa, which corresponds to an applied stress of under 100 kPa based on the approximate contact area between the force transducer and the FRSA tip. See the supplemental information (Notes S2 and S3) for additional details on the actuators’ characterizations.

Soft magnetometric force sensors

The laparoscopic grasper is equipped with soft magnetometric force sensors as an additional safety measure to monitor excessive tissue interaction forces. The main role of these sensors is to detect if the applied forces are above the threshold for risk of perforation and alert the user. The working principle of magnetometric force sensing relies on a magnetic field sensor with a fixed position, which senses the displacement of a magnet suspended in an elastomeric matrix.60–62 Based on the stiffness of the matrix and displacement of the magnet, the sensor can be calibrated to yield up to three-dimensional (3D) force sensing. This sensing principle has been exploited to develop multi-axis sensors that were integrated on the FRSAs. In this paper, we demonstrate the ability to equip such sensors on soft actuators that undergo large deformations.

A pair of these magnetometric force sensors are fixed to the underside of each FRSA to relay tissue interaction data to the surgeon (Figure 3). The sensors consist of a triple-axis magnetometer (MLX90395, Melexis) and a miniature 1.6 mm diameter, 0.8 mm thick N52 neodymium magnet (D101-N52, K&J Magnetics) embedded in and separated by an elastomeric silicone matrix. Forces applied to the sensor cause a displacement of the magnet, which leads to a variation in the magnetic field sensed by the magnetometer. The force sensors are capable of measuring applied forces by deducing the position of the magnet based on magnetic field vector measurements. Since the magnet is embedded in prestretched silicone, it returns to its original position upon the release of stress. Electrical signals are routed to the magnetometers using a 110 μm thick custom flexible polyimide printed circuit board, or PCB (JLCPCB); the flexibility of the PCB allows it to conform to the curvature of the FRSA.

As shown in Figure 3A, the magnetometers are soldered to the flexible PCB in locations spaced 25 mm apart. The soft sensors on each of the two FRSAs are offset so that the grasper can close fully (Figure 2C); on one FRSA, the distal soft sensor is positioned 2.5 mm from the tip, whereas on the other FRSA, the distal soft sensor is positioned 17.5 mm from the tip. The flexible PCBs are adhered to a 250 μm thick layer of cured Ecoflex 00–10 (Smooth-On) using a light coat of Sil-Poxy (Smooth-On) (Figure 3B). Another 250 μm thick layer of Ecoflex 00–10 is deposited upon the adhered PCB by spin coating the uncured polymer onto an acrylic plate at 1,000 rpm for 5 s, with a 5 s ramp time at the beginning and end (Figure 3C). The polymer is then cured for 20 min at 70°C using a convection oven (Thermo Scientific). After the Ecoflex 00–10 has cured, another layer of Ecoflex 00–10 is deposited using the same process. Before curing at 70°C, Ecoflex 00–30 (Smooth-On) rectangular parallelepipeds containing the 1.6 mm diameter, 0.8 m thick NdFeB magnets are placed over the magnetometers (Figure 3D). Upon curing, excess Ecoflex 00–10 is removed from the sensors (Figure 3F), which are depicted in Figure 3G. At this point, the flexible PCBs containing the soft sensors can be adhered to each of the two FRSAs (Figure 3H).

The magnetic force sensors’ responses to applied normal force and shear were characterized using a Universal Robots U5e testing system (Figures 4A and 4B). A total of 100,000 calibration data points consisting of the 3D magnetic field measurements of the magnetometers and corresponding xyz force readings of the NANO17 sensor were collected for each sensor, using a sampling rate of 25 Hz. Using TensorFlow in Python, each of the sensors was modeled as separate neural networks. The magnetometer and force data were randomized and given an 80–20 split such that 80% was used for model training and 20% was used for testing. While modeling was done separately for each sensor, all four of the models followed the same neural network architecture. The architecture consisted of an initial layer of three neurons with magnetometer data as inputs, three hidden layers, and a final layer of three neurons with force data as outputs. Between the input and output layers, the hidden layers consisted of 9, 81, and 9 neurons, in that order, and each layer used rectified linear units as activation functions. An Adam optimizer was used during model training, and mean absolute error was used to evaluate loss. Training was completed over 100 epochs, and 20% of the training data was used for model validation. When testing the models, the models for each of the sensors would take 1 ms per step and calculate the predictions for the entire test sets in about 1 s, ensuring that the models could be used in real time. Figures 4C–4E plot the actual force values, measured by the NANO17, against the force values predicted by the neural network using the magnetometer measurements as inputs. Lines of best fit are also depicted, along with their equations and R2 values (Figures 4C–4E). The high level of agreement between the actual and predicted xyz forces is indicated by the closeness of the best-fit line slopes and R2 values to unity and the low deviation of their y intercepts from zero.

Because mitigation of iatrogenic trauma is the foremost purpose of the soft magnetometric force sensors, their ability to detect normal forces via the neural networks was evaluated. The soft sensors were equipped on FRSAs, which were subsequently inflated to 250 kPa while constrained. A NANO17 force sensor was then arbitrarily pushed against the sensors, and the output of both the NANO17 and the soft sensors were recorded (see Video S1). Due to a latency in the inter-integrated circuit (I2C) communication of the soft sensors compared to the analog readings of the NANO17, a 3-point offset was used to align the two datasets. The datasets were then fed into the sensor models in postprocessing. Figures 5A–5D depict the normal force output of the soft force sensors (“predicted”) compared with the NANO17 measurements (“actual”) in a single trial. Table 1 depicts the overall results of this testing across five trials. The sensors exhibit mean absolute errors under 1 N and max errors that are only a fraction of the intestinal perforation thresholds reported in the literature.11–15 The highest error reported among all the sensors during validation was 2.69 N, which corresponds to a stress of 26.94 kPa or 18% of the lowest reported intestinal damage threshold, which is 150 kPa.11,12 Regardless, utilization of safety factors is necessary due to inter-patient variability in intestinal tissue perforation forces.

The impact of FRSA inflation upon baseline sensor readings was also evaluated, as shown in Figures 5E and 5F, and the results are tabulated in Table 2; see also Video S2. The maximum absolute deviation from zero was recorded to be less than 0.2 N, indicating low crosstalk as the FRSA inflates.

Deployment mechanism

After a preliminary grip on the target intestinal segment has been established using the auxiliary suction functionality, the grasper deploys its sensorized pneumatic FRSAs via a miniaturized scissor-lift mechanism (MSLM), which is capable of adjusting the separation of the FRSAs anywhere between the closed configuration and a maximum gap of 40 mm. Given that the average diameter of the small intestine is approximately 25 mm, this grasper can envelop even severely dilated and diseased bowels.18

The MSLM, based upon a pantograph mechanism, consists of a rail with one pair of sliding hinges and one pair of fixed hinges; each hinge forms a pivot joint with one of four stainless-steel linkages (Figure 6A). The mechanism is actuated by pulling backwards on a plunger attached to the moving hinge of the central rail (Figure 6A), which results in the expansion of the MSLM (Figures 6B and 6C). The prongs of the MSLM always remain parallel to one another and the central rail, in contrast to traditional laparoscopic graspers whose “jaws” splay and are only parallel when in the fully closed configuration. The parallel prong design allows for a more even distribution of forces along grasped intestinal segments.

The MSLM is actuated using a linear motor located within the user handle, which is connected to the MSLM plunger via a 3 mm brass rod (Figures 6B and 6C). This brass rod will henceforth be referred to as the vertical actuation rod. The forward (extending) motion of the linear actuator closes the MSLM, decreasing the separation of the FRSAs, whereas the backward (retracting) motion of the linear actuator opens the MSLM, increasing the separation of the FRSAs. Although such a mechanism enables the user to control the vertical position (and thus the separation) of the prongs, it does not provide control over their horizontal position along the length of the instrument. Controlling the horizontal position of the FRSAs is necessary to deploy from behind the suction unit, thus granting the clinician unencumbered use of the vacuum functionality during the initial approach upon the target intestinal segment, and it allows the clinician to control the length of FRSA that extends past the tip of the instrument (i.e., the suction unit) for tighter, more stable grasping.

To independently control both the vertical and horizontal positioning of the prongs, the MSLM is encased in a modular carriage (Figure 6D). The modular carriage is connected to a second linear motor (also located in the handle of the device) via a 4 mm outer diameter (OD), 3.5 mm inner diameter (ID) brass rod (henceforth referred to as the horizontal actuation rod), which slides concentrically to the vertical actuation rod (Figures 6D and 6E). The arrangement of the linear motors necessary to control the vertical and horizontal positions of the FRSAs along the body of the instrument is shown in Figure 6E. This arrangement grants the surgeon decoupled control over the horizontal and vertical position of the FRSAs; when the linear motors move in tandem, the vertical position of the FRSAs is maintained while their horizontal position changes, and when only the linear motor controlling the vertical positioning rod moves, the separation of the FRSAs (the vertical position) is adjusted. This functionality is demonstrated in Video S3.

The horizontal and vertical positions of the FRSAs are controlled using a rocker switch (R1966IBLKBLKHF, E-Switch) and a potentiometric trigger (3102, Adafruit Industries; see Figure 2C). The rocker switch is momentary (non-latching) and adjusts the horizontal position of the modular carriage. The potentiometric trigger controls the separation of the FRSAs; pulling the trigger toward the user causes the grasper to close, whereas pushing the trigger away from the user causes the grasper to open.

Additional details on the design of the MSLM are reported in the supplemental information (Note S5).

Grasping validation

The device’s grasping capabilities were evaluated via in vitro and ex vivo testing. The proposed grasper end effector was equipped on a U5e Universal Robot Arm for grasp testing. The use of a robotic arm for these characterizations allowed better control of the grasper positioning and approach angle during grasping. With the grasper end effector equipped, the robot arm was programmed to descend vertically and establish an initial hold on the target intestinal segment using its distal suction unit, which was pulling a pressure of −60 kPa. The robot arm then ascended a few centimeters upwards with vacuum active, maintaining a suction hold on the tissue sample. The FRSAs were then inflated to a desired pressure, and the suction functionality was subsequently deactivated. Given that the surgeon’s working angle depends on trocar placement and target intestinal segment location, it was deemed necessary to evaluate grasping at different angles. Figure 7 depicts the MAIR end effector holding an explanted porcine small intestine at three angles that may be formed by the trocar-abdominal wall fulcrum: 90°, 50°, and 30° (see Video S4).

The same experimental setup was used to evaluate the end effector’s grasping capabilities in other clinically relevant scenarios. Figure 8A depicts the MAIR holding a silicone intestine with 500 g attached to represent a heavy specimen, and Figure 8B represents a scenario in which the local mesentery of the intestinal segment is left intact rather than cut to facilitate retraction. Figures 8C and 8D demonstrate that the end effector is capable of grasping explanted porcine small intestines that have been dilated with air and water, respectively, to diameters of or exceeding 3 cm. Such specimens represent the intestinal dilation that occurs in cases of small bowel obstruction19,20 (see Video S5). Each of these tests indicates the ability of the MAIR to effectively establish and maintain a hold on intestinal segments in diverse situations. No sign of trauma was observed on any of the ex vivo specimens after testing. Additionally, the integrated sensors did not register traumatic force levels in any of the tests.

We validated the lack of damage while grasping and holding ex vivo tissue. The testing consisted of establishing a vacuum hold on a target intestinal segment, lifting the intestine using only suction, and inflating the FRSAs to envelop the intestinal segment. During these procedures, suction was activated for no more than 2 min and was deactivated once the FRSAs had successfully enveloped the intestinal segment. The intestine was then held with the FRSA for 1 h and then visually inspected by a laparoscopically trained clinician. No sign of trauma was identified on the ex vivo samples after testing (see supplemental information).

End effector removal and anchoring to the abdominal wall

Retraction of organs during laparoscopy occupies clinical personnel and surgical access ports.34,35 The MAIR is designed to have a detachable end effector that can be anchored to the abdominal wall via a magnetically guided needlescopic anchoring system. This system was designed to exploit a magnetic coupling for alignment between an external handle and the detached grasper and then use a needlescopic approach to guarantee reliable anchoring. If desired, the surgeon can detach the end effector that is grasping the intestines and anchor it at a more surgically convenient location in the abdominal wall. This detachment provides the benefit of freeing the 18 mm trocar such that other surgical instruments can be used without the need for further incisions during the procedure. Multiple studies have demonstrated reduced pain, reduced hospital stays, lower complication rates, and better cosmesis as a result of needlescopic vs. laparoscopic approaches.54–56

Detachment of end effector

Detachment of the MAIR end effector is accomplished using an auxiliary tool capable of both grasping and heating, such as the electro-cautery device depicted in Figure 9A. This electro-cautery device consists of a traditional rigid laparoscopic grasper with coils of wire on the instrument jaws, which heat up via Joule heating upon the application of 3 W.

To initiate the detachment process, the positioning rods extend in tandem (maintaining the vertical position of the FRSAs but moving them forward horizontally) until a NdFeB magnet on the front of the modular carriage makes contact with an identically sized magnet on the distal wall of the instrument (D41AD-P, K&J Magnetics). The attraction between the two magnets connects the modular carriage (and thus the MSLM) to the instrument rail, mitigating relative motion and allowing them to be detached as one unit (Figure 9B). The surgeon then heat seals the pneumatic tubing of the FRSAs to prevent them from depressurizing upon detachment (Figure 9C). This can be accomplished using the same electro-cautery device, which seals shut the RenaPulse 37 tubings upon application of 2.5 V at 1.25 A to the copper coils. Successful sealing is verified by visual inspection. Upon disconnecting the pneumatic pressure supply, further visual inspection showing that the FRSAs remain inflated confirms proper sealing.

Once the pneumatic tubing leading to the FRSAs has been heat sealed, the user pushes a red latching push button on the user handle (Figure 2D) to disconnect the end effector from the body of the instrument. At this point, a 1.5 V power supply delivers 2 W to a pair of shape-memory alloy (SMA) wires (1053N4, McMaster-Carr) on the instrument rail. The SMAs are latched onto the end effector portion of the external rail via a 3D-printed component, which mechanically joins the end effector portion of the external rail to the rest of the instrument (Figure 9D). In this way, no power is required to ensure that the SMAs maintain the end effector connected to the body. The SMA is only actuated during the detachment phase for 60 s. Furthermore, the high surface area-to-volume ratio of the SMAs enables rapid heat dissipation when the power supply is deactivated. The SMAs are positioned on the sides of the device to minimize the likelihood of contact between tissue and the heating elements; however, the user would need to make sure that the area in which the SMAs reside is not in contact with tissues while actuating them. Joule heating of the SMAs causes them to bend into a programmed L-shape, which unlatches the end effector external rail and allows it (and the magnetically joined modular carriage) to be detached (Figure 9D).

The end effector is pushed off of the MAIR instrument body by the actuation rods, which move in tandem. The end effector slides off of four aluminum tubes that protrude from the instrument; these tubes, which supply vacuum to the suction unit and compressed air to the FRSAs, serve as pneumatic interfaces between the instrument body and the detachable end effector (Figure 9E). A servo motor located in the user handle (Figure 6E) rotates the vertical positioning rod, which has a 10 mm long M1 screw located on its distal tip, 180° counterclockwise relative to the user. This rotation disconnects the M1 screw from an M1 hex nut embedded in the MSLM plunger, detaching the vertical positioning rod from the MSLM plunger (Figure 9F).

The final stage of detachment requires the surgeon to manually pull the end effector off of the horizontal positioning rod (Figure 9G) using an auxiliary laparoscopic grasper, such as the custom electro-cautery tool depicted in Figure 9A; various grip points are located on the end effector external rail to facilitate this task (Figures 2A–2C). The modular carriage is joined to the horizontal positioning rod via an easily separable magnetic junction. The annular magnet contained within the horizontal actuation rod interface (R422-N52, K&J Magnetics) (Figure 9F) is attracted to an A36 low-carbon steel annulus (1388K144, McMaster-Carr) (Figure 9G) in the modular carriage, and these are separated by a 0.4 mm gap. This corresponds to a maximum attractive force of approximately 4.6 N. On the other hand, the two magnets that join the modular carriage with the external rail, which are in direct contact with one another, have an attractive force of 5.3 N. Thus, when the end effector is pulled, the magnet-steel junction between the modular carriage and the horizontal rod interface yields first.

Separation of the horizontal positioning rod from the modular carriage removes two copper-clad FR4 rectangular sliders that are slotted into the modular carriage (Figure 9G). While inside the modular carriage, these sliders physically separated two cylindrical magnets located within the MSLM plunger (D14-N52, K&J Magnetics) from discrete, magnetically attractive slots (B6301, K&J Magnetics). Now that the sliders are no longer present, the cylindrical magnets are free to move and insert themselves into the nearest magnetic slot; this locks the vertical position of the FRSAs within the detached end effector, thus preventing accidental widening or narrowing of the FRSA separation (Figure 9H). The detached end effector, depicted in Figure 9I, can now be anchored to the abdominal wall. The full end effector detachment process is depicted in Videos S6 and S7, and the overall functional capabilities are shown in Video S8.

If the heating from the SMA is a concern, we developed a design alternative of the MAIR such that the end effector is held in place without the SMA latches by purely using friction between the conjoined components. We have performed characterization of the strength and reliability of this alternative strategy and shown that the end effector requires 17.5 ± 7 N to be removed from the grasper body. This value was measured by conducting force testing in which the end effector was pulled via an Instron testing machine at a rate of 25 mm/min while recording the force necessary for separation. The friction at the interface between the end effector and the device body is sufficient to hold the end effector in place during grasping but can still be pushed off by the linear actuator during detachment.

Anchoring of end effector to abdominal wall

Once the end effector is detached from the instrument body, it can be utilized as a passive retraction mechanism. Using an auxiliary laparoscopic grasper, the surgeon positions the detached end effector so that it is in contact with the interior abdominal wall at a desired location. A 0.4 mm thick flexible magnet (5756K51, McMaster-Carr) is present on the back of the end effector. A 3D-printed magnetic anchor, which contains a 38.1 mm OD, 12.7 mm thick annular magnet with a 3.2 mm ID (RX828, K&J Magnetics), is positioned in the same location on the exterior abdominal wall to guide the end effector into place via magnetic attraction. However, reliance on only the magnetic attraction across the abdomen is not ideal given the variability in abdominal wall thicknesses,44,45 which affects the magnetic strength. Increasing magnets’ strength can lead to trauma to the internal wall of the abdomen.63 In the MAIR, we use a hybrid magnetic-needlescopic abdominal anchoring where the magnet is used to aid the alignment between the external handle and the internal end effector. When the external anchor and the end effector are separated by 25 mm, which is comparable to human abdominal wall thicknesses,44,45 the force of magnetic attraction is ≈0.3 N, which corresponds to a pressure on the abdominal wall of 1.18 kPa, substantially below the threshold of 46.75 kPa, which is known to cause damage.63 A needlescopic approach52 is used to withstand the retraction force needed to maintain the anchoring of the end effector. This consists of an M3 stainless-steel screw with an M1 screw at its tip that is inserted into the inner hole of the annular magnetic anchor and penetrated through the abdominal wall (Figure 10). Because the magnetic field generated by the magnetic anchor is strongest along its central axis, the end effector is inclined to align itself along this axis; this facilitates interlocking the needlescopic M1 screw with the M1 nut inside the end effector when the surgeon rotates the handle of the needlescopic rod. The surgeon then rotates a large M3 nut located on the needlescopic screw, securing the needlescopic mechanism and enabling the end effector to serve as a robust passive retraction system. More information on the magnetic anchoring approach can be found in the supplemental information (Note S4).

End effector retrieval

After the surgeon has completed intestinal retraction (Figure 11A), they can remove the end effector through the same 18 mm trocar from which it was deployed. To accomplish this, the surgeon begins by cutting or melting the sealed pneumatic tubing that maintained pressure in the FRSAs (Figure 11B). Once the FRSAs deflate, the surgeon can decouple the end effector from the needlescopic anchoring mechanism by rotating the external screw. Then, the end effector is detached from the abdominal wall by pulling on it with a laparoscopic grasper or the custom electrocautery tool (Figure 11C).

To recouple the body and end effector, the user inserts the MAIR through the 18 mm trocar port. Magnetic attraction between the distal end of the horizontal actuation rod and the end effector promotes recoupling in the correct orientation, and the surgeon can manually link the end effector into place with a standard grasper (Figures 11D and 11E). Once reattached, the vertical actuation rod pushes forward, collapsing the MSLM and FRSAs down to their original 18 mm diameter for removal (Figures 11F and 11G).

In vitro testing with abdominal phantom

To demonstrate the overall surgical workflow shown in Figure 1, the complete MAIR prototype depicted in Figure 2C was used in an in vitro abdominal simulator (see Video S9). The MAIR was operated via the handle shown in Figure 2. The abdominal simulator, depicted in Figure 12, is a mock-up of the laparoscopic workspace. Strata corresponding to the dermis, adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle layers of the abdominal wall were fabricated using Ecoflex 00–50 and Dragonskin 10 (Smooth-On) and are supported using acrylic scaffolding. DISCUSSION

In this paper, we present the MAIR, a soft robotic laparoscopic grasper for safe grasping and retraction of the intestine. The MAIR combines a suction functionality to isolate the target tissue, an expandable mechanism to enable grasping of severely dilated intestines, and soft actuation for safe tissue interaction to establish a robust, reliable grip on the intestine. This aggregation of features is intended to address the risk of iatrogenic trauma in laparoscopic procedures involving the small intestine, especially when attempting to laparoscopically manipulate dilated intestines.

Ex vivo and in vitro validations show that the device can grasp and retract bowel segments at different orientations, thus providing the user the ability to approach the tissue at multiple angles and orient it without the risk of losing the grasp. The MAIR can grasp intestines even when the mesentery is not cut thanks to the compliance of the FRSA, which can adapt to the shape of the intestine. Ex vivo testing showed a lack of damage to ex vivo tissues after being grasped and retracted by the MAIR, even when simulating severely dilated intestines. The MAIR complies with the constraints of MIS and is capable of entering the abdominal cavity via an 18 mm surgical port, but its expandable MSLM endows it with the ability to grasp and retract intestinal segments dilated to >3 cm. While 15 mm trocars are more common, 18 mm trocars have previously been documented and demonstrated in the literature of laparoscopic surgery and are clinically available.64–67 The 18 mm trocar can also be implemented as a port through the umbilicus, as is common in single port surgery, to minimize visible scarring.68

The detachability of the MAIR end effector means that the device can be converted to a passive retraction mechanism using a hybrid needlescopic-magnetic anchoring mechanism, freeing up the 18 mm access port for use by another instrument and eliminating the need for a clinician to maintain manual retraction of the intestine. Via the hybrid magnetic-needlescopic anchoring, it is possible to exploit magnetic attraction to align the external handle with the internal device and then exploit a non-surgical needlescopic incision to anchor the device to the abdomen. This architecture overcomes the limitations of purely magnetic anchoring (i.e., maximum magnetic force is a function of the abdomen thickness) and eases the process of adopting needlescopic approaches via magnetic guidance. Using the MAIR as a passive retractor frees up the initial trocar entry point for use by other surgical tools, while the needlescopic anchoring allows for positioning of the end effector such that the surgical target is properly exposed without performing an additional surgical incision. This passive retraction method can allow the surgeon and assistants to focus on different parts of the procedure rather than requiring personnel to actively maintain retraction. We also integrated soft sensors that detect excessive forces during grasping. The sensors were integrated as a supplementary safety feature of the soft actuators to further reduce any risk of trauma, especially in the case of grasping dilated intestines. To that end, the force range of the sensors was calibrated up to 10 N to detect potentially dangerous stress concentrations. These sensors can monitor forces in three directions, and by further developing their 3D sensing capabilities, they would enable additional features, such as slip detection. While such a feature is not essential for non-robotic surgery, since the surgeon has a view of the grasped intestine and can visually assess the stability of a grasp, such functionality could provide useful feedback to robotic surgical systems. To avoid overloading the surgeon with alarm fatigue, the device is not intended to provide a continuous stream of force-sensing values. Instead, the system is designed to cease inflation of the FRSAs if a potentially traumatic force threshold of 10 N is reached. Inflation of the FRSAs is then restarted to establish a safer grasp on the target intestinal segment. We believe that our device does expand on the state of the art in multiple ways. For example, while the device in O’Shea et al.49 enables lateral bowel movement, our use of bimanual robotic grippers enables grasping and retraction at different angles and positions within the insufflated abdomen. Building upon our device’s diverse grasping capabilities, we have also integrated sensing to provide the surgeon with force feedback, allowing them to adjust their angle and position to avoid harming the bowel. Adaptable grasping and force feedback, when taken together, allow our device to retract dilated bowels, too. Finally, the ability to disconnect the end effector from the main body of the robot to anchor it on the abdominal wall, while maintaining a grasp on the target intestine, enables passive retraction without the need for external vacuum lines or other forms of force transmission.

Building upon the preclinical validations introduced alongside the MAIR, future works will test the device with multiple users, both in vivo and in cadavers. In doing so, we will seek to understand the time requirement of the device in a controlled clinical setting and compare it to conventional laparoscopic methods. We expect that while the detachment and anchoring of the end effector will add extra time to the retraction phase of the procedure, this time may be recouped by ensuring a consistent surgical field of view and freeing personnel to perform more important tasks. Future user studies will be needed to compare the time needed for using the device with respect to standard instrumentation. Additionally, these subsequent validations will be necessary to further assess the safety of the device in in vivo settings.

Future works will also focus on transitioning from rapid prototyping techniques (i.e., stereolithography printing using ultraviolet-curable resin) to fabricating the instrument out of machined and injection-molded parts. Departure from rapid prototyping would ease the use of autoclavable materials. Improvement of manufacturing techniques would also enable further miniaturization of the MAIR. Using heat sealing allows the actuators to remain pressurized during passive retraction without the need to incorporate additional electromechanical components such as valves, which would increase the complexity and cost of the device. Miniaturization would provide the benefit of deploying the instrument through a smaller access trocar and could enable incorporating additional functionalities, such as check valves, to control the FRSAs’ pressure during detachment instead of using heat sealing. The use of heat sealing is an effective strategy, but it is irreversible; thus, if the user needs to actuate the FRSA again after anchoring, they would need to use a new end effector. Future user studies will also help determine which side of the tradeoff between size and procedural complexity is more important. In addition, decreasing the length of the detached end effector would also be beneficial, as that would reduce the vertical space that the device occupies in the abdominal cavity. If the length of the linkages were to be reduced, then it would also decrease the distance that the MSLM plunger needs to move in order to open the MSLM, as expressed in Equation 3. However, this would decrease the linkages maximum separable distance, which would diminish the device’s ability to grasp large organs. Therefore, the solution would be to stack linkages on top of one another, as is done in industrial scissor lifts to achieve greater heights. If Equation 4 is multiplied by a factor n, representing the number of stacked linkage pairs, and simplified, then the result is h=2nLsincos-11-xL+c. Because n,c, and L are constants, the total height of the MSLM h can be calculated as a function of the distance x moved by the plunger.

Finally, future work will feature improvements to device ergonomicity. Due to the presence of the linear motors and various other electronic components within the handle, the user interface of the MAIR is currently quite bulky (Figure 2); miniaturization of the MAIR control electronics would facilitate handheld usage of the device by the surgeon.

That being said, the MAIR has potential for use as an end effector in robotic surgery, especially for patients with pathologically dilated tissues or in situations where passive intestinal retraction is necessary. Laparoscopic procedures require extensive surgeon training, well-equipped facilities, and trained personnel in addition to the surgeon.34 With novel robotic instrumentation like the MAIR, we can increase safety, reduce procedural complexity, and free up both space and extra personnel for more important surgical tasks. This combination of factors can offset potential additional costs and setup time associated with the device.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

FRSA characterizations

The strokes of the FRSAs were characterized using an electromagnetic (EM) position tracking sensor (Aurora, Northern Digital) and image processing. Blocked force tests were performed on both bare FRSAs and sensor-equipped FRSAs up to inflation pressures of 250 kPa. The FRSAs were also subjected to blocked force testing using a force sensor (ATI Industrial Automation, NANO17) and a custom-built acrylic setup (see supplemental information; Figure S3B). Upon constraining the FRSAs, force measurements were obtained at the distal tip of each FRSA. Five trials were conducted for each of the three actuators. Additional details are reported in the supplemental information.

Soft sensor characterization

To characterize the magnetic force sensors’ response to an applied force in the normal direction, the Universal Robot end effector was equipped with a NANO17 force sensor (ATI Industrial Automation) (Figure 4A), and the robot was programmed to repeatedly perform 4 mm movements in the four cardinal xy directions (+x,-x,+y,-y), with the y axis parallel to the central axes of the soft PCBs, as depicted in Figure 4B. Each time the robot completed all four movements at a certain height along the z axis (Figure 4B), it would shift downwards 0.1 mm and repeat the four movements at the new z position; this was repeated 15 times. The data collection by the NANO17 force sensor was synchronized with the collection of magnetic field data from the soft magnetic force sensors, which communicate with an Arduino Nano via the I2^}C serial communication protocol.
