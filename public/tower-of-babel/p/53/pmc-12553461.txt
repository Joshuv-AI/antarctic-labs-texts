Circulating Mucosal‐Associated Invariant T Cells Are Associated With Acute Human Ischemic Stroke and Predict Poor Outcome
Authors: Tadashi Ozawa, Asako Chiba, Hiroko Hayakawa, Tsukasa Ohmori, Sachiko Miyake, Shigeru Fujimoto, Ryota Tanaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMucosal‐associated invariant T (MAIT) cells are involved in acute ischemic stroke in mice models. This study aimed to clarify the dynamics and role of circulating MAIT cells in patients with acute ischemic stroke.MethodsPatients with acute ischemic stroke were classified according to the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale into severe (score ≥ 10) and mild (score < 10) groups; outpatients with matched sex and age were selected as controls. Circulating MAIT cells, activation (CD69+), and cytokine production (IFN‐γ [interferon‐gamma] + and IL‐17 [interleukin‐17]+) on days 3, 10, and 17 after stroke, along with invariant natural killer T cells, gamma delta T cells, CD4+, and CD8+ T‐cell populations, were analyzed by flow cytometry. The relationship between MAIT cell dynamics and clinical outcomes was examined.ResultsOne hundred participants (30 severe, 40 mild, 30 controls) were included. On day 3, patients with severe stroke had a significantly lower proportion of MAIT cells than the mild group and controls (severe, mild, control [median]: 0.09%, 0.33%, 0.38%, respectively; P < 0.001), which gradually recovered on day 17. Severe stroke MAIT cells showed higher frequencies of CD69 expression and IL‐17 production. Multivariate analysis showed patients in the lowest MAIT cell population quartile on day 3 had a significantly higher probability of poor outcomes at 3 months than those in the highest quartile (odds ratio, 21.64 [95% CI, 1.41–331.58]; P = 0.027).ConclusionsAn early decrease in MAIT cells with higher activity and proinflammatory cytokine production correlated with stroke severity and poor outcomes, suggesting a significant role of MAIT cells in acute cerebral infarction and unfavorable outcomes.Keywords: gamma delta T cells, invariant natural killer T cell, ischemic stroke, mucosal‐associated invariant T cell, poor outcomeSubject Categories: Inflammation, Ischemic Stroke

Stroke remains the second leading cause of death after ischemic heart disease worldwide, with ischemic stroke accounting for 62.4% of all stroke cases. 1 Advances in intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy for the treatment of acute ischemic stroke have significantly improved patient outcomes. 2 Nevertheless, the benefits of aggressive recanalization therapies remain limited to only a few patients, leaving the majority with a diminished quality of life. 3 In addition to direct cell death caused by ischemia, excessive inflammation plays a significant role in exacerbating lesions during the acute phase of cerebral infarction. 4 However, the involvement of the immune and inflammatory mechanisms remains unclear, and no drugs targeting the immune system have been used in clinical practice for the treatment of ischemic stroke. Therefore, further elucidation of the immune mechanisms involved in cerebral infarction is required.

Mucosal‐associated invariant T (MAIT) cells are innate‐like T cells that account for approximately 0.1% to 10% of peripheral blood CD3‐positive cells in humans. 5 MAIT cells are involved in autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus, multiple sclerosis (MS), and rheumatoid arthritis, and it has become apparent that their effects extend beyond autoimmune diseases, including infections and malignant tumors. 6 We have previously reported that when mice with suppressed MAIT cell function were subjected to cerebral ischemia, the infarct lesion was significantly decreased by regulating neuroinflammation, and neurological prognosis was favorable. 7 These findings suggest that MAIT cells are involved in the pathophysiology of acute ischemic stroke.

However, to date, no studies have been conducted to determine the association between human acute ischemic stroke and MAIT cells. This study was conducted to investigate the dynamics, activation, and function of circulating human MAIT cells and to clarify their role in the prognosis of human acute ischemic stroke.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Participants

Study participants included patients with acute ischemic stroke and controls without acute ischemic stroke. Patients with acute ischemic stroke were recruited from patients admitted to Jichi Medical University Hospital. Control patients were enrolled from outpatients at Jichi Medical University Hospital, who were sex and age matched and included those with vascular risk factors or chronic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases. The inclusion criteria for patients with acute ischemic stroke were as follows: (1) age 20 to 89 years, (2) presymptomatic modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score 0 to 2, and (3) hospitalization within 48 hours of stroke onset. According to the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores at admission, patients were divided into the following 2 groups: mild (<10) and severe (≥10) stroke. 8 , 9 Patients who had an NIHSS score ≥ 10 at admission but improved by more than 10 within the initial 24 hours to NIHSS score < 10 due to intravenous thrombolytic therapy or endovascular therapy were classified into the mild group. The inclusion criteria for the control patients were as follows: (1) age 20 to 89 years and (2) mRS score 0 to 2. The exclusion criteria for patients with acute ischemic stroke and controls were as follows: (1) active malignant tumor, (2) autoimmune diseases, (3) use of immunosuppressants, (4) apparent infection symptoms at admission (patients with acute ischemic stroke) or at registration (control patients), (5) history of ischemic stroke within 1 year, and (6) brainstem or cerebellar strokes (patients with acute ischemic stroke).

Blood samples were obtained from patients with acute ischemic stroke on days 3, 10, and 17 after stroke onset, between 6 and 9 am. For control patients, blood samples were collected once on the day of enrollment. T‐cell populations, including MAIT, invariant natural killer T (iNKT), gamma delta T (γδT), CD4+ T, and CD8+ T cells, were evaluated using flow cytometry. The following clinical data were obtained: age; sex; premorbid mRS score; body mass index; and history of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, coronary heart disease, atrial fibrillation, ischemic stroke, alcohol consumption, and smoking. For patients with acute ischemic stroke, the infarct volume at admission, NIHSS score from admission to discharge, infection after admission, and mRS score at discharge and after 3 months were collected. Infection was confirmed if a temperature > 37.5 °C in 2 determinations or > 37.8 °C in a single determination was detected in patients with suggestive symptoms (ie, cough, dyspnea, urinary tract symptoms), white blood cell count >11 000/mL or < 4000/mL, pulmonary infiltrate on chest X‐rays, or cultures positive for a pathogen. 10 Additionally, favorable or poor prognosis groups were established based on the mRS score 3 months after stroke onset. Patients with favorable outcomes were defined as those without increasing disability at 3 months (mRS score 0 or 1 for patients without prestroke disability [mRS score, 0–1] and returning to prestroke mRS score for patients with prestroke mRS score 2); the remaining patients were defined as having a poor outcome. 11 To investigate the association between MAIT cells and the prognosis of ischemic stroke at 3 months, the proportion of MAIT cells on day 3 was stratified into 4 groups by quartiles (Q1: lowest group to Q4: highest group), and the relationship between the 4 groups and poor prognosis was examined.

Flow Cytometry

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from whole blood using a BD Vacutainer CPT™ Mononuclear Cell Preparation Tube with sodium heparin (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) in a density gradient centrifugation. The peripheral blood mononuclear cells were subsequently stimulated with phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate and ionomycin for 4 hours or maintained unstimulated on ice. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were cultured with TexMACS (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) and stimulated with phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (50 ng/mL; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and ionomycin (1 μg/mL; Sigma‐Aldrich) for 4 hours at 37 °C in a 5% carbon dioxide incubator. GolgiPlug (0.7 μg/mL; BD Biosciences) was added for the last 2 hours of stimulation to prevent cytokine secretion. Cells positive for Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 780 (eBioscience, Waltham, MA, USA) were electronically gated as dead cells and excluded from the analysis. The cells were incubated with FcR blocking reagent (BD Biosciences) to block nonspecific antibody binding. Cell surface staining was performed using the following monoclonal antibodies against human cell surface antigens: anti‐TCR PAN γ/δ‐FITC (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), anti‐CD69‐BUV395, anti‐CD3‐Brilliant Violet 480, anti‐CD4‐Brilliant Violet 605, anti‐CD8‐Alexa Fluor 700 (all from BD Biosciences), and anti‐TCR Vα7.2‐PE (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) (Table S1). MAIT cells were identified using 5‐OP‐RU‐loaded MR1 (major histocompatibility complex class I‐related protein) tetramer‐Brilliant Violet 421 (National Institutes of Health Tetramer Core Facility, Atlanta, GA, USA) and iNKT cells were identified using PBS‐57‐loaded CD1d tetramer‐APC (National Institutes of Health Tetramer Core Facility). After staining cell surface antigens, cells were fixed and intracellular cytokines were stained using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit (BD Biosciences), anti‐IL‐17 (interleukin‐17) A‐PE/Cyanine7, anti‐IFN‐γ (interferon‐gamma)‐PerCP/Cyanine5.5 (all from BioLegend), or their isotype control antibodies (Table S1). Data were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa X‐20 (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.9.0; TreeStar, Ashland, OR, USA). MAIT cells were identified as CD3+ TCR PAN γ/δ− PBS‐57‐CD1d tetramer− TCR Vα7.2+ 5‐OP‐RU‐MR1 tetramer+ cells, γδT cells as CD3+ TCR PAN γ/δ+ cells, and iNKT cells as CD3+ TCR PAN γ/δ− PBS‐57‐CD1d tetramer+ cells. CD4+ T cells were identified as CD4+ CD3+ cells and CD8+ T cells were identified as CD8+ CD3+ cells, excluding MAIT, γδT, and iNKT cell populations (Figure S1, S2).

Assessment of Cerebral Infarct Volume

Diffusion‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography was used for stroke volume segmentation. We used the 3D Slicer Software, which is an open‐source medical image computing platform for biomedical research and is freely downloadable (3D Slicer, available: http://www.slicer.org). Stroke segmentation was performed semiautomatically slice by slice for every data set using the Segment Editor tool, and the volume was automatically calculated using the Segment Statistics tool (Figure S3).

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using JMP Pro 17.2.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The planned number of patients recruited was 60 with acute ischemic stroke (30 mild and 30 severe) and 30 controls. The sample size estimation was based on previous results of comparable studies, 12 , 13 assuming 80% power at a significance level of 0.05. The characteristics of the participants were compared using the chi‐square test for categorical variables and the t test for continuous variables such as age, body mass index, body temperature, and blood pressure. For other continuous variables with nonnormal distributions, the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used for 2‐group analysis, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for 3‐group analysis. In the flow cytometry analysis, the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used for comparisons between the group with ischemic stroke and the control group. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons between the mild ischemic stroke, severe ischemic stroke, and control groups; in the mild group between days 3, 10, and 17; and in the severe group between days 3, 10, and 17. The Steel–Dwass test was used for comparisons between each pair of groups. The correlation between 2 continuous variables was analyzed using Spearman's correlation coefficient. To determine the association between MAIT cells and the prognosis at 3 months, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated using multivariate logistic regression analysis.

Ethical Approval and Consent to Participate

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jichi Medical University Hospital for Clinical Research (approval no. 20–135). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their families if the patient was unable to sign the consent form.

Results

Baseline Cinical Characteristics of Enrolled Patients

A total of 70 patients with acute ischemic stroke (40 with mild ischemic stroke and 30 with severe ischemic stroke) and 30 control participants were enrolled. The baseline clinical characteristics and vascular risk factors did not differ among the 3 groups; however, the prevalence of atrial fibrillation was significantly high, which was associated with a high prevalence of cardioembolic stroke in patients with severe ischemic stroke (Table 1, Figure S4). The initial NIHSS score (median: 19.0 [interquartile range (IQR), 13.8–24.0] in the severe group, 2.5 [IQR, 1.3–5.0] in the mild group; P < 0.001) and stroke volume (61.0 cm 3 [IQR, 37.3–178.9] in the severe group, 1.1 cm 3 [IQR, 0.1–2.9] in the mild group; P < 0.001) were significantly higher in the severe group than in the mild group. The mRS score and the prevalence of poor prognosis at 3 months were significantly higher in the severe group than in the mild group (mRS score: 5 [IQR, 3.8–5] versus 1 [IQR, 0–2.8]; P < 0.001; poor prognosis 28 [93.3%] versus 14 [35.0%]; P < 0.001), and 6 patients in the severe group died within 3 months.

Comparison of Circulating MAIT, γδT, and iNKT Cells After Acute Ischemic Stroke

We performed a comparative analysis of MAIT, γδT, and iNKT cell populations in all patients with acute ischemic stroke relative to the control group (Figure 1A). On day 3, a statistically significant decrease in MAIT cells was observed in the group with stroke (stroke, control [median: 0.16% [IQR, 0.09–0.42], 0.31% [IQR, 0.19–0.91], respectively; P = 0.002). Conversely, no significant alterations were detected in γδT or iNKT cells. No significant changes were observed on days 10 or 17 in any of the cell populations compared with controls.

Circulating MAIT, γδT, and iNKT cells from patients with mild and severe ischemic stroke were analyzed and compared with controls (Figure 1B‐E). Because the number of iNKT cells was very small and insufficient to calculate the proportion of CD69 positivity and production of IFN‐γ or IL‐17 among iNKT cells, no data could be provided. The proportion of MAIT cells in the severe group on day 3 was significantly lower than that in the control group (severe, mild, and control [median: 0.09% [IQR, 0.07–0.15], 0.33% [IQR, 0.15–0.92], and 0.38% [IQR, 0.19–1.09], respectively; P < 0.001). The same trend was observed on days 10 and 17; however, the proportion of MAIT cells progressively recovered, and a statistically significant increase in the proportion of MAIT cells was observed in the severe group from day 3 to days 10 and 17. The proportion of γδT cells tended to decrease slightly in the severe group; however, the difference was not statistically significant. The proportion of iNKT cells exhibited a statistically significant decrease on day 3 in the severe group compared with controls; however, this difference was less pronounced than that in MAIT cells (Figure 1B).

The proportion of CD69‐positive MAIT cells was significantly higher in the severe group than in the control group, and this trend persisted until day 17 (Figure 1C). A similar trend was observed in the mild group compared with controls on days 10 and 17. γδT cells exhibited a substantial increase in CD69 positive cells in the severe group on days 10 and 17 (Figure 1C).

An increase in the proportion of MAIT cells producing IL‐17 was particularly notable only in the severe group on days 3 and 10 compared with controls (Figure 1D). In contrast, no definite differences in IL‐17‐producing γδT cells were observed between the mild and severe groups and the controls. Whereas, IFN‐γ production decreased in both MAIT and γδT cells (Figure 1E). IFN‐γ‐producing MAIT cells significantly decreased in the severe group on day 3 and in the mild group on days 3, 10, and 17 compared with controls. IFN‐γ‐producing γδT cells were also significantly decreased in the severe group on days 3, 10, and 17, and in the mild group on days 3 and 17, compared with controls.

Analysis of T cells (CD3+), CD4+, and CD8+ T cells revealed no statistically significant differences over time when comparing patients with acute ischemic stroke and control group (Figure S5A). When comparing the mild and severe acute ischemic stroke groups with the control group, a slight increase in CD4+ T cells was observed in the mild group and a slight decrease in CD8+ T cells was observed in the severe group; however, neither change was statistically significant compared with controls (Figure S5B).

Association of Innate‐like T‐Cell Characteristics and Poststroke Infection

Poststroke infections were significantly higher in patients with severe stroke than in those with mild stroke (Table 1). No statistically significant differences were found in the features of MAIT, γδT, or iNKT cells between infected and noninfected patients. Even within the severe stroke group, which had a high prevalence of infections, no significant differences in MAIT cells were noted between the infected and noninfected patients (Figure S6A). There were also no significant differences in CD69, IL‐17, and IFN‐γ expression in MAIT cells (Figure S6B‐D). However, a lower proportion of MAIT and γδT cells was correlated with an earlier onset of infection in the group with severe stroke (Figure S7A, B), whereas the percentage of iNKT cells did not correlate with the infection onset date (Figure S7C).

Aging and Circulating MAIT Cells in Patients With Acute Ischemic Stroke

A number of circulating MAIT cells have been shown to physiologically decrease and change their characters over age. 14 In this study, the proportion of circulating MAIT cells decreased with age in the control group (Figure. S8A). In both the mild and severe stroke groups, as well as in the controls, the proportion of MAIT cells tended to decrease with age (Figure S8B, C). Although there was no association between age and the expression of CD69 or IFN‐γ in the control group and the group with stroke, IL‐17 expression significantly increased with age only in the control group (Figure S8D‐L).

Association Between Circulating MAIT Cells and Stroke Severity or Short‐Term Outcome

A significant inverse correlation was observed between the NIHSS score at admission and the proportion of MAIT cells on day 3 (r = −0.4262, P< 0.001) (Figure 2A). However, there was no significant correlation between the NIHSS score at admission and the proportion of γδT or iNKT cells on day 3 (Figure 2B, 2C).

No correlation was observed between the proportion of γδT cells on day 3 and mRS score at 3 months (Figure 2E). A significant inverse correlation was observed between the proportion of iNKT cells on day 3 and the mRS score at 3 months (r = −0.2707, P = 0.023); however, there was noticeable variability depending on the case (Figure 2F).

Higher CD69 expression in MAIT cells on day 3 tended to be associated with poor functional outcomes, and higher expression on days 10 and 17 showed a significant association (Figure 3D‐F). IL‐17 expression in MAIT cells on day 3 tended to be associated with poor functional outcomes, but this association was significantly greater on day 10 (Figure 3G‐I). However, there was no clear association between IFN‐γ expression in MAIT cells and the outcomes (Figure 3J‐L).

Proportion of MAIT Cells on Day 3 Predicted the Short‐Term Poor Prognosis

We investigated the relationship between the proportions of MAIT, γδT, and iNKT cells during the acute phase of cerebral infarction and poor prognosis at 3 months. There were significant differences between the groups with favorable and poor prognosis in terms of age, atrial fibrillation, ischemic stroke subtype, NIHSS score at admission, infection, and administration of recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (Table 2). Among MAIT, γδT, and iNKT cells, only MAIT cells exhibited a statistically significant difference, demonstrating a notable decrease in the proportion of MAIT cells in the poor outcome group.

Discussion

Sterile inflammation plays a critical role in brain damage during acute cerebral infarctions, 4 and circulating immune cells, including T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils, infiltrate brain lesions from the day of onset. 15 , 16 These infiltrating immune cells play a critical role in exacerbating neuroinflammation and are associated with poor outcomes after ischemic stroke. 4 In contrast, ischemic brain injury influences the peripheral immune system and is associated with the immune deficiency syndrome after stroke. 17 , 18

Unconventional T cells, such as MAIT, γδT, and iNKT cells, recognize a variety of self‐ and non‐self‐antigens presented by nonclassical major histocompatibility complex molecules and have innate and adoptive characteristics. 19 MAIT, γδT, and iNKT cells play important roles in various disease processes and have received considerable attention in central nervous system disorders, including stroke. 20 Our data demonstrated that the proportion of circulating MAIT cells, but not iNKT or γδT cells, was significantly reduced in the early phase of ischemic stroke compared with controls. This significant change was more specific to the severe form of ischemic stroke but not to the mild form. Furthermore, the proportion of MAIT cells on day 3 correlated with the severity of the NIHSS score at admission and poor outcome at 3 months.

γδT cells are the most investigated innate‐like T cells in ischemic stroke. γδT cells infiltrated the ischemic hemisphere and significantly increased in number and proportion on day 3 after transient focal cerebral ischemia in mice. 21 IL‐17A secreted by γδT cells leads to neutrophil recruitment, and blockade of the IL‐17A pathway significantly decreases infarct volume and improves clinical outcome. 21 IL‐17 has also been associated with a delayed phase of ischemia/reperfusion injury, and depletion of γδT cells significantly reduced inflammatory cytokine levels in infiltrating cells and ameliorated ischemic brain injury in mice. 22 In an analysis of patients with acute ischemic stroke, circulating γδT cells have been reported to be reduced in number or proportion compared with healthy controls. 12 , 23 , 24 Another study showed that the proportion of circulating γδT cells was higher in the acute stage of atherosclerotic infarction than in the controls. 25 Circulating γδT cells produce inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐17 during acute ischemic stroke; however, the association between circulating γδT cells and stroke volume or severity is controversial. 12 , 24 , 26

The association between iNKT cells and stroke has received less research attention. Mice treated with α‐galactosylceramide, a glycolipid antigen that specifically activates iNKT cells, resulted in an increased number of iNKT cells both in the peripheral blood and ischemic hemisphere and significantly increased infarct volume with worse neurological deficits. 27 These detrimental effects were accompanied by elevation of TNF‐α (tumor necrosis factor‐alpha) and IFN‐γ levels in both the serum and ischemic brain. 27 However, other studies failed to demonstrate neuroprotective effects in iNKT‐deficient mice (CD1d−/−) after focal cerebral ischemia. 28 , 29 In some analyses of patients with acute ischemic stroke, no consistent trend has been observed regarding alterations in the number of circulating iNKT cells. 13 , 23 , 24 A decrease in the number of circulating iNKT cells has been associated with disease severity and poor prognosis, 18 whereas CD69 activation in iNKT cells correlates with disease severity, 26 suggesting a potential relationship with poor prognosis; however, the underlying mechanism remains unclear.

In addition to temporal proportional changes in circulating MAIT cells, these cells increased their activity in patients with both mild and severe acute ischemic stroke and the production of the proinflammatory cytokine IL‐17 in patients with severe stroke on days 3 and 10 compared with that in the control group. Increasing evidence suggests that MAIT cells are associated with the pathogenesis of various diseases. 6

Peripheral blood MAIT cells generally decrease with advancing age. 14 Conversely, the rate of CD69‐positive MAIT cells is elevated, and the production rates of IFN‐γ and IL‐17 increase with advancing age. 14 We found a similar trend of age‐dependent decrease in MAIT cells in the control group, and higher expression of CD69 and IL‐17, but not INF‐γ, was observed in this study. A similar trend in the proportion of MAIT cells was also observed in patients with stroke, but the age‐dependent expression of CD69 and IL‐17 was weaker compared with controls. The decrease in circulating MAIT cells was much larger in patients with severe stroke compared with those in the control group, and its activation and IL‐17 production rates also increased, although there was no significant difference in age between the controls and patients with stroke. Thus, we believe that circulating MAIT cells react to acute ischemic brain injury regardless of aging.

A reduction in circulating MAIT cells has been observed in various diseases including systemic lupus erythematosus, MS, diabetes, obesity, and cancer 6 . Although the number of MAIT cells decreases in the peripheral blood, these can infiltrate and exhibit increased activity in affected tissues. 6 In patients or models of MS, a representative autoimmune disorder of the central nervous system, the proinflammatory or regulatory role of MAIT cells in MS lesions remains unresolved. 30 Circulating MAIT cells are significantly reduced in patients with relapsing remitting MS compared with healthy controls, and the decreased frequency of MAIT cells is more profound in disease activities. 31 Other studies have demonstrated a reduced proportion of circulating MAIT cells in primary progressive MS but not in relapsing remitting‐MS or healthy controls. 32 In Alzheimer's disease (AD), MR1 molecules are substantially elevated in microglia surrounding plaques in the brains of both mice and humans. 33 MAIT cells increase in number and are highly activated in the AD mice brain, whereas MR1‐deficient AD mice have a reduced plaque burden compared with wild‐type AD mice. 33 Changes in the frequency and activation of MAIT cells in the liver of AD mice have also been reported, indicating the importance of circulating MAIT cells in AD pathology. 34 These data suggest that MAIT cells play an important role not only in neuroimmunological but also in neurodegenerative disorders.

In our study, the proportion of MAIT cells in the severe group decreased on day 3 and then rapidly recovered on days 10 and 17 compared with that on day 3. In the early stage of ischemic stroke, large cerebral ischemia induces excessive neuroinflammation. Brain inflammation peaks during early ischemia and then gradually decreases. 4 Previous studies have shown that circulating MAIT cells decrease during severe inflammation in viral infections and MS and recover after the peak of inflammation. 31 , 35 Similarly, circulating MAIT cells decreased in blood during the acute phase of severe ischemic stroke. Thus, we think that the decreased proportion of MAIT cells reflects the early severity and excessive neuroinflammation after stroke. Moreover, the marked rebound of MAIT cells is supposed to reconstruct circulating MAIT cells due to the decrease of neuroinflammation in the subacute stage of ischemic stroke. There are 2 potential explanations for the reduction of circulating MAIT cells during periods of high disease activity. Circulating MAIT cells may decrease due to their migration and recruitment to the ischemic brain. 7 The reduction of MAIT cells is also due, at least in part, to activation‐induced cell death. 36 These potential synergistic effects will be associated with the reduction of circulating MAIT cells. Further investigations are necessary to confirm the pathophysiology of MAIT cell dynamics during acute ischemic stroke.

Activation or increased levels of IL‐17 production in MAIT cells are associated with disease status, as well as the reduction of MAIT cell frequency in noninfectious diseases. 37 Increased IL‐17 production by MAIT cells is observed in the peripheral blood in type 2 diabetes, obesity, and ankylosing spondylitis, and the increase in IL‐17 production has also been detected in affected tissues. 6 , 38 , 39 These results indicate that the status of circulating MAIT cells can predict the pathogenesis of the affected tissue in each disease. 6 IL‐17 is an important proinflammatory cytokine that promotes neuroinflammation after ischemic stroke. 40 IL‐17 derived from γδT cells amplifies the inflammatory cascade and deteriorates infarct tissue. 22 , 41 MR1 deficient mice reduce IL‐17 production in the ischemic hemisphere, resulting in attenuation of microglial response and reduced infarct volume with less neurological severity after focal ischemic stroke. 7 These data support our current findings that MAIT cell activation and increased IL‐17 production are actively involved in the pathophysiology of ischemic brain injury and are associated with poor outcomes after human ischemic stroke.

In the current study, MAIT cells and γδT cells produced less IFN‐γ in the acute ischemic stroke group than in the control group. Cerebral infarction induces hyperactivation of the sympathetic nervous system and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis. 42 , 43 This cascade suppresses the immune response and inhibits IFN‐γ production in the peripheral circulation. 42 , 43 Reductions have been demonstrated in serum IFN‐γ levels and elevations in IL‐17 levels at 1, 3, and 7 days in patients with acute ischemic stroke compared with healthy controls. 43 In a study investigating innate‐like T cells in response to severe coronavirus disease 2019, MAIT, γδT, and iNKT cells demonstrated decreased IFN‐γ and increased IL‐17 production compared with that in healthy individuals. 44 These findings support our current results that circulating MAIT cells suppress IFN‐γ production and upregulate IL‐17 production during acute ischemic stroke.

In this study, the severe group, which had significantly lower circulating MAIT cells, experienced significantly more infections and recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt‐PA) administration than the mild group. A meta‐analysis of poststroke infections reported that pneumonia was associated with poor outcomes. 45 Among these studies, one investigation demonstrated that the association with poor outcomes was no longer statistically significant in the multivariate analysis, and stroke severity, as measured by the NIHSS, had the greatest impact on poor outcomes, which was same as our findings. 46 Our data showed no statistical difference in circulating MAIT cells between patients with and without infection, that suggest poststroke infection did not affect the number or behavior of MAIT cells. However, a reduced proportion of MAIT and γδT cells was associated with earlier onset of infection in the severe stroke group. These findings may suggest that patients with severe stroke are at an early risk of infection through an immunosuppressive mechanism.

The administration of rt‐PA is the first line and strongly recommend therapy for patients with hyperacute stroke, but it also has toxic effects such as mobilization of immune cells, disruption of the blood–brain barrier, activation of glial cells, and enhancement of excitotoxic tissue damage. 47 Our data showed that administration of rt‐PA did not affect any differences in the proportion of MAIT cells (Figure S9A), CD69‐positive MAIT cells (Figure S9B), and the production of IL‐17 (Figure S9C) or IFN‐γ (Figure S9D) on days 3, 10, and 17 in the severe stroke group. Thus, we think that rt‐PA administration is not associated with MAIT cell behavior. In contrast, the mild stroke group included 3 patients who received revascularization therapy (rt‐PA and mechanical thrombectomy, n = 2; rt‐PA only, n = 1) and exhibited rapid improvement. These patients demonstrated characteristics comparable to those in the mild stroke group with respect to cell proportion (Figure S10A), activity (Figure S10B), and production of IL‐17 (Figure S10C) and IFN‐γ (Figure S10D). These findings indicate that the recruitment and behavior of circulating MAIT cells are more drastic and can be flexibly changed depending on the magnitude of ischemic brain damage.

Our data showed that the proportion of MAIT cells on day 3 of cerebral infarction was significantly associated with a poor prognosis at 3 months. Recent studies have investigated the potential of MAIT cells as prognostic indicators for various diseases. In patients with liver failure, a decrease in MAIT cells was an independent cause of viral infection and was associated with poor long‐term survival rates. 48 Some subsets of circulating MAIT cells have also been shown to be predictors for patients who respond to anti‐PD‐1 (programmed cell death protein 1) immunotherapy in patients with nonsmall cell lung cancer. 49 The low cytotoxic profile of MAIT cells, based on frequency, Ki‐67, and granzyme B levels, may represent a promising prognostic factor for lupus nephritis remission 1 year after induction therapy. 50 These data and our current findings suggest that the frequency and activity of MAIT cells could be potential biological indicators of a poor short‐term prognosis in acute ischemic stroke.

The impact of a reduced proportion of MAIT cells on functional outcomes at 3 months persisted on days 10 and 17. Regarding MAIT cell activity and cytokine production, higher CD69 expression on days 10 and 17 and IL‐17 expression on day 10 were also significantly associated with poor functional outcomes. These phenotypic changes in MAIT cells might reflect the impact of neuroinflammation in cerebral ischemic lesions or systemic activation of MAIT cells by ischemic stroke. Thus, not only reduced proportion of MAIT cells but also continuous activation or IL‐17 production in circulating MAIT cells might be related to poor prognosis. Although a higher proportion of CD69 positive MAIT cells was observed over time, no clear association was observed with the proportion of MAIT cells (Figure S11A‐C). This is consistent with the result that CD69‐positive MAIT cells increased in both the groups with mild and severe stroke. In contrast, an inverse correlation was identified between the proportion of MAIT cells and IL‐17 production (Figure S11D‐F). Comparatively, the correlation between the proportion of MAIT cells and IFN‐γ was weaker than that with IL‐17 (Figure S11G‐I). These results suggest that circulating MAIT cells are readily activated and lower proportion of MAIT cells and increased IL‐17 expression are associated with poorer prognosis after ischemic stroke.

This study had some limitations that should be considered. First, not all the patients were evaluated until day 17. The reason for this was that most severe cases were fatal, whereas mild cases were transferred to other hospitals earlier. Second, the number of patients enrolled was relatively small: 70 patients with acute ischemic stroke and 30 control patients. Third, based on our findings, it is challenging to confirm whether the dynamics of circulating MAIT cells are a cause or a consequence of the severity of the initial stroke. To confirm the roles of MAIT cells, further sophisticated experiments are warranted using not only human blood samples but human brain tissue with acute ischemic stroke.

Conclusions

In conclusion, circulating MAIT cells were significantly reduced but were more activated and had increased IL‐17 production during the acute stage of ischemic stroke, and this was more pronounced in patients presenting with severe ischemic stroke. The reduction in the proportion of circulating MAIT cells, but not other innate‐like T cells, in the early phase of ischemic stroke was a good predictor of poor outcomes at 3 months. Previous and current data indicate that MAIT cells are involved in the pathophysiology of acute ischemic stroke. Further studies are warranted to better understand the pathological role of MAIT cells in human ischemic brain injuries.
