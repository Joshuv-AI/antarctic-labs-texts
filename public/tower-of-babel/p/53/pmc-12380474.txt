Creating Colostomies for Sacral Pressure Ulcers: A Single‐Centre Retrospective Study
Authors: Hsu‐Ping Tseng, Yuan‐Sheng Tzeng, Shu‐Yu Wu, Chien‐Ju Wu, Lin‐Yin Wang, Shih‐Wei Yang, Shih‐Hung Liu, Ching‐Han Chang, Kuan‐Hung Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFaecal contamination of sacral pressure ulcers occurs frequently, so, theoretically, faecal diversion using colostomies is a useful procedure. We retrospectively analysed the data of adult patients for whom colostomies were created to enhance wound healing and compared patients with sacral pressure ulcers who received colostomies and those who did not during the same period. Patients' characteristics analysed included age, gender, comorbidities, WBC count, serum CRP level and microbial profile (before creating colostomy). Additionally, we examined whether the wound was closed, the recurrence rate after wound closure, and mortality outcomes. Regression analysis indicated that colostomy creation was associated with fewer species of gut microbiota cultured and lower rates of wound dehiscence after closure; no association was found between colostomy and mortality. Colostomies help promote wound healing of sacral pressure ulcers after closure by eradicating wound infection, and do not increase patients' mortality rates.Keywords: colostomy, faecal diversion, mortality, pressure ulcer, wound dehiscence, wound infection

Introduction

The National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP) defines a pressure injury as localised damage to the skin and underlying soft tissue, usually over a bony prominence or associated with using a medical or other device [1]. Approximately 70% of pressure ulcers occur over the sacrum, ischial tuberosity and greater trochanter [2]. Once the ulcer has established, contamination by faeces appears to deteriorate the healing process [3, 4]. Faecal incontinence increases the probability of pressure ulcers in the general adult hospitalised population by 22 times [4, 5]. Thus, theoretically, faecal diversion with colostomies is useful to enhance the healing process of pressure ulcers, if they do form.

However, results of recent studies are divergent and some suggest that creating colostomies is a risky procedure. For example, limited data derived from a cohort of bedbound patients supported better healing and decreased recurrences after colostomy [4, 6], and another recent study that identified patients who underwent elective diverting colostomy for sacral pressure ulcers found a high mortality rate [7].

In our clinical experience with such patients, that is, those who received perioperative care for both colostomy creation and pressure ulcer reconstruction, lower rates of mortality were observed as well as higher rates of wound reconstruction. Given this discrepancy between previous studies and our experience, this study aimed to re‐evaluate the effects of creating colostomies in treating sacral pressure ulcers, including mortality rates and enhancement of wound healing.

Patients and Methods

This retrospective cohort study reviewed the medical records of 100 inpatients in a tertiary centre from 2019 to 2023 who had visible faecal contamination of pressure ulcers near the anus. The option of faecal diversion with colostomy was discussed with the patients and their families, and finally 23 patients underwent the operation and were included in the present study. Only adults above 20 years of age were included. All data were analysed retrospectively.

Patients who had already undergone local or distant flaps for wound closures were excluded because only limited choices of flaps remained and wound closure was more difficult. If patients had a microbial profile recorded for different wounds, medical records were reviewed and if the microbial profiles were not clearly labelled for a certain wound, that patient was excluded as well. Only patients who underwent surgical debridement by plastic surgeons were included to ensure consistent techniques and table judgement.

Main Outcome Measures

Patients ages, sex, comorbidities, white blood cell (WBC) count, serum C‐reactive protein (CRP) level, serum albumin level, microbial profile (before colostomy creation, if created) and outcomes were recorded. Outcomes included the times of surgical debridement, microbial profile after the creation of colostomy, whether the wound was reconstructed, 1‐month follow‐up after wound reconstruction, length of hospital stay, and mortality.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were made using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and the independent t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate.

The primary outcome measures were the rates of wound closure and mortality. Secondary outcome measures included the counts of microorganisms on wound culture and the rates of wound dehiscence. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

To compare the counts of microorganisms on wound culture between the non‐colostomy, pre‐colostomy and post‐colostomy groups, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. Post hoc comparisons were performed using the Tukey–Kramer test. For paired comparisons between pre‐colostomy and post‐colostomy groups, the paired t‐test was used.

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to analyse the differences in age between groups defined by wound closure status, complete healing and mortality. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and a p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among the 23 patients who underwent colostomy creation for faecal diversion due to pressure ulcers, five patients had more than one wound requiring microbial culture, including one perianal abscess. Three patients underwent local or distant flaps for closure before creation of colostomies. One patient never underwent surgical debridement. A total of 14 patients were enrolled for further analysis.

Among the 77 patients who did not undergo creation of colostomies, 29 patients had more than one wound requiring microbial culture. A total of 12 patients underwent local or distant flaps for closure before 2019. One patient had more than one wound requiring microbial culture and underwent local or distant flaps for closure before 2019. Four patients had undergone creation of colostomy already before 2019. As a result, only 31 patients were enrolled in the non‐colostomy group.

Sex, age, habit of smoking and comorbidities were similar between the non‐colostomy and colostomy groups. All patients had Stage 3 or 4 pressure injuries, and the distribution was similar between both groups. WBC counts before the first surgical debridement were similar, while serum CRP levels and ASA scores were significantly higher in the colostomy group. Both groups had similar mortality rates and achieved similar rates of wound closure after receiving standard wound care in our institution (Table 1). All deaths in the colostomy group were reviewed. Causes included recurrent pneumonia (n = 1), metastatic cancer (n = 1), blood stream infection with sepsis (n = 1) and cachexia with palliative withdrawal (n = 1). One‐month mortality was recorded separately (Table 2). Patients in the colostomy group before colostomy creation had similar counts of micro‐organisms on wound culture to those of patients in the non‐colostomy group (Figure 1).

Discussion

Faecal diversion with stoma for the treatment of pressure ulcers is controversial, mainly because the high mortality rate and yet low healing rate discussed in previous literature cannot outweigh improved quality of life. Authors of a retrospective review of all disabled patients who underwent surgery for medically intractable pressure ulcers from 1993 to 2001 concluded that stoma construction is a safe procedure with low morbidity and mortality that helps heal pressure ulcers and decreases the incidence of recurrence. They also found that quality of life and bowel care were also much improved by the colostomy [6]. A descriptive analysis also championed the idea of considering stoma formation in cases of medically intractable pressure injuries located in the anal region, especially before any reconstructive surgery [8]. But another study included 27 consecutive patients who underwent colostomy for healing of pressure ulcers and found that 15% of patients died. In that study, only 32% of patients had completely healed ulcers during follow‐up. Thus, the authors considered that the high mortality and low rate of pressure ulcer healing should temper enthusiasm for performing colostomy in this setting [9]. The construction of an intestinal stoma is fraught with complications and should not be considered a trivial undertaking [10]. Similarly, a recent study that identified patients who underwent elective diverting colostomy for sacral pressure ulcers reported a high mortality rate of 6.7% at 30 days postoperative. According to the authors of that study, diverting colostomy performed for patients with sacral pressure ulcers is associated with a substantial risk of postoperative death [7]. However, mortality is associated with factors including age of patient, urgency of surgery and diagnosis [11]. In the present study, both the non‐colostomy and colostomy groups had similar rates of short‐term in‐hospital mortality. According to this result, we suggest that the high mortality rate is a result of the comorbidities of the patients, and diverting colostomy should be considered safe and does not alter mortality.

A recent retrospective cohort study investigated the impact of a stoma in the context of the treatment of anus‐near pressure ulcers in patients with spinal cord injuries. The authors concluded that stoma patients need more time until complete wound healing without any other benefits regarding adverse events, conceivably related to selection bias [4]. However, in our experience, other aetiologies such as insults to the central nervous system also resulted in a large proportion of patients with anus‐near pressure ulcers. Thus, we included all patients with pressure ulcers in our study, regardless of the underlying aetiology. Furthermore, to reduce selection bias, we excluded patients who underwent local flap reconstruction. Such patients, if still searching for complete healing, left surgeons with a limited choice of flaps and have greater soft tissue defects due to previous flap loss. This condition may alter the result of the study, so patients who underwent local flap reconstruction are excluded from our investigation.

Many factors are involved in wound healing. Successful eradication of infection is one of the keystones, which we attempted to aid with the creation of colostomies and avoiding faecal contamination. To detect faecal contamination, we performed wound biopsies rather than superficial wound swabs to avoid skin flora from contaminating the specimen. We also documented results including Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium, Streptococcus, Enterobacteriaceae, Enterococcus, Clostridium, Lactobacillus and Ruminococcus, which are the predominant luminal microbial genera and can be identified in stool [12], as well as Acinetobacter species which are also reported to colonise in the gut [13, 14]. After colostomy creation, the count of micro‐organisms listed above on wound culture was significantly lower compared to that in both the non‐colostomy group and pre‐colostomy group, suggesting a lower bacterial burden from faecal contamination. However, a recent matched case–control study demonstrated different results [15]. The study, which was limited to patients with spinal cord injury and included all bacterial species cultured, found that the most common species cultured was Staphylococcus spp., and the only significantly different primary colonisation affected was Escherichia coli . While E. coli was found in the stoma patients less often, the time to complete cure took longer in the stoma group. In our study, we excluded Staphylococcus spp., collected data regarding gut microbiota only, and found yeast, which was cultured less often in patients with colostomy, the only significantly different species between groups. Although E. coli—a reliable indicator of faecal contamination—did not show a difference between groups in our study, we observed a significant reduction in the counts of microorganism species following stoma creation when all gut microbiota were considered. Based on the observation that patients with stomas experienced fewer wound dehiscences following pressure ulcer reconstruction, we believe that faecal diversion through stoma formation and the subsequent reduction of gut microbiota at the wound site may play a role in promoting wound healing.

There have been attempts to reduce faecal contamination without diversion, one of them being negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT). A recent retrospective study showed that NPWT effectively decreased the risk of faecal contamination of wounds in comparison to conventional dressing methods [16]. Airtight wound sealing is essential for the maintenance of negative pressure of proper intensity and therefore there is almost no possibility of solid or liquid material seeping into the dressing [16], if the wound sealing can be maintained properly. However, moist skin in the perineal area makes it difficult to maintain an effective seal of the occlusive dressing used in NPWT. Furthermore, in cases of faecal incontinence, the dressings may become saturated with stool, further compromising their ability to remain sealed. In our study, 20 patients in the non‐colostomy group and five patients in the colostomy group received NPWT. A total of 11 (44%) patients experienced negative pressure dressing failure (loss of airtight), requiring immediate repair by a physician. In some cases, stool infiltration into the dressing necessitated urgent removal. After stoma creation, 12 patients received NPWT, and failure of negative pressure dressing was only noted in two (16.7%) patients. Although the sample size was limited, a noticeable decrease in the rate of negative pressure failure was observed following stoma creation.

There are non‐invasive faecal management systems that can achieve the same goal of faecal diversion. A study retrospectively evaluated 15 patients who made use of the ConvaTec Flexi‐Seal Faecal Management System (FMS), and concluded that FMS can be considered before stoma creation [17]. Another retrospective review recorded 50 patients and suggested that intrarectal catheters such as the FMS are safe and effectively contained faecal material when used judiciously and checked regularly [18]. Most patients in this retrospective review experienced no complications (37, 74%), while four (8%) experienced temporary anal atony, and two (4%) suffered excessive leak of stool around the device. But although we had the capability of implanting FMS in our hospital and reported our experience, demonstrating the feasibility and effectiveness of the faecal management system in the treatment of perianal wounds [19], elder patients or younger patients with spinal cord injury often have absent or minimal anal tone, making catheter expulsion highly likely and cannot be used most of the time. Besides, diet change was needed for soft or liquid stool to pass through the catheter smoothly without occlusion [19]. Such diet changes are frequently intolerable to young patients with spinal cord injury, and diarrhoea can cause complications such as dehydration and kidney problems [20]. A comparison of diverting colostomy and bowel management catheter applications in Fournier Gangrene cases demonstrated shorter hospital stay and decreased total costs by shortening total duration of hospital stay [21]. The relief on economic burden may vary as in our healthcare system, hospitalisation, debridement and stoma creation surgeries are covered by National Health Insurance, while bowel management systems such as the ConvaTec Flexi‐Seal Faecal Management System are not supported according to current regulations. Four patients in our study received faecal diversion by FMS, and one was before stoma creation. Patient 18 in the non‐colostomy group received FMS for 21 days and experienced catheter dislodgement three times. The wound was sutured after placement of the faecal diversion pouch, and the faecal collection tube was not reinserted after its third dislodgement. Patient 20 experienced poor wound healing following the failure of negative pressure dressings due to faecal infiltration into the dressing. The wound was subsequently sutured after faecal diversion was achieved using FMS. On Day 18, the faecal collection tube dislodged; by that time, the wound had already healed. Despite the instability of the FMS, both patients were fortunate to achieve successful wound healing. However, Patient 22 in the non‐colostomy group never had the wound sutured after dehiscence due to faecal contamination and FMS rescue. The patient was never suited for surgery, as he developed severe gastrointestinal tract bleeding and acute renal failure followed by demise as a complication from major cardiac surgery. Patient 11 in the colostomy group also experienced wound dehiscence due to faecal contamination that could not be rescued by FMS. After dislodgement of the faecal collection tube, the patient agreed to stoma creation, and the wound completely healed after five additional surgical debridements. Although there were only four cases, we observed that in patients with pressure ulcers contaminated by faecal incontinence, FMS could not be consistently maintained in the correct position, and its success appeared to depend to some extent on chance.

In addition to preventing contamination with its occlusive dressings, NPWT also promotes wound healing by stabilising the wound environment, reducing wound oedema/bacterial load, improving tissue perfusion, and stimulating granulation tissue and angiogenesis [22]. With the increasing adoption of NPWT, we incorporated NPWT into our surgical treatment and strategy years ago, especially if the initial defect was large [23]. Closed‐incision negative pressure wound therapy (ciNPWT) also represented an alternative reconstruction option for patients with pressure ulcers, especially in those for whom prolonged anaesthesia is unsuitable in our hospital [24], while studies regarding ciNPWT for reconstruction of sacral pressure sores showed encouraging results by reducing wound dehiscence and infection [16]. In cases where FMS fail to reliably divert stool and maintain the effectiveness of NPWT, performing a stoma to prevent faecal contamination and preserve NPWT function may be considered as a viable option to promote wound healing.

As routine, while nursing staff assist paralysed patients with repositioning every 2 h, we also assess and clean up their bowel movements. Nurses report to physicians if patients have more than three bowel movements per day or experience diarrhoea, allowing for appropriate adjustment of antidiarrheal medications. In our hospital's general wards, a family member or caregiver usually stays with the patient and assists in patient care. Nursing staff provide instruction on repositioning techniques and bowel care to these caregivers, both to enhance the quality of care during hospitalisation and to support continued care after discharge. We consulted infectious disease doctors for antibiotics and provided the wound culture results to aid in their decision. We also adopted aggressive wound care suggested in previous literature if NPWT was not used, including quarter‐strength sodium hypochlorite or alternative solution, wet‐to‐moist twice a day, and as needed after bowel movement (BM) [25]. Silver dressings, including commercial foam dressing containing silver which provide additional pressure relief other than regularly changing body positions, were also included as alternative wound coverage. Although the rate of pressure ulcer healing was still low (32.26% and 57.14% in the two groups, respectively), specifically, patients in the colostomy group whose wounds were successfully reconstructed had a lower rate of wound dehiscence, along with fewer species of bacteria discovered after the creation of colostomy Thus, we consider eliminating faecal contamination of sacral pressure ulcers with the creation of colostomies to be an effective method by which to promote wound healing.

Other factors such as age and nutrition status may alter the result. In our study, age was not related to the rate of wound closure, wound dehiscence after closure, or mortality. As for nutrition status, a previous study conducted at our institute concluded that albumin level was associated with the recurrence of pressure ulcers [26]. But in this study, we focused on the rate of wound dehiscence 1 month after closure, and albumin level was not associated with wound dehiscence. Faecal contamination may have played a more significant role than nutrition status in the early stages of wound healing. However, with the retrospective nature of the present study, this is not a true double‐blind study and results cannot be generalised to other populations. Despite that, the medical team trained in the same hospital and attended weekly in‐service meetings to promote and strengthen wound care standards, substantial differences may still be observed between medical researchers in perioperative care.

Within 1 month after reconstruction of pressure ulcer, 17 patients in the non‐colostomy group experienced poor wound healing, compared to two patients in the colostomy group. Among the 17 patients in the non‐colostomy group, two were lost to follow‐up. Of the remaining 15, seven were readmitted for further treatment, and eight underwent a total of 19 additional debridement surgeries during the same hospitalisation. In the colostomy group, one patient declined further surgical intervention, while Patient 4 underwent four additional debridement procedures during the same hospital stay, which lasted 86 days. Patient 4 also suffered from the most severe form of soft tissue infection: necrotising fasciitis. Prior to undergoing the first wound reconstruction procedure, the patient had already undergone 12 debridement surgeries. Additionally, Patient 11, who was categorised into the colostomy group based on the inclusion criteria, actually underwent five surgical debridement procedures before receiving a stoma due to poor wound healing caused by faecal contamination. Following stoma creation, the patient underwent five additional debridement and flap surgeries and eventually achieved successful wound healing. The total length of hospitalisation of Patient 11 was 121 days. These two patients contributed to a significantly higher number of debridement procedures in the colostomy group compared to the non‐colostomy group. The hospital stays of the colostomy group were also slightly longer, although the difference was not statistically significant.

Besides, a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis found that in the setting of prevention after anterior resection, patients with loop ileostomy demonstrated significantly more complications related to the stoma than patients with loop colostomy [27, 28]. Although there is no definite evidence that suggests that ileostomies have a higher mortality rate, in our hospital, colorectal surgeons always opted for transverse loop colostomies while performing faecal diversion for patients with pressure ulcers to prevent complications. Parastomal infection and wound dehiscence were the only stoma‐related complications observed, and both occurred in Patient 11 in the colostomy group. This patient was critically ill, admitted with severe pneumonia complicated by septic shock, and developed a severe pressure ulcer infection due to faecal contamination. Prior to stoma creation, the patient's serum albumin level was only 2.7 g/dL, and the CRP level was elevated at 16.44 mg/dL. Although the pressure ulcer ultimately healed well following stoma creation, the patient unfortunately passed away during hospitalisation due to recurrent pneumonia complicated by septic shock. Patient 4 in the colostomy group, previously described as the case with necrotising fasciitis, also had Stage IV prostate cancer. After 12 weeks of hospitalisation, the family opted for palliative care. Life‐sustaining interventions were subsequently withdrawn, and the patient passed away. Additionally, Patient 5 in the colostomy group was admitted due to a newly diagnosed pontine infarction and subsequently died from fungemia complicated by septic shock, while Patient 7, who suffered from cachexia, ultimately opted for palliative care. As a result, the in‐hospital mortality rate in the colostomy group was higher than that in the non‐stoma group, although the difference was not statistically significant.

As a retrospective study, we could not analyse the quality of life after stoma creation. A recent review of literature found nine out of 11 studies reported an improvement in quality of life (QoL) after stoma formation in patients with spinal cord injury, while the other two studies showed no difference [29]. Another previous study demonstrated improvements to quality of life by colostomy in patients with medically intractable pressure ulcers [6]. But stoma formation itself is associated with psychosocial problems, including anxiety, depression, poor body image, sexual problems, impairment in social relations and adjustment problems [30], etc. This may explain why Patients 8, 12 and 13, despite successful wound healing after reconstruction, still opted to undergo stoma reversal surgery within 3–6 months following stoma creation. All three were victims of spinal cord injury. Notably, Patient 13 returned for treatment due to a recurrence of pressure ulcers 6 months after undergoing stoma reversal surgery. Following a second stoma creation and successful pressure ulcer reconstruction, the patient has not requested another stoma reversal. We look forward to conducting a prospective study to demonstrate a positive impact on postoperative life quality with the colostomy‐first policy from our colorectal surgeons, further affecting patients' decisions on whether to receive ostomies or not.

Conclusions

Although previous studies have demonstrated that colostomy for the healing of pressure ulcers results in high mortality rates and low rates of healing, the present study conducted over the past 4 years suggests otherwise. In patients with visible faecal contamination of pressure ulcers and who did not undergo any local or distant flaps, results showed similar rates of patient demise during hospitalisation between patients who did and did not undergo colostomy creation. After closure of the wounds, both the bacterial load and the rate of dehiscence were lower in the colostomy group.

As a result, we conclude that colostomy may promote wound healing of sacral pressure ulcers by eradicating wound infection, and appears to be a safe procedure that does not increase patient mortality. However, given the small sample size and the fact that patients in the colostomy group tended to be younger and had fewer comorbidities such as hypertension, coronary artery disease and smoking history, we suggest that colostomy may be most appropriate for selected patients with similar characteristics.
