Some Considerations on the Treatment and Prognosis of the Most Common Malignant Tumors of the Larynx
Authors: Fernando López, Primož Strojan, Alfio Ferlito
Published: 2024

Introduction

The most common laryngeal tumor is squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). As histopathological studies have progressed, the incidence of this type has decreased from 99% of all laryngeal malignant tumors in the 1970s to approximately 85–90% in recent years [1]. Currently it is recognized that a significant minority of patients with laryngeal cancer present with either a variant of SCC or a histology other than SCC. Because these cases are relatively rare, less information is available about their prognosis and treatment and they are based on assumptions regarding studies of patients with the conventional SCC. In addition, the different specific histological subtypes also indicate possible specific prognostic features. The phenotype should be considered an important factor in making therapeutic decisions [2].

Although several histological subtypes have been described [3], arising from mucosal, cartilaginous and neurovascular structures, their low incidence and the difficulty in achieving an accurate diagnosis make the conclusions of studies inconsistent. Concerning typical carcinoid, atypical carcinoid, small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, and large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the larynx, it is virtually impossible to obtain an accurate incidence of these tumors. The overall literature is significantly clouded by probably misdiagnosed cases, a situation that is understandable owing to the application of differing diagnostic criteria and lack of standardization in diagnostic terminology [4]. In this editorial, we will refer to those subtypes for which the diagnosis is precise, and the studies are conclusive.

General Considerations

The main objective of laryngeal cancer treatment is to get the best possible cancer outcome while minimizing side effects that may interfere with normal breathing, swallowing, and speaking. When treating laryngeal tumors, we have various options, such as surgery, radiation therapy (RT), chemotherapy (ChT), molecular targeted therapies, and immunotherapy, which can be applied individually or in conjunction. Typically, patients in the early stages (I–II) receive single-modality therapy, such as surgery or RT. However, those with advanced-stage tumors often need more intensive treatment that involves multiple modalities. Primary treatment choices are influenced by the patient’s performance status, medical comorbidities and preferences, the extent and location of the tumor, the treatment team’s expertise, the adequacy of follow-up care, and long-term expectations for voice and swallowing functions.

Conventional SCC

Conventional laryngeal SCC is characterized by its aggressive behavior and propensity to metastasize to regional lymph nodes, particularly from non-glottic regions. The treatment for patients with laryngeal SCC is determined by the disease’s stage and the tumor’s location. At the time of diagnosis, about 60% of patients are found to have advanced-stage (stage III or IV) disease [5].

There are three main ways to treat early stage (T1-T2N0) laryngeal SCC: RT, transoral surgery (TS), and open partial laryngectomy [6]. However, the latter is usually avoided because it tends to cause more functional problems compared with RT and TS [7]. Something important to consider with supraglottic cancers is the chance, even at this early stage, of cancer cells having spread to the lymph nodes in the neck (greater than 20%). Because of this, treating the neck is usually part of the plan. The neck is typically treated with the same approach (surgery or RT) used for the main tumor [8]. Either treatment option, surgery or RT, achieves excellent cure rates and functional outcomes. The chances of achieving local control after RT or TS are similar, ranging from 85 to 95% [7]. An advantage of RT is that it can be used for all patients with T1–T2N0 SCCs; in contrast TS is not suitable for patients with tumors that are difficult to expose or for those with certain anatomical limitations or with diffusely spreading tumors with no clear margin, particularly when crossing the anterior commissure to the contralateral cord. Additionally, patients with significant medical issues who are not good candidates for anesthesia may be better treated with RT. For glottic tumors, it is important to consider that a larger resection can lead to poorer voice quality. Therefore, RT might be the preferred option for patients who have high demands for maintaining voice quality. Whichever approach is chosen, clinicians should monitor their own patients’ functional and oncological outcomes rather than relying on the best results from leading institutions. Evaluation of outcomes should include tumor control, survival rates, functional outcomes (such as voice quality), and larynx preservation rates.

The primary objectives in treating locally advanced laryngeal SCC (T3–T4) are to achieve local disease control, ensure patient survival, and, whenever feasible, preserve the structure and functions of the larynx (including speech, swallowing, and airway patency). The discussion regarding the optimal treatment approach for locally advanced laryngeal cancer has been longstanding and remains unresolved. For many years, total laryngectomy, followed by postoperative (ChT)RT for high-risk cases, was the only treatment option for patients with intermediate to advanced laryngeal SCC. In addition, either ipsilateral or bilateral neck dissection should be performed, depending on the location of the tumor and the extent of nodal metastasis, if present [6]. Over the past two decades, significant advances have been made in the management of this disease, with different approaches focused on laryngeal preservation changing the treatment landscape. In contrast to the common practice of total laryngectomy, non-surgical approaches are often referred to as “organ-sparing” strategies. Many institutions view these as distinct from surgical approaches, although surgical and nonsurgical organ preservation strategies are also available. The essential aim of both approaches is to preserve laryngeal function. A new paradigm has emerged in which both surgical and nonsurgical approaches have equal value in preserving laryngeal function. According to treatment guidelines, concurrent RT with cisplatin, with salvage surgery in cases of persistent disease, is the current standard of care for patients with late T2 and T3 laryngeal SCC seeking non-surgical organ preservation. Alternatively, induction ChT may be used, followed by definitive RT or ChT-RT, or surgery based on clinical response. With these approaches, about two-thirds of patients retain their larynx. However, in T4a tumors, there are much fewer candidates with sufficiently preserved laryngeal function for nonsurgical larynx spearing treatment and also laryngeal preservation rates are significantly lower in these cases, often resulting in complications that additionally compromise laryngeal function. In addition, patients with T4a tumors who undergo initial surgery have better overall survival compared with those treated with ChT-RT, making total laryngectomy the recommended approach in these cases [9].

Prognostic factors affecting survival in laryngeal SCC have been explored and have identified that age, sex, performance status, comorbidities, tumor stage and subsite, type of therapy, and treatment of recurrences are closely related to this outcome. Five-year disease-specific survival is excellent in early-stage patients (stage I > 95% and stage II > 80%) and can be considered good (70–75%) in intermediate stage patients (stage III) [7, 10–14]. In advanced disease stages (stage IV) 5-year survival rates drop to 50–60%. The main cause of death is local–regional recurrence, with distant metastasis being infrequent (less than 10%) [7].

Verrucous Carcinoma

Laryngeal verrucous squamous cell carcinoma (LVSCC) is a unique pathological and clinical subtype of well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, accounting for 1–3.4% of laryngeal cancers [15]. Confirming LVSCC through histology is often challenging and typically requires multiple biopsies, which can result in significant delays in making a diagnosis [16].

At diagnosis, 80% of tumors are classified as T1 or T2. Historically, LVSCC has been seen as relatively resistant to radiation, making surgery the main treatment choice. The extent of the surgery is based on the T stage: smaller tumors are treated with local excision, while larger tumors might necessitate partial or total laryngectomy [17]. Laser cordectomy has proven effective for early-stage patients [18]. Total laryngectomy is rarely needed since less than 5% of patients have locally advanced disease [17]. True LVSCC cases do not show cervical or distant metastases, and enlarged lymph nodes in these patients usually exhibit only reactive changes on histologic examination, making elective neck treatment unnecessary [3, 19]. However, hybrid LVSCC cases with SCC foci on histopathology should be treated as aggressively as conventional SCC, and regional therapy (neck dissection or post-operative RT) should be considered on the basis of the primary tumor’s pathological characteristics. Other treatment options for LVSCC include RT and ChT, which are recommended for patients unable to undergo major surgery or those with extensive tumors requiring total laryngectomy [19]. RT is also suitable for older patients and those with multiple health issues. There is currently no high-level evidence of adverse effects of RT for LVSCC, such as anaplastic transformation [20].

Local control is the most important clinical issue in LVSCC given its locally destructive nature. As such, LVSCC has a favorable prognosis with local control rates ranging from 77 to 100%, regardless of T stage or therapy [17]. Overall survival in patients with LVSCC exceeds 80%, which is significantly higher than in other types of laryngeal malignancies (5-year overall survival of 61%) [6, 17].

Spindle Cell Carcinoma

Spindle cell carcinomas (SpCCs) account for 2–3% of larynx tumors [21]. These tumors can be misdiagnosed as reactive lesions or mesenchymal malignancies because of their confusing morphological features. Many names have been applied to this neoplasm over the years, including carcinosarcoma or sarcomatoid carcinoma [22].

Surgery is generally the preferred primary treatment for SpCC, as it has been shown to offer better outcomes than RT [21, 23]. SpCC tumors are often resistant to RT and are more prone to recurrence with nonsurgical treatments [21]. For tumors staged T2 or below, a conservative approach with partial laryngectomy may be considered, with the focus on preserving organ function surgically. However, T3–T4 tumors usually require total laryngectomy, sometimes combined with lymph node dissection. The role of adjuvant RT remains uncertain as disease-specific survival rates are comparable whether patients receive postoperative RT or not [21]. RT is usually reserved for cases of disease recurrence after salvage surgery. Similar surgical treatment principles apply to both SCC and SpCC lesions, supporting elective surgical management of the neck in SpCC cases, particularly when the primary lesion is advanced.

Dubal et al. [21] in a large population-based analysis found a 5-year survival rate of around 74% with superior outcome of 84.0% for glottic tumors compared to 51.9% for nonglottic tumors. Both local recurrence and distant metastasis are common in SpCC. The clinical behavior of SpCC is similar to that of SCC, where outcomes are influenced by factors, such as tumor size, location, and stage. Poor prognostic factors include tumors diagnosed at higher T stage, large tumors (> 3 cm), regional or distant metastases, prior radiation, nonglottic tumors, histological presence of necrosis, and loss of immune-expression of epithelial markers [21, 22]

Basaloid Squamous Cell Carcinoma

Basaloid squamous cell carcinoma (BSCC) is a very rare subtype of laryngeal cancer, making up less than 1% of all cases [24]. This subtype of cancer is often linked to the presence of the human papillomavirus (HPV) [24]. However, there is disagreement on whether HPV affects how well patients respond to treatment [25]. Unlike regular SCC, BSCC tends to be more advanced at diagnosis [26]. This means patients with BSCC are more likely to have the cancer spread to nearby lymph nodes in their neck at the time of diagnosis. Studies show that up to two-thirds of BSCC patients have lymph node involvement, with some even having very advanced neck disease (13% with N2b and 18% with N2c stages) [27]. BSCC is also more likely to have spread to distant parts of the body compared with regular SCC. At diagnosis, about 12% of BSCC patients have distant metastases compared with only 3% of patients with SCC. This number can be even higher, reaching 35–50%, when following patients for a longer period [27].

Literature supports surgery as the primary treatment approach for most cases of laryngeal BSCC. However, early-stage laryngeal tumors (T1 and T2, N0) are typically treated with either primary RT or TS. The specific type of surgery depends on the location and extent of the disease. Studies have shown that a supraglottic BSCC recurs more often in patients who had a limited surgery compared with a control group consisting of patients with conventional SCC. This suggests that caution should be taken when recommending conservative surgery for patients with BSCC [28]. Advanced-stage tumors often require total laryngectomy followed by postoperative RT. However, some researchers have proposed organ-preserving protocols, similar to those used for conventional SCC, for locally advanced BSCC, considering the high risk of distant metastasis and surgical morbidity [29]. Therapeutic neck dissection is recommended if nodal metastases are detected on preoperative imaging, and elective neck dissection is advised for cN0 necks owing to the high likelihood of occult cervical lymph node metastases [30].

Patients with BSCC tend to have a lower survival rate at 5 years (around 50%) compared with those with regular squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) (around 60–70%) [31]. However, some studies suggest that survival rates might be similar for BSCC and SCC if patients have the same cancer location, stage, and treatment [32].

Chondrosarcoma

Despite representing only 1% of laryngeal tumor pathology, chondrosarcomas are the most frequent nonepithelial neoplasms [33]. Chondrosarcoma is a slow-growing malignant tumor that occurs in 10–20% of cases in the head and neck area [34]. In the larynx, they are primarily located on the inner surface of the posterior part of the cricoid cartilage, but it can also be found in the thyroid, arytenoid, and epiglottis cartilage. Chondrosarcomas are divided into grades on the basis of their histopathology [35]: grade I or low-grade (locally aggressive) and grade II or intermediate-grade are well-differentiated tumors. Grade III are high-grade lesions. Other less common types of chondrosarcomas are dedifferentiated, myxoid and clear cell chondrosarcoma. Laryngeal chondrosarcomas are less aggressive than in other locations and low-grade and intermediate-grade chondrosarcomas are the most common. They are slow-growth tumors, more commonly diagnosed in the early stages. Moreover, metastases rarely occur [33].

There are no official guidelines regarding the treatment of laryngeal chondrosarcoma. The treatment is based mainly on surgical excision, which can be achieved endoscopically or by open technique. Larynx-preserving surgeries (even without free margins) should be prioritized for this cancer because results of such procedures do not reduce the survival rate and enable maintenance of a functional larynx [36, 37]. Endoscopic and median thyrotomy surgeries are usually limited to smaller lesions [38–40]. They involve partial removal of tissue, making it challenging to assess the surgical margins and increasing the likelihood of recurrence. Partial laryngectomies are typically difficult owing to the significant removal of the posterior cricoid cartilage, which results in an unstable subglottic airway [39]. For larger lesions and high-grade tumors, total laryngectomy is indicated [36]. ChT and RT are not recommended as initial therapies, although successful treatment with definitive proton radiotherapy have been reported recently [41].

The prognosis for patients with laryngeal chondrosarcoma is good with a 10-year disease-free survival of over 90% [33, 34], regardless of the type of surgical approach used.

Conclusions

SCC is the most common histological type of laryngeal carcinoma. However, there are other histologies that are less common but may have different nuances in the treatment and condition the prognosis. Though in locally advanced classical SCC, nonsurgical treatment seems to have gained the upper hand, in more aggressive variants, such as BSCC, or SpSCC, or in chondrosarcomas, surgery remains the main therapeutic option.
