The cumulative impact of clinical risk on brain networks and associations with executive function impairments in adolescents with congenital heart disease
Authors: Melanie Ehrler, Anna Speckert, Oliver Kretschmar, Ruth Tuura O'Gorman, Beatrice Latal, Andras Jakab
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with congenital heart disease (CHD) demonstrate altered structural brain network connectivity. However, there is large variability between reported results and little information is available to identify those patients at highest risk for brain alterations. Thus, we aimed to investigate if network connectivity measures were associated with the individual patient's cumulative load of clinical risk factors and with family‐environmental factors in a cohort of adolescents with CHD. Further, we investigated associations with executive function impairments. In 53 adolescents with CHD who underwent open‐heart surgery during infancy, and 75 healthy controls, diffusion magnetic resonance imaging and neuropsychological assessment was conducted at a mean age of 13.2 ± 1.3 years. Structural connectomes were constructed using constrained spherical deconvolution tractography. Graph theory and network‐based statistics were applied to investigate network connectivity measures. A cumulative clinical risk (CCR) score was built by summing up binary risk factors (neonatal, cardiac, neurologic) based on clinically relevant thresholds. The role of family‐environmental factors (parental education, parental mental health, and family function) was investigated. An age‐adjusted executive function summary score was built from nine neuropsychological tests. While network integration and segregation were preserved in adolescents with CHD, they showed lower edge strength in a dense subnetwork. A higher CCR score was associated with lower network segregation, edge strength, and executive function performance. Edge strength was particularly reduced in a subnetwork including inter‐frontal and fronto‐parietal‐thalamic connections. There was no association with family‐environmental factors. Poorer executive functioning was associated with lower network integration and segregation. We demonstrated evidence for alterations of network connectivity strength in adolescents with CHD — particularly in those patients who face a cumulative exposure to multiple clinical risk factors over time. Quantifying the cumulative load of risk early in life may help to better predict trajectories of brain development in order to identify and support the most vulnerable patients as early as possible.

INTRODUCTION

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common congenital malformation, affecting approximately 1% of all neonates (Van Der Linde et al., 2011). About one third of these children require infant cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) surgery. Improved surgical and medical management in recent decades has led to reduced mortality and morbidity and a consequent shift of attention toward the long‐term outcome of patients with CHD. Despite favorable cardiac outcome in the majority of such patients, neurodevelopmental impairments are frequently observed across the lifespan and have been recognized as the most prevalent noncardiac comorbidity in CHD (Marino et al., 2012). A meta‐analysis found an increased risk of mild intellectual and executive function impairments in children and adolescents with CHD who had undergone CPB surgery. Importantly, all executive function domains were similarly affected with no predominant pattern. These domains included working memory, inhibition, cognitive flexibility, fluency, and planning (Chandrasekharan et al., 2021). Importantly, evidence indicates that executive function problems persist into adulthood (Schlosser et al., 2021). These difficulties have been associated with lower quality of life (Sanz et al., 2018), higher need for educational support (Spillmann et al., 2023), and co‐existing socio‐behavioral difficulties and anxiety symptoms (Ehrler et al., 2023). Understanding the neuronal alterations that lead to neurodevelopmental impairments is essential given their impact on everyday functioning.

Neuroimaging studies have elucidated brain alterations in patients with CHD compared to healthy individuals. Fetal studies showed that brain development is altered intrauterine, and brain developmental trajectories continue to be impaired after birth. Fetuses with complex CHD exhibit reduced brain growth and delayed cortical development, at least partly comparable to preterm babies (Donofrio et al., 2011; Khalil et al., 2016; Peyvandi et al., 2021). Brain growth remains altered across the pre‐ to postnatal periods (Ortinau et al., 2018; Rollins et al., 2021) and is associated with a higher risk of postnatal white matter injury (Peyvandi et al., 2021). Studies using diffusion‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) in neonates with CHD have demonstrated altered integrity of white matter tracts (Birca et al., 2016; Hagmann et al., 2016; Karmacharya et al., 2018; Kelly et al., 2019; Miller et al., 2007; Mulkey, 2014) and structural network connectivity (Bhroin et al., 2020; Feldmann et al., 2020; Schmithorst et al., 2018). According to these studies, brain development in CHD is characterized by altered network‐level characteristics of the structural connectome. These connectomic changes likely result in less efficient neuronal communication and delayed maturation of structural networks (Bhroin et al., 2020; Feldmann et al., 2020; Schmithorst et al., 2018). A recent study found that lower preoperative network integration was associated with a higher severity score calculated from alterations of fetal substrate delivery: how much the cardiac malformation alters typical fetal circulation. In contrast, postoperative network integration was associated with such perioperative factors as longer bypass and ECMO times (Votava‐Smith et al., 2022). The clinical relevance of these alterations is underlined by another study, which demonstrated that parent‐reported externalizing problems at the age of 2 years were associated with lower edge strength of a frontal‐limbic network assessed preoperatively (Bonthrone et al., 2022).

Currently, evidence on whether the altered network connectivity observed in neonates persists throughout the lifespan is limited. Although a body of research has shown alterations of microstructural integrity in several white‐matter tracts of adolescents and adults with CHD (Brewster et al., 2015; Easson et al., 2020; Ehrler et al., 2022; Rivkin et al., 2013; Watson et al., 2018), only two studies have investigated structural network connectivity beyond the neonatal period. One study in adolescents with transposition of the great arteries (TGAs) showed higher network segregation and associations with lower cognitive performance in patients relative to healthy controls (Panigrahy et al., 2015). A study in 13 children with single‐ventricle physiology and 13 controls did not detect any group differences in network integration or network segregation (Williamson et al., 2023).

Across these dMRI studies, between‐study variability is high, likely due to the heterogeneity of patients included, such as differing CHD diagnoses with varying clinical risk profiles, and imaging approaches including various dMRI sequences and processing pipelines. Consequently, dMRI alone currently cannot be used as a biomarker for aberrant brain developmental trajectories in individuals with CHD. More emphasis needs to be placed on characterizing vulnerable patients specifically with markers of CHD severity and other clinical risk factors. Moreover, although family‐environmental factors have been identified as strong determinants of neurodevelopment and behavior in this group (Naef et al., 2019; Werninger et al., 2020), their influence on long‐term brain development is understudied. Studies in typically developing individuals have found that environmental factors such as socioeconomic status impact brain development. It has been suggested that high socioeconomic background is related to extended cortical development and increased segregation of functional networks in adolescence, which indicates greater refinement and efficiency of neuronal processes (Tooley et al., 2021). The role of socioeconomic background and other family‐related factors in the brain development of patients with CHD is to be investigated. In addition, studies investigating associations between alteration of structural brain networks and neurodevelopmental impairments are needed to deepen our understanding of the neuronal processes contributing to the developmental problems observed in these patients.

Therefore, we aimed to identify alterations of network connectivity measures derived from dMRI in adolescents with CHD relative to healthy controls. Network connectivity measures of interest included (a) network integration, measured by global efficiency; (b) network segregation, measured by local efficiency; and (c) network edge strength. These measures were chosen, as they have been reported previously in neonates with CHD (Bhroin et al., 2020; Feldmann et al., 2020; Schmithorst et al., 2018) and allow a comparison of findings across studies and developmental stages in CHD. Further, we aimed to investigate whether more network connectivity alterations were associated with a higher cumulative load of clinical risk factors and with unfavorable family‐environmental factors (low parental education, low parental mental health, and poor family functioning). Finally, we aimed to determine the relationship between network connectivity alterations and poorer executive function performance.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample and study design

The data used for this analysis is derived from a prospective cohort study, the Teen Heart Study (Ehrler et al., 2019). This study investigated neurodevelopmental outcomes and cerebral MRI in adolescents with CHD and was conducted at the University Children's Hospital Zurich between 2019 and 2021.

The study included patients with CHD who underwent CPB surgery between 2004 and 2012 at the University Children's Hospital Zurich. Patients were eligible if they underwent CPB surgery within their first year of life, had not been diagnosed with a genetic disease or syndromic disorder and were between 10 and 15 years of age at the time of assessment. Of 178 eligible patients, 100 patients participated in the current study (participation rate: 56%). A control group of 104 healthy adolescents was cross‐sectionally recruited as friends of participating patients, and through print and online advertisement. Inclusion criteria for controls were birth after 36.0 weeks of gestation and no diagnosis of a neurological or developmental disorder such as learning disorder or attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. The recruitment procedure is illustrated in Supplementary Figure 1. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Canton of Zurich, Switzerland (KEK 2019–00035). Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation from the participants' legal guardians and from participants who were 14 years or older. De‐identified data and statistical scripts supporting our findings can be shared with other research groups on reasonable request. Patient data cannot be made openly available for patient privacy reasons.

Clinical risk factors and family‐environmental factors

Clinical risk factors were collected prospectively from patients' clinical records. A cumulative clinical risk (CCR) score was calculated from the sum of dichotomized risk factors (1 = risk, 0 = no risk). The risk factors were selected following a literature search as previously reported (Werninger et al., 2020), and the risk thresholds were defined by dichotomizing in a consensus discussion of clinical considerations between OK (cardiologist) BL (developmental pediatrician) and ME (psychologist). Fourteen dichotomized risk factors were selected: (1) single‐ventricle physiology, (2) cyanotic physiology, (3) preterm birth (<37 weeks of gestation), (4) low birthweight (<5th percentile adjusted for gestational age and sex), (5) low head circumference at birth (<5th percentile, adjusted for gestational age and sex), (6) preoperative O2 saturation < 70%, (7) >1 open‐heart surgery, (8) deep hypothermia during first open‐heart surgery (<28°C), (9) extracorporeal circulation time >120 min, (10) >2 weeks of intensive care unit stay following first surgery, (11) been on ECMO, (12) having had a stroke identified on routine and/or research cerebral MRI, (13) having had a clinically noticeable seizure, and (14) need for cardiac medication at time of assessment as a measure of impaired heart function. The CCR score could range from 0 to 14. This is a novel approach to investigate cumulative risk in a straightforward manner that is easily applicable in clinics and research by utilizing data from patients' medical charts.

Four family‐environmental factors were assessed at the time of assessment: parental education, maternal mental health, family functioning, and financial resources. Parental education was calculated by the sum of maternal and paternal education, each assessed on a 6‐point scale (1 = no high‐school degree, 2 = high‐school degree, 3 = apprenticeship, 4 = higher diploma for craftsmen or craftswomen, 5 = advanced diploma of higher education, 6 = university degree) (Largo et al., 1989). Maternal mental health was measured with the Brief Symptom Inventory, a standardized questionnaire that was completed by the mothers and assesses symptoms of depression, anxiety, and somatization. An age‐ and sex‐adjusted total score was calculated from representative German norms (Franke, 2017). Family functioning was assessed with the Family Relationship Index, a standardized questionnaire that was completed by the mothers and assesses the domains of cohesion (degree of commitment, help, and support); expressiveness (degree of expressed feelings and open interaction); and conflict (degree of anger and aggression expressed). A total score for overall quality of family functioning was calculated by the sum of the three subdomains after reversal of the conflict subdomain. Higher scores reflect better‐quality family functioning (Hoge et al., 1989). Financial resources were reported by the parents on a 6‐point Likert scale which assessed whether their financial resources were sufficient to cover their living costs (1 = “not at all” to 6 = “fully agree”).

Cognitive performance

Executive functions were investigated with an extensive standardized neuropsychological test battery assessing the five domains working memory, inhibition, cognitive flexibility, verbal and design fluency, and planning (Ehrler et al., 2019). Details about the neuropsychological test measures of executive functions are displayed in Table 1. A standardized summary score for executive function performance was built based on these tests. Data of the healthy controls were used for standardization since the available test norms had substantial qualitative differences in respect to age‐adjustment, regional origin and language of the normative sample, and timeliness. In brief: First, each test measure was standardized using least square regression, adjusting for age and using the natural logarithm test measurement to get approximately normally distributed residuals. Then, the standardized test measures were averaged for each of the five executive function domains. The standard deviations were harmonized by a standardization constant tau. At last, an executive function summary score was obtained by averaging the standard scores of the five executive function domains. Please refer to the Supplementary Text 1, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 for further information. Previous studies in patients with CHD and other populations have used mean scores of different executive function test (e.g., Bellinger et al., 2015; Nosarti et al., 2008; Schnider et al., 2020). However, we used advanced statistical estimation to account for data distribution and different variances of the test measures. The use of a summary score is supported by a previous meta‐analysis reporting that all executive function domains are equally affected in CHD (Chandrasekharan et al., 2021).

MRI acquisition

Cerebral MRI was performed on a 3‐Tesla whole‐body system (GE 3T MR750 MRI scanner, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI). A diffusion imaging sequence was acquired and oriented parallel to the anterior commissure—posterior commissure plane. The following parameters were used: repetition time/echo time (TR/TE) = 7500/89 ms; acquisition matrix = 96 × 96; field of view = 280 mm, slice thickness = 3.6 mm. A total of 35 diffusion‐weighted gradient directions were acquired with b = 1000 s/mm2 and four non‐diffusion weighted images. Seventeen patients had an MR‐conditional implant and thus the TR was prolonged (range 7500–11,000 ms) to ensure that the specific absorption rate remained within the specified limits. All other sequence parameters apart from the TR remained the same.

A three‐dimensional spoiled gradient echo (SPGR) pulse sequence was performed to obtain high‐resolution T1‐weighted images. The following parameters were used: TR/TE = 11/5 ms; inversion time = 600 ms; flip angle = 8°; reconstructed matrix = 256 × 256; field of view = 260 mm, slice thickness = 1 mm. dMRI and SPGR images were visually assessed for artifacts and incidental findings.

Diffusion weighted imaging processing

A custom pipeline relying on previously published algorithms and standard recommendations for connectomic analysis in MRtrix was used to process the diffusion imaging data with Python version 3.8. In addition to the standard recommendations, first, the denoising of diffusion imaging data was conducted with the deep learning algorithm patch2self from the DIPY package in Python (Fadnavis et al., 2020). In a next step, Gibbs ringing correction from MRtrix3 was conducted to remove high‐frequency oscillations (i.e., ringing artifacts) (Kellner et al., 2016; Tournier et al., 2019). Subsequently, to reduce artifacts from motion and spurious image shifts, slice‐to‐volume and eddy current correction was conducted with the CUDA 8.0 implementation in the FSL software version 6.00 (Smith et al., 2004). This function estimates the motion between adjoining slices and replaces outliers. Then, slices are warped into a common space and the three‐dimensional volume is reconstructed. Four standard deviations were defined as a threshold for outliers. For five subjects, a threshold of 8 was used because of selective artifacts outside of the white matter. One subject was excluded due to intractable algorithmic failure when processing the dMR images. At last, B1 bias field correction was conducted with MRtrix3. A separate B1 field map was generated and was used to estimate the intensity of the radiofrequency transmit field across the diffusion image which is then scaled to correct for the nonuniformity (Tournier et al., 2019). The processed images were visually checked by ME and AS for remaining artifacts and participants with extensive artifacts affecting the white matter were excluded.

Structural network construction

MRtrix3Tissue was used to estimate the whole‐brain probabilistic tractograms and the connectome matrices. First, the response functions were estimated for white matter, gray matter and cerebrospinal fluid and were averaged per tissue type and across participants (Dhollander et al., 2019). Further, single‐shell three‐tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (SS3T‐CSD) was conducted to estimate the fiber orientation distribution of each voxel. This method considers crossing fibers, as more than one fiber direction can be estimated per voxel (Dhollander et al., 2019; Dhollander & Connelly, 2016). Probabilistic anatomically constrained tractography was performed to model the white matter fiber orientation distribution with the iFOD2 algorithm. A total of 10 million streamlines were estimated (SIFT2) and the function “backtracking” was activated to allow tracks to be truncated and re‐tracked in case of poor structural termination. Dynamic seeding was used and a stopping criteria of streamline estimation was implemented based on the five‐tissue‐type (5tt) image. The 5tt images were generated by segmenting the 3D T1 images with FreeSurfer 5.3. The 5tt label image defines the white matter, subcortical and cortical gray matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and pathological tissue and ensures that streamlines are only estimated within the white matter. Biologically accurate weights were generated (μ = fiber density SIFT2 proportionality coefficient) for each subject and were used to normalize for inter‐subject connection density. Thus, structural connectivity was quantified as the sum of weighted streamlines (SIFT2 × μ) connecting each pair of regions.

The AAL3.v1 atlas was used to define the nodes. The AAL3.v1 atlas was registered to each participant's T1‐weighted image with SyN using an affine and deformable transformation from the software advanced normalization tools ANTs (Avants et al., 2009). Then, these data were registered to the participant's diffusion image (median of the non‐B0 images) with TRSAA (linear transformation) and with SyNOnly (nonlinear transformation). GenericLabel was used to interpolate the atlas labels into the diffusion space. Four anatomical regions with small volumes were excluded from the analysis of all subjects due to insufficient registration across several subjects leading to partial voluming and overlap with the ventricles in several cases (Thalamus reuniens left and right, Locus coeruleus left and right). Registration quality was checked independently by two raters (ME and FM). (A) Cortical outlines, (B) cerebellar outlines, and (C) thalamus and caudate nucleus were classified as 2 = good registration, 1 = medium registration, and 3 = bad registration.

In summary, weighted, undirected structural connectivity networks were formed based on whole‐brain probabilistic tractography resulting in 162 × 162 matrices where edges correspond to the sum of weighted streamlines (SIFT2 × μ) and nodes correspond to the AAL3v1 labels.

Network connectivity measures

Graph theory measures

Several graph theory measures were estimated with the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (BCT; Rubinov & Sporns, 2010) in MATLAB version R2022b to quantify the topological organization of the brain networks. Threshold‐free network density was measured as the observed edges relative to all possible edges (Kaiser, 2011). The “density_und” function in BCT was used. Network integration was quantified by global efficiency, which was defined as the inverse of the average shortest path length between nodes (Achard & Bullmore, 2007). Network segregation was quantified by mean local efficiency, which was defined as the inverse of the average shortest path length connecting all neighbors of an individual node averaged across all nodes. Cost thresholding was applied to account for variance in network density between subjects. To do so, the lowest possible network cost across the study cohort was selected and used as an upper value of the proportional threshold procedure defined as Tmax (van den Heuvel et al., 2017). Then, an iterative approach was used with a set of thresholds from 0.05 to τ max = 0.46 increasing in steps of 0.01, because the choice of a single cost threshold may be arbitrary. For cost thresholding, the “threshold_proportional” function in BCT was used. Global efficiency and local efficiency were calculated at each cost‐thresholding level with the “efficiency_wei” function in BCT (Rubinov & Sporns, 2010). Nodes were portioned into well‐connected core regions with strongly interconnected hubs or weakly connected periphery regions (Borgatti & Everett, 1999). A common core–periphery pattern was defined across the study sample, using the Tmax thresholded networks, by classifying those nodes that were identified as either core or periphery in ≥90% of all subjects. Other nodes were classified as neither. The coreness statistic was estimated by the degree of separation between core and periphery nodes as a measure of goodness of fit. The “core_periphery_dir” function in BCT was used.

Network‐based statistics

The MATLAB toolbox network‐based statistics (NBS) was used to test the associations between edgewise structural connectivity and factors of interest such as group differences, association with CCR score, and executive function summary score (see Section 2.8 for details). Networks were thresholded at a cost level of τ max = 0.46. A general linear model was used with 5000 permutations to determine significance. First, mass univariate testing was conducted for each edge with a T threshold of 3.0. Post hoc, analyses were repeated with a T threshold of 3.5 to identify the strongest components of the network. Second, all edges exceeding the T threshold were admitted to a set of suprathreshold connections. Then, topological clusters among the set of supra‐threshold connections were identified. Connections for which the null hypothesis had been rejected were arranged in an interconnected configuration rather than as single or isolated connections. Third, family‐wise error rate correction was conducted for each component using permutation testing. The component size corresponds to the total number of connections it comprises (extent). Finally, p values <.05 were considered significant.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2022) if not stated elsewise.

Study sample and characteristics: Sample characteristics were compared between patients and controls with t tests for normally distributed continuous data, Mann Whitney U tests for non‐normally distributed continuous or ordinal data, and Chi‐squared tests for categorical data. Registration quality was compared between patients and controls with a Mann Whitney U test. Missing values of clinical risk factors were imputed by chained equations to maintain statistical power for calculating the CCR score. The “mice” R package was used with one imputed data set and five iterations (Van Buuren & Groothuis‐Oudshoorn, 2011).

Differences between patients and controls: Network connectivity measures were first compared between patients and controls. To test for group effects on threshold‐free network density, a linear regression model was estimated with network density as dependent variable, group as independent variable, and age and sex as covariates. Global and local efficiency were investigated with a mass univariate approach. First, global efficiency was log‐transformed to achieve normal distributions of residuals for all subsequent analyses. Linear regression models were estimated separately for each threshold level from 0.05 to 0.46 in 0.01 intervals with threshold‐specific global efficiency as dependent variable, group as independent variable, and age, sex, and parental education as covariates. Group effects were FDR‐corrected. The same procedure was repeated for local efficiency. Subnetwork edge strength was identified by NBS. Edgewise general linear models were estimated with edge strength as dependent variable, group as independent variable, and age, sex, and parental education as covariates in NBS (see further details in Section 2.7.2). The mean network strength of an identified subnetwork was calculated for each individual and compared between groups with a two‐sampled, two‐sided t test. Identified networks were visualized with BrainNet Viewer in MATLAB and with circular graphs in R with the “ggplot2,” “igraph,” and “ggraph” libraries.

Associations between network measures, risk factors, and executive functions in patients: Linear regression models were estimated with three network connectivity measures, which were considered as dependent variables in separate models: (a) global efficiency, averaged across all cost thresholds; (b) local efficiency, averaged across all cost thresholds; and (c) mean subnetwork edge strength, identified by NBS‐derived group comparison. Independent variables were (1) CCR score; (2) individual clinical risk factors, estimated in separate models due to potential collinearity (FDR‐corrected p values are reported); (3) family‐environmental factors; and (4) executive function summary score. All models were corrected for age and sex. Post hoc, subnetwork edge strength specifically linked to the CCR score was identified by NBS within the patient group. To do this, edgewise general linear models were estimated with edge strength as dependent variable, the CCR score as independent variable, and age and sex as covariates with NBS (see further details in Section 2.7.2). The same procedure was repeated for the executive function summary score as independent variable. Post hoc, it was tested if the association between the CCR scores and the executive function summary scores was mediated by network connectivity measures using the “lavaan” R package. All linear regression models were checked for normal distribution of residuals and collinearity of the factors included.

RESULTS

Study sample and characteristics

Of 204 subjects who participated in the study (100 patients, 104 controls), 156 underwent cerebral MRI (60 patients, 95 controls). Primary reasons for not undergoing cerebral MRI were implants not approved for research MRI such as pacemaker, stents, and clips or anxiety of the participant. In two individuals, the dMRI sequence was not performed. Participants were excluded due to artifacts from dental braces (n = 20), movement artifacts in the dMRI sequence (n = 2) and in the SPGR sequence (N = 1), artifacts after eddy‐current correction (n = 1), insufficient data registration (n = 1), and a large cyst (n = 1). Eventually, 128 subjects were included in the current analysis. The included subjects did not differ from excluded subjects in respect to age (t (df) = 1.95 (202), p = .052), sex (Χ 2 (df) = 0.02 (1), p = .889), parental education (W = 3837, p = .104), or proportion of patients with a cyanotic CHD (Χ 2 (df) = 2.07 (1), p = .150), or univentricular CHD (Χ 2 (df) = 1.08 (1), p = .298).

A total of 53 patients (10% univentricular CHD, 66% cyanotic CHD) and 75 healthy controls were analyzed. Subjects were between 10.1 and 15.9 years of age. Detailed participant characteristics stratified by group are reported in Table 2. A full list of CHD diagnoses is presented in Supplementary Table 3. Patients were significantly older (Cohen's d = 0.52, moderate effect) and had significantly lower parental education (Wilcoxon's r = .36, small to moderate effect) than healthy controls. There was no significant difference between patients and controls in sex, gestational age, birth weight, or head circumference at birth. Patients had significantly lower total IQ and lower executive function summary scores relative to healthy controls (IQ [100 ± 15]: patients' mean = 100.7 ± 12.2, controls' mean = 111.1 ± 9.6, p < .001; executive function summary score [0 ± 1]: patients' mean = −1.0 ± 1.1; controls' mean = −0.1 ± 1.0, p < .001). Of all CHD patients, 13% had an IQ score below the normal range (<1 SD, i.e., IQ < 85) in comparison to 0% of controls (X 2 = 8.08, p = .004). Then, 42% of patients had an executive function summary score below the normal range (<1 SD, i.e., z < −1) in comparison to 19% of controls (X 2 = 6.93, p = .008).

Associations between network connectivity measures and executive functions

The executive function summary score was significantly associated with global efficiency (β (CI‐95) = 0.34 (0.07–0.61), p = .015) and local efficiency (β (CI‐95) = 0.34 (0.08–0.61), p = .013) in patients. The association between executive function summary score and subnetwork edge strength was not significant (β (CI‐95) = 0.18 (−0.08 to 0.44), p = .170). We tested whether a specific local subnetwork was associated with executive function performance using NBS. However, no specific network was identified by NBS (p = .380).

Finally, we tested whether the association between CCR scores and executive function summary scores was mediated by local efficiency. However, no significant mediation effect was evident (β = −.048, standard error = 0.029, z‐value = −1.627, p = .104). Only local efficiency was tested as a mediator since this was the only network connectivity measure that significantly correlated with both, CCR and executive functions and thus, qualified as a potential mediator.

DISCUSSION

This study of adolescents with CHD showed that cumulative exposure to clinical risk factors is associated with brain‐wide alterations in structural connectivity. We found an association between more CCR with lower network segregation and lower edge strength of a subnetwork including frontal interconnections and fronto‐parietal‐thalamic connections. Further, we showed that poorer executive functioning was associated with lower network integration and lower segregation.

When comparing adolescents with CHD to healthy controls, network integration and segregation was preserved, but adolescents with CHD showed lower edge strength in a dense, widespread network connecting structures of all major brain regions. Interconnections of the frontal lobe, interhemispheric connections, cortico‐subcortical, and cerebrocerebellar connections exhibit particularly low edge strength.

Risk factors for altered network connectivity

Cumulative clinical risk

This is the first study to investigate how structural brain networks are affected by exposure to a cumulative burden of clinical risk factors. We found that a higher CCR was associated with lower network segregation and lower edge strength. A post hoc analysis showed that CCR was strongly associated with lower edge strength in a subnetwork involving frontal interconnections and fronto‐parietal‐thalamic connections.

Although neurogenesis is largely complete by 20 weeks of gestation, structural networks continue to develop by axonal and dendritic growth, proliferation, followed by cell loss, synaptic pruning and axon myelination throughout childhood and adolescence (Collin & Van Den Heuvel, 2013; Morgan et al., 2018). During this phase, patients with complex CHD undergo cardiac surgery and stay on the ICU; some have continued altered heart function and face severe cardiac and neurological events such as cardiac arrest, seizure, and stroke. These repeated disturbances may further disrupt brain development and thus extend alterations in network connectivity over time.

We found that CCR was particularly related to reduced edge‐strength in a subnetwork including fronto‐parietal‐thalamic connections. Thalamocortical connections are established prenatally and develop strongly during the second and third trimesters (Dubois et al., 2015; Kostović & Judaš, 2010). Because this phase is marked by increased metabolic demands, altered cardiovascular physiology and restricted substrate delivery may prevent normal brain development of thalamocortical connections, particularly in those patients with more severe CHD (Lee et al., 2021). Across childhood and adolescence, structural networks are still maturing, especially the frontal cortex (Sousa et al., 2018). The accumulation of clinical risk factors over time may further impact the development of brain networks, and alterations may become more evident. We have shown that the accumulation of clinical risk is linked to more network alterations indicating that structural brain networks remain vulnerable to recurring clinical risk beyond the fetal and neonatal periods.

We showed that creating a cumulative risk score from predefined and previously identified risk factors is a valid and straightforward approach to predict both alterations in structural brain networks and executive function impairments. Consequently, such a risk score may serve as a biomarker, and we suggest that this approach can be applied in other studies to predict neurodevelopmental outcome and brain development. Longitudinal studies are particularly needed to investigate the effect of CCR on neurodevelopmental trajectories and to confirm causality. Eventually, a cumulative risk score may be used in clinical practice to predict outcome, evaluate the need for early interventions, and inform caregivers. However, although the effect sizes are moderate to strong, a considerable amount of unexplained variance and thus uncertainty in prediction remains.

Separate clinical risk factors

To further establish which risk factors may play leading roles in the development of structural brain networks, we conducted univariate linear regression models to investigate each risk factor separately. This analysis of single risk factors showed that prolonged stay on the ICU was the strongest predictor of lower network segregation and lower edge strength in adolescence. On one hand, a prolonged ICU stay may reflect a more severe CHD and associated cardiac complications, which our CCR score may also partially capture. On the other hand, a prolonged ICU stay may indicate the impact of a stressful environment on the developing brain. The ICU is known to be a stressful environment, characterized by factors such as noise, altered day‐night rhythms, potentially hurtful interventions, repeated examinations, and parental separation (D'Agata et al., 2017). Cardiac‐intensive care strategies include modifiable variables and thus constitute a target for improving brain health in these children. Strategies to promote developmental care in the ICU may prevent or at least reduce the extent of brain alterations. These strategies may include promoting parent–child bonding with kangaroo care and music therapy, reducing noise, and implementing cycled lighting to maintain the circadian rhythm (Ortinau et al., 2022).

In addition, lower edge strength was associated with univentricular CHD, more than one CPB surgery, and cardiac insufficiency determined by the need for cardiac medication at the time of assessment. Univentricular physiology may impact brain development already during the fetal development due to severe alterations of cerebral blood perfusion, hypoxia, and other factors (Szwast et al., 2012; Volpe, 2022). While these factors may be more pronounced in univentricular CHD, they have also been demonstrated for biventricular types of CHD. To date, comprehensive, well‐powered studies are lacking that compare cerebral MRI markers of fetuses with uni‐ and biventricular CHD. Beyond the fetal and neonatal period, patients with a univentricular CHD require more than one surgery with a repeated risk for subsequent acquired brain injury. Further, the univentricular CHD is palliated rather than corrected CHD leading to a continuously altered blood perfusion and oxygen saturation which may impact brain development beyond the fetal and neonatal period (Selvanathan et al., 2022). Interestingly, we showed that network edge strength was particularly associated with univentricular CHD, whereas network integration and segregation were not associated with univentricular CHD. We propose that different risk factors and potentially also their timing and duration may impact the structural network connectivity at various levels.

Family‐environmental factors

We further investigated if family‐environmental factors are related to structural network alterations in adolescents with CHD. In patients, we found that parental education, maternal mental health and family functioning were not significantly associated with any structural network measure. However, we found that the families' financial resources were significantly associated with more network segregation, but there was no significant association with network integration or network edge strength. Furthermore, parental education was included as a covariate when comparing structural network measures between patients and controls. Interestingly, in the overall sample of patients and controls, parental education was significantly associated with network integration and segregation. Nevertheless, when examining patients only, this effect diminished and became non‐significant. This could be due to reduced variability and power in the smaller sample of patients.

Previously, it has been suggested that higher socioeconomic status is related to extended cortical development and increased segregation of functional networks in adolescence, which indicates greater refinement and efficiency of neuronal processes (Tooley et al., 2021). Furthermore, socioeconomic status, parental well‐being and a positive family environment have been linked to better neurodevelopmental outcome in patients with CHD (Bonthrone et al., 2021; Chew et al., 2024; McCusker et al., 2007; Naef et al., 2019; Werninger et al., 2020). However, the potentially mediating role of brain alterations in CHD remains poorly explored. First evidence suggests that prenatal maternal distress is associated with reduced brain volume in fetuses with CHD but the long‐term consequences for neurodevelopmental outcome have not been explored in this study (Wu et al., 2020). Another study in young adults with CHD has found that parental socioeconomic status moderates the association between brain volume and executive function performance (Naef et al., 2021). This is first evidence, that family‐environmental factors may influence brain development in CHD with a potential consequence for long‐term neurodevelopmental outcome. The current study could not provide clear evidence for the impact of family‐environmental factors on brain network alterations. Future research in this direction is clearly needed.

Altered global and local network connectivity in patients with CHD

Results from graph theory analysis

We used graph theory to demonstrate that adolescents with CHD had lower total network density compared to healthy controls. After controlling for parental education, we did not find a significant group difference in network segregation or integration. Similar to our findings, a previous study in adolescents with TGA did not detect significant group differences in network integration as measured by global efficiency. In contrast to our findings, this study showed significantly increased network segregation as measured by modularity (Panigrahy et al., 2015). There are some methodological differences between the two studies that hinder a direct comparison of results. That study used FA‐weighted connectome data, whereas we used sum of weighted streamlines generated by CSD. CSD enables the modeling of crossing fibers within voxels and thus overcomes a major limitation of FA analyses (Dhollander et al., 2019). Of note, we used a single‐shell model with a b‐value of 1000 and CSD may be more effective with additional higher b‐values (Jeurissen et al., 2014). Another reason for different findings between the two studies may be that the connectome data of the study in TGA patients was not cost corrected, whereas our data were thresholded at various cost levels to eliminate false‐positive connections and avoid biasing the comparison of network connectivity with network density. At last, while one study included TGA patients only and could report on a specific type of CHD, the other study included several types of cyanotic and acyanotic CHD (with TGA being the largest subgroup). The differing results of the two studies may thus be attributed to methodological differences (Morgan et al., 2018). Future studies are needed to establish the extent and direction of global topological differences between adolescents with CHD and healthy controls. We also suggest that other graph theory metrics, like clustering coefficients, betweenness centrality or small worldness, should be investigated in future well‐powered study samples to provide additional information on topological network alterations in CHD. We focused on global and local efficiency to compare our results to previous dMRI studies in neonates with CHD which reported these metrics. Indeed, dMRI studies in neonates with CHD have demonstrated altered global network connectivity during the pre‐ and postoperative periods. However, specific findings are heterogeneous and contradictory in the significance and direction of effects for network density, segregation (local efficiency), and integration (global efficiency) (Bhroin et al., 2020; Feldmann et al., 2020; Schmithorst et al., 2018). The heterogeneous findings across dMRI studies both in neonates and adolescents with CHD may be better understood by replicating processing pipelines and using meta‐analytic approaches to summarize effect sizes and potential biases.

Evidence from studies in children and adolescents born preterm — who exhibit somewhat comparable neurodevelopmental profiles and white matter microstructural alterations to those with CHD (Easson et al., 2019; Easson et al., 2023; Wehrle et al., 2022) — suggests structural network alterations compared to their term‐born peers, including reduced global efficiency (Fischi‐Gomez et al., 2016; Irzan et al., 2021). A study in preterm and term‐born neonates has shown that the degree of prematurity contributed to altered global topology of the structural brain network (Batalle et al., 2017). These findings suggest that structural network alterations seem to be evident in preterm neonates and remain evident in later childhood and adolescence. The differences between preterm birth and CHD in regards to maturation at birth, clinical presentations, and perinatal courses could lead to variations in the topological development of white matter networks, although the overall integrity of the white matter itself might be somewhat similarly affected.

Results from network‐based statistics (NBS)

We used NBS to investigate local network connectivity and provide first evidence that adolescents with CHD have lower edge strength in a dense, widespread subnetwork including interconnections of the frontal lobe, interhemispheric connections, cortico‐subcortical, and cerebrocerebellar connections. Thus, a decrease in network strength of a brain region indicates not only a localized change but also a reduction in its connections to all other regions it interacts with. Further, we found that 43 of 108 nodes were classified as core nodes, which are by definition strongly interconnected hubs. Cortical interconnections, along with the connections to and from the thalamic nuclei—which were classified as peripheral nodes when investigated separately but as a core region when the thalamus was examined as a whole—were found to particularly exhibit lower edge strength in patients compared to controls.

A body of research has demonstrated that structural brain alterations in CHD are present as early as the prenatal and neonatal periods (Lee et al., 2021). It has been suggested that reduced cerebral oxygen delivery, altered cerebral blood perfusion, and placenta abnormalities in fetuses with CHD may disrupt the development of the vascular‐rich subventricular zone and the fetal subplate. The subventricular zone is relevant to neurogenesis and oligodendrocyte myelination (Morton et al., 2017), and the fetal subplate is relevant to cortical maturation (Wu et al., 2021). Early disruption of these core regions relevant for brain maturation may alter further development of structural network strength throughout the fetal and neonatal period. Importantly two previous studies have conducted NBS in neonates with CHD. Bhroin et al. found reduced edge strength in a cortico‐striatal‐thalamic subnetwork connecting 23 of 93 nodes (24%) in neonates with CHD compared to healthy neonates (Bhroin et al., 2020). Feldmann et al., found reduced edge strength in several smaller subnetworks of neonates with CHD compared to healthy neonates (preoperatively 26 of 90 nodes [29%], postoperatively 13 of 90 nodes [14%]) (Feldmann et al., 2020). The subnetwork which we identified in adolescents with CHD included 268 edges connecting 108 of 162 nodes (67%) and was thus substantially larger and denser compared to those found in neonates. Additional vulnerability during childhood, as described in the section above, may increase the extent of network strength alterations. Another reason could be that once the networks become more strongly connected during childhood and adolescence, network alterations become more evident and statistical effects are stronger. We suggest that longitudinal studies should examine changes in network strength throughout various developmental stages to pinpoint specific time periods and brain regions that are particularly vulnerable.

Association with executive function performance

We found that lower executive functioning in adolescents with CHD was associated with lower network integration and segregation. Our findings are in line with a previous study by Baum and colleagues that investigated FA‐weighted connectomes of 882 healthy individuals between 8 and 22 years of age. Those researchers found that networks become more segregated with increasing age, which was associated with better executive functioning. They also found that networks become globally more integrated, indicating that the connectivity within modules is efficiently organized and that hub edges create strong links between otherwise segregated modules (Baum et al., 2017). While both studies show that better executive functions are linked to more network segregation and integration, the context differs. In typically developing adolescents, increasing age leads to better executive functions (through enhanced network segregation and integration. In contrast, adolescents with CHD who demonstrate lower executive functioning exhibit lower levels of these network properties. Thus, our study highlights how CHD may interfere with the normative process of network maturation described by Baum et al., resulting in lower executive functioning. Understanding these differences underscores the importance of targeted interventions to support neurodevelopment in adolescents with CHD.

However, our findings contrast with those of another study in adolescents with TGA, which found that higher network segregation, assessed by modularity, was associated with poorer executive functioning. As described previously, our results deviate broadly from the findings of that study, likely due to methodological differences. Additional studies are needed that replicate previously conducted processing pipelines and statistical analyses to consolidate the evidence from current research.

Although studies investigating associations between structural network connectivity and executive functioning remain sparse, other studies have shown that poorer executive functioning is associated with locally altered microstructural integrity in adolescents and young adults with CHD (Ehrler et al., 2021; Ehrler, Latal, Kretschmar, et al., 2020; Rollins et al., 2014). The use of various methodological approaches across studies prevents direct comparison of results, but all these studies have repeatedly shown that patients with CHD demonstrate altered structural networks and microstructural integrity and that these alterations are linked to poorer executive functioning. Adolescence is a sensitive developmental period characterized by the reorganization of structural brain networks and maturation of white matter tracts. Brain plasticity, which plays a crucial role in this phase of development, might be promoted by specific therapeutic and interventional approaches. Initial studies have shown that computerized working‐memory programs in patients with CHD can improve executive functioning, but the improvement is limited (Calderon et al., 2020; Jordan et al., 2020). One study is currently being conducted combining computerized cognitive training with weekly coaching sessions with a psychologist (Schmid et al., 2023). We are optimistic that research using advanced imaging methodologies, longitudinal study designs, and tests of executive function interventions will advance our understanding of the neurological underpinnings of executive function impairments in patients with CHD.

Brain alterations in CHD: A lifespan approach is needed

As elaborated above, we and others have shown that alterations in structural network connectivity in patients with CHD seem to be evident from the neonatal period into adolescence. Moreover, we have shown in a pooled case–control study using diffusion tensor imaging that white matter microstructural alterations in patients with CHD persist throughout childhood into young adulthood (Ehrler et al., 2022). Notably, the brain alterations described in patients with CHD are linked to executive function impairments that we frequently observe in this population. These brain alterations may reduce the “brain reserve” of patients with CHD. People who have sufficient brain reserve tolerate age‐related changes better without showing clinically relevant symptoms of cognitive decline (Meng & Arcy, 2012). This is of particular importance when adopting a lifespan perspective. In healthy adults, natural age‐related changes occur in the white matter through volume loss, microstructural changes in specific white matter tracts, and changes in structural network connectivity, most prominently in the frontal and temporal lobes. These changes are thought to cause the structural substrate underlying cognitive decline in later adulthood (Lebel et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2015). In patients with CHD, white matter alterations may reduce their brain reserve, and thus amplifying the consequences of additional age‐related changes in white matter. As brain reserve is known to protect against later dementia (Meng & Arcy, 2012), limited brain reserve in CHD may accelerate age‐related cognitive decline. Indeed, studies have shown that adults with CHD are twice as likely to develop early dementia (Bagge et al., 2018; Downing et al., 2022). In addition, microvascular changes in adults with CHD may lead to accelerated vascular aging, which in turn can increase the risk of brain alterations. Thus, a lifespan approach incorporating neuropsychologists and neurologists is required when treating patients with CHD.

Limitations

Some limitations need to be considered when interpreting our results. We conducted a single‐shell dMRI sequence with a b‐value of 1000 and 35 diffusion‐weighted gradient directions. Thus, the angular resolution of our dMRI data is limited. We applied SS3T‐CSD to estimate the fiber orientation distribution of each voxel (Dhollander et al., 2019; Dhollander & Connelly, 2016). This approach enables the modeling of crossing fibers within voxels, which can overcome a major limitation of diffusion tensor imaging and improves the reliability of the connectomic analysis. However, using a single b‐value of 1000 may limit the detection of crossing fibers, as CSD has been shown to perform better with additional higher b‐values (Jeurissen et al., 2014). In addition, the voxel dimensions of the dMRI protocol were non‐isotropic, as the minimum slice thickness was dependent on the spectral‐spatial pulse used for water excitation (Zur, 2000) and the slice thickness (3.6 mm) was larger than in some other dMRI studies.

We showed that several measures of altered network connectivity were associated with executive performance. However, the data were collected cross‐sectionally, such that no conclusion can be drawn about causality. Longitudinal studies are needed with repeated MRI examinations and neurodevelopmental follow‐ups to model developmental trajectories and infer causality. The same limitation applies to the family‐environmental factors (parental mental health, family functioning, and financial resources) that were assessed cross‐sectionally. Although the current state of parental mental health and family functioning may not influence adolescents' brain networks directly, disrupted family well‐being during early childhood may alter the trajectory of brain development. For instance, Wu et al. have shown that maternal depression during pregnancy is associated with lower brain growth in fetuses with CHD (Wu et al., 2020). This is particularly important because parents of children with CHD are at increased risk for mental health issues including posttraumatic stress disorder, depression, and anxiety (Woolf‐King et al., 2017). Further longitudinal studies in this direction are essential.

We included adolescents with several types of CHD, who underwent infant open‐heart surgery, in our study. Thus, we cannot draw any conclusion regarding specific types of CHD. However, the inclusion of various CHD types introduced some variability and allowed for the investigation of disease severity using our CCR score.

The control group of this study population was a high‐functioning group with a relatively high mean IQ of 111 and high socioeconomic background. This bias is frequently observed when recruiting volunteer healthy controls for observational studies and has been recognized by the scientific community (Chandrasekharan et al., 2021; Russell & Florida, 2005). The recruitment of the healthy controls was not matched to the patient group but group comparison analyses were controlled for parental education, sex and age to account for confounders. Network analysis is complex, and additional unknown factors may influence the results.

From the original sample size of 204, which was decided by a power calculation (Ehrler et al., 2019), 76 participants were excluded due to missing dMRI data or imaging artifacts. This reduced power may have led us to miss smaller effects that could have provided additional information on network alterations in CHD. Nevertheless, our sample size was similar to previous connectomic studies in neonates with CHD (Bhroin et al., 2020; Schmithorst et al., 2018) and thus enable between‐study comparison.

Conclusion

In this study, we used a novel dMRI approach, CSD, to model crossing fibers and estimate structural networks, in adolescents with CHD and healthy controls. We used graph theory and NBS to demonstrate evidence of altered network connectivity in adolescents with CHD, most prominently in those patients who had faced cumulative exposure to multiple clinical risk factors. This was the first study to investigate the CCR to patients with CHD associated with structural brain networks and executive function performance. Quantifying the CCR to patients with CHD may help better predict trajectories of brain development. This would allow us to identify and support the most vulnerable patients as early as possible. Further, better understanding of the role of clinical risk factors for brain development may inform clinical and surgical decision‐making.
