Identifying critical windows of air pollution exposure and semen quality: A cross-sectional study in Chinese fertile men
Authors: Jialin Feng, Yan Yuan, Yuting Cheng, Peihao Wu, Yixuan Yan, Qi Liu, Feng Wu, Jing Wei, Hongshan Ge, Qiuqin Tang, Wei Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough human studies on the hazard that air pollution represents to male reproductive health, there are wide variations in exposure concentration, key components, semen quality indicators and the identification of susceptible exposure windows. The study aimed to further explore the relationship between air pollution and semen quality and the more accurate sensitive exposure windows.MethodsWe investigated a cross-sectional study consisting of 1554 fertile men from Nanjing, China and evaluated their exposure to PM10, PM2.5, CO, SO2, NO2 and O3 in the air. Multiple linear regression was utilized to estimate the correlation between weekly air pollution exposure and semen quality, as well as weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression and bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) for the effect of mixtures. We also established the distributed lag non-linear model to delineate exposure-lag-response relationship.ResultsOur analysis linked PM2.5 exposure at week 10 to reduced total motility (β = − 2.06, 95% CI: (− 3.71, − 0.41)) and progressive motility (β = − 1.79, 95% CI: (− 3.23, − 0.34)). WQS regression showed co-exposure inversely correlated with total motility (β = − 1.49, 95% CI: (− 2.63, − 0.35)) and progressive motility (β = − 1.27, 95% CI: (− 2.30, − 0.25)) at week 11, where PM2.5 contributed the most. A great lagged impact resulting from PM2.5 on sperm motility peaked 10 to 11 weeks after exposure. We also found that PM2.5 exposure at week 10 increased the risk of having below-referenced progressive motility (< 32%) by the method of BKMR. Based on these results, the early stage of spermatogenesis (from week 10 to week 11 before semen collection), was recognized as the susceptible exposure window. Furthermore, BKMR analysis suggested co-exposure to major components of the primary contributor, PM2.5, during this period were negatively correlated with sperm motility, including NO3−, NH4+, SO42−, black carbon and organic matter, and SO42− was the most weighted composition.ConclusionsIn both the single pollutant models and the multi-pollutants models, PM2.5 and its chemical components inversely correlated with sperm motility in a relatively early window (from week 10 to week 11 before semen collection) which was identified to be more sensitive to air pollution.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24275-x.Keywords: Air pollution, Semen quality, Sperm motility, Major chemical composition of PM2.5, Critical window

Background

Air pollution exposure remains a critical public health challenge, contributing to substantial disease burden and socioeconomic costs for China and the global community [1]. For instance, ambient air pollution can lead to an increased incidence of cardiovascular diseases [2], respiratory diseases [3, 4] and reproductive disorders [5–9]. Over 90% of urban residents remain exposed to air pollution levels that violate the standards of the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines [10]. In recent years, reproductive health has suffered a rapid decline [11]. The decrease in sperm quality leads to male infertility prevalently, responsible for 50% of infertile cases [12]. The decline has not been explained, however, several studies suggest that it may be associated with age, smoking, drinking, environmental toxicants and so on [13–18].

In recent years, growing attention has been paid to the effects of air pollution on reproductive health in both males and females. Limited research indicated that environmental air pollutants can make damage to male fertility, including interference of testosterone levels and changes of semen quality [19–21]. Meanwhile, epidemiological studies have also discovered the alterations in sperm motility and count, which are concerned with exposure to environmental air pollutants, but there are inconsistencies between the results [20, 22–24].

The majority of recent studies concentrated on a comparatively wide range of periods before semen collection, such as 90 days or 1 year [25–27], or detected according to the spermatogenic cycle [20, 28, 29]. Consequently, the aforementioned studies could not reveal the comprehensive association between air pollutants exposed during the spermatogenic cycle and semen quality, nor could they capture the susceptible exposure windows more accurately. Besides, most existing studies focused on the effects or mechanisms of single exposure to one kind of air pollutant on semen quality [30, 31]. However, the joint influence of exposure cannot be ignored.

To investigate the impacts of single or combined exposure to environmental air pollution on semen quality in fertile men, we analyzed semen samples alongside air pollution monitoring data and speculated on susceptible exposure windows in greater detail. However, as an exploratory study, this research did not pre-specify hypotheses regarding critical exposure substances or time windows. Instead, we employed systematic analysis to evaluate multiple potential associations.

Materials and methods

Study participants

All participants were recruited from the NMU-LIFE cohort, with detailed selection criteria previously published [26]. Simply put, January 1, 2014 through December 31, 2016, 1607 fertile men were included as their partners performed prenatal registration at Nanjing Medical University Affiliated Hospital. 53 men were excluded: 17 with unclear examination dates, 21 with incomplete semen reports, and 15 with missing address. Finally, 1554 men were selected as the study subjects.

The demographic information of subjects, including age, body mass index (BMI), ethnicity, family income, educational level, cigarette and alcohol consumption and residence address, was routinely collected through interviews face-to-face by trained medical researchers.

Exposure assessment

As is well known, the spermatogenesis is approximately 90 days. We further divided the period into 13 weeks to assess the individual weekly average exposure of air pollution on this basis (weeks 1–2: sperm motility development and stored in the epididymis, weeks 3–10: spermatogenesis stage II, weeks 11–13: spermatogenesis stage I) [32]. The daily air pollution data (PM10, PM2.5, CO, SO2, NO2, and O3) was obtained from 24 monitoring stations across Nanjing and six neighboring cities (Chuzhou, Jurong, Ma’anshan, Wuhu, Yangzhou and Zhenjiang) during 2014–2016. The dataset of PM2.5 composition (OM, NO3−, NH4+, SO42−, and BC) was acquired from the Tracking Air Pollution in China (http://tapdata.org.cn). The daily temperature of Nanjing was sourced from the China Meteorological Data Service Centre (http://data.cma.cn/).

Based on the daily air pollution data of nine monitoring stations in Nanjing from 2014 to 2016, the inverse distance weighting (IDW) interpolation was used in ArcGIS software for spatial interpolation. The participants’ addresses were geocoded as coordinates, and IDW interpolation estimated the exposure levels of six air pollutants for each residence address, as well as the average temperature, through the weighted average of adjacent sites [26].

Semen analysis

Participants collected semen into sterile plastic specimen containers via masturbation, which would be liquefied at 37℃ after collection. Following the WHO guidelines [33], we performed computer-assisted semen analysis (CASA) within 30 min of ejaculation. The assessment included not only five conventional parameters: total sperm count (106), sperm concentration (106/ml), total motility (%), progressive motility (%) and semen volume (ml), but also nine sperm motion parameters: amplitude of lateral head displacement (ALH, µm/s), beat cross frequency (BCF, Hz), wobble (WOB, %), average path velocity (VAP, µm/s), curvilinear velocity (VCL, µm/s), straight-line velocity (VSL, µm/s), mean angular displacement (MAD, ◦), straightness (STR, %), and linearity (LIN, %). It should be noted that we converted sperm motility into binary variables based on WHO reference values, namely total motility < 40% and progressive motility < 32%.

Covariates

All models were adjusted for age (continuous), ethnicity (Han, others), BMI (< 18.5, 18.5–24.0, 24.0–28.0, ≥ 28.0 kg/m2), educational level (middle school and lower, high school and secondary school, college degree and higher), family income (< 100,000, 100,000-200,000, ≥ 200,000 yuan per year), cigarette and alcohol consumption (never, ever), days of abstinence (< 3, 3–5, ≥ 5 days), season of semen collection (winter, autumn, summer, spring) and temperature.

Statistical analysis

Firstly, we employed the descriptive statistics to express individual exposure of the participants. Means ± standard deviation was summarized for continuous variables. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was assessed among air pollutants. Semen parameters were transformed using the Box-Cox method to approximate a normal distribution.

Multiple linear regression was utilized to estimate the correlation between weekly individual air pollutant exposure and semen quality across sperm development to identify specific exposure windows. Considering the potential lagged effect of air pollutants, we established the distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) to delineate exposure-lag-response relationship with semen quality.

We employed weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression to examine associations between weekly mixed air pollutant exposure and semen quality. In the condition of completing identifying the susceptible exposure window and air pollutant with the highest weight, the WQS regression was used to evaluate the combined action of the main compositions of the key pollutant on semen quality during the susceptible exposure window. We also applied bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) model to account for potential nonlinear relationships, analyzing semen parameters as both binary and continuous variables.

All data was managed and analyzed using R software (Version 4.1.0). “gWQS”, “dlnm” and “bkmr” packages were used respectively for the analysis of WQS regression, DLNM model and BKMR model. In order to control the false discovery rate (FDR) of multiple comparisons, the Benjamin-Hochberg correction was applied to correct the P-value to reduce the false positive rate. The corrected two-tailed P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic information

We finally recruited 1554 fertile men from the NMU-LIFE study as previously described [26]. Specifically, participants were at an average age of 30.92 (SD: 4.16) years, and most (96.98%) were Han. About half (57.02%) of the men were overweight or obese (BMI: 24–28 or > 28 kg/m2). Among these people, 66.80% had college degree and above, and 1.22% received an education less than middle school. 18.66% of the participants had a family income of more than 20,000 yuan per year. Over half of the observations were never consumed cigarette (62.29%) or never consumed alcohol (55.47%).

Distribution of individual exposure and semen quality

Our prior analysis demonstrated distinct seasonal patterns in air pollutant concentrations (2014–2016), with conventional pollutants peaking in winter and troughing in summer, while O3 exhibited an opposing trend [26]. The mean concentrations during 0–90 days preceding semen collection were 61.49, 102.87, 1038.91, 21.18, 52.07 and 92.84 µg/m3 respectively for PM2.5, PM10, CO, SO2, NO2, and O3 [26]. Effects of single exposure and semen quality

Figure 1 showed the results related to the associations between individual air pollutant exposure and semen quality, controlling for covariates. Except for PM10, the other air pollutants had significant correlations with semen quality. Negative correlations were observed between PM2.5 and sperm motility. Furthermore, we identified negative correlations between exposure to both SO2 and NO2 with sperm concentration at week 10 prior to semen collection. In contrast, O3 was observed to be positively correlated with five semen parameters. After applying FDR correction, PM2.5 exhibited inverse correlation with total motility (β = −2.06, 95% CI: (−3.71, −0.41)) and progressive motility (β = −1.79, 95% CI: (−3.23, −0.34)) at week 10 before semen collection (Table S1).

Effects of co-exposure and sperm motility

The WQS regression was employed to assess the impact of mixed exposure on semen quality. We found that sperm motility was negatively correlated with co-exposure to six air pollutants at week 11 before semen collection (Fig. 2) after FDR correction (Table S2, total motility: β = −1.49, 95% CI: (−2.63, −0.35); progressive motility: β = −1.27, 95% CI: (−2.30, −0.25)), and PM2.5 was considered as the most weighted pollutant in this correlation (Fig. 3).

Major chemical components of PM2.5 and sperm motility

All five components of PM2.5 showed inverse correlation with total motility, however, after FDR correction, NH4+ (β = −2.15, 95%CI: (−3.43, −0.87)), NO3− (β = −2.24, 95%CI: (−3.69 −0.80)), OM (β = −1.64, 95%CI: (−2.95, −0.33)), and SO42− (β = −2.08, 95%CI: (−3.15, −1.01)) were significantly inversely correlated with progressive motility at week 10 (Table 2) with no significance at week 11 (Table S3). We also found that the components of PM2.5 were significantly negatively correlated with VCL, VSL, and VAP, affecting the speed of sperm activity. Moreover, the WQS regression revealed inverse associations between co-exposure to major chemical components of PM2.5 and sperm motility from week 10 to week 11 before semen collection, presented in Table 3. Notably, SO42− emerged as the predominant contributor to this adverse association (Figure S3).

Discussion

In the above study, we first used single-pollutant models to reveal the effects of exposure to air pollutants on semen quality and identified the critical exposure window. Our analysis indicated a significant inverse association between PM2.5 and sperm motility at week 10. Multi-pollutant analysis showed that air pollution co-exposure contributed to reduced sperm motility at week 11, with PM2.5 emerging as the dominant contributor. The findings aligned with our single-pollutant models. Combined with the results of the DLNM models, a relatively early stage of spermatogenesis, from week 10 to week 11 before semen collection, was found to be the potentially sensitive window that spermatogenesis was affected by air pollution. Afterward, similar analysis was conducted to assess the correlation between major chemical components of PM2.5 and semen quality. The correlation turned out to be negative and SO42− contributed the most.

Most previous studies put eyes on the whole period of spermatogenesis, throughout 90 days before semen collection [23, 25, 34] and a few studies performed research during three windows (including 0–9, 10–14, 70–90 days before semen collection) on the basis of spermatogenetic cycle [28, 35, 36], while there is relatively little research exploring across various stages of sperm development. Identifying the key exposure window and refining the analysis are both essential for facilitating our ability to find out the possible reason for declined semen quality. Sun et al. held the opinion that the period from week 6 to week 12 before semen collection was the susceptible window for particulate matter exposure [30], which was relatively consistent with our findings.

Our research suggested that being exposed to PM2.5 in an early window of sperm development would do harm to sperm motility and increase the risk of having below-reference progressive motility (< 32%). A study conducted in Shijiazhuang, China, showed significant negative correlations linking ambient particulate matters (PM) exposure with sperm motility. However, no statistically significant associations were found between PM and total sperm count or sperm concentration [37]. Ma et al. demonstrated inverse dose-response relationships that sperm motility declined with higher levels of PM2.5 and PM10 70–90 days before semen collection [38]. Moreover, based on the WQS regression and the BKMR models, we proposed that being co-exposed to air pollution was negatively correlated with sperm motility from week 10 to week 11, which meant 64–77 days before semen collection, partly consistent with the studies mentioned above.

Compared with the published articles, there are still some differences in our study. The cohort study on 1759 men in Anhui, China found that PM exposure adversely affected sperm concentration and total sperm count, not sperm motility [20]. Still having is, controversy exists over the relations between O3 and semen quality. Zhang et al. reported that the decrement in total sperm count was correlated with O3 [28], while we figured out the opposite result. It came out that O3 had negative correlation with the other five air pollutants according to the Spearman correlation coefficient from our study. Probable causes of these discrepancies may include the following conditions. Firstly, studies differed in study population and some had relatively insufficient sample sizes, which made them unrepresentative. Secondly, different studies adjusted for different confounding factors. Lastly, most studies adopted averages to evaluate the levels of individual exposure, which would disguise the true exposure status.

In addition, our analysis indicated that PM2.5 decreased sperm motility and contributed the most based on the WQS regression, so that we further investigated the associations between PM2.5 chemical components and semen quality. We found that NH4+, NO3−, and SO42− led to lower progressive motility and all five components inversely associated with total motility at week 10 before semen collection, which was considered susceptible to air pollution, and SO42− contributed the most. Lopalco et al. observed the clinical phenomenon that patients with oligoasthenozoospermia had higher cholesterol sulfate/seminolipid ratio than normal subjects [39]. One possible explanation was that sulfate could accumulate in the human body and resulted in oxidative stress injury [40].

The underlying biological mechanisms responsible for damage of semen quality from long-term inhalation of air pollution have not been elaborated yet. Previous researches suggested that increased oxidative stress resulting from air pollution exposure might lead to inflammation and methylation in germ cells, causing cell injury and apoptosis [41], further disrupting hormone secretion and reducing semen quality [36, 42]. The latest research has found that PM2.5 enhances m6A modification by promoting METTL3 expression in Leydig cells, leading to abnormal autophagy and declined testosterone levels in the testes and serum, resulting in testicular dysfunction and impaired semen quality [43]. It is reported that particulate matters may also give rise to the arrest of spermatocyte cycle [44–46].

The research subjects are the population with natural fertility desire in the Nanjing area, which has good representativeness. We found that PM2.5 reduced sperm motility, which was particularly sensitive in the stages of sperm production (from week 10 to week 11), consistent with other reported epidemiologic studies [1, 34, 47]. Therefore, during the preparation period, we suggest that the father should also attend to the air pollution and take relevant protective measures. Another strength of our study is the simultaneous consideration of the influence of single exposure and co-exposure to pollutants. What’s more, we also discussed the impact of major chemical components of PM2.5 and more comprehensive semen quality parameters, providing clues for researchers to plumb the potential mechanisms of PM2.5 leading to decreasing semen quality.

However, there are limitations to our research that need to be stated. Firstly, we evaluated individual exposure based on residential addresses lacking the information of indoor or occupational exposure. Additionally, using the IDW method to measure the level of individual exposure was not accurate enough. As IDW was based on static spatial interpolation without considering the hourly/daily fluctuation of pollutants, and the ground monitoring station cannot reflect the vertical concentration difference, these might underestimate the impact of pollutants. Moreover, although all participants were reported fertile when asked about population information, in fact, not all participants had normal semen quality. Finally, to some extent, this can lead to bias as each participant only provided one semen sample.

Conclusions

Being exposed to PM2.5 during the critical window of spermatogenesis could damage semen quality, especially sperm motility. Our study provided evidence that PM2.5 was inversely correlated with sperm motility in both the single pollutant models and the multi-pollutants models in an early window, from week 10 to week 11 before semen collection. These findings would be imperative to make public health interventions and promote male reproductive health.
