Association of dietary omega-3 fatty acids intake with all-cause and cardiovascular disease-specific mortality among individuals with cardiovascular disease
Authors: Yifei Yan, Huan Li, Xue Fen Yang, Yang Yang, Ying Zeng, Yilin Cai, Jianchang Li, Xiangyong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Omega-3 fatty acids (omega-3) are widely recognized for their potential benefits in promoting cardiovascular health. However, the precise relationship between dietary omega-3 fatty acid intake and mortality outcomes in participants with established cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains a subject of ongoing debate and considerable uncertainty. Clinical trials have yielded conflicting results, creating a need for further clarification. To address these uncertainties, we undertook a prospective study to examine the long-term associations between omega-3 fatty acids, including their specific subtypes, and both all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality in participants with CVD. This study aimed to explore the possible link between dietary intake of omega-3 fatty acids and both all-cause mortality and CVD-specific mortality in patients with cardiovascular conditions. Utilizing publicly accessible National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey linked mortality files up to December 31, 2019, we identified mortality rates and specific causes of death related to heart disease. To determine the association between omega-3 fatty acid consumption and mortality risk, we employed multivariable Cox regression analyses, generating hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) while adjusting for a variety of demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and comorbid conditions. Moreover, we utilized Kaplan-Meier survival curves and conducted subgroup analyses to further examine these associations. To assess how varying levels of dietary Omega-3 fatty acids influence the risk of mortality in patients with CVD, we employed restricted cubic spline models under various conditions. A total of 3,826 participants with CVD completed the final cohort, averaging 7.59 years of follow up. Following adjustments for multiple variables, a pronounced inverse association was noted for participants in the highest quintile of total Omega-3 consumption and overall mortality, indicated by the HR of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.60–0.99). Likewise, increased total Omega-3 consumption was correlated with a reduction in CVD-specific mortality, with the HR of 0.63 (95% CI: 0.42–0.95), which was confirmed statistical significance by trend tests (P for Trend < 0.05). In the separate analysis of individual subtypes of the omega-3 fatty acid family, the consumption of alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) was obviously inverse-associated with CVD-specific mortality (HR of 0.64, 95% CI: 0.44–0.95). Nonetheless, the consumption of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), and docosapentaenoic acid (DPA) did not show a significant association with mortality risk (P > 0.05). In the dose-response relationship analysis, total omega-3 fatty acid intake and ALA intake were observed to have an “L”-shaped nonlinear relationship with cardiovascular mortality, with the inflection points at 2.12 (g/day) and 2.03 (g/day), respectively. In summary, our research indicates that both total omega-3 fatty acids and ALA are inversely linked to the risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in patients with CVD. We recommend a daily intake of 2.12 g of total omega-3 fatty acids, with an optimal intake of 2.03 g/day for ALA for CVD patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21193-1.

Introduction

CVD represent a major factor in the global disease burden and continue to be the predominant cause of death worldwide, significantly diminishing human health and life expectancy1,2. This spectrum of fatal diseases encompasses coronary heart disease (CAD), angina pectoris, congestive heart failure (CHF), stroke, myocardial infarction (MI), and other cardiovascular conditions3. In addition to conventional clinical treatments, dietary modifications have emerged as a crucial strategy for both the prevention of CVDs and the improvement of their prognosis4,5, suggesting that lifestyle interventions potentially have a key influence on mitigating incidence and intensity of cardiovascular ailments.

Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), essential fatty acids for human health, are predominantly present in fish of the deep sea (including salmon, cod, and tuna) along with certain plant sources like flaxseed and perilla (an herb of the mint family native to East Asia)6,7. These fatty acids are vital for multiple physiological processes in the human body. They enhance vascular dilation responses, improve arterial compliance8, and are integral to brain development9 and the visual system advancement10. Previous randomized controlled trials and epidemiological studies have demonstrated that omega-3 fatty acids can improve clinical outcomes in CVD and slow disease progression11–14. Similarly, our previous study also found that fish consumption, especially the consumption of long-chain (LC) omega-3 fatty acids, was inversely linked to the risk of stroke15. Therefore, increasing Omega-3 intake or consuming omega-3-containing pharmaceuticals have been linked to a decreased incidence of CVD and a reduced risk of mortality associated with these conditions.

Nevertheless, recent meta-analyses16 have raised an ongoing controversy regarding the role of omega-3 fatty acids in cardiovascular health. A notable discrepancy exists between the nature of omega-3 intake in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and in real-world dietary consumption, which can complicate the translation of trial findings to dietary guidelines. RCTs typically administer high-dose pharmaceutical-grade supplements of purified EPA and DHA, a level far exceeding that of average dietary intake in most populations. Furthermore, these trials test isolated fatty acids, removed from the complex food matrix in which they are naturally found. In contrast, dietary intake from sources like fish provides omega-3s alongside a suite of other nutrients, such as selenium, vitamin D, and high-quality protein, which may have synergistic health effects. Therefore, a comprehensive study focusing specifically on the impact of omega-3 fatty acids derived from food sources is urgently needed to assess their impact on the prognosis of CVD patients, independent of pharmaceutical or supplement use. This would provide critical evidence for the tertiary prevention of CVD.

To address this issue, we initiated a prospective study leveraging data sourced from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). This study endeavors to explore the following points: (1 the association between the intake levels of omega-3 fatty acids, including their subtypes, and both all-cause and CVD-specific mortality in CVD patients; (2 the relationship between varying doses of omega-3 fatty acids and their specific subtypes with the effect size on all-cause and CVD-specific mortality in these patients.

Method

Study design and participants

NHANES is a nationwide, multicenter program designed to provide researchers with instant access to information from nationally representative surveys based on U.S. adults. This meticulously formulated program systematically evaluates the physical health and nutritional condition of the U.S. adults. It utilizes a sophisticated, layered, multi-step sampling approach and has been widely used as a sizable long-term study. This sample accurately mirrors the nation demographic composition through connections to a subsequent survey17. NHANES data acquisition methodology includes initial in-home interviews, health screenings at mobile examination centers, and the further investigation of telephone interviews. To evaluate the dietary habits of the U.S. adults, the intake levels of dietary total Omega-3 fatty acids and its subtypes intake were computed leveraging the Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies developed by USDA. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, received ethical authorization from the NHANES review board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. We conducted a review of participants from 9 consecutive cycles (2001–2018) of the NHANES program, encompassing a total of 5,774 participants who self-reported a physician diagnosis of CVD. From this initial cohort, we excluded 8 participants who were pregnant, 4 participants with missing survival data, 1,265 participants without data on Omega-3 fatty acids intake, 544 participants with missing essential covariate data (such as education, smoking, BMI, etc.), and 127 participants with unreliable energy intake18. This resulted in a final cohort of 3,826 participants for inclusion in the analysis. Over a mean follow-up period of 7.59 years, 1,608 all-cause deaths were documented, which included 639 deaths from cardiovascular causes (Fig. 1).

Dietary omega-3 fatty acids intake

The intake of omega-3 fatty acids in this study was derived solely from the consumption of foods and beverages, specifically excluding sources from either dietary supplements or omega-3 containing pharmaceuticals. The food items collected were coded using the Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS) to calculate total Omega-3 fatty acid and its subtypes intakes for each participant on both the initial and subsequent recording dates. The total dietary omega-3 fatty acids intake was defined as the sum of the intakes of eicosapentaenoic acid (20:5); alpha-linolenic acid (18:3); docosahexaenoic acid (22:6); and docosapentaenoic acid (22:5)19–21. Both the total omega-3 fatty acid intake and the intake of each omega-3 fatty acid subtype were categorized into quintiles to investigate their effects on the incidence of all-cause and CVD-related mortality among CVD patients.

This research utilized the average intake of Omega-3 fatty acids, obtained through two 24-hour dietary recalls, with the first being gathered by experienced interviewers, and the latter being obtained through telephone interviews conducted 3 to 10 days later.

Ascertainment of outcome

The diagnosis of CVD in patients was defined by self-report of one or more of six CVD subtypes: heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, or stroke22–25. This was ascertained through a standardized composite questionnaire asking, “Has a medical practitioner or healthcare provider ever informed you that you have experienced a [condition]?” If a participant responded affirmatively to one or more of these conditions, they were classified as having CVD. The questionnaire does not differentiate between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke subtypes.

The all-cause and CVD disease mortality rates among patients were ascertained through probabilistic matching of unique identifiers with the National Death Index26. Participants without a corresponding record were presumed to be alive as of the specified date. The follow up duration was quantified in person years, spanning from the baseline assessment to either the participant’s demise or the conclusion of the follow up period on December 31, 2019. Following the guidelines of the ICD-10, deaths due to all causes and CVD were identified. All-cause mortality encompassed deaths resulting from CVD diseases (codes I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51, and I60-I69), pneumonia and influenza (codes J09-J18), malignant neoplasms (codes C00-C97), along with all other causes. Additionally, only participants who died from heart-related causes were included in a separate subgroup analysis to determine whether the independent variable had a distinct effect on the cause of heart-related deaths.

Covariate assessment

Our covariates included age, education level, hyperlipidemia27,, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, BMI, total energy, sex, ethnicity, protein, carbohydrate, total sugars intake, vitamin B2(Riboflavin) intake, zinc intake, and total folate intake, saturated fatty acids intake, monounsaturated fatty acids intake, cholesterol intake, dietary fiber intake, smoking, alcohol. Smoking status was categorized as follows: never smokers (participants who have smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime), former smokers (participants who have smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime but have since quit), and smokers current (participants who have smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and continue to smoke presently). Alcohol use patterns were classified into five categories: heavy, moderate, mild, former, and never28,29.

The criteria for diagnosing hypertension included self-reported diagnosis, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of 90 mmHg or higher, systolic blood pressure (SBP) of 140 mmHg or higher30, or the use of antihypertensive medications. Diabetes Mellitus (DM)31,32 and related abnormalities were classified as follows: (1) IGT: impaired glucose tolerance; (2) IFG: impaired fasting glycemia; (3) DM: diabetes mellitus; (4) no diabetes. Detailed definitions of general population characteristics, lifestyle habits, and disease conditions are provided in Supplemental Methods.

Statistical analysis

As a summary of baseline characteristics, Frequencies (percentages) were used to represent categorical variables, while means (standard errors) were utilized to depict continuous variables. Quintiles of omega-3 fatty acids intake were documented (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4, Q5). The calculation of follow up person years began from the baseline date and continued until the first occurrence of either death, loss to follow up, or the study’s conclusion on December 31, 2019. In all analyses, we applied the specialized dietary weights “wtdr2d” and “wtdr4yr” provided by NHANES to account for the complex survey design and non-response, thereby ensuring our results are representative of the U.S. non-institutionalized population.

We stratified the average Omega-3 fatty acids intake by age (12 groups: 20–25, 25–30, 30–35, 35–40, 40–45, 45–50, 50–55, 55–60, 60–65, 65–70, 70–75, 75–80 years), BMI (5 groups: <18.5, 18.5–25, 25–30, 30–35, ≥ 35 kg/m²), education level, and ethnicity. To illustrate variations in Omega-3 fatty acids consumption across different demographic groups, we generated bar charts and spline curves. These visualizations enabled us to analyze trends in average Omega-3 fatty acids intake among groups with different attributes. Differences in characteristics, disease status, and other parameters between Omega-3 fatty acids intake quintiles were examined using weighted Pearson’s Chi-squared tests for categorical variables and weighted Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables.

To evaluate the association between the consumption of omega-3 fatty acids and their subtypes with all-cause mortality and CVD-specific mortality among patients with CVD, we employed multivariable Cox regression models. Hazard ratios and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated to quantify the effect sizes. Three progressively adjusted models were employed: Initially, adjustments were made solely based on age (years, continuous) (Model 1). Subsequently, we included additional covariates such as race, sex, protein intake, smoking status, BMI (kg/m², continuous), total energy intake, carbohydrate intake, and total sugar intake (Model 2). Further adjustments were then made to account for mortality risk factors, including diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and alcohol use, as well as nutrient intakes associated with omega-3 fatty acids, specifically vitamin B2, zinc, and folate, saturated fatty acids intake, monounsaturated fatty acids intake, cholesterol intake, dietary fiber intake (Model 3). Additionally, multivariate Cox regression analyses were stratified by age groups, BMI groups, hyperlipidemia, total energy intake, and ethnicity. We introduced a multiplicative interaction term between the omega-3 intake quintiles and each stratification variable in the main model. The significance of each interaction term was then assessed using a survey-weighted Likelihood Ratio test.

To visualize and compare the survival probabilities associated with different levels of total omega-3 fatty acid intake, we constructed weighted Kaplan-Meier survival curves, stratified by intake quintiles. Age-standardized mortality rates were calculated using the 2000 Census population as a reference. We also employed restricted cubic spline curves to delve into the relationship between total omega-3 fatty acid and subtypes intakes with all-cause and CVD-specific mortality. The entirety of the data analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.4.3), and statistical significance was established at a P-value under 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

According to the established exclusion standards, a total of 3,826 CVD patients were enrolled in the study, comprising 1,671 females (43.7%) and 2,155 males (56.3%) (Fig. 2A). The age distribution of the patients revealed that the majority were aged between 65 and 80 years (40.7%) and 50–65 years (27.5%), while those under 35 years (2%) constituted only a small fraction of the cohort (Fig. 2B). Throughout an average monitoring span of 7.59 years (29,063 total person-years), the study documented 1,608 deaths, including 639 cases of cardiovascular-related mortality among the participants. Table 1 presents the sample sizes and weighted percentages for various baseline characteristics, representing 17,001,933 non-hospitalized patients with CVD in the USA. As shown in the table, several differences were observed in demographic and clinical features between participants with lower omega-3 fatty acids intake (Q1) and those with higher intake (Q4/Q5). For example, participants with higher Omega-3 fatty acids intake generally had higher intakes of total energy, protein, and folate. Crude mortality rates were also higher among participants with lower levels of omega-3 fatty acids intake. The omega-3 fatty acids intake was higher in participants who were at a higher level of education and in participants of White race (Supplemental Fig. 1). Additionally, baseline characteristics stratified by survival status are elaborated in Supplemental Table 1.

Omega-3 fatty acids intake and mortality

In the fully adjusted multivariable model, a higher dietary intake of total Omega-3 fatty acids was associated with a lower risk of mortality compared to the lowest intake quintile. Specifically for all-cause mortality, participants in the fourth (HR: 0.76; 95% CI: 0.58–0.99), and fifth quintiles (HR: 0.77; 95% CI: 0.60–0.99) had a significantly lower risk. A similar inverse association was observed for CVD-specific mortality in the fourth (HR: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.40–0.88), and fifth quintiles (HR: 0.63; 95% CI: 0.42–0.95) (Table 2). The trend across quintiles was statistically significant for both outcomes (P for trend < 0.05). In subtype analysis, only alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) intake was significantly associated with a lower risk of CVD-specific mortality. In contrast, no significant associations were found for EPA, DPA, or DHA with mortality risk in patients with CVD (Supplemental Tables 2–5).

Multivariable adjustment Cox regression incorporating RCS was employed to investigate the dose-response association between dietary omega-3 fatty acids consumption and the risk of mortality. Our analysis reveals a distinct association between total omega-3 fatty acid intake and mortality risks among CVD patients. Specifically, the all-cause mortality risk decreases in a monotonically ‘L’-shaped pattern as the total omega-3 fatty acid intake increases (P for overall = 0.0014; P for nonlinear = 0.0027) (Fig. 3). The association with cardiovascular-specific mortality follows an ‘L’-shaped curve in connection with total omega-3 fatty acid consumption, with an inflection point where the curve flattens at an intake level of 2.12 g/day. Similar dose-response patterns were noted for alpha-linolenic acid, where the association with cardiovascular mortality risk also exhibited an ‘L’-shaped relationship, with an inflection point at an ALA intake of 2.03 g/day (Supplemental Fig. 2). In contrast, no significant nonlinear trends were identified for EPA, DHA, or DPA (Supplemental Fig. 3–5).

The Kaplan-Meier survival curve assessment indicates that CVD patients with high omega-3 fatty acid intake (Q4/Q5) exhibit lower overall mortality and CVD-specific mortality compared to those with low Omega-3 fatty acid intake (Fig. 4). Age-standardized mortality rates, which were calculated based on the 2000 census, revealed that for participants with CVD in the lowest quintile of omega-3 fatty acid intake (Q1), the age-standardized mortality rates were 41.6% for men and 24.1% for women. In contrast, those in the highest quintile of Omega-3 fatty acid intake (Q5) demonstrated substantially lower age-standardized mortality rates, at 18.5% for men and 10.0% for women (Fig. 5).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

After multivariable adjustments, the subgroups were stratified by age (< 50, 50–69, and ≥ 70 years), ethnicity, hyperlipidemia status, daily energy intake (< 1500, 1500–2000, > 2000 kcal/d), and BMI categories (< 25, 25–35, > 35 kg/m²), Smoke status, Hypertension, Diabetes mellitus.

Our stratified analysis revealed that the highest quintile of omega-3 fatty acid intake significantly reduced all-cause mortality among CVD patients within specific subgroups. These subgroups included participants aged ≥ 70 years, White and Other Hispanic ethnicities, those with hyperlipidemia, and a BMI group 25–35 kg/m², Current Smoke. The trend tests for these subgroups were significant (P for Trend < 0.05), indicating that the hazard ratios decreased as Omega-3 fatty acid intake increased across quintiles. No interaction effects were detected among these subgroups (P for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 6).

To assess the robustness of our results, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. These included: (1) treating age and BMI as continuous variables in the model; (2) further adjusting for vitamin C and vitamin K as covariates; (3) excluding patients with follow-up durations shorter than 12 months; and (4) utilizing the Willett nutrient residual model to create energy-adjusted values for omega-3 fatty acids. Across these analyses, the associations between Omega-3 intake and both all-cause and CVD-specific mortality remained consistent with our primary findings, with no meaningful changes to the effect estimates (Supplemental Tables 6–9).

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the relationship between omega-3 fatty acid intake and the risk of death in patients with CVD using a prospective cohort study model by analyzing data from NHANES. According to our findings, a notable negative association was observed between the intake of omega-3 fatty acids and all-cause mortality among CVD patients, and this relationship remained robust at the time of cardiovascular-specific mortality. Additionally, when analyzing individual subtypes of the omega-3 fatty acid family, the intake of ALA demonstrated similar results. Dose-response curve analysis indicated that the ideal consumption of omega-3 fatty acid for CVD patients is 2.12 g/day.

Our study has several notable strengths. First, its prospective design minimizes the potential for recall bias regarding the outcomes. Second, we utilized data from NHANES, a large, nationally representative sample of the U.S. population, which enhances the generalizability of our findings to a broader American population with CVD. Third, we were able to adjust for a wide range of potential confounders, including demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and dietary variables, strengthening the internal validity of our results. Finally, the long follow-up period allowed us to accrue a sufficient number of events to provide statistically robust estimates.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, our dietary assessment relied on two 24-hour dietary recalls. This method, like all self-report dietary assessments, is susceptible to measurement error, including both random and systematic inaccuracies in reporting. Furthermore, these short-term recalls may not fully represent an individual’s long-term habitual diet. Second, as an observational study, our findings demonstrate association, not causation. Despite adjusting for numerous covariates, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured or imprecisely measured variables cannot be entirely excluded. Third, dietary intake was assessed only at baseline, and we could not account for potential changes in diet over the long follow-up period. Finally, the study population was composed of US adults, and given the known global variations in dietary patterns, the generalizability of our findings to other populations may be limited33.

Dietary intake of omega-3 fatty acids, a nutrient essential for the optimal physiological function of the human body, has long been recognized for its benefits to the cardiovascular system. Since Albert et al.34 introduced the renowned “Eskimo Paradox” in 1998, numerous studies have sought to substantiate the predominance of omega-3 fatty acids in combating CVD13,35, particularly EPA and DHA. Uauy et al.36 reported that DHA and EPA lessen the clinical hazards of CVD by modulating lipid profiles and hemostatic factors, including bleeding time and platelet aggregation. Additionally, omega-3 fatty acids influence heart rate and arterial pressure by increasing nitric oxide production37 and mitigating blood vessel constriction reactions to norepinephrine and angiotensin II38. Interestingly, our previous study also found that fish consumption, especially the intake of LC omega-3 fatty acids, was inversely associated with the risk of stroke15. In conclusion, the above studies highlight the critical role of omega-3 fatty acids in cardiovascular health.

However, recent studies have challenged the notion that omega-3 fatty acids are a universal “panacea” for the treatment of CVD. According to an article by the American Heart Association39, Omega-3 supplements may diminish mortality in patients with coronary artery disease primarily by lowering the incidence of ischemic sudden death. But these supplements do not seem to reduce the recurrence of non-fatal myocardial infarctions. Even more, a meta-analysis16 suggested that Omega-3 intake was not significantly associated with a reduced risk of mortality from CVD disease. Although subsequent studies6 have criticized this meta-analysis for focusing excessively on statistical metrics while neglecting the substantial dosage variations of Omega-3 across different studies, the issue remains unresolved. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of omega-3 fatty acids as pharmacological supplements or dietary sources is needed through randomized controlled trials or epidemiological research. In the present study, the primary focus is to appraise whether dietary-sourced omega-3 fatty acids, in their various subtypes, can significantly impact CVD outcomes in real-world settings. Additionally, we aim to establish the optimal Omega-3 intake levels to minimize mortality risk among cardiovascular patients by utilizing dose-response curves.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, our dietary assessment relied on two 24-hour dietary recalls. While this method is detailed, it is susceptible to recall bias and may not fully represent an individual’s long-term habitual diet. Second, as an observational study, our findings demonstrate association, not causation. Despite adjusting for numerous covariates, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured or imprecisely measured variables cannot be entirely excluded. Third, dietary intake was assessed only at baseline, and we could not account for potential changes in diet over the long follow-up period. Finally, the study population was composed of US adults, and given the known global variations in dietary patterns, the generalizability of our findings to other populations may be limited.

Our findings not only delineate the effect of omega-3 fatty acids on mortality among CVD patients, but also highlight the significant protective effects with the high dietary intake of omega-3 fatty acids. Specifically, participants in the highest quintile of omega-3 fatty acids consumption (Q5) exhibited an age-adjusted HR for all-cause mortality of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.59, 0.87), and a multivariable-adjusted hazard ratio of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.60, 0.99). For cardiovascular mortality, the age-adjusted HR was 0.59 (95% CI: 0.44, 0.81), and the multivariable-adjusted HR was 0.63 (95% CI: 0.42, 0.95), and the test of trend confirmed statistical significance (P for Trend < 0.05). Furthermore, when examining specific omega-3 fatty acids, ALA demonstrated a significant inverse relationship with cardiovascular mortality (HR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.44, 0.95) in multivariable-adjusted models. Conversely, no statistically significant associations were identified between the intake of EPA, DPA, or DHA and mortality outcomes. Thus, the results of our study imply that incorporating ALA into the diet may provide substantial advantages for patients suffering from cardiovascular conditions.

Utilizing restricted cubic spline models, we have elucidated the dose-response association between omega-3 fatty acids intake and its subtypes with mortality risk among patients with CVD. Our findings indicate that total Omega-3 intake exhibits an “L”-shaped nonlinear relationship with all-cause mortality (P for nonlinear = 0.0027) and an “L”-shaped nonlinear association with CVD-specific mortality (P for nonlinear = 0.0006), with an inflection point in the curve around 2.12 g/day of Omega-3 intake. Similarly, ALA demonstrates an “L”-shaped association with cardiovascular mortality, with the inflection point occurring at an ALA intake of 2.03 g/day. Conversely, no significant nonlinear associations were observed in the dose-response curves for EPA, DPA, and DHA. Moreover, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that CVD patients with higher dietary omega-3 fatty acid intake (Q4/Q5) exhibited a significantly greater survival rate compared to those with lower Omega-3 intake (P < 0.05), which highlights the beneficial influence of omega-3 fatty acids on survival outcomes. Moreover, data on population-based age-standardized mortality rates indicated that men in Q5 of omega-3 fatty acid intake exhibited a 23.1% reduction in standardized mortality rates compared to those in Q1. Similarly, females in Q5 of omega-3 fatty acid intake had a 14.1% lower standardized mortality rate than their counterparts in Q1.

According to our results, marine-derived DPA, DHA, and EPA40 were determined to have no significant relationship with mortality risk in CVD patients. In contrast, plants-derived ALA41 has an obvious negative association with both all cause and CVD-specific mortality. Despite the above, these results seem to contradict previous randomized controlled trials. However, our supplementary figures reveal that the dietary intake levels of DHA and EPA in the general population are exceedingly low, even the highest quintile of DHA and EPA intake is far lower than the International Society for the Study of Fatty Acids and Lipids advised dosage of at least 500 mg/day of DHA and EPA combined42. Recent analyses have begun to explore whether the failure to achieve therapeutic concentrations of omega-3 fatty acids in some randomized controlled trials may be the cause of the lack of observed benefits43,44, a non-statistical factor not accounted for in meta-analyses. Furthermore, considering that marine fish, a plentiful source of omega-3 fatty acid, can also be high in heavy metals45,46, it raises the question of whether excessive consumption of deep-sea fish to obtain adequate DHA and EPA might represent a “Pyrrhic victory” due to potential health risks associated with heavy metal exposure.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates an inverse association between total omega-3 fatty acids and both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in patients with CVD. When analyzing individual members of the omega-3 fatty acid family, ALA was found to be inversely associated with CVD prognosis similarly, whereas EPA, DPA, and DHA showed no significant association. Based on dose-response analysis, we recommend a daily intake of 2.12 g of total omega-3 fatty acids for patients with CVD, with an optimal intake of 2.03 g per day specifically for ALA.
