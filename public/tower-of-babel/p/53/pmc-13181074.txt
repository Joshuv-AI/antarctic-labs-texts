Life cycle externalities and abatement costs of public transport electrification using fuel cell and battery electric buses in Putrajaya city
Authors: Jing Wen Chong, Marlia M. Hanafiah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
City-level bus electrification is needed for urban decarbonization. Previous research shows varied preference between battery electric buses (BEBs) and fuel cell electric buses (FCEBs), due to region-specific factors. Hence, to determine the more practical option for deployment in a Malaysian city, this case study evaluates environmental and economic performance through life cycle assessment (LCA) and total cost of ownership (TCO) analysis, based on simulated energy consumption. The system boundary covers both vehicle and fuel cycles, assessing fossil-, solar-, and biomass-based hydrogen pathways. Results show that BEBs exhibit the lowest values across all three endpoint environmental impact categories, compared to all other FCEBs scenarios, with its environmental externalities priced at USD 831,587. They also achieve the lowest life-cycle GHG emissions at 58.1 kgCO2/100 km under Malaysia’s fossil-heavy grid, compared with the best FCEB scenario of 121.2 kgCO2/100 km. BEBs also yield the lowest TCO at 8.9 million USD and the lowest GHG abatement cost of 0.39 USD per kgCO2. The findings show BEBs deliver greater environmental-cost benefits than FCEBs, but future improvements in hydrogen supply chain might narrow this gap. Future work should assess whether FCEB deployment could be justified as a strategic option in helping a national hydrogen ecosystem development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46665-w.

Introduction

The global climate crisis has intensified, with rising temperatures now projected to exceed the 1.5 °C threshold set by the Paris Agreement1, leading to alarming consequences, including sea-level rise, displacement of communities, and biodiversity loss, all largely driven by anthropogenic greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions2. Key contributors to these emissions include the energy, industrial, and transport sectors. Transport alone accounts for approximately 15% of global GHG emissions, as it remains heavily reliant on fossil fuels for powering passenger vehicles, long-distance travel, aviation, and shipping3. In 2019, the transportation sector in Malaysia contributed 64,973.10 Gg CO2eq (approximately 19.5% of the country’s total GHG emissions), according to Malaysia’s Fourth Biennial Update Report (BUR4) to the UNFCCC4. The intergovernmental panel on climate change (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) highlights two key transport-related mitigation strategies: transport electrification and modal shift. The International Energy Agency (IEA), in its Net Zero by 2050: A Roadmap for the Global Energy Sector (2021), also sees electrification is the “backbone” of decarbonizing transport, particularly for light-duty vehicles5. Both IPCC AR6 and IEA stress the importance of targeting public transport fleets. By 2030, all new urban buses should be zero-emission to align with net-zero goals. Urban buses are especially critical due to their high usage and operation in dense, polluted cities. Bus electrification is reported as a cost-effective decarbonization strategy, which delivers high emissions reductions per dollar invested, particularly in high-density urban contexts with high travel demand5.

Malaysia’s National Energy Transition Roadmap (NETR) reflects this global momentum, emphasizing the electrification of public transport, particularly buses, as a key strategy to meet the country’s net-zero target by 2050. Among available technologies, battery electric vehicles (BEVs) are the most established and offer the greatest decarbonization potential for land-based transport6, while fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs) and synthetic fuels are better suited for long-haul or specialized applications7. Given buses’ high utilization, fixed routes, and substantial per-vehicle emissions, it is a strategic starting point for Malaysia’s shift toward low-carbon mobility. Accordingly, this paper focuses on comparing battery electric bus (BEB) and fuel cell electric bus (FCEB) as two viable pathways for decarbonizing Malaysia’s public bus fleet.

Overview of global and Malaysian bus electrification projects

The global transition towards electrified buses, BEBs and FCEBs, follows distinct development paths shaped by respective technical challenges and market readiness. Both technologies began gaining attention in 1990s through pilot projects aimed to demonstrating their concepts8,9. Between 2000 and 2010, pilot deployments expanded with small fleets, including Europe’s CUTE and HyFLEET-CUTE projects for FCEBs, and Foothill Transit’s EcoRide BEBs in the United States, developed by Proterra and powered by lithium-ion batteries (see Fig. 1). BEB deployment accelerated in the 2010s, driven by strong industrial policy and investment in China. Shenzhen became a global benchmark by fully electrifying its 16,000-bus fleet by 2017, portraying commercial viability, and catalysing international adoption. In contrast, FCEBs have progressed more slowly. Many projects were paused or discontinued due to high vehicle and infrastructure costs, as well as limited hydrogen supply chains. As a result, BEBs have reached commercial maturity in many regions, while FCEBs remain at the pilot scale and continue to rely heavily on public funding.

In Malaysia, a key contributor to hydrogen-based public transport electrification is Sarawak Metro, which plans to deploy hydrogen-powered autonomous rapid transit (ART) vehicles and FCEBs under the Kuching Urban transportation system (KUTS). This represents one of the country’s most ambitious FCEB projects since 2020. However, BEB implementation began earlier with the 2015 launch of the Sunway BRT Line. Both BEBs and FCEBs are included in national mobility strategies, such as the Low Carbon Mobility Blueprint Focus Area 2. The question remains as to which technology is better suited to the local context.

Past literature and research gaps

It is well established in literature that conventionally fuelled buses, such as diesel buses, needs to be phased out for sustainability reasons, even though they remain more economical. Electrifying the bus fleet with FCEBs and BEBs consistently results in lower life cycle emissions, including GHGs, VOCs and NOx10,11, contributing to cleaner air and improved regional air quality12, as well as reduced noise pollution13. In terms of cost superiority, though, electrification will require subsidies for electric buses and the imposition of taxes on diesel buses14.

Recent research has increasingly focused on “zero-emission buses,” with a gradual rise in comparative studies between FCEBs and BEBs over the past five years (see Table 1 for the summary). However, studies that evaluate both environmental and cost impacts together remain limited. These include Eckert, et al.15, who used real-world data from European sites to analyse their carbon footprint (CF), total cost of ownership (TCO), and operational performance, concluding that BEBs are more cost and energy efficient, because FCEBs require additional energy in its life cycle for hydrogen production. Kügemann and Polatidis16, who developed a Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis framework with Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) to select buses using criteria from Life Cycle Sustainability Analysis (LCSA), determined that the FCEBs, despite being expensive, are favoured, especially in scenario of local fuel production, due the low weightage score assigned to the Life cycle cost (LCC) criteria. Hensher, et al.17 developed a Decision Support System, combining CO2 emissions and costs factors, for 2021–2030 transition of Australian fleet. They found that, subject to the relative costs of electricity and green hydrogen, mixed fleet could offer an optimal solution for reducing emission and achieving cost savings, but given current Australia market, BEBs are more cost-effective. Muñoz, et al.18 conducted the comparison using integrated index combining normalized energy efficiency, emissions, and economic indices. BEBs lead in integrated performance between 2023 and 2025, but FCEVs overtake by 2027–2030, propelled by projected cost reductions in fuel cells and hydrogen, supported further by a maturing hydrogen infrastructure. Another comparative study that covered both carbon footprint and cost analysis is done by Kuyumcu, et al.19, on BEBs and buses powered by hydrogen internal combustion engine (H2ICE), instead of fuel cell technology, as a more practical entry point for introducing hydrogen in the transportation industry.

Urban public transport networks primarily rely on both bus and rail systems to manage high-density mobility20. However, the aforementioned works consistently focused on urban bus fleets operating on city routes as substitutes for conventional internal combustion engine buses, with case studies from cities such as Berlin, Australia, and Argentina (Table 1). This emphasis largely reflects the high visibility of urban buses and their relatively low barrier to entry for transport decarbonization. Bus fleet transitions are inherently more modular: unlike rail systems, which often require extensive track-side electrification, investment in new buses can be implemented incrementally through bus procurement, delivering more immediate emissions abatement21. As a result, urban buses are commonly identified as the most practical and flexible entry point for city-wide decarbonization strategies22.

There are more studies that centred their comparative analyses on a single aspect, primarily emissions, as seen in Zhao, et al.23, Grazieschi, et al.24, Lubecki, et al.25, Ren, et al.26, and Chang, et al.27. These studies base their conclusions on emissions outcomes, without integrating other critical sustainability dimensions. While having a refined scope allows for a more detailed study of environmental performance, it limits the broader discussion required for evaluating sustainable mobility, which considers both economic affordability and ecological integrity. Nevertheless, these studies made notable contributions by exploring various innovative hydrogen production methods beyond conventional fossil-based and electrolysis-derived hydrogen. Examples include hydrogen from electrolysis powered by waste-to-energy (WtE) plants25 and by-product hydrogen such as coke-oven gas26. Indeed, the main goal in these cases was to assess how different fuel sources affect life cycle emissions, but a common shortcoming is the omission of the vehicle life cycle (manufacturing, maintenance, and end-of-life of the buses), which introduces potential bias in the conclusion. Furthermore, none of the reviewed studies consider biomass-based hydrogen, which remains an overlooked area of exploration.

Finally, preferences between BEBs and FCEBs vary across studies’ findings, largely influenced by regional energy profiles, grid mixes, and strategic development directions. For instance, Zhao, et al.23’s study excludes green hydrogen in the 2022 and 2030 baseline scenarios due to limitations in Saudi Arabia’s renewable energy infrastructure. The result shows that FCEBs are favoured over BEBs because their power sector is still dominated by fossil fuels, making grid electricity carbon-intensive. Conversely, Ren, et al.26 finds FCEBs unsuitable for China’s specific conditions. FCEBs show a 23% lower carbon footprint than BEBs in Taiwan27, while BEBs demonstrate lower cumulative energy demand (CED), even when assessed using Italy’s partially fossil-based grid electricity24.

Therefore, the following gaps have been identified in the current literature:

(i)The cost and environmental trade-offs of replacing urban diesel buses with FCEBs or BEBs are insufficiently understood under Malaysia’s grid composition and fuel supply conditions;(ii)While a variety of hydrogen fuel sources are considered, biomass-based hydrogen remains unexplored in the application as fuel source for FCEBs.

Objectives and expected contribution

The primary objective of this study is to determine whether FCEB or BEB offers greater environmental sustainability and cost efficiency within the Putrajaya urban transit network. This is achieved by conducting a comprehensive life cycle assessment (LCA) to identify the environmental externalities of adopting these two bus technologies, alongside a total cost of ownership (TCO) evaluation to evaluate the GHG abatement costs of public transport electrification. Several hydrogen production pathways, potentially available in Malaysia, are examined for FCEBs to evaluate whether comparable or superior performance can be attained under different fuel supply scenarios in a city setting.

This study addresses the gaps in the literature by incorporating a regional dimension by considering hydrogen derived from oil palm residues, a feedstock abundantly available in Malaysia, thus providing insights that are transferable to other biomass-rich economies. Finally, this research extends the system boundary beyond fuel cycle and integrate with vehicle cycle to include the manufacturing stage of key powertrain components such as hydrogen tanks, fuel cells, and traction batteries. The study is expected to offer policy-relevant evidence for the nation’s public transport electrification strategy and decarbonization pathway.

Case study background

Study location and bus routes

Putrajaya, with an area of 49 km2 and located near to the nation’s capital city (See Fig. 2), is established as Malaysia’s Federal Government Administration Centre in 1995. Standing as one of Southeast Asia’s most ambitious planned urban developments, Putrajaya serves as a representative model of Malaysia’s urban composition. The city’s ongoing development is guided by the Putrajaya Climate Action Plan (PACP) 2030, with goal set to reduce the peak temperature by 2°c compared to the 2005 level.

A core component of Putrajaya’s low-carbon strategy is the transformation of its transportation system through bus electrification. This initiative features an EV Bus Demonstration Project from 2015 to 2022, carried out in collaboration with New Energy and Industrial Technology Development Organization (NEDO)28, though it remained a trial with no clear public outcome29. At present, the city’s stop-and-go bus network, Nadiputra, is managed by Prasarana Malaysia Berhad, which also owns and operates other urban rail and bus services nationwide.

The Nadiputra bus service operates seven routes (P101-P108) within the city, with frequencies adjusted for peak and off-peak hours between 6:30 a.m. and 8:00 p.m. The number of buses assigned and the total distance per round trip vary by route, resulting in different average daily distances for each bus (See Table 2). Concerns about additional replacements of BEBs or FCEBs due to detours for recharging or refuelling30 are not applicable in this study, as the required daily travel distances remain within their driving ranges.

Technical specification of buses

Both bus models are assumed to be manufactured in China and shipped to Malaysia as complete units. This assumption is supported by existing trial deployments in Malaysia, which have adopted Chinese models due to their geographical proximity and lower overall costs. The selected models are FeiChi Technology’s FSQ6121FCEVGA FCEB and BYD’s K9 BEB. A service lifetime of 15 years is applied for both buses, in line with the Land Public Transport Agency’s operating permit standard, beyond which the buses are expected to be retired31. Charging, refuelling infrastructure and energy sources

Based on Table 3, the estimated average daily distance per bus is between 135 and 207 km, which is within the maximum range of both bus types. This indicates that neither the FCEB nor the BEB requires detours for mid-day recharging or refuelling, allowing full-day operation without interruption. Therefore, a depot-based charging and refuelling system is deemed sufficient for the fleet. For the BEBs, overnight plug-in charging at 50 kW per charger lasting 6 to 7 h will be adopted. Assuming each charger is capable of serving two buses in a night, a total of 10 chargers will be required to serve the fleet of 17 buses. For the FCEBs, the depot-based hydrogen refuelling station is designed with a daily throughout of 500 kg of hydrogen to meet the needs of the entire fleet. Such refuelling takes approximately only 3 to 5 min per bus35. The station will consist of a compressor, hydrogen storage vessels (200 and 500 bar) and a dispenser. The buses, equipped with type III tanks, are refuelled at 350 bars, eliminating the need to pre-cool the hydrogen at the station dispenser36.

At the base scenario, the electricity used for charging the BEB is sourced from the current Peninsular Malaysia electricity grid. For the FCEB, fossil-based hydrogen is considered as the baseline hydrogen fuel, given that approximately 95% of hydrogen in Malaysia is produced from fossil sources. This hydrogen is largely used as industrial feedstock in the local market, with steam methane reforming (SMR) being the main production method (about 68% of the supply)37. Regardless, the current landscape is expected to evolve as Malaysia seeks to expand its green hydrogen sector by advancing a range of technologies including SMR coupled with carbon capture and storage (CCS), renewable-powered water electrolysis (e.g., solar and hydropower), and biomass gasification, each offering distinct advantages and constraints38,39.

As of 2025, several hydrogen projects have been announced in Malaysia, as indicated in Fig. 3. These includes green hydrogen initiatives such as the Semarak Project, Kenyir-Kerteh Corridor, Kota Marudu Project, and the H2ornbill and H2biscus Projects, as well as blue hydrogen project in the Kerteh Industrial Complex which involves the development of a carbon capture and storage (CCS) facility to offset emissions from existing hydrogen production. Focusing on Peninsular Malaysia, the announced green hydrogen projects are primarily solar-powered, and although they are still in their nascent stages, both green and blue hydrogen are expected to contribute to the supply of hydrogen refuelling stations in Putrajaya.

Nevertheless, Malaysia’s significant biomass resources, particularly residues from the oil palm sector, as show strong promise for biomass-based hydrogen production40, such as through biomass gasification41, though large-scale projects in this area have yet to be announced. Oil palm biomass waste with potential for hydrogen production include empty fruit bunches (EFB), oil palm trunks (OPT), oil palm fronds (OPF), mesocarp fibers (MF), and palm kernel shells (PKS)42. Beyond centralized off-site hydrogen production, on-site modular production technologies are also emerging. A local start-up is promoting “plasmalysis” technology i.e. plasma-based methane pyrolysis as a pathway to produce turquoise hydrogen directly at the demand site43. This approach taps into Malaysia’s established bio-methane supply chain by enabling direct conversion to hydrogen on-site. Unlike off-site production, on-site systems eliminate the need for delivery, though storage and dispensing infrastructure remain necessary in both cases.

Materials and methods

Methodological framework and scope definition

The methodological framework of the study is presented in Fig. 4. It begins with scope definition, which sets the system boundaries and scenarios considered, followed by simulation to estimate the energy consumption of FCEBs and BEBs operating on Putrajaya’s routes. The simulation outputs serve as the primary input for both the environmental and economic assessments.

Subsequently, environmental and cost analyses are conducted in parallel. For the environmental assessment, a standard LCA is performed in compliance with ISO 14,040 and ISO 14,044, encompassing the four conventional phases: (1) goal and scope definition, which is already integrated within the overall framework; (2) life cycle inventory (LCI) analysis; (3) life cycle impact assessment (LCIA); and (4) interpretation. The LCIA results are then monetised through the valuation of environmental externalities, allowing environmental externalities to be expressed in a common monetary unit and enabling direct comparison with economic metrics.

The economic analysis evaluates the TCO of adopting both electric bus technologies over 15 years in the Putrajaya city. The TCO results are further interpreted as abatement costs, representing the economic cost associated with achieving environmental impact reductions through the deployment of alternative bus technologies. This framing enables a direct linkage between cost effectiveness and environmental performance. The final stage involves interpretation supported by sensitivity and uncertainty analyses. The study concludes with a discussion of environmental prices and abatement costs to culminate in policy insights.

Fig. 4Simplified methodological framework and the system boundary of the study.

Simplified methodological framework and the system boundary of the study.

The scope of this study includes both the fuel cycle (well-to-wheel) and the vehicle cycle (cradle-to-grave), covering the following processes: (1) electricity production; (2) hydrogen production; (3) hydrogen distribution and storage; (4) bus body production; (5) bus shipping; (6) bus operation; and (7) end-of-life treatment. Excluded from the system boundary are the construction of hydrogen production plants, power plants, refuelling or charging stations, and bus depots. This exclusion is based on the principle that expanding the environmental scope in the LCA does not necessarily enhance the quality of decision-making. However, for the cost analysis, the capital costs of constructing the refuelling and charging station are included. These stations involve significant upfront investment, making them major factors in the total cost comparison to reflect meaningful and real-world economic feasibility comparison between the two bus options.

The different scenarios tested involve the variation in energy sources including first grid decarbonization strategy by increasing RE capacity in the grid in the next 15 years (baseline being current grid mix in 2025), also the variation in hydrogen sources other than the baseline with SMR, including blue hydrogen SMR with CCS (S1), green hydrogen solar-powered PEM electrolysis (S2), biomass gasification (S3) and turquoise hydrogen plasma-based biomethane pyrolysis (S4).

Energy consumption analysis

Various factors on the road could be contributing to the variation in energy consumption, including driving pattern, number of stops and surrounding temperature. For example, a higher number of stops results in greater energy consumption of the buses44. Factors such as rolling resistance, aerodynamic drag, along with weight reduction, were identified as contributors to reduced energy consumption. Therefore, average fuel economy (as specified in Table 3) based on specification or assumption is not fully applicable for a comprehensive study tailored to Malaysian case study, instead, real-world driving scenario is applied.

An energy simulation model based on forward-facing approach was developed using MATLAB/Simulink. Speed-time profiles of each of the seven defined route are constructed based on measured GPS logger data, which was then fed into the Simulink model as the source driving cycle. The list of simulation parameters, along with two complete FCEB and BEB models, is provided in the Supplementary Material Table S11. The main outputs of the models are hydrogen consumption and electricity consumption; each normalized per distance travelled. Due to limited capacity and the scope of this paper, representative and region-specific custom driving cycles were not constructed. Without a statistically representative driving cycle for Malaysian urban buses, the results in the study cannot be generalized beyond this case study, and hence is acknowledged as a limitation of the study.

Life cycle assessment (LCA)

This section provides a detailed explanation of the Life Cycle Inventory (LCI) and Life Cycle Impact Assessment (LCIA) method, as two key steps in the LCA process45.

Life cycle inventory

Electricity source

For both BEB charging and hydrogen production via SMR (base), SMR + CCS (S1), biomass gasification (S3) and plasma-based biomethane pyrolysis (S4), grid electricity is used. The grid electricity mix is modelled based on the national installed capacity for the base year and future projections for Peninsular Malaysia (See Supplementary Material Table S1), as reported by the Energy Commision46. Malaysia has a clear commitment to increase renewable energy (RE) capacity, with a national target to raise the share of installed RE capacity from 40% in 2040 to 70% by 2050, according to Malaysia’s National Energy Transition Roadmap (NETR)47. It should be noted that increasing RE capacity in the grid mix affects only operational-phase energy use, not upstream bus manufacturing, because the buses are not manufactured locally.

Hydrogen sources

Data inventory of hydrogen sources for base scenario and S1-S4 is detailed in Table 4. Hydrogen production via SMR (base) involves mixing methane with steam and directed through an externally heated reactor operating at high temperatures to produce hydrogen and carbon monoxide. A subsequent water-gas shift reaction generates more hydrogen and carbon dioxide, after which the hydrogen is purified for use48.

In S1, SMR + CCS produces blue hydrogen using the same methane and water inputs as SMR. However, with the integration of CCS, process emissions are captured rather than released into the atmosphere, resulting in significantly lower emissions, albeit with increased electricity demand to operate the carbon capture unit49.

In S2, green hydrogen is produced via solar-powered PEM electrolysis, selected as it is already a commercialized technology. The main inputs are water and electricity. The environmental performance of electrolysis depends strongly on the electricity source; therefore, coupling the process with renewable energy such as solar power is essential to ensure its low-emission potential50.

In S3, hydrogen is produced via biomass gasification, which is also a type of green hydrogen, utilizing surplus biomass waste from oil palm plantation sites. In this controlled process, biomass is converted at high temperatures (> 700 °C) into hydrogen without combustion, achieving conversion efficiencies of up to 69%51.

In S4, hydrogen is produced via plasma-based biomethane pyrolysis, also known as turquoise hydrogen. While the use of biomethane allow the process to be considered as green hydrogen pathways, definitions remain unclear52. Regardless of the colour definition, the process is drawing attention for coproducing hydrogen and carbon black53. Plasma reactors are used for methane decomposition, with associated systems such as plasma torches designed in modular configurations.

Hydrogen distribution and storage

The produced hydrogen from each source is transported to the hydrogen refuelling station (HRS) in Putrajaya via tube trailers, with delivery distances subject to each source location. Tube trailers, which use steel or composite pressure vessels mounted on trailers, remain the most economical option for HRS with low daily demand (about 500 kg/d)58. For this study, hydrogen is delivered in high-pressure tanks at 500 bar, as pressures above 250 bar can reduce HRS costs59. The road transport energy consumption (see Supplementary Material Table S2) accounts for fuel use and pre-compression from 30 to 500 bar, which requires 2.1 kWh/kg H260.

For the designed HRS with a capacity of 500 kg H2 per day, the total electricity consumed is estimated at 0.767 kWh/kg H261, mainly supplied to the compressor, along with the dispenser unit and auxiliary systems such as control and electronic components. In comparison, higher electricity consumption ranging from 1.41 to 1.96 kWh/kg H2 has been for HRS with similar capacity (~ 550 kg/day)62. This higher demand is likely attributed to compression to 87.5 MPa for high-pressure on-site storage systems (HPSS), as well as the inclusion of a pre-cooling unit required for dispensing hydrogen at 700 bars for passenger FCEVs.

In contrast, the HRS design in this study aligns more closely with the conditions described in Eißler, et al.61, as hydrogen is only compressed up to 50 MPa for on-site storage and dispensed at 350 bar for bus refuelling. This suggests that HRSs designed for passenger vehicles tend to have higher operational energy consumption than those specifically configured for bus fleets.

Production of bus body and components

Obtaining the complete primary data for bus production was beyond the scope and resources of this study. Nonetheless, data for manufacturing of both buses by specific manufacturers is confidential and not publicly available. Hence, its data inventory is sourced from the existing literature. The basic material composition of the bus body was estimated based on weight proportions reported by Zhao, et al.23 and Wang, et al.63, scaled according to the curb weight of the bus (Table 5). As no significant differences were identified in the bus body components, a standardized configuration was used for both buses, which serves the purpose of impact quantification, with no in-depth discussion to be made on the bus body.

The main difference lies in their powertrain components: the BEB includes only a battery, while the FCEB incorporates a battery, fuel cell system, and hydrogen storage tank. Bus operation

During the bus operation phase, the main parameters influencing both environmental impact and cost are the energy consumption determined by driving fuel economy (estimated using the energy consumption model described in Sect. 3.2), the performance degradation of the battery and PEM fuel cell, which leads to increased energy demand over time, as well as their replacement. As the battery pack and PEM fuel cell stack reach the end of their lifespans, replacements are required (see Table 6). The PEM fuel cell stack reaches end-of-life when its voltage drops below 90% of the nominal value66, whereas for the battery, end-of-life is defined as its state-of-health (SoH) decline to 80% of the initial level.

For PEM fuel cells, buses powered by PEMFCs have displayed lifetimes exceeding 30,000 operating hours67, equivalent to approximately nine years of service assuming an average daily operation of nine hours. Mlakar, et al.68 reported that under dynamic conditions typical of transportation, the average fuel cell voltage degradation rate is 16 µV h⁻¹, with energy efficiency decreasing by about 0.88% per 1,000 operating hours. This can be interpreted to mean that for the same output, energy consumption increases by approximately 0.88% per 1,000 h due to efficiency loss. Considering a total 3,285 annual operating hours, the energy consumption increases by 2.89% per year.

Battery degradation arises from both calendar aging and cycle-related losses69. Reported lifespans range between five and ten years, depending on operating conditions such as temperature and charging practices. Under best practices (including liquid-cooled thermal management, moderate climate, and slow, controlled charging), annual degradation rate of battery can be limited to below 3%, yielding an estimated seven-year service life70. Over this period, degradation reduces driving range by 26% and increases energy demand by about 7% due to greater charging requirements71. In this study, the 7% increase was assumed to progress linearly, with electricity consumption rising by 1% annually, which was factored into the calculations, though relatively minor.

Life cycle impact assessment

LCIA is conducted using SimaPro v10.3 software, using the ReCiPe 2016 (hierarchist) characterization method, which covers a broad environmental profile of 18 midpoint (problem-oriented) and 3 endpoint (damage-oriented) impact categories. ReCiPe 2016 is a harmonised LCIA framework that translates emissions and resource flows into impact indicator results through its updated and scientifically robust characterization factors74. The characterization phase involves linking the life cycle inventories, as described in Sect. 3.3.1, to relevant impact categories and calculating their contributions using characterization factors75. All 18 midpoint impact categories are included in the LCIA but only six impact categories will be displayed in the results and discussed. They are selected based on normalised values’ ranking, where normalization helps to convert category results with different units into the same relative scale to support further interpretation of the study.

Environmental externalities

While the LCIA using ReCiPe 2016 characterization framework quantifies environmental cause-effect chains in physical units of the environmental burdens, the comparisons across impact categories remain challenging due to their heterogeneous units76. To address this limitation, this study links midpoint LCIA results to environmental prices, which represent the social cost of pollution in terms of economic welfare losses associated with an additional unit of environmental damage77.

Environmental externalities, in terms of environmental prices, complement the LCIA results, whereby they provide an integrated environmental-economic perspective that supports a single-score interpretation based on environmental externalities78. Unlike the environmental prices discussed in the Sect. 3.3.3, the cost analysis represents actual financial expenditures incurred by stakeholders. Potential reductions in individual cost components and their influence on the overall TCO are also evaluated over the 15-year study period. The TCOs for electric buses’ adoption is then inferred as the abatement cost needed to mitigate future negative environmental consequences arisen from current conventional public transportation practices.

Abatement cost analysis

The TCO results are further translated into “GHG abatement costs” to reflect the economic efficiency of deploying electric buses as a mitigation measure relative to conventional diesel buses. First, net GHG abatement of BEB and FCEB is calculated as the difference between the life-cycle emissions of a diesel bus—covering vehicle manufacturing, well-to-tank (WtT), and tank-to-wheel (TtW) stages—and the corresponding emissions of BEBs and FCEBs, which include vehicle manufacturing and WtT emissions only, as their TtW emissions are zero. These life-cycle GHG emissions associated to diesel bus are drawn from the Thailand LCA study by Gabriel, et al.89, while emission values for BEBs and FCEBs are derived from this work. The study by Gabriel, et al.89 covers both the diesel-bus manufacturing and operation phases. While bus manufacturing emissions are not expected to differ substantially, operational-phase emissions may differ between Thailand and Malaysia due to differences in fuel characteristics (e.g., upstream supply chains) and operating conditions (e.g., route profiles, congestion, and passenger loads). Nevertheless, the Thailand-based estimates are used as the baseline due to the lack of Malaysia-specific diesel-bus LCA data and because Thailand is a nearby country with broadly comparable regional contexts, providing a reasonable proxy.

The GHG abatement cost is then calculated by dividing the TCO with the net abated GHG emissions, expressed in $ per kgCO2−eq abated. This indicator is especially useful for informing policy decisions by assessing the cost-effectiveness of GHG mitigation measures90.

Sensitivity and uncertainty analyses

Sensitivity and uncertainty analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the life cycle and cost assessment results. Sensitivity analysis was first performed to identify key input parameters that have a significant influence on the outcomes and to examine how variations in these parameters affect the results. For the environmental assessment, the sensitivity analysis focuses on the effect of electricity generation mix, particularly grid decarbonization, on the GHG emissions of BEBs and FCEBs over the 15-year operational lifetime. This allows the evaluation of how future changes in electricity carbon intensity may alter the comparative environmental performance of the two technologies.

For the cost analysis, several influential economic parameters were varied by ± 10% to assess their impact on the minimum and maximum TCO. These parameters include the celec, CH2prod, CH2trans, CH2store, Cbat, CFC. In addition, hydrogen price evolution was modelled to reflect a learning effect, where hydrogen costs gradually decrease over the 15-year period due to technological improvements and economies of scale. These influential factors are then carried forward into the uncertainty analysis. A Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 iterations was performed to capture the combined variability of these inputs and to evaluate the robustness of the results. The outputs are presented using 95% confidence intervals to enhance the credibility and reliability of the final environmental and cost assessment results.

Results and discussion

Energy consumption and efficiency

The analysis begins with estimating energy consumption using simulations based on actual driving cycles from seven bus service lines in Putrajaya. The energy consumption metric is reported in kWh per 100 vehicle-km, instead of per 100 passenger-km, to avoid introducing ridership or load-factor assumptions that are outside the vehicle energy model91. For BEBs, electricity consumption ranges from 88.16 to 90.14 kWh/100 km, with an average of 88.9 kWh/100 km. For FCEBs, hydrogen consumption ranges from 9.034 to 10.78 kg H2/100 km, with an average of 9.51 kg H2/100 km. The literature has reported electricity consumption values of 95.5 kWh/100km92 in Athen, Greece and 130.5 kWh/100 km93 in the U.S. cities for a 12-meter BEB, both of which are higher than the value in this study (88.9 kWh/100 km). Similarly, an average hydrogen consumption of 10.25 kg H₂/100 km94 in European cities has been reported for a comparable FCEB, which is also higher than the value in this study (9.51 kg H₂/100 km). These differences are likely attributable to different route characteristics across the cities, including stop frequency, average speed, and road grade, which influence traction energy demand and auxiliary energy use.

Energy efficiency is calculated on the basis of hydrogen’s higher heating value (HHV), under which FCEBs perform significantly worse than BEBs. FCEBs require about 1350 MJ to travel 100 km, whereas BEBs require only 320 MJ for the same distance. This significant gap arises from the lower fuel cell conversion efficiency (55%) compared to the higher charging-discharging efficiency of batteries (90–95%)95.

Figure 5 presents the annualized energy consumption for bus operations. The analysis accounts for efficiency degradation over time, reflected as a 1.0% and 2.89% annual increase in energy consumption for BEBs and FCEBs, respectively, based on the degradation estimation described in Sect. 3.3.1e. Consequently, the annual energy demand increases progressively, as additional energy is required to compensate for declining system efficiency each year. Nonetheless, an obvious reduction in energy consumption for FCEBs after 2034 (9th year of operation), is observed in Fig. 5. This is a result from the replacement of the fuel cell stack upon reaching its expected lifespan, after which a new stack with restored high efficiency is installed.

At the end of 15 years, operating one FCEB in the city consume more energy, totalling 14,683 GJ in the form of fuel, compared to BEB, which consume 3,215 in the form of direct electricity, indicating that BEBs are 4.6 times more energy efficient than FCEBs. It should be noted that the values shown does not include the energy required for production of the hydrogen fuel or electricity. Subsequently, the average energy consumption per distance travelled is used in assessing the environmental and cost impacts of replacing all existing diesel buses with BEBs or FCEBs across the seven bus service lines.

Environmental impacts

In this section, the midpoint and endpoint environmental impacts of adopting BEBs or FCEBs in Putrajaya city with four alternative scenarios are presented in an aggregated format, in Figs. 6 and 7 respectively, where the assessment accounts for the entire service life of a single bus, covering both the fuel cycle and vehicle cycle. Among the 18 midpoint impact categories assessed, seven with relatively high normalized values include human carcinogenic toxicity, freshwater ecotoxicity, marine ecotoxicity, terrestrial ecotoxicity, fossil resource scarcity, and global warming (See Supplementary Material Table S13).

In Fig. 6, FCEB (S4) occupies the smallest polygon area in the radar chart, pointing to the lowest environmental impacts across the assessed categories compared to the other scenarios, including BEB. The life cycle of BEB shows the highest impacts in both freshwater and marine ecotoxicity, 7.32 × 104 and 9.82 × 104 kg 1,4-DCB-eq respectively, mainly due to the bus manufacturing phase and battery replacement. This can be attributed to the production of the 2,700 kg Li-ion battery pack on board, where the bus component itself exerts a greater burden on aquatic life and ecosystems than the use phase96. These impacts arise primarily from lithium chemical refining and upstream reagent supply (e.g., sodium hydroxide and carbonate), rather than from battery assembly97.

Fig. 6Absolute midpoint level impacts of BEB and FCEB with 4 alternative scenarios, aggregated over total operational life (15 years).

Absolute midpoint level impacts of BEB and FCEB with 4 alternative scenarios, aggregated over total operational life (15 years).

FCEB (S2), shows considerable burden on human carcinogenic toxicity (3.21 × 105 kg 1,4-DCB-eq) and terrestrial ecotoxicity (9.22 × 106 kg 1,4-DCB-eq), mainly due to the solar electricity used for hydrogen production. The installation and panel manufacturing of solar systems98 require large amounts of metals such as copper, aluminium, and silver, with significant impacts arising from their extraction, smelting, and refining99. Similarly due to hydrogen fuel production, FCEB (S3) causes the highest freshwater eutrophication impact (1.15 × 103 kg P-eq), potentially attributable to the phosphorus (or other nutrient) leakage in wash water during the wet pre-treatment of biomass waste feedstock100.

The endpoint impact assessment groups all midpoint indicators into three areas of protection: human health, ecosystem quality, and resource availability. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the highest contribution to human health damage is observed for the FCEB (S3) scenario, where oil palm biomass gasification is used as the hydrogen fuel source. This scenario records a total human health impact of 3.17 DALY, of which particulate matter emissions alone account for 49.8% of the total damage. The elevated particulate emissions are mainly associated with the gasification process, where partial oxidation and high-temperature reactions promote particle formation and fragmentation, leading to increased inhalable pollutants101.

Absolute endpoint level impacts of BEB and FCEB with 4 alternative scenarios, aggregated over total operational life (15 years).

In contrast, the BEB scenario consistently shows the lowest impact across all three endpoint categories, with values of 2.8 DALY for human health, 3.72 × 10− 3 species.yr for ecosystem and 5.30 × 104 USD2013 for resource depletion. Although BEB systems rely on electricity generation and battery materials, their overall resource depletion impact remains lower than fuel-based systems due to higher energy efficiency and the absence of continuous fuel extraction. This contributes to the lowest resource damage value among all scenarios. In contrast, fuel-cell-based options exhibit higher resource and health burdens due to upstream fuel processing, feedstock preparation, and conversion inefficiencies.

Global warming impact

This section focuses on the buses’ life-cycle global warming impact. BEB records the lowest emissions at 762 tCO2-eq (see Fig. 8(a)), while FCEB (base) reaches 2,485 tCO2-eq, the highest among all scenarios, across the vehicle cycle’s four life cycle phases: bus production, shipping, operation, and end-of-life.

Fig. 8(a) Life cycle GHG emissions divided into four phases, with specific WtT GHG emissions per 100 km shown by red dots; (b) proportional breakdown of GHG emissions during the operation phase. (Comp.: component; dist.: distribution)

(a) Life cycle GHG emissions divided into four phases, with specific WtT GHG emissions per 100 km shown by red dots; (b) proportional breakdown of GHG emissions during the operation phase. (Comp.: component; dist.: distribution)

During the bus production phase, BEB exhibits a slightly higher impact (7%) than FCEB due to the difference in their powertrain components. BEB relies on a large traction Li-ion battery, which results in a higher front-loaded manufacturing carbon footprint from material refining and cell fabrication102. By contrast, FCEB has a lower GHG burden in its powertrain, which comprises of the PEM fuel cell stack, BoP, and hydrogen tank. Interestingly, the main contribution does not arise from the fuel cell but from the hydrogen tank’s carbon fiber reinforced plastic (CFRP) shell, which involves energy-intensive processes production but projected to reduce GHG emissions over the life cycle, as their lightweight properties improve vehicle fuel efficiency and offset their initial production impacts103. The shipping phase contributes insignificantly, whereas end-of-life recycling of the bus body and recovery of precious metals from the battery and fuel cell provide credits that partially offset the production impacts. Metal recovery from both batteries and fuel cells is receiving increasing research attention and progressing toward more mature processes104,105. Finally, and unsurprisingly, the operation phase dominates, accounting for 90–97% of total global warming impacts.

The operation phase consists of maintenance activities and the fuel cycle, i.e., fuel consumption along its supply chain. As shown in Fig. 8(b), battery replacement contributes 8.4% of the total operational impacts in BEB, reflecting two replacement cycles, whereas fuel cell replacement accounts for less than 0.2% across all FCEB scenarios. For FCEBs, GHG emissions are largely governed by the hydrogen fuel cycle, particularly well-to-tank (WtT) processes, including hydrogen production, distribution, and storage.

FCEB (Base) (221.5 kg CO2-eq/100 km) and FCEB (S1) (146.9 kg CO2-eq/100 km) source hydrogen from similar routes, but the latter employs blue hydrogen with CCS, which substantially reduces emissions and shifts the delivery process to become the dominant contributor. FCEB (S2), with the lowest emissions (121.2 kg CO2-eq/100 km), uses hydrogen from PEM water electrolysis powered by solar energy. Here, the delivery stage again dominates since production is nearly emission-free. FCEB (S3) records higher emissions (186.7 CO2-eq/100 km) despite using renewable oil palm biomass gasification, with production and delivery contributing almost equally. FCEB (S4), representing on-site hydrogen production, avoids delivery impacts entirely, resulting in a global warming performance that is on par of S2 (130 kg CO2-eq/100 km).

Increasing the proportion of renewable energy (RE) in the national grid mix reduces the GHG intensity of electricity used for both charging BEBs and producing, compressing, and storing hydrogen along the supply chain. A 5% increase in RE share in the Malaysian grid mix is projected within a 15-year span. As shown in Fig. 9, the WtT GHG emissions of BEB and FCEB (S3) decrease by 18%, followed by reductions in S1 (16%), S2 and S4 (14%), and the base case (10%). No notable improvements are observed for solar-based electrolysis in S2, since it is already assumed to operate on RE. The S1 case consumes more electricity for its carbon capture operation106 while the base case relies more on natural gas resources such as methane than on electricity. Overall, grid decarbonization yields only limited benefits in improving global warming performance across all scenarios.

Cost Impacts

TCO are quantified to evaluate and justify the fleet investment decision on FCEBs and BEBs (see Table 9). The highest TCO is incurred by FCEB (S1), followed by FCEB (base), both exceeding $ 1 million, making them infeasible for investment. These are followed by FCEB (S2) and then FCEB (S3). The FCEB (S4) case records the lowest cost over 15 years among all FCEB cases, but it still does not outperform the BEB, which incurs the lowest overall cost, as both CAPEX and OPEX are significantly lower.

BEB’s absolute CAPEX is 63% lower than FCEB’s CAPEX as a result of both lower purchase price and less costly charging infrastructure in comparison. In this analysis, the entire city bus fleet investment is assumed to occur as a one-time purchase in 2026. As a result, future declines in purchase prices do not affect the present investment decision, which is instead determined by the current TCO based on today’s purchase prices. Once the buses are acquired now, they will be operated for 15 years to maximize both cost efficiency and environmental benefits. Nonetheless, over the next 15 years, no projection from the literature has shown that the purchase price of FCEBs will not fall enough to reach cost parity with BEBs107. Secondly, charging station technology is already mature, with modular installation at relatively low cost, whereas hydrogen storage and dispensing equipment remain more expensive.

Among FCEBs, the most significant cost contributor is the fuel, covering hydrogen production, transportation, and storage. Delivery via compressed gas tube trailers is the main cost driver, meaning the distance from the hydrogen source plays a key role since all cases use the same transport method. Transportation cost is, therefore, one of the bottlenecks in scaling up the hydrogen economy. The use of liquefied transport or even pipelines could significantly reduce costs108, but this option is not investigated as the development of hydrogen delivery infrastructure in Malaysia remains uncertain.

FCEB (S4) performs relatively well largely due to on-site hydrogen production, suggesting that for small-scale consumption (500 kg/day), on-site hydrogen generation is economically preferable to daily mass delivery. Transport for plasma-based biogas pyrolysis involves bio-CNG delivery, which relies on a mature and affordable network already factored into the hydrogen production cost in S4. Since electrolysis in S2 is also a flexible technology, eliminating transportation costs provides additional benefits. If transportation is excluded, the total cost decreases by up to 75%, lowering the specific TCO to $132 per 100 km, which is highly comparable to S4 at $129 per 100 km. This demonstrates that on-site production can be more cost-efficient than off-site pathways, and this is consistent with the findings of Caponi, et al.109.

Interpretation of environmental externalities and abatement costs

In this section, the environmental and economic impacts of adopting BEB and FCEB in the Putrajaya city are compared based on their environmental prices and GHG abatement costs, respectively. The environmental prices represents the monetized environmental externalities, aggregating all midpoint impact categories, including but not limited to climate change, ozone depletion, ecotoxicity and acidification79. The monetized environmnental externalities presented in the analysis should not be interpreted as actual economic losses; rather, they reflect the estimated social costs of pollution77. By expressing diverse environmental damages in a common monetary unit, the results enable comparison across impact types on a consistent scale and facilitate single-score comparisons between BEB and FCEB scenarios78.

Among all evaluated cases, the environmental price increases in the following order: BEB < FCEB (S4) < FCEB(S1) < FCEB (Base) < FCEB (S2) < FCEB(S3) (Table 10). Although FCEB (S2) achieves the lowest GHG emissions among all FCEB scenarios (See Sect. 4.2.1), its overall environmental price is the second highest when all the environmental impact are considered simultaneously. This outcome is mainly attributed to its high contributions from terrestrial ecotoxicity ($5,015.76), land use ($5,361.83), and mineral extraction ($99.49), which are the highest among all assessed scenarios (Supplementary Material Table S26). These results indicate that reductions in climate-related emissions alone do not necessarily translate into lower overall environmental burdens when other midpoint impacts are taken into account.

Based on the aggregated environmental price indicator, FCEB (S4) emerges as the most environmentally favourable option among the FCEB scenarios. Nevertheless, BEB still demonstrates the best overall environmental performance, indicating a more balanced environmental profile across multiple impact categories. This suggests that BEBs provide broader environmental advantages beyond climate mitigation alone.

Over a 15-year period, the net GHG abatement from replacing diesel buses with BEBs exceeds that of FCEB (S2) and FCEB (S4) by 46% and 53%, respectively. Net abatement accounts for the potential burden shift from bus operation to vehicle manufacturing and fuel supply chains to prevent mere emission shifts instead of emission cutting112. To better capture the trade-off between environmental performance and cost, the concept of GHG abatement cost is applied, treating all investments in the decision of bus electrification as the cost of avoiding emissions.

Figure 11(b) shows the unit abatement cost in descending order: FCEB (S2) > FCEB (S4) > BEB, with values of 7.44, 2.01, and 0.39 $ per kg CO2, respectively. Despite achieving the lowest carbon footprint among FCEB scenarios, FCEB (S2)’s abatement cost is about one order of magnitude higher than that of BEB, rendering this pathway not economically viable to be considered as one of the climate mitigation measures in Putrajaya, Malaysia.

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations related to its scope, assumptions, and scenario design, which should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the electrification analysis focuses only on stop-and-go bus operations; therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to intercity or long-distance bus services that operate under different driving patterns and energy demands. Second, the case study assumes that the entire diesel bus fleet of the NadiPutra line in the Putrajaya city is replaced at once in order to quantify the potential GHG abatement benefits, which may not reflect a realistic phased transition strategy. In addition, the relatively small fleet size (n = 17) does not require the modelling or optimization of charging or hydrogen refuelling infrastructure; however, if the fleet were scaled up, infrastructure network and planning constraints could significantly influence both costs and operational performance. On top of the planning issues, at a higher demand of the hydrogen fuel, the logistics of hydrogen fuel may also shift to the more cost-optimal delivery options as it exhibits strong economies-of-scale characteristics. For example, liquid hydrogen delivery costs decline when supplied from larger liquefaction and terminal facilities113 and pipelines tend to become the more cost-effective transport option when volumes are large114.

Furthermore, the energy consumption simulation considers only actual city road conditions and excludes several external factors that may affect real-world energy use. These include idling behaviour, variations in passenger load, and additional heating, ventilation, and air-conditioning (HVAC) demand caused by ambient temperature changes, all of which are treated as constant in this study. Future projections also do not account for potential improvement in hydrogen delivery systems, such as pipeline-based distribution or emerging solid-state hydrogen storage and transport technologies, which could improve efficiency and reduce costs115. Similarly, the possibility of repurposing end-of-life electric vehicle batteries for second-life applications, such as stationary battery energy storage systems116, is not incorporated, even though such reuse could further enhance overall economic and environmental performance.

In addition, on-site solar-powered water electrolysis for hydrogen production is not considered in the system boundary. The analysis also excludes any additional economic value that could arise from carbon black, a potential co-product of plasma-based electrolysis, because its commercial market remains underdeveloped and uncertain. Moreover, no scenario analysis is performed to examine how policy instruments such as subsidies, carbon pricing, or fiscal incentives might improve cost-effectiveness, as the assessment assumes the absence of government intervention. Finally, the study does not account for additional costs associated with unexpected system failures or unplanned maintenance, which could affect long-term operational reliability and financial outcomes.

Policy Implication

GHG abatement demands enormous green finance117; hence assessments like the one conducted in this study are intended to provide insights to prevent misaligned investments with low returns in emission reduction. Given the more cost-efficient GHG abatement costs of BEBs, the government should prioritize BEB fleets when deciding on the electrification of the urban bus network. FCEBs are not only more GHG-intensive during operation across all hydrogen production pathways, but they are also more expensive; hence at present, they are not encouraged to be considered by the government as an initial measure for urban decarbonization. Given the availability of a more effective BEB pathway, climate-benefit arguments alone may be insufficient to support prioritizing FCEB deployment. FCEBs should be reconsidered at a later stage (potentially after one BEB investment cycle), provided that clean hydrogen can be supplied reliably at a competitive cost and the TCO reach parity with BEBs.

Nonetheless, it remains reasonable if the government is aiming for a broader goal, such as using fuel cell bus deployments in certain populated cities to help kick-start and stimulate a local hydrogen economy. For example, the Hydrogen Economy and Technology Roadmap (HETR) envision Malaysia in leading the ASEAN region in the development of a robust hydrogen ecosystem. Since FCEBs are publicly operated, their consistent usage of hydrogen infrastructure guarantees demand, potentially leading to cumulative deployment of hydrogen technologies. As hydrogen fuel prices become more competitive, this could drive higher demand for fuel cell technologies in the market, creating a virtuous cycle. This warrants further investigation into the viability of the system dynamics as a whole to address the “chicken-and-egg” dilemma118 between hydrogen infrastructure availability and fuel cell technology adoption. Based on the study’s findings, the flagged cost components that require direct government subsidies support119 include the purchasing and hydrogen infrastructure, specifically refuelling station construction and delivery methods. In particular, capital support would need to cover the upfront hydrogen refuelling station construction and the recurring logistics costs tied to the hydrogen delivery mode. Without offsetting these two cost blocks, the unit hydrogen costs and the TCO of FCEB remain structurally high, which limits its competitiveness.

Although this study focuses on Malaysia, the analytical framework can be applied to other ASEAN countries with similar urban bus operations and data availability. By updating country-specific inputs, including national grid mix, electricity prices, prospective hydrogen supply pathways and delivered costs, charging/refuelling infrastructure costs, taxes, local route profiles, the same calculations can estimate and set the baseline of life-cycle GHG emissions, total cost of ownership, and abatement costs for BEBs and FCEBs. This supports evidence-based planning and cross-city comparisons of bus electrification pathways across emerging Southeast Asian cities.

Conclusion

The study concludes that BEBs are currently the more viable option for urban bus electrification in Malaysia cities, outperforming FCEBs and all relevant alternative scenario in terms of energy efficiency, environmental sustainability, and cost. From an energy standpoint, FCEBs are markedly inefficient, requiring 4.2 times more energy than BEBs to travel 100 km in urban conditions, largely due to the lower efficiency of electricity-to-hydrogen conversion and gradual performance degradation. In environmental terms, FCEBs using on-site hydrogen production through plasma-based biogas pyrolysis achieve relatively low midpoint impact when compared to other alternative FCEBs scenarios. When focusing on global warming impact, BEBs achieve the lowest life-cycle GHG emissions. Even with Peninsular Malaysia’s fossil-heavy electricity grid (26% renewable share), BEBs emit only 58.1 kg CO2 per 100 km during operation, which shows their climate advantage. On top of that, BEB also consistently exhibits lowest overall environmental damages across the endpoint impacts categories, which represent real-world outcome derived from midpoint indicator through established cause-effect pathways.

Economic assessment further strengthens the case for BEBs. Their TCO stands at $8.9 million, making them the least expensive option to be deployed as the Putrajaya electric bus fleets when accounting for their purchase and operation stages. In contrast, FCEBs reach a best-case TCO of $21 million, and neither fossil-based, renewable-based, nor biomass-based hydrogen pathways—nor projected cost reductions—achieve cost parity with BEBs within a 15-year horizon. The economic gap is driven not only by higher purchase costs but more significantly by recurring operational expenses related to hydrogen delivery. This stage, along with broader operation-related GHG emissions, remains the most critical barrier for FCEB adoption. Building a sufficient hydrogen supply chain would therefore be essential before large-scale deployment can be considered. A key limitation is that the results are case-study specific and conditional on the assumed fleet size and infrastructure arrangement. As such, the findings should be interpreted as scenario-based analysis rather than generalisable results in all settings.

Overall, the findings highlight greater environmental performance and cost-effectiveness of BEBs, with the lowest environmental externalities cost of $831,587 and GHG abatement cost 0.39 $ per kg CO2. However, adopting FCEBs for Putrajaya’s public bus fleet could serve as a strategic pilot to catalyse a wider hydrogen ecosystem, which warrants further study. It is also recommended for future work to build on this study’s analytical framework and expand city-specific driving-cycle simulations to more Malaysian cities, use system dynamics to test long-term scale-up impacts, and assess whether fuel-cell vehicles are better suited to long-haul or longer-range public transport.
