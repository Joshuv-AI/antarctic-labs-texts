Anatomical insights: meningo-orbital foramen and its clinical relevance
Authors: Karuna Hanmant Katti, Naila Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstract:ObjectivesThe meningo-orbital foramen (MOF) is an inconsistently present bony canal that connects the orbit with the cranial cavity, commonly the middle cranial fossa. It may transmit the meningolacrimal artery supplying orbital contents, making it clinically relevant in procedures involving the lateral orbital wall. This study aimed to investigate the morphometry and anatomical positioning of the MOF and discuss its surgical implications.Methods and analysisA total of 127 adult dry human skulls (254 orbits) were examined. Gender was estimated morphometrically, while ethnicity was unknown. The incidence, laterality and connection patterns of the MOF were evaluated. Additionally, the distance between the MOF and the frontozygomatic suture (FZS) was measured.ResultsPatent MOF was identified in 51.2% of skulls (65/127), with bilateral presence in 41.5% of those. By orbit count, the incidence was 36.2%. Accessory MOFs were found in 3% of orbits, including seven cases with two and one case with three foramina. Nearly half of the MOFs were located in the greater wing of the sphenoid, and over 95% communicated with the middle cranial fossa. The mean MOF-FZS distance on the left side was significantly greater in males (p=0.02), while the right side showed no significant gender difference.ConclusionMOF has high surgical relevance due to its frequency and association with critical vascular structures. Precise morphometric knowledge can help minimise intraoperative risk. Further cadaveric investigation is suggested to investigate the course of the meningolacrimal artery and improve surgical outcomes.Keywords: Orbit, Anatomy, Treatment Surgery, Medical Education, Embryology and development

Introduction

The pyramidal-shaped bony orbital cavity houses and protects the eyeball. Several foramina connect the orbit to the cranial cavity, transmitting nerves and vessels supplying orbital structures. While foramina such as the optic canal, superior orbital fissure, inferior orbital fissure and ethmoidal foramina are well documented, the meningo-orbital foramen (MOF) also known as the orbitomeningeal foramen, cranio-orbital foramen, sinus canal foramen, foramen of Hyrtl or lacrimal foramen1 remains a variable structure (in this study, the foramen will be referred as MOF). The earliest definitive description of the MOF as a distinct anatomical entity appears in research published by Diamond.2

The MOF establishes a connection between the orbit and the middle (occasionally the anterior) cranial fossa. Its incidence varies widely, reportedly ranging from 6% to 82.5%.3,6 When patent, the MOF typically transmits the meningolacrimal branch of the middle meningeal artery, which anastomoses with branches of the lacrimal artery, serving as an accessory blood supply to orbital structures. However, sometimes it may replace the lacrimal artery and become the main source of blood supply to the lateral orbital contents, posing a significant concern for surgeons operating in this area.3 7 8 Although authors could not find reported frequency in the literature indicating how often the meningolacrimal branch becomes the main supply to the lacrimal gland, common ischaemic postoperative complications following lateral wall orbital surgery include unexpected haemorrhage, visual disturbances, diplopia, orbital dystopia, enophthalmos and facial asymmetry.1 Furthermore, these communicating vessels, apart from supplying the periorbital region, can act as feeder vessels for meningiomas, schwannomas and haemangiomas, particularly important for neurosurgeons.3

A thorough understanding of the normal and variant anatomy of the orbit is integral to successful procedures or surgeries in this complex anatomical region rich in neurovascular structures. Recognising the potential presence of the meningolacrimal artery in patent MOFs—which can be the primary source of blood supply to the orbit—and knowing where to locate it is critical to avoiding intraoperative haemorrhage and postoperative orbital ischaemia.8,10 Additionally, accurate knowledge of the MOF’s location relative to the palpable frontozygomatic suture (FZS) is essential for surgical navigation in this region.11

Material and methods

A total of 127 adult dry skulls (254 orbits) were examined for the presence of MOFs. For this study, only a patent foramen opening in the bony orbit lateral to the superior orbital fissure, communicating with the middle or anterior cranial fossa, was considered as MOF.

The sex of the skulls was estimated independently by two trained anatomists (authors KHK and NA) based on craniofacial morphology traits,12 including the mastoid process, supraorbital ridge, superciliary arches and zygomatic prominence as well as the size and architecture of each skull. Although both anatomists strictly adhered to the criteria proposed by Walker,13 the non-quantitative nature of these measurements may introduce limitations.

The incidence of MOFs was recorded bilaterally along with variations in location and communication. The MOF nearest the superior orbital fissure was classified as the primary MOF, while more lateral foramina were categorised as accessory MOFs. Patency was assessed using a fine metal probe (<1 mm in diameter). The distance of the MOF from the FZS at the lateral orbital margin was measured (figure 1) to a resolution of 0.01 mm using digital sliding callipers (Duratool, model D03196).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.29, with frequency distribution, descriptive statistics, correlation and linear regression tests performed. A random selection generator was used to select 15 skull specimens from the sample to analyse intraobserver and interobserver error. Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient test was performed to measure the correlation between measurements, which was then evaluated using Pearson values to determine its level of agreement.

Results

Prevalence of MOF

Patent MOF was identified in 51.2% (65/127) of examined skulls and 36.2% (92/254) of orbits. Among the 65 skulls exhibiting MOFs: 27 (41.5%) displayed bilateral MOFs; 20 (30.8%) exhibited left-sided MOFs only; 18 (27.7%) exhibited right-sided MOFs only. Thus, no significant lateral preference was observed (table 1).

Occurrence of accessory MOFs

Accessory MOFs were observed in eight skulls (8.7%). Notably, a single left-sided accessory MOF was identified in male skulls (table 1).

Anatomical location of MOFs

Over half of the MOFs were situated within the greater wing of the sphenoid bone. The remaining MOFs were located either at the frontosphenoidal suture or within the frontal bone. In female skulls, right-sided MOFs were more evenly distributed across these three anatomical sites (table 2).

Communication with cranial fossae

96.7% MOFs (97.9% left and 95.6% right) were communicated with the middle cranial fossa and were classified as M-type. The slightly reduced overall percentage (<100%) was attributed to variations seen in female skulls as all MOFs in male skulls communicated exclusively with the middle cranial fossa. All MOFs located in the greater wing of sphenoid were of M-type. Only MOFs located in the frontal bone and right frontosphenoidal suture in females displayed communication with the anterior cranial fossa and were classified as A-type (table 2).

MOF to FZS distance analysis

The overall mean distance between the MOF and the FZS was 25.1±3.7 mm, with left MOF-FZS measuring 25.4±3.6 mm and right MOF-FZS at 24.9±3.8 mm. Accessory MOFs, when present, were consistently located more laterally than primary MOFs (table 2).

Sex-based comparison revealed a significantly greater MOF-FZS distance on the left side in males than females (26.5 mm vs 24.1 mm, p=0.02). No significant difference was found on the right side (25.1 mm in males vs 24.7 mm in females, p=0.6).

Additionally, MOFs located within the frontal bone were associated with a significantly shorter MOF-FZS distance compared with those in the greater wing of the sphenoid (p=0.009).

Discussion

The incidence of MOF demonstrates substantial variability across studies, ranging from 25% to 71.3% in orbital assessments and 42.6% to 98.7% in cranial evaluations. The highest reported rates—71.3% in orbits and 98.7% in skulls—were noted in a Turkish study by Servet et al.1 Turkish and Indian populations exhibited higher incidence rates as seen in table 1. In the present study, MOF was identified in 51.2% of skulls and 36.2% of orbits, either unilaterally or bilaterally. This cranial incidence is slightly higher compared with 42.6%,3 43.3%,14 43.5%,10 46.3%20 but slightly lower than 59.5%19 and 60%.18 These differences are likely attributable to variations in geoethnicity, sample size and the inclusion of all MOFs irrespective of patency.

No statistically significant sex-based difference was observed in the current study, with MOFs present in 53.3% of males and 46.7% of females. This is consistent with findings from Macchi et al,19 who reported 42.6% incidence in males and 41.1% in females, indicating no marked gender predilection. By contrast, Kwiatkowski et al10 found a significantly higher prevalence in female skulls (40.5%) compared with male skulls (14%), although their study was limited by a small sample size (n=46) compared with those of the present study (n=127) and Macchi et al (n=920).

Regarding MOF communication and the distance from the FZS, the present study observed that over 96% of MOFs were M-type, a finding likely influenced by the inclusion of only patent foramina. Macchi et al19 reported 72.2% M-type MOFs with a mean MOF-FZS distance of 24.8 mm (SD=3.9), while Mahajan et al2 documented 77% M-type with a mean MOF-FZS distance of 24.9 mm (SD=3.4). The current study yielded a slightly greater mean distance of 25.1 mm (SD=3.7). A statistically significant gender difference in MOF-FZS distance was observed on the left side, with males exhibiting a mean of 26.5 mm compared with 24.1 mm in females (p=0.02).

As discussed earlier, knowledge of the presence of MOF and its distance from FZS is crucial during surgical procedures to avoid intraoperative and postoperative complications due to injury to the meningolacrimal artery. Understanding the development of arch arteries, particularly the second arch artery, could explain the variant presence of the meningolacrimal artery. During embryonic development, six arterial arches appear at the cranial end of the embryo. The second arch artery, known as the hyoid artery, elongates laterally and passes through the crus of the stapes, hence being termed as the stapedial artery. The stapedial artery divides into two branches: the supraorbital artery (SOA) and the intracranial part of the middle meningeal artery (MMA). The SOA is annexed by the primitive ophthalmic artery, following which the transsphenoidal part of the SOA regresses.22 These annexations and regressions occur in 20–24 mm embryos during Paget stage VI.23

As seen in figure 2, the failure of this regression can give rise to a persistent communicating branch between the middle meningeal and lacrimal arteries. This communicating branch known as meningolacrimal artery may completely replace the lacrimal artery if annexation with a primitive ophthalmic artery has failed. If both primitive ophthalmic arteries regress, an aberrant origin of the ophthalmic artery from the MMA may enter the orbit through the superior orbital fissure or MOF.16

If the meningolacrimal artery is the primary supply to the lateral orbital contents, cauterisation could lead to ischaemia of the lacrimal gland, resulting in dry eye syndrome or reduced tear production. The lateral orbital wall may suffer from ischaemic bone changes, especially if collateral supply is insufficient. This could lead to osteonecrosis, delayed healing after surgery or increased susceptibility to infection.

The region is close to branches of cranial nerves III, IV, V1 and VI. Thermal injury or ischaemia could result in diplopia, ptosis or sensory deficits in the forehead and upper eyelid. Inadvertent damage during cauterisation could cause bleeding, haematoma formation or orbital compartment syndrome. MOF is a key site for extracranial–intracranial anastomoses. Cauterising vessels here may disrupt compensatory blood flow routes, increasing risk during future embolisation or surgery.24 Thus, knowing the distance of MOF from palpable bony landmark like FZS can help surgeons locate any aberrant or anomalous vessels and prevent injury to them.

Knowledge of the variable presence of MOF is also significant for radiologists and other diagnostic imaging professionals. If there is a strong clinical suspicion of an intraocular foreign body, a CT scan might be the investigation of choice. MOF can be misdiagnosed as an intraocular foreign body on CT scans and plain radiographs, since it is likely to be seen as a radiopaque area with central radiolucency. However, a foreign body is usually uniformly radiopaque. In the case of MOF, the radiopaque area remains stationary with changes in head positions, while intraocular foreign bodies would tend to move.25 It is easier for the imaging team to differentiate MOF from an intraocular foreign body if they are aware of the distance of MOF from FZS, allowing them to verify if it is MOF or a foreign body by changing the head position.

Limitations

Limitations of this study include the unknown ethnicity of the dry skull collection, precluding comparison of incidence in different population groups. Additionally, the gender of the skulls was estimated by morphological method, which may not be 100% accurate. A more precise method such as DNA analysis may enhance the accuracy of gender determination.

Another limitation is the potential variability in the classification of patent versus non-patent MOFs across different studies, which may affect the comparability of incidence rates. Furthermore, the sample size of this study, while adequate, may still not capture the full spectrum of variability in MOF incidence and characteristics. Using dry skulls prevents visualisation of associated soft tissue structures, particularly the meningolacrimal artery. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples including cadaveric and/or imaging studies would help to better understand the course of meningolacrimal artery and clinical significance of MOFs.

Conclusion

A patent MOF is a variably present bony canal connecting the orbit with the cranial cavity, typically located in the greater wing of the sphenoid, lateral to the superior orbital fissure. In this study, its incidence rate was over 50%, with approximately 95% of MOFs being M-types, connecting with the middle cranial fossa. Persistence of the transsphenoidal segment of the SOA likely explains the patency of the MOF in some individuals and can be a major source of haemorrhage during lateral wall orbital surgery, anterior skull base reconstruction surgery, pterion flap surgery and in a transorbital approach to the cavernous sinus. It can also serve as a pathway for communication between intracranial and extracranial vessels.

Given its surgical significance, understanding the morphometry of this common foramen and the structures passing through it is crucial for ophthalmologists, neurosurgeons and radiologists. In this study, the MOF was located at a mean distance of 25.1 mm from the FZS, with a significantly greater distance on the left side in males compared with females. As the FZS can be used as a navigational bony landmark to locate the MOF and vessels passing through it during orbital procedures, this knowledge will be a valuable tool for surgical planning and avoiding complications.

Additionally, recognising the potential presence and anatomical variations of the MOF is essential for accurate diagnostic imaging as misinterpretation of the MOF as an intraocular foreign body can lead to unnecessary interventions.

Future research should focus on larger and more diverse samples to further explore the incidence and clinical significance of MOFs across different populations. Advanced radiological and cadaveric studies may provide deeper insights into the vascular patterns associated with the MOF, ultimately enhancing surgical outcomes and patient safety.
