Use of 2 Ventricular Assist Devices as a Total Artificial Heart for Heart Failure

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImplantation of two HeartMate 3 devices in a total artificial heart configuration, known as the HeartMate 6 (HM6) approach, can be used to manage severe biventricular heart failure in a patient who is not eligible for transplantation.Case SummaryWe report a case in which a young patient presented with heart failure of unknown etiology. History of Presentation

A 29-year-old woman presented to a tertiary academic center with 1 week of chest pressure, shortness of breath, and lower extremity edema. She was initially tachycardic (124 beats/min), with a blood pressure of 103/74 mm Hg. However, shortly after presenting, she developed hemodynamically unstable ventricular tachycardia (VT).

Medical History

Her medical history included a prior presentation to another hospital for shortness of breath, where she was diagnosed with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. She was also diagnosed with myasthenia gravis; she had an anterior mediastinal mass and diplopia.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential included giant cell myocarditis, sarcoidosis, and seronegative myasthenia gravis.

Investigations

The patient had a leukocytosis, elevated pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (28,600 pg/nL), elevated aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase (168/144), and elevated lactate (3.1 mmol/dL). Computed tomography angiography revealed a pleural effusion and a decrease in the previously seen mediastinal mass, consistent with residual thymus.

Initial Management and Hospital Course

The patient was cardioverted and started on heparin, amiodarone, and dobutamine. After transfer to the intensive care unit, she had recurrent pulseless VT. She was intubated and placed on peripheral venoarterial extracorporeal mechanical circulation for management of VT storm. Next, she was taken to the operating room for left heart catheterization and Impella CP (Abiomed) placement for left ventricular (LV) venting, which was later exchanged for an Impella 5.5. Echocardiography showed right ventricular (RV) thrombus (Figure 1).

On hospital day 2, she developed multiple cranial nerve abnormalities. She was started on steroids and intravenous immunoglobulin. Heart transplant evaluation was initiated, but work-up with right heart catheterization or biopsy were contraindicated owing to RV thrombus.

The patient's course was complicated further by oropharyngeal bleeding requiring blood transfusions. Her initial transplant status was 1, but she had sensitization after transfusions, with a flow crossmatch calculated panel-reactive antibody (cPRA) of 99.45%. Twelve days after presentation, she developed hemiparesis due to a frontal intraparenchymal and subarachnoid hemorrhage. Because of the stroke and sensitization, she was delisted for transplant. Given these complications and after multidisciplinary discussions, we proceeded with placement of two HeartMate 3 ventricular assist devices (VADs) (Abbott) in a total artificial heart (TAH) configuration.

Operation

A sternotomy was performed, followed by thymectomy. The patient was weaned off extracorporeal mechanical circulation and Impella, as she was transitioned to cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Bicaval cannulation was performed, aortic cross-clamp was applied, and cardioplegia was administered. Left and right ventriculectomies were performed using electrocautery, starting from the inferior regions of each ventricle. A 3- to 4-inch rim of ventricular tissue was left along each ventricle. Both the RV and LV outflow tracts were resected with electrocautery. Cuffs were created for the LV and RV. The left atrial (LA) appendage and coronary sinus were each ligated. Felt was sutured in place circumferentially around the inner surface of the LV as well as 270° around the external surface, sparing the septum1,2 (Figure 2). VAD rings were attached to the ventricles with horizontal mattress sutures. Each VAD was inserted into its respective ring. The left ventricular assist device (LVAD) and right ventricular assist device (RVAD) outflow grafts were connected to the aorta and pulmonary artery, respectively, in an end-to-end configuration (Figure 3). The LVAD was de-aired, and the cross-clamp was removed. To wean from CPB, the LVAD flow was increased first, followed by RVAD flow, until the patient was fully supported by the VADs. Transesophageal echocardiography was used to assess atrial septum deviation and to optimize VAD flows. A purse-string suture was placed at the dome of the LA, and an LA pressure line was inserted through the abdominal fascia into the mediastinum and the LA.

Postoperative Course

Initial LVAD settings were 4.8 L/min, 5,200 rpm, 3.6 W, and pulsatility index 1.4. The RVAD was at 4.0 L/min, 4,200 rpm, 2.6 W, and pulsatility index 1.8. The patient was extubated on postoperative day 5. Pathology revealed myocardium with extensive fibrosis and chronic inflammatory cells, consistent with chronic active myocarditis, as well as benign thymic tissue.

The patient had several postoperative complications. On postoperative day 9, she had a common femoral vein thrombosis and subsequently had an inferior vena cava filter placed. On postoperative day 20, she had hematemesis and was reintubated; an esophagogastroduodenoscopy showed a gastric ulcer requiring embolization. On postoperative day 21, she was diagnosed with pulmonary embolus associated with RVAD thrombus, which was managed with tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and increasing RVAD flows.

Regarding this patient's allosensitization, she started desensitization therapy with daratumumab, belatacept, and steroids at 6 weeks postoperatively. After desensitization, her cPRA was 75%, and she was relisted.

Outcome

The patient underwent successful HM6 explantation and heart-kidney transplant approximately 6 months after the HM6 implantation. She was discharged home at 6 weeks postoperatively.

Discussion

TAH devices are used as a bridge to transplant or as destination therapy for patients who are not transplant candidates. The only TAH device approved for bridge to transplantation in the U.S. is SynCardia (SynCardia Systems).3 A limitation of this device is that it is not commonly used at many centers, despite being approved by the U.S. Food & Drug Administration. One alternative is the HM6 approach, which refers to the implantation of two HeartMate 3 VADs in a TAH configuration.4 The first reported HM6 implantation was in Germany in 2011,1 and it was subsequently studied and used in the U.S.2,4,5

We describe a patient who benefitted from HM6 implantation. This technique was chosen because the patient met the criteria for implantation, other TAH devices were not familiar to our staff, and the HM6 size was favorable. Although SynCardia could have been an option, the HM6 approach was optimal for this patient at our institution. Other centers that are not familiar with conventional TAH devices may also find this technique feasible. There is also a theoretical durability benefit with HeartMate 3 devices owing to their magnetic levitated technology (as compared with pneumatic pump devices).6

Intraoperative Considerations

The operative technique of HM6 implantation most commonly involves bilateral ventriculectomy and sewing the HeartMate 3 devices onto bilateral atria. The intraoperative steps have been described.4,5 An alternative configuration is a transatrial RVAD (as in our case) but with a standard LVAD placement (without left ventriculectomy). This configuration requires sufficient space in the chest.

Weaning off CPB and onto VAD support requires careful attention. We often use interventricular septum deviation on transesophageal echocardiography to guide volume management and VAD speed adjustment to transition to VAD support. However, after bilateral ventriculectomy, the interventricular septum cannot be used. We used atrial septum deviation as a surrogate to come off bypass. Once on VAD support, we increased speeds to achieve appropriate flows. We did not perform pulmonary artery banding, as we achieved adequate flows and planned for early anticoagulation. We also chose not to place a tissue expander at the end of the procedure, though this has been described as a technique to facilitate room for a future heart transplant.4 The right atrium–to–LV apex distance as determined by preoperative computed tomography was used to estimate the space needed in the chest to make this decision.

Because there is little research describing intraoperative anesthetics in HM6 implantations, current techniques draw from placement of LVADs and conventional TAHs.7,8 As with TAH recipients, recipients of HM6 implantations are critically ill, often with multiorgan failure and requiring hemodynamic support, which should be considered during induction and maintenance of general anesthesia. Transesophageal echocardiography is performed before and after CPB, with emphasis on looking for compression of the vena cava (inferior more than superior) and pulmonary veins (left more than right).9

Perioperative Management

Balancing LV and RV outputs is an important consideration for TAH devices because the LV is consistently higher than the RV. A lack of balance can result in significant atrial hypertension.10 Placing bilateral VADs in a TAH configuration requires management of each VAD to achieve this balance. For postoperative management of VAD speeds (and therefore flows), we used an LA dome catheter placed intraoperatively and central venous pressure to guide changes. The LA dome catheter was useful for titrating flows, given that a swan catheter was prohibited by the VADs. LVAD flows were higher than RVAD flows as we came off bypass, and they remained around 1 L higher postoperatively, which is an interesting consideration for future TAH development. This LA dome catheter was removed postoperatively without complication.

Some notable complications in our patient were thrombotic in nature. The patient had a pulmonary embolism postoperatively despite an inferior vena cava filter, which likely resulted from an internal jugular dialysis catheter. In patients with inferior vena cava filters, large cannulas in the jugular veins should be avoided when possible. She also had an RVAD thrombus postoperatively. This was resolved by tPA administration and increasing RVAD speeds, which resulted in lysing the thrombus. This reflects management of an HM6 complication in a similar manner to that of an RVAD patient.

Conclusions

HM6 implantation is an option for managing a patient with severe biventricular heart failure who is not a candidate for heart transplantation. We have described a young patient presenting with a refractory arrhythmia who was listed for transplant but required delisting because of a stroke and allosensitization. Conventional TAH devices were unavailable at our institution, so an HM6 implantation was performed. Specific considerations for intraoperative and postoperative care include strategies for weaning from CPB, management of bleeding and thrombosis postoperatively, and adjustment of VAD speeds and flows.
