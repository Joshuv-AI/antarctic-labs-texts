Disruption of Reelin signaling in a dual-hit mouse model of schizophrenia: impact of postnatal Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol exposure in a maternal immune activation model
Authors: Celia Martín-Cuevas, Víctor Darío Ramos-Herrero, Álvaro Flores-Martínez, Irene González-Recio, María Luz Martínez-Chantar, Juan Carlos Leza, J Javier Meana, Benedicto Crespo-Facorro, Ana C Sánchez-Hidalgo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince the discovery of the first antipsychotic, pharmacological treatment of schizophrenia (SCZ) has primarily relied on dopamine D2 receptor antagonist. However, variability in treatment responses highlights the need for novel biomarkers and therapeutics targets. Here, we developed a dual-hit mouse model combining maternal immune activation (MIA) induced by polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (Poly(I:C)) with adolescent exposure to Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the main psychoactive component of cannabis. We evaluated the face validity of this model and investigated potential alterations in the Reelin signaling pathway. Behaviorally, the dual-hit condition induced alterations across multiple domains, including social deficits and increased repetitive behavior, consistent with schizophrenia-relevant phenotypes. These effects were particularly evident in males, supporting a domain-specific interaction between prenatal immune activation and adolescent THC exposure rather than a uniform synergistic effect. Structurally, THC exposure was associated with reduced cortical thickness, while dual-hit mice showed decreased dendritic spine density in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus. At the molecular level, Reelin pathway alterations were observed in a sex-dependent manner. In males, reduced Reelin levels were most prominent following THC exposure alone, whereas in females, a general reduction was observed across treatment groups. Additionally, a decrease in the number of Reelin-positive cells was detected. These findings indicate that adolescent THC exposure is a major driver of Reelin alterations, with prenatal immune activation potentially modulating this effect. Overall, this model recapitulates selective SCZ-relevant phenotypes and highlights the complex, domain-specific interaction between environmental risk factors. Our results support a role for Reelin signaling alterations in SCZ-related processes, while emphasizing that their contribution depends on the nature and timing of environmental insults. Introduction

Since the discovery of the first antipsychotic, chlorpromazine, schizophrenia (SCZ) treatment has primarily targeted D2 dopamine receptors [1]. However, patient responses vary widely; about 60% show significant improvement, while 30% remain resistant to standard therapies [2, 3]. SCZ exhibits earlier onset, higher incidence and severity in men, indicating sex differences [4–7]. This variability highlights the need to better understand the biological mechanisms underlying SCZ to develop more effective, targeted treatments. Importantly, while current D2 receptor–based therapies are relatively effective in alleviating positive symptoms, they have limited impact on negative symptoms and cognitive deficits, which are major determinants of functional outcome and treatment resistance [8, 9]. In this context, alternative mechanisms such as Reelin signaling, which is critically involved in synaptic plasticity and cortical circuit organization, may provide a more direct link to these domains and represent a promising avenue for therapeutic development.

SCZ is a chronic, severe psychiatric disorder with heterogeneous symptoms categorized as positive, negative, and cognitive [8–14]. Both genetic and environmental factors especially during prenatal stages, contribute to its etiology [15, 16]. This has led to the neurodevelopmental hypothesis, which posits that prenatal and perinatal disturbances, genetic or environmental, interact with later factors to trigger disease onset during development [17].

Animal models enable controlled study of SCZ alterations, behavioral phenotypes, and therapeutic testing [7, 18]. Most current models include only one type of alteration (genetic or environmental) and fail to reproduce the full spectrum of symptoms seen in the disorder. Therefore, combining multiple risk factors may more accurately replicate the clinical presentation of SCZ and further validate the hypothesis of neurodevelopmental disturbances. In light of this, the dual-hit hypothesis of SCZ has been proposed [19–22]. This hypothesis suggests that a prenatal insult, such as an immune challenge during pregnancy leading to maternal immune activation (MIA), predisposes the individual to the development of SCZ [23–25], and a second postnatal insult further increases the risk, exceeding the threshold for the onset of the disorder [16, 26–29].

Importantly, although the dual-hit hypothesis proposes that a second insult may exacerbate vulnerability and contribute to surpassing the threshold for disease onset, accumulating evidence indicates that the interaction between MIA and adolescent THC exposure is more complex than originally proposed. Indeed, some studies have reported that adolescent THC exposure may attenuate specific neurobiological alterations induced by MIA, particularly within the dopaminergic system, rather than exacerbate them. Furthermore, recent preclinical studies have shown that THC does not consistently worsen MIA-induced behavioral or molecular phenotypes, and in some cases no interaction or even compensatory effects have been observed [30]. Similarly, other reports indicate that THC exposure during adolescence can produce bidirectional outcomes depending on factors such as developmental timing, dose, and the specific behavioral or neurobiological domain assessed [31]. In line with this, recent reviews emphasize that findings from two-hit models involving MIA and cannabinoids remain heterogeneous, with several studies failing to support a simple additive or synergistic effect [32]. Therefore, rather than a uniform exacerbation, the interaction between these two insults likely reflects a dynamic and context-dependent process, highlighting the need for further studies to disentangle the conditions under which THC exposure may either aggravate, mitigate, or not affect MIA-induced alterations.

To better model the interaction between early-life and adolescent risk factors in SCZ, we developed a dual-hit mouse model combining MIA and adolescent THC exposure, and used it to investigate Reelin signaling as a potential mechanistic and biomarker pathway. MIA was induced via polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (Poly(I:C)) injection, a synthetic double-stranded RNA that triggers pro-inflammatory cytokine expression [33, 34]. During adolescence, offspring received Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the psychoactive cannabis component that agonizes cannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2 [19, 35]. Cannabis use in adolescence increases SCZ risk [19, 36] and is linked to long-term cognitive deficits [15], cortical thinning [37], and reduced dendritic spine density and complexity in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and hippocampus (HP) [38]. Importantly, given the well-established sex differences in SCZ and in endocannabinoid system function, we specifically aimed to assess sex-dependent effects, including potential differential alterations in Reelin signaling between males and females.

This dual-hit model provides a platform to identify SCZ therapeutic targets and advance treatment development. Reelin, an extracellular matrix (ECM) glycoprotein, has emerged as a promising SCZ biomarker [16, 39, 40]. Reelin is crucial for neuronal migration and cortical layer formation during development [41], dendritic spine growth, synaptogenesis, synaptic plasticity [39, 42, 43], and adult neurogenesis [44]. Importantly, Reelin signaling is also tightly linked to the regulation of GABAergic interneuron function and the maintenance of excitation/inhibition balance in cortical circuits [45]. Accordingly, alterations in Reelin expression have been associated with disrupted cortical organization, synaptic dysfunction, and cognitive deficits, all of which are core features of SCZ pathophysiology [39, 43].

Reelin binds apolipoprotein E receptor 2 (ApoER2) and very-low-density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR), activating phosphorylation of Disabled homolog-1 (Dab1), an intracellular adaptor. Reelin includes a signal peptide, an F-spondin homology domain, a unique region, and a main body with eight repeats (R1–R8) plus a 33-amino acid basic extension [39, 41, 44, 46]. Upon secretion, Reelin is cleaved at two sites, generating five fragments: N-R2, R3-6, R7-8, N-R6, and R3-8 [43, 47–49]. Genetic alterations or decreased expression of Reelin and its signaling components have been documented in postmortem SCZ brains [40, 43, 50] and Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), where such disruptions correlate with amyloid-beta accumulation and cognitive decline [51–53].

This study aims to investigate novel therapeutic targets for SCZ, focusing on validating Reelin as a potential biomarker within the context of a dual-hit model combining MIA and THC exposure (see Graphical abstract).

Methods

Animals

Adult C57BL/6 J mice (8–10-weeks-old; Charles River Laboratories, France) were housed at the Instituto de Biomedicina de Sevilla (IBiS) in standard cages (max. 5 same-sex animals/cage) with corncob bedding and environmental enrichment (pieces of corrugated cardboard packing material) under controlled conditions (22 ± 1 °C; 12 h light/dark cycle, lights on at 8:00a.m.). Food and water were provided ad libitum. Mice were acclimated for 1–2 weeks before mating, in which one female was paired per cage with one male. Copulatory plugs were checked daily (GD0=plug detection), and males were separated immediately thereafter. All procedures followed ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Use Committee (13/10/2021/157). Animals and samples were coded prior to analysis, and all quantitative measurements were performed blind to both experimental group and sex.

Dual-hit model paradigm

Maternal immune activation

Poly(I:C) sodium salt (P1530, Sigma-Aldrich, batch number #0000125513) was freshly dissolved in sterile endotoxin-free 0.9% saline (1 mg/ml) and administered intraperitoneally at 5 mg/kg on GD9 to randomly assigned pregnant dams; controls received saline. Room temperature at the time of injection was 22 ± 1 °C. To minimize handling stress, dams received the injection in the same home cages where mating had previously taken place (9:00–10:00 a.m.) [54]. We selected GD9 due to its correspondence with a critical neurodevelopmental period in the mouse cortex, equivalent to the first/second trimester of human pregnancy [27, 55, 56]. This time point was chosen to model early gestational immune activation relevant to the dual-hit hypothesis of schizophrenia, as MIA at early versus late gestational stages produces distinct embryonic responses and behavioral phenotypes [33]. Selecting GD9 allows targeting critical cortical neurodevelopmental processes and assessing potential alterations in Reelin signaling [27, 55, 56]. A dose of 5 mg/kg was chosen based on existing literature [27, 33, 57, 58]. A single Poly(I:C) batch was used throughout to avoid inter-batch variability [59].

MIA induction was evaluated by monitoring maternal weight and temperature up to 48h-post-injection. At 3 h, spleen and embryo/placenta samples were collected from a subset of dams (n = 4 per treatment) for cytokine analysis by qRT-PCR as validation of MIA evaluation. The validation results (maternal physiological parameters and cytokine expression) are presented in Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Material 1. The number of offspring obtained from each pregnant dam, including total litter size and sex distribution, is provided in Supplementary Material 2. The MIA reporting checklist proposed by Kentner et al. [54] has been completed and is provided in Supplementary Material 3. Clinical signs of sickness behavior were monitored qualitatively. Weaning was performed at PND30 to ensure stable postnatal development and minimize variability associated with early-life stress, particularly in MIA-exposed offspring. Offspring were housed by sex and litter (max. five per cage). The same environmental enrichment was provided to all animals in accordance with national animal-experimentation regulations; specifically, cages contained pieces of corrugated cardboard packing material [60]. Both males and females were used in subsequent experiments.

THC exposure

THC (T2386, Sigma-Aldrich) was diluted in vehicle (5% ethanol, 5% cremophor, 90% saline) and administered intraperitoneally at 10 mg/kg once daily for six consecutive days during adolescence (PD55–60), modeling subchronic THC exposure. This subchronic regimen was selected based on previous studies demonstrating that short-term adolescent cannabinoid exposure is sufficient to induce behavioral alterations while minimizing long-term compensatory adaptations [38]. The vehicle (5% ethanol, 5% cremophor, 90% saline) was administered to control groups to match THC-treated animals, thereby minimizing potential confounding effects of ethanol, which was present at identical concentrations across vehicle and THC-treated groups. At this low concentration, ethanol is not expected to produce significant behavioral or neurobiological effects on its own, particularly under matched vehicle-controlled conditions [38]. PD55–60 was selected to target the adolescent period in mice, a developmental stage characterized by heightened brain plasticity and increased susceptibility to cannabinoid-induced alterations. This window models early adolescent exposure, which is epidemiologically associated with a faster onset of psychotic-like symptoms [15, 61]. Importantly, previous studies indicate that cannabinoid exposure during adulthood often fails to produce dual-hit-specific alterations, highlighting adolescence as a particularly sensitive period for interaction with prenatal immune activation [16, 38]. Offspring were randomly assigned to one of four experimental groups: Saline+Vehicle (control), Poly(I:C)+Vehicle, Saline+THC, and Poly(I:C) + THC (dual-hit). Body weight was monitored on PD55, PD60, and PD75 (n = 12–15 per group and sex; Supplementary Fig. S1).

Behavioral assays

Behavioral testing was conducted between PD61–74 on a total of 107 mice from the four experimental groups. The final sample sizes included in the behavioral analyses were: males (n = 14–15 per group) and females (n = 12–14 per group). One behavioral test was conducted per day (9:00 a.m.–5:00 p.m.) under dim lighting, following an order from least to most stressful; the complete experimental timeline is provided in Supplementary Fig. S2. All animals were assessed using the SHIRPA protocol (Suppl. Mat. 1 for protocol) [62]. Mice were acclimated in the behavioral room for 30 min before the examination. Apparatuses were cleaned with 2% Derquim between subjects. Researchers conducting and analyzing behavioral tasks were blinded to treatment groups. Tests assessed phenotypes related to positive (open field, self-grooming test, startle reflex and prepulse inhibition, rotarod, and hotplate), negative (three-chamber test, tube test) and cognitive symptoms (novel object recognition test and y-maze test) (Suppl. Mat. 1 for protocols). Animals were sacrificed on PD75 by cervical dislocation or transcardial perfusion under thiobarbital anesthesia (Braun).

Biochemical analysis

PFC was dissected using a stainless brain matrix (Agnthos, 1 mm) and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. PFC was selected due to its well-established involvement in SCZ-related pathology. Total protein was extracted from PFC across all experimental groups (n = 3–5 mice per group and sex). Tissue was lysed in 150–200 µl of lysis buffer, homogenized with a syringe, and centrifuged at 10,000–17,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermofisher).

Proteomic analysis

Proteomic analysis was carried out in the Proteomic services at IBiS. Proteins were reduced and cysteine residues blocked with 50 mM tris-(2-carboxyetyl) phosphine (TCEP, AB Sciex) and cloroacetamide (CAA, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C with shaking. Proteolysis was carried out at 37 °C with trypsin (Promega, Fitchburg, WI, USA) at 1:20 (enzyme to substrate) at 37 °C overnight with agitation. The resulting peptide samples were then quenched with formic acid (pH3) and desalted using a Stage-tip (Omix C18 tips, Agilent). Analysis of digested peptides was performed by LC-MS/MS with an Easy-nLC 1000 HPLC system (Thermofisher) coupled to a Q Exactive Plus Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermofisher). LC separations were performed on C18 HPLC precolumn (75 μm × 2cm) and Easy Spray HPLC column (75 μm × 25cm, 2 μm, 100 Å). Gradient elution was performed with a binary system consisting of (A) 0.1% aqueous formic acid and (B) 0.1% formic acid in CH3CN during 120 min from 5–40% gradient of B with a flow rate of 250 nl/min. A MS survey scan was obtained using a top 15 method with 100–2000m/z mass range. The MS/MS Spectra Acquisition was obtained using Higher-energy Collisional Dissociation (HCD). An isolation mass window of 2 m/z was used for the precursor ion selection, and normalized collision energy of 27% was used for fragmentation. Ten second duration was used for the dynamic exclusion.

Protein was quantified by spectrometry-based label-free identification. Mass spectrometry data analysis was performed through Proteome Discoverer (Thermofisher) with search an Uniprot database for Mus Musculus with a false discovery rate (FDR) less than 1%. Ratios for each experimental group were calculated relative to the control group. For pathway analysis, only proteins showing statistically significant differences between groups were included. Canonical pathway enrichment was performed in a hypothesis-free manner using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA, Qiagen), and significance was determined using Fisher’s exact test as implemented in the software.

Western blot

Equal amounts of protein (25–40 µg) were denatured by boiling in Laemmli buffer (5–10 min) and separated by SDS-PAGE using 6–12% polyacrylamide gels. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore), blocked in 5% non-fat milk or BSA in TBS-T, and incubated with primary antibodies: mouse anti-Reelin (1:1000, MAB5364, Sigma-Aldrich), rabbit anti-Dab1 (1:500, #3328, Cell Signaling) or mouse anti-β-Actin (1:1000, A5316, Sigma-Aldrich). Immunoreactivity was detected using secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (1:5000): goat anti-mouse-HRP (NA931, Amersham) or donkey anti-rabbit-HRP (NA934, Amersham). Detection was performed using Clarity ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad) on a Chemidoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

Histology

Immunofluorescence

Animals were perfused with PBS (n = 5–7 per group and sex). Brains were extracted, fixed overnight at 4 °C in 4% PFA and immersed in 30% sucrose for 48 h. Coronal sections of 25 µm thickness were obtained using a cryostat (Leica). Sections were incubated in a sodium citrate solution, and blocked with 10% goat serum in PBS-T for 1 h. Sections were incubated with mouse anti-Reelin (1:500, MAB5364, Sigma-Aldrich) and goat anti-mouse Alexa 594 (1:500, A-21203, Life Technologies), counterstained with DAPI and mounted on Superfrost-Plus slides with DAKO (Agilent). Images were acquired in a Thunder microscope (Leica, 20x objective) and analyzed using the ImageJ software. Density of Reelin+ cells was automatically quantified using a minimum of 4 slices per mice, averaging three randomly selected fields. Analyses focused on prelimbic prefrontal cortex (PrL) (bregma 2.96–1.54 mm) and infralimbic prefrontal cortex (IL) (bregma 1.94 to 1.34 mm). Cortical thickness measurement was carried manually out using the DAPI staining in PrL (bregma 2.96–1.54 mm) and IL (bregma 1.94–1.34 mm) and the primary somatosensory area (bregma 0.62–0.26 mm). Images were calibrated in ImageJ, and measurements were performed manually in anatomically matched regions across animals. Total cortical thickness was defined as the distance from the pial surface (including layer I) to the white matter boundary (corpus callosum). Layer-specific thickness (layers I, II/III, IV, V, and VI) was determined based on cytoarchitectonic boundaries identified in the DAPI signal. All measurements are expressed in micrometers (µm). Measurements from at least four sections per animal were averaged for statistical analysis.

Golgi-Cox staining

Golgi-Cox staining was performed as described previously [63]. Animals were anesthetized, decapitated, and the brains were extracted and hemisected (n = 5–16 per group). The hemispheres were immersed in Golgi-Cox solution for 3 weeks. Coronal sections of 200 µm were cut using a vibratome (Leica) and incubated with 14–16% ammonium hydroxide (1 h). Analyses focused on PrL (bregma 2.96–1.54 mm) and CA1 region of the hippocampus (bregma −1.34−2.80 mm). Dendritic spine analysis was performed in both PFC and hippocampal CA1, as hippocampal pyramidal neurons represent a classical model for spine density studies in neuropsychiatric disorders. Sections were then treated with sodium thiosulfate (7 min), dehydrated through an ethanol series (50–100%) and cleared with xylene. Sections were mounted in Superfrost-Plus slides with DPX. Z-stacks images of a 0.75 μm step size were acquired in a BX-61 bright-field microscope (Olympus, 100x objective). Dendritic spines were manually counted using ImageJ software and spine density was averaged for each neuron.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from homogenized maternal spleen or embryos (with placenta) with the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen). For each sample (n = 4 dams and n = 6 embryos per group), 1 µg of total RNA was used for reverse transcription to cDNA (qScript cDNA supermix, Quantabio). qPCR was performed with iTaq Universal Probes Supermix (Bio-Rad) and Taqman probes (IL-6: Mm00446190_m1; TNFα: Mm900443258_m1; GADPH: Mm99999915_g1, Thermofisher Scientific) on a ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR system (Thermofisher). Gene expression levels were quantified using the comparative Ct (2^-ΔΔCt) method. Ct values were normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH, and results were expressed as relative gene expression (fold change) relative to the control group. Each sample was analyzed in duplicate.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical procedures were performed using GraphPad Prism™ 8 software (San Diego, CA, USA). Significance was defined as p < 0.05. Prior to statistical testing, data distribution normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Brown–Forsythe test as implemented in Prism. When assumptions for parametric testing were violated, data were analyzed using the corresponding non-parametric test. The experimental design followed a 2 × 2 × 2 factorial structure with prenatal treatment (Poly I:C vs Saline), postnatal treatment (THC vs Vehicle), and sex (male vs female) as fixed between-subject factors. For single-timepoint outcome variables, data were analyzed using three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate main effects and interactions among prenatal treatment, postnatal treatment, and sex. When significant interactions were detected, simple effects were explored using two-way ANOVA or planned pairwise comparisons, as appropriate. For experiments including repeated measurements across time or trials, repeated-measures ANOVA was performed with time/trial as the within-subject factor and prenatal treatment, postnatal treatment, and sex as between-subject factors. Post hoc multiple comparisons were corrected using Tukey’s test for all pairwise comparisons or Sidak’s test for planned comparisons. For repeated-measures analyses, the Geisser–Greenhouse correction was applied when sphericity assumptions were not met (as implemented in Prism). The statistical test used for each dataset, including factors included in the model and sample sizes, is specified in the corresponding figure legend. Sample size was determined based on previous studies using similar experimental designs and effect sizes. No animals or samples were excluded from the analysis.

Results

Behavioral characterization of the dual-hit model

Dual-hit exposure impair locomotion and selectively increase repetitive behavior in male mice without altering anxiety-like behavior or sensorimotor gating

The effects of prenatal Poly(I:C) and postnatal THC on locomotion and anxiety were evaluated using the open field test. Anxiety-like behavior was assessed by quantifying the number of entries into the center area, as well as the time spent and distance traveled in the center of the arena. No significant differences were observed between groups for these parameters; therefore, they are not shown. While THC does not affect these parameters after an extended drug-free period [64, 65], acute THC exposure can alter anxiety and locomotion in mice [65]. A three-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of sex and THC on both the total distance traveled (Sex: F(1, 99) = 6.069, p < 0.05; THC: F(1, 99) = 14.21, p < 0.001) and the mean velocity (Sex: F(1, 99) = 6.142, p < 0.05; THC: F(1, 99) = 14.51, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). In male mice, a two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of THC on both the total distance traveled (F(1, 52) = 10.27, p < 0.01) and the mean velocity (F(1, 52) = 10.39, p < 0.01). Post-hoc comparisons showed that Saline+THC group exhibited reduced locomotor activity and mean velocity compared to Poly(I:C)+Vehicle group (p < 0.05). Similarly, Poly(I:C) + THC group also displayed a significant decrease in mean velocity relative to Poly(I:C)+Vehicle group (p < 0.05). Additionally, there was a trend toward decreased distance traveled in Poly(I:C) + THC compared to Poly(I:C)+Vehicle (p = 0.051). In female mice, a two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of THC on both total distance traveled (F(1, 47) = 5.363, p < 0.05) and mean velocity (F(1, 47) = 5.516, p < 0.05). Although post-hoc analyses did not detect statistically significant pairwise differences between groups, a general reduction in locomotor activity was observed in THC-treated groups. Overall, these findings suggest that THC impairs locomotion regardless of prior immune activation, although the combination with Poly(I:C) may exacerbate the effect.

Also regarding positive-like symptoms, the self-grooming test was used to assess repetitive behavior, which can reflect compulsive-like responses or alterations induced by psychotomimetic drugs [66]. Several MIA models have reported an increased grooming time [33, 67, 68]. A three-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of Poly(I:C) (F(1, 90) = 4.183, p < 0.05) and a trend toward an interaction effect of Poly(I:C) x THC (p = 0.070) (Fig. 1B). In males, a two-way ANOVA showed significant effects of both THC (F(1, 49) = 7.196, p < 0.01) and Poly(I:C) (F(1, 49) = 4.361, p < 0.05). Post-hoc analyses indicated increased grooming time in the Poly(I:C) + THC group compared to controls (p < 0.01) and Poly(I:C)+Vehicle mice (p < 0.05). Additionally, there was a trend toward increased grooming time in Poly(I:C) + THC group compared to Saline+THC group (p = 0.063). In females, no significant differences were observed in the two-way ANOVA.

Importantly, the increase in grooming was not associated with qualitative changes in grooming pattern, as no evident alterations in the sequence or structure of grooming behavior were observed. To further characterize this behavior, the number of grooming events was also analyzed. A three-way ANOVA revealed a non-significant interaction between THC and Poly(I:C) (p = 0.065). In a two-way ANOVA collapsing across sex, a trend toward significant effects of THC (p = 0.098) and the THC × Poly(I:C) interaction (p = 0.061) were observed. Post-hoc analyses did not reveal significant differences between groups (Supplementary Fig. S3). Together, these findings indicate that the combined Poly(I:C) + THC exposure increases the total time spent grooming in males, without affecting the frequency or pattern of grooming behavior, suggesting a quantitative rather than qualitative alteration in this measure.

On the other hand, cannabis exposure affects motor learning and performance [69] as well as movement speed and balance [70]. In the rotarod test, a repeated-measures three-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of day (F(1.63, 84.93) = 41.32, p < 0.0001) and a significant day × Poly(I:C) x THC interaction (F(2, 104) = 4.193, p < 0.05). Post-hoc comparisons showed a general trend of improved motor performance in all treatment groups, reflected by increased latency to fall compared to control animals (Fig. 1C). In female mice, the analysis revealed a significant main effect of day (F(1.81, 85.12) = 47.44, p < 0.0001) and day x THC interaction (F(2, 94) = 6.736, p < 0.01). However, post-hoc comparisons did not detect statistically significant differences between treatment groups at any individual time point.

There were no significant differences between groups or sexes in heat sensitivity (hotplate) (Supplementary Fig. S4). In the startle reflex and prepulse inhibition test, a main effect of stimulus intensity was observed in both the prepulse inhibition phase (F(1.47, 47.08) = 33.02, p < 0.0001) and the acoustic startle response phase (F(2.33, 74.53) = 169.2, p < 0.0001), confirming the expected sensorimotor responsiveness across conditions. However, no main effects of Poly(I:C), THC or their interaction were detected (Supplementary Fig. S5). Thus, neither prenatal immune activation nor adolescent THC exposure, alone or in combination, altered sensorimotor gating under the present experimental conditions.

Prenatal Poly(I:C) and adolescent THC exposure impair social novelty recognition additively and disrupt social dominance in a sex-dependent manner

SCZ patients display negative symptoms such as social withdrawal and anhedonia [38] which are modeled in animals through social tests. In the social preference phase of the three-chamber test, no significant effects were detected in the three-way ANOVA in the social index (Fig. 2A). However, in the social novelty phase, the analysis showed significant main effects of both Poly(I:C) (F(1, 99) = 7.125, p < 0.01) and THC (F(1, 99) = 11.39, p < 0.01) in the memory index (Fig. 2B). As sex showed no significant main effect, data were pooled across sexes. A two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of both prenatal Poly(I:C) (F(1, 103) = 7.482, p < 0.01) and postnatal THC (F(1, 103) = 11.86, p < 0.001) on the memory index. Post-hoc analysis showed a significant reduction in the memory index in the Poly(I:C) + THC group compared to controls (p < 0.001), as well as in the Saline+THC group (p < 0.01) and the Poly(I:C)+Vehicle group (p < 0.05), indicating that both treatments independently and additively impaired recognition memory performance. Importantly, control animals showed a numerical preference for the social stimulus over the object, indicating intact baseline sociability.

Analysis of paired treatments is available in Supplementary Fig. S6. In male mice, a two-way ANOVA showed a significant main effect of Poly(I:C) on the percentage of wins (F(1, 49) = 4.483, p < 0.05), along with a trend toward a main effect of THC (p = 0.058). Post-hoc comparisons showed that Poly(I:C) + THC male mice displayed a significantly lower proportion of wins compared to controls (p < 0.05) and Saline+THC animals (p < 0.05). Additionally, a trend toward reduced dominance was observed when comparing Poly(I:C) + THC males to Poly(I:C)+Vehicle animals (p = 0.077), suggesting a potential synergistic effect of the two exposures on social dominance behavior. Regarding females, a two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of both Poly(I:C) (F(1, 41) = 10.01, p < 0.01) and THC (F(1, 41) = 10.49, p < 0.01). Post-hoc comparisons showed that Saline+THC females exhibited a significantly lower proportion of wins compared to the Poly(I:C)+Vehicle group (p < 0.001). Additionally, there was a trend toward reduced dominance in the Saline+THC group compared to both the controls (p = 0.079) and the Poly(I:C) + THC group (p = 0.072), suggesting that THC alone may impair social dominance behavior in females, independently of MIA.

Object recognition memory deficits emerge selectively in Poly(I:C)-exposed female mice

THC exposure in rodent models causes short-term recognition memory alterations [37, 71], as well as prenatal Poly(I:C) infection [34, 72, 73]. In the novel object recognition test, regarding the memory index, a three-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Poly(I:C) (F(1, 78) = 8.708, p < 0.01), and sex x THC interaction (F(1, 78) = 5.139, p < 0.05). In males, a two-way ANOVA showed no significant differences between groups. However, in females, there was a main effect of Poly(I:C) (F(1, 36) = 6.467, p < 0.05) and THC (F(1, 36) = 8.294, p < 0.01). Post-hoc analysis revealed a reduced memory index in Poly(I:C)+Vehicle mice compared to controls (p < 0.05), Saline+THC (p < 0.01) and Poly(I:C) + THC (p < 0.05) mice (Fig. 2D). There were no differences in the y-maze test (Supplementary Fig. S7). These findings suggest that Poly(I:C) selectively impairs object recognition memory in female mice, while no significant effects were observed in males.

A higher proportion of altered proteins was observed in female dual-hit mice compared to their controls (Fig. 3B). These results suggest that the dual-hit paradigm induces a more extensive protein expression response in females than in males, highlighting potential sex-specific mechanisms underlying this condition. A similar pattern was also observed in the analysis of altered canonical pathways, showing a higher number in female dual-hit mice compared to their controls and Saline+THC male mice (Fig. 3C). In contrast, alterations in interleukin (IL) signaling—specifically IL-1, IL-2, IL-3, IL-8, IL-12, and IL-17A—as well as in synaptogenesis, long-term depression, and glutamatergic, GABAergic, and dopamine receptor signaling pathways were predominantly detected in dual-hit female mice relative to their controls (Fig. 3E). Notably, the Reelin pathway in neurons emerged as particularly noteworthy due to its relevance in SCZ. This pathway was significantly altered in Saline+THC males compared to their controls (Supplementary Fig. S8), and across all treatment groups (Poly(I:C)+Vehicle, Saline+THC y Poly(I:C) + THC) when compared to their respective female controls, highlighting its potential role within the dual-hit paradigm. Similar results were observed in the analysis comparing Saline+THC and control mice (Supplementary Fig. S8). Briefly, a total of 293 proteins were found to be altered uniquely in male Saline+THC mice, and 303 in female mice, with 91 altered proteins common to both groups. Reelin signaling components are differentially altered by Poly(I:C) and THC in a sex-dependent manner

The alteration in the Reelin pathway was confirmed via Western blot analysis in PFC samples by evaluating the expression levels of Reelin, its N-R2 fragment and Dab1 (Fig. 4A). Regarding Reelin expression, a three-way ANOVA reveled a significant main effect of sex (F(1, 16) = 5.408, p < 0.05), and Poly(I:C) x THC interaction (F(1, 16) = 10.21, p < 0.01). Additionally, there were trends toward significance for the main effects of THC (p = 0.051) and Poly(I:C) (p = 0.072). In males, the analysis showed a main effect of Poly(I:C) (F(1, 8) = 10.07, p < 0.05) and Poly(I:C) x THC interaction (F(1, 8) = 13.27, p < 0.01) (Fig. 4B). Post-hoc analysis reported that Reelin expression was decreased in Saline+THC male mice compared to the dual-hit group (p < 0.01). In females, a two-way ANOVA revealed a trend toward a main effect of postnatal THC treatment (p = 0.051) (Fig. 4C). Although post-hoc comparisons did not reach statistical significance, THC-exposed groups showed a general reduction in Reelin expression compared to controls.

Regarding N-R2 fragment, a three-way ANOVA reported significant main effects of sex (F(1, 16) = 5.542, p < 0.05) and THC (F(1, 16) = 9.872, p < 0.01). Additionally, a trend toward a Poly(I:C) x THC interaction was observed (p = 0.077), suggesting a possible combined influence of prenatal and postnatal hits. In males, there was a main effect of THC (F(1, 8) = 5.934, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4B). However, post-hoc comparisons did not identify significant pairwise differences between groups. Despite this, a general reduction in N-R2 expression level was observed across both THC-treated groups compared to controls. In females, a two-way ANOVA revealed a trend toward a main effect of THC (p = 0.059) (Fig. 4C). Although post-hoc comparisons did not reach statistical significance, a consistent pattern of reduced N-R2 expression was observed across all treated groups compared to controls. Therefore, a reduction in both full-length Reelin and its N-R2 fragment levels was observed, rather than an accumulation of the fragment. This suggests that the decrease in Reelin expression is unlikely to be attributed to enhanced proteolytic processing of its N-terminal region.

With respect to Dab1, a three-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of sex (F(1, 16) = 15.51, p < 0.01) and THC (F(1, 16) = 10.05, p < 0.01), as well as a significant sex x THC interaction (F(1, 16) = 18.20, p < 0.001). In males, a two-way ANOVA did not show any effects (Fig. 4B). However, in females, there was a main effect of THC (F(1, 8) = 16.17, p < 0.01), and post-hoc analysis revealed an increased Dab1 expression in dual-hit mice compared to controls (p < 0.05) and Poly(I:C)+Vehicle mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). Dab1 expression has been utilized as an indicator of Reelin signaling activity, given that Reelin binding to its receptors triggers Dab1 phosphorylation [46]. Therefore, the observed increase in Dab1 levels is consistent with the reduction in Reelin expression.

In summary, the non-significant reduction in expression levels of both Reelin and its N-R2 fragment in the Saline+THC group in males, and across all three experimental groups in females, and the significantly altered Dab1 expression levels in females, collectively support the disruption of the Reelin pathway observed in the proteomic study.

THC exposure reduces Reelin+ cell density predominantly in female PFC

The density of Reelin+ cells in PrL and IL areas of PFC was quantified. A three-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of sex (F(1, 40) = 6.252, p < 0.05) and THC (F(1, 40) = 24.32, p < 0.0001) in PrL. In male mice, there was a main effect of THC (F(1, 21) = 10.35, p < 0.01), but post-hoc analysis did not show any significant differences between groups. In female mice, there was a main effect of THC (F(1, 18) = 12.31, p < 0.01) and a trend toward a main effect of Poly(I:C) (F(1, 18) = 4.072, p = 0.058). Post-hoc analysis revealed a reduction in Poly(I:C) + THC (p < 0.01) and THC treatment alone (p < 0.01) compared to controls. Moreover, the Poly(I:C) group showed a marginally significant decrease in Reelin+ cell density compared to the control group (p = 0.084) (Fig. 4D).

In IL, a three-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of sex (F(1, 39) = 7.347, p < 0.01. In male mice, post-hoc comparisons showed no significant differences between groups. In female mice, a main effect of THC was observed (F(1, 18) = 3.187, p < 0.01), and post-hoc analysis indicated that the Poly(I:C) + THC group showed a non-significant reduction (p = 0.072) compared to controls, while the Poly(I:C) group exhibited a significant reduction (p < 0.05) relative to controls (Fig. 4D).

Overall, the pattern observed in IL was similar to that found in PrL, although the effects were less pronounced. These results are consistent with the findings from the Western blot experiments, as a reduced density of Reelin+ cells was observed in the same groups where the lower expression levels of Reelin protein were detected.

SCZ patients exhibit reduced cortical thickness across several brain regions, such as PFC [74, 75]. Similarly, MIA models also show alterations in cortical thickness [23, 76–78]. Additionally, cannabis use can impact cortical thickness in SCZ patients [79–81].

In PrL, a three-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Poly(I:C) in total cortical thickness (F(1, 41) = 7.25, p < 0.05) and layer V (F(1, 41) = 10.25, p < 0.01), but post-hoc analysis did not reveal any significant differences between groups. In IL, in layer I cortical thickness, a three-way ANOVA revealed a main effect of sex x THC interaction (F(1, 41) = 4.39, p < 0.05), and post-hoc analysis showed a reduced layer I thickness in Saline+THC mice compared to Poly(I:C)+Vehicle mice (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. S9).

In the primary somatosensory area, since no significant effects of sex were detected in the three-way ANOVA, data from both sexes were combined. A two-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of THC in total cortical thickness (F(1, 33) = 15.33, p < 0.001). Post-hoc analysis reported a significant reduction in Saline+THC mice compared to controls (p < 0.05) and Poly(I:C)+Vehicle (p < 0.01). Poly(I:C) + THC showed a non-significant reduction in total cortex compared to Poly(I:C) alone (p = 0.058). THC also showed a significant effect when cortical layers were analyzed separately, specifically in layer IV (F(1, 33) = 17.23, p < 0.001). Post-hoc analysis revealed a reduction in layer IV in Saline+THC mice compared to controls (p < 0.05) and Poly(I:C)+Vehicle (p < 0.01). Dual-hit mice also showed a reduction compared to Poly(I:C)+Vehicle (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). Taken together, the results suggest that postnatal THC exposure is the main factor contributing to the reduction in cortical thickness. Although the effect in the dual-hit group did not reach significance, a similar trend was noted.

Discussion

We established a dual-hit model showing alterations in Reelin signaling, suggesting Reelin as a potential target for SCZ and supporting the dual-hit hypothesis. Behavioral phenotypes linked to positive symptoms appeared in males and negative symptoms in both sexes. THC led to reduced cortical thickness and dendritic spine density. Offspring viability was not affected, and MIA was confirmed in all dams. Both sexes were included to assess sexual dimorphism [82]. We observed sex differences in behavior, proteomics, and Reelin pathway alterations. Importantly, behavioral testing was conducted shortly after the final THC administration to capture the short-term impact of subchronic adolescent exposure. This design was chosen to model clinically relevant scenarios in which cannabis use can precipitate the onset of psychotic-like symptoms in vulnerable individuals, and to increase sensitivity for detecting dual-hit interactions, which are often not observed when testing is delayed until adulthood [38].

Poly(I:C)-exposed models show altered compulsive and social behavior, memory, cognitive flexibility, and anxiety [83, 84]. In line with this literature, our behavioral analyses revealed alterations in social and repetitive domains in dual-hit mice, consistent with adolescence as a critical period for cannabis-induced symptoms emergence [38]. Importantly, in male mice, the combined Poly(I:C) + THC condition produced effects across multiple behavioral domains, including reduced locomotor activity, increased self-grooming time, impaired social memory as reflected by a reduced memory index in the three-chamber test, and decreased social dominance in the tube test.

Locomotor alterations likely reflect, at least in part, residual effects of the last THC administration, as the open field test was conducted 24 h after the final injection. Although the regimen represents subchronic adolescent exposure, this timing may capture transient effects in addition to longer-lasting behavioral changes [65]. However, the additional deficits in repetitive behavior, social memory, and dominance are unlikely to be explained by acute drug effects alone. Notably, several of these alterations were either absent or less pronounced in the single-hit conditions, indicating that their emergence depends on the combination of prenatal immune activation and adolescent THC exposure. This pattern supports a domain-specific interaction consistent with a dual-hit framework, rather than fully independent contributions of each factor. Importantly, these findings do not indicate a uniform synergistic interaction across all behavioral domains. Rather, they support a model in which the impact of combined prenatal immune activation and adolescent THC exposure is domain-specific and dependent on the underlying neural circuits involved. In this context, the absence of widespread synergistic effects is consistent with growing evidence that dual-hit interactions do not necessarily produce global exacerbation, but instead reveal selective vulnerabilities. This may reflect differential sensitivity of neural systems to each insult, where certain circuits (e.g., those underlying repetitive behavior or social processing) are more susceptible to cumulative disruption, whereas others may be resilient or even compensated.

Poly(I:C) + THC males exhibited reduced dominance, an effect not seen in females, possibly due to higher baseline male dominance making changes more detectable [85–88]. No significant cognitive impairments were observed in the dual-hit condition, which may be related to the early timing of Poly(I:C) administration [38, 89], as later gestational exposure has been associated with more pronounced deficits linked to GABAergic system development [89]. Importantly, the lack of additive or synergistic effects in this domain may reflect a complex interaction between prenatal immune activation and adolescent THC exposure. Previous studies have reported that cannabinoid exposure can attenuate or normalize MIA-induced alterations in specific behavioral domains, including cognition, rather than exacerbate them [30]. In this context, the absence of deficits in the dual-hit females may result from a compensatory or opposing effect of THC on MIA-induced impairments. Additionally, sex-dependent differences in neurodevelopmental trajectories and endocannabinoid system sensitivity may further contribute to these divergent outcomes, leading to selective vulnerability rather than cumulative impairment.

Notably, no alterations were observed in PPI. While PPI impairments are commonly reported in some MIA models, the absence of effects in the present study is not unprecedented and may reflect several experimental factors. First, the timing of the prenatal immune challenge is critical. Poly(I:C) administration at GD9, as used here, has been associated with milder or more variable behavioral phenotypes compared to later gestational exposures, which more strongly impact neurodevelopmental processes such as GABAergic interneuron maturation [90]. This may reduce the likelihood of detecting robust PPI deficits. Second, the timing and nature of the second hit may also influence outcomes. In our study, THC exposure was administered during late adolescence (P55–P60), and behavioral testing was conducted shortly thereafter. This design may preferentially capture subchronic effects of THC rather than long-term neurodevelopmental disruptions required to impact sensorimotor gating. Third, variability in the effects of Poly(I:C) across studies has been widely reported and may depend on factors such as batch variability, immune activation intensity, animal strain, and environmental conditions [91]. These variables can critically modulate the magnitude and reproducibility of MIA-induced phenotypes, including PPI deficits.

Importantly, the lack of PPI alterations in the present study does not preclude the validity of the model but rather highlights the domain-specific and context-dependent nature of dual-hit interactions. Indeed, behavioral alterations were detected in other domains, including social and repetitive behaviors, suggesting that the combined Poly(I:C) + THC exposure induces selective rather than global SCZ-relevant phenotypes. The use of a subchronic (6-day) exposure paradigm further supports the interpretation of our findings as reflecting short-term and interaction effects rather than long-term consequences of prolonged cannabinoid exposure.

At the molecular level, proteomic analysis revealed widespread alterations in pathways related to immune response, synaptic transmission, and cytoskeletal organization. Notably, changes in interleukin signaling pathways and glutamatergic and GABAergic systems are consistent with well-established mechanisms implicated in schizophrenia. Similarly, alterations in actin cytoskeleton, integrin signaling, and synaptogenesis suggest disruptions in structural plasticity and connectivity. These findings indicate that the dual-hit paradigm impacts multiple biological systems, supporting a model in which schizophrenia-like phenotypes arise from the interaction of several dysregulated pathways rather than a single molecular mechanism.

Proteomic analyses also revealed significant alterations in the Reelin pathway across most treatment comparisons, especially in females. The co-occurrence of behavioral alterations and Reelin pathway disruption suggests that these effects may arise from convergent circuit-level dysfunctions, although a direct relationship cannot be established from the present data. Importantly, the proteomic and Western blot analyses are not directly equivalent measures. While proteomics identifies relative changes at the peptide level and highlights pathway-level alterations, Western blot specifically quantifies selected protein isoforms, including full-length Reelin and its fragments. Therefore, the apparent differences between approaches do not necessarily reflect contradictory findings, but rather distinct aspects of pathway regulation. Taken together, both analyses support a disruption of Reelin signaling rather than a uniform directional change in Reelin protein levels. Notably, Reelin was overexpressed in the dual-hit group rather than reduced, differing from models showing Reelin downregulation after prenatal insults. This may reflect long-term modifications in the endocannabinoid system induced by prenatal Poly(I:C), modulated by THC exposure, though further study is needed [26]. Importantly, previous studies have linked MIA to alterations in Reelin signaling [34, 72, 92]. Prenatal exposure to immune challenges such as Poly(I:C) has been reported to reduce Reelin expression and disrupt GABAergic interneuron development in cortical regions, potentially through epigenetic mechanisms affecting the gen promoter [34, 93]. These findings support the idea that early inflammatory insults can interfere with extracellular matrix organization and neurodevelopmental trajectories relevant to SCZ. In contrast, the effects of adolescent THC exposure on Reelin signaling remain less well characterized. However, accumulating evidence indicates that THC disrupts cortical maturation, synaptic plasticity, and inhibitory circuitry [37, 38], processes in which Reelin plays a critical role. Thus, although direct evidence is still limited, it is plausible that adolescent cannabinoid exposure may also impact Reelin-related pathways, either directly or indirectly.

Within this framework, the present study extends previous literature by examining Reelin alterations in a dual-hit model combining prenatal MIA and adolescent THC exposure. Our findings suggest that Reelin dysfunction may represent a convergent mechanism through which distinct neurodevelopmental insults interact to produce schizophrenia-like phenotypes. This supports the notion that extracellular matrix alterations, and specifically Reelin signaling deficits, could constitute a common pathway linking multiple environmental risk factors to long-term neuropsychiatric outcomes.

Importantly, accumulating evidence indicates that interactions between MIA and adolescent THC exposure are not uniformly additive or deleterious, but instead reflect a complex and context-dependent process shaped by developmental timing, neural circuitry, and functional domain. Several studies have demonstrated that adolescent THC exposure can attenuate or bidirectionally modulate MIA-induced alterations rather than consistently exacerbating them. For instance, Lecca et al. [26] reported that adolescent THC exposure attenuates dopaminergic dysfunction induced by MIA, suggesting compensatory remodeling of mesolimbic circuits [26]. Similarly, Guma et al. [23] described domain-specific effects in a comprehensive dual-hit model, with differential outcomes across behavioral, neurochemical, and structural levels [27]. More recently, Lamanna-Rama et al. [35] showed that THC exposure can improve SCZ-like behavioral deficits in Poly(I:C)-exposed offspring [35]. These findings are further supported by recent integrative studies and reviews [30–32], which highlight that adolescent cannabinoid exposure may induce long-lasting reprogramming of the endocannabinoid system, leading to either exacerbation or partial compensation of prenatal immune-driven alterations. In this context, our findings, particularly the predominance of THC-driven effects, are consistent with a model of dynamic neurodevelopmental plasticity rather than a strictly additive dual-hit mechanism.

Females showed a non-significant trend toward decreased Reelin, suggesting THC influences Reelin expression more than prenatal Poly(I:C). The reduction in Reelin was hypothesized to result from increased N-terminal proteolytic cleavage causing N-R2 fragment accumulation [47, 48, 94]. However, N-R2 expression mirrored full-length Reelin, ruling out this mechanism. Dab1, whose phosphorylation indicates Reelin receptor activation, was measured to infer signaling potency [46]. Dab1 levels were unchanged in males but significantly increased in dual-hit females compared to controls and Poly(I:C) alone, supporting disruption of the Reelin pathway consistent with reduced Reelin levels.

The neurobiological interplay between MIA and THC remains incompletely understood. Prior work suggests convergent mechanisms involving neuron-microglia-astrocyte communication in response to dual insults [28]. We extend this view by proposing ECM, particularly Reelin’s role in neuron-glia interactions, as a key convergent factor within the dual-hit hypothesis [16].

Reduced cortical thickness has been consistently reported in SCZ patients [74, 75] individuals with chronic cannabis use [80]. Although reduced cortical thickness has been reported in MIA models, our findings indicate that, in this study, cortical thinning is primarily driven by postnatal THC exposure rather than by prenatal immune activation alone. Indeed, significant reductions were mainly observed in THC-treated groups, particularly in IL cortex and the primary somatosensory area, while the Poly(I:C)+Vehicle group did not show significant alterations. Moreover, the dual-hit condition did not result in a clear additive or synergistic effect on cortical thickness. These results suggest that cannabis exposure during adolescence may represent a more prominent factor contributing to cortical thinning in this model than MIA, at least at the level of gross anatomical measurements. Further studies quantifying neuronal density are needed to determine if thinning reflects neurodegeneration, cell loss, or other causes.

Additionally, reduced dendritic spine density, a hallmark of SCZ [95] was evident in dual-hit mice. Reelin secretion during prenatal stages primarily originates from Cajal-Retzius cells in the cortical marginal zone [43, 49]. The dendritic spine impairments likely result from decreased Reelin expression in these cells due to Poly(I:C), further exacerbated by THC exposure [46].

A prominent feature of our findings is the presence of sex-dependent effects, particularly at the molecular and structural levels. While behavioral alterations were more evident in males across certain domains, females exhibited a more pronounced response in proteomic changes and in the disruption of the Reelin pathway. This dissociation suggests that males and females may differ not only in the expression of behavioral phenotypes but also in the underlying neurobiological mechanisms engaged by the dual-hit paradigm. Several factors may contribute to this differential vulnerability. First, sex-specific neurodevelopmental trajectories, including differences in cortical maturation and synaptic pruning, may influence the sensitivity to early-life immune challenges and adolescent cannabinoid exposure [96]. Second, the endocannabinoid system, a primary target of THC, shows well-documented sex differences in receptor expression, signaling efficiency, and developmental regulation, which may lead to distinct long-term outcomes following adolescent exposure [97]. Third, interactions with sex hormones and neuroimmune signaling pathways may further modulate the impact of Poly(I:C) and THC, particularly in females, where immune and inflammatory responses are often more robust [83, 98]. Together, these mechanisms may account for the more extensive molecular alterations observed in females despite the relatively modest behavioral phenotype. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating sex as a biological variable in dual-hit models and suggest that vulnerability to schizophrenia-related phenotypes may arise through partially distinct pathways in males and females.

Several limitations must be noted. First, focusing on PFC limits generalization to other brain regions. Second, mouse models, while controlled, cannot fully capture SCZ complexity. Third, additional behavioral tests for fear conditioning, spatial learning, and anxiety would broaden phenotyping. Lastly, THC dosage and administration route affect outcomes; exploring inhalation or other delivery methods could improve translational relevance. These factors should be considered when interpreting results.

Importantly, although our findings suggest an association between Reelin signaling alterations and the behavioral, molecular, and structural phenotypes observed in the dual-hit model, causality cannot be established from the present data. Future studies using targeted manipulation of Reelin expression (e.g., overexpression approaches) will be necessary to determine whether restoring Reelin levels can rescue these alterations [99, 100]. Such experiments would provide direct evidence supporting a mechanistic role of Reelin and further validate its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target in SCZ within the dual-hit framework. Additionally, future studies should assess behavioral outcomes at later adulthood stages (e.g., PND90) to determine the persistence and stability of the alterations observed at early adulthood.

Notably, this interpretation is supported by extensive evidence from human studies. Reelin, a major component of the extracellular matrix, has been consistently reported to be reduced in patients with SCZ [101]. Converging data indicate that alterations in Reelin signaling contribute to the pathophysiology of the disorder [34, 43], with genetic variants affecting Reelin and its downstream pathways being associated with increased disease susceptibility [40, 102, 103]. In addition, postmortem studies have consistently shown decreased Reelin expression in cortical regions [50, 104–106], together with epigenetic repression mechanisms such as hypermethylation of the gen promoter in GABAergic neurons of PFC [107], further strengthening the link between Reelin dysfunction and schizophrenia risk [108].

Importantly, this translational relevance is reinforced by preclinical evidence showing that reduced Reelin expression in animal models leads to SCZ-like phenotypes, including stereotyped behavior, increased anxiety, and cognitive deficits [107]. Accordingly, Reelin has been proposed as a robust biomarker altered across multiple SCZ-related models, particularly those based on genetic or prenatal insults [34]. In this context, our findings provide additional support for both the validity of the dual-hit model and the relevance of Reelin as a candidate biomarker, suggesting that ECM-related mechanisms may represent a key convergent pathway linking neurodevelopmental insults with long-term neuropsychiatric outcomes.

In conclusion, we developed a dual-hit model combining Poly(I:C) with THC exposure. Male dual-hit mice displayed stereotyped behavior and social deficits consistent with positive and negative SCZ symptoms, while females showed social impairments. THC exposure led to reduced Reelin expression and N-R2 fragment in PFC, especially in females, along with decreased Reelin+ cell density and increased Dab1 levels, indicating adolescent THC primarily drives Reelin signaling disruption. THC also caused cortical thinning, and dual-hit mice showed reduced dendritic spine density in PFC and HP. This paradigm is a valid model for investigating the dual-hit hypothesis in SCZ, highlighting Reelin as a potential biomarker.
