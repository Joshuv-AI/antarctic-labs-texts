Baicalin modulates metabolic and inflammatory proteins and attenuates neuronal damage in a rat model of ischemic stroke
Authors: Hyun-Kyoung Son, Dong-Ju Park, Hun-Hwan Kim, Ju-Bin Kang, Phil-Ok Koh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceCerebral ischemia arises from insufficient blood flow to the brain, resulting in substantial metabolic disturbances. Baicalin, a flavonoid compound derived from Scutellaria baicalensis, has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties.ObjectiveTo identify proteins regulated by baicalin in a rat model of ischemic stroke.MethodsAdult male Sprague Dawley rats underwent middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) or sham surgery and received intraperitoneal baicalin (100 mg/kg) or vehicle 1 h before surgery. Neurobehavioral assessments were performed 24 h after MCAO, and cerebral cortex tissues were collected. Cortical damage was evaluated using 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride staining and hematoxylin-eosin staining. Protein expression changes between groups were assessed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry, and selected targets were validated by reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction.ResultsMCAO induced marked neurological deficits, infarction, and histopathological damage, all of which were significantly attenuated by baicalin treatment. MCAO decreased adenylate cyclase type 1 and solute carrier family 25 member 12 levels, and baicalin mitigated these reductions. Baicalin also reduced MCAO-induced increases in C-reactive protein and apolipoprotein C-II, as well as alpha-1 microglobulin, murinoglobulin, and hemoglobin subunit B, proteins associated with inflammation, lipid metabolism, mitochondrial function, and cellular injury.Conclusions and RelevanceBaicalin exerts neuroprotective effects in cerebral ischemia by modulating proteins involved in energy metabolism, myelination, and neuroinflammation. These findings support baicalin as a promising therapeutic candidate in experimental stroke models.Keywords: Baicalin, ischemic stroke, LC-MS/MS, neuroprotection

INTRODUCTION

Stroke is a cerebrovascular disease with the highest mortality rate in humans [1]. It is classified into ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke, with ischemic stroke accounting for more than 80% of cases [2]. Ischemic stroke occurs due to a blockage of blood supply to the brain tissue, leading to significant pathophysiological changes [3]. Oxygen deficiency induces cellular excitotoxicity and excessive glutamate release, which severely disrupt neuronal function [4]. Additionally, oxidative stress elevates reactive oxygen species production and impairs mitochondrial function [5]. Ischemic stroke compromises the blood-brain barrier, increasing immune-cell infiltration and causing cerebral edema [6]. Moreover, it activates several cell death processes, including autophagy, apoptosis, and necrosis, resulting in neurological deterioration [7]. The pathophysiological changes caused by ischemic stroke involve complex and diverse mechanisms, ultimately leading to severe neuronal damage [7].

Baicalin is a flavone glycoside extracted from the dried root of Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi, commonly known as Baikal skullcap. Baicalin exhibits a wide range of pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-cancer effects [8,9,10]. It ameliorates neurological deficits and reduces infarct volume caused by middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) damage [11]. Baicalin inhibits the inflammatory response and apoptosis in cerebral ischemia [12,13]. Specifically, baicalin inhibits the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-1β, attenuates neuroinflammation in ischemic stroke [12]. It exerts the neuroprotective effects against MCAO-induced injury by enhancing neuronal superoxide dismutase activity and reducing oxidative stress [14]. Baicalin regulates apoptosis in cerebral ischemia by upregulating anti-apoptotic proteins such as bcl-2 and downregulating pro-apoptotic markers such as bax and caspase-3 [13]. It also maintenances the blood brain-barrier integrity in ischemic stroke injury by reducing matrix metalloproteinase-9 expression and conserving tight junction proteins [15,16]. Although the neuroprotective effects of baicalin in ischemic brain injury have been reported, its exact mechanism of action and the full spectrum of proteins regulated by baicalin during MCAO-induced damage remain unclear. We hypothesized that baicalin exerts its neuroprotective effects through the regulation of specific protein expression in MCAO-induced damage. This study aimed to identify and characterize various protein sequences regulated by baicalin in animal models of stroke. This study elucidates the neuroprotective effects of baicalin and identifies specific proteins regulated by baicalin using the liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) technique.

METHODS

Experimental animal preparation and drug treatment

Male Sprague Dawley rats (7 weeks old, 210–220 g, n = 42) were obtained from animal breeding centers (Samtako Co., Korea). Animals were randomly assigned to four experimental groups: vehicles + sham, baicalin + sham, vehicles + MCAO, and baicalin + MCAO. Baicalin (100 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and administered via intraperitoneal injection 1 h prior to MCAO surgery. The dosage and route of administration were selected based on a previous study that demonstrated the neuroprotective effects of baicalin at this concentration in a rat model of cerebral ischemia [11], as well as on our prior research, which confirmed its efficacy in reducing ischemia-induced oxidative damage [13]. Vehicle-treated animals were given only PBS. Four animals that died within 24 h of MCAO surgery and two animals with a neurological deficit score of 0 were excluded from the analysis. Thus, a total of 36 rats were included in the final analysis. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Gyeongsang National University (Approval No. GNU-240527-R0110). Zoletil was used as an anesthetic agent in compliance with the institutional license for psychotropic drug handling, approved by the ministry of food and drug safety, and the supporting documents were included in the IACUC protocol.

MCAO surgery

Surgical methods were performed according to previously reported method [17,18]. Anesthesia for surgery was induced by intramuscular injection with Zoletil. A midline surgical approach was performed to expose the right common carotid artery. The occlusion was achieved by inserting a silicone rubber-coated monofilament (Doccol, USA) from right external carotid artery into the right internal carotid artery. The monofilament was inserted until it felt a little resistant to block the origin of the middle cerebral artery. In the sham-operated animals, the same surgical procedure was performed except for the insertion of filaments.

Neurologic deficit score test

Neurological defect assessment was performed 24 h after MCAO surgery and conducted according to the following five-point scoring system: score 0 (no deficits, normal nerve function), score 1 (minor deficits, partial extension of the opposite forelimb), score 2 (voluntary turning behavior toward the contralateral side), score 3 (severe deficit, contralateral falling, seizures, no response to stimuli), and score 4 (very severe deficit, complete absence of movement and loss of consciousness).

Grid walking test

The grid-walking assessment was conducted using a metal square grid apparatus (dimensions: 40 × 40 cm2, individual grid cell dimensions: 3 × 3 cm2, elevation height: 41 cm). Each animal was centrally placed on the apparatus and allowed a 5 min exploratory period. Forepaw slip incidents were categorized as follows: complete miss of a grid during a placement attempt, a descent of the forepaw between grids, or a weight-bearing slip at a grid position. Successful placement was defined as forepaw contact with the grid without a subsequent slip. Analysis continued until the sum of steps and slips for both forepaws reached 100. The final measurement was expressed as the percentage of slips by the affected-side forepaw relative to the total number of bilateral forepaw events (steps plus slips).

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

Brain tissues were fixed in 4% dl-3-n-butylphthalide for 24 h, washed with tap water, dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (70% to 100%), cleaned with xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax using an Embedding center (Leica, Germany). Paraffin blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm using a rotary microtome (Leica) and mounted onto glass slides. The sections were deparaffinized and stained with Harris' hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 3 min and eosin Y for 2 min (Sigma-Aldrich). They were dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (70% to 100%), cleaned with xylene, and mounted with a Permount mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Microscopic images were captured using an Axioscope 5 microscope (ZEISS, Germany).

Protein sample preparation for the LC-MS/MS analysis

The samples tissues were crushed using a mortar and pestle under liquid nitrogen. Subsequently, 30 mg of each sample were lysed in a lysis buffer (8 M urea, 0.1 M Tris-HCl, pH 8.5) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were sonicated for 20 min at 15°C using a Covaris S2-focused ultrasonicator (Covaris, USA). Protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein samples were reduced with 5 mM Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine at 37°C for 30 min, alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetic acid at 25°C for 1 h in the dark, and digested with trypsin (enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:50) at 37°C for 18 h. The resulting peptide mixture was acidified with 15 µL of formic acid to inactivate the trypsin. Peptides were desalted using a C18 spin column and eluted with 80% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid. The collected peptides were dried using a centrifugal vacuum concentrator.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Protein samples dissolved in 0.1% formic acid were analyzed using a Q-Exactive Orbitrap hybrid mass spectrometer coupled with an Ultimate 3000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and using a TripleTOF 6600 mass spectrometer (SCIEX; NFEC-2017-05-237814, High-Tech Materials Analysis Core Facility at Gyeongsang National University, Korea) coupled with an Eksigent Ekspert nanoLC 415 syetem (SCIEX). An ID trap column (2 cm × 75 μm) packed with C18 resin (3 μm) and an ID analytical column (50 cm × 75 μm) packed with C18 resin (2 μm) were employed based on peptide hydrophobicity. The liquid chromatography system utilized two mobile phase components: solvent A (0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid), maintaining a constant flow rate of 300 nL/min throughout the analysis. The mobile phase composition was adjusted over time using a multi-step gradient. The process involved a rapid increase in solvent B from 4% to 8% over 1 min, followed by isocratic retention at 8% solvent B for 5 min. Solvent B was gradually increased from 8% to 30% over 124 min and further to 54% over 30 min. A rapid increase to 96% solvent B occurred within 0.1 min and was maintained for 9.9 min. Finally, the system returned to 4% solvent B within 0.1 min and held this composition for 14.9 min to re-equilibrate the column. The mass spectrometer operated in data-dependent acquisition mode, wherein the ten most intense precursor ions from each MS1 scan were automatically selected for fragmentation. MS1 scans were conducted at a high resolution of 70,000 and MS2 scans at a resolution of 17,500 (both measured at m/z 400). The scans covered a mass range of 400 to 2,000 m/z. Precursor ions were fragmented using 27% normalized collision energy. To minimize redundant analysis, a dynamic exclusion period of 30 sec was implemented, during which previously analyzed ions were excluded from further fragmentation.

Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Total RNA of the right cerebral cortex was extracted by using the Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A total of 500 ng of RNA was used for complementary DNA synthesis and the reverse transcription step was performed using GoScript reverse transcriptase (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR protocol consists of initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min, denaturation at 95°C for 30 sec, annealing at 60°C for 30 sec, elongation at 72°C for 30 sec, and extension at 72°C for 5 min. The PCR step was performed with 30 cycles. Specific primers were used for gene amplification (Table 1). The amplified products were separated by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel for 15 min, visualized using Chemi-Doc (Bio-Rad), and captured. Band intensities were quantified using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, USA) and normalized to β-actin expression. The results of vehicle + sham animals were set to 1.0.

Statistical analysis

The results were expressed as mean values accompanied by their respective standard deviations. To assess the statistical significance of differences between groups, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Scheffe’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was established at a threshold of p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Alleviation of neurological behavior deficits with baicalin treatment in animals with MCAO

We evaluated the neuroprotective properties of baicalin in relation to neurobehavioral deficits. In the MCAO group, animals treated with the vehicle predominantly exhibited severe neurological impairments, such as seizures or a lack of spontaneous motor activity. In contrast, animals treated with baicalin displayed milder symptoms, including circling behavior and forelimb flexion posture. Sham-operated animals, irrespective of drug treatment, showed no signs of abnormal behavior. Quantitative analysis revealed that the neurological function scores were 3.9 ± 0.15 and 2.9 ± 0.35 in the vehicle + MCAO and baicalin + MCAO groups, respectively (F(1, 32) = 12.46, p = 0.0013, Fig. 1A). Baicalin treatment alleviated the MCAO-induced reduction in contralateral forelimb wall-touching behavior. The percentage of bilateral forelimb use was zero in the vehicle + MCAO group and 11.6% ± 1.78% in the baicalin + MCAO group (F(1, 32) = 76.17, p < 0.0001, Fig. 1B).

Alleviation of neuronal damage with baicalin treatment in animals with MCAO

The results of hematoxylin and eosin staining showed that sham-operated animals exhibited typical pyramidal cells with large, round nuclei (Fig. 1C). Conversely, the cerebral cortex of MCAO animals exhibited significant histopathological alterations, including deformed pyramidal cells with condensed nuclei, shrunken dendrites, and numerous cytoplasmic vacuoles. Baicalin administration alleviated these histopathological changes. Quantitative analysis showed that the percentage of damaged neurons was 81.6% ± 3.04% and 56.6% ± 3.48% in the vehicle + MCAO and baicalin + MCAO groups, respectively (F(1, 12) = 22.91, p = 0.0004, Fig. 1D).

Identification of differentially expressed proteins following baicalin treatment in animals with MCAO

A comprehensive proteomic analysis was conducted on brain tissue samples using high-performance LC-MS/MS analysis. The analysis identified 3,064 proteins, of which 2,808 were successfully quantified (Fig. 2). Proteins were classified as differentially expressed proteins based on their quantitative variation, specifically those showing a statistically significant fold change of < 0.5 (indicating downregulation) or > 2 (indicating upregulation) (Fig. 2A and B). Based on the established criteria, a total of 81 differentially expressed proteins were identified when comparing the baicalin + MCAO group with the vehicle + MCAO group (Table 2). Among these, 44 proteins were upregulated and 37 were downregulated (Fig. 2C and D). The heatmap presented in Fig. 2C provides a visual representation of expression patterns, and the full list of corresponding proteins is provided in Table 2. This proteomic profiling provides valuable insights into the molecular alterations associated with baicalin treatment in animals with MCAO.

Analysis of specific proteins regulated by baicalin treatment in animals with MCAO

The results of reverse transcription-PCR analysis showed changes consistent with those observed in the LC-MS/MS analysis. The mRNA expression levels of adenylate cyclase type 1 and solute carrier family 25 member 12 (SLC25A12) were downregulated in the vehicle + MCAO group, but this downregulation was attenuated in the baicalin + MCAO group (Fig. 3A). Adenylate cyclase type 1 mRNA levels were 0.56 ± 0.03 and 0.79 ± 0.11 in the vehicle + MCAO and baicalin + MCAO groups, respectively (F(1, 12) = 8.847, p = 0.0042). The mRNA expression levels of SLC25A12 were 0.49 ± 0.01 and 0.93 ± 0.03 in the vehicle + MCAO and baicalin + MCAO groups, respectively (F(1, 12) = 531.8, p < 0.0001, Fig. 3B). In contrast, the mRNA expression levels of C-reactive protein and apolipoprotein C-II were upregulated in the vehicle + MCAO group, and this upregulation was mitigated in the baicalin + MCAO group (Fig. 3A). C-reactive protein mRNA levels were 1.18 ± 0.10 and 0.49 ± 0.26 in the vehicle + MCAO and baicalin + MCAO groups, respectively (F(1, 12) = 27.72, p = 0.0001). Apolipoprotein C-II mRNA levels were 1.39 ± 0.03 and 0.88 ± 0.03 in the vehicle + MCAO and baicalin + MCAO groups, respectively ((F(1, 12) = 45.17, p < 0.0001, Fig. 3B). The mRNA expression levels of alpha-1 macroglobulin, hemoglobin subunit B1, and murinoglobin were elevated in the vehicle + MCAO group, but these increases were alleviated in the baicalin + MCAO group (Fig. 4A). The mRNA levels of alpha-1 macroglobulin were 0.94 ± 0.18 and 0.42 ± 0.17 in vehicle + MCAO and baicalin + MCAO animals, respectively (F(1, 12) = 10.32, p = 0.0032). The expression levels of murinoglobin mRNA were 1.18 ± 0.16 and 0.75 ± 0.09 in the vehicle + MCAO and baicalin + MCAO groups, respectively (F(1, 12) = 7.162, p = 0.0199). Hemoglobin subunit B1 mRNA levels were 1.21 ± 0.08 and 0.92 ± 0.08 in the vehicle + MCAO and baicalin + MCAO groups, respectively (F(1, 12) = 9.476, p = 0.0001, Fig. 4B).

DISCUSSION

We previously showed that baicalin mitigates lipopolysaccharide-induced cell damage by inhibiting glial cell activation and inflammatory factor expression [19]. This study confirmed the neuroprotective effects of baicalin through various experimental approaches, including neurobehavioral assessments and histopathological evaluations in an animal model of cerebral ischemia. In this study, neurobehavioral evaluation and histological examination were performed 24 h after MCAO surgery. This time point was determined because it is widely recognized as a standard for evaluating neurological deficits and cerebral infarction size in a permanent MCAO model. Many studies have previously reported the neuroprotective effects of baicalin 24 h after MCAO [11,14,20]. Earlier time points (3–12 h after MCAO) are more suitable for examining the molecular and cellular mechanisms involved in the initial response, while later time points (48–72 h after MCAO) are used to study recovery or delayed responses. Therefore, our experimental design is considered suitable for observing pathological changes and functional outcomes after cerebral ischemia. MCAO induced significant behavioral and cognitive impairments, but baicalin administration significantly alleviated neurobehavioral damage and improved neurological function. Baicalin treatment reduced histopathological cellular damage caused by MCAO. These findings confirm that baicalin exerts neuroprotective effects against focal cerebral ischemia. This study employed LC-MS/MS and bioinformatics analyses to evaluate various proteins regulated by baicalin in cerebral ischemia. We identified proteins regulated by baicalin treatment in the cerebral cortex of animals with MCAO and focused on the following specific proteins: adenylate cyclase type 1, SLC25A12, C-reactive protein, apolipoprotein C-II, alpha-1 macroglobulin, murinoglobin, and hemoglobin subunit B1. These proteins are involved in diverse functions, including energy metabolism, neuroplasticity, myelination, inflammation, and lipid metabolism.

Adenylate cyclase catalyzes the conversion of ATP to cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) and is implicated in various biological processes [21]. It plays a critical role in cellular defense by producing cAMP in ischemic conditions and modulating intracellular signaling pathways essential for cellular responses to ischemic stress [22,23]. Adenylate cyclase activity initially increases during acute cerebral ischemia but decreases in later stages, disrupting cAMP-dependent pathways [23]. Additionally, lysosome-associated membrane protein 2A mediates the reduction of adenylate cyclase activity, triggering acute neurodegenerative responses and neuroinflammation [24]. Adenylate cyclase type 1 is primarily expressed in the hippocampus and cerebellum, where it is concentrated at synaptic sites, particularly in the postsynaptic densities [25]. This specific localization highlights its involvement in synaptic transmission and neuroplasticity. Following transient forebrain ischemia, adenylate cyclase type 1 immunoreactivity in the hippocampal CA1 region initially rises but subsequently declines, correlating with delayed neuronal death [24]. These findings indicate that adenylate cyclase type 1 activity is critical for neurodevelopment and neuroplasticity [25]. However, changes in adenylate cyclase type 1 expression in cerebral ischemia have not been previously reported. This study demonstrated that adenylate cyclase type 1 expression was reduced following ischemic stroke, and this decrease was attenuated in animals treated with baicalin. Specifically, this study focused on the modulation of adenylate cyclase type 1 expression by baicalin treatment in the context of cerebral ischemia. Baicalin has been reported to influence the adenylate cyclase/cAMP/protein kinase A signaling pathway [26]. It alleviates neuropathological changes by enhancing neuronal plasticity and reducing neuroinflammation in cerebral ischemia [13,20]. Given the role of adenylate cyclase type 1 in neuroplasticity, we suggest that baicalin alleviates the decrease in adenylate cyclase type 1 levels in cerebral ischemia, maintains neuroplasticity, and protects neurons against ischemic damage.

SLC25A12 is a key component of the malate-aspartate shuttle, which plays a crucial role in cellular energy metabolism [27]. Ischemic injury disrupts energy metabolism by downregulating proteins involved in glycolysis, the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, and the tricarboxylic acid cycle [28,29]. The reduction of SLC25A12 contributes to a metabolic imbalance between the cytosol and mitochondria, resulting in energy metabolism failure under ischemic conditions [30]. cAMP response element-binding protein regulates the expression of SLC25A12 in neuronal cells [31]. Additionally, SLC25A12 is upregulated during neuronal differentiation [31]. Disruption of SLC25A12 in animal models results in hypomyelination and abnormal accumulation of neurofilaments, suggesting that SLC25A12 plays a crucial role in myelin production and axonal transport [32]. A previous study of infarcted brain specimens has shown reduced expression of SLC25A12, which highlights the important role of SLC25A12 in ischemia-induced metabolic dysfunction [33]. We observed a reduction in SLC25A12 expression in animals with MCAO; this decrease was alleviated by baicalin treatment. The decline in SLC25A12 expression indicates impaired energy metabolism and disrupted myelination, which leads to neurodegeneration. Our results showed that baicalin regulates SLC25A12 expression levels in cerebral ischemia. To date, the relationship between SLC25A12 and baicalin has not been identified. This study is the first to demonstrate that baicalin regulates SLC25A12, however, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Although further research is needed to elucidate the specific pathways through which baicalin regulates SLC25A12, our findings suggest that baicalin protects neurons against ischemic damage by mitigating the reduction in SLC25A12 expression.

C-reactive protein is an acute-phase protein produced in response to inflammatory cytokines [34]. Its levels can increase rapidly in response to trauma, infection, or inflammation, making it a useful marker for monitoring these conditions [35]. Beyond being a marker, it is also a potential mediator of atherothrombotic disease, as it activates both inflammatory and coagulation pathways [36]. Additionally, C-reactive protein infusion has been shown to exacerbate endothelial dysfunction and increase procoagulant activity [37]. Ischemic stroke leads to abnormalities in hemostasis, such as increased blood coagulability [38]. C-reactive protein levels are consistently elevated in ischemic stroke patients and experimental models, and have been shown to be associated with increased stroke severity and mortality [38]. Upregulation of C-reactive protein in cerebral ischemia was previously reported [39]. Our results are consistent with previous findings and support that C-reactive protein is a biomarker and potential contributor to stroke pathology. This study demonstrated an increase in C-reactive protein expression in ischemic stroke and further revealed that baicalin alleviated the increase in C-reactive protein expression caused by MCAO damage. Moreover, baicalin has been shown to decrease serum levels of C-reactive protein in spontaneously hypertensive rats, demonstrating its effectiveness in reducing systemic inflammation associated with hypertension [40]. Additionally, in a rat model of Alzheimer’s disease, baicalin significantly reduced C-reactive protein expression in brain tissue, further supporting its anti-inflammatory properties [41]. Baicalin exerts anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and neuroprotective effects in both depressive and ischemic conditions, and improves cognitive deficits by reducing neuroinflammation in lipopolysaccharide-induced damage [42]. These findings suggest that baicalin regulates C-reactive protein expression, influencing inflammatory responses and coagulation processes.

Apolipoproteins bind lipids to form lipoproteins, which are essential for the transport and metabolism of lipids. Among the different types of apolipoproteins, apolipoprotein C-II is known for its role in activating lipoprotein lipase, an enzyme crucial for the hydrolysis of triglycerides in lipoproteins [43]. It is a vital activator of lipoprotein lipase, promoting the hydrolysis of triglycerides in lipoproteins such as chylomicrons and very-low-density lipoproteins [44]. Apolipoprotein C-II plays an important role in maintaining normal blood lipid levels and preventing hypertriglyceridemia, which is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease [44]. Increases in apolipoprotein C-II levels have rarely been reported in ischemic stroke models, making our findings potentially novel. In the present study, we observed an increase in apolipoprotein C-II levels in ischemic stroke models, which was alleviated by baicalin treatment. Although the precise mechanism remains unclear, our findings indicate that baicalin modulates apolipoprotein C-II expression, potentially contributing to its neuroprotective effects in ischemic brain injury. Thus, these findings suggest that baicalin mitigates brain damage by regulating apolipoprotein C-II.

Alpha-1 macroglobulin is a small, spherical protein found in all vertebrates and distributed in plasma and extracellular fluids of all tissues [45]. It belongs to the alpha-macroglobulin-complement family and acts as a proteinase inhibitor. It is an antioxidant protein with reductase activity that binds to heme and radicals, its expression is upregulated following oxidative stress [45]. Alpha-1 macroglobulin is associated with white matter lesions and are influenced by the chronic pathological progression of cerebral small vessel disease [46]. It is also recognized as biomarker for acute ischemic stroke [46]. A previous stroke-related study has reported increased expression of alpha-1 macroglobulin, which is consistent with our findings [46]. This study demonstrated that baicalin attenuates the MCAO-induced elevation of alpha-1 macroglobulin expression.

Furthermore, murinoglobulin is a novel protease inhibitor found in the plasma of rodents. It is a member of the alpha-macroglobulin family, which includes proteins that inhibit proteases [47]. Macrophages regulate neutrophil degranulation by secreting murinoglobulin-1 in myocardial infarction [48]. Murinoglobulin is involved in protease inhibition and immune regulation [48]. Our findings showed that MCAO injury induced an increase in murinoglobulin expression, which was alleviated by baicalin treatment. Changes in hemoglobin levels are significant predictors of outcomes in patients with ischemic stroke [49]. Increased hemoglobin levels are associated with early neurological deterioration [49]. They also imply high mortality, particularly within the first month after a stroke, finally can predict poor functional outcomes and a higher risk of stroke recurrence [49]. Hemoglobin subunit B1 has also been identified as a stroke-associated protein in human extracellular vesicle proteomics. Recent clinical study has demonstrated that hemoglobin subunit B1 is significantly upregulated in extracellular vesicles isolated from the blood of ischemic stroke patients [50]. Our findings are consistent with the results of previous study and further demonstrate that baicalin regulates hemoglobin expression. In this study, MCAO injury increased hemoglobin expression, which was mitigated by baicalin treatment. The findings of this study confirmed increases in the expression of alpha-1 macroglobulin, murinoglobulin, and hemoglobin in cerebral ischemia, which were attenuated by baicalin treatment. The increase in these proteins indicates cell damage, highlighting their potential as biomarkers for disease diagnosis and prognosis. Thus, our findings demonstrated that baicalin reduces cell damage and exerts protective effects against focal cerebral ischemia.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that baicalin alleviates neurobehavioral disorders and attenuates histopathological changes caused by MCAO damage. We identified the downregulation of adenylate cyclase 1 and SLC25A12, as well as the upregulation of C-reactive protein, apolipoprotein C-II, alpha-1 macroglobulin, murinoglobulin, and hemoglobin subunit B in animals with MCAO. Baicalin treatment alleviated these MCAO-induced protein changes. These proteins play critical roles in energy metabolism, myelination, and neuroinflammation. Collectively, our findings show that baicalin exerts neuroprotective effects by regulating various proteins, providing important insights into the mechanisms underlying ischemic stroke and the neuroprotective actions of baicalin.
