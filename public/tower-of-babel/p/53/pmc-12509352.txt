Limbal stem cell sheet bandage for short-term treatment of corneal regeneration
Authors: Fatemeh Sanie-Jahromi, Masoomeh Eghtedari, Mahmood Nejabat, Ramin Salouti, Hossein Jamali, Shahram Bamdad, Mohammad Hassan Jalalpour, Amir Khosravi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractThis study investigates a novel short-term treatment for Limbal Stem Cell Deficiency (LSCD) using a limbal stem cell (LSC) sheet bandage. The method utilizes polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membranes to harvest and transplant LSC sheets for corneal surface regeneration. In cases where autologous tissue was not available, limbal biopsies obtained from patients’ relatives were cultured to generate LSC sheets, which were subsequently transferred onto PTFE scaffolds using a bottom-up cell transfer technique. Eight patients with treatment-resistant, end-stage LSCD underwent the procedure and examined during a six-months follow-up. Pre-transplantation assessments confirmed the viability and marker expression of the LSCs. Immunocytochemistry and microscopy validated the structural integrity of the LSC sheets on PTFE, and toxicity assays indicated the scaffold’s biocompatibility. Post-operative evaluations showed improvements or stabilization in visual acuity, along with a reduction in the use of lubricating eye drops during a 1-month period. No immediate severe complications were observed. Short-term outcomes were promising, showing corneal surface regeneration and improved patient satisfaction and quality of life. However, long-term success varied at the 6- month follow-up. This LSC sheet bandage acted as a cellular bandage, rapidly restoring the corneal surface and facilitating further therapeutic procedures. The technique may serve as a potential interim solution for patients ineligible for conventional corneal transplants.Trial registrationThe study was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT) on 2020-05-06 (approval number: IRCT20180724040574N1).Keywords: Limbal stem cells, Cell sheet, PTFE scaffold, LSCD, Corneal transplantation

Introduction

Limbal epithelial stem cells (LSCs), residing in the limbal basal epithelium, are pivotal for the regeneration of the corneal epithelium and the preservation of ocular surface integrity [22]. Damage to these cells can result in limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD), a debilitating condition characterized by chronic corneal epithelial defects, neovascularization, visual impairment, and discomfort [15]. The etiology of LSCD is multifactorial, encompassing genetic defects and secondary insults to LSCs and their niche, leading to abnormal corneal epithelial wound healing and conjunctivalization of the corneal surface [4, 33].

The traditional mainstay treatments for LSCD have been procedures such as conjunctival-limbal autograft (CLAU), kerato-limbal allografts (KLAL), cultivated limbal epithelial transplantation (CLET), and simple limbal epithelial transplantation (SLET). These treatments have been extensively documented in peer-reviewed literature over the past three to four decades, with CLAU, KLAL, and CLET being recognized for their ability to restore the corneal surface by transplanting grafts harvested from the limbus of a healthy eye or cultured limbal cell sheets [13, 36]. KLAL is particularly used for bilateral LSCD when autologous tissue is unavailable, typically sourcing donor tissue from cadavers. SLET, a more recent technique, combines the benefits of CLAU and CLET and has shown success rates comparable to these established procedures, offering advantages such as a reduced risk of iatrogenic LSCD and the elimination of the need for cell culture facilities [13, 36].

Despite the effectiveness of these treatments, challenges such as donor shortages, limited corneal tissue availability, high surgical risks, and transplant failure persist [26, 38], necessitating the exploration of alternative strategies for corneal repair. In recent years, stem cell therapy has emerged as a promising avenue [3, 8], with LSCs demonstrating high proliferative potential in vitro and the ability to form a monolayer with tight intercellular connections, making them suitable for cell-based therapies in corneal tissue engineering [7, 10, 17, 21, 39].

Traditional methods often involve a top-down strategy, constructing the cell substrate through enzymatic treatment and seeding floating cells onto the scaffold. However, this approach presents challenges such as non-uniform cell distribution and low cell density [16]. To overcome these limitations, innovative strategies, including the bottom-up approach, have been introduced [2, 12, 27]. This novel method allows for the harvesting of cell sheets with robust intercellular connections while preserving their natural architecture [31, 35]. For this approach, a biocompatible scaffold with an adhesive surface and strong intercellular connections in the target cell sheet is essential.

This study employed polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membranes for harvesting LSC sheets bandage using a bottom-up strategy for corneal transplantation. The cell substrates were analyzed and applied in a clinical study to assess the safety and efficacy of regenerating damaged corneal tissue. This technique could address previous challenges in tissue engineering (including enzymatic treatment and maintaining uniform, intact cell architecture) and represented a novel short-term treatment for LSCD.

Materials and methods

Clinical design and patient selection

In this non-randomized clinical trial, eight patients with end-stage LSCD, who were unresponsive to conventional treatments, were included. Prior to enrollment, all participants had undergone various standard therapies without satisfactory outcomes, including autologous or allogeneic direct limbal tissue transplantation, SLET, amniotic membrane transplantation, and autologous serum eye drops. Despite these interventions, the patients continued to experience significant symptoms and visual impairment. Due to repeated prior treatments, the corneal surfaces of some patients were no longer suitable for further graft transplantation. These individuals were recruited for cell sheet bandage transplantation to regenerate the corneal surface and prepare them for subsequent treatment. All participants were referred by cornea specialists and met the inclusion criteria of having at least light perception and no concurrent ocular conditions that could limit visual improvement.

Patients underwent a comprehensive re-examination by the principal investigator (PI) surgeon to confirm their eligibility. This included capturing photo-slit lamp images of the corneal surface, using impression cytology to assess the ocular surface condition, and conducting tests for visual acuity, intraocular pressure, and fundus examination. Patients with significant stromal vascularization, which could compromise the success of transplantation, were excluded from the study.

Harvesting donor limbal tissue

In cases where autologous tissue was not available, donor limbal tissue was harvested from patients’ relatives under local anesthesia, ensuring a biopsy size of approximately 1 × 2 mm². Due to ethical and practical constraints related to voluntary family donations, detailed donor and ocular health data were not collected. However, the limbal biopsy dimensions and subsequent procedures were standardized across all participants to ensure consistency. The harvested donor tissue was immediately transported to a Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-certified clean room for cell culture processing.

LSC culture

The cell expansion procedure followed GMP protocols and was conducted in a clean room. All specimens were tested for viral and microbial contamination to ensure sterility. A clinical-grade culture medium, consisting of DMEM/F12 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% human AB serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Shellmax), was used. Human AB serum, a viable and cost-effective alternative to fetal bovine serum (FBS) in clinical settings, has been shown to be effective in supporting a wide range of cell types, including mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) [1, 5]. Its lack of ABO antibodies reduces the risk of adverse reactions, making it the preferred choice for serum substitution [20].

All cultures were established under controlled conditions to minimize external variables influencing cell sheet quality, focusing on the consistent production of viable LSC sheets from the harvested tissue.

Limbal explants were placed on culture plates with the epithelial layer facing upward. After an initial 15-minute adherence period, the culture medium was gently added to the plate. LSCs began to grow and migrate from the explants approximately 7–10 days after the initial culture . The cultures were maintained in a 37 °C incubator, with half of the medium replaced every five days. Once the cells reached 60–70% confluence, they were transferred to the PTFE scaffold.

Cell transfer to PTFE

The method employed in this research for cell transfer to PTFE is based on the principles delineated in a patent recently registered with the Iran Patent and Trademark Office (patent number: 101322;https://ipm.ssaa.ir/SearchResult?page=1%26;DecNo=139750140003009429%26;RN=101322). In this procedure, clinical-grade PTFE (Millicell-CM, hydrophilic PTFE, Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA) was used to harvest LSCs in preparation for transplantation. The culture medium was first aspirated from the LSC layer, which was then allowed to air-dry for 60 s to promote optimal cell attachment. A pre-cut hydrophilic PTFE disc, 12 mm in diameter, was gently placed onto the dried LSC layer, ensuring contact for no less than 30 s to achieve successful cell transfer. The PTFE disc, now bearing the LSC monolayer, was cautiously lifted and immersed in 5 ml of complete medium (Fig. 1). Under an inverted microscope, the structural integrity and proper arrangement of the LSCs were confirmed. Following validation of the LSC monolayer’s integrity, the PTFE-LSC composite was transported to the designated medical facility for immediate surgical application, in compliance with the prevailing ethical standards and institutional guidelines.

LSCs characterization

Characterization procedure was conducted either before or during cell culture in a replicate plate to evaluate the suitability of the produced cell substrate. LSCs markers, including cytokeratin 19 and vimentin (Santa Cruz), P63-α (Cell Signaling Technology), CD44, and ABCG2 (BD Pharmingen), were assessed in the expanded cells before cell transfer to the PTFE scaffold. The evaluation was performed using both immunocytochemistry and flow cytometry, following established protocols as described previously [25]. The effect of PTFE on cell viability was assessed using an MTT assay, a colorimetric assay for assessing cell metabolic activity, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. This analysis aimed to determine the impact of the PTFE scaffold on cell viability, providing insights into its compatibility with LSCs. After the transfer of LSCs to the PTFE scaffold, a detailed examination was conducted using inverted microscopy. This real-time evaluation allowed the assessment of cell morphology, attachment to the scaffold, the area covered by the transferred cells, and the integrity of the cell layer. Marker characterization (immunocytochemistry and flow cytometry) was performed on replicate cultures prior to PTFE transfer, while the PTFE-adhered sheet was evaluated for morphology, integrity, and viability. This examination ensured substrate readiness and confirmed the presence of an adequate cell population for transplantation.

Surgical procedure and follow-up

Four to six weeks after obtaining the specimen and following the cell culture procedure, the patient was admitted for cell sheet bandage transplantation. The transplantation procedure was conducted under sterile conditions and local anesthesia. A peritomy was carefully performed, the corneal epithelium debrided, and hemostasis achieved prior to the placement of the PTFE-cell construct. The membrane was securely attached using cyanoacrylate glue onto the conjunctiva, followed by the application of a contact lens to aid in stabilization. Postoperatively, the patient received levofloxacin eye drops every 6 h, non-preserved artificial tears every 4 h, and prednisolone eye drops every 6 h. After complete corneal epithelialization, levofloxacin was discontinued and topical cyclosporine 2% eye drops were initiated every 8 h. Cyclosporine was prescribed to prevent infection and manage immune response. Within two to three weeks postoperatively, the PTFE membrane underwent autolysis, while the cyanoacrylate adhesive gradually dislodged from the corneal surface. Follow-up examinations were scheduled at 1 and 6 month-post-surgery, with additional referrals to a rheumatologist for immunosuppressive therapy as deemed necessary. Accordingly, local immunosuppression (Cyclosporine) was administered to all patients. While systemic immunosuppression (Immuran) was reserved exclusively for cases in which autologous tissue from the patient’s lateral eye was unavailable and allogeneic grafts from related donors were utilized. Patient satisfaction was assessed qualitatively, based on patients’ subjective reports, and no questionnaire was used.

The study’s primary objective was to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of this approach by assessing visual acuity, corneal integrity, and patient satisfaction before and after the procedure.

Results

Characterization of LSCs markers, integrity and viability on PTFE scaffold

This study demonstrated that LSCs could attach to the PTFE scaffold within approximately 1 min. Immunocytochemical tests confirmed the expression of cytokeratin 19 and vimentin markers in the cultured LSCs. Cytokeratin 19, a protein associated with epithelial tissues, signified the development of epithelial cells. Vimentin served as a marker for mesenchymal cells, indicating the regenerative potential of the cultured LSCs (Fig. 2A-H).

Flow cytometry analysis further validated the expression of markers essential for LSCs characteristics, including CD44 (16.2%), a cell surface glycoprotein of LSCs; P63-α (75%, Fig. 2I), a transcription factor crucial for epithelial development; and ABCG2 (11.3%), a transporter protein associated with stem cell function. Microscopic images showed that LSCs maintained their original architecture and intercellular connections on PTFE, forming an intact cell sheet (Fig. 3). Notably, the MTT assay, a measure of cell viability, indicated no cytotoxic effects of PTFE on LSCs (data not shown).

Clinical outcomes

The pre-treatment characteristics and ocular conditions of the patients are summarized in Table 1. As indicated in Table 2, all eight patients experienced improvement or stability in visual acuity at the 1-month follow-up, with most reporting a decreased need for lubricating drops. However, most patients were hesitant to start or continue systemic immunosuppressive medications due to various reasons, including concerns about side effects, the impact on daily life, and personal beliefs. This led to discontinuation before the 6-month evaluation. Although the initial outcomes were promising, the 6-month follow-up showed a regression to pre-treatment vision levels for the majority, with photophobia persisting in three cases. The management of photophobia was not explicitly documented.

Discussion

Various scaffolds have been explored for the culture and transplantation of corneal stem cells, including amniotic membranes, contact lenses, collagen plates, fibrin gels, and synthetic polymers [32]. Among these, amniotic membranes are known for their ability to mimic the extracellular matrix and support cell adhesion and growth. However, they present significant limitations, such as the risk of viral contamination and limited availability [14]. Fibrin gels, while biologically active, often display inconsistent degradation rates and mechanical properties, which may affect their reliability in clinical applications [28]. Furthermore, the adhesion and expansion of cells on amniotic membranes can be time-consuming, which may delay treatment and compromise the uniformity of cell differentiation [11]. In this study, the efficiency of the PTFE scaffold for use in LSCs sheet bandage transplantation was demonstrated, particularly its ability to form robust connections with LSCs. This scaffold significantly reduces the preparation time required for creating viable cell substrates. Our bottom-up approach eliminates the need for enzymatic treatments, which can damage cell surface proteins and impair the cells’ differentiation potential [18, 30]. This method preserves the natural architecture and intercellular connections of the LSCs during the transfer to the scaffold. By retaining the basal membrane and adherent proteins, the scaffold has the potential to facilitate the rapid establishment of new cellular connections in the target tissue [6, 27, 37].

Previous research has identified PTFE as an optimal membrane for binding to corneal surface cells, particularly in impression cytology applications [19, 29]. Additionally, PTFE has been recognized for its applications in medical and tissue engineering contexts [29]. Although PTFE is classically described as an inert “non-stick” polymer, the membranes employed in this study were hydrophilic PTFE (Millicell-CM), which exhibit surface modifications that enhance wettability and cellular interactions. In addition, the intact LSCs have strong intercellular connections and cell surface proteins which likely mediated rapid adhesion to the PTFE within seconds without enzymatic treatment. PTFE’s unique properties—such as its biocompatibility, transparency in aqueous media, and rapid cell adhesion capability—make it an ideal carrier for LSC transplantation. The PTFE scaffold offers a more controlled environment for cell growth compared to alternatives like amniotic membranes or fibrin gels. Its consistent structure and reduced risk of contamination make it particularly suitable for emergency situations requiring quick preparation. Compared to standard procedures such as SLET and CLET, the PTFE-based technique simplifies the transplantation process and may shorten overall treatment time [13, 34].

The handling properties of PTFE provide distinct advantages in cell transfer and transplantation. Its transparency allows for live monitoring of cell attachment and transfer during the procedure. Microscopic imaging confirmed that LSCs maintained their structural integrity and intercellular connections after transfer to the PTFE scaffold, forming an intact cell sheet. MTT assays further verified the non-cytotoxic nature of PTFE, highlighting its safety as a scaffold for repairing the corneal surface or as a temporary bandage for patients with end-stage LSCD. The use of PTFE in this study aligns with recent advancements in tissue engineering, where it has been employed for cell culture and as a cell carrier [9, 23]. Our bottom-up strategy enhanced this application by enabling the rapid harvesting and transfer of LSC sheets, offering a novel method for LSC transplantation to the corneal surface.

The procedure, performed under topical anesthesia, was quick and straightforward, eliminating the need for sutures and further reducing the duration of the transplantation process.

Despite its advantages, the use of PTFE in LSC transplantation has limitations. The cost of PTFE scaffolds remains relatively high, and the reluctance of patients—particularly younger individuals—to undergo long-term immunosuppressive treatments presents a significant challenge. The quality and long-term viability of LSC sheets can be influenced by multiple factors, including donor characteristics and the in vitro culture environment. While our culture procedures adhered to GMP protocols and utilized standardized methods for cell expansion, detailed donor-specific data (e.g., age, comprehensive health status) were not systematically collected beyond basic suitability assessments, due to the nature of family donations in this compassionate-use clinical trial. This omission may contribute to some of the observed variability in patient outcomes.

However, a critical factor influencing the variability in outcomes, particularly at the 6-month follow-up, was the observed patient non-adherence to systemic immunosuppressive therapy, many patients discontinued immunosuppression prematurely due to concerns about side effects and impact on daily life. This likely played a substantial role in the regression of visual acuity and persistent symptoms in the longer term, dominating potential influences from subtle donor-specific biological differences. While our short-term results were promising, showing modest short-term improvements in visual acuity and a temporary stabilization of the corneal surface condition, long-term outcomes require further investigation, with follow-up studies extending beyond two years to assess potential complications [24]. Future larger-scale studies would benefit from more comprehensive donor characterization, alongside robust patient education and support for long-term adherence to post-transplant regimens, to better understand and mitigate factors contributing to outcome variability.

Moreover, incorporating additional differentiation markers like K12 would offer further insights, and this will be a key consideration for future, larger-scale studies with expanded in vitro characterization components.

The promising short-term outcomes of this technique, including the preservation of LSC architecture, the elimination of enzymatic treatments, and the preparation process, suggested that the LSC sheet bandage could play a potential role in treating patients with end-stage LSCD. This method seems to be particularly beneficial for patients whose corneal surfaces have deteriorated due to extensive prior treatments, rendering them ineligible for traditional transplants. By acting as a temporary cellular bandage, the PTFE-LSC composite initially promotes corneal surface re-epithelialization, providing a window for potential subsequent therapeutic interventions, although long-term improvement may be limited by limbal stem cell failure.

Conclusion

This study introduced a novel, rapid, and efficient bottom-up approach for transferring LSC sheets in corneal tissue engineering, utilizing PTFE as a scaffold. The LSC sheet bandage, which acts as a cellular bandage for the corneal surface, demonstrated its potential as a short-term solution for regenerating the corneal epithelium in patients with end-stage LSCD. By preserving the cells’ natural architecture without the need for enzymatic treatment, this method enables swift preparation and application, making it particularly useful for emergency cases and patients ineligible for traditional transplants.

The favorable short-term outcomes were temporary improvements in visual acuity and corneal epithelialization within the first month after transplantation. However, these improvements were not sustained at 6 months due to limbal stem cell failure. Acting as a cellular bandage, this technique not only accelerates corneal surface regeneration but also prepares the eye for subsequent therapeutic procedures, making it a potential interim solution for complex cases.

Future research should explore the potential to further enhance this cell sheet bandage by integrating growth factors and exosomes, optimizing both short-term and long-term outcomes for LSCD patients. This innovative approach could serve as a critical advancement in corneal regeneration therapies, especially in cases where conventional options are no longer viable.
