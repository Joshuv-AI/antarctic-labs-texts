Allocative efficiency of opioid overdose prevention strategies for people incarcerated in New Jersey
Authors: Joy D. Scheidell, Jillian M. Frechette, Tarlise N. Townsend, Qinlian Zhou, Prima Manandhar-Sasaki, Herbert Kaldany, Krista L. Connelly, Adam P. Cortes, Jasmine I.-Shin Su, Dyanna L. Charles, R. Scott Braithwaite
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople with opioid use disorder (POUD) who are incarcerated are disproportionately impacted by the overdose crisis. We sought to identify overdose policies that allocate resources with maximal efficiency to reduce mortality among POUD in the New Jersey (NJ) Department of Corrections.Main outcomes/measuresWe created a probabilistic state-transition model of a simulated cohort of POUD incarcerated in NJ to simulate maximizing medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD) during incarceration and/or post-release in the community and naloxone in the community. We estimated how maximizing each intervention individually and in combinations compared to current provision would impact five-year overdose deaths (ODDs), life-years (LYs), and quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) among the simulated cohort, who moved between different modeled settings and opioid use statuses. Inputs were derived from literature reviews and expert opinion. Costs were in 2021 USD, employing a health sector perspective in base-case analyses and a limited societal perspective in sensitivity analyses, a 3% discount rate, cost-effectiveness criterion of ≤ $100,000/QALY, and life-year and lifetime horizons.ResultsAt status quo, 141 five-year ODDs will occur in the cohort (n = 2,592), and the cohort will live an average of 17.0 discounted LYs, experiencing 13.3 discounted QALYs.Evaluating interventions individually compared to status quo, maximizing MOUD in incarceration prevents 14 five-year ODDs, adds 0.2 LYs, 0.3 QALYs per-person at a favorable incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER; $34,000/QALY). Maximizing MOUD in the community prevents 40 five-year ODDs, adds 0.9 LYs, 1.1 QALYs at a favorable ICER ($25,000/QALY). Maximizing naloxone prevents 24 five-year ODDs, adds 0.3 LYs, 0.2 QALYs at a favorable ICER ($17,000/QALY). Comparing all combinations of interventions to status quo and each other, the most beneficial combination meeting cost-effectiveness criterion was jointly maximizing community MOUD and naloxone (ICER $25,000/QALY), preventing 56 five-year ODDs, adding 1.2 LYs, 1.3 QALYs. In sensitivity analyses using a limited societal perspective, all interventions were cost-saving. Maximizing all interventions was both most beneficial (42% reduction in death) and cost-saving ($300,000 per capita) over the cohort lifetime.ConclusionMaximizing MOUD and community naloxone in New Jersey can reduce five-year ODDs by 40%. Considering societal cost-savings, maximizing all three also saves money.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12954-025-01327-4.

Introduction

Since 1999, approximately half a million people have died from an opioid-related overdose, and in 2020, over 70% of overdose deaths (ODDs) involved opioids [1]. The crisis continues to evolve, with prescription opioid and heroin ODDs recently decreasing, while synthetic opioid-involved deaths have increased [2]. New Jersey (NJ) is one of few states successful in flattening overdose rates [3]. Disproportionately affected by opioid use disorder (OUD) and overdose, people who have been incarcerated are a priority population. Criminalization of drug use resulted in skyrocketing numbers of people incarcerated in the US over the past 40 years [4]. OUD rates are ten times higher among people who have been incarcerated than the general population, [5] and previously incarcerated people account for up to 50% of ODDs [6, 7]. Risk of opioid-involved ODDs are particularly high upon return to the community after incarceration for reasons including reduced tolerance and increased likelihood of solitary usage [8–15].

Since criminal justice settings provide important potential to address OUD and reduce overdose risk, NJ has been expanding OUD services in carceral settings [16]. Studies assessing medications for OUD (MOUD) provision during incarceration find reduced relapse and recidivism risk after release [17, 18]. A limited body of evidence also suggests reductions in post-release overdose risk [19, 20], potentially halving the odds of post-release overdose death[19, 21]. Simulation modeling indicates expanding MOUD in carceral settings could reduce ODDs among people who have been incarcerated [22, 23] and is cost-effective for reducing recidivism and relapse [24, 25], although cost-effectiveness related to other outcomes including life expectancy and overdose mortality has not been thoroughly examined [26].

Considering up to two-thirds of people with OUD (POUD) cycle through jails each year [27] and approximately 95% of people in prisons are ultimately released [28], a key component of preventing overdose among people who have been incarcerated is treatment continuity and access to harm reduction interventions. NJ has been expanding community services [29], including increasing naloxone distribution to re-entry programs [30]. People starting MOUD during incarceration are more likely to engage in treatment upon release [17, 31, 32], but community treatment rates are relatively low [17, 31, 33] and POUD returning to the community face substantial treatment barriers [34–37]. Access to community-based treatment may be especially salient for people incarcerated in NJ, where recent bail policy reforms have reduced the length of detainment, and community MOUD utilization is low relative to overdose rate [38].

Observational and simulation studies show that expanded provision of naloxone, the antidote for reversing opioid overdose, is associated with reduced overdose mortality of up to 40% [39–43] and cost-effective [44–47]. Ensuring naloxone availability in communities and carceral settings [47, 48] may be especially important for reducing overdose mortality for people with incarceration history due to increased likelihood of observing an overdose, having naloxone administration training, and giving naloxone to someone experiencing an overdose [49].

The aim of this study was to identify the overdose prevention policies options in NJ with the potential to reduce the greatest benefit (i.e., reduction in overdose deaths) given resource constraints, using an allocative efficiency analysis. Allocative efficiency analyses can be thought of as cost-effectiveness analyses applied at-scale to interventions with a particular common goal (e.g., ODD reduction), and are particularly useful when resources are siloed, disbursed by organizations with different budgets and missions, as siloes can lead to population-level inefficiencies [50] (e.g. more ODDs).

In conjunction with NJ Department of Corrections (NJDOC), we developed a microsimulation to evaluate individual and joint impacts of maximizing three interventions on life expectancy and ODDs.

Methods

Description of policy options

We compared maximally scaling beyond current levels for three opioid overdose prevention policy options: a) MOUD provision during incarceration (MOUD-INC); b) community MOUD provision (MOUD-COM); and c) community naloxone distribution (NLX). We compared maximizing each policy option individually or in combination to each other and to status quo, i.e., current levels of treatment and overdose prevention. Current levels are specified as follows: approximately 40% of community-dwelling POUD use MOUD; 10–40% receive naloxone regularly, with rates highest among people who inject drugs (PWID) using syringe service programs (SSPs; see Appendix). We estimated probabilities of receiving MOUD during incarceration: 1% if previously not on treatment nor in remission (-MOUD-remission), with remission defined as absence of non-medical opioid use; 30% if previously not on treatment, in remission (-MOUD + remission); 43% if previously on treatment, regardless of remission status (+ MOUD ± remission).

MOUD options are specifiable proportions of methadone, buprenorphine-naloxone, extended-release naltrexone, and extended-release buprenorphine, assumed constant during maximization (Table 1; Table A5). Benefit differs by MOUD type based on adherence likelihood (Table 1; Appendix). Naloxone distribution options are varying proportions of intranasal and intramuscular forms, kept constant during maximization. Model structure

Research aims, model structure, and parameters were guided by consultation with key NJDOC stakeholders. When possible, we used literature-based estimates or data directly from NJDOC to define model inputs. When this was not available, we made evidence-informed assumptions in consultation with expert partners at NJDOC, Connecticut DOC, and Connecticut Department of Public Health, arriving at consensus before including these assumptions in the model. We used a probabilistic state-transition model similar to ours described elsewhere [62] to simulate a hypothetical cohort of POUD incarcerated in NJDOC to compare each option’s impact on per-person life expectancy (i.e., life-years), per-person quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), number and percentage of ODDs prevented over five years, and total costs. We calculated the cost-effectiveness of each policy option, i.e., the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER): incremental costs divided by incremental benefits, measured in costs per QALY. We compared each possible option by identifying an efficient frontier comprised of options delivering greatest incremental benefit per incremental cost.

Health states were defined as follows: not using opioids without treatment (-MOUD + remission), using opioids without treatment (-MOUD-remission), or on treatment (+ MOUD ± remission). For model simplicity we assumed: 1) during incarceration, individuals cannot access non-prescription opioids and therefore are only on treatment (+ MOUD + remission) or not on treatment (-MOUD + remission); 2) transitions between these states are rare. Upon release from incarceration, individuals transition between any of the three above health states. The model enables varying levels of community MOUD; higher availability increases chances that an individual receives MOUD. For those not in remission (± MOUD), overdoses are possible. Increasing community naloxone distribution does not impact overdose but decreases probability an overdose is fatal. Individuals may die of causes other than overdose in both settings (Appendix).

We used 2020 NJ DOC data concerning the number of people incarcerated in the system (jails and prisons) who had a diagnosis of OUD to establish a simulated cohort of 2,592 POUD, all of whom begin the model incarcerated. Each hypothetical person begins with a starting age, sex, and use status. Simulated individuals then live a virtual life, potentially transitioning between settings and changing OUD statuses, until death. The technical appendix describes simulations in detail.

Within the simulation, an “overdose” event occurs as rapid onset loss of consciousness due to opioids from which arousal is difficult or impossible, and which may lead to death. Overdose rates are assumed to be higher for PWID and people with overdose history (Table 1), while POUD receiving treatment (i.e., + MOUD) experience lower overdose rates. Overdose fatality probability ranges from 1–10% (Appendix).

We assumed 30% of POUD in NJDOC are PWID, based on expert estimation consistent with national data [52, 63]. Compared to non-PWID, PWID have higher overdose rate [44] and are more likely to relapse [64]. Based on Connecticut PWID stakeholder input, we assumed familiarity with needles gives PWID higher probability of successful IM naloxone administration compared to non-PWID.

The model was calibrated by comparing model-produced versus reported 2012–2020 ODDs ([65]; Appendix; Fig. 2). We conducted simulations for 100,000 individuals to reduce random variations before scaling ODDs to the cohort size. The model was developed using Microsoft Visual Studio Community 2022. Code was written in C/C + + . Model rates, costs, and utilities

Model input parameters were derived from literature, community organizations, and expert opinions and are similar to those used in our previous work [62] (Table 1; Appendix Tables). Transition rates between treatment and opioid use statuses in the community were estimated from literature and adjusted so the modeled proportion of POUD receiving MOUD matched community MOUD coverage (see Appendix).

To capture real-world heterogeneity among POUD, we divided the modeled population into higher- and lower-relapse propensity subgroups, with correspondingly differential transition rates to remission. Based on the literature, transition rates from + MOUD states varied by MOUD type (Appendix A3, Table A3). Finally, we modeled the tenfold higher relapse rate in the month post-release [8, 66, 67, 68].

MOUD type during incarceration was determined by NJDOC’s current distribution (Appendix A5). We made the simplifying assumption that all transitions happen at the beginning of incarceration with no change of status and/or MOUD type during incarceration. Utilities were derived from literature and expert opinions. We assumed a 0.82 baseline utility for people + MOUD or -MOUD + remission [57] (Table 1) and a standard willingness-to-pay threshold for US cost-effective analyses of $100,000/QALY [69].

Modeled scenarios

“Maximizing MOUD-INC” results in all POUD incarcerated receiving MOUD regardless of prior OUD status. “Maximizing MOUD-COM” increases MOUD coverage, (i.e., initiation and retention), to reach nearly all (80%) community-dwelling POUD not in remission, compared to current 25% coverage. In base-case analyses, “maximizing NLX” results in 100% of community-dwelling POUD receiving naloxone at least annually, increased from current levels described above. Because people in NJ may not have access to their preferred MOUD, MOUD type re-assignment was possible every time individuals initiated/reinitiated MOUD in the community, upon incarceration, and upon release.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted one-way and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA). In one-way sensitivity analysis, we tested the effect of variations in key inputs on ODDs averted by maximizing MOUD-COM and NLX. In PSAs, values were randomly drawn from specified distributions around each input (Table 1) for 10,000 simulations. Simulation results were used to generate cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs) demonstrating the probability of each option’s cost-effectiveness at different willingness-to-pay thresholds.

Additionally, we tested assumptions regarding “saturation,” distribution levels where additional supply would not induce additional demand. Based on naloxone saturation estimates [70], “maximizing NLX” stipulates 67% of community-dwelling POUD receive naloxone at least annually, while “maximizing MOUD-COM” stipulates approximately 64% of -remission community-dwelling POUD receive MOUD, based on expert opinion. Finally, we examined what would happen if MOUD and naloxone were not available at all.

Sensitivity analyses

Applying a limited societal perspective transformed results; all maximized options become cost-saving. Maximizing all interventions simultaneously saved society $300,000 per capita (Fig. 3) while reducing lifetime ODDs 42%.

Applying saturation assumptions to NLX and MOUD-COM resulted in more modest benefits; however, simultaneously maximizing all three interventions was now on the efficient frontier, averting 42 five-year ODDs (30% reduction) and adding 0.7 LYs and 0.7 QALYs, at a favorable ICER ($71,000/QALY).

In analyses where MOUD and naloxone were not available at all, we found that an additional 54 deaths would have occurred over the next 5 years, a 39% increase from status quo, and the cohort would live an average of 1.1 fewer LYs, accumulating 1.2 fewer QALYs, suggesting NJ has already made substantial progress.

In one-way sensitivity analyses, estimates for ODDs averted were generally robust, including to uncertainty surrounding reincarceration rates, overdose among PWID, and proportion of POUD with history of overdose or incarceration (Fig. 5a). Discussion

The primary goal of this study was to estimate the impact of various overdose prevention policy choices on the lives and costs saved in a simulated cohort of POUD incarcerated in NJ. We found that opioid ODDs could be reduced by 40% over five years through maximizing community MOUD and naloxone provision, while maintaining MOUD provision in carceral settings. Considering a limited range of societal costs outside healthcare and public health sectors, additionally maximizing MOUD during incarceration would be cost-saving due to positive effects that treating OUD has on productivity and crime-related costs. People involved in the criminal legal system are a priority population for opioid overdose prevention and these findings highlight maximizing access to existing evidence-based treatment and harm reduction approaches has the potential to substantially impact the overdose crisis.

NJ is among the few states that have seen relatively stable opioid overdose mortality rates recently [3], and NJDOC has prioritized evidence-based treatment provision for OUD for people who are incarcerated. Indeed, we found that just by maximizing MOUD during incarceration, ODDs could be reduced by 10% among POUD incarcerated in NJ. Our findings align with other studies [20, 26]. For example, a retrospective cohort study in Rhode Island (RI) found ODDs were approximately 12% lower among people who had been incarcerated following implementation of a new OUD screening and MOUD protocol during incarceration [20]. NJ and RI are two states exemplifying treatment models providing the full range of MOUD to individuals during incarceration and upon release [71]. It will be important to consider variation in carceral setting as research on OUD treatment during incarceration expands to other jurisdictions. NJ and therefore our model use both jails and prisons, while RI uses a unified system. Our results do not examine in which setting (e.g., jail versus prison) maximizing MOUD is likely to have the greatest effect, and each setting likely has key barriers and facilitators [72]. For example, there may be difficulty initiating MOUD in jail where detainment duration is typically brief, particularly in settings with bail reform.

Our study is one of the few allocative efficiency analyses informing intervention approaches for POUD [73]. Our results suggest OUD treatment and harm reduction programs deliver favorable value (health-benefit-per-cost) as currently funded, and that greater resource allocations would avert additional overdoses at high value. In sensitivity analyses considering societal (crime and productivity loss) costs, additionally scaling interventions led to great cost-savings. Our allocative efficiency analyses yield the surprising insight that expanding community naloxone and MOUD are extremely efficient ways to improve the health of POUD over their lifespan because they cycle frequently through re- and de-incarceration. These findings reinforce concern regarding MOUD continuity upon release, including lack of linkage to care and returning to communities with low MOUD capacity [38].

The National Institutes of Health and the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Administration set a goal of reducing opioid mortality 40% by 2025 [74]. We estimated a 40% reduction in ODDs among people incarcerated in NJ is achievable within five years by maximizing community MOUD and naloxone coverage and preserving recent gains in MOUD levels in carceral settings. While this would be substantial progress towards achieving the goal within a priority population, our results suggest additional approaches are needed to further reduce mortality among people who are incarcerated and POUD overall. Previous studies also found no single intervention approach sufficient and multiple high-impact interventions acting on both preventing OUD and reducing overdose are critical [75, 76]. In a simulation model among people who use opioids in the US comparing 11 interventions, the three interventions that saved the most lives over ten years were addressing fentanyl-related overdose, increasing naloxone distribution, and supporting those in remission [75, 76]. These approaches may be especially relevant for people who experienced incarceration, although they may be potentially challenging to implement. Harm reduction strategies addressing fentanyl may not be accessible for people with incarceration history who may avoid SSPs or carrying items potentially considered drug paraphernalia, including fentanyl test strips or naloxone [77, 78]. Moreover, while research has shown the importance of social networks and support for reducing overdose risk [79], incarceration itself detrimentally impacts social support [80, 81]. In sum, our study adds to evidence that we can significantly reduce overdose mortality using available effective interventions and reinforces the need to continue to develop and implement interventions targeting the wide range of factors contributing to overdose risk among people who have been incarcerated [12].

We found that for both shorter- and longer-term impact (i.e., ODDs and QALYs, respectively), maximizing MOUD is the most effective strategy. While MOUD offered during incarceration has more modest population-level impacts, continuity of care between settings is important. In the long term, interaction and collaboration between community- and carceral-based interventions, such as the pilot peer health navigation program now offered by New Jersey [82] may counteract inefficiencies caused by funding silos.

There are important limitations to this study. For model parsimony, some assumptions do not reflect the reality of opioid use and overdose. For example, we assumed during incarceration that people are not using opioids and cannot overdose, despite real-life occurrences [83], and therefore our model is underestimating the number of potential overdose deaths. Our categorizations of relapse risk did not change although many factors influence risk of return to use [84, 85]. Because adherence to MOUD is a primary factor in its overall effectiveness, we made the simplifying assumption that differential adherence was the only source of differentiation in effectiveness, which does not account for differences in effectiveness once adherence is obtained; future work should incorporate differential effect sizes. Additionally, some data around POUD, a highly stigmatized and criminalized population, are difficult to collect and were based on expert estimation; similarly, we have conducted our analyses at a population level which does not account for potential subgroup differences, although we do incorporate a high- and low-relapse propensity group for approximation. Moreover, we assumed optimistic “maximization” of overdose prevention strategies, yet these may not be possible in the real world. For example, many people with OUD, including in carceral settings, may not want to initiate MOUD or have particular MOUD preferences [86–89] or may otherwise face barriers to access that would limit full scale-up including stigma, lack of provider knowledge, or policy restrictions [90–93] that will need to be overcome to increase access. Importantly, some of these can be overcome with additional resource investment, such as insurance-based barriers [92, 94], however, especially from a harm reduction approach, it is critical to maximize individual’s choices regarding MOUD, including whether they want it and the type used. A recent modeling study found that almost all states have insufficient naloxone access to meet saturation goals (i.e., naloxone present at 80% of witnessed overdoses) and reaching 100% naloxone coverage may be unlikely [79]. However, our analyses are robust to the application of more realistic “saturation” estimates of coverage. Overall, our findings should be interpreted as estimates of potential benefits in optimal uptake and coverage scenarios and future work is needed to explore the real-world strategies that carceral settings as well as drug treatment and harm reduction services can implement to address barriers.

The generalizability of findings may be limited because simulations are from a modest cohort in a specific geographic area. Notably, because NJ has already enhanced MOUD in carceral settings, the incremental benefit of maximizing carceral MOUD is smaller than would be true for settings not yet similarly expanding MOUD. Finally, providing an opportunity for further investigation, data were insufficient for stratification by race/ethnicity to perform distributional cost-effectiveness analysis to assess equity alongside efficiency [95–97].

In conclusion, the promising progress NJ has made in curtailing the opioid overdose crisis may be bolstered among people who have experienced incarceration through expanding MOUD and naloxone access in the community while continuing MOUD during incarceration. Although our modeled interventions of full-scale maximization of MOUD and naloxone represent an ideal scenario that does not necessarily reflect real-world constraints such as individual treatment access and preference, provider capacity, and stigma, our findings highlight that addressing these barriers to overdose prevention interventions would ultimately be cost saving to society considering impacts on crime and productivity, and achievable under realistic budget constraints.
