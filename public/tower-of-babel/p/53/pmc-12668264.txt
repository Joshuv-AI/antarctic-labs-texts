Harsh parenting in adolescence and symptoms of depression in emerging adulthood: The moderating role of sleep
Authors: Ryan J. Kelly, Morgan J. Thompson, Mona El-Sheikh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Harsh parenting occurs often in adolescence and confers risk for depression symptoms. However, individual differences exist and explication of variables that exacerbate or attenuate risk is needed. Sleep problems impair coping abilities and may magnify the effects of harsh parenting. Using a 5-wave design spanning 9 years, we investigated adolescents’ sleep as a moderator of relations between harsh parenting in adolescence and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood.Methods:Families participated over 5 waves (child’s M age at each wave was 16, 17, 18, 23, and 25 years; 245 families participated at age 16 [52% female; 67% White/European American, 33% Black/African American]; 132 families participated at all 5 waves). Mothers and fathers reported on the frequency of their own harsh parenting (verbal and physical) at ages 16–18. Actigraphy measured adolescents’ sleep duration (minutes) and quality (sleep maintenance efficiency, long wake episodes) at ages 16–18. Depression symptoms were assessed with self-reports at all waves.Results:After controlling for autoregressive effects, structural equation models revealed that shorter sleep duration (β = −0.16, p = < .03), reduced sleep maintenance efficiency (β = −0.30, p = < .001), and more long wake episodes (β = 0.24, p = .004) in adolescence exacerbated relations between harsh fathering in adolescence and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood. Harsh mothering did not confer risk, regardless of adolescents’ sleep.Conclusions:Interactions between harsh parenting and sleep problems in adolescence may predict depression symptoms in emerging adulthood. The results highlight the importance of considering sleep in attempts to improve mental health in those exposed to harsh parenting.

Harsh parenting (i.e., coercive behavior and negative emotional expression directed toward children by parents1) is a form of family risk that is prevalent in adolescence.2 Studies have investigated the mental health sequelae of harsh parenting in adolescence and associations with depression symptoms are well-documented.3,4 However, questions remain about whether adolescents who experience harsh parenting continue to have depression symptoms in later life stages. Further, the relationship between harsh parenting and depression symptoms is not uniform across all individuals and identifying factors that exacerbate or attenuate risk is needed.5 Investigations of sleep in the family context have proliferated6 and evidence indicates that adolescents’ sleep may influence the extent to which different forms of family risk (e.g., family stress) compromise mental health.7 Extending this literature, we used a rigorous 5-wave design spanning 9 years to examine whether harsh mothering and fathering (verbal and physical aggression) in adolescence predicted depression symptoms in emerging adulthood. Additionally, we examined whether adolescents’ sleep moderated these relationships.

Emerging adulthood is a unique developmental period.8 Depression symptoms are common during this life stage and contribute to significant impairment and distress.9 Accordingly, identifying the antecedents of depression in emerging adulthood is critical. A small but growing literature illustrates that various forms of family risk in youth (e.g., neglect, parental substance use) predicts symptoms of depression in subsequent developmental periods including emerging adulthood.5 Building on this research, we investigated the influence of harsh parenting in adolescence on depression symptoms in emerging adulthood.

Adolescence often involves an imbalance between adolescents’ growing desire for autonomy and parents’ continued desire for control, leading to incidents of harsh parenting.10 Harsh parenting is stressful and contributes to rumination and emotion dysregulation, both of which elevate risk for subsequent depression symptoms.11,12 Prospective studies demonstrate that harsh parenting predicts symptoms of depression over time in adolescence.3,4 Consistent with enduring effects models,13 adolescents may struggle to cope with harsh parenting over extended periods, resulting in depression symptoms in later life stages. Additional longitudinal work is needed to clarify these developmental effects.

In addition to direct links between harsh parenting and depression, identifying factors that modify risk are needed to understand individual differences.5 Adolescents’ sleep problems (e.g., short duration, poor quality) may play a role. A developmental ecological systems perspective adapted for youths’ sleep proposes that family-level variables interact with sleep to affect mental health.6,14,15 These interactions can be understood through multiplicative risk and diathesis stress models. In multiplicative risk, one form of risk (e.g., harsh parenting) interacts with another (e.g., sleep problems) to increase the likelihood of poor outcomes (e.g., depression).16 Similarly, diathesis stress occurs when the negative influence of one condition is strengthened by the presence of another.17 Neurologically, sleep problems may compromise cortical processing in the insula and cingulate cortex, reducing prefrontal regulation of the amygdala.18,19 These impairments increase emotional dysregulation, hindering abilities to cope with family risk.20,21 This is particularly relevant during adolescence because brain regions involved with emotion processing undergo rapid development.20

Multiple studies illustrate that adolescents’ sleep problems exacerbate the impact of family risk and depression symptoms. For example, greater exposure to family-related stressful life events (e.g., parent lost their job) was more strongly related to higher levels of depression symptoms among adolescents with lower actigraphy-derived sleep efficiency (compared with those with higher sleep efficiency).7 Similar patterns have been observed with regard to the concurrent influence that maternal psychological control and parental marital conflict have on adolescents’ internalizing symptoms (including depression symptoms), such that actigraphy-derived sleep problems served as moderators and exacerbated risk.22,23 We extended this literature by investigating whether interactions between harsh parenting and sleep in adolescence predict depression symptoms in emerging adulthood.

Most research on harsh parenting has focused on mothers.4,24 We considered both mothers’ and fathers’ harsh parenting. Although harsh mothering occurs more, harsh fathering may be more intense and distressing, with greater ramifications for children’s development.12,25 However, null effects have also been reported.26 Further examination of differences in the effects of mothers’ and fathers’ harsh parenting is warranted.

Current study

We used a five-wave design spanning 9 years to track a community sample of youth and their families from adolescence to emerging adulthood. We examined adolescents’ (ages 16, 17, and 18) sleep as a moderator of relations between harsh mothering and fathering during adolescence and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood (ages 23 and 25). We assessed established indicators of sleep duration (minutes) and quality (sleep maintenance efficiency, long wake episodes).27 We included an autoregressive path between adolescence and emerging adulthood depression, which allowed for the prediction of residualized change in depression. Anxiety symptoms in emerging adulthood were also covaried, as depression and anxiety are commonly associated yet represent distinct aspects of mental health.28,29 Covarying anxiety symptoms is crucial in investigations aiming to identify unique predictors of depression symptoms.

Based on prior research showing that sleep problems moderate the influence of family risk,7,22,23 we hypothesized that sleep problems in adolescence would exacerbate the relationship between harsh parenting in adolescence and symptoms of depression in emerging adulthood. We conducted exploratory analyses to examine whether the effects of harsh fathering were stronger than harsh mothering.

Methods

Participants

The study is part of the Family Stress and Youth Development Study, an ongoing examination of development in the context of the family from childhood to emerging adulthood. Waves 4–8 were used (collected between 2012 and 2022) and are referenced by youths’ ages (16-, 17-, 18-, 23-, and 25-years-old) (see Fig. 1). At the initial wave (2005–2006), 251 families were recruited through three public schools in semirural Alabama. Letters inviting families to participate were sent home with children in second and third grades, and interested parents were asked to call our lab for screening to determine eligibility. Inclusion criteria at recruitment required that children reside in two-parent homes in which parents lived together ≥2 years. Exclusion criteria included children having ADHD, learning disability, or chronic illness; these criteria were implemented to reduce potential confounds in the larger study yet were not overly conservative. Due to attrition from the original cohort recruited at wave 1, 53 new families were recruited from the same school district at wave 4 (age 16); these families did not differ on demographic or primary study variables compared with the wave 1 sample.

At age 16, the analytical sample included adolescents and their parent(s) with data for ≥ one primary variable (n = 245 families). Adolescents (Mage = 15.74 years; SD = 9.33 months; 52% female [sex assigned at birth]) were from diverse racial/ethnic (67% non-Hispanic White/European American [EA], 33% non-Hispanic Black/African American [AA]) and socioeconomic backgrounds (SES). SES was measured by income-to-needs ratio—the quotient of total income divided by the federal poverty threshold specific to family size.30 A score < 1 is considered below the poverty line, between 1 and 2 is considered near the poverty line, between 2 and 3 is considered lower-middle class, a score between 2 and 3 is considered middle class, and scores > 4 are considered higher income. On average, families were lower-middle class (M = 2.40, SD = 1.30; range = 0.14–6.72).

Most adolescents lived with their biological mother and father at age 16 (57%). An additional 21% of adolescents were from two-parent households but were not biologically related to at least one parent (biological mother and step-father [18%], extended family [2%], and step-mother and biological father [1%]). The remainder resided with their unmarried biological parent(s) (22%), their unpartnered biological mother (10%), their biological mother and boyfriend (9%), their unpartnered biological father (3%), and those with other statuses (e.g., aunt) (1%).a Single-mother status and single-father status were covaried. For simplicity, we refer to children’s primary caregivers as parents (Mothers’ Mage = 41.76, SD = 6.25; Fathers’ Mage = 43.55, SD = 7.49). Two couples who identified as lesbian were excluded given the focus on gender-related effects of harsh parenting.

One year later (M = 366 days, SD = 27), 222 families participated when youth were 17 (88% retained from age 16) (55% female; Mage = 16.79 years, SD = 9.32 months; 67% EA, 33% AA). Seven families who did not participate at age 16 but participated in earlier waves, were part of the analytical sample at age 17.

Nearly 1 year later (M = 349 days, SD = 20), 214 families participated when adolescents were 18 (55% female; Mage = 17.67 years, SD = 8.84 months; 70% EA, 30% AA). Retention was high from age 16 (85%) and age 17 (94%). Three families from the broader investigation that did not participate at age 16 nor 17 returned at age 18. Most adolescents (93%) lived with their parent(s).

Five years later (M = 5 years, SD = 6 months), 194 individuals returned at age 23 (74%, 77%, and 80% retention from ages 16, 17, and 18, respectively). Due to attrition, 46 new individuals were recruited at age 23.b In longitudinal studies, new participants are commonly recruited after the initial wave. This approach includes benefits such as overcoming decreased statistical power related to attrition.31 The new cohort resided in the same region and identical inclusion/exclusion criteria were used. Overall, 240 individuals participated (58% female; Mage = 22.98 years, SD = 9.80 months; 69% EA, 28% AA, and 3% mixed race/ethnicity). Individuals recruited at age 23 did not differ on demographic or primary study variables compared with those recruited earlier.

Nearly 2 years later (M = 684 days, SD = 26), 204 individuals returned (60% female; Mage = 24.88 years, SD = 9.60 months; 70% EA, 27% AA, and 3% mixed race/ethnicity) (63%, 66%, 69%, and 83% retained from ages 16, 17, 18, and 23, respectively); 6% resided with their parent(s). Further, 23% were married and 41% had dating partners. Participants who dropped from the study did not differ from the age 16 sample on study variables.

Procedures

At ages 16, 17, and 18, adolescents wore actigraphs on their nondominant wrists for seven consecutive nights. Actigraphy occurred during the regular school year, excluding holidays. Sleep diaries were used to cross-check actigraphy data. In most cases, families visited our on-campus lab to complete questionnaires shortly following actigraphy assessments (across waves, M = 8.55 days, SD = 8.69). At ages 23 and 25, emerging adults completed questionnaires in the lab or online. The university’s institutional review board approved the study. Consent and assent (when applicable) were obtained prior to participation. Participants were compensated monetarily for their time after each wave.

Measures

Harsh parenting

At ages 16, 17, and 18, parents self-reported on their own harsh parenting in the past year using the Parent-Child Conflict Tactics Scale.32 This measure has demonstrated good psychometric properties.32 Parents completed the Verbal Aggression (5 items; Swore or cursed at him/her) and Physical Aggression scales (7 items; Slapped him/her on the hand, arm, or leg). Response choices ranged from 0 (this has not happened) to 6 (more than 20 times). At each wave, items from the scales were summed together.1 Scale scores could range from 0–72. For mothers and fathers, αs ranged from 0.73 and 0.84 across the waves.

Sleep actigraphy

At ages 16, 17, and 18, sleep was assessed using Octagonal Basic Motionloggers (Ambulatory Monitoring). Data were analyzed using Action W2 software and Sadeh’s scoring algorithm.33 Established sleep parameters were assessed: sleep minutes (minutes spent sleeping between falling asleep and wake time), sleep maintenance efficiency (% of minutes spent sleeping between falling asleep and waking up), and long wake episodes (number of wake episodes ≥5 minutes); these definitions are from the user’s manual (Ambulatory Monitoring Inc, Ardsley, NY). Actigraphy data were included for those with ≥3 nights27,34; cases that had < 3 nights were treated as missing (0%–12% of adolescents across waves). At each wave, the average across available nights was used.

Symptoms of depression

At ages 16, 17, and 18, adolescents completed the 27-item Children’s Depression Inventory, which is valid and reliable for adolescence.35 One item related to suicidal ideation was not administered, and two sleep-related items were excluded. Depression symptoms were measured over the past 2 weeks on a three-point scale (e.g., 0 [Things will work out for me O.K.] to 2 [Nothing will ever workout for me]). Responses were summed. Scale scores could range from 0–48. Internal consistency was good across waves (αs = 0.88–0.89).

Depression in adolescence may differ compared with emerging adulthood and employing different measurement tools across these developmental periods is appropriate.36 At ages 23–25, individuals completed the 20-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale, which has excellent psychometric properties.37 Symptoms of depression were measured over the past week on a 3-point scale ranging from 0 (rarely) to 3 (most of the time). Scale scores could range from 0–60. Internal consistency was good across waves (αs = 0.85 at age 23 and 0.90 at age 25).

Symptoms of anxiety

At ages 23 and 25, individuals completed the 21-item Beck Anxiety Inventory, which has good psychometric properties.38 Participants reported on experiencing common anxiety symptoms over the past month using a 4-point scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (severely). Scale scores could range from 0–84. This measure reliably discriminates anxiety from depression39; αs = 0.93 at age 23 and 0.95 at age 25.

Plan of analysis

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to examine whether adolescents’ sleep moderated relations between harsh parenting in adolescence and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood. Harsh mothering was stable across ages 16–18 (rs = 0.65–0.69) and loaded on a latent construct representing harsh mothering in adolescence. Similarly, harsh fathering was stable across ages 16–18 (rs = 0.42–0.57) and loaded on a latent construct representing harsh fathering in adolescence. Sleep minutes (rs = 0.43–0.59), sleep maintenance efficiency (rs = 0.41–0.55), long wake episodes (rs = 0.40–0.54), and adolescent depression symptoms (rs = 0.59–0.71) were correlated across ages 16–18. For these variables, data from ages 16–18 loaded on latent constructs. Overall, primary variables in adolescence were harsh mothering, harsh fathering, sleep parameters, and depression symptoms. Emerging adult depression symptoms were measured using a similar approach; depression symptoms at ages 23–25 were correlated (r = 0.62) and loaded on a latent construct. For each latent construct, the repeated measures were treated as observed indicators of latent factors that captured between-person variability across the waves.40 The factor loadings for the repeated measures (shown in Figs. 2 and 3) surpassed acceptable cutoffs.41,42 The latent predictors captured the mean and between-person variance of each construct across the waves and helped reduce time-specific influence as measurement error.43

Multiple SEMs were fit. The first model examined the direct influence of harsh parenting and sleep in adolescence on symptoms of depression in emerging adulthood. Mothers’ and fathers’ harsh parenting were examined in the same model to allow for gender comparisons. Sleep parameters were included in the same model, which allowed for examining one sleep parameter while controlling for the others. Depression symptoms in adolescence were controlled; this approach helps reduce bias in parameter estimates and provides information about predicted change.44

Next, interaction terms were added. To create interaction terms, age 16–18 manifest indicators of harsh mothering, harsh fathering, and sleep were aggregated to create separate observed composites of each construct using data from participants who had available data from at least one of these time points. Composites were mean-centered. We multiplied harsh mothering and harsh fathering with each sleep composite, resulting in 6 interaction terms (2 harsh parenting composites × 3 sleep composites). Interaction terms were examined separately in consecutive models (see Figs. 2 and 3 for examples). Interactions were plotted at high and low (± 1 SD) levels of harsh parenting and sleep45 using Preacher’s online utility.46 The “regions of significance” was reported, specifying the range of the moderator where the simple slope differs from zero.

We controlled for known correlates of primary constructs including child gender (1 = male, 0 = female). As part of exploratory analyses, we also examined three-way interactions to explicate whether the influence of harsh parenting × adolescents’ sleep on depression symptoms in emerging adulthood varied by child gender; no gender-related differences emerged. However, child gender was still retained as a covariate. We also controlled for race/ethnicity (1 = EA, 0 = AA), SES at age 16, and anxiety symptoms in emerging adulthood; anxiety symptoms at ages 23–25 years were correlated (r = 0.55) and loaded on a latent construct. Single-mother status and single-father status age 16 were also covaried (1 = single, 0 = not single).

Models were fit using Amos 29. Participants had between 62% and 100% of data among primary variables across the waves. Full-information maximum likelihood (FIML) handled missing data; the amount of missingness was in the acceptable range for FIML. To minimize outlier effects, high-leverage values surpassing 3SDs were replaced at the highest observed value below 3SDs. Between 0 and 5 values were recoded for study variables. Skew was examined using visual observation and kurtosis and skewness statistics (± 2); harsh mothering at each wave in adolescence was skewed and log-transformed. In the SEMs, significantly associated exogenous variables were allowed to correlate. Paths between the covariates and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood were estimated. Multiple model fit indices were reported: χ2, χ2/df, CFI, and RMSEA; all models fit the data well.

Results

Preliminary analyses

In total, 132 families participated at all five waves (43% of all participating families), 48 participated at 4 waves (16%), 41 participated at 3 waves (13%), 63 participated at 2 waves (20%), and 24 participated at 1 wave (8%). Table 1 includes descriptive statistics for primary variables. Table 2 includes bivariate correlations. Across the three waves in adolescence, between 57% and 80% of mothers and 52%–64% of fathers reported using ≥ one form of harsh parenting. Mean comparisons were conducted to determine whether averages of primary variables differed across waves. Rates of harsh mothering were higher at age 16 than ages 17 (t[173] = 6.99, p < .001) and 18 (t[154] = 8.67, p < .001). Harsh fathering occurred more at age 16 than at ages 17 (t[118] = 3.48, p < .001) and 18 (t[118] = 4.15, p < .001). Regarding gender comparisons, harsh mothering was more prevalent than harsh fathering at age 16 (t[99] = 2.20, p = .03).

Direct influence of harsh parenting and sleep in adolescence on depression symptoms in emerging adulthood

An SEM was fit to examine the influence of harsh parenting and sleep in adolescence on depression symptoms in emerging adulthood, χ2(259) = 426.10, p = .001; χ2/df = 1.65; CFI = 0.96; RMSEA = 0.03 ns, 95% CI [0.02–0.04]. Of the covariates, depression symptoms in adolescence (B = 0.49, β = 0.29, p = .04) and anxiety symptoms in emerging adulthood (B = 0.82, β = 0.79, p < .001) were related to depression symptoms in emerging adulthood. Harsh mothering and harsh fathering were not related to depression symptoms in emerging adulthood.

Harsh mothering × sleep in adolescence and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood

Interactions were added to assess whether adolescents’ sleep moderated relations between harsh mothering in adolescence and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood. SEMs were fit to assess the moderating roles of sleep minutes (model fit: χ2[275] = 457.65, p = .001; χ2/df = 1.66; CFI = 0.94; RMSEA = 0.03 ns, 95% CI [0.03–0.04]), sleep maintenance efficiency (χ2[275] = 454.17, p = .001; χ2/df = 1.65; CFI = 0.95; RMSEA = 0.03 ns, 95% CI [0.02–0.04]), and long wake episodes (χ2[275] = 453.63, p < .001; χ2/df = 1.65; CFI = 0.95; RMSEA = 0.03 ns, 95% CI [0.03–0.04]) (models not depicted). No interactions emerged.

Harsh fathering × sleep in adolescence and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood

An SEM assessed sleep minutes as a moderator of relations between harsh fathering in adolescence and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood, χ2(276) = 445.19, p = .001; χ2/df = 1.61; CFI = 0.95; RMSEA = 0.03 ns, 95% CI [0.03–0.04] (Fig. 2). The interaction was significant. More harsh fathering forecasted higher levels of depression symptoms in emerging adulthood for those with fewer sleep minutes in adolescence (Fig. 4a; predicted M = 5.01 and 20.61 at lower and higher levels of harsh fathering, respectively). Based on the “regions of significance,” this association was significant for those with sleep minutes < 323.17 (n = 22). The simple slope was not significant for those with more sleep minutes in adolescence; these individuals had relatively average levels of depression symptoms regardless of harsh fathering (predicted 11.44 and 14.50 at lower and higher levels of harsh fathering, respectively).

An SEM was fit to examine sleep maintenance efficiency as a moderator of relations between harsh fathering in adolescence and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood, χ2(276) = 460.69, p = .001; χ2/df = 1.67; CFI = 0.95; RMSEA = 0.03 ns, 95% CI [0.03–0.04] (Fig. 3). The moderation effect was significant. Harsh fathering was related to depression symptoms in emerging adulthood for those with reduced sleep maintenance efficiency (Fig. 4b; predicted M = 8.81 and 18.20 at lower and higher levels of harsh fathering, respectively). This association was significant for those with sleep maintenance efficiency < 84.25 (n = 37). The simple slope was not significant for those with higher sleep maintenance efficiency who had relatively average levels of depression symptoms regardless of harsh fathering (predicted M = 12.40 and 11.34 at lower and higher levels of harsh fathering, respectively).

An SEM assessed whether long wake episodes moderated relations between harsh fathering in adolescence and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood, χ2(276) = 445.20, p = .001; χ2/df = 1.61; CFI = 0.95; RMSEA = 0.03 ns, 95% CI [0.03–0.04] (model not depicted). The interaction was significant (B = 0.46, β = 0.24, p = .004). Harsh fathering predicted more depression symptoms over time for those who had greater long wake episodes (Fig. 4c; predicted M = 9.50 and 17.50 at lower and higher levels of harsh fathering, respectively). The association was significant for those who experienced > 3.44 wake episodes (n = 49). The simple slope was nonsignificant for those with fewer wake episodes (predicted M = 12.03 and 12.30 at lower and higher levels of harsh fathering, respectively).c

Discussion

We examined whether adolescents’ sleep moderated relations between harsh parenting in adolescence and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood. We used a rigorous 5-wave design spanning 9 years and used actigraphy to measure sleep. Our investigation included mothers and fathers, which is uncommon in this literature.4,24 Shorter and poorer-quality sleep exacerbated relations between harsh fathering and depression symptoms over time. Findings shed light on predictors of depression symptoms in emerging adulthood, and interactions between harsh parenting and sleep.

Harsh mothering and fathering were not directly associated with symptoms of depression in emerging adulthood. However, adolescents’ sleep modified the effect of harsh fathering. Reduced sleep duration, poorer sleep maintenance efficiency, and more frequent long wake episodes exacerbated the relation between harsh fathering in adolescence and depression symptoms over time, even after controlling for autoregressive effects and multiple covariates. The influence of harsh fathering was less evident when adolescents’ sleep was more optimal. These patterns indicate that the multiplicative impact of harsh fathering and sleep problems forecasts symptoms of depression. Most emerging adults in our sample did not live at home, indicating that the combination of harsh fathering and sleep problems in adolescence confers risk beyond the parental home. The results provide further support that familial risk relates more strongly to depression symptoms among those with sleep problems.22,23

Past research has demonstrated relations between harsh parenting and depression symptoms in emerging adulthood.3,4 Our findings build on this work and are among the first to indicate that depression symptoms related to harsh parenting in adolescence may manifest in emerging adulthood, particularly for those with sleep problems. Future work that seeks to explicate health-risk trajectories associated with harsh parenting may benefit by considering the conjoint influence of sleep.

Our findings are consistent with a developmental ecological systems perspective, suggesting that relations between family functioning and mental health may be better understood in the context of sleep.6,14,15 They also support multiplicative and diathesis stress models, as interactions between harsh fathering and adolescents’ sleep were influential in predicting depression symptoms. Repeatedly experiencing harsh parenting in adolescence may heighten sensitivity and emotion dysregulation to future family conflicts and other social stressors (e.g., peer conflict).47 Sleep problems in adolescence increase vulnerability for emotional reactivity and reduce tolerance to frustration,20 further impairing coping with harsh parenting.20,21 In contrast, more optimal sleep may enhance stress resilience and mental health. Additionally, our results align with a broader literature suggesting that psychological stress and sleep problems together contribute to allostatic load,48 dysregulating stress response systems in the context of environmental stress and adversely impacting mental health over time.49

The influence of harsh parenting was evident for fathers but not mothers. Although findings on sex/gender differences are mixed, several studies show that harsh fathering more robustly relates to children’s depression symptoms.12,25 Harsh fathering may elicit greater feelings of threat and fear,11,12 potentially increasing depression risk. We offer this explanation tentatively pending further work on sex-/gender-related effects.

The results have important implications. Most adolescents in our community sample experienced at least one form of harsh parenting. Some programs have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing harsh parenting50 and continued efforts are needed. Our findings highlight the importance of considering sleep in attempts to improve mental health in those exposed to harsh parenting. Adolescents’ sleep may be modified by targeting school start times, sleep health education, and living conditions.51

This study has limitations. We focused on harsh parenting and sleep in adolescence; it is unknown if similar interactions in earlier periods affect depression in emerging adulthood. Further, we used a community sample from one state in the southeastern United States, and findings may not extrapolate to other geographical regions. In addition, although many correlates of harsh parenting and sleep were covaried, other untested variables might be influential. Moreover, an established measure of harsh parenting was used, yet some parents may have misreported their own level of harshness. Despite these limitations, our findings provide novel evidence that interactions between harsh parenting and sleep problems influence mental health across developmental periods.
