Research on hippocampal positioning and navigation model based on energy fields
Authors: Ying Liu, Chuankui Yan, Ichiro Tsuda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Place cells in the hippocampus are crucial components of the brain’s internal spatial positioning system, involved in constructing cognitive maps of the external environment for animals. However, many existing neuron models that simulate neural activities in the brain require extensive and complex computations. This study presents a place cell neural network model based on the Wang–Zhang model, using a neural energy coding approach. It quantitatively describes the attenuation pattern of place cell cluster firing power and constructs an energy field model. The model employs energy field gradients to address positioning and navigation tasks. Comparative experiments with the Hodgkin-Huxley (HH) model evaluate the navigation efficiency of rodents under different neuron models. The research shows that, compared to the HH model, the Wang–Zhang model has lower computational complexity and higher navigation efficiency. It rapidly constructs and updates cognitive maps, facilitating efficient pathfinding. Additionally, obstacle avoidance and detour experiments are performed using the Wang–Zhang model. Results demonstrate the model’s ability for flexible navigation in dynamically changing mazes, validating the Wang–Zhang model and energy coding theory’s unique functionality and robust advantages in neural modeling and information processing. This supports the effectiveness of energy coding in spatial memory and path exploration. Moreover, the additive property of neural energy provides significant advantages in neural modeling and computational analysis, offering a viable method for simulating large-scale neural networks and providing a theoretical basis for understanding the neurodynamic mechanisms of spatial memory.

Introduction

Spatial cognitive ability is an innate capacity in humans and many animals. It aids in understanding and utilizing the environment, facilitating effective analysis of its composition and relevant information for navigation. The concept of cognitive maps, proposed by behavioral psychologist Tolman, addresses navigation issues within an environment (Tolman 1948). He suggested that as rodents move through an environment, they form a cognitive map in the brain that represents changes in the surroundings, thus solving spatial localization challenges. This concept explains how mice learn the spatial layout of a maze during movement, and it applies not only to rats but also to other organisms, including humans (Ekstrom et al. 2003). Some researchers argue that the cognitive map is merely a mental representation used to acquire, encode, store, and decode relative positional information and attributes of observed objects. However, evidence supporting this view was lacking until the discovery of various types of spatial cells (Hafting et al. 2005).

Place cells are the biological foundation of the cognitive map, first discovered in the hippocampus by O’Keefe and Dostrovsky using electrophysiological methods (O’Keefe and Dostrovsky 1971). They found that place cells discharge when a mouse is in a specific location (O’Keefe and Nadel 1978). In other words, for a given spatial environment, each place cell has a fixed spatial location response field. The firing frequency of these place cells increases sharply as the mouse passes through this place field. Place cells encode information about a rat’s location in real time, generating a topological representation of its spatial environment in the rat’s brain, thus providing a non-egocentric cognitive map (Mehta 2015). This indicates that place cells in the hippocampus are crucial for the rat’s representation of its surrounding environment, effectively encoding physical spatial information (Alme et al. 2014). Subsequently, Wilson et al. recorded the firing patterns of place cells during spatial exploration experiments, revealing the representation of animal positions in a two-dimensional plane and confirming the effectiveness of population coding (Wilson and McNaughton 1993). This theory suggests that the concentrated firing of place cells is linked through interactions among closely connected neurons (Redish and Touretzky 2015). Further studies have shown that population coding can be reorganized based on environmental changes (Bostock et al. 1991; Muller and Kubie 1987; Gothard et al. 1996). Based on these theories, Muller et al. proposed that simultaneously firing place cells can form a network, enabling synaptic connections between place cells (Muller et al. 1991). Jake Ormond et al. recorded CA1 place cells while rats navigated a goal on a honeycomb maze (Ormond and O’ Keefe 2022). The results suggest that the hippocampus creates a vector-based model to support flexible navigation, allowing animals to select optimal paths to destinations from any location in the environment. These physiological experiments demonstrate a mechanism in place cell clusters that enables organisms to understand their location and navigate effectively. Exploring this mechanism is a crucial scientific question and the starting point of our research. We proposed the W–Z neuronal model (Wang et al. 2015) to describe this mechanism. This is supported by evidence showing that the W–Z and H–H models are equivalent neuronal models (Wang et al. 2018, 2023; Wang and Zhang 2006) and share the same molecular neurobiological basis (Zheng et al. 2014, 2016). It has been shown that the W–Z model is able to explain an important experimental observation: based on the blood oxygen level-dependent contrast (BOLD), when a vibration stimulus is applied to the finger, the blood flow to the contralateral cerebral hemisphere increases by 31%, with a corresponding increase in oxygen consumption of only 6% (Zheng et al. 2016). The model reproduces the hemodynamic phenomena of functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) from an energy perspective. This suggests that the W–Z model has some biological plausibility.

The brain consumes a significant amount of metabolic energy when processing various sensory inputs (Kety 1957). Data show that while the mammalian brain accounts for only 2% of total body weight, it consumes approximately 20% of the total metabolic energy (Rolfe and Brown 1997; Clarke and Sokoloff 1999). Additionally, more than 70% of the energy used in the cortex is dedicated to neural signal processing within subcellular cortical circuits (Howarth, Gleeson 2012). This substantial metabolic energy expenditure may influence the brain’s design, function, and evolution, suggesting a need for extremely high energy efficiency (Aiello and Wheeler 1995; Niven and Laughlin 2008). Consequently, studying the energy efficiency of neural networks is crucial. Recently, energy coding theory has garnered increasing attention from researchers (Aydın 2023; Yurchenko 2023; Yuan et al. 2021). Wang et al. introduced and developed the energy coding of cognitive processes in a series of studies (Wang et al. 2018; Wang and Zhang 2006, 2007; Wang and Wang 2018). The core argument of these studies is to explore the global neural encoding of brain function based on the energy characteristics of neuronal activity. Using a dynamical analysis approach, they formulated the Wang–Zhang neuronal energy model, which provides a theoretical framework for the global brain model and neural coding (Wang et al. 2023). Additionally, energy coding’s additive nature facilitates computation and analysis (Wang et al. 2018; Li et al. 2023). Wang et al. applied this neural encoding theory to mental exploration, examining the spatial distribution of hippocampal place cell energy as a neural energy field (Wang et al. 2017). However, this study did not model individual neurons, defining energy consumption directly via a Gaussian function in the place field. It did not account for the energy consumed by neurons themselves, particularly the energy used by various ion channels during action potential generation, which lacks biological plausibility. Our proposed model addresses this issue by modeling at the cellular level and calculating the energy field based on neuronal activity. This model offers better biological interpretability than previous work.

After deciding to model at the cellular level, a key question is which base model to choose for describing place cells. Common neuronal electrical models include the Integrate-and-Fire model (Brunel and Rossum 2007), the Hodgkin-Huxley model (Moujahid et al. 2011), and the FitzHugh-Nagumo model (Fitzhugh 1961). Additionally, considering time discretization and simplifying ordinary differential equations, neuron mapping models such as the Izhikevich model (Izhikevich 2003) can represent neuronal dynamics. Most conventional neuron models are based on molecular-level parameters or use nonlinear differential equations, which can lead to significant computational complexity when scaled to large neuronal clusters. Given the simplicity and global applicability of energy encoding, we model individual place cells using the Wang–Zhang model (Wang and Zhang 2006, 2007). The Wang–Zhang model, founded on the concept of energy coding, is well-suited as the basis for the energy field model. Subsequent numerical results demonstrate that this model indeed offers high computational efficiency.

Models and methods

Neuronal electrical model

Place cells are a type of discharge cell that responds selectively to specific spatial locations. They exhibit discharge activity only when a rat is in particular positions within an environment (Wilson and McNaughton 1993), remaining inactive elsewhere. Figure 1 illustrates the electrophysiological characteristics of a place cell in a two-dimensional setting (Moser et al. 2008). The black line in Fig. 1 represents the rat’s trajectory, with each red dot indicating a position where the place cell discharged. The discharge of this place cell is concentrated in the upper-middle section of the environment, demonstrating clear spatial selectivity. The cluster of red dots in the figure represents the place field of this place cell. A place cell encodes a specific spatial location, and when a sufficiently large number of place cells are present, their place fields collectively cover the entire spatial extent of the environment. Decoding the discharge patterns of these place cells allow for the determination of an animal’s position within the space.

In 2006, Wang et al. proposed a neural energy coding model to describe the biological discharge phenomenon of individual neurons, known as the Wang–Zhang model (Wang and Zhang 2006, 2007), as illustrated in Fig. 2.

In the neuronal model, the inner and outer membranes form capacitors, ion channels form inductors, and sodium-potassium pumps act as voltage and current sources by actively transporting ions. Additionally, the model considers the coupling effects of surrounding connected neurons on a given neuron, meaning that the membrane potential and membrane current of any neuron are regulated by the activity state of its connected neighbors. This establishes an electrical model capable of describing the electrical characteristics of neurons and the interactions between neurons.

Synaptic connection model

When animals move through space, place cells respond to specific spatial locations with high-power activity. If two place cells fire with high power successively, the synaptic connection between them undergoes a round of enhancement [13–14]. Neural energy field model

Considering the varying energy consumption at different locations for place cells, a spatially distributed place cell model is constructed using energy encoding methods to represent spatial location distribution. Additionally, a neural energy field is constructed using the firing power of a cluster of place cells. Assuming that the place fields of place cells are uniformly distributed on a two-dimensional virtual plane, n place cells correspond to n distinct positions in space. Due to the lack of a complete correspondence between the spatial topology and the arrangement of cells, a specific permutation matrix can be applied to the group of place cells. This transformation allows the place cells to correspond one-to-one with the spatial positions. Consequently, every node in the grid corresponds to the place field center of the respective place cell, enabling the spatial positions to be expressed by the cluster of place cells, as shown in Fig. 4. Each grid node in Fig. 4 can be matched with the center of the place field of a specific place cell. In this way, we can use place cell clusters to accurately express spatial location. The energy field is a scalar field defined on this virtual plane, where each node represents the place field center of a place cell. The firing power of the place cell at a given moment is defined as a scalar at that point. Consequently, if an animal is at a position \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X\left( t \right)=\left( {{x_1},{x_2}} \right)$$\end{document} on the plane, the z-coordinate represents the power of the specific place cell at that time.

According to this correspondence rule, each node on the virtual plane has a corresponding firing power. Unlike a continuous field in mathematics, this neural energy field is discrete because it is defined only at each node, where each node corresponds to the place field center of a place cell.

When animals move through space, place cells respond to specific spatial locations with high-power activity. If two place cells consecutively exhibit high-power activity, it indicates that these positions are adjacent along the animal’s path. According to Eq. (8), the synaptic connection between the corresponding place cells is strengthened, and this path information is stored. Simultaneously, as animals navigate towards a goal, place cells representing both the goal and the current location fire simultaneously at high power. This establishes synaptic connections between the current place cell and the goal place cell. Animals store spatial memory and path information in these synaptic connections, which alters the distribution of the energy field (Moser et al. 2008). In the model used in this study, synapses can be categorized into two main types. One type arises from adjacent place cells firing successively with relatively high power, while the other type arises from the simultaneous firing of place cells representing the current and goal locations during pathfinding. Navigation model

Applying the gradient operator to the newly constructed neural energy field produces a vector field related to energy, known as the neural energy gradient field. This gradient field reflects the changing characteristics of the firing power of the cluster of place cells and defines the energy field gradient as the navigation vector for the animal’s next movement. We define the end of real-time exploration as occurring when the distance from the current position to the goal position is less than a certain threshold, referred to as the critical distance.

Path planning and energy field correction

Path planning and navigation based on the cognitive map of place cells are achieved through energy field correction, which occurs in two stages. The first stage is the construction phase, where, given a navigation task, rats explore randomly. At each step, the rats determine whether they have reached the goal location. Upon reaching the goal, the cognitive map and scene information are encoded as an energy field, thereby mapping the entire spatial layout and the goal position, and constructing a complete virtual plane of place cells. The second stage is the learning phase, where neural energy gradients are utilized for behavioral decision-making and guiding the rat’s navigation. During exploration, rats learn and memorize information, gradually correcting the energy field to update the cognitive map. After several rounds of exploration and environmental learning, rats navigate to the goal point via an efficient path. The diagram of path planning and navigation is shown in Fig. 5.

In summary, cognitive maps or energy fields are not preprogrammed in the brain but are learned from the environment. During exploration tasks, rats record routes based on the high-power firing of place cells at specific times and locations, and then strengthen the synaptic connections along the corresponding paths, continuously modifying the energy field. After multiple rounds of environmental learning, the cognitive map is constructed.

A simulation experiment was conducted with 400 place cells in a 20 × 20 two-dimensional environment. The goal place cell was set at coordinates (15,15), with a step length of 1 and a critical distance of 1. The model simulated a rat’s movement on the two-dimensional virtual plane, starting from the initial position (1,1). The results of the first 9 rounds are shown in Fig. 6. When the rat first locates the goal, the place cell at that position is activated, and subsequent navigation is guided by the energy field gradient. With continued environmental learning, the rat’s cognitive ability gradually improves, allowing it to acquire more accurate position information and activate the corresponding place cells. Conclusion

Based on the Wang–Zhang model, we established a new place cell model to quantitatively describe the attenuation of firing power, utilizing the specific discharge characteristics of place cells at particular locations. This model successfully reproduces the place field as shown in reference (O’Keefe and Dostrovsky 1971). Given that energy is additive, this greatly facilitates computation and analysis (Wang et al. 2018; Li et al. 2023).

We consider the spatial distribution of the collective firing power of all place cells as an energy field, which stores environmental information learned by rats. The Hebb learning rule indicates that when two neurons fire successively, the connection between them is strengthened (Hebb 1949). Consequently, we define a class of Hebbian energy learning mechanisms with time delay that continuously modifies the synaptic connection strength between place cells, thereby altering the distribution patterns of the energy fields.

In contrast to Wang et al., who defined energy consumption using a simple two-dimensional Gaussian distribution (Wang et al. 2017), our study accounts for both neuronal energy expenditure and information transfer between neurons, which is a significant factor (Howarth, Gleeson 2012). Building on neuron-based models, we use the Wang–Zhang model to model hippocampal place cells, calculating their firing rates to construct a neural energy field with greater biological plausibility. Compared to previous work, our approach is modeled at the cell level, offering improved biological interpretability since the Wang–Zhang and Hebbian-Hebbian models share the same biological basis (Wang et al. 2023).

We utilize neural energy field gradients to simulate spatial localization and navigation. Simulation results show that our place cell model, based on neural energy fields, can form and update cognitive maps. It uses these energy fields for environmental exploration, cognition, and navigation, ultimately identifying the optimal path. We also compared the navigation efficiency of rats under the Wang–Zhang model and the Hebbian-Hebbian (HH) model. This study explores location and navigation within a maze environment and recreates the simplified Tolman classic rat detour experiment ((Tolman 1948; Ciancia and Tolman 1930) from an energy encoding perspective. Simulation results demonstrate that the Wang–Zhang model generates more effective navigation vectors, guiding rats to reach the goal location.

Additionally, we examined the scenario where the maze environment changes after the target is located. The results indicate that rats can still update their cognitive map through the energy learning mechanism and replan their paths using energy gradients, even when the maze environment is altered. The simulation results demonstrate that our model supports flexible navigation in dynamically changing mazes and can adapt to dynamic environments (such as door openings and closings), further validating the effectiveness of energy encoding theory in spatial memory and path exploration. The additive property of neural energy significantly facilitates neural modeling and computational analysis. From a computational perspective, the simplicity and global nature of energy coding in our model enable more straightforward computations, greatly reducing the cost of analytical research.

This study simultaneously explores the spatial memory and navigation processes of rats in a maze environment using energy coding methods. When rats move from one location to another, transferring from the place field of one place cell to another in the cognitive map, the path is considered learned. When the rat reencounters this previously traversed path, activated (high-power firing) place cells can stimulate cells ahead on their respective paths through synapses. During this random exploration, the rat’s cognitive map of the environment gradually improves and self-organizes. The neural energy field is then constructed based on this cognitive map to plan and navigate the rat’s path (Yamada 2022). From the perspective of energy coding theory, this study highlights the critical role of synaptic plasticity in spatial memory and path exploration, reaffirming the significant advantages of applying global brain coding theory to neural information processing (Wang et al. 2018; Wang and Zhang 2006, 2007; Yuan et al. 2021; Wang and Wang 2018). The results also validate the effectiveness of energy coding theory in spatial localization and navigation, suggesting numerous potential applications in artificial intelligence.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the neural energy field model developed here can efficiently identify optimized paths, perform obstacle avoidance, and conduct detour experiments, illustrating the learning process of rats in spatial navigation. From the standpoint of energy coding, this study confirms the crucial role of synaptic plasticity in spatial memory and navigation, underscoring the considerable advantage of applying the brain’s global coding theory to neural information processing and offering new insights into the neurodynamic mechanisms of spatial memory.
