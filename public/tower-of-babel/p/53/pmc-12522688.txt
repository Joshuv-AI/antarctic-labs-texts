Efficacy of problem-based learning in enhancing health education skills, self-directed learning, and critical thinking among nursing interns: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Huarong Xue, Yan Lu, Liyan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraditional didactic teaching methods have been the cornerstone of nursing education, the Problem-Based Learning (PBL) has gained recognition in healthcare education as a student-centered instructional strategy with the potential to enhance critical thinking.ObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the effect of PBL on the health education ability of nursing interns in order to promote the development of nursing education.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted with 142 nursing interns randomly assigned to either a control group (traditional teaching method) or an observation group (PBL teaching method), with 71 individuals in each group. The control group used the traditional teaching mode, and the observation group used the PBL teaching mode for 2 months. The primary outcome measures included assessment scores, health education ability, self-directed learning ability, critical thinking ability, and overall teaching satisfaction.ResultsThe comparison of assessment scores revealed that the PBL Teaching Group demonstrated significantly improved post-test scores compared to the Traditional Teaching Group (P < 0.05). Additionally, the PBL Teaching Group displayed significantly higher post-assessment scores in health education ability (P < 0.05), self-directed learning ability (P < 0.05), and critical thinking ability (P < 0.05). The results show a significant positive correlation between health education skills and self-directed learning (r = 0.478, P < 0.001), significant positive correlation between health education skills and critical thinking (r = 0.854, P < 0.001),significant positive correlation between self-directed learning and critical thinking (r = 0.553, P < 0.001).In addition, the overall satisfaction level of PBL group (28.69 ± 6.28) was significantly higher than that of traditional teaching group (25.14 ± 7.78) (P < 0.05).ConclusionThis study provides evidence to support the use of PBL as an effective teaching method to improve the health education ability of nursing interns, and provides new ideas and methods for nursing education.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07936-8.

Introduction

The preparation and education of nursing interns play a pivotal role in shaping the competencies and capabilities of future healthcare professionals [1, 2]. As the landscape of healthcare delivery continues to evolve, the need for nursing interns to possess strong health education capabilities, critical thinking skills, and a profound understanding of patient care has become increasingly essential [3, 4]. Traditional didactic teaching methods have long been the cornerstone of nursing education, providing foundational knowledge and skills to students. However, the effectiveness of these methods in fostering the comprehensive, integrative understanding and application of healthcare concepts has been called into question [5]. In response, the integration of innovative and evidence-based teaching modalities has garnered increasing attention, aiming to optimize the education and preparation of nursing interns [6].

Problem-Based Learning (PBL) originated in 1969 when American neurology professor Barrows introduced it to the field of medical education, and has since been widely applied across multiple disciplines [7]. PBL is a problem-based learning method that emphasizes student autonomy and collaborative learning [8]. At its core, it drives learning through problem-solving, placing it in real, meaningful contexts. One such teaching method that has gained recognition in healthcare education was PBL [7–9]. PBL was a student-centered instructional strategy that presents learners with complex, real-world problems and challenges, guiding them to explore, analyze, and resolve these issues through collaborative and active learning approaches [10, 11]. The utilization of small group discussions, self-directed inquiry, and problem-solving activities characterizes the PBL methodology, emphasizing the integration of theoretical knowledge with practical application and the development of critical thinking skills [12, 13]. The pedagogical foundation of PBL lies in constructivist and self-directed learning theories, which support the idea that students learn more effectively when they engage in solving authentic problems, reflect on their learning processes, and collaborate with peers, skills that are essential for clinical nursing practice.

The potential of the PBL teaching method in healthcare education, particularly in nursing intern programs, has garnered considerable interest. In PBL teaching, students need to study around a specific problem or case, master relevant knowledge and skills through self-study, group discussion and case analysis, and develop critical thinking, teamwork and communication skills [14]. A study have explored the impact of PBL on various aspects of healthcare education, such as critical thinking, problem-solving abilities, and collaborative teamwork among learners [15]. The research of Wosinski shows that the ability of independent learning is one of the key elements for the improvement of professional skills of nurses. PBL teaching method effectively improves students’ ability of independent learning by guiding them to carry out independent learning and cooperative learning [16]. While the evidence supporting the effectiveness of PBL in enhancing these competencies was robust, the application of this teaching method specifically to improve the health education capabilities of nursing interns warrants further investigation.

While previous studies have demonstrated the benefits of PBL in improving critical thinking and learning motivation, few have systematically evaluated its impact on nursing interns’ ability to deliver patient education, a core but often overlooked competency in clinical training. Therefore, the primary aim of this study is to assess the effectiveness of PBL in improving nursing interns’ health education skills, self-directed learning ability, and critical thinking in a real-world clinical context. By strengthening these interconnected competencies, this study seeks to offer empirical support for more active, student-centered teaching approaches and provide actionable insights for curriculum reform in clinical nursing education.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was a prospective cohort study, and a total of 142 nursing interns who underwent departmental rotations at our hospital from September 2023 to September 2024 were selected as the study population. During the randomization process, we used a random number generator to assign each intern a unique random number. Subsequently, we divided the participants into two groups based on the parity of these numbers: interns with odd numbers were assigned to the PBL group, while those with even numbers were placed in the control group. Before the randomization, basic information of participants was collected to ensure comparability in baseline characteristics between the two groups. The randomization was conducted by an independent researcher who did not participate in the subsequent intervention implementation or outcome assessment to minimize potential biases. Participants were randomly assigned to either the control group (traditional teaching method) or the observation group (PBL teaching method), with 71 individuals in each group. Outcome assessments were conducted by evaluators blinded to group assignment.

Sampling and participants

To ensure the scientific and impartiality of this study, we used a rigorous randomization method to allocate participants. Sample Size Calculation: SPSS 23.0 statistical software was used in this study to provide a statistical description of the research data, including composition ratios, means, standard deviations, and other statistical indicators. The estimated sample size was denoted as N, with n1 and n2 representing the sample sizes of the control and observation groups, respectively, where N = n1 + n2. The ratio of the two sample sizes was denoted ask, with k = n1: n2 = 1. The standard mean difference was represented by σ, with σ = 3.57, and ε represented the mean difference between the two groups, with ε = 2.05. α = 0.05, β = 0.1. According to the calculation formula, the calculated sample size was N = 128 (individuals). Considering potential non-compliance in questionnaire completion and loss to follow-up, a 10% attrition rate was factored in, thus requiring an additional 10% increase in the study sample, resulting in the final determination: N = 128 (1 + 10%) ≈ 142 (individuals).

Inclusion criteria [17]: (1) Age ≥ 20 years; (2) Completion of school studies and currently in the final year of study, entering the clinical internship phase; (3) Educational attainment of associate degree or above; (4) No mental illness or psychological abnormalities; (5) Awareness of the purpose of the study and voluntary participation.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Interruption of internship due to special circumstances; (2) interns taking continuous leave for more than 3 days or cumulative leave exceeding 5 days during the internship period; (3) Participation in training on other teaching methods; (4) Instructors with educational attainment below the undergraduate level, less than 5 years of work experience, and without qualification certificates for carrying out teaching; (5) Incomplete data.

Data collection methods

A variety of data collection methods, including questionnaire surveys, skill assessments, self-evaluation reports, and knowledge tests, are employed to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of the PBL teaching method in enhancing the health education skills, self-directed learning, and critical thinking of nursing interns. Additionally, during the data collection process, emphasis is placed on ensuring data accuracy and reliability, protecting participants’ privacy and rights, and minimizing biases and errors.

Teaching methods

Control group

Interns received conventional lecture-based teaching, including PowerPoint-based theoretical instruction and classroom Q&A. Teaching content was based on the standard nursing curriculum and delivered twice weekly by senior faculty members over the same two-month period. The interns were divided into multiple groups, each comprising 5–6 interns, to independently study trauma nursing theory weekly over a two-month period, with each session lasting approximately 90 min. To ensure that each group of interns acquired comprehensive theoretical knowledge, the instructors guided them in mastering literature search methods and understanding the sources of clinical research evidence. The instructors regularly demonstrated skills and general procedures, and presented diagnostic and treatment processes and key nursing content to the students. No case-based or problem-solving tasks were included. The training period lasted for two months.

Observation group

The PBL intervention was conducted weekly over a two-month period, with each session lasting approximately 90 min. The interns were divided into multiple groups, each comprising 5–6 interns and was facilitated by a trained instructor. Nursing interns underwent PBL combined with comparative nursing rounds teaching, with specific methods as follows:

Preparation Stage: The teaching team meticulously selects cases with educational significance one week prior, prioritizing clinically common types such as limb fractures and hepatosplenic ruptures. Based on the textbook content, the teachers devise a series of related questions aimed at assessing interns’ understanding and application of basic disease knowledge. They pose questions according to the textbook and assign two problem-solving tasks, involving literature retrieval, to prepare for subsequent nursing ward rounds teaching. Interns are required to preview textbook materials, read course materials to address the teachers’ questions, attempt to answer them independently, and engage in public discussions. Additionally, an online or offline preview discussion session is organized to encourage interns to share their learning insights, raise questions, and facilitate initial knowledge exchange and interaction among team members.Nursing Ward Rounds: Intern nurses report on the conditions of the patients they are responsible for. The teachers summarize these reports, highlighting any similarities and differences in clinical manifestations and disease characteristics among the cases. The teachers personally examine the patients, explaining as they operate, pointing out specific issues in nursing practice, and providing on-site guidance to interns on adopting correct nursing measures. Meanwhile, questions related to the previously assigned problem-solving tasks are posed to test interns’ preparedness and application abilities, and interns are asked to respond to their assigned tasks. The teachers’ primary responsibility is to integrate actual clinical cases with theoretical knowledge and expand on any related professional knowledge.Summary: Teachers evaluate interns’ performance during the ward rounds, explaining and organizing the conditions, key points, and difficulties of nursing. They systematically review the nursing conditions, key points, and difficulties encountered during the ward rounds, presenting them intuitively through charts, cases, and other forms to enhance understanding. Finally, they pose questions to guide interns in discussing the issues on site and encourage active participation.Post-Ward Round Activities: Following the ward rounds, teachers assign additional research tasks based on new issues identified or points not fully explored during the process, encouraging interns to continue their exploration. Interns are required to further consult relevant literature in their spare time, organize and analyze the materials, and write research reports or summaries, not only improving their literature retrieval skills but also enhancing their research awareness and writing abilities. Solving problems through teamwork and independent learning can help develop students’ practical skills and improve their overall quality, including critical thinking, problem solving and teamwork skills, as well as teaching satisfaction. This method aims to enhance interns’ thinking skills and research consciousness. The training period lasts for two months.

Observation indicators

In addition to assessing health education ability as the primary outcome, the study also included self-directed learning ability, critical thinking ability, and satisfaction level as secondary outcomes. These competencies were selected because they are conceptually and pedagogically linked to health education delivery. Prior studies have shown that effective health education requires not only knowledge, but also critical thinking, autonomous learning, and confidence, all of which can be fostered through PBL [12, 13]. Including these outcomes allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of how PBL enhances the overall readiness of nursing interns to educate patients in clinical practice.

Baseline data

Baseline data of the interns participating in the experiment were collected, including age, gender, ethnicity, place of residence, only-child status, educational level, and GPA during school.

Assessment scores

After two months of teaching, theoretical and practical assessments were carried out for both groups. The theoretical assessment was conducted in the form of a closed-book exam, comprising 50 multiple-choice questions, each worth 2 points. Correct answers were awarded 2 points, incorrect answers scored 0 points, with a total score of 100. Higher scores indicated better assessment performance. The practical assessment involved nursing interns conducting health education for patients in a clinical setting, with instructors grading the performance on a scale of 100. Higher scores reflected better assessment outcomes. The questionnaire was developed for this study and is detailed in the Supplementary Table 1. The supplementary file contains detailed descriptions of the PBL intervention protocol, complete assessment instruments, and the practical scoring rubric.

Health education ability

Assessing Two Groups of Students Before and After Teaching Using a Health Education Evaluation Scale. The questionnaire used in this study comprises four sections, including assessment (11 items), planning (8 items), implementation (13 items), and evaluation (6 items), totaling 38 items. The development of the questionnaire was based on review of literature and expert opinions [14]. Each item was scored using a Likert 5-point scale, ranging from “completely non-compliant” to “completely compliant,” with scores ranging from 38 to 190. Higher scores indicated stronger health education ability.

Self-directed learning ability

Pre- and post-teaching evaluations of the two groups were conducted using a self-directed learning ability assessment scale for nursing major students. The assessment comprised three dimensions: self-management ability (10 items), information competency (11 items), and learning cooperation ability (7 items), scored using a Likert 5-point scale, with scores ranging from 28 to 140. Higher scores indicated stronger self-directed learning ability. The total scale registered a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.916, while the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the three dimensions ranged from 0.765 to 0.861 [18].

Critical thinking ability

Pre- and post-teaching evaluations of the two groups were conducted using a critical thinking ability scale, encompassing seven dimensions, each worth 60 points, with a total score of 420. Higher scores indicated stronger positive critical thinking ability. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.960 [16].

Teaching satisfaction

Nursing students’ learning satisfaction and self-confidence levels regarding teaching activities were explored with reference to Liu YM et al. [19]. The content regarding learning satisfaction included nine items, namely, “I feel the content is substantial,” “I feel that the teaching materials and aids are very practical,” “I feel that the teaching schedule is arranged appropriately,” and “I feel that the teaching materials and activities help me to learn.” The scoring method of the 5-point Likert Scale was adopted, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total score ranged from 9 to 45; higher scores designated better learning satisfaction. The Cronbach’s α value was 0.970, indicating favorable internal consistency reliability.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the institutional teaching and research office of Shuguang Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. The research involved no patients and posed no risk of physical or psychological harm. All nursing interns participated voluntarily and anonymously, and written informed consent was obtained. As the study involved routine educational evaluation and did not collect sensitive personal data, it was exempt from full review by the institutional ethics committee, and no formal approval number was issued.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 29.0 statistical software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables were presented as [n(%)] format. When the sample size was ≥ 40 and the theoretical frequency T was ≥ 5, the basic formula was applied for the chi-square test. When the sample size was ≥ 40 but the theoretical frequency was 1 ≤ T < 5, the corrected formula for the chi-square test was utilized. For sample sizes < 40 or theoretical frequencies T < 1, statistical analysis was performed using Fisher’s exact probability method. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normal distribution of continuous variables. For normally distributed continuous variables, the results were expressed as (Mean ± SD) and analyzed using the corrected variance t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented in the form of median (25th percentile, 75th percentile) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the linear correlation between the three outcome measures of health education ability, directed learning ability and critical thinking ability. |r|≤ 0.3 indicates no linear correlation, 0.3≤|r|≤0.5 indicates a low degree of linear relationship, 0.5≤|r|≤0.8 indicates a significant linear relationship, and |r|>0.8 indicates a high degree of linear relationship. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Based on the comparison of baseline characteristics between the Traditional Teaching Group (n = 71) and the PBL Teaching Group (n = 71), no statistically significant differences were observed in age (24.56 ± 2.34 vs. 25.02 ± 2.45, t = 1.153, P = 0.251), gender distribution (χ2 = 0.028, P = 0.867), ethnicity (χ2 = 0.132, P = 0.716), residence (χ2 = 0.260, P = 0.610), only-child status (χ2 = 0.716, P = 0.398), education level distribution (χ2 = 0.797, P = 0.671), and GPA (4.27 ± 0.44 vs. 4.36 ± 0.57, t = 1.066, P = 0.289) (Table 1). These results indicate that the two groups were comparable in demographic and academic characteristics at baseline, ensuring group equivalence prior to intervention.

In comparing the assessment scores between the Traditional Teaching Group and the PBL Teaching Group, the pre-test scores showed no statistically significant difference (65.14 ± 7.32 vs. 64.89 ± 4.82, t = 0.244, P = 0.808). This suggests that PBL enhanced the students’ theoretical and practical learning outcomes.

Discussion

The assessment scores revealed that the PBL Teaching Group exhibited significantly improved scores in the post-test assessment compared to the Traditional Teaching Group. This study highlights the effectiveness of PBL in promoting students’ active learning and critical thinking skills, consistent with Bains’ research, which provides advantages for the teaching of anatomy and physiology to physical therapy students through the application of PBL pedagogy. It can serve as an effective method to promote students’ self-regulated learning, increase engagement, and improve course outcomes [20]. The higher post-test scores in the PBL Teaching Group indicate the effectiveness of this method in enhancing theoretical and practical knowledge, which was essential for the development of competent healthcare professionals. Unlike traditional didactic lectures, PBL presents students with complex, real-world problems and encourages them to explore and analyze the issues, conduct research, and collaborate with peers to develop solutions [21, 22]. This active engagement fosters a deeper understanding of the subject matter and allows students to apply theoretical concepts to practical scenarios, thus enhancing their education capabilities. By immersing themselves in these problem-solving activities, nursing interns were better equipped to transfer their knowledge and skills into real clinical settings, leading to improved assessment scores and increased confidence in their professional competence [23, 24].

Furthermore, the study demonstrated that the PBL Teaching Group displayed significantly higher post-assessment scores in health education ability, self-directed learning ability, and critical thinking ability compared to the Traditional Teaching Group. These findings were consistent with the core principles of PBL, which emphasizes the integration of theoretical knowledge with practical application and the development of problem-solving skills [20]. The positive impact of the PBL teaching method on these education capabilities was essential for the preparation of nursing interns to deliver high-quality patient care and engage in lifelong learning and professional development [25, 26]. By guiding students to actively seek learning resources, make learning plans and monitor the learning process, PBL helps to cultivate their awareness and ability of independent learning and critical thinking, which is the key ability for nursing interns to make correct decisions in complex clinical situations.

The results show a significant positive correlation between health education skills and self-directed learning (r = 0.478, P < 0.001), indicating that the improvement in health education skills among nursing interns is closely related to the enhancement of their self-directed learning abilities. Similarly, there is also a significant positive correlation between health education skills and critical thinking (r = 0.854, P < 0.001), suggesting that the improvement in health education skills is accompanied by an increase in critical thinking. Furthermore, there is a significant positive correlation between self-directed learning and critical thinking (r = 0.553, P < 0.001), further emphasizing the close relationship among these three factors.

The overall satisfaction levels in the PBL group also reflect the positive reception of the PBL teaching method among the nursing interns. The higher satisfaction levels may indicate greater engagement, perceived relevance of the educational experiences, and confidence in applying the acquired knowledge and skills. This aligns with the tenets of learner-centered education, where active participation, relevance to practice, and learner satisfaction are pivotal for successful educational outcomes.

The findings of this study were in line with existing literature [27, 28] that has documented the benefits of PBL in healthcare education, particularly in fostering autonomous learning, independent thinking, and problem-solving skills, competencies that are essential for preparing students for real-world clinical challenges. These findings carry meaningful implications for clinical nursing education. In particular, the demonstrated improvements in health education ability, self-directed learning, and critical thinking suggest that PBL is not only effective as an instructional strategy, but also instrumental in preparing nursing interns for real-world clinical demands [29]. Given the increasing complexity of patient care, modern nursing practice demands professionals who can deliver accurate health education, make autonomous clinical decisions, and adapt to dynamic care environments, competencies that are closely aligned with those developed through problem-based learning, which fosters critical thinking, teamwork, motivation, and self-directed learning in nursing students [15]. Our results highlight the potential of PBL to complement traditional teaching methods in structured clinical rotations. Institutions may consider integrating PBL modules into existing curricula to cultivate reflective thinking and student engagement, especially in resource-constrained environments. Previous studies have demonstrated that PBL fosters the development of critical thinking, problem-solving, and decision-making skills, which were critical attributes for healthcare professionals. Additionally, PBL has been associated with enhanced motivation, self-directed learning, and collaborative teamwork, all of which were essential competencies for nursing practice in dynamic clinical settings [30]. Therefore, the present study contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the effectiveness of PBL in healthcare education, specifically in the context of nursing internship programs.

Overall, our study might provide theoretical and practical guidance for enhancing the design and delivery of nursing education, particularly in clinical internship settings. Theoretically, this study reinforces the value of constructivist and self-directed learning theories in nursing education. The significant improvements observed in critical thinking, autonomous learning, and health education competencies align with the foundational principles of PBL, which emphasizes learner-centered, inquiry-driven, and problem-oriented engagement. These findings support the integration of educational theory into clinical teaching practice and add empirical evidence to the theoretical discourse on active learning in health professions education. Practically, the results suggest that PBL is a feasible and effective approach for enhancing core competencies among nursing interns in real-world clinical training environments. Given the increasing emphasis on competency-based education, these findings have implications for curriculum developers, clinical educators, and policy-makers. Incorporating structured PBL modules may facilitate greater learner engagement, improve knowledge retention, and better prepare students for the multifaceted demands of modern nursing. In addition, targeted instructor training and the development of PBL case libraries may enhance the scalability and sustainability of this teaching model in diverse institutional settings. Beyond individual teaching practice, the findings of this study may also inform broader educational policies in nursing. As healthcare systems increasingly emphasize competency-based education and lifelong learning, the integration of evidence-based teaching models like PBL can be encouraged through curriculum accreditation frameworks and national internship standards.

Innovation

The findings of this study offer insights for future research and clinical practice in healthcare education. The results not only confirm the effectiveness of PBL in enhancing the overall quality of nursing interns but also provide new perspectives and ideas for the development of nursing education theory. First, PBL emphasizes a student-centered teaching philosophy, breaking away from the traditional teacher-centered model and offering a new paradigm for nursing education. Second, by guiding students to actively explore and solve problems, PBL helps cultivate their problem-solving and innovation skills, infusing new vitality into nursing education. Finally, PBL stresses interactive learning methods such as teamwork and role-playing, which aid in developing interns’ communication and collaboration skills, providing more comprehensive training objectives for nursing education.

From a clinical practice perspective, the integration of PBL into nursing education programs has the potential to enhance the preparedness of nursing interns for the dynamic and complex healthcare environment. By fostering critical thinking, problem-solving, and collaboration, PBL can contribute to the development of competent and adaptable healthcare professionals capable of delivering high-quality patient care. Furthermore, healthcare organizations and educational institutions should consider the alignment of teaching methods with the evolving demands of modern healthcare practice to ensure that nursing interns were equipped with the necessary skills and competencies to thrive in their professional roles [31].

Limitations

While the results of this study support the use of PBL to enhance the education capabilities of nursing interns, several limitations need to be acknowledged. Firstly, the study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare education settings. Future research should consider multi-center studies to ensure the broader applicability of the results. Secondly, due to limited sample size, subgroup analyses based on baseline characteristics such as gender or education level were not conducted. Future studies with larger, more diverse samples are needed to explore potential moderating effects. Thirdly, the short intervention duration of two months may not be sufficient to capture lasting or longitudinal changes in educational competencies. Due to institutional curriculum structure and ethical approval constraints, it was not feasible to extend the intervention period in the current study. As such, the findings primarily reflect short-term educational outcomes. Future studies should consider longer-term interventions with multiple follow-up assessments to evaluate the sustainability of improvements in health education ability, critical thinking, and self-directed learning over time. Lastly, the study’s reliance on self-reported satisfaction surveys may introduce response bias, and future research could benefit from mixed-method approaches to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the student experience.

Conclusion

PBL significantly enhanced all three outcome domains compared to traditional teaching, with moderate to strong positive correlations observed among them. Nursing interns in the PBL group also reported higher levels of satisfaction with their learning experience. The positive impact of PBL on assessment scores, educational competence, and satisfaction underscores its potential to enrich nursing education and contribute to the development of skilled healthcare professionals. As nursing education increasingly emphasizes competence over content, structured and scalable PBL approaches may offer a sustainable path toward improving workforce readiness and care quality.

While the findings are promising, the study is limited by its single-center design and relatively short intervention period, which may affect the generalizability and long-term applicability of the results. Future research should involve multicenter, longitudinal studies with diverse samples, and explore the sustained impact of PBL on clinical performance, professional development, and teaching practice across nursing education settings.
