Psychiatric comorbidity in DiGeorge association: Suicidal ideation and bipolar disorder
Authors: Roman Nakhamiyayev, Michelle Perry, Sahil Sood, Shivam Kamboj, Emmanuel Khodra, Bamidele Agada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiGeorge syndrome, also known as 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, is a multisystem condition associated with congenital cardiac defects, craniofacial abnormalities, immune dysfunction, endocrine disturbances, and a high lifetime risk of psychiatric disorders. Bipolar I Disorder is a severe mood disorder with a substantially elevated risk of suicidal ideation and attempts, especially during manic episodes with psychotic features. This report describes a woman in her 20’s with 22q11.2 Deletion Syndrome and Bipolar I Disorder who presented with acute suicidal ideation in the context of a manic episode. She responded to inpatient stabilization and responded well to valproic acid and olanzapine. This case highlights the substantial clinical impact of psychiatric illness in 22q11.2 deletion syndrome and reinforces the need for heightened attention to mood instability and suicide risk in affected individuals.Keywords: 22q11.2 deletion, Bipolar Disorder, Suicidal Ideation, Neurodevelopmental Disorder, DiGeorge syndrome

Introduction

DiGeorge syndrome, also known as 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (22q11.2DS), is a clinically recognized multisystem genetic disorder caused by a hemizygous microdeletion on chromosome 22q11.2. The syndrome is classically associated with congenital cardiac defects, thymic hypoplasia with immune dysfunction, and hypocalcemia due to parathyroid hypoplasia; however, its phenotypic spectrum extends well beyond this triad. Additional manifestations commonly include palatal abnormalities such as cleft palate or velopharyngeal insufficiency, renal and gastrointestinal anomalies, skeletal findings, developmental delay, and learning disabilities [1]. The disorder arises from loss of dosage-sensitive genes critical to early embryologic development, particularly those involved in neural crest cell migration and pharyngeal arch formation, and occurs in approximately 1 in 4000 live births, with most cases arising from de novo deletions [1], [2].

In addition to its congenital features, 22q11.2 deletion syndrome confers a substantial burden of psychiatric illness. Large epidemiologic studies have demonstrated that nearly half of affected adults carry at least one psychiatric diagnosis, with mood disorders among the most prevalent. Bipolar disorder has been reported in approximately 7% of hospitalized adults with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, supporting its recognition as a meaningful component of the psychiatric phenotype rather than an incidental finding [3]. Suicidal ideation has also been documented in this population, and the presence of psychiatric comorbidity has been associated with worse hospitalization outcomes, including longer lengths of stay and increased likelihood of discharge against medical advice [1].

Bipolar I disorder is a chronic mood disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of mania and depression, with a pathophysiology involving fronto-limbic dysregulation, neurotransmitter imbalance, neuroinflammation, and impaired neuroplasticity. The lifetime risk of suicide in bipolar disorder is markedly elevated, with rates estimated to be 20–30 times higher than in the general population, and suicidal ideation is common during acute mood episodes [4]. Despite this recognized psychiatric vulnerability, detailed clinical reports describing acute manic episodes with concurrent suicidal ideation in individuals with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome remain limited. We present a case of a young adult with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome and Bipolar I disorder who presented with acute suicidal ideation during a manic episode, underscoring the clinical impact of psychiatric illness in this population and the need for heightened awareness, screening, and proactive management of mood instability and suicide risk.

Case presentation

A woman in her 20’s with a history of DiGeorge syndrome and Bipolar I Disorder presented with suicidal ideation after expressing a plan to ingest all of her prescribed medications. Bipolar Disorder symptoms first manifested during late adolescence with the patient's first hospitalization in late 2024. This episode was characterized by elated mood, increased energy as well as a decreased need for sleep for several days, irritability, aggression towards family members, and disorganized behavior requiring inpatient hospitalization, at which time a diagnosis of Bipolar I Disorder was established. She reported severe insomnia for one week, increased energy, irritability, and pressured speech. On examination, the patient was agitated, pacing, and exhibited rapid, tangential speech with persecutory delusions that others intended to harm her. She denied recent ingestion, substance use, or physical complaints. Collateral information from her outpatient psychiatrist revealed inconsistent adherence to valproic acid in the preceding weeks. Laboratory tests and toxicology screening were unremarkable. A diagnosis of Bipolar I Disorder, current manic episode with psychotic features, was made. The patient was admitted for inpatient psychiatric stabilization and restarted on valproic acid 500 mg daily and olanzapine 10 mg which was tapered during admission as symptoms improved. During hospitalization, her mood and sleep progressively improved, psychotic symptoms resolved, and she no longer endorsed suicidal thoughts. Her medical history was notable for congenital anomalies consistent with DiGeorge syndrome, including a cleft lip and palate, syndactyly of the fingers, and a history of cardiac abnormalities manifested by heart murmurs, suggestive of an underlying congenital heart defect. Specific details regarding the exact cardiac lesion were not available at the time of admission. No documented immunodeficiency or thyroid dysfunction was identified during this hospitalization. She was discharged on valproic acid and olanzapine with outpatient psychiatric follow-up, medication-adherence counseling, and a safety plan, highlighting the need for vigilant psychiatric evaluation and structured suicide-risk screening in individuals with complex congenital anomalies.

Discussion

This case illustrates the complex interplay between genetic vulnerability, neurodevelopmental abnormalities, and severe mood disorder in a young adult with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (DiGeorge syndrome) and Bipolar I Disorder. Individuals with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome carry one of the highest known genetic risks for major psychiatric illness, including mood disorders, psychotic disorders, and suicidality [1], [5]. The patient’s acute manic presentation with psychosis and suicidal ideation highlights the multifactorial psychiatric vulnerability inherent to this genetic condition.

Neurodevelopmental and genetic considerations

DiGeorge syndrome results from a heterozygous microdeletion on chromosome 22q11.2 that encompasses approximately 30–50 genes critical to neurodevelopment [6]. Several genes within this locus have been implicated in dopaminergic regulation, synaptic plasticity, and cortical maturation, all of which are relevant to mood and psychotic disorders.

One of the most extensively studied genes in this region is COMT (catechol-O-methyltransferase), which plays a central role in dopamine degradation in the prefrontal cortex. Haploinsufficiency of COMT in individuals with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome results in altered dopamine signaling, impaired executive function, and increased vulnerability to psychosis and mood dysregulation [7]. Longitudinal studies have demonstrated that COMT variation predicts cognitive decline and psychotic symptom emergence in this population [8], suggesting that dopaminergic dysregulation may contribute not only to schizophrenia-spectrum disorders but also to bipolar disorder with psychotic features.

Another gene of interest, PRODH (proline dehydrogenase), influences glutamatergic and dopaminergic neurotransmission through its role in proline metabolism. Dysregulation of PRODH has been associated with elevated proline levels, altered NMDA receptor signaling, and behavioral abnormalities in both animal models and human studies [9]. Hyperprolinemia in individuals with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome has been linked to increased risk of psychosis and cognitive impairment, supporting a role for glutamate–dopamine interaction in psychiatric vulnerability.

Additionally, DGCR8, a gene essential for microRNA biogenesis, is disrupted in 22q11.2 deletion syndrome and has been shown to affect synaptic development and neuronal plasticity [10]. Impaired microRNA processing may result in widespread dysregulation of gene expression during brain development, further increasing susceptibility to mood instability and psychotic symptoms.

Neuroimaging studies of individuals with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome have demonstrated reduced cortical thickness, abnormal gyrification, and disrupted connectivity within frontolimbic networks involved in emotional regulation and executive control [11], [12]. These findings overlap significantly with neurobiological abnormalities observed in bipolar disorder and support a neurodevelopmental model in which gene dosage effects converge on circuits critical to mood regulation.

Although schizophrenia-spectrum disorders are most frequently emphasized in 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, bipolar disorder, particularly with psychotic features, is increasingly recognized as a significant clinical manifestation [13]. The patient’s presentation with mania and persecutory delusions is consistent with evidence that dopaminergic dysregulation associated with COMT haploinsufficiency may amplify psychotic features during mood episodes [5], [7].

Psychiatric vulnerabilities and suicide risk

Individuals with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome demonstrate markedly elevated rates of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts, even after controlling for psychiatric diagnosis [14]. This heightened risk is thought to reflect the combined effects of mood instability, impulsivity, cognitive limitations, impaired stress tolerance, and social dysfunction [1]. In the present case, suicidal ideation emerged in the context of untreated mania, severe insomnia, and medication nonadherence, all of which are well-established precipitants of acute suicide risk in Bipolar I Disorder [15].

Medication nonadherence is particularly prevalent among individuals with neurodevelopmental disorders and may be exacerbated by cognitive impairments, reduced insight during mood episodes, and limited psychosocial support [16]. The patient’s inconsistent use of valproic acid likely contributed to mood destabilization, with symptomatic improvement observed during medication adherence and relapse occurring in the context of non-adherence, underscoring the importance of structured adherence interventions.

Clinical implications

This case highlights the need for heightened clinical vigilance for mood and psychotic disorders in patients with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, particularly during periods of sleep disruption or medication nonadherence [5]. Acute behavioral changes or suicidal ideation in this population warrant prompt psychiatric evaluation and often inpatient stabilization due to the risk of rapid clinical deterioration [14].

Pharmacologic management should follow established guidelines for Bipolar I Disorder while accounting for medical comorbidities commonly associated with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, including congenital cardiac abnormalities and seizure risk [6]. The patient’s favorable response to valproic acid and olanzapine aligns with evidence supporting mood stabilizers and second-generation antipsychotics in bipolar disorder with psychotic features [15], [17]. Nonpharmacologic interventions, including psychoeducation, medication-adherence counseling, and coordinated multidisciplinary care, are essential to long-term management.

Future directions

Future research should focus on clarifying genotype–phenotype relationships in 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, particularly the roles of COMT, PRODH, and DGCR8 in mood regulation and psychosis risk. Longitudinal studies integrating genetic, neuroimaging, and clinical data may help identify biomarkers predictive of illness course and treatment response [8], [10]. Early psychiatric screening and longitudinal monitoring in genetically at-risk individuals may allow for earlier intervention and potentially reduce long-term psychiatric morbidity.

Conclusion

This case underscores the significant psychiatric vulnerability associated with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome and highlights bipolar disorder with psychotic features as an important manifestation within this genetic population. Disruption of genes involved in dopaminergic regulation and neurodevelopment, including COMT, PRODH, and DGCR8, likely contributed to the severity of mood dysregulation and psychosis observed in this patient [7], [9], [10]. Integrated, multidisciplinary care and vigilant longitudinal monitoring are essential to improving outcomes and reducing suicide risk in individuals with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome and comorbid Bipolar I Disorder.
