Introduction

ECG artifacts — waveforms from non-cardiac sources that mimic heart disease — pose diagnostic challenges in emergency care. While common artifacts are well-documented, their potential to generate localized repolarization abnormalities remains underexplored. Such artifacts may mimic acute coronary syndrome (ACS), leading to unnecessary invasive angiography [1–3]. Arterial pulsation artifacts can cause limb-lead ST/T changes that follow the “single-limb lead exemption principle” [4–6]. However, to our knowledge, no prior reports describe isolated mid-portion T-wave inversions in precordial leads — a previously unreported variant pattern observed in our case. This unusual pattern may serve as a valuable sign for differential diagnosis.

Here, we present a case where radial artery pulsation artifact mimicked ST-elevation myocardial infarction, initially prompting evaluation for ACS. Resolution of these ECG changes after electrode repositioning confirmed the artifact, highlighting the need for careful artifact recognition.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old woman presented to our emergency department with a one-hour history of chest tightness. Her medical history included hypertension for 3 years, managed with oral antihypertensive agents, and she reported adequate blood pressure control. Initial vital signs were: Blood Pressure (BP) 130/80 mmHg, Heart Rate (HR) 72 beats per minute (bpm), Respiratory Rate (RR) 22 breaths per minute, Temperature 37 °C. Initial laboratory investigations, including troponin, other cardiac biomarkers, liver function, renal function, and electrolytes, were unremarkable. An electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed ST-segment changes (Fig. 1).

Initial management and diagnostic dilemma

The junior emergency physician, considering the patient’s age, hypertension history, chest tightness, and the ECG findings, suspected possible Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) and recommended invasive coronary angiography. However, the senior emergency physician reviewed the ECG and noted atypical features compared to classic myocardial infarction patterns, raising suspicion of artifact, although the specific source was unclear. A repeat ECG (Fig. 2) showed persistent but diminished ST-segment elevation. Cardiology consultation was sought for further evaluation.

Timing of ECGs

The first ECG was recorded immediately upon presentation (t = 0 min). A repeat ECG was obtained approximately 10–15 min later after initial electrode inspection (t = 15 min, Fig. 2). The third ECG was recorded immediately after repositioning the limb electrodes (t = 20 min, Fig. 3).

Final electrocardiogram after definitive limb electrode repositioning. Complete resolution of all ST-segment and T-wave abnormalities, confirming the artifactual origin of the initial changes

Electrode placement

The left arm electrode was initially placed over the palpable brachial artery pulse at the mid-medial aspect of the left upper arm. The right arm electrode was placed over the deltoid muscle, away from the radial pulse. After recognizing the spared lead II pattern, the left arm electrode was repositioned approximately 3 cm laterally, away from the brachial artery pulsation. Normalization of the ECG after this maneuver confirmed that the left arm electrode was the source of the artifact.

Cardiology consultation and resolution

The consulting cardiologist considered that the ECG abnormalities were likely caused by an arterial pulsation artifact. Initial suspicion was directed toward the right radial artery based on its palpable pulsation. However, application of the exemption principle (spared lead II) pointed definitively to the left arm electrode as the source. After repositioning the limb electrodes away from the brachial artery pulsation, a third ECG was performed (Fig. 3). This revealed complete resolution of the ST-segment and T-wave abnormalities, showing a normal tracing (ECG not shown, but normalization described). To definitively rule out Non-ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (NSTEMI), an emergency Coronary Computed Tomography Angiography(CCTA) was performed, which revealed mild (< 30%) stenosis in the proximal left anterior descending (LAD) artery.The diagnosis was established as Coronary Artery Atherosclerosis. The chest tightness was attributed to anxiety following the initial ECG findings. No anti-ischemic medications (e.g., nitroglycerin, aspirin) were administered. The symptoms resolved spontaneously with reassurance and observation after the ECG was confirmed to be an artifact. This further supports that the symptoms were not due to acute ischemia.

Discussion

This case highlights the capacity of arterial pulsation to produce deceptive ECG changes and delineates a previously unreported variant pattern of mid-portion T-wave inversion in precordial leads, which may aid in artifact recognition.

Origins and localization of electrocardiographic artifacts

The accurate identification of ECG artifacts begins with recognizing their sources, which are broadly classified as technical, physiological, or environmental [7–11]. Our case focuses on a physiological source: arterial pulsation artifact. Such artifacts frequently obey the “single-limb lead exemption principle” [1, 2, 9], which is derived from the Einthoven triangle and Wilson Central Terminal (WCT) theory. This principle provides a vital diagnostic key: Specifically, a right arm artifact spares lead III, a left arm artifact spares lead II, and a left leg artifact spares lead I. Furthermore, interference from a limb electrode does not remain isolated; it can disseminate to the precordial leads because the WCT itself becomes contaminated and serves as the reference for all chest leads (V1-V6) [1]. The theoretical basis of the WCT and its role as a reference for unipolar leads is described in standard bioelectromagnetism texts [12–14].

Applying this framework to our case, the complete sparing of lead II was an pivotal observation. It definitively pointed to the left arm as the source of the artifact, thereby correcting our initial suspicion of the right radial artery. This demonstrates that a systematic approach—first analyzing the ECG for a spared lead to localize the source, followed by physical verification of the electrode—is fundamental to unmasking pulsation artifacts.

Mid-portion T-wave inversion in precordial leads

A Previously Unreported Variant Pattern. The most striking feature was a previously unreported variant pattern of repolarization artifact: isolated mid-portion T-wave inversions across leads V2–V6. Its signature characteristics—preserved initial T-wave morphology, a shallow focal mid-T notch (< 1 mm), and immediate normalization after technical correction—are pivotal for diagnosis. The key distinction from pathological T-wave inversions (e.g., the symmetric depth of Wellns’ or the asymmetric breadth of LVH) lies in its dynamic reversibility and precise spatiotemporal specificity.

To understand why the artifact selectively involves the mid‑portion of the T‑wave, we draw upon two complementary concepts: repolarization heterogeneity and an electromechanical hypothesis.

Repolarization heterogeneity as a background framework. The ventricular action potential duration differs across the myocardial wall (epicardium, M‑cells, and endocardium). These regional differences create a temporal window — typically corresponding to the mid‑portion of the T‑wave — during which epicardial repolarization is largely complete while M‑cell and endocardial repolarization are still ongoing [15]. This dispersion of repolarization means that net repolarization current is present but has already lost its earliest and latest components. Contemporary electrocardiography recognizes this principle; for example, the frontal QRS‑T angle, which reflects discordance between depolarization and repolarization vectors, has been shown to predict long‑term mortality in hemodialysis patients [16].

Electromechanical hypothesis as the primary mechanism. We hypothesize that the artifact in our patient is primarily electromechanical in nature. The mid‑systolic arterial pulsation may mechanically lift the overlying electrode for a brief duration (typically 40‑80 ms). This transient loss of stable electrode‑skin contact could generate a short‑lived impedance change, producing an electrical artifact that coincides temporally with the mid‑portion of the T‑wave. This hypothesis explains why the affected zone spans from the ST segment through the T‑wave, as the mechanical pulse wave has a finite duration. A common bedside observation supports this interpretation: in intensive care settings, when the ECG and oscillometric non‑invasive blood pressure waveform are displayed simultaneously, the trajectory of the oscillometric pulse wave coincides precisely with the time window during which this type of artifact is recorded on the ECG. We emphasize that this electromechanical hypothesis remains speculative and requires direct validation — for example, by simultaneous recording of the ECG and an arterial pulse waveform in future studies.`.

Differential diagnosis

The ECG pattern in our patient — inferior lead ST elevation with precordial T‑wave inversions — could, in other clinical contexts, represent several serious conditions that must be excluded. We considered the following:

Early myocardial infarction: ruled out by normal serial troponin, absence of dynamic ST‑T evolution after artifact resolution, and CCTA showing only mild (< 30%) LAD stenosis [18].

Limitations

Several limitations of this case report warrant acknowledgment. First, this is a single observation; the findings may not be generalizable to all patients with similar ECG changes. Second, the electromechanical hypothesis we propose — that mechanical lifting of the electrode by arterial pulsation generates the artifact — could not be directly confirmed, as simultaneous arterial pressure waveform recording was not performed during ECG acquisition. Third, the precise timing relationship between the radial pulse and the mid-T artifact was not documented with synchronized recordings. Fourth, we did not record video of electrode placement or the repositioning process, which could have provided visual confirmation. Future studies with simultaneous ECG and arterial waveform monitoring, as well as larger patient series, are needed to validate our observations and hypothesis.

Conclusion

We present, to our knowledge, the first documented case of a pulsation-induced ECG artifact manifesting as isolated mid-portion T-wave inversions in precordial leads — a previously unreported variant pattern of arterial pulsation artifact. This pattern, characterized by its focal appearance at the peak of repolarization and immediate reversibility upon electrode adjustment, may serve as a useful electrocardiographic clue for differentiating artifact from true myocardial ischemia. The integration of the limb lead exemption principle for source localization with the recognition of this precordial sign provides a practical diagnostic combination. We underscore the critical importance of a systematic diagnostic approach that includes lead-specific analysis and dynamic electrode repositioning in clinical practice. Adopting this strategy may help improve diagnostic accuracy, thereby reducing the risk of misdiagnosis and preventing unnecessary, invasive interventions in patients presenting with challenging ECG findings.
