Trends in prevalence of Trichomonas vaginalis among patients of an STI clinic in the Netherlands: a 9-year retrospective study
Authors: Carolina J G Kampman, Femke D H Koedijk, Froukje Bosma, Jeannine Hautvast, Christian JPA Hoebe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesPrevalences of Trichomonas vaginalis (TV) in Europe are low and consequently marginally tested by Dutch sexually transmitted infections (STI) clinics. We routinely tested all patients on all anatomical sites. We aimed to assess trends, prevalences among key populations and at different anatomical sites, as well as predictors of being TV positive to inform future testing practices and guideline development.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was performed between 2014 and 2022, using STI clinic data. Trends and positivity rates among key populations and anatomical locations were assessed using descriptive analyses. Sociodemographics and sexual behaviour variables were analysed to assess predictors of being TV positive, using multivariable regression analysis.ResultsA total of 37 577 consultations were performed in which patients were tested for TV; 16 075 among women and 21 502 among men. Overall TV positivity was 0.9% (145/16 075) among women and 0.3% (54/21 502) among men, and highest among women with a Netherlands Antillean ethnic background with 5.2% (11/212). TV positivity was lowest at the pharyngeal anatomical site (0.1%), followed by anorectal site (0.2%) and the urogenital anatomical site (0.5%).The following predictors of being TV positive were tested significant for men: being older (≥40 years, OR=5.1), having a Surinamese (OR=6.8) or Netherlands Antillean (OR=7.4) ethnic background and being gonorrhoea positive (OR=2.1). For women, the following predictors tested significant: being older (≥40 years, OR=3.3), having an Eastern European (OR=3.1), Western European (OR=2.2), Netherlands Antillean (OR=7.8) or Mid and South African (OR=4.0) ethnic background, having STI symptoms (OR=2.6) and being gonorrhoea positive (OR=2.6).ConclusionsBased on our results of 9 years of screening in an STI clinic setting, routine universal testing for TV is unnecessary. Testing patients, particularly women, is only required in symptomatic cases and might be considered in cases with an Eastern European, Netherlands Antillean or Mid and South African ethnic background.Keywords: TRICHOMONAS, Communicable Diseases, Epidemiology, HEALTH SERVICES RESEARCH, POLICY

Introduction

Trichomonas vaginalis (TV) is a parasite capable of inhabiting the human urogenital tract. Infections of the female genital tract can cause a range of symptoms including vaginitis and cervicitis. In men, TV infections are generally asymptomatic, but urethritis or prostatitis can occur. TV has also been associated with severe complications, including cervical cancer, prostate cancer and adverse pregnancy outcomes.1–3

TV is the most prevalent non-viral sexually transmitted infection (STI) worldwide. According to the WHO, approximately 156 million new cases occurred globally among individuals aged 15–49 in 2020, with 73.7 million cases in women and 82.6 million in men. Positivity rates of TV vary significantly across regions and populations, reflecting disparities in diagnostic practices, healthcare access and public health infrastructure.4 5 A systematic review reported a global prevalence of 5.3% in women (varying from 1.6% to 11.7%) and 0.6% in men (varying from 0.2% to 1.3%).6 The highest estimates are reported in Africa (35.8 million cases) and South America (20.2 million cases).4 6 In contrast, the prevalence of TV in Europe is comparatively low, with 4 million cases estimated by WHO, translating to 1.7% among women and 0.2% among men.3

In the Netherlands, patients attending STI clinics are not routinely tested for TV and are rarely tested based on symptomatic indications or a partner notification.7 Because of this, the number of TV diagnoses at the STI clinics in the Netherlands is declining. In 2023, 82 TV diagnoses were nationally reported among women, 7 among heterosexual men and 4 among men having sex with men (MSM) among all patients attending an STI clinic, while in 2009 a total of 223 patients tested positive for TV, of whom 169 were women.8 9 Such data are not available for Dutch general practitioners (GPs). However, two other Dutch studies systematically assessing TV positivity in a large group of patient samples (n=2079 and n=2592) from individuals undergoing STI testing at GPs found positivity rates of 1.5% (exclusively in women) and 0.4%, respectively.10 11

Extragenital TV, such as pharyngeal or anorectal infections, is less common than urogenital infections, but studies are very scarce and mostly based on selection of patient groups.12 One study among 500 men in the USA reported an incidence of anorectal TV of 0.6%.13 Though the efficacy, benefit and cost-effectiveness of extragenital testing on TV are unknown, most international guidelines do not recommend extragenital testing.3 14 15

Various international guidelines do not recommend routine universal testing on TV, due to low prevalences. The USA and UK recommend testing when women seek care when symptoms arise and in high prevalence areas.14 16 Dutch STI clinic guidelines recommend testing women with symptoms of vaginitis and men with a persistent urethritis and when a partner notification by current partner with STI symptoms is received. Routine universal testing for TV is considered not to be of public health importance.7 These recommendations are identical to the guidelines of the Dutch GPs.17

Given the limited testing efforts in Europe, the scarcity of robust studies on TV, and the potential biases caused by selection effects and underdiagnosis in most existing research, there remains a significant knowledge gap regarding positivity rates of this infection. Furthermore, little to no data are available on TV infections at pharyngeal and anal sites, highlighting a critical area for further investigation. To address this gap, our study leveraged data from 9 years of our STI clinic, where systematic and routine universal testing for TV was conducted across three anatomical sites (urogenital, pharyngeal and anorectal). This approach enables us to provide comprehensive insights into positivity rates, trends within key populations and determinants of TV positivity, thereby informing future testing practices and guideline development.

Methods

Study design, study population and data collection

A retrospective cross-sectional study was performed using STI clinic data of one STI clinic in the eastern part of the Netherlands, based in Enschede, between 2014 and 2022. Dutch STI clinics provide free of charge and anonymous STI testing and screening for key populations such as MSM and sex workers. Patients met the inclusion criteria when at least a genital swab was taken at a consultation during the study period and when patients were 16 years or older at inclusion time. Except for MSM and sex workers, not all patients were routinely tested on pharyngeal or anal anatomical sites. Only when patients reported having unprotected pharyngeal and/or anal sex they were tested on those sites. All swabs collected during consultations in the study period were tested for Neisseria gonorrhoea, Chlamydia trachomatis and TV, regardless of anatomical site, using an in-house developed, target-specific real-time PCR. A consultation was regarded as TV positive when the patient tested positive for TV on one or more anatomical sites within a consultation.

We excluded 110 (0.3%) consultations because of missing data in some of the variables; 79 consultations among persons who declared to be of ‘other’ gender, 11 consultations among patients with an unknown sexual preference and 20 consultations among patients with an unknown ethnic background. Finally, 37 577 consultations were included in the final analyses.

Repeat consultations were assessed based on an unique patient identifier. Based on this identifier, a total of 19 257 different patients had one or more consultations from July 2014 onwards. Until July 2014, a total of 1651 patients did not have this identifier; they were therefore excluded from the assessment of repeat consultations.

Variables

Demographic variables included sex (female or male), age (categorised in 16–24 years, 25–39 years and 40 years and older) and ethnic background. Consultations among persons who declared to be of ‘other’ gender were excluded from analysis, because of the risk of misclassification (79 consultations in total). Ethnic background was based on the person’s own country of birth combined with parental country of birth. We categorised Europeans as Dutch, Eastern European and Western European. Male sexual behaviour was categorised as heterosexual or MSM, based on reported same-sex sexual contact.

The following sexual behaviour variables were collected: number of partners (0–2 partners or 3 or more partners) and engaging in sex work (yes or no). Data on the presence of STI symptoms (yes or no) were collected, but not specified; symptoms specifically for TV were not routinely obtained. Receiving a partner notification (yes or no) could mean a notification for any STI, not specifically TV. In addition, being chlamydia or gonorrhoea positive (yes or no) within the same consultation was assessed.

Data analysis

The trends in TV positivity were assessed with a p<0.05 being statistically significant. Descriptive analyses were performed for all variables. Univariable and multivariable backward logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors for being TV positive, separately for men and women. All variables with a p value <0.2 in univariable analyses were included in the multivariable analyses. Predictors with p<0.05 were considered statistically significant in the multivariable analysis. ORs and 95% CIs were presented to show the size of the associations between the predictors and the outcome. Analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows, V.28.0 (IBM Inc, Somers, New York, USA).

Results

Study population and trends

From 2014 to 2022 a total of 37 577 consultations met our inclusion criteria and were included in our study population, 16 075 consultations were included among women and 21 502 among men. The overall TV positivity among women was 0.9% (145 diagnoses among 16 075 consultations) and 0.3% (54 diagnoses among 21 502 consultations) among men. The positivity rate of TV was highest among women over the years (compared to men), with a peak positivity rate of 1.6% in 2020 (18/1101). The increase in TV positivity over the years was statistically significant for women (p<0.01), but not for men (p=0.055), see figure 1.

Of all patients attending an STI clinic more than once (n=6360), 15 patients had one repeat TV infection, with a minimal interval period of 2.5 months between diagnoses. One patient was TV positive four times during the study period (in seven consultations) (data not shown).

In table 1, descriptive analysis results and positivity rates are displayed for men and women separately. After Dutch patients (n=12 670), most consultations were performed among Western (n=642) and Eastern European (n=554) women. The highest TV positivity rate among all patients regardless of ethnicity was found among women with a Netherlands Antillean background (5.2%). In total, 264 men reported doing sex work, none of them was TV positive, while there were 27 TV diagnoses among 1049 female sex workers (positivity rate 2.6%). Higher TV positivity rates were found among men and women who were also gonorrhoea positive upon consultation (0.8% in men and 5.7% in women).

TV and anatomical sites

Comparing all anatomical sites, pharyngeal testing yielded the lowest positivity rates (0.1% in men and 0.2% in women, 0.1% overall), followed by anorectal testing (0.1% in men and 0.5% in women) and urogenital testing (0.2% in men and 0.9% in women). When routinely tested on all three anatomical sites (10 736 consultations among men and 2247 consultations among women), one man tested positive for TV on all three sites and four women also did (data not shown).

Predictors of TV positivity

The predictors of TV positivity are shown in table 2. In multivariable analyses, men and women who are 40 years or older are more likely to be TV positive (men: OR 4.1, 95% CI 2.1 to 12.2; women: OR 3.3, 95% CI 2.0 to 5.4). Compared with Dutch attendees, men and women with a Netherlands Antillean ethnic background were more likely to be TV positive (men: OR 7.4, 95% CI 2.8 to 19.6; women: OR 7.8, 95% CI 3.9 to 15.2). Women with an Eastern European (OR 3.1, 95% CI 1.6 to 6.0) and Mid and South American (OR 4.0, 95% CI 2.0 to 8.1) ethnic background were also more likely to be TV positive. Furthermore, when experiencing symptoms, women were more likely to be TV positive (OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.8 to 3.7). For both men and women, being gonorrhoea positive on consultation was an independent predictor of being TV positive (men: OR 2.1, 95% CI 1.0 to 4.3; women: OR 5.4, 95% CI 3.3 to 9.0).

Discussion

Statement of principal findings

This study provides novel and comprehensive data on TV positivity across multiple anatomical sites, addressing key knowledge gaps identified in previous literature. In our large cohort of routine universally tested male and female patients on TV, the positivity rate was highest among women over the years. TV positivity was 0.9% among women and 0.3% among men, showing women have a higher burden of disease than men. Among patients tested on extragenital sites, TV positivity was lowest on the pharyngeal site (0.1%), followed by anorectal site (0.2%), compared with urogenital site (0.5%), showing that testing on anatomical sites other than urogenital is not warranted.

The results of the multivariable analysis show that being older and being gonorrhoea-positive was an independent predictor of being TV positive for both genders. Also, symptomatic women are more likely to be TV positive. Regarding ethnic background, men and women of Netherlands Antillean background, men of Surinamese background, women of Eastern, Western European and Mid and South American background are more likely to be TV positive.

Strengths and weaknesses

A major strength of this study is its systematic, universal testing approach across a large cohort in a low prevalence setting, making it, to our knowledge, the first European study to do so. Given that TV is not routinely tested in the Netherlands, true positivity rates have remained largely unknown. In this study, all patients were consistently tested for TV at all relevant anatomical sites and thus provided valuable and novel insights into TV positivity rates beyond the urogenital tract.

This study also has limitations. It was conducted at a single STI clinic located in the eastern region of the Netherlands, an area characterised by lower urbanisation, compared with other regions. As a result, the generalisability of our findings to more urbanised populations may be limited. However, a study performed in Amsterdam among male STI clinic patients found comparable low TV positivity of 0.3%.19 Second, the STI clinics in the Netherlands provide low threshold STI care for people at risk of an STI and/or belonging to a key population (eg, being <25 years, MSM, sex workers, having STI symptoms, etc), otherwise patients go to their GP for STI testing. This might cause some selection bias, favouring young patients to receive STI testing. In addition, this limits the generalisability of our findings to only (our) STI clinic patients. However, data from the same study period and region regarding TV testing at the GPs showed similar results: 83% of all STI tests performed by the GP included testing for TV, with a positivity of 0.9% (468/62,646) which is only a little higher but fairly similar to our results from the STI clinic (data not shown or published).

Comparison to other studies

Our findings demonstrate a positive association between older age and TV positivity, which is consistent with previous research. In a US-based study, higher TV positivity among older women was partly attributed to testing practices, as symptomatic testing was more common in older age groups, while younger women were more often tested through routine universal screening.20 A study involving 454 male STI clinic attendees in the USA found significantly higher positivity rates among men older than 30 years compared with those 30 years or younger (5.1% vs 0.8%).21

Several ethnic backgrounds have been shown to be associated with being TV positive for both men and women in our study. Several studies have demonstrated an ethnic disparity of TV, with the infection being more prevalent in non-Hispanic black women in the USA.22 23 In a US study among young adults, prevalence of TV was highest among black women (10.5%) and lowest in white women (1.1%). In men, the prevalence was highest among native Americans (4.1%) and lowest in white men (1.3%).22 In the UK, a systematic review showed that TV is disproportionately affecting people of black Caribbean ethnic background.24 Another UK study performed among the British general population, aged 16–44 years with at least one or more lifetime sexual partner(s), showed low overall prevalences of TV (weighted prevalence estimate of 0.3%), but higher in women with black or mixed ethnic background (2.7%).25

Being gonorrhoea positive is a strong independent predictor of being TV positive for both men and women in our study. This finding is not in line with most studies. In a US study among 7593 women aged 18–89 years, coinfections of TV with chlamydia (1.3%) and gonorrhoea (0.6%) were relatively low.20 In another US study, aiming to show whether TV is a reliable predictor of coinfection with chlamydia and/or gonorrhoea, findings show that the presence of TV made it 2.9 times less likely to have a coinfection with gonorrhoea and/or chlamydia.26 A possible explanation for the low coinfection rates of TV and other STIs observed in several studies is the age-related distribution of these infections. TV is more commonly detected in older adults, whereas Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae are predominantly found in younger populations.22

Interpretations

Our study shows that testing women who report having STI symptoms is more likely to be TV positive, a finding in line with the recommendation in the Dutch guideline. However, our dataset did not allow for the selection of specific vaginitis-related symptoms, limiting our ability to identify which symptoms may be indicative of a TV infection.

Furthermore, our study shows that extragenital testing is not warranted, due to low positivity rates of TV found at both pharyngeal and anorectal anatomical sites. Our recommendation is in line with other international guidelines, stating that the efficacy, benefit and cost-effectiveness of extragenital testing on TV are unknown.3 14 15

Dutch guidelines do not recommend targeted testing for TV based on ethnic background in either women or men. An interesting avenue for future research would be to distinguish between first-generation and second-generation immigrants when examining TV positivity rates, thereby studying whether second-generation immigrants may have a similar risk of TV infection as native Dutch individuals.

The finding that testing positive for gonorrhoea was an independent predictor of TV in our study, whereas chlamydia was not, is not fully understood. One possible explanation lies in the patient selection criteria applied at our STI clinic. Routine chlamydia screening is primarily targeted at younger individuals, while patients over 25 years of age are generally tested only when presenting with clear STI-related symptoms, such as urethral discharge or urethritis. This symptom-based testing approach may inadvertently enrich the tested population for gonorrhoea, which is more likely to present symptomatically than chlamydia, thereby increasing the likelihood of detecting co-infection with TV in this subgroup.

Conclusion

This large and systematic study, based on 9 years of routine universal testing data in an STI clinic in the Netherlands, provides valuable insights into the epidemiology of TV in a low-prevalence setting.

Given the overall low positivity rates observed across anatomical sites and populations, our results support a targeted rather than universal testing approach. Current Dutch guidelines—which advise TV testing in symptomatic women, in men with persistent symptoms beyond 4 weeks, and following partner notification—appear adequate for controlling the burden of TV. These findings may also be applicable to other low-prevalence settings, reinforcing the importance of evidence-based, risk-guided testing strategies over universal screening.
