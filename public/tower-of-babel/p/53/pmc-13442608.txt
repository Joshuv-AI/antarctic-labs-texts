First record of the parasite fauna of Serrasalmus geryi Jegú & Santos, 1988 (Characiformes: Serrasalmidae) and ecological implications of its invasion in a Neotropical floodplain
Authors: Gabriela Michelan, Lidiany Doreto Cavalcanti, Wagner Toshio Hasuike, Atsler Luana Lehun, Gabriela Cassia Zanon Reinas, Guilherme Guerino Altoé, Rodrigo Bravin Narciso, Ricardo Massato Takemoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiological invasions can alter ecological interactions, including parasite–host dynamics, with effects that are often gradual and difficult to predict. Serrasalmus geryi, native to the Tocantins–Araguaia basin, was recently recorded in the upper Paraná River floodplain (PIAP), Brazil. This study aimed to characterise, for the first time, the parasitic fauna of this species at an early stage of its introduction and to explore potential interactions with congeneric species. A total of 55 non-native individuals were collected between 2021 and 2024 and parasitological analyses included identification of ecto- and endoparasites. Additionally, a literature review was conducted to compare parasite assemblages with those of the native Serrasalmus maculatus and the established invasive Serrasalmus marginatus. Eleven parasite species were recorded, all reported for the first time in S. geryi, with three representing new records for the PIAP. Overall, parasite prevalence and abundance were low, and the assemblage was dominated by generalist taxa already present in the system. These findings are consistent with expectations for early invasion stages, suggesting that S. geryi is currently experiencing reduced parasitic pressure, possibly due to enemy release. The limited overlap with congeneric species indicates that parasite sharing and community restructuring are still incipient processes. This study provides a rare early-stage baseline of parasite–host interactions during a biological invasion in a natural system. The results contribute to invasion ecology by enabling future assessments of parasite acquisition, co-introduction, and transmission dynamics, and are relevant for understanding long-term ecological impacts on Neotropical floodplain ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00436-026-08716-z.Keywords: Invasive species, Parasitic ecology, Parasite-host interaction, Freshwater fish

Introduction

Long-term environmental monitoring is essential for identifying changes in the environment and its biological communities, whether these changes result from natural or anthropogenic processes (Roberto et al. 2009; Lehun et al. 2022; Michelan et al. 2024). These changes may include variations in abiotic factors, extinctions of local species, or the arrival of non-native individuals (Karling et al. 2013; Orlandi Neto et al. 2024; Rauber et al. 2025). Thus, the data obtained can support the prediction and mitigation of environmental impacts, contributing to the planning of management measures, the designation of permanent conservation areas, the prevention of animal mortality, and the improvement of water availability for various uses (Bailly et al. 2021; Dias et al. 2022; Espínola et al. 2025). In Brazil, the floodplain of the upper Paraná River (PIAP) has been monitored seasonally since 1986, with the collection and analysis of abiotic and biotic data from various communities, such as phytoplankton, periphyton, macrophytes, protozoa, zooplankton, macroinvertebrates, ichthyoplankton, fish, and ichthyoparasites.

The arrival of a non-native species in a given location is rarely detected, and few studies are able to track the invasion process from its earliest stages (Gozlan et al. 2010; Lockwood et al. 2013; Cavalcanti et al. 2024). The fish Serrasalmus geryi Jégu & Santos, 1988, for example, is native to the Tocantins-Araguaia basin, but was recorded in the Paranaíba River in 2010, which belongs to the Paraná basin, where it was introduced (Deprá et al. 2021). In 2019, individuals were captured in the PIAP, and their movement to the site is believed to have been facilitated by the presence of successive reservoirs that connect ecosystems within the basin and act as stepping stones, particularly for invasive species (Liew et al. 2016; Deprá et al. 2021). In this context, continuous monitoring of this site allowed the species to be recorded at an early stage of its arrival and establishment process, which will facilitate investigations into its adaptation and its interactions with the environment and native species.

When an organism arrives in a new environment, its introduction may be accompanied by its parasites (co-introduction) (Lymbery et al. 2014); thus, early documentation of the parasitic fauna associated with invasive fish species is essential for several reasons. First, co-introduced parasites may be transmitted to native hosts (spillover), whereas invasive hosts may acquire native parasites and contribute to their amplification and subsequent transmission within the invaded community (spillback) (see Esposito et al. 2023; Díaz-Morales et al. 2025; Rodríguez et al. 2025). Both processes have the potential to affect native biodiversity and ecosystem health. Therefore, characterizing the parasite communities of invasive species makes it possible to identify potential ecological risks, guiding management actions to prevent or mitigate outbreaks that threaten native species (Gobbin et al. 2024; Kudlai et al. 2024). Furthermore, these parasites can serve as bioindicators of environmental quality, revealing subtle environmental changes before they become detectable in free-living organisms (Lawson Handley et al. 2011; Rubio-Godoy and De León 2023; Occhibove et al. 2025).

Several theories seek to understand the process of invasion by parasites (see Roy and Lawson Handley 2012; Sarabeev et al. 2019), but many share the same limitations, such as a lack of information on the onset or timing of the invasion and a lack of knowledge about the native parasitic fauna of the environment, that is, prior to the arrival of the invader (Sarabeev et al. 2022; Michelan et al. 2024). In this regard, these records obtained in the early stages are essential for distinguishing between co-introduced parasites, locally acquired parasites, or even those lost during the dispersal process (enemy release), enabling more robust interpretations of transmission, parasite adaptation, and potential risks to the local biota (Kelly et al. 2009; Lymbery et al. 2014; Moraes et al. 2025).

Takemoto et al. (2009) and Lehun et al. (2020) compiled the parasitic fauna previously recorded in the PIAP, including parasites associated with two congeneric species of S. geryi: a native species, Serrasalmus maculatus Kner, 1858, and an invasive species, Serrasalmus marginatus Valenciennes, 1837. The latter was recorded in the PIAP after the formation of the Itaipu reservoir in 1982 (Agostinho and Júlio Junior 2002; Ota et al. 2018). The construction of the dam, located downstream of the plain, connected the lower and upper reaches of the Paraná River, allowing various species previously restricted to the lower Paraná to migrate and colonize the area (Cecilio et al. 1997; Júlio Júnior et al. 2009). Studies have examined the interaction between these two species in the PIAP, including competition for resources, feeding, reproduction, and parasitic fauna (Casali and Takemoto 2016; Alves et al. 2017; Rodrigues et al. 2021; Casali et al. 2023). In this scenario, the arrival of a third biologically and phylogenetically closely related species, S. geryi, may pose new challenges and potential impacts on the native congener and other local species (Rodrigues et al. 2021; Gouveia et al. 2025).

The survey of the parasitic fauna of the first non-native S. geryi individuals captured at PIAP will aid in future analyses of the interactions between their parasites and the local fauna over time. Furthermore, these data will serve as a basis for filling gaps in ecological theories regarding the dynamics of parasitism during and after the invasion process. Therefore, the objective of this study is to document, for the first time, the parasitic fauna of S. geryi collected in the PIAP since its introduction, as well as to provide insights into the interspecific interactions involving the congeneric species S. maculatus (native) and S. marginatus (invasive).

Materials and methods

Study area and host collection

The Paraná River is the main tributary of the La Plata basin and the second-longest river in South America, stretching approximately 3,089 km (Thomaz et al. 2004). Downstream of the Porto Primavera dam lies the region known as upper Paraná, characterized by an extensive floodplain (PIAP), especially on its right bank, which is seasonally flooded during high-water periods (Fig. 1). This stretch represents the only undammed portion of the Paraná River in Brazil, where the Núcleo de Pesquisas em Limnologia, Ictiologia e Aquicultura (Nupélia) in the Universidade Estadual de Maringá.

Map of the upper Paraná River floodplain, Brazil (PIAP), where specimens of Serrasalmus geryi Jégu & Santos, 1988 were collected

To survey the parasitic fauna of S. geryi, 55 juveniles and adult specimens were collected at PIAP between 2021 and 2024. The fish were sampled during all four seasons in different habitats of the floodplain as part of the Long-Term Ecological Research Project (LTER-CNPq). The hosts were captured using set nets of different mesh sizes and euthanized in accordance with SISBIO collection authorization Nº 52596-6 and CEUA/UEM approval Nº 9251160223. For each specimen, the sampling site, total and standard length (in centimeters), and total mass (in grams) were recorded, the standard length ranged from 7.7 to 21.2 cm (mean ± SD = 16.82 ± 2.45) and weight ranged from 13.15 to 477.16 g (mean ± SD = 201.35 ± 93.33). They were then sent to the Laboratório de Ictioparasitologia of the Nupélia and eviscerated for parasitological analysis.

Processing and identification of parasites

To compare the parasites recorded in this study in S. geryi with those of the two related species found in the PIAP, a literature review was conducted of all the parasitic fauna found in S. maculatus and S. marginatus. Parasites identified at the family taxonomic level were considered. The parasites collected from S. geryi individuals were preserved and prepared according to the parasitological methods described by Eiras et al. (2006). Initially, the external surface of the fish was examined to detect ectoparasites; subsequently, the nostrils and gills were analyzed using a stereomicroscope, and the Monopisthocotylea and Copepoda found were fixed in 70% alcohol. After removing the gills, a ventral incision was made in the fish to determine their sex; subsequently, the internal organs were removed, separated, and examined individually under a stereomicroscope. The endoparasites found were also preserved in 70% alcohol, and the nematodes were cleared with lactic acid. Parasite identification followed references by Moravec (1998), Thatcher (2006), Cohen et al. (2013), identification keys, and current articles in the field. Representative helminth specimens were deposited in the Coleção Helmintológica do Instituto Oswaldo Cruz (CHIOC).

Data analysis

To analyze the S. geryi community in the PIAP, the indices of prevalence (P), mean intensity (MI), and mean abundance (MA) of the parasitic infection/infestation were used, in accordance with Bush et al. (1997). Prevalence is the number of host individuals infected by a given parasite species, divided by the number of host individuals analyzed and multiplied by 100 (result expressed as a percentage; %). The average abundance is the total number of parasites of a given species divided by the total number of fish sampled. Finally, the average intensity is the total number of parasites observed of a given species divided by the number of infected host individuals.

Results

Ectoparasites

Of the 55 fish analyzed, 23 were infested with at least one species of ectoparasite. In total, 78 parasite individuals were collected, belonging to five species, four of which were Monopisthocotylea: Anacanthorus rondonensis Boeger & Kritsky, 1988 (CHIOC 40892), Anacanthorus serrasalmi Van Every & Kritsky, 1992 (CHIOC 40891), Notothecium modestum Kritsky, Boeger & Jégu, 1998 (CHIOC 40893), and Rhinoxenus euryxenus Domingues & Boeger 2005 (CHIOC 40894); and one copepod of the family Ergasilidae Burmeister, 1835: Gamispatulus sp., recorded in the nasal cavities. The highest prevalence rate was observed for R. euryxenus, with 21.82% of hosts parasitized by at least one individual of this species, followed by N. modestum at 9.09%, which also had the highest average abundance, while the highest average intensity was recorded for A. serrasalmi (Table 1).

Endoparasites

Of the 55 fish analyzed, 16 were infected with at least one species of endoparasite. In total, 76 parasite individuals were found, distributed among six species, including one Digenea: Austrodiplostomum compactum (Lutz, 1928), and five Nematoda: Contracaecum sp. Parasitic fauna of congeners

In the survey of parasites recorded for species congeneric to S. geryi in the PIAP, 36 species were identified, including 16 for S. maculatus (native) and 20 for S. marginatus (invasive). Of the total recorded, 12 species were ectoparasites and 24 were endoparasites, with the phylum Nematoda standing out as the most diverse, with 20 species. Individuals of S. geryi share three species with S. maculatus: P. (S.) inopinatus, Contracaecum sp. type 1, and Contracaecum sp. type 2, in addition to two taxa identified only at the genus level (Eustrongylides and Cucullanus). Serrasalmus geryi shares two species with the invasive S. marginatus: P. (S.) inopinatus and Contracaecum sp. type 1, in addition to a taxon identified at the genus level (Cucullanus). Finally, none of the ectoparasites recorded in this study for S. geryi had been previously reported in the congeners occurring in the PIAP. Discussion

Eleven parasite species were recorded for the first time in non-native S. geryi individuals collected from the Paraná River, including five ectoparasites and six endoparasites. Furthermore, three of these species represent new records for the PIAP (A. rondonensis, A. serrasalmi and N. modestum), expanding our understanding of regional parasitic biodiversity. This result is particularly relevant because the hosts were sampled only two years after the species was first recorded at the site (Deprá et al. 2021), making this study unprecedented due to the parasitological characterization conducted during an early stage of invasion in a natural environment (Bernery et al. 2022).

Early documentation of the parasite fauna associated with an invasive fish is essential because many ecological theories predict different possibilities for parasite adaptation during a biological invasion (see Torchin et al. 2003; Telfer and Bown 2012; Goedknegt et al. 2016; Sarabeev et al. 2017). However, the lack of pre-invasion parasitological data (e.g., from native fauna, congeneric species, or established invasive species) and the scarcity of records obtained immediately after the arrival of a non-native species are limiting factors for testing these hypotheses (Calhoun et al. 2018; Pofuk 2021). Without this prior, baseline knowledge, it becomes difficult to identify which parasites were already present in the system, which are introduced with the new host, and which may be acquired or lost during the process of establishment and expansion. Thus, the data presented here not only document the parasitic diversity associated with S. geryi in the introduced environment, but also establish an essential baseline for understanding future changes in the composition and structure of these communities, as well as for assessing ecological risks arising from the maintenance and expansion of the species in the Paraná River.

Ectoparasites

Among the ectoparasites, four of the five species found belong to the Subclass Monopisthocotylea, with R. euryxenus being the second most prevalent in this study. It is common for species of the genus Rhinoxenus Kritsky, Boeger & Thatcher, 1988 to parasitize the nasal cavity of their hosts, especially Characiformes (Teleostei, Ostariophysi) (Domingues and Boeger 2005; Rossin et al. 2019). In the PIAP, three species of this genus were found in seven fish species (see Lehun et al. 2020), including R. euryxenus, which was originally described parasitizing the invasive congener S. marginatus in the Paraná River (Domingues and Boeger 2005). As for the other species of the genus Notothecium and Anacanthorus, in addition to being recorded for the first time in S. geryi in this study, they also represent the first records in the Paraná River basin. Previous records of N. modestum, for example, are from the Amazon basin in individuals of Serrasalmus spilopleura Kner, 1858 (Kohn and Cohen 1998; Kritsky et al. 1998). Lehun et al. (2020) recorded Notothecium sp. in S. marginatus at PIAP; however, the identification was limited to the genus level, making it impossible to determine whether it is the same species observed in this study.

Species of the genus Anacanthorus are commonly associated with hosts of the family Serrasalmidae. Several species have been reported infecting serrasalmid fishes, including congeners of S. geryi (Alexandre and Yamada 2022; Silva et al. 2024). In addition to representing the first record in S. geryi, the occurrence of A. rondonensis and A. serrasalmi in the Paraná River basin expands the known geographic distribution of these parasites. Previous studies indicate that many Anacanthorus species exhibit high host specificity, possibly reflecting coevolutionary processes within Serrasalmidae (Ortí et al. 2008; Moreira et al. 2019).

The other ectoparasite found was a copepod of the family Ergasilidae. Species of the genus Gamispatulus have previously been reported parasitizing the nasal cavities of Characiformes from the PIAP, including S. marginatus and S. maculatus (Lacerda et al. 2007; Narciso et al. 2025). However, records of this genus associated with Serrasalmus species remain limited. The Gamispatulus sp. specimen found in S. geryi showed morphological differences from previously described species (Narciso and Da Silva 2020), reinforcing the need for future integrative taxonomic studies involving morphological and molecular approaches.

Endoparasites

Most species belong to the phylum Nematoda and are considered generalists, as they parasitize various fish species (Takemoto et al. 2009; Lehun et al. 2020). It is worth noting that there were already records of these nematodes in both congeners of S. geryi occurring in the PIAP (Casali and Takemoto 2016; Lehun et al. 2020), with the sole exception being Eustrongylides sp. (larva), for which, in the study area, there were reports only in the native S. maculatus (Lehun et al. 2020). This parasite, belonging to the family Dioctophymatidae Railliet, 1915, as well as the larvae of the genus Contracaecum (Anisakidae) that were found, are parasites with zoonotic potential (Lima et al. 2009; De Souza et al. 2016; Melo et al. 2016).

The low abundance of larval nematodes and metacercariae suggests that S. geryi may act as an intermediate or paratenic host for these parasites. The most abundant parasite, Procamallanus (Spirocamallanus) inopinatus, although this is the first record in S. geryi, has a wide geographic distribution in South America (Neves et al. 2020; Cárdenas et al. 2022). Furthermore, this species has already been reported in several host families, including Serrasalmidae, where it has been recorded in the following genera: Serrasalmus, Pygocentrus, Myloplus, Metynnis, Colossoma, and Mylossoma (Neves et al. 2020).

Austrodiplostomum compactum is a widely distributed digenean reported from several fish hosts, including congeners of S. geryi such as S. maculatus and S. elongatus (Yamada et al. 2008; Souza et al. 2017). In these hosts, fish act as second intermediate hosts harboring metacercariae in ocular tissues (Ramos et al. 2013; Affonso et al. 2017).

Ecological implications of the introduction of Serrasalmus geryi

Invasion biology generally recognizes three main stages in the invasion process: introduction, establishment, and dispersal with ecological impacts (García-Berthou 2007). In this case, S. geryi has spent a relatively short time in the PIAP, compared to the timescale over which many impacts become evident (Gozlan et al. 2010; Britton 2023). Previous studies indicate that the effects of introduced species can vary over time, being, in some cases, noticeable right at the beginning of the introduction and subsequently attenuated, while in other systems the impacts appear after many years (Závorka et al. 2018; Bernery et al. 2024).

The presence of phylogenetically closely related species inhabiting the PIAP, such as S. maculatus (native) and S. marginatus (invasive), may indicate that the environment offers suitable conditions for establishment, since the availability of resources may allow for the coexistence of ecologically similar species (Chesson 2000; McPeek 2022; Rodrigues et al. 2018), and this similarity may facilitate the survival and reproduction of non-native fish (Xu et al. 2024). On the other hand, close kinship also increases the potential for negative interactions, such as competition for resources and hybridization, processes capable of altering population dynamics and compromising the genetic integrity of the native species over time (Xu et al. 2024; Gouveia et al. 2025; Haubrock et al. 2025). In this context, community responses and future trajectories are particularly uncertain and potentially nonlinear.

At present, it is not possible to determine whether S. geryi will become invasive in the PIAP. However, continued monitoring of its distribution, abundance, and interactions with native congeners will be important to evaluate possible long-term ecological effects. In addition, the occurrence of S. geryi in the PIAP may represent a facilitated range expansion rather than a classical human-mediated translocation, which should be considered when interpreting the invasion process and associated ecological dynamics.

Given that the species was introduced only a few years ago, both coexistence and future invasive potential remain plausible. The coexistence of native and non-native congeners may provide opportunities for future studies on interspecific interactions in this system. Long-term monitoring of the abundance, dispersal, and ecological interactions of S. geryi will be important to evaluate its establishment and ecological effects in the PIAP.

Parasitological implications of the introduction of Serrasalmus geryi

The recent occurrence of S. geryi in the PIAP represents an early stage of range expansion/invasion, and the present study provides only an initial snapshot of its parasite fauna. Because no temporal comparisons or parasitological data from the native range are available, processes such as enemy release, parasite acquisition, spillover, or spillback cannot be directly tested here. Several studies conducted in freshwater and marine systems indicate that, in the early stages of introduction, non-native hosts often exhibit low parasite richness compared to native counterparts or older invaders (Sheath et al. 2015; Deflem et al. 2022). This pattern is attributed to the enemy release theory (see Torchin et al. 2003), in which only a small fraction of the original parasitic fauna is retained during the translocation process (Sarabeev et al. 2017; Michelan et al. 2023). Thus, the low parasite richness and abundance observed in S. geryi, compared with sympatric congeners, is consistent with patterns expected during early invasion stages and may reflect reduced parasite accumulation in the newly established population (Jiménez-Sánchez et al. 2022).

The co-introduction of parasites represents a second significant process during the early stages of invasion (see Taraschewski 2006). Global reviews show that helminths are often transported along with their introduced hosts (Esposito et al. 2023), although not all of them are able to complete their life cycles in the receiving area. As for ectoparasites, they are generally expected to remain confined to the new host during the initial co-introduction process (Sarabeev et al. 2022). This restriction is more likely in parasites with high host specificity or complex life cycles (Schatz and Park 2023). However, given the presence of congeners, the sharing of habitats and food webs may, over time, create opportunities for these parasites to expand to other phylogenetically closely related hosts (Braga et al. 2015; Šimková et al. 2019).

If the population persists and expands, the acquisition of native parasites may increase over time, as reported for other introduced fishes (Hohenadler et al. 2019). It is known that non-native fish frequently acquire local generalist parasites, which can lead to a gradual increase in total parasite richness, approaching that observed in native species (Sheath et al. 2015). In this context, parasites already recorded in S. maculatus and S. marginatus, especially those with complex life cycles and trophic transmission, can be the most likely to colonize S. geryi in the medium term. If the population density and spatial distribution of the new host increase, S. geryi could act as an additional reservoir, amplifying the transmission of these parasites and promoting spillback effects on native and older invasive species (Kelly et al. 2009; Rodríguez et al. 2025).

However, the acquisition of native parasites does not always result in negative consequences. Serrasalmus geryi may not be a potential host and, consequently, may reduce infection pressure on its conspecifics, promoting a dilution effect (see Telfer and Bown 2012). Alternatively, the species may acquire only a subset of native parasites, without contributing significantly to their transmission, due to differences in habitat use, diet, or behavior (Goedknegt et al. 2016).

Different parasite-related processes, such as acquisition, spillover, spillback, or dilution, have been described in other invasion systems and may also occur in the long term in S. geryi (Fig. 2) (Kołodziej-Sobocińska et al. 2018; Llopis-Belenguer et al. 2020; Llaberia-Robledillo et al. 2022). However, these processes were not evaluated in the present study and remain hypothetical at this stage of the invasion. The presence of three congeneric species in the system may facilitate future changes in parasite associations and transmission dynamics (Costa et al. 2025).

Conceptual model of the expected parasitic scenarios following the introduction of Serrasalmus geryi Jegú & Santos, 1988 into the upper Paraná River floodplain (PIAP), along a temporal gradient of invasion. In the initial stages, the release of predators of the introduced species tends to occur, leading to a reduction in parasite diversity and abundance. The co-introduction of parasites may occur, initially restricted to the new host. With the increase in abundance and ecological overlap between Serrasalmus geryi, the native congener (Serrasalmus maculatus Kner, 1858) and the invasive congener (Serrasalmus marginatus Valenciennis, 1837), processes of native parasite acquisition become more frequent, potentially leading to spillback or spillover and, consequently, to the restructuring of host-parasite interactions

Conclusion

This study represents the first survey of the parasite fauna of Serrasalmus geryi in the upper Paraná River floodplain (PIAP) and expands the knowledge of parasite diversity associated with this recently established species. The parasite community was composed of ecto- and endoparasites already known from other serrasalmid fishes, including species shared with native and invasive congeners occurring in the PIAP. In addition, some parasite species recorded in this study represent new host and geographic records.

The low parasite richness and abundance observed in S. geryi are consistent with patterns commonly reported during early invasion stages and provide an initial baseline for future studies on host–parasite dynamics in this system. Because the occurrence of S. geryi in the PIAP appears to be recent, long-term monitoring will be important to evaluate possible changes in parasite composition, host associations, and ecological interactions over time. Further studies including populations from the native range of S. geryi will also be necessary to better understand the origin and acquisition of the parasite fauna recorded in the invaded area.
