Chemically profiled total saponins of Panax japonicus C.A. Meyer attenuate rheumatoid arthritis-associated bone erosion and modulate osteoclastogenesis-associated SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB signaling
Authors: Mei Yang, Jingkai Zhang, Xiang Guo, Yushen Chen, Zhigang Mei, Xiaoqiang Hou, Zhitao Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRheumatoid arthritis (RA)-associated bone erosion is largely driven by excessive osteoclast differentiation and activation. Total saponins of Panax japonicus C.A. Meyer (TSPJs), a ginseng-derived botanical fraction, show anti-inflammatory activity in RA-related settings, but their effects on osteoclast-mediated bone destruction remain unclear.MethodsTranscriptomic datasets from RA synovial tissues and RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation were integrated to identify shared pathogenic signals. TSPJs were chemically profiled by HPLC and evaluated in collagen-induced arthritis mice and RANKL-stimulated RAW264.7 cells. Osteoclast formation and function were assessed by TRAP staining, F-actin ring analysis, gene expression, bone resorption assays, histopathology, radiography, and serological measurements. Network pharmacology, molecular docking, CETSA, immunofluorescence, Western blotting, and immunohistochemistry were used to explore candidate mechanisms.ResultsIntegrated bioinformatics highlighted NF-κB-related signaling as a shared pathway linking RA and osteoclastogenesis. HPLC identified four representative saponins: araloside A, chikusetsusaponin IVa, ginsenoside Rg2, and ginsenoside Ro. In CIA mice, TSPJs reduced arthritis severity, improved joint pathology, lowered osteoclast-associated serum markers, and attenuated radiographic and histological bone erosion. In vitro, TSPJs inhibited RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation, disrupted F-actin rings, downregulated osteoclast-related genes, and reduced bone resorption. The overall evidence was consistent with modulation of SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB signaling, while docking and CETSA supported SRC/NFATc1/FOS-related nodes as candidates for further validation.ConclusionTSPJs attenuated RA-associated bone erosion and restrained osteoclastogenesis, supporting further evaluation of this Panax japonicus-derived saponin preparation.Keywords: Bone erosion, Collagen-induced arthritis, Osteoclastogenesis, Panax japonicus, Rheumatoid arthritis, Total saponins

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder in which persistent synovitis progressively culminates in structural joint damage [1]. Erosive change carries clear clinical consequences; once established, it is associated with functional impairment and long-term disability [2]. A substantial part of this destructive phenotype is osteoclast-driven. Even in the era of disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs and biologics, many patients do not achieve sustained disease control, and treatment decisions are frequently constrained by infection risk, cost, and tolerability over time[[3], [4], [5]]. In this setting, interventions that directly attenuate osteoclast differentiation and bone destruction remain of practical interest [6,7].

Osteoclastogenesis is coordinated by an interconnected signaling network in which the Rous sarcoma proto-oncogene (SRC) and inflammatory cascades occupy a central position[[8], [9], [10]]. SRC, a non-receptor tyrosine kinase, is required for cytoskeletal organization and the resorptive machinery of mature osteoclasts [11]. SRC also interfaces with TRAF6-linked signaling to influence NF-κB activation, a pathway positioned at the intersection of inflammatory programs and osteoclast differentiation [12,13]. Yet, in RA, how the SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB axis integrates into the pharmacological modulation of erosive disease remains incompletely defined.

Panax japonicus C.A. Meyer is a medicinal species enriched in triterpenoid saponins and has been increasingly investigated for anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and vasculature-related activities [14,15]. Total saponins of Panax japonicus C.A. Meyer (TSPJs) were selected because saponin-rich Panax japonicus preparations have shown activity in RA-relevant inflammation, angiogenesis, macrophage polarization, and tissue-protective contexts [16,17].

These observations provide a pharmacological rationale for evaluating TSPJs in erosive arthritis, where inflammation and osteoclast-driven bone destruction are closely coupled. However, whether TSPJs directly attenuate osteoclast-mediated bone erosion, and which signaling events are most closely associated with this effect, remain insufficiently defined.

In this study, transcriptomic evidence was integrated with HPLC-based chemical profiling, network pharmacology, and in vivo/in vitro validation to evaluate TSPJs in RA-associated bone erosion. RA synovial datasets, a receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL)-driven osteoclast model, collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mice, and complementary cellular readouts were used to assess whether TSPJs restrain osteoclastogenesis and to delineate the pathways most consistent with the observed effects. Our data indicate that TSPJs alleviate joint injury and bone erosion while suppressing osteoclast differentiation. The SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB pathway emerged as a plausible mechanistic hub, although pathway dependency requires further validation.

Materials and methods

Drugs and main reagents

Key reagents and materials included TSPJs; kits for cell viability and TRAP staining; recombinant murine RANKL; ELISA kits for TRAP, CTSK, and MMP9; rhodamine–phalloidin; reverse transcription and SYBR-based qPCR reagents; bovine bone slices; RIPA lysis buffer and a BCA protein assay; primary antibodies against SRC, TRAF6, NF-κB p65, NFATc1, FOS, and GAPDH; and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Vendor and catalog details are listed in Supplementary Table A1.

Bioinformatics analysis of RA bone erosion

Public transcriptomic datasets of RA synovial tissue (GSE89408) and RANKL-driven osteoclast differentiation (GSE74847) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Differential expression was evaluated using prespecified thresholds of P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 0.585. Overlapping DEGs between the two datasets were identified by Venn-based intersection analysis using SangerBox [18]. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were subsequently conducted to nominate biological processes and signaling pathways that may contribute to RA-associated bone erosion. Bioinformatics findings were used as hypothesis-generating evidence and were not interpreted as proof of pathway dependency.

CIA mouse model

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experimentation of China Three Gorges University (Ethics No. 2019B0501) and were carried out in accordance with institutional guidelines. Male DBA/1J mice were used to establish the CIA model. Briefly, bovine type II collagen was emulsified with complete Freund's adjuvant for primary immunization on day 0 and with incomplete Freund's adjuvant for booster immunization on day 21, following a standard DBA/1J CIA protocol [19]. Model establishment was evaluated by paw erythema, swelling, and arthritis scoring after immunization. Mice were then randomly assigned to control, CIA, low-dose TSPJs, and high-dose TSPJs groups. TSPJs were administered intragastrically at 30 or 150 mg/kg/day, respectively [16]. Paw swelling, arthritis score, the number of swollen joints, and overall condition were monitored longitudinally throughout the observation period. All in vivo animal experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates; in each independent experiment, five mice were included per group to minimize procedural variability. At study termination, mice were euthanized, and blood and joint tissues were harvested for downstream analyses.

Overall animal experimental schedule

The overall experimental workflow consisted of primary collagen immunization, booster immunization, clinical monitoring for CIA development, daily TSPJs intervention after model establishment, terminal blood collection, and joint tissue harvesting for radiographic, histological, biochemical, and molecular analyses.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

At the end of the experiment, blood samples were collected, allowed to clot, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min to obtain serum. Serum levels of TRAP, CTSK, and MMP9 were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers' instructions. Absorbance was recorded with a microplate reader, and concentrations were calculated based on standard curves.

Histopathological analysis

Joint tissues were fixed, decalcified, paraffin embedded, and sectioned sagittally at 5 μm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Safranin O/Fast Green (SO/FG), and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) using standard procedures. Histopathologic features, including synovial inflammation, cartilage damage, and bone erosion, were evaluated from coded sections in a blinded manner [20]. Cartilage injury was scored using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) system [21]. TRAP-positive multinucleated osteoclasts were enumerated in representative joint sections as previously reported [22]. For image-based quantification, identical regions of interest and consistent threshold settings were applied across groups.

Radiographic X-ray analysis

Endpoint X-ray images of the hind paws and ankle joints were acquired under identical imaging conditions for all groups. Images were coded before analysis. Radiographic bone preservation was evaluated by comparing cortical continuity, joint-space destruction, and X-ray absorption values in identical regions of interest. The X-ray data were used as supportive structural evidence together with histopathology and osteoclast-related markers.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis

Dried rhizomes of Panax japonicus were pulverized and extracted with ethanol to obtain the total saponin fraction. The chemical profile of TSPJs was analyzed by HPLC [23]. Representative chromatographic peaks were assigned by matching to reference standards, including araloside A, chikusetsusaponin IVa, ginsenoside Rg2, and ginsenoside Ro. Because quantitative marker content, extraction yield, total saponin content, and batch-to-batch consistency were not systematically determined in the present study, the preparation is described as chemically profiled rather than fully chemically standardized.

Network pharmacological analysis

A component-target-pathway network was constructed using Cytoscape (version 3.10.2) to visualize the potential relationships among major TSPJ components, predicted targets, and RA bone erosion-related pathways. Protein-protein interaction analysis was used to identify candidate hub targets for subsequent validation.

Target prediction and standardization of TSPJs components

The two-dimensional structures and Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) strings for the four representative TSPJs components were obtained from publicly accessible databases. Putative protein targets were predicted using the corresponding in silico platforms, standardized using the UniProt database, and intersected with RA bone erosion-related targets to identify candidate therapeutic nodes.

Molecular docking

Crystal structures of SRC; PDB ID: 1A07), NFATc1 (PDB ID: 5SVE), FOS (PDB ID: 1FOS), and MMP9 (PDB ID: 1GKC) were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank. Molecular docking was then carried out to estimate the predicted binding tendency of representative TSPJs components toward these candidate targets. Docking scores and binding conformations were leveraged for comparative assessment [24]. Docking results were interpreted as computational predictions that require experimental confirmation.

Cell culture and safe drug concentration screening

RAW264.7 cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Cell viability following TSPJs exposure was evaluated by the CCK-8 assay to define a non-cytotoxic concentration range for subsequent osteoclast differentiation experiments.

Osteoclast differentiation and drug intervention

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 1 × 10^4 cells/well and cultured with RANKL to induce osteoclast differentiation. Cells were assigned to the control, RANKL, TSPJs low-, middle-, and high-dose groups, and, where indicated, a positive-control group. TSPJs were added at the designated concentrations during the differentiation period, and the cells were collected for morphological, molecular, and functional assays.

TRAP staining

After RANKL-driven differentiation, cells were fixed and stained using a TRAP kit. TRAP-positive multinucleated cells containing at least three nuclei were counted as osteoclasts. Three randomly selected high-power fields per well were analyzed in a blinded manner using ImageJ.

Rhodamine-phalloidin staining

Cells were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and incubated with rhodamine-phalloidin for 30 min. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min at room temperature. F-actin ring structures were observed under a fluorescence microscope, and ring integrity and size were quantified using ImageJ.

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript Reverse Transcription Kit. Quantitative PCR was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system using SYBR Green Master Mix. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table A2. GAPDH served as the internal control, and relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method.

Bone erosion assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded onto bovine bone slices (2 × 10^4 cells/well) and differentiated under the indicated treatment conditions. After 4 days, cells were removed, and the bone slices were cleaned by ultrasonication for 10 min. Resorption pits were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). SEM images were captured from comparable fields on each bone slice, and the resorbed area was quantified with ImageJ using identical threshold criteria across groups.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

For CETSA, RANKL-stimulated cells were assigned to two conditions: RANKL alone and RANKL plus TSPJs (40 μg/mL, 12 h). Cells were lysed, and equal volumes of lysate were dispensed into PCR tubes and subjected to a temperature gradient (37, 44, 51, 58, 65, or 72°C) for 4 min, followed by rapid cooling on ice for 3 min. Following centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min), soluble supernatants were collected for immunoblotting of SRC, NFATc1, and FOS [25,26]. Band intensities were normalized to the 37°C condition, and thermal stability profiles were plotted in GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Immunofluorescence analysis

To assess NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation, cells or sections were incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C and then reacted with FITC-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Representative images were captured under a fluorescence microscope, and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor, and protein concentration was determined by the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against SRC (1:5000), TRAF6 (1:5000), NF-κB p65 (1:15000), NFATc1 (1:8000), FOS (1:10000), and GAPDH (1:5000). Membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:3000) for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized by ECL and quantified using ImageJ as the ratio of target protein to GAPDH.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase was quenched with 3% H2O2, and nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% normal goat serum (30 min, room temperature). Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against SRC, TRAF6, NF-κB p65, NFATc1, and FOS (1:100), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody incubation (50 min, room temperature). Signal was developed with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. Staining was quantified as integrated optical density (IOD) using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism 9. Comparisons among groups were performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple-comparison post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results.

Results

Identification of common pathogenic targets and signaling pathways between RA and osteoclasts

To explore the molecular link between RA and osteoclast-mediated bone erosion, we integrated transcriptomic data from GEO. In the RA synovial tissue dataset GSE89408, 9175 DEGs satisfied the prespecified thresholds (P < 0.05; fold change ≥1.5), and multiple osteoclast-related genes, including SRC and NFATc1, were enriched among the upregulated transcripts (Fig. 1A). In the RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation dataset GSE74847, 727 DEGs were identified (Fig. 1B). Venn analysis revealed 264 overlapping genes between the two datasets (Fig. 1C), suggesting a shared transcriptional basis between the RA synovial microenvironment and osteoclast differentiation. GO analysis showed enrichment in biological processes related to osteoclast differentiation, function, and bone response (Fig. 1D), whereas KEGG analysis highlighted the NF-κB and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways (Fig. 1E). These findings provided the rationale for focusing on inflammatory osteoclastogenic signaling in subsequent analyses.

(B) Volcano plot of DEGs in the GSE74847 dataset (osteoclast differentiation). (C) Venn diagram showing 264 shared DEGs between the two datasets. (D) The top 10 Gene Ontology biological process (GO-BP) terms enriched by the shared DEGs include “osteoclast differentiation” and “bone resorption.” (E) The top 10 KEGG pathways enriched by the shared DEGs include the NF-κB signaling pathway.

Network pharmacology and protein-protein interaction analyses of predicted TSPJ targets further nominated SRC, FOS, and NFATc1 as hub proteins and highlighted the NF-κB-related signaling pathway as a prominently enriched pathway. On this basis, the SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB axis was selected for focused experimental assessment, while other enriched pathways were considered exploratory.

Chemical profiling and standardization of TSPJs by HPLC

HPLC profiling delineated four representative saponins in TSPJs, namely araloside A, chikusetsusaponin IVa, ginsenoside Rg2, and ginsenoside Ro (Fig. 2A). This chromatographic profile established a chemical basis for subsequent pharmacological evaluation. Because quantitative marker assignment and batch-consistency testing were not included, the HPLC data are presented as chemical profiling rather than complete chemical standardization.

A Cytoscape-based component–target–pathway network (Fig. 2B) suggested that the representative constituents converge on osteoclast-relevant signaling via a limited set of hub nodes, including SRC, FOS, MMP9, and NFATc1. Consistent with this network structure, Gene Ontology enrichment of the predicted targets highlighted skeletal and osteoclast-associated biological processes (Fig. 2C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis further highlighted NF-κB- and MAPK-related pathways (Fig. 2D) and supported NF-κB as a plausible intersection between RA-associated inflammatory programs and osteoclast differentiation.

PPI network analysis further identified nine core targets, including SRC, FOS, and NFATc1, as candidate nodes in the predicted action profile of TSPJs (Fig. 2E). Molecular docking analysis (Supplementary Figure A1) suggested favorable predicted binding tendencies between the main TSPJs components and these candidate proteins (Fig. 2F). All four representative components displayed relatively strong predicted affinity toward FOS, followed by NFATc1 and SRC. Taken together, the results supported a multi-component, multi-target hypothesis for TSPJs in regulating osteoclastogenic signaling. With the chemical and network basis established, we next evaluated the in vivo efficacy of TSPJs in the CIA model.

TSPJs alleviate joint inflammation and bone erosion in CIA mice

Gross observation showed obvious paw swelling and erythema in CIA mice, confirming successful clinical induction of arthritis (Fig. 3A). After TSPJs administration (30 or 150 mg/kg), joint swelling was visibly attenuated, and the improvement was more pronounced in the high-dose group. Quantitative evaluation confirmed that CIA mice displayed increased paw thickness, a greater number of swollen joints, and higher arthritis scores than control mice (Fig. 3B–D). TSPJs reduced these indices in a dose-dependent manner. X-ray imaging, used here as a supportive radiographic readout, further showed improved skeletal integrity and increased X-ray absorption values after TSPJs treatment (Fig. 3E and F). In parallel, serum levels of MMP-9, TRAP, and CTSK were markedly elevated in CIA mice but decreased after TSPJs administration in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3G–I). These results indicate that TSPJs alleviated joint inflammation and reduced systemic markers associated with osteoclast activation and bone erosion in vivo.

TSPJs improves joint pathology in CIA mice

To further assess joint pathology, cartilage integrity, osteoclast accumulation, and overall tissue injury were examined in CIA mice (Fig. 4). SO/FG staining in Fig. 4A was used to evaluate cartilage preservation rather than to demonstrate CIA induction. CIA induction was supported by the clinical arthritis indices in Fig. 3A–D and by H&E evidence of synovial hyperplasia, inflammatory infiltration, cartilage destruction, and subchondral bone erosion in Fig. 4G–I. SO/FG staining revealed decreased cartilage staining intensity together with higher OARSI scores in the ankle and knee joints of CIA mice, consistent with cartilage matrix loss and structural damage (Fig. 4A–C). TSPJs treatment increased cartilage staining intensity and reduced OARSI scores, with the high-dose group showing the greatest improvement. TRAP staining also demonstrated increased abundance of TRAP-positive multinucleated osteoclasts in CIA joints, whereas TSPJs reduced osteoclast numbers across doses (Fig. 4D–F). Together, these findings show that TSPJs improved cartilage preservation, reduced osteoclast accumulation, and mitigated overall joint pathology in CIA mice.

TRAP staining showed a clear expansion of TRAP-positive multinucleated osteoclasts (≥3 nuclei) in the ankle and knee joints of CIA mice relative to age-matched controls (Fig. 4D). This pattern is in line with heightened osteoclast activity in CIA, a pathological correlate of RA-associated erosive disease. TSPJs treatment reduced TRAP-positive osteoclast counts across doses, and the high-dose group exhibited the greatest decrease relative to the CIA model group (Fig. 4E and F). Overall, these findings support that TSPJs limit osteoclast accumulation in vivo and are associated with decreased joint-local bone destruction in CIA mice.

H&E staining demonstrated severe synovial hyperplasia, inflammatory cell infiltration, articular cartilage degradation, and subchondral bone erosion in the ankle and knee joints of CIA model mice—pathological changes that were absent in control mice (Fig. 4G). Consistent with these morphological alterations, histological scores (encompassing synovitis, cartilage damage, and bone erosion) were significantly elevated in the CIA model group compared with controls. TSPJs intervention attenuated joint pathological lesions across doses, and the high-dose group showed a significant reduction in histological scores relative to the model group (Fig. 4H and I). These results further support that TSPJs ameliorates overall joint tissue damage in CIA mice and establish a foundation for subsequent investigation of the molecular mechanisms by which TSPJs modulates RA-associated bone and joint pathology.

TSPJs inhibit osteoclast differentiation and F-actin ring formation

To establish an appropriate concentration range for the in vitro assays, we first evaluated the effect of TSPJs on RAW264.7 cell viability. As shown in Fig. 5A, TSPJs at concentrations up to 40 μg/mL did not significantly affect cell viability at 24 or 48 h, indicating that this range was appropriate for subsequent osteoclast differentiation experiments. TRAP staining showed that RANKL robustly induced multinucleated osteoclast formation, whereas TSPJs reduced the number of TRAP-positive cells across doses (Fig. 5B–D). At 40 μg/mL, the inhibitory effect of TSPJs was comparable to that of the positive-control treatment.

Rhodamine–phalloidin staining showed well-formed, continuous F-actin rings in RANKL-induced osteoclasts (Fig. 5C). With TSPJs treatment, the ring structure became progressively fragmented as concentrations increased. Consistent with the imaging readout, quantification showed fewer nuclei per osteoclast and a smaller F-actin ring area in the 40 μg/mL group (Fig. 5E and F). Together with the viability data, these findings support that TSPJs impair osteoclast formation and maturation under non-cytotoxic conditions.

TSPJs reduce bone erosion in vitro by inhibiting osteoclast gene expression

To evaluate whether TSPJs-mediated inhibition of osteoclast differentiation was accompanied by functional changes, osteoclast-related transcripts and resorptive activity were quantified. RANKL increased the mRNA abundance of Trap, Ctsk, Mmp9, and Itgb3, whereas TSPJs reduced these osteoclast-associated transcripts (Fig. 6A–D). On bovine bone slices, SEM revealed extensive resorption pits in the RANKL condition; with escalating TSPJs doses, pit formation became less prominent, and the resorbed area decreased (Fig. 6E and F). Taken together, these results support that TSPJs attenuate osteoclast gene programs and limit osteoclast-mediated bone resorption in vitro.

TSPJs suppresse the SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB signaling axis and shows evidence consistent with target engagement

To explore the mechanism underlying TSPJs-mediated inhibition of osteoclast differentiation, the SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB axis was examined on the basis of integrated transcriptomic and network pharmacology prioritization. CETSA was subsequently applied as an orthogonal readout to evaluate whether TSPJs modulate the apparent thermal stability of selected pathway-associated proteins. In this assay, cells were treated with TSPJs prior to lysis and subsequent heating across the temperature gradient. Soluble SRC, NFATc1, and FOS were retained to a greater extent in lysates from TSPJ-treated cells than in the RANKL-only condition (Fig. 7A–D). These nodes are linked to NF-κB-driven osteoclastogenic programs. CETSA does not by itself establish direct binding, particularly for a complex saponin fraction; therefore, this result is interpreted as evidence consistent with target engagement and with involvement of SRC/NFATc1/FOS-related nodes in the TSPJs response.

Immunofluorescence staining was then used to assess NF-κB p65 activation during RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation. In the RANKL group, NF-κB p65 exhibited evident activation with nuclear translocation, whereas both TSPJs treatment and SRC inhibitor intervention markedly reduced nuclear p65 signals (Fig. 7E and F). Western blot analysis further showed that RANKL increased SRC, TRAF6, NF-κB p65, NFATc1, and FOS protein levels, while TSPJs reduced these protein levels in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 7G–L). Taken together, these findings indicate that TSPJs suppress osteoclastogenic signaling associated with the SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB axis, and the docking and CETSA data are consistent with the involvement of SRC/NFATc1/FOS as candidate nodes.

TSPJs attenuate bone erosion in CIA mice in association with SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB signaling modulation

To assess whether the same pathway was modulated in vivo, SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB signaling and osteoclast effector proteins were evaluated in ankle joint tissues by immunohistochemistry (Fig. 8). Relative to controls, CIA mice exhibited increased staining for SRC, TRAF6, p65, FOS, and NFATc1, consistent with heightened osteoclastogenic signaling within arthritic joints. TSPJs treatment reduced these signals at both doses, with a more pronounced decrease in the high-dose group. Representative staining images and quantitative analyses consistently showed weaker positive staining after TSPJs treatment (Fig. 8A–J). These findings support that TSPJs attenuated bone erosion in CIA mice in association with suppression of SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB pathway components and downstream osteoclast effector proteins (Fig. 9).

Proposed mechanism by which TSPJs are proposed to suppress osteoclastogenesis and mitigates RA-associated bone erosion. In the inflammatory RA microenvironment, RANKL–RANK signaling activates SRC and TRAF6, leading to NF-κB (p65) activation and nuclear translocation. NF-κB then promotes osteoclastogenic transcriptional programs involving NFATc1 and FOS, which upregulate osteoclast-related genes (e.g., Trap, Ctsk, Mmp9, and Itgb3) and ultimately enhance osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption. TSPJs are proposed to dampen this cascade by suppressing SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB signaling and downstream NFATc1/FOS-associated osteoclastogenic programs, which may contribute to reduced osteoclast formation and RA-associated bone erosion.

Discussion

The current work supports a coherent model in which TSPJs mitigate RA-associated bone erosion primarily by attenuating osteoclast differentiation and downstream resorptive activity [2]. Evidence converged across multiple layers, including integrated transcriptomics, network-based target nomination, the CIA model, and RANKL-driven cellular assays, and consistently pointed to suppression of osteoclast-linked readouts alongside improved joint structure. Within this framework, the SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB cascade showed the closest correspondence with the observed changes [27]. The pharmacological findings were accompanied by HPLC-based chemical profiling [14]. HPLC identified four representative saponins, providing a phytochemical footing for the botanical fraction while leaving full quantitative standardization for future work.

In the integrated bioinformatics analysis, genes shared between RA synovium and osteoclast differentiation were predominantly assigned to NF-κB-centered inflammatory programs, in keeping with the established role of NF-κB in osteoclastogenesis and inflammatory bone loss [13,28]. In RA, synovial inflammation and erosive damage are mechanistically linked rather than parallel processes, and inflammatory cues can sustain osteoclast activation and thereby accelerate local tissue injury [2]. In this context, the in vivo profile we observed—reduced serum osteoclast markers (TRAP, CTSK, and MMP9), fewer TRAP-positive multinucleated osteoclasts in joint sections, and improved cartilage pathology assessed by SO/FG staining with OARSI-based grading—suggests that TSPJs influence not only inflammatory manifestations but also the structural component of disease progression. The in vivo and in vitro functional readouts provide the primary support, whereas the bioinformatics integration is used to prioritize the signaling context.

The cellular assays reinforce this interpretation. At non-cytotoxic concentrations, TSPJs reduced RANKL-induced multinucleated osteoclast formation, disrupted the F-actin ring architecture, downregulated osteoclast-associated transcripts, and attenuated bone resorption on bovine slices. The pattern was not confined to a single assay. This combination of differentiation and function-linked readouts supports that TSPJs influence both osteoclast maturation and the acquisition of resorptive capacity, consistent with at least a partial osteoclast intrinsic component [29]. Such an effect is relevant because lowering inflammatory tone alone does not necessarily confer protection from erosive damage, whereas the present data are more consistent with modulation of osteoclast biology. Nevertheless, additional contributions from the inflammatory microenvironment cannot be excluded, and this complexity is not fully recapitulated in the RAW264.7 model [30].

Mechanistically, network pharmacology analysis highlighted SRC, FOS, and NFATc1 as hub proteins associated with the major saponin constituents of TSPJs [31]. These nodes are biologically plausible. SRC is essential for osteoclast cytoskeletal organization and resorption; FOS is required for osteoclast lineage commitment; and NFATc1 serves as a master transcriptional regulator of osteoclastogenesis [9]. In the current study, TSPJs reduced SRC, TRAF6, p65, NFATc1, and FOS protein expression both in vitro and in joint tissues from CIA mice. The accompanying immunofluorescence data also showed attenuated nuclear p65 signaling after TSPJs treatment. Overall, these findings support the interpretation that modulation of the SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB axis is closely associated with the anti-osteoclast effect of TSPJs. However, the present study does not include rescue experiments, gene knockdown/overexpression, or pathway-dependency assays, and therefore cannot prove that this axis is strictly required for the observed biological effects.

At the same time, the target-engagement evidence should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Molecular docking suggested favorable binding poses between representative saponins and several candidate proteins, and CETSA indicated increased thermal stability of SRC, NFATc1, and FOS following TSPJs exposure. These findings are consistent with target engagement; however, they do not, on their own, establish definitive direct binding for individual compounds. A more careful reading of the data is that TSPJs likely act through multi-component, multi-node regulation, with SRC/NFATc1/FOS-related signaling being part of that response network.

From a development standpoint, our findings also speak to ginseng-derived product research. TSPJs is a multicomponent botanical fraction rather than a single chemical entity, and its translational value will ultimately depend on the rigor of standardization. The HPLC profile presented here provides a starting point, but development-oriented work typically requires quantitative marker assignment, extraction-yield reporting, total saponin content, prespecified batch-consistency criteria, pharmacokinetic characterization, and a broader safety package before meaningful extrapolation can be considered.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the in vitro findings were mainly based on a RAW264.7 osteoclast differentiation model, which may not fully represent primary osteoclast precursor biology or the complex synovial microenvironment in RA. Further validation using bone marrow-derived macrophages, primary osteoclast cultures, co-culture systems, or other disease-relevant models is needed [6,32]. Second, bone-protective effects were evaluated by X-ray imaging, histopathology, TRAP staining, and osteoclast-related markers, but quantitative micro-CT analysis of parameters such as BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th was not performed. Third, the in vivo sample size and quantitative sampling were limited, and future studies should include larger cohorts and standard anti-rheumatic comparators. Finally, although the SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB axis was consistent with our experimental results, other enriched pathways require further investigation. Comprehensive pharmacokinetic, toxicological, and chemical standardization studies are also needed before further translational development of TSPJs.

Conclusion

In summary, TSPJs attenuated RA-associated bone erosion in CIA mice and suppressed RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis in vitro. The collective evidence is most consistent with involvement of the SRC/TRAF6/NF-κB signaling axis, and HPLC profiling identified representative saponins that help define the botanical composition of the preparation. Overall, these results provide a pharmacological and phytochemical basis for continued evaluation of TSPJs as a standardized Panax japonicus-derived candidate for RA-associated bone destruction.
