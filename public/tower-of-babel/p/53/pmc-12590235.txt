Trends in baseline HbA1c and body-mass index in randomised trials of people with type 1 diabetes from 1993 to 2025: an IMI2 SOPHIA systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Amar DE van Laar, Laura Gallardo-Nuell, Sofia Pazmino Lucio, Margaretha M Visser, Ann Mertens, Roman Vangoitsenhoven, Pieter Gillard, Ebaa Al Ozairi, Carel W Le Roux, Jonathan Rosen, Carmen Hurtado del Pozo, Yenny Leal, José Manuel Fernández-Real, Chantal Mathieu, Nele Steenackers, Bart Van der Schueren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe prevalence of obesity has increased globally over the past few decades for the general population and people with type 2 diabetes. Existing research suggests that the body-mass index (BMI) of individuals living with type 1 diabetes has also increased substantially. This double burden of type 1 diabetes and obesity is concerning and may further increase the risk for microvascular and macrovascular complications associated with type 1 diabetes. We aimed to evaluate trends in BMI (as an indicator for adiposity/obesity) and haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c; as a measure of long-term glucose management) in people with type 1 diabetes, based on clinical trial data.MethodsIn this systematic review and meta-analysis, PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched for clinical trials involving individuals with type 1 diabetes, covering all publications between January 1, 1993, and May 20, 2025. Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) were included when performed in adults, with baseline data on BMI and HbA1c, and featuring a sample size exceeding 100 participants. Studies were excluded if they applied overly narrow cut-off ranges (difference between upper and lower cut-off) for BMI (<5 kg/m2) or HbA1c (<3%) or if they focused solely on individuals with either overweight or underweight. Spearman correlation and linear regression were applied to evaluate trends in baseline HbA1c and BMI, as the primary outcomes of interest the study. Subgroup analyses were performed for age, diabetes duration, sex and the type of intervention. The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool was used to assess study quality, with disagreements resolved through consultation. Funnel plots were used to evaluate publication bias. The robustness of the study results was checked by performing leave-one-out sensitivity analyses for BMI and HbA1c. Strength of the body of evidence was assessed using GRADE. This work was registered with PROSPERO, CRD42023449198.Findings12,346 unique studies were identified, of which 148 were RCTs with a combined population of 56,411 participants were included. Included studies represented research conducted in Europe (55 studies), North America (25 studies), Asia (13 studies) and Oceania (2 studies), in addition to 53 multicontinental studies. BMI increased from 25.0 (24.7–25.3) kg/m2 in the first decade studied (1993–2002) to 26.9 (26.4–27.3) kg/m2 in the last decade (2016–2025). Baseline HbA1c was 7.9% (7.6–8.2) for 1993–2002 and 7.9% (7.8–8.0) for 2016–2025.InterpretationThe increasing trend in BMI may indicate that weight gain is also occurring in people with type 1 diabetes. More research is needed on the BMI and HbA1c trends in people with type 1 diabetes, using real-world data on the individual level and investigating these trends in relation to the insulin dose.FundingThe Stratification of Obesity Phenotypes to Optimize Future Obesity Therapy (SOPHIA) project has received funding from the Innovative Medicines Initiative 2 Joint Undertaking under grant agreement No. 875534, supported by the European Union's Horizon 2020 research and innovation program and EFPIA, with additional support from T1D Exchange, Breakthrough T1D (former JDRF), and Obesity Action Coalition.Keywords: Meta-analysis, Type 1 diabetes, Obesity, Clinical trials, BMI, HbA1c

Research in context

Evidence before this study

A literature search on PubMed with the search terms ‘obesity trends’ OR ‘trends in obesity’ or ‘obesity increase’ OR ‘adiposity increase’ OR ‘adiposity trends’ OR ‘BMI trends’ OR ‘trends in BMI’ OR ‘trends in body-mass index’ OR ‘BMI increase’ (Title/Abstract) yielded 17,683 articles between January 1, 1993 and December 31, 2023. This search demonstrated that there is ample evidence that body-mass index (BMI) has increased over the past decades in the general population. Recently, it has been shown that BMI has also increased over the past 35 years in clinical trials involving people with type 2 diabetes, whereas HbA1c levels have decreased. The recent trends in BMI and HbA1c in people with type 1 diabetes included in clinical trials are not known. We aimed to address this knowledge gap.

Added value of this study

This systematic review of clinical trials involving people with type 1 diabetes suggests that the increase for BMI that has been reported for the general population and for people with type 2 diabetes, also occurred in people with type 1 diabetes. However, HbA1c levels did not change over time in people with type 1 diabetes participating in randomised clinical trials, which contrasts with the finding reported for people with type 2 diabetes.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our finding of a pronounced increase in BMI suggests that obesity is becoming a major issue for people with type 1 diabetes. This increase in BMI may be more problematic for people with type 1 diabetes compared with the general population, considering the inherent risks of long-term complications, such as cardiovascular disease due to type 1 diabetes, in addition to obesity complications. More research is warranted to investigate how BMI and glucose management evolved in recent decades in daily clinical practice to contextualise these findings.

Introduction

The prevalences of overweight and obesity have increased steadily over the past decades, and these conditions currently affect more than two billion people worldwide, while increasing the risk for cardiometabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes.1,2 Diabetes is characterised by elevated blood glucose levels, which for type 2 diabetes are a result of an impaired sensitivity for the hormone insulin and/or insufficient insulin production.3,4 In contrast, type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune disease in which a lack of insulin production causes elevated glucose levels.4 Therefore, people with type 1 diabetes require exogenous insulin regardless of weight or insulin resistance. However, by increasing insulin resistance, obesity may exacerbate problems related to glycaemic control in type 1 diabetes, increasing the amount of insulin required to inject.5,6 In addition, obesity may further increase the risk for micro- and macrovascular complications among individuals living with type 1 diabetes.6 This health burden of the combination of type 1 diabetes and obesity is becoming an issue of increasing concern as studies suggest that the body-mass index (BMI) of individuals living with type 1 diabetes has increased substantially over the years. Conway et al. reported a sevenfold increase in the obesity prevalence among individuals living with type 1 diabetes during 18-year follow-up (starting 1986–1988 till 2004–2007).7 Furthermore, studies indicate that obesity is currently similarly common among individuals living with type 1 diabetes as in the general population, although these findings may be region-dependent and mostly applicable for western Europe, since data from Kuwait and Mexico showed that obesity prevalences were much lower among individuals living with type 1 diabetes compared to the general population.8,9 Despite the increasing prevalence of obesity among individuals living with type 1 diabetes and the increased health burden resulting from it, this problem is still overlooked, both in medical practice and research.10

Tight glucose management is crucial for preventing macro- and microvascular complications in type 1 diabetes, even more so than in type 2 diabetes.11, 12, 13 However, intensive insulin therapy aiming at improving management of blood glucose may lead to weight gain,14 which can be detrimental in terms of the development of diabetes complications. Medical and technological advances may help to reduce problems related to markedly elevated blood glucose levels and weight gain in type 1 diabetes. Traditionally, individuals living with type 1 diabetes were dependent on manual injections of insulin, which can be challenging and involves the risk of dropping blood glucose levels too low (hypoglycaemia).15 Hypoglycaemia and fear for hypoglycaemia can contribute to additional food intake to prevent hypoglycaemic episodes, ultimately promoting weight gain further.16,17 Currently, new insulin analogues can help to control blood glucose levels with less hypoglycaemia and weight gain, which may be achieved by reducing appetite via hypothalamic appetite regulation.18, 19, 20 Moreover, automated insulin delivery systems comprised of continuous glucose monitoring and continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion by a pump and a controller offer a more automated process for glucose management for individuals living with type 1 diabetes and studies have reported that these innovations can improve glucose management and reduce the occurrence of hypoglycaemic episodes.15,21,22

Although there is evidence that specific technological innovations may improve glucose management in individuals living with type 1 diabetes, there is still a need to assess whether glucose management has also improved in the larger population of people with type 1 diabetes. Ideally, this should be analysed as part of a more elaborate analysis on prevention of complications, taking adiposity into account as well. Since the number of clinical trials on diabetes has increased over the years, it is now possible to study how haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and BMI at baseline have changed over the years in populations with diabetes. Using this strategy, Hu et al. have shown that glucose management for individuals living with diabetes has indeed improved, at least for those living with type 2 diabetes.23 In fact, this systematic review showed that BMI increased during 1987 and 2022 in people with type 2 diabetes participating in clinical trials, while their glucose management (reflected by a decline in HbA1c) markedly improved during this period.23 However, this may differ for people with type 1 diabetes as tight glucose management is very difficult to achieve in these populations,11,24 intensive insulin therapy is more commonly used among people with type 1 diabetes, weight-loss inducing glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists are not approved for type 1 diabetes, and a recent global overview of the changes in both BMI and HbA1c in the same populations of individuals living with type 1 diabetes, is still missing.

Therefore, we aim to provide insight into the trends in BMI (as an indicator for adiposity/obesity) and HbA1c (as measure for long-term glucose management) in people with type 1 diabetes, using a systematic review and meta-analysis approach similar as applied by Hu et al. for people with type 2 diabetes. Additionally, we aimed to provide information on factors that could impact these trends, such as age, sex, ethnicity, and selection bias.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.25 The protocol was registered on the PROSPERO international Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews platform (CRD42023449198). Neither ethical approval nor informed consent was required for this study as it analysed the mean values reported for previously performed clinical trials.

Search strategy and selection criteria

Following the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews, a comprehensive search strategy was developed in consultation with a professional librarian for the databases PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and ClinicalTrials.gov, covering all publications up to May 20, 2025. The search terms applied included a comprehensive range of MeSH terms and keywords related to type 1 diabetes, and randomised controlled trials. Full details of the search strategy and specific terms applied per database can be found in the supplementary material (Supplementary Material A). Endnote (Clarivate) was used to gather records and remove duplicates.

Inclusion criteria for this systematic review encompassed randomised controlled trials involving adult human participants (≥18 years) with type 1 diabetes, published prior to May 20, 2025, providing baseline data on HbA1c and BMI, and featuring a sample size exceeding 100 participants. Randomised controlled trials were excluded if their inclusion criteria restricted baseline body-mass index (BMI) to a narrow range (small difference between the upper and lower cut-off) of less than 5 kg/m2 or HbA1c to a range of less than three percentage points, as these stringent baseline criteria could limit generalisability of BMI and HbA1c trend assessment. Moreover, trials were excluded if they exclusively targeted people with underweight, overweight, or obesity as these selective BMI criteria could compromise the generalisability of the BMI trend analyses, potentially skewing outcome assessments. Other exclusion criteria comprised non-RCT designs (e.g., observational, cross-sectional studies), animal studies, publications not in English, artificial (in silico) data, editorials, conference abstracts, grant proposals, cost-effectiveness analyses, study protocols, post-hoc- or subgroup analyses, as well as studies involving paediatric or pregnant individuals.

Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) was used for title/abstract screening and full text review. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts of potentially eligible studies in duplicate, resolving any disagreements by consulting a third- and fourth reviewer. References from relevant reviews were also screened to identify additional studies.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers extracted data using an extraction template developed in Covidence, capturing study characteristics (first author, publication year, country, sample size, and mean duration of follow-up), participant characteristics (age in years, sex, race, diabetes duration in years, baseline HbA1c in %, and BMI in kg/m2), and intervention type studied (drug types and dosages, if applicable). Data was extracted in duplicate to establish reliability. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved by consensus by consulting a third reviewer. After data extraction, variance measures were converted to standard error of the mean to calculate 95% confidence intervals. The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool was used to assess study quality, with disagreements resolved through consultation. Funnel plots were generated where possible to evaluate publication bias. Asymmetry of the funnel plots was assessed with trim-and-fill analyses, regression tests (analogous to Egger's test, regtest () function in R) and rank correlation tests (analogous to Begg's test, ranktest () function in R). The robustness of the study results was checked by performing post-hoc leave-one-out sensitivity analyses for BMI and HbA1c. Strength of the body of evidence was assessed using GRADE.

Data analysis

Analyses were performed with the data available, without imputations, since availability of BMI and HbA1c data was an inclusion criterium. Pooled effect sizes were calculated for baseline HbA1c and BMI using a multilevel random-effects model with the year of publication and study ID as random effect to account for heterogeneity among studies. In these analyses, weighted means were calculated for baseline HbA1c and BMI. Weighted means take the number of participants in the different trials into account, allocating more weight to the means from studies with a larger sample size. Sub-analyses were performed for sex, age, disease duration, and type of intervention. Spearman correlation and linear regression were applied to evaluate trends in baseline HbA1c and BMI over time. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic (DL method), and additional post-hoc sensitivity analyses were performed based on the risk of bias (excluding studies with a high risk of bias for 2 or more types of bias) and for impactful BMI and HbA1c cut-offs (lower BMI cut-off, lower HbA1c cut-off of ≥7%, or upper HbA1c cut-off of ≤8.5%). Additional analyses were performed for the overall trends in BMI and HbA1c, determining mean values per decade and the proportion of studies with mean values above defined BMI (<25 and ≥ 25 kg/m2) and HbA1c (<7, 7–7.9, 8–9.9, ≥10%) cut-offs throughout the study period. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1 with significance set at p < 0.05. BMI and HbA1c data, as the main outcomes of the systematic review, are presented as weighted mean and 95% confidence interval, with a slope or Spearman correlation for the trends.

The quality assessment indicated varying risks for bias in the different RCTs, as detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Since all studies were randomised trials, none had a high risk for bias related to the sequence. A high risk for bias was most commonly related to a lack of blinding. The intervention was not blinded in 107 (72%) of the trials included, and 58 (39%) of the trials did not blind the outcome. The regression test for the funnel plots indicated no significant asymmetry for either BMI (p = 0.46) or HbA1c (p = 0.35). The rank correlation test also showed no significant asymmetry for HbA1c (p = 0.09), while it did reveal significant asymmetry for BMI (p < 0.001). The trim-and-fill analysis indicated that there were 25 studies missing for BMI and 0 for HbA1c. Funnel plots with trim-and-fill analysis are shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Trends in BMI and HbA1c

Table 2 illustrates that BMI showed an increasing trend over the years from 25.0 (24.7–25.3) kg/m2 in the first decade studied (1993–2002) to 26.9 (26.4–27.3) kg/m2 in the last decade (2016–2025). As indicated in Fig. 2A, the trendline for BMI increased approximately 0.7 kg/m2 per decade (p = 0.02, I2 = 2.3%) with a slope of 0.05. This corresponded with a positive correlation of 0.59 between BMI and the year of publication in the crude analysis. Baseline HbA1c was 7.9 (7.6–8.2)% in the first decade studied and 7.9 (7.8–8.0)% in the last decade, and fluctuated around 8% for the entire study period without a significant trend (slope = −0.01, p = 0.23, I2 = 71%), as illustrated by Table 2 and Fig. 2B.

In a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Figure S2) for the risk of bias, excluding studies with a high risk of bias for at least two types of bias, there was still an increasing trend for BMI (slope r = 0.05, p = 0.02) and no significant trend for HbA1c (slope r = −0.01, p = 0.20).

Diabetes duration

Fig. 5C visualises that there was a significant increase in BMI (±0.5 kg/m2 per decade, r = 0.52, p = 2.6∗10−6) within studies with an average disease duration of <20 years, unlike in studies with an average disease duration of ≥20 years (r = −0.01, p = 0.96).

Fig. 5D portrays no significant trend in HbA1c in studies with a diabetes duration of <20 years (r = −0.08, p = 0.48), whereas a decreasing trend of 0.2% per decade was observed for baseline HbA1c in studies with an average disease duration of ≥20 years (r = −0.44, p = 0.02).

Sex

Fig. 5E depicts a BMI increase of 0.9 kg/m2 per decade (r = 0.42, p = 2.4∗10−5) in studies with a majority of male participants after starting with an almost 1 kg/m2 lower BMI. There was no significant trend in BMI in studies with a majority of female participant (r = 0.06, p = 0.68).

Fig. 5F illustrates that there was a declining trend (r = −0.35, p = 0.02) for baseline HbA1c of 0.1% per decade in studies with a majority of female participants.

Intervention

The increasing trend in BMI was observed in both studies with insulin (r = 0.65, p = 4.1∗10−10) and adjunct treatments (r = 0.49, p = 6∗10−3), whereas it was not observed in studies with other types of interventions (r = −0.09, p = 0.66), as depicted in Fig. 5G.

Fig. 5H shows that there was no significant trend for HbA1c in studies with insulin (r = −0.15, p = 0.21) and adjunct (r = 0.22, p = 0.24) treatments, whereas HbA1c levels dropped by 0.5% per decade (r = −0.61, p = 6.9∗10−4) in studies with other types of interventions, in which HbA1c started at a substantially higher level.

Discussion

In this article, we described the characteristics of study populations consisting of individuals living with type 1 diabetes, focusing on how baseline BMI and HbA1c have changed over time in clinical trials conducted in this population. Hereby, we identified an increase of the weighted mean for BMI over time between 1993 and 2025, without observing an overall change in HbA1c during the same period. Since trends were not corrected for potential confounders due to the absence of data on the individual level, findings should be interpreted with caution.

The increase in BMI observed in our analyses is similar as the trends (0.5–0.7 kg/m2 increase per decade) that have been reported for the general population and for people with type 2 diabetes,23,174 suggesting that individuals living with type 1 diabetes have experienced a similar increase in BMI as the rest of the population. Furthermore, our findings may indicate that the increase in BMI in people with type 1 diabetes previously reported in regional and individual studies,7,175 has also occurred on a global scale in people with type 1 diabetes participating in clinical trials. Therefore, it appears that BMI has been increasing over time in the past 30 years in different populations, including those with type 1 diabetes. It should be mentioned that while the BMI trends appear to be similar as in type 2 diabetes, the average BMI and prevalence of obesity are lower for people with type 1 diabetes. When comparing the BMI trends in Fig. 2B with the trends reported by Hu et al. for type 2 diabetes, it can be seen that across the entire study period, mean BMI is 3–4 kg/m2 lower in clinical trials involving people with type 1 diabetes.23 This difference in BMI can be explained by the differences in physiology, where obesity contributes to insulin resistance as the primary cause of impaired glucose homeostasis in type 2 diabetes, whereas type 1 diabetes is a result of an autoimmune reaction targeting pancreatic beta-cells.3,4

Research on progress in glucose management in type 1 diabetes provides a diversified view, with substantial improvements observed in some, but not all studies. Firstly, the use of new technologies such as continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (hybrid closed loop) and continuous glucose monitoring among individuals living with type 1 diabetes has increased in recent years and over the past two decades.176,177 However, even studies reporting an increase in the use of hybrid closed loop systems, such as T1D Exchange, do not always report major improvement in glucose management.176 In the American T1D Exchange study, HbA1c decreased slightly from 8.7% in 2016–2017 to 8.4% in 2021–2022.176 Furthermore, this is not an improvement compared to the 8.3% reported for the T1D Exchange clinical registry in 2012.178 The absence of a significant decrease in HbA1c in the past decades, matches the trends that we observed for HbA1c in our systematic review. When comparing our results to other studies and interpreting the results, it is important to realise that clinical trials include a selection of people with type 1 diabetes, that may differ from people with type 1 diabetes within the general population. The common HbA1c cut-off range of 7–11% for example excludes individuals who are more towards the ends of the spectrum regarding glucose management. In the Belgian public health IQED study without HbA1c cut-offs, a small decrease in HbA1c from 8.0 to 7.6% was observed between 2001 and 2022, while BMI increased from 25.4 to 26.7 kg/m2 during the same period.179 In a Slovenian study performed with the entire national paediatric type 1 diabetes population, a more substantial decline of HbA1c from 9.4% to 7.8% was observed between 2000 and 2011.180 Compared to this study and the systematic review of Hu et al. with people with type 2 diabetes, average HbA1c values in our systematic review were similar (7.9% versus 7.8 and 7.9%) at the end of the period, whereas at the start of the period studied, HbA1c levels were lower (7.9% versus 9.4 and 10.6%) in our systematic review. Therefore, the potential improvement in HbA1c was smaller in our systematic review. Moreover, it is important to note that in our systematic review, a pronounced declining trend for HbA1c was observed for the one subgroup with HbA1c levels above 9% at the start of the study period (the population receiving interventions other than insulin or adjunct treatment), confirming that declining HbA1c trends are mostly observed when the starting levels are higher. The lower HbA1c levels at the start of our analysis may be related to the fact that 107 clinical trials in our analysis used HbA1c values as inclusion criterium, with many clinical trials having an upper HbA1c cut-off similar as the average HbA1c baseline values reported in the earliest studies included in the analysis of Hu et al. Interestingly, in our systematic review only 39% (11 out of 28) of studies with other types of interventions used an upper HbA1c cut-off, compared to 80% (63 out of 79) and 59% (20 out of 34) of the studies with insulin and adjunct treatments, respectively. The less frequent use of HbA1c inclusion criteria in clinical trials with other interventions, may be a reason why a significant decreasing HbA1c trend was observed in specifically these studies. However, when the analysis was performed without all clinical trials using potentially impactful cut-offs for inclusion (a lower BMI cut-off or HbA1c cut-offs of ≥7% or ≤8.5%), the same trends for BMI and HbA1c were observed as with all 141 clinical trials included in the analysis.

The overall trends of an increasing BMI and stable HbA1c were found in most, but not all studies in the subgroup analysis. These analyses have shown that the increasing trend for BMI was not significant in studies with most female participants, studies with other types of interventions and studies with a mean disease duration of ≥20 years, and that there was instead a significant trend for a decrease in HbA1c in these three types of studies, as well as in studies with a mean age of ≥20 years. It should be noted that even the trendlines not showing a significant increase in BMI, made notable upwards movements. The smaller sample sizes may partly explain why there was no significant increase in BMI in studies with a majority of female participants or a mean disease duration of ≥20 years, despite the upward movement of the trendline. Alternatively, it may be that women with type 1 diabetes already had a higher BMI, while men are now catching up. This is in line with the trends in Fig. 5E coming together, and studies such as Giandalia et al. reporting that the percentage of people with type 1 diabetes having a BMI of at least 25 km/m2 is higher among women.181 Most of the significant decreasing trends for HbA1c in the subgroup analyses were subtle, whereas the HbA1c trend in studies with other types of interventions was as pronounced as the one reported for people with type 2 diabetes. Interestingly, there appears to be an overlap of studies in which BMI has not increased significantly over the years, and studies in which HbA1c has improved. This could suggest that the increase in BMI in these subgroups impacts glucose homeostasis, or that other factors impact both BMI and HbA1c in these subgroups. Although our data on the study level do not allow to evaluate whether an increase in BMI is accompanied by impaired glucose management on the individual level, it was recently reported by Bailey et al. that weight gain of ≥10 kg results in an increase in HbA1c despite increased insulin dose among individuals living with relatively well-controlled type 1 diabetes.182 Subgroup analyses previously performed for clinical trials involving people with type 2 diabetes have shown the same trend that baseline differences in HbA1c between subgroups have decreased or even disappeared over the past 30 years,23 which could be an indication of improved diabetes care or more equality in diabetes care.

Although a meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials is the strongest study design for providing scientific evidence, our study had a number of limitations. Firstly, there were indications for potential publication bias for BMI trends in the funnel plots indicated by asymmetry in the rank test and the missing studies in the trim-and-fill analysis. In contrast, differences between studies observed in the quality assessment (such as a lack of blinding of the intervention or outcome) affect outcomes instead of baseline characteristics, and are therefore not a major concern for this meta-analysis. It should be mentioned that the trends for BMI and HbA1c were not corrected for confounders, since we lacked the data on the individual level to accurately do so. Additionally, our results may be impacted by the substantial differences in HbA1c cut-off values between studies, interfering with the analysis over time, as these can create study populations that, by definition, differ substantially in average HbA1c levels (for example when HbA1c cut-off are <8% for one study and >8.5% for another). Moreover, the more impactful HbA1c cut-offs of ≥7% (lower) or ≤8.5% (upper) became more common over time, with 19% (11 out of 59) of clinical trials before 2010 and 45% (40 out of 89) of clinical trials since 2010 using these cut-offs. Cut-off values may impact the trends for BMI as well, although to a lesser degree since less studies have a cut-off for BMI and these cut-offs are in general less narrow. Even though the clinical trials with the tightest (less than 3% difference between lower and upper HbA1c cut-off) were excluded for this systematic review, many of the selected clinical trials still exclude individuals with the highest and lowest HbA1c levels, which could affect the analysis. However, we have taken cut-offs into account in a sensitivity analysis, which still showed the same overall trends for BMI and HbA1c. An additional limitation may be that BMI does not take body composition into account and may therefore not always provide a good estimate of the adiposity that promotes the risk for complications.183 Interpretation of the results is limited to the information that has been reported in individual studies, which does include BMI and HbA1c at baseline, but usually not factors impacting the trends, such as the type of technologies and the dose of insulin used. Moreover, the representativeness of the results may be impacted by the fact that most of the selected studies had little (less than 15%) representation of people with another ethnicity than Caucasian. In contrast, our analysis could be considered fairly representative for the adult population of people with type 1 diabetes regarding age and sex, with almost half of the studies having a population with a majority of females and people ranging in age with a mean age of ±40 years. As only adults were included, our results may not be extrapolated to younger individuals living with type 1 diabetes. Additional limitations apply to the subgroups analyses performed. Since we relied on information on the averages within studies, we could not split the data of individual studies to evaluate the differences between subgroups. As a consequence, we do not know whether it actually is the males experiencing the largest increase in BMI when there is a larger increase in BMI in studies with a majority of male participants. Moreover, subgroup analyses for ethnicity could not be performed, considering that almost all studies had a predominantly White population.

The findings of this systematic review could have several implications. Firstly, an increase in BMI in individuals living with type 1 diabetes may contribute to the development of additional complications,6 and emphasises the need for more focus on weight management in care provided to individuals living with type 1 diabetes. Although no significant overall trend was observed for HbA1c, the inclusion criteria in clinical trials complicated the analysis further, and may cause deviations from the trends in people with type 1 diabetes within the general population. As a result, we cannot confirm nor contradict that HbA1c levels have also decreased in the past 32 years for the larger population of people with type 1 diabetes. More real word data are required to provide more insight into the BMI and HbA1c trends for the larger population of people with type 1 diabetes. These future analyses should ideally use data that allows a correction for confounding. It would be useful for future research involving people with type 1 diabetes to report on the type of therapy, and dose of insulin, that the participants receive as part of the baseline characteristics. This information is relevant to contextualise the increase in BMI and trends in HbA1c over the years. Since increased adiposity may contribute to the development of insulin resistance, it is relevant to know whether higher insulin dosages are used to keep HbA1c levels in check, and whether the increasing trend for BMI is more pronounced for people using higher doses of insulin. Further insight in the impact of obesity on people with type 1 diabetes could be obtained by including measures for insulin resistance, such as the HOMA-IR index. Future research should also focus on the subgroup differences in type 1 diabetes. Firstly, sex-based stratification of weight gain would be valuable to clarify whether males with type 1 diabetes are indeed experiencing more weight gain over the years. Secondly, the observation that most of the studies performed with adults living with type 1 diabetes did not provide information on ethnicity, combined with the strong overrepresentation of White/Caucasian people, suggests that ethnicity is an overlooked topic in type 1 diabetes research. Since people with type 1 diabetes are in general already a population with reduced access to new treatments, that have proven to be successful in type 2 diabetes, these disparities within type 1 diabetes may cause a double disadvantage. Previously, Akturk et al. already reported that American studies performed with people with type 1 diabetes include a larger proportion of White participants than the proportion of White individuals living with type 1 diabetes in the American population.184 Since studies that looked at the impact of ethnicity in the context of type 1 diabetes indicate that ethnic minorities experience additional problems such as lesser glucose management and worse health outcomes,185,186 future research on type 1 diabetes should have more attention for the ethnic representation as well.

Our analysis for the 32-year period between 1993 and 2025 with trends corrected for the random effect of time, without further correction for potential confounders, suggests that BMI has increased to a similar degree among individuals living with type 1 diabetes as in the general population. Meanwhile, baseline HbA1c has not changed in clinical trials performed with individuals living with type 1 diabetes, which may not necessarily apply to the larger population of people with type 1 diabetes as well, considering that HbA1c cut-offs in clinical trials can result in rather select populations. There is a need for additional research on metabolic control and weight gain in people with type 1 diabetes to determine whether this observation in clinical trials applies to the larger population of people with type 1 diabetes as well, and whether it persists after correction for confounders. Future research on trends in people with type 1 diabetes would benefit from the inclusion of more ethnic minorities in studies, as well as from studies reporting on the method of insulin provision and the insulin dose at baseline.
