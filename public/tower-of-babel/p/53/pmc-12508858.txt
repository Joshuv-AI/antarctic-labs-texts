Impact of molecular and immune signature on endometrial biopsies with atypical hyperplasia with and without concurrent endometroid carcinoma
Authors: Terrence Wong, Joellen Fresia, Nicholas Adzibolosu, Logan Corey, Sharon Wu, Larissa Mattei, Joanne Xiu, Kurt Hodges, Matthew Oberley, Premal H. Thaker, Rami Musallam, Mira Kheil, Sudeshna Bandyopadhyay, Ira Winer, Robert Morris, Rouba Ali-Fehmi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights:•Premalignant lesions with/without concurrent endometrial cancer (EC) exhibit different molecular and immune signatures.•Premalignant biopsies with subsequent diagnosis of EC (EC-B) exhibit more frequent canonical mutations.•MSI-High and CTNNB1 genomic alterations were unique to EC specimens.•Immune checkpoint expression was higher in EC-B specimens compared to biopsies without subsequent diagnosis of carcinoma.•EC-B specimens exhibit higher proportional pro-inflammatory infiltrates of myeloid and lymphoid immune cells.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is the most common gynecologic malignancy in the United States accounting for approximately 69,000 new cases and 14,000 related deaths in 2025 (Siegel et al., 2025). The incidence and mortality rates for EC have consistently risen over the last two decades (Siegel et al., 2025), attributable in part to rising rates of obesity (Onstad et al., 2016). The most common histologic subtype of EC is endometrioid adenocarcinoma, and its pathogenesis from precursor lesions – atypical endometrial hyperplasia (AEH) or endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia (EIN) – is well established.

Definitive treatment of AEH/EIN is a total hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, but progestin therapy is a commonly prescribed alternative for patients who desire to maintain fertility or are not otherwise suitable candidates for surgery (Management of Endometrial Intraepithelial Neoplasia or Atypical Endometrial Hyperplasia: ACOG Clinical Consensus No. 5. Obstet Gynecol., 2023). Management of these precancerous lesions can be challenging due to the use of multiple classification systems (Kim et al., 2020, Mutter, 2000), poor interobserver diagnostic reproducibility among pathologists (Zaino et al., 2006), and the increasing burden of disease among younger patients who may desire future fertility (Francoeur AA, Liao CI, Chang J, et al. Associated Trends in Obesity and Endometrioid Endometrial Cancer in the United States. Obstet Gynecol. Published online January 2, 2025). Given the risk of concurrent occult carcinoma from as low as 10 % to almost 60 % (Trimble et al., 2006, Antonsen et al., 2012), early identification and nuanced triage of these premalignant lesions may allow for more timely and personalized management, particularly among patients who desire fertility-sparing treatment.

As the treatment landscape for EC continues to evolve and incorporate both molecular and immunologic characteristics, it is unknown whether expanding these paradigms to premalignant lesions will further improve individualized risk stratification and treatment. Given the difficulty in predicting concurrent carcinoma in patients with AEH/EIN using traditional clinicopathologic techniques, our study aims to compare the molecular and immune signatures in pre-operative AEH/EIN specimens in cases with and without a subsequent diagnosis of concurrent carcinoma after hysterectomy.

Methods

After obtaining Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval, the electronic medical record system at our institution was queried for all patients with a pre-operative diagnosis of AEH/EIN from years 2001–2021. Patients were eligible if both pre-operative diagnostic (endometrial pipelle biopsy or endometrial curettings) and hysterectomy formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue specimens were available for further testing. A total of 34 patients were identified and included in our study. Clinicopathologic factors were extracted from the medical record for descriptive comparisons. Representative hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained sections for each patient were re-reviewed by two gynecologic pathologists (R.A. and S.B.) to confirm pre-operative and post-operative diagnoses. All specimens then underwent comprehensive profiling with Caris Life Sciences (Phoenix, AZ).

Whole exome sequencing (WES)

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed on genomic DNA isolated from FFPE tumor samples. The SureSelect XT assay (Agilent) was used to enrich all DNA samples for sequencing. A total of 592 whole-gene targets were enriched for NextSeq (Ilumina) sequenced tumors, and 700 relevant genes at high read-depth plus > 20,000 additional genes at lower read-depth were enriched for NovaSeq 6000 (Ilumina) sequenced tumors. Genetic variant calling was performed by board-certified molecular geneticists as previously described (Shaikh et al., 2021). Microsatellite instability (MSI) status was determined by counting the number of loci with insertions or deletions, a tumor was MSI-high (MSI-H) if it possessed ≥ 46 loci. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was measured by counting all non-synonymous missense mutations in each sample, and TMB-high (TMB-H) was defined as ≥ 10 mutations per megabase.

Whole transcriptome sequencing (WTS)

All FFPE specimens were reviewed by pathologists, and microdissection was performed in areas with a minimum of 10 % tumor content. Tumor-specific RNA was then extracted and enriched using RNeasy FFPE Kit (Qiagen). The TapeStation system (Agilent) was used to determine RNA quality and quantity. Biotinylated RNA baits were hybridized to purified synthetic cDNA targets, and then these bait-target complexes were amplified in a post-capture PCR reaction. Sequencing was performed using Novaseq 6000 (Ilumina) to an average of 60 million reads. Raw data was de-multiplexed using Dragen Bio-IT accelerator (Ilumina), and then further trimmed, counted, filtered of PCR duplicates, and aligned to human reference genome hg19 using STAR software. For transcription counting, the Salmon expression pipeline was used to generate transcripts-per-million (TPM) molecules.

Immune cell deconvolution & signature scores

Within the tumor microenvironment, the relative abundance of immune cell infiltrates was calculated from bulk RNA-sequencing data using quanTIseq (Finotello et al., 2019). T cell inflammation scores (TIS) were calculated using a validated T cell inflamed gene expression signature (Spranger et al., 2016). MAPK pathway activity scores (MPAS) were calculated using a validated 10-gene set that included: PHLDA1, SPRY2, SPRY4, ETV4, ETV5, DUSP4, DUSP6, CCND1, EPHA2, and EPHA4 (Wagle et al., 2018). Interferon scores (IFN) were calculated using a validated 6-gene signature including: IDO1, CXCL10, CXCL9, HLA-DRA, STAT1, and IFNG (Ayers et al., 2017).

Statistical analysis

Comparative analyses were performed using Chi-square, Fisher Exact, Welch’s, and Mann-Whitney U tests when appropriate. p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Benjamini-Hochberg test such that a false discovery rate corrected q-value of < 0.05 was considered significant, and the unadjusted existing p-value of < 0.05 was considered only a trend.

Results

Patient Characteristics.

Approximately three-fourths of the patients (73.7 %) in the AEH/EIN cohort underwent dilation & curettage (D&C) prior to hysterectomy compared to only one-fourth of the patients (26.7 %) in the EC cohort. The median amount of time from diagnosis of AEH/EIN to hysterectomy for all patients was approximately 9 weeks (range 2–228). Reasons for treatment delay exceeding 12 weeks included: unknown (n = 4), poor surgical candidacy requiring medical optimization (n = 3), and active treatment of another malignancy (n = 1). There were 4 patients who had a delay of greater than 12 months from time of AEH/EIN diagnosis to hysterectomy (1 patient in the AEH/EIN cohort vs. 3 patients in the EC cohort).

All patients in the EC cohort were diagnosed with endometroid adenocarcinoma (Table 2). Of the 15 patients with EC, 2 patients (13.3 %) had a presumed surgical stage higher than FIGO 2009 stage IA following hysterectomy. EC tumor sizes ranged from 2 mm to 80 mm. No patient had a tumor with lymphovascular space invasion, 1 patient (6.7 %) had a tumor with myometrial invasion into the outer third, and 2 patients (13.3 %) had tumor grades higher than FIGO grade 1. Of the patients with presumed FIGO 2009 stage I disease, 1 patient (7.1 %) was considered to have high-intermediate risk disease (Keys et al., 2004) and 10 patients (71.4 %) met Mayo criteria for low-risk disease (Mariani et al., 2000) (Supplemental Table 1). Molecular landscape

Of the 68 samples sent for comprehensive molecular profiling, 44 samples (64.7 %) were successfully characterized. Molecular analyses were not possible in the remaining samples (35.3 %) secondary to sample inadequacy. The distribution of genomic alterations in a selection of 20 biomarkers across the AEH/EIN and EC cohorts are illustrated in Fig. 1. The most common mutations in the AEH/EIN cohort were PTEN (57.7 %), KRAS (38.5 %), FGFR3 (11.5 %), and SOX10 (11.5 %) (Fig. 1A). In the EC cohort, the most frequent alternations included PTEN (83.3 %), MSI-H (22.2 %), PIK3R1 (22.2 %), CTNNB1 (16.7 %), KRAS (11.1 %), FGFR3 (11.1 %), SOX10 (11.1 %), and CTNNA1 (11.1 %) (Fig. 1B). There were no MSI-H, CTNNB1, or CTNNA1 alternations in the entire AEH/EIN cohort. There were also no tumors with POLE or TP53 mutations (not illustrated). There were several notable differences when comparing only the pre-operative specimens of the AEH/EIN (AH-B) and EC cohorts (EC-B). Mutations in PPP2R1A and SMAD2 were unique to the AH-B cohort. In contrast, only specimens in the EC-B cohort had ARID2, FAT1, MSI-H, SOS1, and CTNNA1 alterations. The percentage of biopsy specimens that were TMB-H was also greater at 20.0 % in the EC-B cohort compared to 9.1 % in the AH-B cohort (Supplemental Fig. 1). However, there were no significant differences in any single molecular alteration between the cohorts (Supplemental Table 2). Of note, there was one patient (ID #5) in the AEH/EIN cohort whose biopsy had several alterations not found in the entire EC cohort including: MUTYH, EZH2, FH, CCND1, NF1, and TNFRSF14.

When considering only matched specimens (i.e. both the pre-operative and hysterectomy specimens were successfully sequenced for a patient), there were 9 pairs in the AEH/EIN cohort and 4 pairs in the EC cohort. The concordance of molecular alternations between matched pairs ranged from approximately 50–100 % in the AEH/EIN cohort and 85–95 % in the EC cohort (Supplemental Table 3).

Immune Landscape

The tumor immune microenvironments of all AEH/EIN and EC samples were characterized using a variety of methods. The expression of ten immune checkpoint (IC) genes was quantified: CD80, CD86, CD274, CTLA4, HAVCR2/TIM3, IFNG, IDO1, LAG3, PDCD1, and PDCD1LG2 (Fig. 2). There were no significant differences in expression of immune activation across all samples between the AEH/EIN and EC cohorts (q > 0.05). Conclusion

Our study characterizes both molecular and immune differences between pre-operative AEH/EIN biopsies with and without a subsequent diagnosis of concurrent EC following hysterectomy. In our predominantly Black and urban cohort, the rate of concurrent carcinoma was consistent with the literature at 44 % (Trimble et al., 2006). We found that premalignant lesions adjacent to endometrioid tumors demonstrate both a higher number of genomic alterations and overall greater immunogenicity. The potential clinical implications of our findings are important in context of the evolving treatment landscape of AEH/EIN.

Our institution previously reported a high level of molecular concordance between pre-operative biopsy and hysterectomy specimens in sequence for patients with EC irrespective of pre-operative diagnosis (Abdulfatah et al., 2019). Since then, molecular classification of pre-operative EC specimens has become incorporated into routine clinical practice to guide management. The present study expands on these findings by analyzing molecular differences between pre-malignant, pre-operative biopsies with and without occult EC on a matched hysterectomy specimen. Prior studies profiling AEH/EIN and EC lesions have done so within the same specimen but not in sequential specimens. In these studies, investigators identified TCGA-defined canonical mutations in the AEH/EIN component (e.g. PTEN, CTNNB1, PIK3CA, ARID1A, KRAS), and also found that all paired lesions shared at least one somatic mutation (Russo et al., 2017, Li et al., 2021). We similarly found a diverse genomic landscape in AEH/EIN lesions and a high degree of molecular agreement across 20 biomarkers between matched specimens using comprehensive next-generation sequencing. As expected, the mutational burden (i.e. TMB-H) and frequency of target alterations were both greater in the EC specimens (Chapel et al., 2019).

In our study, MSI-H and CTNNB1 alterations were unique to the EC cohort. This finding is significant as there is very limited data to suggest that these genomic changes occur in premalignant lesions in isolation without concurrent malignant transformation (van der Putten et al., 2017, Zauber et al., 2015). Identification of mismatch repair deficient (i.e. MSI-H) tumors is of particular clinical interest given their strong association with Lynch syndrome and observed resistance against progestin therapy (Agusti et al., 2024). CTNNB1 mutations in low grade, early-stage EC are observed more frequently in younger women and are also strongly associated with a higher risk of cancer recurrence (Kurnit et al., 2017). Given these findings, any pre-operative, premalignant biopsies exhibiting these two molecular characteristics should be highly scrutinized, especially in patients desiring fertility-sparing treatments. PTEN and KRAS mutations were the two most common alternations found across all specimens. Consistent with our findings, these two mutations have been implicated in early endometrioid EC tumorigenesis (Russo et al., 2017, Li et al., 2021, van der Putten et al., 2017); however, their inactivation alone in premalignant lesions has not been associated with direct progression to carcinoma (Lacey et al., 2008). As more sequencing data becomes available for AEH/EIN lesions, then we may identify actionable molecular biomarkers to guide treatment.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to utilize immune cell deconvolution to explore differences within the tumor microenvironment (TME) between AEH/EIN specimens with and without occult endometrioid tumors. While the prognostic significance of immune cell infiltrates in EC remains undetermined (Horeweg et al., 2020), investigators have attempted instead to discriminate EC molecular subtypes (i.e. POLE-mutated, MSI-H, CN-low, CN-high) by defining their associated immune signatures (Dessources et al., 2023). Premalignant lesions adjacent to malignant tumors presumably also trigger a distinct host immune response against cancer cells, and our findings suggest that pre-operative biopsies can be differentiated by their immune cell composition. Immune checkpoints (IC) are complex inhibitory pathways critical to the immune system for maintaining self-tolerance under normal physiologic conditions, but ICs become dysregulated by tumors to promote immune escape and further proliferation (Hu et al., 2021). In our study, the absolute expressions of all ten immunoregulatory genes analyzed were higher in AEH/EIN pre-operative biopsies with a subsequent diagnosis of EC. Higher infiltrates of regulatory T cells in context of this elevated IC expression likely reflects inappropriate IC activation and active immunoediting to promote tumor progression (Dunn et al., 2004). At the same time, we also observed higher proportional infiltrates of various tumor-suppressing myeloid (neutrophils and M1 macrophages) and lymphoid (CD8 + T cells and NK cells) immune cells in these same biopsy specimens (Salgado et al., 2015). The only immune cell infiltrate distinctively lower in the EC specimens was pro-tumorigenic M2 macrophages (Salgado et al., 2015). Altogether, these findings suggest that AEH/EIN lesions with concurrent carcinoma still exhibit an overall pro-inflammatory TME. A more complete understanding of how premalignant lesions interact with host immune defenses with and without concurrent EC may allow future investigators to define specific immune signatures for prognostication.

Given the observed differences in immune contexture between AEH/EIN biopsies, we wanted to determine whether any existing immune scoring systems could be implemented into the pre-operative evaluation. T cell inflamed (TIS) and interferon (IFN) scores have been validated for predicting response to immune checkpoint inhibitors in solid tumors (Spranger et al., 2016, Ayers et al., 2017), and MAPK pathway activity (MPAS) scores predict sensitivity to RAF/MEK/ERK inhibitors and also clinical outcomes in multiple cancer types (Wagle et al., 2018). These three scoring systems have already been investigated in EC (Bruce et al., 2023), but we did not identify any prior studies exploring their use in premalignant lesions. Our findings here do not support the use of TIS, IFN, or MPAS scores to help distinguish between AEH/EIN biopsies with and without concurrent carcinoma.

Management of AEH/EIN is currently within the scope of practice for both benign gynecologists and gynecologic oncologists. However, given the relatively high rate of concurrent occult carcinoma, many studies have attempted to improve risk stratification to help triage patients to the most appropriate surgeon. Diagnostic sampling strategies such as using hysteroscopy with targeted biopsy, routine dilation and curettage, and multiple sequential biopsies have all been associated with a decreased likelihood of unexpected carcinoma (Suh-Burgmann et al., 2009). Utilization of an endometrial thickness cutoff of 15–20 mm to predict the presence of malignant tumors has also been reported (Abt et al., 2022). For patients undergoing definitive hysterectomy, the accuracy of intra-operative gross and pathologic assessment (i.e. frozen section) has also proven to be unreliable – the percentage of cases upstaged to cancer from preliminary to final diagnosis ranges from 30 % to 75 % in the literature (Sullivan et al., 2021, Indermaur et al., 2007). Considering the limitations of both pre-operative and intra-operative evaluations, some experts advocate that AEH/EIN should be managed exclusively by gynecologic oncologists capable of performing routine lymph node assessment (Chaiken et al., 2022). However, universal adoption of this treatment paradigm would increase healthcare costs and subject more patients to the morbidity associated with lymph node surgery (Dioun et al., 2021). Lastly, for patients who desire future fertility or are not surgical candidates, reported regression rates of AEH/EIN with progestin therapies range anywhere from 50 % to 90 % (Zaino et al., 2006). Further research is necessary to provide insight into which patients will achieve complete response, the expected duration of response, and early identification of tumors at risk for progression to EC. Therefore, incorporation of molecular and immune signatures to complement standard clinicopathologic studies has the potential to improve diagnostic precision and patient outcomes.

We acknowledge several limitations of our study including small sample size, incomplete sequencing data, and retrospective design. Several patients were excluded from enrollment because one of their matched FFPE tissues were either unavailable or inadequate for profiling. Using only archival specimens may have also contributed to a lower rate of successful NGS, further limiting our sample size. As a result, robust statistical comparisons between cohorts were not possible, and our conclusions are mostly descriptive. We did include three patients in the EC cohort that had treatment delays of greater than 3 months into our analysis. It is possible that these EC tumors developed after the initial diagnosis of AEH/EIN and therefore were not concurrent at time of original biopsy – however, the estimated risk of progression from AEH/EIN to EC is approximately 8 % at four years (Lacey et al., 2010). We also recognize that mutations in cancer-relevant genes can accumulate in histologically normal endometrial tissue as part of the natural aging process (Pandya et al., 2024). Tumor heterogeneity in both premalignant and malignant components may have influenced our findings since we did not profile multiple tissue sites within each specimen (Gatius et al., 2018). Nonetheless, our analysis is hypothesis generating and validation in a larger patient cohort is anticipated.

In conclusion, our study illustrates the molecular and immune differences between AEH/EIN pre-operative specimens with and without a concurrent diagnosis of EC. Further characterization of these differences may lead to advances in predictive capabilities to identify patients most appropriate for fertility-sparing treatment versus surgical management, and to determine whether they should be managed a general gynecologist or gynecologic oncologist.
