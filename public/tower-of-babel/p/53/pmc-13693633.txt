Anthracofibrosis: an under‐recognised mimic of malignancy and tuberculosis in a migrant population
Authors: Wai Yiu, Paul T. King, Martin I. MacDonald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnthracosis, usually regarded as a benign and uncommon bronchoscopic finding, was identified in 108 (9.9%) of 1154 bronchoscopy procedures at two Australian hospitals, with almost a third showing bronchial narrowing consistent with anthracofibrosis. Anthracofibrosis was rarely anticipated before bronchoscopy, and most procedures were undertaken to investigate suspected tuberculosis or malignancy, which were ultimately confirmed in only a minority of cases. Frequent readmissions, often for respiratory infection, support anthracofibrosis as a chronic airways disease and highlight the need to improve awareness, optimise investigation pathways and prioritise long‐term respiratory and infection‐prevention care.

Airway anthracosis describes the deposition of black, carbon‐containing pigment on the bronchial mucosa. In its uncomplicated form, it is generally regarded as a benign finding. 1 When this pigmentation is accompanied by bronchial narrowing, the condition is termed anthracofibrosis and is associated with marked airway stenosis (Fig. 1). 2 Although rarely reported in Western populations, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 anthracofibrosis is well described in Middle Eastern and South and East Asian countries. 2 , 7 , 8 With Australia's growing migrant population, this highlights the need for greater clinician awareness of a hitherto unfamiliar condition.

Anthracofibrosis is most often reported in older women without coal or tobacco exposure but with long‐standing biomass fuel exposure from cooking or heating in poorly ventilated dwellings. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 A strong association with tuberculosis (TB) has been described, with some authors proposing that TB‐related chronic airway inflammation may promote carbon deposition and impair its clearance, contributing to disease development. 11

Simple anthracosis is generally asymptomatic and discovered incidentally during bronchoscopy. By contrast, its more destructive form, anthracofibrosis, may present with cough, dyspnoea and, less frequently, haemoptysis, chest pain or constitutional symptoms. 2 Some patients develop features of chronic bronchitis or obstructive airways disease, with recurrent exacerbations and pneumonia. 7

Radiological abnormalities are common, including lymph node calcification, bronchial calcification and stenosis, mass‐like opacities and segmental collapse, 12 often prompting investigation for malignancy or TB with positron emission tomography (PET) imaging and tissue sampling. Differentiation from infection, granulomatous disease and cancer is therefore challenging and can lead to repeated, invasive and costly investigations, especially when clinicians are unfamiliar with the condition. We aimed to describe the prevalence and clinical characteristics of anthracosis and anthracofibrosis in a metropolitan Australian bronchoscopy cohort, with findings that are relevant to bronchoscopy practice worldwide.

We conducted a retrospective review of all bronchoscopies and endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) procedures performed at two metropolitan hospitals in Australia between October 2022 and October 2024, with approval from the local Office of Research and Ethics (RES‐24‐0000‐883Q). Procedural reports were screened for documented anthracosis (carbon pigmentation without structural distortion) or anthracofibrosis (anthracosis with bronchial narrowing or occlusion). 2 Clinical and radiological data were extracted from electronic medical records.

Patient demographics, country of origin, radiological findings, pre‐procedural clinical suspicion, final diagnoses and 12‐month readmission and mortality were recorded. TB was diagnosed by positive mycobacterial culture or polymerase chain reaction. Malignancy was confirmed by cytology or histopathology.

Statistical analyses were performed using stata MP 14.1. Continuous variables were compared using Student t test or Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. Categorical variables were analysed using χ 2 or Fisher exact test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Among 1154 bronchoscopy procedures, 108 patients (9.9%) had anthracosis (n = 77, 71.3%) or anthracofibrosis (n = 31, 28.7%). Patients with anthracofibrosis were significantly older (mean age 69.5 vs 62.7 years; P = 0.02), more frequently female (83.9% vs 58.4%, P = 0.01) and predominantly of Afghan origin (58.1% vs 24.7%, P = 0.001) compared with simple anthracosis cases (Table 1). No anthracofibrosis patients were Western born.

Radiological abnormalities were present in all anthracofibrosis cases, with lymphadenopathy being significantly more common than in simple anthracosis (83.9% vs 46.7%; P < 0.001). Clinical suspicion of anthracofibrosis before bronchoscopy was documented in fewer than 10% of cases, with no radiological reports suggesting this diagnosis. Bronchoscopy was most often performed to investigate possible TB (74.2%) or malignancy (48.4%), which were frequently suspected as competing differential diagnoses. Yet TB was ultimately confirmed in only five (16.1%) of 31 patients and malignancy in just one patient (3.2%, diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma). In over 80% of anthracofibrosis cases, no alternative diagnosis was identified (Table 2).

Among anthracofibrosis patients with available follow‐up data, no mortality occurred at 12 months. However, emergency hospital readmission within 12 months was common (32.2%), with 40% of readmissions attributed to pneumonia or respiratory tract infection.

Discussion

Anthracosis was common in this metropolitan Australian bronchoscopy cohort, with a prevalence close to 10%. Although simple anthracosis accounted for most cases, almost a third were complicated by airway stenosis (anthracofibrosis), challenging the perception of anthracosis as a largely benign finding and highlighting its frequent association with significant structural and radiological abnormalities (Fig. 1). Anthracofibrosis in this cohort predominantly affected older women of South Asian background, particularly of Afghan origin, where domestic biomass burning for cooking and heating is common, consistent with reports of anthracofibrosis in similarly exposed populations. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10

In Australia, the Afghan‐born population has more than doubled over the past decade, from 39 770 in 2013 to 88 730 in 2024, and now represents the 27th largest migrant community. 13 As migration from high‐prevalence regions continues, the burden of anthracofibrosis in Western health systems is likely to increase, reinforcing the need for greater clinician recognition and clearer investigation pathways.

Despite this, clinician awareness appears low. Anthracosis or anthracofibrosis was included as a pre‐procedural differential diagnosis in fewer than 10% of bronchoscopy requests, despite being the final explanation in most cases. Bronchoscopy and EBUS were most often performed to investigate possible TB, yet active TB was identified in only a minority of patients, in contrast to higher TB rates reported in overseas anthracofibrosis cohorts. 7 , 11 , 12 This lower prevalence may reflect robust TB screening and treatment programmes for migrants in Australia, but further work is needed to determine whether TB‐anthracosis associations reported elsewhere reflect referral bias, shared environmental exposures or a direct pathogenic relationship.

Investigation for malignancy or unexplained lymphadenopathy was the second most common indication, but only a single case of malignancy (diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma) was confirmed among anthracofibrosis patients. This disconnect between radiologic interpretation, clinician suspicion and bronchoscopic diagnosis means that many patients are channelled into workups for malignancy or TB, with anthracofibrosis only recognised late in the diagnostic pathway.

Radiological abnormalities were almost universal, typically lymphadenopathy, nodule or mass, and parenchymal changes that closely resemble malignancy or TB. Although certain CT features such as calcified ('brilliant') lymph nodes, hyperattenuating lung and atelectasis have been proposed as relatively specific for anthracosis, 14 imaging alone remains unreliable for definitive distinction from malignant or granulomatous disease, leaving bronchoscopy effectively unavoidable at present for definitive diagnosis in most patients. 7 , 12 , 15

In this cohort, a large proportion of patients with anthracofibrosis underwent fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)‐PET, with most demonstrating increased metabolic uptake, thereby closely mimicking malignancy and consistent with previously reported series. 16 , 17 Histopathological studies describe carbon‐laden macrophages, granulation tissue and fibrosis in anthracofibrotic lung, 18 supporting the concept that persistent anthracotic deposits drive ongoing macrophage activation and inflammation, analogous to nanoparticulate carbon deposition seen with cigarette smokers. 19 This chronic inflammatory activity may account for sustained FDG avidity even after exposure ceases. High FDG uptake in anthracotic lymph nodes therefore represents a key diagnostic pitfall, with important implications for procedural burden and patient anxiety.

These findings have clinical implications for diagnosis and management. First, clinicians should routinely consider anthracosis and anthracofibrosis in older women of South Asian or Afghan origin presenting with unexplained thoracic imaging abnormalities, and targeted history‐taking should include domestic biomass fuel exposure in early life. Second, recognising anthracofibrosis as a chronic, structurally disruptive airway disease is important because it appears to confer a substantial risk of recurrent respiratory tract infections and pneumonia, as reflected in the high 12‐month readmission rate. Finally, timely identification of the condition may help avoid repeated investigations – serial CT scans, PET‐CTs and repeated bronchoscopies – once a confident bronchoscopic diagnosis has been established, allowing clinicians to pivot from searching for occult malignancy or TB to optimising long‐term respiratory care and infection prevention in this vulnerable group.

Given that anthracosis can both coexist and mimic TB and malignancy on CT and PET, there is a pressing need for non‐invasive diagnostic or risk‐stratification tools that could safely reduce the need for immediate bronchoscopy or EBUS. Larger, prospective studies are required to refine diagnostic, surveillance and management strategies, particularly for asymptomatic patients, and to explore biomarkers or imaging features that might distinguish anthracotic disease from infection and cancer more reliably.

The retrospective nature of this study, reliance on bronchoscopist reporting and concentration of anthracofibrosis cases within a South Asian migrant subgroup may have led to underestimation of true prevalence and limit generalisability.
