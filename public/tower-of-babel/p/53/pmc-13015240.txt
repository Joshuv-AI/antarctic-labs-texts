Improving overall child survival in conflict and fragile settings via integration of medical innovations into broader health system strengthening efforts: lessons from six rural hospitals in Somalia
Authors: Yauba Saidu, Budzi Michael Ngenge, Igor Rudan
Published: 2026

Over the past century, the world has witnessed notable advances in medical innovation, including medical devices. Yet a key lesson from this progress is that while medical technologies can offer robust solutions, they also expose the limits of innovation when deployed in fragile health systems. One such example is a recent study by Ali and colleagues in eBioMedicine, which illustrates a public health success story involving a medical oxygen innovation in one of the most challenging health systems in the world.1 However, their findings also uncover a sobering reality: even with reliable access to oxygen from an innovative solar-powered oxygen delivery system, the risk of death from birth asphyxia is nearly four times higher than that from pneumonia (adjusted HR 3.93; 95% CI 1.17–13.22). In this commentary, we discuss the premise that medical oxygen innovations function most powerfully not as standalone interventions, but as catalysts for broader health system strengthening efforts.

In their study, Ali and colleagues examined survival outcomes among 1460 hypoxaemic patients who received oxygen therapy from solar-powered concentrators across six rural hospitals in Somalia. Over 93% of the patients were neonates and children under five, admitted for birth asphyxia (32%), pneumonia (34.9%), and other respiratory illnesses. Their findings demonstrate that facilities without grid electricity can effectively deliver life-saving oxygen to patients via solar-powered concentrators, without relying on fossil fuels or unstable electrical grids. This scalable and innovative oxygen delivery system offers significant promise for several conflict-affected countries, including Afghanistan, Chad, the Central African Republic, South Sudan, and Yemen, where access to medical oxygen remains a distant goal.

While their study results are promising, the high mortality rate among neonates with birth asphyxia highlights a critical medical challenge, lending support to current knowledge that birth asphyxia remains a leading cause of neonatal death, even in countries equipped with advanced neonatal intensive care units.2 Unlike pneumonia, where oxygen directly corrects alveolar hypoventilation, birth asphyxia involves irreversible injury that oxygen cannot reverse, reaffirming that oxygen therapy alone cannot repair multi-organ injuries resulting from birth asphyxia. Accordingly, birth asphyxia carried a higher mortality hazard than pneumonia among infants surviving 24 h (aHR 2.79; 95% CI 1.07–7.31). Additionally, the study showed that higher oxygen saturation at admission improved survival, with a 10% hazard reduction for each 1% increase in SpO2, emphasising the importance of early oxygen therapy before severe hypoxic injury occurs.

These findings raise a central policy question for fragile settings: whether to prioritise universal oxygen access for all hypoxaemic patients or to differentiate care based on underlying causes of hypoxemia. Ali and colleagues’ results support both approaches. The first approach involves investing in solar-powered oxygen delivery systems to ensure universal access to oxygen for conditions treatable with oxygen, such as pneumonia, trauma, and surgical needs.3 However, achieving equitable national coverage would require deployment of this system across all off-grid facilities. This undertaking could be time-consuming and prohibitively expensive for many resource-poor and conflict-affected countries.4 Nonetheless, deploying such systems would inevitably strengthen health infrastructure in these countries. For example, solar systems can electrify off-grid facilities, enabling nighttime lighting for labor and delivery rooms and improving the management of deliveries and the timely resuscitation of neonates, thereby reducing the risk of birth asphyxia.5

The second option prioritises prevention of conditions such as birth asphyxia, which oxygen cannot reverse. In Somalia, shaped by decades of conflict, fragile health systems, and very high neonatal mortality rates, prevention may be the most practical route to reducing asphyxia-related deaths. In 2021, birth asphyxia accounted for 21% of neonatal deaths, alongside prematurity (13%) and neonatal sepsis (9%), together responsible for nearly half of all neonatal mortality in the country.6 The unfortunate reality is that the country lacks the healthcare infrastructure—including skilled birth attendants, well-equipped pediatric intensive care units, and essential health supplies—to address this mortality through treatment services.7 Therefore, the country should also focus on deploying a combination of proven primary health care strategies, including antenatal risk screening, Basic Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care training for birth attendants, and neonatal resuscitation programs,8,9 delivered at scale through community health workers and frontline birth attendants.

We advocate a strategic approach that treats prevention and treatment as complementary and interdependent investments within a single continuum of care, rather than as competing or sequential priorities. As shown by Ali and colleagues, this combined approach is essential for Somalia and other resource-limited settings, where shared infrastructure can improve efficiency. Given that investment in oxygen alone is unlikely to achieve national mortality reduction targets for birth asphyxia—a point long emphasised by the Lancet Commission on Medical Oxygen Security, countries should consider integrating oxygen system projects into broader health system strengthening efforts.10 To this end, countries prioritise investing simultaneously in upstream interventions to prevent hypoxaemic injuries and downstream interventions like medical oxygen systems to address injuries from hypoxemia. Therefore, health policymakers should not view solar-powered oxygen systems as an endpoint, but as a foundational platform, whose impact on child survival will be maximised only when deliberately paired with strong primary health care and perinatal prevention strategies.
