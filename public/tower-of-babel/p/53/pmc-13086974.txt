Non-coding RNA regulation of the radiation-induced DNA damage response and its translational relevance
Authors: Christine Wilson, Anmol Gaur, Priyanka Swaroop, Vaishnavi Vishwas, Sachin Kumar, Sampa Ghose, Anita Chopra, D. N. Sharma, Sameer Bakhshi, Surender K. Sharawat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radiotherapy outcomes are strongly influenced by the DNA damage response (DDR), a coordinated network of signalling proteins, that detects breaks in DNA, and governs DNA repair, cell cycle checkpoint activation and cell-fate decisions. While canonical DDR control has traditionally been attributed to protein kinases such as ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM), ATM and rad3-related (ATR), and DNA-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs), accumulating evidence demonstrates that non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), circular RNAs (circRNAs), and emerging small RNA classes, serve as important upstream regulators of these pathways. In this review, we synthesise current evidence on how ncRNAs regulate core DDR signalling networks and direct the balance between homologous recombination (HR) and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), thereby influencing repair fidelity and overall cellular responses to radiation. We outline how ncRNAs regulate early DDR events in response to ionizing radiation, including the sensing of DNA double-strand breaks and the chromatin remodelling required for efficient recruitment of repair factors. In addition, we summarise emerging radiation-responsive RNA species such as DNA damage response RNAs (DDRNAs), vault RNAs (vtRNAs), and tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs) that contribute to genome stability and stress adaptation. Clinically, circulating ncRNAs represent minimally invasive biomarkers of radiation response, and their targeted modulation offers an opportunity for improving radiotherapy outcome, highlighting the translational relevance of defining ncRNA-mediated DDR regulation.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Radiotherapy is administered to approximately half of all cancer patients, reflecting its central role in modern oncologic practice [1]. Its therapeutic effectiveness is determined not only by advances in dose delivery and treatment planning but also by the intrinsic capacity of tumour and normal tissues to detect and repair ionizing radiation (IR) induced DNA damage. Ionizing radiation induces clustered DNA lesions, including DNA double-strand breaks, single-strand breaks, and oxidative base damage, which serve as the primary signals that activate the DNA damage response (DDR) [2, 3]. The DDR comprises an interconnected network of pathways that sense DNA lesions, activate cell-cycle checkpoints at the G1/S, intra-S, and G2/M transitions, and direct repair through mechanisms such as homologous recombination (HR) and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ). Differences in the fidelity, kinetics, and coordination of these processes underlie the variable responses of tumours and normal tissues to radiation, and they create therapeutic vulnerabilities that can be exploited clinically [4]. Although DDR signaling has traditionally been understood through the activity of protein kinases such as ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM), ATM and rad3-related (ATR), and DNA dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK), evidence accumulated over the past decade has expanded this framework [2, 4, 5]. Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) including microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs) are integral regulators of genome stability, participating in transcriptional control, RNA stability, protein scaffolding, chromatin modulation, and intercellular communication [3, 6]. Within the framework of radiation-induced genomic stress, experimental studies have begun to define ncRNA-mediated regulation of the DNA damage response, with evidence derived predominantly from tumour-specific model systems, most notably breast cancer, lung cancer, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma [7–9]. In parallel, radiation-responsive circulating microRNAs have been identified primarily through blood-based biomarker studies following ionizing radiation exposure [10, 11].

This review aims to systematically integrate current evidence on ncRNA-DDR interactions, highlighting the distinct regulatory layers through which ncRNAs influence damage sensing, checkpoint regulation, and repair pathway choice, and assessing their translational relevance for radiosensitivity, biomarker development, and therapeutic intervention. We first provide a concise overview of the DDR network and its relevance to IR response, followed by a brief introduction to major ncRNA classes, and their canonical mechanisms of action. In the subsequent sections, we examine ncRNA-DDR interactions in detail and discuss how these pathways may be leveraged for biomarker development and therapeutic intervention.

Overview of canonical DNA damage response after radiation

A hallmark of IR exposure is the generation of clustered DNA damage, where multiple lesions such as base modifications, abasic sites (apurinic/apyrimidinic lesions arising from hydrolytic loss of the nucleobase, leaving an intact deoxyribose-phosphate backbone), or strand breaks occur within one to two helical turns of the DNA duplex. These clustered lesions are difficult to repair because excision of one lesion can destabilize the DNA backbone at a nearby site, often converting otherwise reparable lesions into double stranded breaks (DSBs). This complexity underlies much of the cytotoxic and mutagenic potential of IR [12, 13]. While SSBs are less lethal, their persistence can collapse replication forks and generate secondary DSBs [14]. DSBs, in contrast, are intrinsically hazardous, and even a few unrepaired breaks are sufficient to trigger cell death [15].

Initiation of the DNA damage response relies on lesion-sensing complexes that recruit and activate phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase-related kinases (PIKKs). At double-strand breaks, the MRN complex composed of MRE11 (Meiotic recombination 11), RAD50 (an ATP-binding tethering factor), and NBS1 (a regulatory adaptor, also known as Nibrin), recognizes broken DNA ends and facilitates the recruitment and activation of the kinase ATM [16]. At sites of replication stress where persistent single-stranded DNA accumulates, replication protein A (RPA) coats the ssDNA and serves as a platform to recruit ATR through its binding partner ATRIP [17]. For DSBs repaired by non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), the Ku70/Ku80 heterodimer rapidly binds DNA termini and forms a ring around them, providing a loading site for the catalytic subunit DNA-PKcs to assemble the active DNA-PK holoenzyme [18, 19]. Once recruited and activated, ATM, ATR, and DNA-PKcs phosphorylate histone H2AX at serine-139, generating γH2AX. This chromatin mark spreads over megabase regions flanking the break and functions as a chromatin scaffold for the assembly of mediator proteins. Among these, MDC1 binds γH2AX to amplify the DDR signal, while 53BP1 accumulates at the modified chromatin to promote end-joining repair and influence repair pathway choice [20]. Together, these sensors, kinases, and mediators establish the signaling architecture that couples DNA damage detection to repair and checkpoint activation.

Repair pathway choice is influenced by cell-cycle phase and by the characteristics of the break. NHEJ predominates throughout the cell cycle and involves Ku70/Ku80, DNA-PKcs, and the XRCC4-Ligase IV complex; however, because no sequence homology is required, small insertions or deletions are common [8]. HR, by contrast, is a high-fidelity process restricted to S/G2 phases, requiring end resection, RAD51 filament assembly, and strand invasion into the sister chromatid [8, 9]. Checkpoint signaling is mediated by the effector kinases CHK1 and CHK2, which act downstream of ATR and ATM, respectively. Their activation enforces cell-cycle arrest at the G1/S, intra-S, and G2/M transitions, thereby stabilizing stalled replication forks and providing additional time for repair [21]. The downstream consequences of DDR signaling depend on the extent of damage and cellular context. Successful repair allows proliferation to resume, whereas persistent damage can trigger p53-dependent apoptosis, permanent cell-cycle exit via senescence, or autophagy, which may function either in cytoprotection or in promoting cell death after irradiation [21, 22].

Collectively, the radiation-induced DNA damage response constitutes a multilayered signaling network that couples lesion recognition to pathway-specific repair, checkpoint activation, and cell-fate outcomes. The balance between accurate repair and defective resolution of complex lesions determines whether irradiation promotes genomic stability, cell death, or therapeutic resistance. Beyond canonical effector protein factors, emerging evidence shows that regulatory non-coding RNAs intricately modulate these DDR processes, adding an additional regulatory layer with direct implications for radiosensitivity and resistance.

Major classes of non-coding RNAs and their roles in gene regulation

Non-coding RNAs, once regarded as transcriptional byproducts, are now recognized as functionally important regulators of gene expression with roles in both physiological and pathological processes, including cancer. Extensive work over the past decade has established ncRNAs as key regulators of cellular signaling networks and as potential therapeutic targets in oncology [23]. Broadly, ncRNAs can be categorized based on transcript length into small non-coding RNAs such as microRNAs, transfer RNAs (tRNAs), PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), and small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs), and long non-coding RNAs.

MicroRNAs are of approximately 18–25 nucleotides that are processed from precursor transcripts and incorporated into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). Through sequence-specific base pairing, miRNAs suppress gene expression primarily by promoting mRNA degradation or translational inhibition. By simultaneously targeting multiple transcripts, miRNAs fine-tune critical signaling pathways in a context-dependent manner and can function as either tumor suppressors or oncogenes depending on cellular and genetic background [24]. Circular RNAs represent another class of ncRNAs characterized by a covalently closed, single-stranded structure generated through back-splicing, a non-canonical splicing event that joins a downstream 3′ splice site to an upstream 5′ splice site. CircRNAs exhibit enhanced stability relative to linear RNAs and display diverse subcellular localizations. Functionally, they participate in regulatory networks through interactions with RNA, DNA, and proteins, and in some cases encode functional peptides, thereby contributing to context-dependent regulation of gene expression and cellular homeostasis [25].

Long non-coding RNAs on the other hand, are transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides, and data from the ENCODE project suggest that the human genome encodes more than 28,000 lncRNA species, many of which remain poorly annotated and functionally uncharacterized [26]. Their length facilitates the formation of complex secondary and tertiary structures, allowing lncRNAs to act as molecular scaffolds and interact with proteins, mRNAs, genomic DNA, and regulatory elements. Through these interactions, lncRNAs influence chromatin organization, transcriptional regulation, and post-transcriptional control, contributing to cancer development and progression [3].

Consistent with this multifunctionality, lncRNAs have been implicated in diverse cellular processes, including the DNA damage response. Repair of DNA double-strand breaks is primarily mediated by homologous recombination and non-homologous end joining, both of which are subject to RNA-mediated regulation. NcRNAs influence these pathways by modulating the expression, localization, and assembly of DNA repair proteins, as well as by scaffolding repair complexes and stabilizing DNA ends during processing and resection. Coordinated RNA-dependent regulation therefore provides an additional layer of control over pathway choice and repair fidelity in the DNA damage response [27].

MicroRNA control of DNA damage sensors, cell-cycle checkpoints and chromatin signaling following ionizing radiation

ATM and DNA-PKcs are central PIKK kinases that initiate DSB repair signaling. The microRNA miR-101 directly targets the 3′UTRs of ATM and PRKDC (DNA-PKcs), reducing protein expression, impairing DSB repair, and enhancing radiosensitivity in xenograft models of glioblastoma, lung, and prostate cancers [29]. MiR-421 similarly suppresses ATM, attenuating phosphorylation of p53 and CHK2, which weakens checkpoint activation and drives premature cell-cycle re-entry [30]. In glioblastoma cells, miR-26a also represses ATM, impairing DSB repair and sensitizing U87 cells to irradiation [31]. Additionally, miR-18a-5p targets both ATM and HIF-1α, producing radio-sensitization in lung cancer stem-like cells, thereby linking DDR suppression with hypoxia signaling [32].

Ionizing radiation-induced cell-cycle checkpoint activation are regulated by multiple miRNAs. The p53-responsive miR-34a reinforces checkpoint arrest leading to apoptosis through repression of BCL2 and cyclin/CDK regulators. Its overexpression sensitizes non-small cell lung cancer cells to irradiation by promoting apoptosis and reducing clonogenic survival [33, 34]. Dynamic changes in miR-34a expression have been documented in irradiated mouse tissues and in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of patients undergoing total-body irradiation, suggesting utility as a biomarker of radiosensitivity [35]. Interestingly, this p53-inducible miR-34a is not only dynamically regulated within irradiated tissues but is also selectively enriched in extracellular vesicles released after ionizing radiation. These vesicle-associated miR-34a molecules propagate DDR-related signals to recipient cells and can be detected in circulation, emphasizing their translational potential as minimally invasive biomarkers of radiosensitivity and treatment monitoring [13, 35]. The microRNA miR-181a is another example of post-transcriptional silencing of the RAD17 protein, which leads to inhibition of ATR/CHK1 signaling and weakened G2/M checkpoint control [36].

Efficient DNA damage response fidelity depends on chromatin-based recruitment of repair proteins. The microRNA miR-24 downregulates H2AX, the histone variant that becomes phosphorylated to γH2AX at sites of DNA double-strand breaks. Reduced H2AX levels impair γH2AX focus formation, compromise repair factor recruitment, and attenuate checkpoint signaling. In hematopoietic progenitors and fibroblasts, miR-24 expression lowered H2AX levels, decreased γH2AX accumulation after irradiation, impaired G2/M checkpoint arrest, and enhanced radiosensitivity [37].

Modulation of homologous recombination and NHEJ effectors by microRNAs in response to IR

Several miRNAs directly suppress core homologous recombination proteins. MiR-182 binds the 3′UTR of BRCA1, a critical mediator of end resection and RAD51 loading. Loss of BRCA1 shifts repair from HR to error-prone end joining, with miR-182 overexpression impairing DSB repair and enhancing radiosensitivity in breast cancer and glioblastoma models. Conversely, inhibition of endogenous miR-182 restored BRCA1 expression and HR efficiency [38]. Similarly, miR-34a targets RAD51, reducing IR-induced foci formation, impairing HR capacity, and sensitizing tumors to radiation in vivo [39]. In addition, miR-96 exerts a dual effect by suppressing both RAD51 and the translesion synthesis polymerase REV1, thereby generating defects in HR and translesion DNA repair and further enhancing radiosensitivity in prostate and breast cancer cells [33].

Non-homologous end joining is the predominant pathway for repair of DNA double-strand breaks and occurs throughout all phases of the cell cycle. MicroRNAs can modulate NHEJ efficiency by targeting the 3′-UTRs of mRNAs encoding core NHEJ components, including KU70/KU80, DNA-PKcs, and the XRCC-LIG4 complex. Suppression of these factors compromises accurate DSB repair, resulting in persistence of DNA lesions and enhanced cellular radiosensitivity following ionizing radiation [34, 35].

The microRNA miR-145, for example, directly binds the 3′-UTR of DNA-PKcs mRNA and inhibits its translation, leading to reduced protein levels of DNA-PKcs. Attenuation of DNA-PKcs delays or impairs DSB repair, evidenced by sustained γH2AX foci, a marker of unrepaired breaks. Consistent with defective repair, HeLa and U2OS cells expressing miR-145 exhibit increased sensitivity to ionizing radiation, reflected by reduced clonogenic survival [36].

In BRCA1-deficient high-grade serous ovarian cancer, therapeutic agents such as platinum compounds and PARP inhibitors induce cytotoxicity primarily through accumulation of DSBs. Elevated miR-622 expression in this context targets KU70 and KU80 mRNAs, suppressing NHEJ activity specifically during S phase and contributing to altered DNA repair pathway utilization [40]. Similarly, miR-101 directly targets the 3′-UTRs of both DNA-PKcs and ATM transcripts, resulting in reduced expression of these key DNA damage response proteins. Downregulation of DNA-PKcs and ATM leads to inefficient or delayed DSB repair and enhances cellular radiosensitivity [29].

Beyond direct targeting of NHEJ components, miRNAs can regulate NHEJ indirectly through upstream signaling pathways. For example, miR-7 binds to the 3′-UTRs of EGFR and PI3K mRNAs, leading to suppression of the EGFrR-PI3K-AKT axis, a pathway that promotes DNA-PKcs activation. Overexpression of miR-7 therefore reduces DNA-PKcs activity, impairs repair of radiation-induced DSBs, and increases radiosensitivity in cancer cells [41].

Regulation of radiation-induced chromatin dynamics and signaling by lncRNAs

Other lncRNAs influence DDR by shaping chromatin accessibility or upstream signaling pathways. The mechanisms of HOTAIR (HOX transcript antisense intergenic RNA) are context-dependent. In colorectal cancer, HOTAIR is induced after irradiation and enhances radio resistance by stabilizing ATR-dependent checkpoint signaling. Its silencing disrupts the ATR-ATRIP regulatory complex at DNA damage sites, impairs DNA repair, and increases IR-induced apoptosis [17]. In breast cancer, HOTAIR functions as a ceRNA for miR-218, where its knockdown elevates miR-218 levels and radio-sensitizes cells via the HOTAIR/miR-218 axis [44]. The lncRNAs LINC00261 and LINC00665, have been implicated in radiotherapy adaptation. After irradiation of prostate cancer cells, LINC00261 and LINC00665 are upregulated and its depletion increases residual γH2AX/53BP1 foci, and alters DNA-repair gene expression (CtIP/RBBP8, XPC), indicating a role in modulating HR/NER programs during the radiation response [45].

NORAD (Non-coding RNA Activated by DNA Damage) is a highly abundant and DNA-damage-induced lncRNA that plays a key role in maintaining genomic integrity. It functions in the cytoplasm as a multivalent decoy for PUMILIO (PUM1/2) RNA-binding proteins, sequestering them away from mRNAs encoding essential mitotic, DNA replication, and repair factors. Loss of NORAD leads to unchecked PUMILIO activity, which suppresses these critical transcripts and, in turn, precipitates profound chromosomal instability, including aneuploidy [46]. More recently, NORAD has also been shown to promote the formation of phase-separated PUMILIO condensates, termed NP bodies, thus compartmentalizing PUMILIOs and preserving their proper regulation. Disruption of this phase separation similarly results in genome instability, emphasizing a phase separation–dependent mechanism of DDR regulation [46]. Importantly, in the context of ionizing radiation, NORAD expression is strongly induced in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, where it enhances radio resistance by activating the EEPD1/ATR/Chk1 signaling axis and by inhibiting pri-miR-199a1 processing and the exosomal transfer of miR-199a-5p. These findings highlight a dual mechanism whereby NORAD both reinforces checkpoint signaling and suppresses the paracrine spread of radio sensitizing miRNAs, thereby promoting therapeutic resistance [47]. Considering these genome-stabilizing and radiation-adaptive functions, NORAD represents a critical but incompletely characterized regulator of radiosensitivity in cancer.

LncRNAs as regulators of HR and NHEJ pathways in response to IR exposure

The lncRNA PCAT-1 (Prostate Cancer Associated Transcript 1) suppresses BRCA2 post-transcriptionally, impairing HR and sensitizing prostate and breast cancers to radiation [48]. Conversely, lnc-RI (Long Non-Coding RNA Radiation Induced) stabilizes LIG4 mRNA, thereby enhancing NHEJ capacity and supporting survival after irradiation in colorectal cancer [49]. In addition, lnc-RI can act through competing endogenous RNA mechanisms, it binds miR-4727-5p to enhance LIG4 expression and miR-193a-3p to regulate RAD51 expression, demonstrating that a single lncRNA species can modulate both NHEJ and HR to fine-tune DNA repair [50].

Several lncRNAs act as molecular scaffolds that stabilize DDR protein complexes. Among these, lncRNA LINP1 (lncRNA in non-homologous end joining pathway 1) is the best characterized, which facilitates NHEJ by bridging Ku70/80 and DNA-PKcs to stabilize the repair complex [40, 41]. LINP1 is selectively upregulated in triple-negative breast cancer compared to other breast cancer subtypes [42]. Functionally, LINP1 depletion diminishes NHEJ efficiency, prolongs γH2AX persistence, and sensitizes tumor models to irradiation, consistent with delayed DSB repair kinetics [43].

The lncRNA LINC-PINT (Long Intergenic Non-Protein Coding RNA, P53 Induced Transcript) functions as a negative regulator of NHEJ in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC). LINC-PINT is damage-responsive and binds DNA-PKcs, reducing its expression and recruitment to DSB sites, thereby inhibiting DNA repair and increasing radiosensitivity in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, LINC-PINT dampens ATM/ATR-CHK1/CHK2 signaling and limits DNA-PKcs-dependent end joining, yielding a heightened IR response in NPC models [51].

Similarly, lncRNALINC00312 functions as a radio sensitizing lncRNA in nasopharyngeal carcinoma by directly binding DNA-PKcs, disrupting its recruitment to Ku80-bound DNA ends, and thereby impairing the formation of the DNA-PK repair complex. Overexpression of LINC00312 diminishes NHEJ repair efficiency, slows resolution of IR-induced DNA breaks, as shown by prolonged comet tail moments, and suppresses downstream signaling via the ATM/ATR and AKT pathways. Functionally, this results in increased radiosensitivity of NPC cells both in vitro and in vivo, an effect that correlates with improved radiotherapeutic outcomes in patients expressing higher LINC00312 [44].

LncRNAs as competing endogenous RNAs and miRNA sponges in radiation-induced DDR

Many lncRNAs exert DDR functions indirectly by acting as ceRNAs that sequester miRNAs targeting DDR repair genes. Through this mechanism, they de-repress HR and NHEJ effectors such as BRCA1 and RAD51, reinforcing DNA repair programs [49]. For example, HOTAIR functions not only as a chromatin-associated regulator but also as a competing endogenous RNA. By sequestering miR-218, HOTAIR prevents miR-218-mediated repression of DNA repair and survival genes, thereby sustaining ATR-dependent checkpoint signaling and enhancing post-irradiation survival of breast cancer cells [44]. Similarly, in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, radiation-induced upregulation of lncRNA NORAD promotes radio resistance by facilitating EEPD1/ATR/Chk1 checkpoint signaling and by acting as a ceRNA that inhibits pri-miR-199a1 processing and reduces the exosomal transfer of miR-199a-5p, thereby sustaining DNA repair and survival pathways [47].

CircRNA-mediated regulation of the DNA damage response to IR exposure

Circular RNAs are covalently closed RNA molecules generated by back-splicing of pre-mRNAs, characterized by higher stability than their linear counterparts. Compared to lncRNAs and miRNAs, circRNAs are only beginning to be investigated in the context of ionizing radiation. Current evidence indicates that circRNAs can regulate DDR through sponging of miRNAs, binding RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), or modulating transcriptional programs that impact DNA repair, checkpoint control, and oxidative stress [52]. Radiation has been shown to alter circRNA expression profiles across tumor types, and specific circRNAs correlate with radio response and prognosis [53, 54]. Their relative stability and detectability in biofluids also suggest potential use as biomarkers of radiation response [55]. The circRNA circCDYL2 has been identified as a radiation-inducible circRNA in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, where it directly modulates homologous recombination repair. Mechanistically, circCDYL2 interacts with the translation initiation factor EIF3D, promoting ribosome recruitment to the 5′UTR of RAD51 mRNA and thereby increasing RAD51 protein synthesis. Upregulation of circCDYL2 enhances RAD51-dependent repair, accelerates resolution of ionizing radiation-induced DNA double-strand breaks, and contributes to radiotherapy resistance. In contrast, depletion of circCDYL2 reduces RAD51 translation, impairs HR capacity, and results in accumulation of unresolved DNA damage. Functionally, circCDYL2 knockdown sensitizes nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells to irradiation both in vitro and in xenograft models, highlighting its role as a circRNA regulator of homologous recombination and radio resistance [56].

CircRNAs can also function as competing endogenous RNAs, evidence connecting circRNA-mediated miRNA sequestration to DNA damage response and cell survival is currently limited to one reported case. The circular RNA circPVT1, is frequently upregulated in diverse cancers and contributes to therapy resistance. In non–small cell lung cancer, circPVT1 acts as a molecular sponge for miR-1208, reducing its availability to target downstream effectors involved in DNA damage repair and pro-survival signaling. Silencing circPVT1 restores miR-1208 activity, leading to repression of repair and survival genes, accumulation of DNA damage, increased apoptosis, and reduced clonogenic survival after irradiation. Functionally, this ceRNA interaction establishes circPVT1 as a mediator of radio resistance in NSCLC, while its depletion enhances radiosensitivity both in-vitro and in-vivo [57].

Beyond its role in NSCLC radio-sensitization, circPVT1 is embedded within the oncogenic PVT1 locus and intersects with c-MYC signaling. Through RNA-binding protein interactions and ceRNA networks, circPVT1 modulates transcriptional and post-transcriptional programs that converge on DDR and cell-cycle regulation under genotoxic stress. CircPVT1 is therefore a nodal regulator with functions extending beyond IR response, linking genome maintenance with established oncogenic signaling pathways [58].

CircRNAs originating from chromatin-remodeling loci may intersect with DDR pathways by modulating genome stability. CircSMARCA5, derived from the SMARCA5 locus encoding the chromatin remodeler SNF2H, has been associated with chromatin organization and glioma biology. Since SNF2H is required for nucleosome remodeling at DNA double-strand breaks, γH2AX domain spreading, and efficient recruitment of BRCA1 and RAD51 during homologous recombination [59], circSMARCA5 may plausibly intersect with DDR signaling and radiation response by modulating chromatin accessibility [60]. Since SNF2H itself is targeted by radiation-induced miR-99 family members and its suppression reduces BRCA1 recruitment and repair efficiency after fractionated irradiation, it would be valuable to investigate whether circSMARCA5 expression or function is similarly altered following ionizing radiation. Such studies could clarify whether circSMARCA5 contributes to repair efficiency or radiosensitivity in tumors exposed to radiotherapy.

CircRNAs do however remain less extensively characterized than miRNAs or lncRNAs in radiation oncology, but emerging studies highlight their potential to influence DDR and radiation response.

Role of emerging non-coding RNAs in ionizing radiation-induced DDR

Beyond miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs, additional RNA species have been implicated in DNA damage signaling. Damage-induced RNAs (DDRNAs), processed from break-proximal lncRNAs by DROSHA and DICER, are required for the recruitment of MDC1, 53BP1, and BRCA1 at DSBs and amplify DDR signaling [61–64]. Vault RNAs (vtRNAs), particularly vtRNA2-1, modulate apoptosis by inhibiting PKR and are incorporated into the vault complex with MVP, whose overexpression correlates with poor radiotherapy outcomes [65, 66]. Other ncRNA classes are now beginning to reveal direct links to radiation-relevant DNA repair dynamics; Y RNA fragments, in complex with YBX1, regulate PARP1 occupancy at DNA double-strand breaks, altering PARP1-mediated repair pathway choice. Because PARP1 is a key determinant of radio-sensitization, this mechanism suggests that Y RNAs could modulate therapeutic response to ionizing radiation [67]. Likewise, snoRNAs are now recognized to influence ATM/ATR checkpoint activation, oxidative stress tolerance, and chromatin structure during DNA repair. Considering that these pathways are strongly activated after irradiation, radiation-induced snoRNA dysregulation could directly shape radiosensitivity and treatment outcomes [68]. tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs) also contribute to translational control of repair and stress-response genes. For example, a 5′-tRF from tRNAGlyGCC represses ribosomal protein transcripts by displacing the RNA-binding protein YBX1, redirecting translation toward adaptive stress pathways during oxidative stress [69]. Similarly, tRF-LysCTT and tRF-GluCTC are upregulated under genotoxic insults such as doxorubicin or cisplatin and have been linked to altered DNA repair gene expression and checkpoint control [70]. While direct evidence under ionizing radiation remains limited, such findings suggest that tRFs induced by oxidative and genotoxic stressors could likewise influence DDR signaling and radiosensitivity [71, 72]. Although direct evidence under ionizing radiation remains limited, these tRFs are induced by stress and contribute to DDR-related translational rewiring, suggesting that their dysregulation could modulate radiosensitivity [43, 72, 73]. These ncRNA classes, already proven to shape DDR in other contexts, may represent hidden determinants of radiosensitivity and therapeutic resistance in cancer, and future studies examining their roles in the context of IR are warranted.

Limitations of current evidence

Evidence linking non-coding RNAs to radiation-induced DNA damage response regulation is derived largely from in vitro cancer cell line studies, with support from selected xenograft models and limited patient-based analyses. While these studies establish ncRNAs as regulators of DDR signaling, systematic in vivo validation and large-scale clinical investigations are required.

A central limitation across ncRNA classes is context dependence. The impact of ncRNA modulation on radiosensitivity varies with tumor type, genetic background including p53 status, dominant repair pathway usage, and irradiation conditions, limiting generalizability across malignancies and treatment settings. Mechanistic characterization of lncRNAs and circRNAs is frequently confined to specific tumor models, and functional conservation across cancers remains largely untested. Emerging ncRNA classes, including DDR-associated RNAs and Y RNA-derived fragments, have been implicated in DDR factor recruitment and PARP-related repair, but there are no studies with direct validation under clinically relevant irradiation conditions. Overall, ncRNAs should currently be viewed as context-dependent regulators rather than universal determinants of radiosensitivity. Defining clinically actionable ncRNA-DDR interactions will require expanded in vivo validation, integration with tumor genetic and microenvironmental features, and studies in patient-derived cohorts.

Translational and therapeutic opportunities in radiation-induced DDR regulation by ncRNAs

The integration of non-coding RNAs into the framework of radiation oncology opens several translational opportunities. Circulating ncRNAs are potential minimally invasive biomarkers of radio response. A prominent example includes the p53-inducible miRNA, miR-34a detectable in plasma, and released from cells in response to radiation stress [74, 75]. The dysregulation of non-coding RNAs in cancers following radiotherapy, such as LINC00261 and LINC00665 in prostate cancer, circPVT1 in NSCLC, and lncRNA NORAD in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, highlights the potential of studying other radio-responsive ncRNAs as candidates of liquid biopsy to stratify patients by intrinsic radiosensitivity and to monitor treatment response during radiotherapy [45, 47, 57, 76]. Although radiation-responsive ncRNAs are detectable in circulation, their proposed use as biomarkers is currently supported largely by preclinical and exploratory clinical studies, highlighting the need for systematic prospective validation, with detailed reporting of diagnostic performance metrics.

Therapeutically, ncRNA-directed interventions have the potential to modulate DNA repair capacity in tumors. Antisense oligonucleotides or siRNAs directed against oncogenic lncRNAs (LINP1, HOTAIR) could impair NHEJ or checkpoint signaling and sensitize resistant tumors to IR, whereas restoration of tumor-suppressive ncRNAs such as LINC00312 or miR-182 inhibitors could restore HR fidelity [38, 44, 51, 77]. Considering the stability of circRNAs and their nodal regulation of DDR, effectors like RAD51 are potentially underexplored targets. Importantly, these strategies could be rationally combined with existing DDR inhibitors, such as PARP or ATR inhibitors, to exploit synthetic lethality and widen the therapeutic window of radiotherapy, which could be explored further [10, 78–80].

The emerging ncRNA classes such as DDRNAs, vault RNAs, Y RNA fragments, snoRNAs, and tRNA-derived fragments highlight additional RNA-dependent layers of DNA damage regulation in the radiation response [65–67, 69]. Although still incompletely characterized, these RNAs have been implicated in chromatin remodeling, modulation of sensor-kinase signaling, and stabilization of repair complexes at DNA lesions [67, 81, 82]. Their involvement suggests that RNA-mediated regulation of radiosensitivity could extend beyond miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs, and raises the prospect that these newer ncRNA species could be harnessed as biomarkers of treatment response or as therapeutic targets to modulate DDR in radiotherapy.

Challenges and future directions

Despite promising advances, several challenges remain in translating ncRNA biology into radiotherapy. A central limitation is the specificity and delivery of ncRNA therapeutics. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) or mimics must be delivered efficiently to tumors while minimizing uptake in normal tissues, because systemic administration often results in low bioavailability, rapid clearance, off-target accumulation particularly in liver and kidney, and potential toxicities, thus necessitating tumor-specific delivery systems such as conjugates or nanoparticle platforms [83–86]. Additionally, the context-dependent functions of ncRNAs pose a challenge, where the same RNA may promote repair and survival in one tumor type but act as a suppressor in another, reflecting both tumor heterogeneity and dynamic radiation responses [87]. Discussion

NcRNAs regulate a certain DNA damage response process that have been shown to influence radiation sensitivity largely in in vitro and pre-clinical studies. Across tumor types, these processes include checkpoint enforcement, encompassing ATM/ATR-CHK signaling and checkpoint recovery dynamics. DNA repair pathway utilization, reflected in the balance between homologous recombination and non-homologous end joining, and repair efficiency, determined by the speed and completeness of lesion resolution. Among these, checkpoint control is a particularly consistent point of ncRNA-mediated regulation. MiRNAs, in particular, frequently modulate checkpoint robustness by reducing the abundance of key DDR sensors or mediators, or by attenuating chromatin-based signaling required to sustain cell-cycle arrest. For example, suppression of ATM and DNA-PKcs signaling can shorten effective checkpoint enforcement and impair downstream repair coordination. The miR-101 directly targets ATM and PRKDCs, reducing protein levels and increasing radiosensitivity in vitro and in xenograft settings [29].

In addition, chromatin-associated signaling can represent a limiting step for effective checkpoint activation and DNA repair. For example, miR-24 targets H2AX, lowering γH2AX focus formation and compromising recruitment of repair/checkpoint factors, which translates into increased radiation sensitivity in hematopoietic contexts [37]. In contrast, several lncRNAs act in the opposite direction by reinforcing checkpoint signaling or stabilizing repair-permissive arrest, thereby promoting post-IR survival. A clear example is NORAD in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, where radiation induction promotes resistance through activation of the EEPD1/ATR/Chk1 axis and through suppression of pri-miR-199a1 processing plus reduced exosomal transfer of a radiosensitizing miRNA i.e. miR-199a-5p [47].

Another major convergence is on repair pathway utilization and pathway choice, where ncRNAs can either deplete core repair capacity, often miRNAs or stabilize and assemble repair machinery such as lncRNAs/circRNAs. For example, in vitro studies show that the lncRNA LINP1 can function as a scaffold that physically bridges Ku80 and DNA-PKcs to support efficient NHEJ. LINP1 depletion increases γH2AX persistence, impairs repair, and sensitizes tumors to radiotherapy in triple-negative breast cancer models, with regulation linked to EGFR signaling and p53 [77]. Conversely, some lncRNAs function as suppressors of end-joining repair. For example, LINC-PINT impairs DNA-PKcs-mediated repair and enhances radiotherapeutic sensitivity in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [91]. Therefore, the same pathway node could be pushed towards promoting either resistance or sensitivity depending on the ncRNA and cellular context.

At the HR node, regulation occurs beyond transcription. For example, circRNAs can promote radioresistance by facilitating translation of HR effectors. The circular RNA circCDYL2 has been shown to increase RAD51 translation initiation (via EIF3D-mediated ribosome recruitment), enhancing HR repair and radiotherapy resistance in nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Its depletion impairs HR capacity and sensitizes tumors to irradiation [56].

Additionally, an increasingly well-supported convergence point is RNA-dependent amplification of DDR signaling at breaks. Break-proximal transcription and processing can generate small RNA species required for effective DDR focus formation and propagation. Studies on DROSHA/DICER-dependent DDR-associated RNAs supports a model in which RNA processing is dispensable for primary sensing but contributes to secondary recruitment/accumulation of key DDR factors at damage sites [63].

Emerging ncRNA classes also influence factors that bias DNA damage processing toward specific repair pathways. An example is Y RNA-derived fragments acting with the RNA-binding protein YBX1 to influence PARP1 at DNA damage sites. Findings also support increased PARP1 at DSBs through an RNA–protein complex mechanism, linking small ncRNA fragments to PARP-dependent repair dynamics [67]. For example, in colorectal cancer models, HOTAIR has been reported to enhance radioresistance by stabilizing ATR-ATRIP checkpoint signaling and sustaining post-irradiation cell-cycle arrest, whereas in other tumor contexts, HOTAIR depletion increases radiosensitivity through ceRNA-mediated derepression of miRNAs, such as miR-218, leading to attenuation of DNA repair and pro-survival signaling pathways [51].

Although this review focuses on ncRNA-mediated regulation of intrinsic DNA damage response pathways, radiation response is also shaped by extrinsic radiobiological determinants that intersect with DDR signaling. Tumor hypoxia and metabolic state influence DNA repair efficiency, checkpoint activation, and redox balance, and may indirectly modulate ncRNA expression or function following irradiation. In addition, TGF-β signaling has emerged as an important modifier of DDR activity, including ATM activation and checkpoint control, with recent evidence supporting functional crosstalk between TGF-β pathways and DDR signaling in the context of radiotherapy [92]. Immune and tumor microenvironment interactions further influence radiation outcomes through inflammatory signaling and immune-mediated clearance of damaged cells [93]. Extracellular vesicle-mediated ncRNA transfer provides a mechanism for non-cell-autonomous propagation of DDR-associated phenotypes, for example, the NORAD-associated modulation of exosomal miRNA export in radioresistant tumors, which has been described above [47]. Together, these factors act as additional regulators of DDR signaling and may shape how ncRNA-mediated regulatory programs influence radiotherapy response across tumor contexts.

From a translational perspective, ncRNA-driven reinforcement of specific DDR dependencies, such as reliance on ATR-Chk1 signaling, DNA-PK-mediated end joining, or PARP-associated repair offers a rationale for combination strategies that pair radiotherapy with targeted DDR inhibitors. Non-coding RNA expression patterns may help identify tumors most likely to benefit from ATR, DNA-PK, or PARP inhibition in conjunction with irradiation. Evidence supporting ncRNAs as biomarkers of radiosensitivity remains largely exploratory. Systematic analyses of circulating miRNAs have primarily focused on radiation exposure rather than treatment response. For example, a meta-analysis by Małachowska et al. identified reproducible radiation-responsive circulating miRNAs across studies but highlighted substantial heterogeneity in study design, sample timing, and analytical platforms, precluding robust diagnostic performance estimates or clinical implementation [10]. Similarly, Song et al. proposed a panel of radiation-upregulated miRNAs as a signature of ionizing radiation exposure, demonstrating discriminatory capacity in experimental settings; however, these studies did not establish validated sensitivity, specificity, or AUC metrics for predicting tumor radiosensitivity or patient outcomes [11]. Mechanistic studies with translational relevance, such as miR-182-mediated regulation of BRCA1 impacting DNA repair capacity and therapeutic sensitivity, provide strong biological rationale but stop short of biomarker validation [38]. More recently, lncRNAs including LINC00261 and LINC00665 have been associated with long-term tumor adaptation following radiotherapy in prostate cancer, suggesting potential links to radioresistant phenotypes, yet without prospective validation or defined predictive performance parameters [45]. Collectively, while these studies support the biological plausibility of ncRNAs as radiation response biomarkers, no circulating or tissue-based ncRNA biomarkers for radiosensitivity have yet been prospectively validated with published AUC, sensitivity, or specificity, highlighting a gap between mechanistic insight and clinical readiness.

Depending on whether an ncRNA acts through transcript repression, molecular scaffolding, translational regulation, or RNA-dependent assembly of DNA damage response complexes, RNA-directed approaches such as antisense oligonucleotides or RNA interference are potential therapeutic strategies. However, as the majority of current evidence linking ncRNAs to radiotherapy response is derived from in vitro and preclinical models, further validation in in vivo systems and expression analysis in patient-derived cohorts with defined performance metrics will be essential to establish their clinical relevance in the field of radiobiology.

Conclusion

A large body of preclinical evidence has demonstrated that non-coding RNAs are important regulators of the DNA damage response, influencing repair pathway choice, checkpoint activation, and cell-fate decisions after irradiation. Evidence across miRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and newer RNA classes emphasizes their roles as regulators of radio resistance and as potential therapeutic targets. Circulating ncRNAs detectable through liquid biopsy offer opportunities as minimally invasive biomarkers, while ncRNA-directed therapeutics could be combined with DDR inhibitors to widen the therapeutic window of radiotherapy. Future progress will depend on overcoming delivery barriers, defining tumor-specific functions, and integrating ncRNA biology into precision frameworks to personalize radiotherapy and improve outcomes.
