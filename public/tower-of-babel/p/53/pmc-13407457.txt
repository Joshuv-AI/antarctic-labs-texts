Identification of targets for high penicillin G production in an industrial strain of Penicillium rubens
Authors: Kaiqi Dou, Yuanyuan Pan, Yongpeng Yao, Xiuyun Tian, Ning Guo, Gang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractTo elucidate the mechanisms underlying high penicillin G (PenG) production, transcriptomic analysis was conducted on 36 samples from the reference Penicillium rubens Wisconsin 54-1255 and its industrial descendant strain IMP002. The resulting dataset was analyzed using weighted gene co-expression network analysis, yielding 26 modules that clustered genes with similar expression patterns. Three modules highly correlated with PenG production were selected to identify key hub genes, whose functions were further validated in vivo. Overexpression of the six identified hub genes, comprising one transporter gene, four regulatory genes, and one N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase gene, increased PenG production in IMP002 by 24.08% to 41.64%. Notably, overexpression of the transporter gene led to a 33.33% increase in intracellular PenG accumulation. Moreover, overexpression of the N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase gene was found to modulate the filamentation of IMP002. Our work provides an effective strategy for identifying novel targets related to high PenG production in the industrial strain.Key points Six key targets for high PenG production were identified in the strain IMP002.Regulator, transporter, and N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase boost PenG production.WGCNA is an effective strategy for identifying novel high-yield targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13885-z.Keywords: WGCNA, Transcriptome, Transporter, Regulator, N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase

Introduction

Penicillium rubens is one of the best representatives of microorganisms applied in the industrial biotechnology (Barreiro et al. 2024; Fierro et al. 2022). After numerous rounds of mutagenesis and screening by classical breeding strategies, the reference strain Wisconsin 54–1255 and its derived high penicillin–producing strains were obtained (Peng et al. 2014; Specht et al. 2014; Ziemons et al. 2017). Correspondingly, the penicillin titer in the current industrial strain has increased by at least three orders of magnitude relative to their ancestors (Hersbach et al. 1984). After years of incessant study, the penicillin biosynthetic pathway has been elucidated. PenG biosynthesis starts with the condensation of three amino acids, α-aminoadipic acid (α-AAA), L-cysteine, and L-valine to form δ-(L-α-aminoadipyl)-L-cysteinyl-D-valine (ACV) by an ACV synthetase in cytoplasm (van der Lende et al. 2002), and then the tripeptide ACV is cyclized under catalysis of the isopenicillin N (IPN) synthase to give IPN (Kreisberg-Zakarin et al. 1999), which is subsequently transported to peroxisomes (Martín et al. 2012). Finally, its α-aminoadipyl group is replaced by phenylacetic acid (PAA) side-chains with acyl-CoA-activated form by an IPN acyltransferase (IAT) to produce PenG (Hamed et al. 2013; Moreno-Vargas et al. 2012; Spröte et al. 2009). Meanwhile, PAA could be also detoxified through the homogentisate pathway, which starts with hydroxylation of PAA to 2-hydroxy phenylacetate catalyzed by the phenylacetate hydroxylase PahA (Campos et al. 2020; Lamas-Maceiras et al. 2006). Since the genome of Wisconsin 54–1255 was sequenced in 2008, much more progress has been made in the biosynthesis of penicillin and its regulation, which has in turn facilitated improvement of the industrial strains (van den Berg et al. 2008).

In recent years, the mechanisms underlying high penicillin production have been progressively elucidated through proteomic analysis and the integrated analysis of multi-omics data (García-Estrada et al. 2020; Jami et al. 2010). Firstly, the penicillin biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) of high-producing strains usually undergoes amplification in comparison with the wild-type strain (Barredo et al. 1989; Fierro et al. 1995). Subsequently, the degradation of PAA, the side-chain precursor of PenG, is reduced. Additionally, the abundances of peroxisomes and the transcripts of the corresponding encoding genes are increased. Adequate NADPH supply from the pentose phosphate pathway and weakening of the relative secondary metabolic pathways are the typical characteristics of the high penicillin producers (Campos et al. 2020; García-Estrada et al. 2013; Kleijn et al. 2007). However, the penicillin BGC is not amplified in some high penicillin producing strains, and lack of a linear relationship between the copy number of the penicillin BGC and penicillin production indicates that the regulation of penicillin biosynthesis is complicated (Newbert et al. 1997; Ziemons et al. 2017). Identifying novel targets to further improve penicillin production has become a major bottleneck.

Transcriptomics is one of the most widely used omics techniques for studying the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, having led to the discovery of numerous responsive genes and pathways in P. rubens (Terfehr et al. 2017; Veiga et al. 2012). However, the routine comparison, which focuses on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between two group pairs, is based on the single dimension theory and difficult to find novel responsive targets. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), as the systematic biological method, is widely used to construct gene association networks across diverse groups or samples (Langfelder and Horvath 2008). This method first identifies modules with highly correlated expression patterns and subsequently explores the relationships between these modules and specific traits. Finally, the hub genes central to the phenotype of interest can be identified (Ahmad 2024). WGCNA has been extensively applied in clinic and botanical research, significantly advancing our understanding of gene interactions, the identification of hub genes, and the prediction of gene functions (Luo et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024).

In this study, WGCNA was performed to identify modules and co-expressed genes correlated with the high PenG production, utilizing 36 transcriptomic datasets from two P. rubens strains (Wisconsin 54–1255 and its descendant strain IMP002). From this analysis, the function of 20 hub genes associated with regulation, transportation or N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase activity was validated in vivo. Of these, six genes were confirmed to have a functional link to the enhanced PenG production. Beyond PenG production improvement, we characterized the transport function of Pc18g04170 and the altered filamentation induced by Pc21g21470 overexpression. Our study highlights WGCNA as a robust strategy for identifying novel targets to improve antibiotic production in the industrial strains.

Materials and methods

Strains, plasmids, media, and the culture conditions

All strains and plasmids used are listed in Table S1. P. rubens Wisconsin 54–1255 and IMP002 were cultured on PNK medium (200 g/L potato extract, 20 g/L glucose, 25 g/L KCl, 5 g NaCl, 20 g/L agar) at 28 °C for 3 and 5 days respectively for sporulation. For fermentation, 3 × 108 spores were collected and inoculated into 40 mL of the seed medium (61 g/L corn steep liquor, 20 g/L sucrose, 5 g/L CaCO3, pH 5.8–6.0) in 250-mL flasks and incubated on the shaker with 250 rpm at 28 °C for 48 h. Subsequently, seed cultures (10%, v/v) were inoculated into 40 mL of fermentation medium (42 g/L corn steep liquor, 1.5 g/L Na2SO4, 4 g/L KH2PO4, 130 g/L lactose, 8 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 5 g/L CaCO3, 2 g/L potassium phenylacetate, 8 mL/L corn oil, pH 5.8–6.0), shaking at 250 rpm at 28 °C for 6 days. YPD medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, and 2% glucose) and Czapek’s medium (30 g/L sucrose, 1 g/L K2HPO4, 5 g/L NaNO3, 0.5 g/L MgSO4·7H2O, 0.5 g/L KCl, 0.01 g/L FeSO4, 20 g/L agar) were used for mycelial growth.

DNA and RNA isolation, and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Isolation of the fungal genomic DNA was performed as previously described (Duan et al. 2024). For RNA isolation, the fungal mycelia were collected in triplicate from both Wisconsin 54–1255 and IMP002 at 12 h, 18 h, 24 h, 48 h, 96 h and 144 h of fermentation respectively. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Total RNA was extracted using the Ultrapure RNA Kit (Jiangsu Cowin Biotech Co., Ltd, China) and treated with DNase 1 (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The purity and concentration of total RNA were analyzed by using Nanodrop 2000 and Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer with RIN number higher than 8.0.

For qPCR, a total of 1 μg RNA was reversely transcribed to cDNA by the HiScript III 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+gDNA wiper) (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, China). The qPCR was performed using the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Without ROX) Kit (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, China). The relative transcriptional levels of the target genes were normalized to the β-actin encoding gene, and the fold changes were calculated using 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001).

Determination of the copy number of penicillin biosynthetic gene cluster

To quantify the copy number of penicillin BGC in IMP002, the plasmids pUC19-actin (4412 bp), pUC19-pcbAB (6860 bp), and pUC19-penDE (3955 bp) were constructed as follows. Fragments of actin, pcbAB and penDE were amplified from the genomic DNA of strains IMP002 or Wisconsin 54–1255. The pcbAB and penDE fragments were subsequently assembled into the EcoRI and KpnI sites of pUC19, respectively, while the actin fragment was cloned into the BamHI and HindIII sites. All primers are listed in Table S2. The concentrations of the purified standard plasmids were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and their corresponding copy numbers were calculated based on the molecular weight and concentration of each plasmid. Standard curves were generated by performing qPCR on a tenfold dilution series (1 × 10−2 to 1 × 10−9) of these plasmids. The logarithms of the copy numbers were plotted against the corresponding cycle threshold (Ct) values to get linear regression equations and correlation coefficients. The regression equations were y = −3.7785x + 43.303 (R2 = 0.9898) for pUC19-actin, y = −3.4506x + 40.847 (R2 = 0.9968) for pUC19-pcbAB, and y = −3.4175x + 41.354 (R2 = 0.9985) for pUC19-penDE. Subsequently, genomic DNA extracted from Wisconsin 54–1255 and IMP002 respectively was subjected to qPCR. The initial copy numbers of the target genes were interpolated from the standard curves using the experimental Ct values. Finally, the absolute genomic copy numbers were determined by normalizing these initial values against the single-copy internal reference gene actin (Li et al. 2021).

The cDNA library construction and strand-specific RNA sequencing

A total of 1 μg of RNA was used to construct the cDNA library with the TruSeqTM RNA Sample Preparation Kit (San Diego, CA). Firstly, mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) beads, fragmented and reversely transcribed to cDNA with the SuperScript Double-Stranded cDNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen, CA). The resulting cDNA was sequentially converted into U-labeled second-stranded DNA, subjected to end-repair, A-tailed, and ligated with the adapters. Subsequently, uracil-N-glycosylase (UNG) was used to digest the U-labeled second-stranded DNA strand. The remaining strand was amplified and insert fragments of approximately 300 bp were selected for sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Data analysis

The raw data was qualified, trimmed, and filtered to produce clean data, which was aligned to the reference genome of Wisconsin 54–1255 and assembled by using HISAT2 and StringTie, respectively. Transcriptome data has been submitted to the NCBI SRA database (accession number PRJNA1263659). Gene abundance was quantified according to TPM by RSEM. Differential expression analysis was performed by using the DEGseq2 (Love et al. 2014), and DEGs with |log2 (fold change)|≥ 1 and P ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. In addition, the enrichment analysis was performed to identify the significant DEGs enriched in GO terms and metabolic pathways with q ≤ 0.05 as the cut-off value with the R package “Clusterprofiler” (version 3.18.0).

WGCNA

The expression matrix from 36 fermentation samples was used to filter the genes with TPM < 1. Co-expression network analysis was performed using the WGCNA package (version 1.71) in R (version 4.1.2) as previously described (Mason et al. 2009). The pickSoftThreshold function was used to achieve the optimal β value to make the constructed network be scale-free. To reduce noise and false correlation, the adjacency matrix was converted to topological overlap matrix (TOM), and the new distance matrix was used to construct co-expression networks or draw TOM graphs. Meanwhile, the average connectivity of all genes in modules was calculated, and the genes with high connectivity were regarded as hub genes. For each module, the eigengene (the first principal component for each module) was determined, and the correlations of eigengenes with PenG production were subsequently calculated.

Construction of the target gene overexpressed strains

For overexpression of the 20 hub genes in IMP002, all plasmids were constructed based on the integrative plasmid pUC19-G418. The G418 resistance cassette in this construct was derived from pAgG418 and recombined into the HindIII site of pUC19 (Duan et al. 2024). The G418 resistance cassette comprises the nptII open reading frame, which is driven by the Neurospora crassa atp-9 (encoding H+-transporting ATPase lipid-binding protein) promoter and flanked by the tub (β-tubulin) terminator. Firstly, the pUC19-G418 was digested with SalI and EcoRI. Next, the gene overexpression cassette containing the entire target gene and its respective promoter and terminator was amplified. Finally, the amplified cassette was assembled to the digested pUC19-G418 to generate the corresponding recombinant plasmids. Subsequently, these recombinant plasmids were introduced into IMP002 by protoplast transformation as previously described (Cantwell et al. 1990), except that VinoTaste® Pro (1 g/10 mL Osmitc Medium) was used for protoplast preparation.

HPLC analysis of PenG and penicillin V (PenV)

For extracellular PenG detection, the fermentation supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane. Intracellular PenG was analyzed according to the method previously described by Horii (Horii et al. 2020) with minor modifications. Briefly, mycelia were collected by centrifugation and ground into powder in liquid nitrogen. Subsequently, 100 mg of the resulting powder was suspended in 500 µL of ethanol for extraction. HPLC was performed with a C18 analytical column (Gemini 250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, Phenomenex) on the Waters 2695 system at the flow rate of 1 mL/min. CH3CN (containing 0.1% formic acid) was linearly increased from 10 to 100% in 15 min, and then washed with 100% CH3CN for 5 min and equilibrated with 10% CH3CN for 8 min. To quantify the PenG and PenV production, standard curves were generated using benzylpenicillin sodium and penicillin V potassium standards across a concentration range of 0.025 to 0.1 g/L. Regression equations were established by plotting the peak areas detected at 210 nm against the known concentration. The resulting equations were y = 76,345,815.5963 x + 758,207.6560 (R2 = 0.9961) for PenG and y = 64,018,610.7143 x + 1,171,618.5357 (R2 = 0.9954) for PenV. Finally, the production of PenG and PenV was determined based on their respective peak areas and standard calibration curves.

Raman spectral acquisition, processing and analysis

The strain P. rubens IMP002/G418 and its recombinant strain OEPc18g04170 were cultured in the fermentation medium for 144 h. Subsequently, 1 mL of the fermentation broth was collected and centrifuged, followed by washing the mycelia three times using 0.9% NaCl solution. The mycelia were then resuspended in the 0.9% NaCl and diluted to a concentration that allowed individual hyphae to be clearly distinguished and captured by the optical tweezers. Single-cell Raman measurements were conducted using a laboratory-built laser tweezers Raman spectroscopic system. This system was equipped with an inverted laser confocal microscope (Olympus IX81, Tokyo, Japan), a 785 nm output of diode laser (08-NLD, Cobolt, Solna, Sweden), and a high-sensitivity charge-coupled device (CCD) detector (MV-GEF401GM-T) as previously described (Lu et al. 2020; Xue et al. 2024). For each measurement, 20 μL of the mycelial suspension was pipetted onto a quartz sample cell. The laser tweezers were manually positioned at the hyphal tips of at least 30 individual hyphae per sample. To acquire Raman spectra, each hypha was excited for 40 s using a 50 mW laser power measured at the sample. Raw Raman spectra were processed as previously described (Xue et al. 2024). The resulting standardized spectra were then used for further analysis. To isolate authentic PenG Raman signals, background subtraction was performed using spectra from the IMP002 strain cultured without PAA supplementation. Subsequently, intracellular PenG levels were quantified using a standard curve constructed from a series of PenG solutions (5, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10 mM). The intensity of the characteristic Raman peak at 1004 cm−1 (corresponding to the benzyl group) was plotted against the corresponding concentration to get a linear regression equation (y = 0.695 x − 2.2959, R2 = 0.989). The background-subtracted Raman intensities from the experimental samples were then fitted to this regression model to calculate the intracellular PenG concentration.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed in triplicate. The averages were calculated as well as standard errors of the mean.

Results

Phenotypic difference between P. rubens Wisconsin 54–1255 and IMP002

A high penicillin producing strain IMP002 was obtained by the high-throughput screening method based on the traditional mutagenesis from its parent strain Wisconsin 54–1255. Although these two strains showed a close relationship, they had obvious morphological difference. The hyphae of IMP002 were thicker than those of Wisconsin 54–1255 when grown on YPD agar after 24-h growth (Fig. 1A). IMP002 started producing spores only after 96-h growth on PNK medium, while Wisconsin 54–1255 began to produce spores after 48-h growth. However, the spore counts of the two strains were comparable after 168 h of growth, reaching approximately 5 × 108 per 9-cm Petri dish (Fig. 1B). The spores color of IMP002 was light green while that of Wisconsin 54–1255 was dark green (Fig. 1C). The PenG production of Wisconsin 54–1255 was 0.43 ± 0.03 g/L after 168 h of fermentation, while that of IMP002 reached 3.54 ± 0.07 g/L, representing an over eightfold increase (Fig. 1D). Meanwhile, the biomass of IMP002 was only 15% lower than that of Wisconsin 54–1255 (Fig. 1E).

Transcriptomic analysis of Wisconsin 54–1255 and IMP002

To reveal the mechanism of high penicillin production in IMP002, the transcriptomic sequencing of Wisconsin 54–1255 and IMP002 was performed. The raw reads of 27729458–42131576 were generated. After removing low-quality reads, the clean reads were 27037678–41599702, and the mapped rates of clean reads to the reference genome were 94.19–98.31%. The statistics on the sequencing data for each sample is shown in Table S3. Pair-wise DEG analysis showed that 1836−3999 of DEGs, accounting for 13.93−30.34% of the total expressed genes (13,180), had significant differences in transcript abundance between Wisconsin 54–1255 and IMP002 (Fig. S1). Transcriptional analysis revealed that the expression levels of all genes within the penicillin cluster—including pcbAB (encoding ACVS), pcbC (encoding IPNS), and penDE (encoding IAT)—were significantly higher in IMP002 than in Wisconsin 54–1255. This transcriptional upregulation (pcbAB = 1.38 ± 0.08 to 28.41 ± 4.87-fold; pcbC = 1.65 ± 0.37 to 23.02 ± 2.23-fold; penDE = 1.62 ± 0.37 to 30.91 ± 10.68-fold) directly correlated with the enhanced PenG production observed in IMP002 (Fig. S2). In IMP002, the transcriptional level of pcbAB increased sharply by 206.65 ± 55.04 folds at 24-h fermentation and that of pcbC increased 8.07 ± 2.76 folds at 48-h fermentation, and then both of them displayed a gradually increasing trend until the end of fermentation. The transcripts of penDE were lower in the early stage of growth, and then reached a peak value of 43.50 ± 1.81-fold upregulated at 48 h, showing that PenG biosynthesis is more strictly controlled (Fig. S3). Meanwhile, the PAA degradation pathway was attenuated via transcriptional downregulation of pahA (encoding phenylacetate 2-hydroxylase) to 0.38 ± 0.11-fold of the baseline at 18 h. Concurrently, PAA conversion was bolstered by the transcriptional upregulation of pclA (encoding phenylacetyl-CoA ligase), which increased from 1.69 ± 0.66-fold to 25.34 ± 9.03-fold during fermentation (Fig. S4).

In addition, the primary metabolic pathways for the precursor amino acids (Cys, Val, and α-AAA) were upregulated, including Pc21g18210 (encoding the serine acetyltransferase CysE) for cysteine biosynthesis; Pc22g20630 (ilvE), Pc18g02530, and Pc13g02430 (ilvD) for valine biosynthesis; Pc22g13190 (lys1) and Pc12g11860 (encoding the 2-aminoadipate transaminase) for α-AAA biosynthesis (Fig. S5). The pentose phosphate pathway for supplying the major NADPH was also upregulated, including genes Pc22g21430 (encoding the triosephosphate isomerase TIM), Pc13g12450 (encoding the transketolase TK), Pc22g21440 (encoding the ribose 5-phosphate isomerase RPI), Pc20g03330 (encoding the glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase G6PD), Pc22g25470 (encoding the 6-phosphogluconolactonase 6-PGL), Pc12g10940, Pc12g08920, and Pc19g00410 (encoding the 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase 6-PGDH) (Fig. S6). Meanwhile, the NADPH concentration was significantly higher in IMP002 than that in Wisconsin 54–1255 after 4, 5 and 6 days of fermentation (Fig. S7A). The qPCR analysis revealed IMP002 contains 8 copies of the penicillin BGC, whereas Wisconsin 54–1255 harbors only a single copy (Fig. S7B), which was highly consistent with PenG production. To further explore the effects of other secondary metabolites on PenG production, the 40 BGCs in common between Wisconsin 54–1255 and IMP002 were analyzed. One core biosynthetic gene from each BGC was selected as the representative to reflect the transcriptional changes of the entire cluster, and its expression fold change in IMP002 relative to Wisconsin 54–1255 was calculated. Results demonstrated that the expression of 16 BGCs (including those for sorbicillin and nidulanin) remained stable or silent, while that of 10 BGCs (including those for ferrichrome and chrysogine) was significantly decreased, the expression of 10 BGCs (including those for macrophorin A, choline and 6-methylbenzoic acid) was significantly upregulated, and another 4 BGCs exhibited unstable changes (Fig. S8). These findings imply the complicated influence of competing secondary metabolites on PenG production. In summary, significant global metabolic responses were observed in IMP002, encompassing an abundant supply of amino acid and NADPH, reduced PAA degradation, enhanced PAA utilization, and altered regulatory mechanisms that influenced other secondary metabolic pathways (Fig. 2).

A proposed mechanism for high penicillin G production in P. rubens IMP002 based on comparative transcriptomic analysis

Construction of the weighted co-expression network modules

The weighted gene co-expression network was constructed by using 36 fermentation samples of P. rubens with a total of 8072 DEGs. All samples were hierarchically clustered (Euclidean distance) based on the gene expression data, and an outlier IP48C was excluded (Fig. S9). Only the transcripts with the TPM higher than 1.0 were retained. Finally, 35 samples with 7977 transcripts were used to construct the co-expression network. To guarantee a scale-free topology network, a soft-thresholding power of β = 9 was used (Fig. 3A). A total of 26 co-expression clustering modules were identified based on the dynamic tree cutting algorithm with the parameters of minModuleSize at 30 and mergeCutHeight at 0.25 (Fig. 3B).

Identification of the key modules associated with high PenG production

Module-trait relationship analysis demonstrated that three modules were highly correlated with high PenG production. Specifically, the module exhibiting the strongest correlation contained 157 genes (r = 0.91, p = 2e−14; designated by the software as the “lightcyan” module). Two additional modules, comprising 327 and 970 genes, also showed high correlations with r values of 0.86 (the “pink” module) and 0.76 (the “blue” module), respectively (Fig. 3C, Fig. S10). Subsequently, the transcriptional profiles of these three modules were analyzed (Fig. S11) and found to be highly consistent with the high PenG production phenotype. Therefore, these three key modules were selected for further analysis.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the key modules

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to gain deep insight into the biological function of the genes in these three key modules (GTKMs). Following screening with a threshold of P < 0.03 (GO) or P < 0.01 (KEGG), the significantly enriched GO and KEGG terms were identified. In the biological process category, GTKMs predominantly participated in cysteine biosynthetic process, ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic process, DNA-templated transcription, and methylation. In the molecular function category, GTKMs were mainly involved in crucial processes such as isopenicillin N synthase activity, zinc ion binding, DNA binding, oxidoreductase activity and N-acetyltransferase activity. In the cellular component category, GTKMs exhibited notable associations with membrane and nucleus (Fig. 4A). In addition, the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis suggested that GTKMs were significantly enriched in the pathways of cysteine and methionine metabolism, valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis, penicillin and cephalosporin biosynthesis, peroxisome, glutathione metabolism, ABC transporters, and primary metabolite pathways including pentose phosphate pathway and citrate cycle (Fig. 4B). These results indicated that the primary metabolism, energy supply, transporting system, regulation, oxidative stress, as well as the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites could be highly related to PenG production.

Identification of hub genes for high PenG production

Based on the hypothesis that the genes with the same expressional patterns had similar functions, we identified the hub genes for high PenG production from three key modules. Firstly, the candidate hub genes were preliminarily screened using a connectivity threshold exceeding 50 in the 157-gene and 327-gene modules (labeled lightcyan and pink, respectively). Due to the larger number of genes in the 970-gene module (labeled blue), top 8% of genes were chosen as the hub candidates. Meanwhile, differential expression and functional annotations were also considered. Given the importance of regulation and transportation in penicillin biosynthesis (Martín, 2020; Martín et al. 2010; van de Kamp et al. 1999), two genes (Pc22g19920 and Pc22g27040) from the 157-gene module, eight genes (Pc21g21470, Pc22g08140, Pc22g19540, Pc22g04580, Pc14g00980, Pc12g10570, Pc22g22560, and Pc21g13820) from the 327-gene module, and twelve genes (Pc20g08230, Pc12g12320, Pc06g02400, Pc18g04170, Pc22g23650, Pc21g15420, Pc21g23060, Pc21g08180, Pc16g10020, Pc14g02060, Pc18g06160, and Pc21g11230) from the 970-gene module were identified as the hub genes for further validation. The workflow for screening and verifying hub genes was shown in Fig. 5A. Detailed information on the selected hub genes from three modules was listed in Table S4.

Identification and validation of hub genes based on WGCNA. WT denotes the reference strain Wisconsin 54–1255

Of the 22 hub genes, there are 14 regulatory genes, 4 transporter encoding genes, and 4 other genes. To evaluate the significance of these hub genes in PenG production, total RNA was isolated from Wisconsin 54–1255 and IMP002 after incubation for 18 h and 24 h, respectively. Transcript levels of the hub genes were analyzed by qPCR. The results showed that the transcripts of 21 hub genes, except for Pc22g19920 (encoding a PhoG-like DNA-binding regulator), were significantly increased in IMP002 compared with Wisconsin 54–1255 (Fig. 5B), confirming a correlation between hub gene expression and PenG production.

Overexpression of hub genes markedly increases PenG production

To test if there is a causal relationship between the hub gene expression and high PenG production, the recombinant strains for hub gene overexpression in IMP002 were constructed and the strain carrying the plasmid pUC19-G418 (IMP002/G418) was used as control (Fig. S12). PenG production in all recombinant strains was quantified by HPLC. Compared with the IMP002/G418 control, overexpression of Pc12g10570 (regulator), Pc18g04170 (transporter), Pc21g08180 (regulator), Pc21g21470 (N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase), Pc22g19540 (regulator), and Pc22g27040 (regulator) individually significantly increased PenG production by 24.01% to 41.61%. Notably, overexpression of Pc18g04170 had the most remarkable effect on PenG production, resulting in an increase of approximately 41.61% (Fig. 6A).

To further explore the function of Pc18g04170, we quantified intracellular PenG production using Raman spectroscopy, with a detection threshold established at 5 mM based on a standard dilution series (1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 mM) (Fig. S13). Below 3 mM, peak splitting and significant background interference compromised signal specificity, rendering quantification unreliable at lower concentrations. To account for background signals arising from non-PenG metabolites, we used the IMP002 strain cultured without the side-chain precursor PAA as a negative control, ensuring observed Raman signatures were specific to PenG. Using this validated Raman assay and a PenG solution standard curve, we found that PenG concentration at the hyphal tips of the OEPc18g04170 strain reached 5.63 mM, whereas it fell below the detection threshold in the IMP002/G418 control (Fig. 6B). However, recognizing that localized hyphal-tip signals might not fully represent overall mycelial PenG levels, total intracellular PenG was further quantified by HPLC analysis of mycelial extracts. Consistent with the Raman-based observations, the HPLC results confirmed that total intracellular PenG in OEPc18g04170 (0.028 ± 0.001 mg/g WCW) surpassed that of the control (0.021 ± 0.001 mg/g WCW) by 33.33% (Fig. 6C). Collectively, these complementary analyses demonstrate that overexpression of Pc18g04170 significantly enhances PenG production, validating its functional role in regulating PenG biosynthesis.

Parallel microscopic analysis of another candidate, OEPc21g21470, demonstrated altered septation and cell swelling compared to the control (Fig. 6D). Specifically, OEPc21g21470 affected the filamentation of IMP002, a phenotypic change potentially linked to PenG biosynthesis. To investigate whether these two mechanisms could be combined to further enhance PenG production, a double-overexpression strain (OEdouble) was constructed (Fig. S14A, B). However, fermentation results showed that OEdouble failed to further increase PenG production compared to the single-gene overexpression strains (Fig. S14C). This suggests that the metabolic or regulatory effects of Pc18g04170 and Pc21g21470 are not synergistic, and their contributions to PenG production may be redundant or involve overlapping pathways.

Discussion

Penicillin production by P. rubens is one of the best representatives to study the metabolic process and compartmentalization of secondary metabolite in filamentous fungi. To date, integrated omics have been applied to reveal the molecular mechanisms underlying high penicillin production, which in turn further facilitate the rational design for industrial strains (García-Estrada et al. 2020). In our industrial strain IMP002, the metabolic process has been changed in large scale, including the abundant supply of NADPH and benzylpenicillin side-chain precursors and the change of regulatory mechanism. It is well-known that penicillin was produced under stress conditions, and the nutrition of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorous, and sulfur had a strong effect, implying its biosynthesis was regulated strictly (Brakhage et al. 2004). Well-known global regulators, such as PacC, CreA, Nre, and PcFKH1, mediated pH-dependent activation, carbon catabolite repression, nitrogen repression, and activation of the penDE and phlA genes, respectively. The global regulator LaeA interacts with the Velvet complex components VeA and VelC positively regulates penicillin biosynthesis (García-Estrada et al. 2020). In addition, specific ligands such as 1, 3-diaminopropane and spermidine could modulate the binding affinity of regulators through interaction (Martín and Liras 2024). However, these are the tip of the iceberg, and more unknown regulators are waiting to be discovered. In this study, overexpression of four predicted regulatory genes of Pc12g10570, Pc21g08180, Pc22g19540, and Pc22g27040 sharply increased the PenG production by 41.08%, 34.19%, 37.37%, and 24.14% in IMP002, respectively. Regulators Pc12g10570 and Pc21g08180 have the common GAL4-like Zn(II)2Cys6 binuclear cluster domain and fungal specific transcription factor domain (fungal_TF-MHR), Pc22g19540 displays only one fungal Zn(II)2Cys6 domain. Interestingly, Pc22g27040 is composed of four tandemly repeated C2H2-type zinc finger domains (Fig. S15). However, their function and action mechanisms on penicillin biosynthesis remain unclear.

Of all candidate hub genes, overexpression of Pc18g04170 significantly enhanced PenG production by 41.61%. Pc18g04170 showed 45.66% identity with the transporter AlcQ involved in aspirochlorine biosynthesis of Aspergillus oryzae (Chankhamjon et al. 2014), implying that Pc18g04170 participated in intracellular and extracellular release of PenG or its intermediate metabolites. Further quantification results showed that Pc18g04170 not only participated in transportation of PenG in the extracellular but also in the intracellular, which was similar to the gliotoxin secretion of Aspergillus fumigatus mediated by transporter GliA (Wang et al. 2014).

Raman spectroscopy has been previously employed to monitor intracellular PenG production in P. rubens‌ (Horii et al. 2020). While the sensitivity of conventional Raman spectroscopy is typically limited, with reported thresholds around 50–60 mM (Clarke et al. 2005), our study successfully established a much lower detection limit of 5 mM through a PenG standard dilution series. This enhanced sensitivity can be attributed to the subcellular compartmentalization of PenG. Rather than being uniformly distributed throughout the cytosol, PenG is highly concentrated within peroxisomes localized near the hyphal tips (Meijer et al. 2010). We propose that this localized “molecular crowding” combined with our refined background subtraction method ensured the acquisition of authentic PenG Raman signals despite relatively low concentrations. However, recognizing that localized hyphal-tip measurements may not fully reflect the metabolic state of the entire mycelium, we further validated our findings using HPLC as a gold-standard reference. The high consistency between these in situ Raman detections and bulk HPLC measurements robustly confirms the enhanced biosynthetic capacity of our engineered strains.

Pc21g21470 encoding N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase is potentially involved in glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) biosynthesis, performing a function similar to PGAP4, which catalyzes the initial step of GPI-GalNAc biosynthesis (Hirata et al. 2018). Pc21g21470 contains three typical transmembrane domains, a structural feature shared with PGAP4 but distinct from the single-pass transmembrane domains found in other Golgi glycosyltransferases (Fig. S16). Although we combined the simultaneous overexpression of Pc18g04170 and Pc21g21470 to further increase PenG production, there was no combined improvement, indicating an independent action mechanism for each gene in PenG production. Similar results were also previously described (Holloway et al. 2022). We speculated that the simultaneous high expression of transporter and N-acetylglucosamine transferase genes might excessively consume energy molecules, disrupt the original metabolic balance, and further inhibit PenG production.

To verify the direct contribution of Pc18g04170 and Pc21g21470 on PenG production, we quantified the copy number of each gene in its corresponding recombinant strain. Our results demonstrated that only one copy of each gene was stably integrated into the genome of its respective recombinant strain (Fig. S17). These findings confirm that the observed increases in PenG production can be directly attributed to the single-copy expression of these candidate genes, thereby providing a definitive assessment of their functional impact on PenG biosynthesis.

To further explore their broader regulatory roles in penicillin biosynthesis beyond PenG, we quantified the production of PenV using phenoxyacetic acid (POA) as the side-chain precursor. The results demonstrated that PenV production in the control strain IMP002/G418 was 0.765 ± 0.102 g/L at 120 h. In contrast, the PenV production of OEPc18g04170 and OEPc21g21470 reached 1.232 ± 0.079 g/L and 0.949 ± 0.032 g/L, representing significant increases of 61.08% and 24.05%, respectively (Fig. S18). These findings suggest that the positive effects of these two genes on penicillin biosynthesis are not precursor-specific. Consequently, we speculate that the expression Pc18g04170 and Pc21g21470 may enhance the overall efficiency of the penicillin biosynthetic machinery. This enhancement likely occurs by stimulating peroxisome proliferation or optimizing the intracellular microenvironment, thereby facilitating the more efficient conversion of both PAA and POA into their respective penicillin products.

Conclusion

In summary, this work identified 20 hub genes correlated to PenG production by WGCNA, and overexpression of these 6 hub genes related to transportation, regulation, and N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase activity increased PenG production in IMP002. Our study highlights the high efficiency of the WGCNA method in identifying novel targets associated with secondary metabolites production in the industrial strains.
