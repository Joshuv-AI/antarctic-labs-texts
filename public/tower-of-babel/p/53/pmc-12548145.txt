Integrated multi-omics analysis reveals NOL11 as a novel prognostic biomarker for hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Zhe Li, Yilong Fu, Yanfei Wei, Yijie Zhu, Bin Han, Maosen Guo, Jiaheng Yang, Lixia Xu, Zi Ye, Feng Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common aggressive malignant tumors worldwide with poor clinical outcomes and high mortality rates, highlighting the pressing need to identify reliable biomarkers. Nucleolar protein 11 (NOL11), as an essential element for ribosomal biosynthesis, has been implicated in the development and progression of various diseases. However, the potential role of NOL11 in HCC remains elusive.MethodsThe expression level of NOL11 was investigated by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases, and the correlation between NOL11 and the clinical characteristics was analyzed. Furthermore, spatial-temporal expression patterns of NOL11 were delineated based on single-cell and spatial transcriptomics. Cox regression and ROC analyses were performed to evaluate the prognostic and diagnostic value of NOL11. Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Ontology (GO), and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were conducted to elucidate the functional pathways and potential mechanisms associated with NOL11. The association of NOL11 with immune infiltration was also explored using single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA). The integrated bioinformatics analysis was utilized to identify the drug sensitivity, and further drug validation was employed via molecular docking. Finally, the biological function of NOL11 was validated through in vitro experiments.ResultsThe expression of NOL11 was significantly upregulated in HCC, especially in tumor cells. Elevated NOL11 levels closely correlated with worse clinicopathological features, poor prognosis, and immune infiltration. Functional enrichment analysis revealed NOL11’s potential involvement in multiple cancer-associated signaling pathways, including the cell cycle, DNA replication, and cell metabolism. Furthermore, we found that common chemotherapy drugs, such as gemcitabine, trametinib, and paclitaxel were sensitive to the expression of NOL11 and exhibited a strong molecular combination with NOL11. Lastly, NOL11 knockdown suppressed the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC cells.ConclusionOur findings suggest that NOL11 is a promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for HCC, providing new insights into the treatment decisions for HCC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15113-9.

Introduction

HCC remains a pervasive malignancy with an ominous prognosis, posing a critical threat to global health [1, 2]. Despite therapeutic advances, early diagnosis is hindered by asymptomatic progression and suboptimal biomarkers such as alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), leaving 60–70% of patients diagnosed at advanced stages [3]. Surgical resection and transplantation constitute curative strategies, yet their applicability is restricted by tumor burden, cirrhosis severity, and donor shortages. Emerging therapies-including multikinase inhibitors, immune checkpoint blockers, and combination regimens-have expanded treatment options, though efficacy is challenged by drug resistance, toxicity, and heterogeneous responses [4, 5]. While locoregional interventions bridge palliative and curative intent, long-term survival improvements remain modest. The molecular complexity of HCC demands continued exploration of liquid biopsies, immunogenomic profiling, and targeted drug conjugates. Consequently, continued research into predictive biomarkers and novel therapeutic targets is essential to improve outcomes and address the unmet needs of advanced HCC.

NOL11 is a critical ribosome biogenesis factor that facilitates pre-rRNA transcription and processing during small ribosomal subunit (SSU) maturation [6]. It interacts with hUTP4/Cirhin, with mutations in this complex implicated in North American Indian childhood cirrhosis (NAIC) [7]. Studies have revealed that NOL11 depletion disrupts nucleolar morphology, impairs 18 S rRNA cleavage, and reduces rDNA transcription efficiency. In vertebrates, NOL11 is essential for craniofacial development, as its deficiency in Xenopus triggers p53-mediated apoptosis in cranial neural crest cells, causing skeletal abnormalities [8]. NOL11 is also associated with ribosomopathies, including Treacher Collins syndrome and Diamond-Blackfan anemia [7]. However, the roles of NOL11 in the progression of HCC and the prediction of its prognosis have not yet been studied.

In this study, we characterized NOL11 expression patterns and their clinicopathological and prognostic correlations by integrating RNA sequencing, single-cell sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics analyses. Functional enrichment analyses revealed potential molecular mechanisms, while immune infiltration assessment indicated NOL11’s multifaceted role in the tumor microenvironment and immunotherapy response. Critically, in vitro experiments confirmed the oncogenic function of NOL11. Our findings establish NOL11 as a promising prognostic biomarker and reveal its potential as a therapeutic target for HCC.

Materials and methods

Data collection and analysis

RNA-Seq data of TCGA and Genotypic Tissue Expression (GTEx) in TPM format were downloaded from the UCSC Xena database (http://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). The clinical characteristics and the relevant data of 374 patients with HCC and 50 healthy individuals involved in the study were obtained from TCGA. Subsequent analyses systematically investigated the prognostic significance of NOL11 expression patterns in HCC patients through survival outcome evaluations.

Tumor sample collection

A total of 25 HCC tissue samples and 25 corresponding normal liver tissues were obtained from patients who underwent surgical resection between March 2023 and December 2024 at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, China. Immediately after tissue resection, the samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNAs were isolated from cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio Rad, 1708891). RT-qPCR analyses were subsequently performed using iQ™ SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio Rad 1708880) and Light Cycler 480. The mRNA expression was normalized to GAPDH by using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are shown as follows: GAPDH forward: 5’-ACAACTTTGGTATCGTGGAAGG-3’, GAPDH reverse: 5’-GCCATCACGCCACAGTTTC-3’; NOL11 forward: 5’-ACACTCTTACTTGGACAAGACGA-3’, NOL11 reverse: 5’-GCTGGACGTATTGGTATCAAGG-3’.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using the RIPA buffer (Epizyme, PC102) supplemented with protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The extracted proteins were then separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Merck Millipore). Primary antibodies were applied to the membranes overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies. Target proteins were detected using the Tanon Imaging System. The antibodies used are as follows: anti-GAPDH antibody (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology, 51332), anti-NOL11 antibody (1:2000, Proteintech, 83391-6-RR).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Hepatocellular carcinoma tissues and adjacent tissues were fixed with formalin, embedded in paraffin, and then sectioned into slices. The tissue sections were degummed with xylene and then rehydrated in the ethanol series. The preheated EDTA antigenic retrieval buffer was used for antigen retrieval. Subsequently, block endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% H₂O₂ in methanol/PBS. Next, incubation with 10% goat serum was conducted to reduce non-specific binding. The tissues were then subjected to overnight incubation with appropriately diluted primary antibody at 4 °C, followed by incubation with a horseradish protein-coupled secondary antibody. The staining was visualized using DAB (Servicebio, G1022) and counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, representative pictures were captured under a microscope.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and functional enrichment analysis

Before the functional enrichment analysis, we first performed the identification of DEGs analysis. Based on the results from TCGA, expression of NOL11 in hepatocellular carcinoma patients was divided into high and low-expression groups. We used |log2 fold change (FC)| >1 and adjusted p < 0.05 as thresholds for DEGs by using the limma R package [9]. Gene Ontology (GO) functional analysis (cellular component: CC, molecular function: MF, biological process: BP) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed in the functional enrichment analysis, after which we used the cluster Profiler package in R (version 3.6.2) to analyze the suggested TESeq2 Differential gene expression, and both Benjamini and Hochberg methods were used for the adjustment [10–12].

Immune infiltration analysis and cell–cell communication analysis

Immune infiltration analysis was conducted as described previously [13–15]. First, immunoassay data were based on genetic marker data from 24 immune cells in TCGA, followed by the GSVA package [16]. The infiltration of 24 immune cells in HCC samples was analyzed using the single-sample gene enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). Also, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was utilized to evaluate the relationship between NOL11 and the tumor mutation burden. Finally, we elucidated the intricate interplay between NOL11 expression and immunotherapeutic responsiveness in HCC through systematic interrogation of the Cancer Immunome Database. CellChat R package was employed for assessing intercellular communication by utilizing the standardized gene expression data [17].

Establishment and analysis of the prognostic model

To assess the prognostic relevance of NOL11 as an independent biomarker, Cox proportional hazards models (univariate and multivariate) were implemented with adjustments for key clinical covariates, including age, gender, histologic grade, and pathologic stage. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05. Then, the nomogram was created to predict the clinical outcomes for HCC patients. We also created calibration plots of nomograms to show the difference between their predicted results and the actual results to determine the accuracy of the established model.

Drug efficacy evaluation and molecule docking analysis

Therapeutic response prediction for HCC patients to chemotherapeutic and targeted agents was conducted based on half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values. The chemical and clinical information for small-molecule drugs was obtained from the DrugBank database. Drug screening and sensitivity analyses were performed using integrated R packages (pRRophetic, limma, ggpubr, ggplot2) with a stringent significance threshold (pFilter = 0.0001). Drug screening and sensitivity analyses were implemented through integrated R packages (pRRophetic, limma, ggpubr, ggplot2), employing a stringent significance threshold (pFilter = 0.0001). Compound structures were retrieved from PubChem, while the NOL11 protein’s predicted conformation was acquired from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Molecular docking simulations (AutoDock Vina) were subsequently conducted to characterize potential binding interfaces between NOL11 and therapeutic compounds, with spatial configurations visualized at atomic resolution.

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from the GEO database (GSE125449) were analyzed to delineate NOL11 expression heterogeneity across cellular subpopulations in HCC. Differential gene expression significance was evaluated using the limma package, followed by comprehensive data preprocessing through the Seurat pipeline, which encompassed quality control, normalization, feature selection, and t-SNE-based dimensionality reduction. High-dimensional data transformations, including filtering, sorting, and aggregation, were streamlined via the dplyr package. Cell-type clustering and annotation were subsequently performed by integrating Celldex reference databases with SingleR computational frameworks, enabling precise mapping of NOL11 expression dynamics at single-cell resolution.

Spatial transcription analysis

Sequencing data of spatial transcriptomics were obtained from HCCDBV2.0 (http://lifeome.net:809/#/repository?from=single-gene%26;type=spatial%26;idType=symbol), including normal, adjacent HCC, and HCC tissues. The generated gene-spot matrices were analyzed with the Seurat package in R.

Cell culture and transfection

The lineage procurement and culture conditions of human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines (HEP3B and HEPG2) and normal hepatocytes (NCs) were implemented in accordance with our peer-validated experimental protocols, as systematically detailed in previous publications [1, 18]. For cell transfection, GenePharma (Shanghai) commercially provided the NOL11-targeted siRNA, (si-NOL11#1: GAGAGTATGTTGTTGTACACGATAA, si-NOL11#2: CAGCTCTACTTAATGCAATTCTTCA) with matched negative control (si-NC: GGGAAGTCAAGGTCAGTACG). Using Lipofectamine® 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), we genetically modified HEP3B and HEPG2 cell lines following dual guidance from our established methodology and commercial technical specifications.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

The CCK-8 assay was used to assess the cell proliferation ability. HCC cells were first inoculated into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells/well, and 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to the medium of each well at 0, 12, 24, and 48 h, respectively. Subsequently, the cells were incubated in a 37 °C incubator for 2 h. The absorbance values (optical density, OD) of each well were measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax i3x multifunctional enzyme marker. Finally, the cell proliferation kinetic curve was constructed by analyzing the OD values.

Transwell assay

Cell migration and invasion capacities were quantified through Transwell assays following established protocols [1, 18]. For migration analysis, lower chambers contained 500 µL of medium supplemented with 10%FBS, while upper chambers received 3 × 10⁴ HCC cells suspended in 250 µL serum-free medium. Following 48-hour incubation, non-migrated cells were removed from the upper surface using cotton swabs. Migrant cells were fixed in 95% ethanol, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and quantified microscopically (Olympus, Japan) using standardized imaging protocols. For invasion assays, filters were pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences) before cell plating, with subsequent procedures mirroring migration assays.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using R software (v3.6.3). Differential expression of NOL11 between HCC and adjacent normal tissues was examined via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Associations between NOL11 expression and clinicopathological parameters were evaluated through non-parametric tests (Kruskal-Wallis/Wilcoxon). Survival outcomes stratified by NOL11 expression levels were analyzed with Kaplan-Meier curves. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

Results

High expression of NOL11 in HCC

Initially, the TCGA database was utilized to assess the pan-cancer expression of NOL11. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, NOL11 expression was upregulated in several cancers, including bladder migratory cell carcinoma (BLCA), breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), and HCC. Consistently, analysis of unpaired (Fig. 1B) and paired samples (Fig. 1C) from GTEx and TCGA databases also indicated elevated mRNA levels of NOL11 in HCC. We further validated NOL11 expression in paired HCC and adjacent normal tissues. RT-qPCR confirmed significantly higher NOL11 transcript levels in HCC versus normal tissues (Fig. 1D). Immunohistochemical analysis using both Human Protein Atlas (HPA) datasets and our clinical specimens similarly demonstrated elevated NOL11 protein expression in HCC tissues (Fig. S1A-B). Taken together, these findings suggest that NOL11 is upregulated in HCC.

Association of NOL11 with clinical characteristics of HCC patients

To explore the association between NOL11 levels and various clinicopathological factors, we analyzed 374 RNA-seq data of HCC patients with clinical information from the TCGA cohort. The analysis revealed that NOL11 expression was significantly correlated with age, histologic grade, pathology stage, AFP level, vascular invasion, and adjacent hepatic tissue inflammation but not with gender (Table 1). Similarly, HCC patients with age ≤ 60, higher histologic grading, higher pathologic stage, higher AFP level, vascular invasion, and adjacent hepatic tissue inflammation had higher NOL11 expression (Fig. 2A-F). These results indicate that elevated expression of NOL11 may contribute to the malignant progression, invasive, and metastatic potential of HCC.

The prognostic and diagnostic value of NOL11 expression in HCC

To investigate the potential of NOL11 as a diagnostic and prognostic marker for HCC, we first conducted a Kaplan-Meier analysis to evaluate the survival differences based on NOL11 expression level in HCC. The results showed that patients with high NOL11 expression exhibited worse overall survival (OS) than those with low NOL11 level (Fig. 3A). A significant positive association was observed between the risk score and patient mortality in HCC (Fig. 3B). Subsequently, we evaluated the diagnostic potential of NOL11 in HCC patients by ROC curve analysis, which showed that NOL11 has a good diagnostic value for HCC with the AUC was 0.919 (95% CI = 0.889–0.949) (Fig. 3C).

Further univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed that NOL11 upregulation was an independent prognostic factor in HCC (Fig. S2A-B). Then, a nomogram model incorporating clinical characteristics was established to predict the rates of OS for patients with HCC at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig. 3D). In addition, the calibration curves showed a great agreement between the predicted survival rates and actual outcomes (Fig. 3E).

Identification of DEGs and functional enrichment analysis

To explore the functional implications of NOL11 expression in HCC, we divided the HCC patients into high- and low-expression groups. Based on |log2 FC| > 1 and adjusted p < 0.05, 1617 significantly upregulated genes and 1037 significantly downregulated genes were identified (Fig. 4A). The relative expression levels of the top 60 DEGs between the two groups were depicted in the heat map (Fig. 4B). Then, we conducted functional enrichment analysis to determine the underlying mechanism of NOL11 involved in HCC. KEGG analysis of differentially expressed genes revealed that the upregulated genes in NOL11 high-expression group were associated with cell cycle- and homologous recombination-related pathway (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis revealed that the upregulated genes in NOL11 high-expression group were predominantly enriched in biological processes such as organelle fission, nuclear division, chromosome segregation and microtubule motor activity, which typically occurred during DNA replication and cell mitosis (Fig. 4D). Additionally, GSEA indicated that fatty acid metabolism and autophagy regulation were differentially enriched in NOL11 high-expression group (Fig. 4E). These data collectively implied that NOL11 may influence HCC progression through cell cycle and cell metabolism regulation.

The correlation between NOL11 expression and immune infiltration

Immune cell infiltration exerted a dualistic influence on the initiation, progression, and treatment of HCC [18]. The composition and functional states of infiltrating immune cells were crucial determinants, as they directly modulated the immunosuppressive properties of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and ultimately dictated the efficacy of anti-cancer therapies [19, 20]. ssGSEA was utilized to analyze the correlation between NOL11 expression and the immune cell infiltration level. The results showed that the infiltration levels of Th2 cells, T helper cells, and Tcm were positively correlated with NOL11 expression, while the infiltration levels of cytotoxic cells, DC cells, T cells, pDCs, B cells, etc. were negatively correlated with NOL11 expression (Fig. 5A-B). Given the profound impact of NOL11 on immune cell infiltration, we conducted an analysis within the single-cell dataset to investigate the cellular crosstalk between malignant cells and other cell subtypes in the microenvironment. Cell–cell communication analysis revealed that tumor cells establish intensive communication networks within the TME, primarily functioning as senders to immune subsets (T cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), B cells). Notably, bidirectional signaling axes existed between tumor cells and both cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and tumor-associated endothelial cells (TECs), where tumor cells acted as both senders and receivers (Fig. S3A-B).

In addition to immune cells, immune checkpoints are also regarded as a crucial component of immunotherapy. The correlation analysis of immune checkpoints revealed that NOL11 exhibited significant positive associations with CD276 and TNFSF4, while demonstrating inverse correlations with IDO2 and TMIGD2 (Fig. 5C). Further analysis revealed that tumor mutation burden was significantly positively correlated with the expression of NOL11 (Fig. 5D). Subsequently, the IPS score was employed for immune response prediction. We categorized HCC patients into 4 groups based on their PD1 and CTLA4 expression. The results showed that all 4 groups of patients with high NOL11 expression had lower IPS scores, which indicated a poorer response to immunotherapy (Fig. 5E-H). The above results suggest that NOL11 overexpression is associated with the immunosuppressive environment and insensitivity to immunotherapy in HCC.

Drug sensitivity analysis in the NOL11 low- and high-expression groups

Next, we evaluated the sensitivity differences in common chemotherapy drugs between HCC patients with high and low expression of NOL11 and found that the IC50 values of the low-expression group to gemcitabine and paclitaxel were significantly higher (Fig. 6A and C), indicating that the high-expression group was more sensitive to these drugs. While, the IC50 values of trametinib were higher in the high-expression group (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, molecular docking experiments were performed through AutoDock to predict the binding sites of gemcitabine (Fig. 6D), trametinib (Fig. 6E), and paclitaxel (Fig. 6F) with NOL11. The results showed that these drugs had strong molecular combination with NOL11, and the docking fractions were − 6.8 kcal/mol (Fig. 6G), −6.5 kcal/mol (Fig. 6H), and − 8.8 kcal/mol (Fig. 6I), respectively. In summary, our findings are conducive to providing promising individualized treatment strategies for HCC patients.

Investigating the expression of NOL11 based on spatial and single-cell transcriptomics

To gain a deeper understanding of the NOL11 expression in HCC, we analyzed the single-cell data GSE125449 from the GEO database. These cells were categorized into 8 cell types based on their markers, including B cells, CAFs, hepatic progenitor cell-like cells (HCC-like), HCC cells, T cells, TAMs, TECs, and unclassified cells (Fig. 7A). Based on the clustering results, we observed that NOL11 was widely expressed in a variety of cells in the tumor microenvironment, mainly in HCC cells (Fig. 7B-C). We further investigated spatial transcription data of HCC from HCCDBV2.0, including normal, adjacent HCC and HCC tissues (Fig. 7D). Based on the unbiased clustering and spot features, spots in tissues were classified as tumor cells, stromal cells, normal cells and immune cells (Fig. 7E). Notably, the analysis of spatial transcriptomics showed a pronounced increase in NOL11 levels in the tumor cells, suggesting a distinct spatial distribution of the gene in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 7F).

NOL11 knockdown inhibited the proliferation, migration and invasion of HCC cells

To investigate the role of NOL11 in HCC, we examined its expression level in HCC cell lines. RT-qPCR and Western blot revealed a significantly increased expression of NOL11 in both HEP3B and HEPG2 (Fig. 8A and S4A). Then, we transfected siRNA to knock down NOL11 in HEP3B (Fig. 8B and S4B) and HEPG2 (Fig. 8C and S4C). The CCK-8 assay showed that NOL11 knockdown remarkably suppressed the proliferative ability in HEP3B (Fig. 8D) and HEPG2 after 48 h (Fig. 8E). Next, the transwell assay indicated that the migration and invasion ability of HEP3B and HEPG2 were significantly attenuated after NOL11 knockdown (Fig. 8F-I). Collectively, these findings confirmed that NOL11 exerts an oncogenic role in HCC.

Discussion

Liver cancer is the sixth most common site of primary cancer in humans globally and one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths, with a 5-year survival rate below 20% [21, 22], of which HCC accounts for 90% [23]. Despite remarkable advances in novel therapies such as targeted molecular therapy and immunotherapies in recent years, the efficacy and prognosis for HCC patients remain worrying [24–27], necessitating the continued search for novel and effective therapeutic targets. In this study, through comprehensive bioinformatics analysis, we revealed that NOL11 is specifically upregulated in HCC and strongly associated with poor prognosis. ROC curve and nomogram models demonstrated the excellent diagnostic and prognostic value of NOL11. Furthermore, in vitro experiment confirmed the functional role of NOL11 in promoting HCC malignant progression. Our research provides novel insights into the role of NOL11 in HCC pathogenesis and offers new directions for targeted therapies of HCC.

As a metazoan-specific nucleolar protein [28], NOL11 is necessary for maintaining the nucleolus number and the transcription and processing of pre-rRNA [7]. Besides, NOL11 has crucial functions in the development of cranial neural crest by regulating the activation of nucleolus stress response and p53 stabilization [8]. Recent studies of NOL11 function have shown the correlation with the progression of various diseases, such as North American Indian childhood cirrhosis [29] and asthma with acute exacerbation [30]. In our research, we reported that NOL11 expression was significantly increased in HCC, served as an independent poor prognostic factor, which was significantly associated with adverse clinicopathologic features including higher pathological stage and histologic grade. In addition, single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomic analysis further indicated the specific enrichment of NOL11 in HCC tumor cells. More importantly, functional experiments demonstrated that NOL11 promoted cell proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC. The above results fully confirmed the potential of NOL11 as both an oncogenic driver and a diagnostic/prognostic biomarker in HCC.

Through bioinformatics analysis and experimental verification, our study revealed that NOL11 promoted the growth and metastasis of HCC. To investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms of NOL11 in HCC tumorigenesis, we performed functional enrichment analysis on NOL11-related differentially expressed genes, which revealed that NOL11 exerted a tumor-promoting function by mediating various oncogenic signaling pathways such as DNA replication, mitosis, and cell cycle regulation. Previous studies have shown that the depletion of NOL11 delayed the entry into the mitotic phase by increasing inhibitory phosphorylation of cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (Cdk1), accompanied by nucleolar disruption during interphase [31]. Another study consistently indicated that the NWC protein complex formed by NOL11 was an essential component to ensure mitosis, by regulating kinetochores and centromeres [6]. In addition, the malignant progression and drug sensitivity of HCC have been widely reported to be related to the uncontrolled cell cycle checkpoints and abnormal mitotic regulatory proteins [32]. Recent research demonstrated that TTC36 promoted the proliferation and cell cycle of HCC cells owing to the accumulation of c-Myc protein [33]. Additionally, Li et al. found that Cucurbitacin B suppressed hepatocellular carcinoma progression through inducing deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage-dependent cell cycle arrest [34]. All in all, we provide compelling evidences to speculate that NOL11 participates in the malignant progression of HCC by regulating the cell cycle process, but the precise molecular mechanisms remain to be further verified.

The tumor immune microenvironment is tightly correlated with the progression of HCC [35]. The rise of immunotherapy has spurred extensive research in recent years, significantly advancing our understanding of the mechanisms underlying the immune microenvironment in HCC. Our study confirmed the impact of NOL11 on the tumor immune microenvironment of HCC. Compared with the NOL11 low-expression group, the enrichment score of Th2 cells was significantly higher in the NOL11 high-expression group, while the enrichment score of cytotoxic cells and DCs was significantly lower. Th2 cells are an important subgroup of CD4⁺ helper T cells that exert pro-tumor effects by inhibiting the activity of CD8⁺ T cells and promoting the expansion of regulatory T cells [36]. Previous studies have found that the IL-4 and IL-13 secreted by Th2 cells can directly inhibit the proliferation and function of cytotoxic T cells and reduce their killing ability against tumor cells, which can stimulate tumor growth and promote the metastasis of HCC [37–39]. DCs are mainly responsible for antigen presentation to activate T cells in the immune system. Additionally, type I interferons (IFN-I) produced by plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs) have been shown to exert promising antitumor activity [40]. In HCC microenvironment, tumor cells suppress the maturation and migration of DCs through the secretion of IL-10, VEGF, and other cytokines, resulting in functional impairment. Cytotoxic cells play a direct killing role in anti-tumor immunity, which are usually inhibited in HCC, thereby causing dysfunction and leading to the deterioration of HCC [25]. Our cell-cell communication analysis demonstrated that tumor cells consistently engaged in interactions with diverse cell populations, including TAMs with tumor-promoting characteristics. Notably, these interactions also involved CAFs and TECs, which formed complex communication networks with other cells in the microenvironment via receptor-ligand interactions, playing significant roles in inflammation, immune infiltration, tumorigenesis, and treatment resistance [41–46]. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that NOL11 promotes HCC progression by modulating the tumor microenvironment.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the sample data were obtained exclusively from public databases, and future studies should establish a clinical HCC cohort to further validate these results. In addition, the tumor-promoting function of NOL11 has been verified only through in vitro experiments, and more in vivo experiments are needed to thoroughly explore the molecular mechanisms of NOL11 in HCC.

Conclusion

Collectively, we demonstrated that NOL11, as an aberrantly upregulated oncoprotein, is closely associated with aggressive clinicopathological features, poor prognosis, and dysregulated immune infiltration in HCC. Functionally, it drives tumor progression by disrupting cell cycle regulation and fostering an immunosuppressive TME. Critically, NOL11 expression predicts sensitivity to common chemotherapeutic agents and demonstrates strong molecular interactions with them, highlighting its utility as a treatment response biomarker. Our findings position NOL11 as both a valuable prognostic indicator and a promising therapeutic target, with inhibition strategies offering potential to suppress tumor growth, reverse immunosuppression, and synergize with conventional chemotherapies to improve HCC outcomes.
