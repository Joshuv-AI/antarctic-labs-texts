Adolescent social isolation associated changes in ethanol-induced dopamine regulation in the ventral pallidum
Authors: Gavin J. Vaughan, Makenzie R. Lehr, Gina M. Magardino, Abigail M. Kelley, Michelle A. Chan, Madison C. Heitkamp, Jordan T. Yorgason, Anushree N. Karkhanis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Adolescent social isolation (aSI) has been demonstrated to increase anxiety-like behavior and ethanol consumption. The ventral pallidum (VP), a basal ganglia structure that receives dopaminergic projections from the VTA, contributes to reward and aversion processing, and is highly responsive to psychosocial stressors. In our study, we sought to characterize the effects of aSI on maladaptive behavioral phenotypes such as anxiety-like behavior, ethanol consumption, and aversion-resistant ethanol-intake as measured by the consumption of ethanol (20 %, v/v) adulterated with the bitterant quinine (0, 3, 10, 30, 100 mg/l). We further used ex vivo fast scan cyclic voltammetry (FSCV) to probe dopamine release kinetics in the VP in response to aSI following both tonic and phasic stimulation. Finally, we measured the impact of an acute application of ethanol (80 mM, 150 mM) on dopamine release kinetics in the VP following both tonic and phasic stimulation. Our results indicated that aSI potentiates anxiety-like behavior and ethanol preference, but does not promote aversion-resistant ethanol drinking. While pallidal dopamine release kinetics were insensitive to aSI at baseline, ethanol perfused over slices containing the VP modulated tonic dopamine release in all rats, albeit at a lower magnitude in aSI rats. Furthermore, ethanol modulated phasic dopamine release in a stress-dependent and sex-specific manner. Thus, dopamine in the VP may contribute to the maladaptive consumption of ethanol following aSI, though the exact mechanism may be different between male and female rats.

Introduction

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) currently affects over 25 million American adults and approximately one million American adolescents [1], and the frequency of alcohol use has risen significantly as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic [2,3]. In the wake of pandemic-era social distancing and lockdown protocols, children and adolescents have reported significantly increased feelings of isolation and loneliness [4–6]. Exposure to such adversity or neglect during adolescence promotes affective disorders including anxiety and depression, and increases the propensity of developing AUD [7] where excessive consumption of alcohol occurs despite its negative health impacts. We do not fully understand the basic neurobiology contributing to the development of AUD. In this study we use a translationally relevant model with high face validity of AUD vulnerability [8–10] and propose a neural target that potentially contributes to aversion-resistant consumption of alcohol.

Similar to humans, adolescent social isolation (aSI) in rodents, augments anxiety-like behaviors [8,9,11,12], as well as ethanol preference and consumption [8,11,13–16]. The impact of aSI is age-dependent, as adult social isolation does not produce the same robust increase in ethanol-consumption [14] or anxiety-like behavior [17].

The maladaptive behavioral outcomes in this model are likely driven by dysregulation of the typical neurodevelopmental trajectory. For example, aSI produces structural changes in the prefrontal cortex, basolateral amygdala and the nucleus accumbens (NAc; [18,19]), and downregulates glutamate transmission [20] while potentiating dopamine transmission [14,17,21] in the NAc. As with behavior, social isolation-associated facilitation of NAc dopamine transmission occurs specifically after social isolation in adolescence, but not in adulthood [17]. While these regions are critical in shaping affect-related behaviors, including negative-affect associated ethanol consumption, the ventral pallidum (VP), a predominantly GABAergic nucleus of the basal ganglia [22], may be a crucial contributor in promoting aversion-resistant ethanol consumption given its role in modulating the value of rewarding and aversive stimuli [23–27]. The VP is also highly responsive to psychosocial stress, as social defeat stress promotes activity of neurons in this region [28]. Furthermore, cycles of repeated methamphetamine exposure and withdrawal result in facilitation of evoked dopamine release selectively in the VP [29], and intra-ventral tegmental area (VTA) microinjection of ethanol increases VTA dopaminergic output to the VP [30]. Dopamine in the VP plays a neuromodulatory role by inhibiting and exciting pallidal neural subpopulations [31,32] , ultimately shaping behavioral outcomes. Particularly, manipulation of the neural activity in VP substantially alters responses to aversive stimuli. For example, the inactivation of VP GABA neurons reduces the motivation to seek reinforcement under risky conditions [24], and VP glutamate neurons are vital to promote taste aversion to the bitterant lithium chloride [33]. Together, these studies highlight the importance of understanding pallidal dopamine dynamics at the intersection of aSI stress and ethanol.

Previous literature examining the impact of stress on ethanol-mediated changes in dopamine has focused on the NAc. There is overall consensus that acute ethanol elevates dopamine levels [34–36]; however, tissue content analysis reveals that this effect is blunted following chronic or repeated stress exposure [37]. In vivo microdialysis assessment demonstrates an aSI-associated potentiation in ethanol-induced elevation in dopamine levels in the NAc [9,14]. Interestingly, in vivo voltammetric assessments show a biphasic effect of ethanol on dopamine, whereby striatal dopamine levels are elevated in defeated mice in a social defeat stress model following low doses and decreased following high doses compared to their undefeated and dominant age-matched controls [38]. To our knowledge, no studies have examined the impact of ethanol on dopamine in the VP following chronic stress exposure.

In this study, we first investigated the impact of aSI on anxiety-like behavior, ethanol consumption, and aversion-resistant ethanol drinking to characterize maladaptive phenotypes associated with adolescent psychosocial stress. We then used ex vivo fast scan cyclic voltammetry (FSCV) to measure dopamine release and quantify release kinetics in the VP before and after the superfusion of ethanol acutely to assess the potential contributions of pallidal dopamine in promoting escalated ethanol intake and aversion-resistant ethanol consumption. We focus on the release kinetics of dopamine, as ethanol-mediated changes in dopamine in terminal regions are driven by alterations in release and not uptake through the dopamine transporter [36,39]. Overall, our results confirm that aSI produces anxiety-like behavior and promotes greater ethanol consumption and higher preference for ethanol, but does not promote aversion-resistant ethanol consumption. Furthermore, our study is the first to show that adolescent psychosocial stress exposure alters the effects of ethanol on dopamine in the VP.

Methods

Subjects and housing manipulation

Male and female Long-Evans rats were bred at Binghamton University using breeders procured from Envigo (California, USA). Rats were weaned at postnatal day (PD) 21, and same-sex non-litter cagemates were housed in groups of three to four. At PD 28, rats were randomly assigned to either be socially isolated (aSI; 20.3 cm X 26.7 cm cages) or remain in groups of three to four (aGH; 33 cm X 50.7 cm cages). Rats were maintained on a 12 hr light-dark cycle with lights on at 1500. Standard rat chow (LabDiet 5L0D - PicoLab Laboratory Rodent Diet, ScottPharma Solutions, Marlborough, MA) and access to tap water was provided to the animals ad libitum. All procedures were conducted in compliance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Binghamton University.

Following the six-week aSI housing paradigm, all animals were tested on one of three measures of anxiety-like behavior (PD 70–75). Animals examined on the elevated plus maze or the successive alleys test were subsequently used for either ethanol drinking or ex vivo fast-scan cyclic voltammetry experiments (described below).

Anxiety-like behavior assays

Open field assay

A 122 cm × 122 cm open-field assay (Fig. 1A) was used to quantify both overall locomotion and anxiety-like behaviors (n = 8 per group). Rats were habituated to the behavior room for 30 min prior to open-field testing. At the end of the habituation period, rats were placed into the center of the apparatus and allowed to freely roam for 30 min while being recorded. The recorded video was analyzed post hoc using Anymaze (version 6.33, Stoelting). Time spent in the innermost zone (30 cm × 30 cm) was recorded as a measure of anxiety-like behavior, where lower inner-zone time equated to greater anxiety-like behavior. Furthermore, latency to exit the inner-zone and latency to re-enter the inner-zone were also recorded as measures of anxiety-like behavior. Finally, the total distance travelled during the 30-minute test period was measured to assess overall locomotor behavior.

Elevated plus maze

The elevated plus maze (EPM; arms: 50.8 cm × 11.5 cm; center zone: 12.7 cm × 11.5 cm; Fig. 2A bottom) was used to quantify anxiety-like behavior (n = 21 (aGH-female); 20 (aSI-female); 20 (aGH-male); 19 (aSI-male)). Rats were habituated to the behavior room for 30 min prior to EPM testing. At the termination of the habituation period, rats were placed into the center zone of the EPM at the beginning of testing and allowed to freely roam the maze. The activity of the rats on the EPM was recorded for 300 s. A lamp (~250 lx) was placed above the open-arms. Videos were recorded and scored for total time spent in the open-arm of the maze, as well as the total number of entries into the open- and closed-arms, where time spent in the open-arms corresponded to lower anxiety-like behavior, and total number of entries were indicative of overall locomotion.

Successive alleys test

The successive alleys test (SAT; Fig. 2A top) is a behavioral assay for measuring anxiety-like behavior. The apparatus is open on top and consists of four zones (45.7 cm length per zone) in a gradient [40]. Specifically, Zone 1 is least anxiogenic zone, resembling the closed-arm of the EPM, and Zone 4 is the most anxiogenic, resembling the open-arm of the EPM. A lamp (~250 lx) was placed above Zone 4. Zones 2 and 3 act as transitional zones between Zones 1 and 4, providing a more nuanced measure of anxiety-like behavior compared to the EPM. Rats (n = 14 (aGH-female); 9 (aSI-female); 14 (aGH-male); 11 (aSI-male)) were habituated to the behavior room for 30 min prior to behavioral testing. Following the habituation period, rats were placed in Zone 1 and allowed to freely roam the apparatus for 300 s. Sessions were video recorded and scored for total time spent in each zone and latency to enter each zone.

Ethanol consumption

We used an intermittent access, two-bottle choice, homecage drinking paradigm. All rats were single-housed for the duration of the paradigm. Beginning two hours into the light cycle (1700), rats (n = 19 per group) were given a bottle of 20 % ethanol diluted with tap water (v/v; 190 proof; Science Store, Binghamton University) and a bottle of tap water only, on three alternate days per week for 24 h each session for eight weeks. Bottles were weighed before placed on the cages, then after 30 min, and finally at the end of 24 h. Major outcomes included overall ethanol consumption, and preference for ethanol compared to tap water, expressed as a percentage of ethanol consumed compared to total fluid volume consumed. Following this eight-week period, drinking sessions continued as described, however during the second session per week the 20 % ethanol solution was mixed with the bitterant quinine to measure aversion-resistant drinking behavior. One concentration of quinine (0, 3, 10, 30, 100 mg/l) was added in ascending order during the second session every week over the next five weeks. Major outcomes included quinine-adulterated ethanol consumption and preference.

Ex vivo fast scan cyclic voltammetry

Animals were euthanized using rapid decapitation under anesthesia (isoflurane) and brains were suspended in ice cold oxygenated (95 % O2/5 % CO2) artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF; 126 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 2.4 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM MgCl2, 25 mM NaHCO3, 11 mM glucose, 0.4 mM l-ascorbic acid). A vibrating tissue slicer (Leica VT1000S, Vashaw Scientific, Norcross, GA) was used to collect 300 μm thick coronal slices containing the VP [41]. These slices were then transferred to a recording chamber and immersed in oxygenated aCSF cycled at the rate of 1 ml/min and maintained at 32 °C throughout collections. Tonic dopamine release was evoked using a single-pulse (4.0 ms, 350 μA, monophasic) electrical stimulation through a bipolar stimulating electrode (8IMS3033SPCE, Plastics One, Roanoke, VA, USA) placed on the surface of the tissue. Interstimulus interval was maintained at 600 s. A carbon fiber electrode was positioned approximately 100 μm below the surface of the tissue and 100 μm away from the stimulating electrode to detect dopamine. A triangular scanning waveform (−0.4 to +1.2 and back to −0.4 V, Ag. vs AgCl at the rate of 400 V/s) was applied through a carbon fiber recording electrode (100–200 μm in length) to measure evoked release of dopamine. For all experiments, baseline signals were collected for at least 60 min and until signals were stable (< 10 % change between three consecutive collections, with no trend in change towards either an increase or decrease in signal). Once stable baselines were established, dopamine release was measured following increasing phasic stimulation frequencies (5 pulses at 5, 10, 20, and 100 Hz). Both tonic and phasic experiments were repeated as described after the superfusion of ethanol (80 and 150 mM cumulative; 200 proof, 100 %; Science Store, Binghamton University). After experimental collections concluded, recording electrodes were calibrated by measuring their response to 3 μM dopamine suspended in aCSF.

All voltammetry data were processed in Demon Voltammetry Analysis software [42]. Parameters for analysis included the peak height of dopamine release (maximal release, μM) and the rate of dopamine release (upward velocity; μM/s) in aSI and aGH rats (n = 11 (aGH-female); 9 (aSI-female); 8 (aGH-male); 8 (aSI-male)), described previously [42,43]. Dopamine release parameters following phasic stimulations were expressed as a percentage of the tonic stimulation value to normalize data and account for differences existing at baseline in aSI and aGH rats (n = 9 (aGH-female); 9 (aSI-female); 8 (aGH-male); 8 (aSI--male)). Dopamine release following tonic stimulation (tonic release) and the application of 80 mM and 150 mM ethanol in aSI and aGH rats (n = 6 (aGH-female); 6 (aSI-female); 7 (aGH-male); 5 (aSI-male)) were expressed as a difference score. Difference score was calculated by subtracting the baseline value from the value in presence of ethanol following tonic stimulation prior to the acute application of ethanol ([Ethanol-DA release - baseline DA release] or [Ethanol-rate of DA release - baseline rate of DA release]). Finally, dopamine release parameters following phasic stimulation (phasic release) and the application 80 mM and 150 mM ethanol were expressed as a percentage of the baseline value at 0 mM ethanol under matched conditions in aSI and aGH rats (n = 6 (aGH-female); 6 (aSI-female); 6 (aGH-male); 5 (aSI-male).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed and figures were created using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA). Behavioral tests of anxiety-like behavior were analyzed using two-way and three-way ANOVA testing, followed by Bonferroni post-hoc analysis for multiple comparisons. Factors included for analysis were sex and housing condition for both the EPM and the open-field assay. Factors included for analysis for the SAT included sex, housing condition, and zone. Sex, housing condition, and time or quinine concentration were included as factors for analysis for standard and aversion-resistant ethanol consumption, respectively. FSCV data were also analyzed using two-way and three-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post-hoc analysis to detect between-group differences for baseline and ethanol-mediated tonic dopamine measures. Sex and housing condition factors were used for the two-way ANOVA comparison for baseline measures following tonic stimulation. Sex, housing condition, and stimulation frequency were included as factors for three-way ANOVA comparing baseline measures following phasic stimulation. Sex, housing condition, and ethanol concentration were included as factors for three-way ANOVA comparing tonic dopamine kinetics following superfusion of ethanol. Stimulation frequency and ethanol concentration were included as factors for a two-way ANOVA comparison of ethanol-mediated changes in phasic dopamine release kinetics. Due to Graphpad Prism (v. 10.0.0) inability to control for multiple comparisons while analyzing for ANOVA three-way interactions, we used R (R v. 4.4.3) to conduct Bonferroni post-hoc testing for significant three-way interactions for the 30-minute ethanol consumption, 30-minute ethanol preference, 24-hour ethanol consumption, and 24-hour water consumption assessments. Finally, because of similar Graphpad limitations in multiple comparison analyses, we again used R (R version 4.4.3) to assess differences in ethanol-mediated phasic dopamine release and rate of release.

Results

Adolescent social isolation promotes anxiety-like behavior: assessment using various assays

Open field

Following the housing manipulation, we first used the open-field assay (Fig. 1A) to measure anxiety-like behavior in aGH and aSI rats (Fig. 1B–E). At the beginning of the assay, the animals were placed in the inner-zone.

Latency to exit the inner-zone (Fig. 1B):

A two-way between-subjects ANOVA (housing × sex) revealed an interaction between housing and sex [F (1, 28) = 16.29, p = 0.0004], with significant main effects of housing [F (1, 28) = 19.59, p = 0.0001] and sex [F (1, 28) = 44.77, p < 0.0001]. These data indicate that aSI rats exited the inner-zone with a lower latency than aGH rats and that male rats exhibited a significantly lower latency to exit the inner zone compared to female rats. Subsequent Bonferroni post-hoc for multiple comparisons showed significantly lower latencies to exit the inner zone in the aSI compared to aGH female group (p < 0.0001), and the aGH male rat compared to aGH female rat group (p < 0.0001).

Latency to re-enter the inner-zone (Fig. 1C):

A two-way between-subjects ANOVA (housing × sex) revealed a significant housing × sex interaction [F (1, 28) = 4.526, p = 0.0423] and a significant main effect of housing [F (1, 28) = 4.591, p = 0.0410]. Bonferroni post-hoc for multiple comparisons indicated that augmented latency effect was predominantly driven by aSI compared to aGH male rats (p = 0.0107).

Total time spent in the inner-zone ( % of total time; Fig. 1D):

A two-way between-subjects ANOVA (housing × sex) indicated a significant main effect of sex [F (1, 28) = 36.13, p < 0.0001], whereby female rats spent significantly more time in the inner-zone compared to male rats; but no significant effect of housing or interaction between the two factors.

Total distance traveled (Fig. 1E):

A two-way between-subjects ANOVA (housing × sex) comparing total distance traveled as a measure of overall locomotion revealed a significant main effect of sex [F (1, 28) = 6.386, p = 0.0174] indicating that female rats exhibited greater overall locomotion compared to male rats, but no significant effect of housing or interaction between the two factors.

Overall, while these data do not show a difference in locomotion between the two housing conditions, it is likely that aSI rats exhibit greater anxiety-like behavior based on lower latencies to exit the inner-zone (aSI female rats) and greater latencies to re-enter the inner-zone (aSI male rats). Detailed statistics are reported in Supplemental Table 1.

Elevated plus maze

We used the EPM as a second assessment to measure anxiety-like behavior in male and female, aGH and aSI rats (Fig. 2A–C).

Open arm time (Fig. 2B):

The time spent in the open arms was used to assess anxiety-like behavior. A two-way between-subjects ANOVA (housing × sex) revealed a main effect of housing [F (1, 76) = 9.837, p = 0.0024], whereby aSI rats spent less time on the open arm compared to aGH rats, but no effect of sex or interaction between the two factors.

Total arm entries (Fig. 2C):

We used total number of arm entries to compare overall locomotion between the groups. A two-way between-subjects ANOVA (housing × sex) revealed a main effect of sex [F (1, 76) = 9.700, p = 0.0026], with female rats entering the arms a greater number of times than male rats. There was no main effect of housing, or interaction between the two factors.

Overall, aSI rats demonstrated an anxiety-like phenotype exhibited by less time spent in the open arms, without differences in overall locomotion. Detailed statistics are reported in Supplemental Table 2.

Successive alleys test

As a third assessment of anxiety-like behavior, we used the SAT (Fig. 2A, d–E) and measured the latency to exit Zone 1, the most anxiolytic area of the apparatus.

Latency to exit Zone 1 (Fig. 2D):

A two-way between-subjects ANOVA (housing × sex) revealed a main effect of housing [F (1, 40) = 4.242, p = 0.0460] indicating that aSI rats took significantly longer to exit Zone 1 compared to aGH rats, with no effect of sex, or interaction between the two factors.

Total zone time (Fig. 2E):

A three-way mixed ANOVA (housing × sex × zone) revealed a significant housing × zone interaction [F (3, 123) = 5.952, p = 0.0008], a sex × zone interaction [F (3, 123) = 12.53, p < 0.0001], and a main effect of zone [F (1.20, 49.21) = 387.6, p < 0.0001]. No main effect of housing, sex, or any other interactions were observed. Bonferroni post-hoc for multiple comparisons indicated that aSI rats spent significantly more time in Zone 1 compared to aGH rats (p = 0.0192; data collapsed across sex). Furthermore, aGH rats spent significantly more time in Zone 2 (p = 0.0332) and Zone 4 (p = 0.0468) compared to aSI rats (p = 0.0468). Finally, female rats spent significantly more time in Zone 1 (p = 0.0008) and significantly less time in Zone 2 (p = 0.0005) and Zone 3 (p = 0.0113) compared to male rats (collapsed across housing condition).

Together, these data suggest that aSI produces anxiety-like phenotype in both male and female rats, and overall female rats exhibit greater anxiety-like behaviors compared to male rats as measured by the SAT. Detailed statistics are reported in Supplemental Table 3.

Adolescent social isolation potentiates ethanol consumption and preference, but does not promote aversion-resistant drinking

Male and female aGH and aSI rats were given access to 20 % ethanol and tap water in an intermittent access two-bottle choice paradigm for 24 h per session. We measured ethanol and water consumption at the 30-minute and 24-hour time points. After eight weeks of baseline ethanol consumption, we added quinine (increasing concentrations - 3–300 mg/L) during one session per week for the next five weeks to assess aversion-resistant ethanol consumption. We will first discuss results for 30-minute ethanol self-administration, followed by 24 h of intake.

Intermittent access, two bottle choice (ethanol and water) at the 30-minute and 24-hour timepoint

30-minute Consumption (Fig. 3A):

A three-way mixed ANOVA (week × housing × sex) assessing the impact of aSI on weekly average ethanol consumption in the first 30 min after bottles were introduced over an eight-week period revealed a significant week × housing × sex interaction [F (7, 504) = 2.561, p = 0.0134], a week × sex interaction [F (7, 504) = 2.488, p = 0.0161], and a main effect of week [F (7, 504) = 8.423, p < 0.0001]. Bonferroni post-hoc for multiple comparisons indicated that aSI females consumed significantly more ethanol compared to aGH females during Week 6 (p = 0.0152).

24-hour Consumption (Fig. 3B):

A three-way mixed ANOVA (week × housing × sex) assessing the impact of aSI on weekly average ethanol consumption in 24 h over an eight-week period revealed a significant week × housing × sex interaction [F [7], 504 = 2.414, p = 0.0194]. Bonferroni post-hoc for multiple comparisons indicated that aSI females consumed significantly more ethanol compared to aGH females during Week 6 (p = 0.0095).

30-minute Preference (Fig. 3C):

We further quantified overall preference for 20 % ethanol over tap water in aGH and aSI male and female rats during the first 30 min. A three-way mixed ANOVA (week × housing × sex) revealed a significant week × housing × sex interaction [F (7, 504) = 2.219, p = 0.0315], with a main effect of housing [F (1, 72) = 4.417, p = 0.0391] and week [F (7, 504) = 2.545, p = 0.0139]. Bonferroni post-hoc analysis indicated that male aSI rats exhibited greater preference for ethanol during weeks 1 (p = 0.0060), 2 (p = 0.0268), 4 (p = 0.0243), 6 (p = 0.0197), 7 (p = 0.0083), and 8 (p = 0.046).

24-hour Preference (Fig. 3D):

We further assessed overall preference for 20 % ethanol over tap water in aGH and aSI male and female rats. A three-way mixed ANOVA (week × housing × sex) revealed a significant main effect of week [F (7, 504) = 4.536, p < 0.0001] and sex [F (1, 72) = 5.620, p = 0.0204], and a statistical trend towards a main effect of housing [F (1, 72) = 3.898, p = 0.0522], with no significant interactions observed.

Overall, these data suggest that while aSI marginally augmented ethanol consumption in a time (week)- and sex-dependent manner, ethanol preference was significantly greater in aSI rats compared to aGH rats. This effect varied by time and was largely driven by male rats. Detailed statistics are reported in Supplemental Table 4.

Two-bottle choice aversion-resistant ethanol intake over 30 min and 24-hours

To quantify aversion resistant drinking behavior, we added scaling concentrations of quinine (0, 3, 10, 30, 100 mg/L) systematically to the 20 % ethanol solution and measured consumption of ethanol+quinine and preference for the solutions over five weeks during the first 30 min of the drinking session (Fig. 3E, G) and over the 24 h (Fig. 3F, H).

30-minute Consumption (Fig. 3E):

A three-way mixed ANOVA (concentration × housing × sex) indicated significant main effects of quinine concentration [F (5, 346) = 42.73, p < 0.0001] and housing [F (1, 72) = 8.213, p = 0.0054]. It is possible that the housing effect was likely driven primarily by differences in baseline consumption.

24-hour Consumption (Fig. 3F):

A three-way mixed ANOVA (concentration × housing × sex) indicated a significant housing × concentration interaction [F (5, 346) = 2.356, p = 0.0402], as well as main effects of quinine concentration [F (5, 346) = 75.45, p < 0.0001] and housing [F (1, 72) = 5.054, p = 0.0276]. Rats exposed to aSI consumed significantly more quinine-adulterated ethanol compared to rats exposed to aGH, however this effect was likely driven by differences in consumption at baseline; Bonferroni post-hoc analysis indicated significant differences at the 0 concentration in both male and female aSI groups (p = 0.0200).

30-minute Preference (Fig. 3G):

A three-way mixed ANOVA (concentration × housing × sex) revealed significant concentration × sex [F (5, 346) = 2.722, p = 0.0199] and housing × sex [F (1, 72) = 4.953, p = 0.0292] interactions, as well as main effects of concentration [F (5, 346) = 27.11, p < 0.0001], housing [F (1, 72) = 4.856, p = 0.0308], and sex [F (1, 72) = 17.44, p < 0.0001]. Preference for ethanol with quinine remained high particularly in male aSI rats however, this was likely driven by the basal ethanol preference differences between the two housing groups. Bonferroni post-hoc for multiple comparisons to measure the impact of sex on quinine-adulterated ethanol preference indicated that male rats preferred the ethanol+quinine solution more compared to female rats at the 3 mg/L (p = 0.0242), 30 mg/L (p = 0.0067), and 100 mg/L (p = 0.0006) concentrations.

24-hour Preference (Fig. 3H):

A three-way mixed ANOVA (concentration × housing × sex) revealed significant concentration × housing [F (5, 346) = 2.946, p = 0.0128] and concentration × sex [F (5, 346) = 4.240, p = 0.0009] interactions, as well as main effects of concentration [F (5, 346) = 69.40, p < 0.0001], housing [F (1, 72) = 6.588, p = 0.0123], and sex [F (1, 72) = 17.45, p < 0.0001]. Preference for ethanol with quinine remained high in aSI rats as well as in male rats with increasing concentrations of quinine. Bonferroni post-hoc for multiple comparisons to measure the impact of aSI (female and male data collapsed) on quinine-adulterated ethanol preference indicated that aSI rats preferred the ethanol+quinine solution more compared to aGH rats at the 3 mg/L (p = 0.0320) and 10 mg/L (p = 0.0217) concentrations. Furthermore, male rats demonstrated significantly greater preference than female rats (data collapsed across the housing condition) at the 0 mg/L (p = 0.0016), 3 mg/L (p = 0.0085), 10 mg/L (p = 0.0009), 30 mg/L (p < 0.0001), and 100 mg/L (p < 0.0001) concentrations.

Overall, aSI rats demonstrated greater consumption of and preference for the ethanol+quinine solution during both the 30-minute and 24-hour timepoints; however, this effect was likely driven by differences in basal ethanol consumption. Interestingly, male rats, irrespective of housing condition exhibited more persistent consumption and preference compared to female rats despite the adulteration of ethanol solution. Detailed statistics are reported in Supplemental Table 5.

Water consumption over 30 min

30-minute Consumption:

We assessed consumption of water in a 30-minute period (Fig. 3I) using a three-way mixed ANOVA (week × housing × sex). Results indicated a significant main effect of week [F (12, 864) = 3.861, p < 0.0001], where water consumption increased over time.

24-hour Consumption:

Finally, we assessed consumption of water in a 24-hour period (Fig. 3J) using a three-way mixed ANOVA (week × housing × sex). Results indicated a significant week × sex × housing interaction [F (12, 864) = 1.804, p = 0.0435] and week × sex interaction [F (12, 864) = 5.569, p < 0.0001], as well as a main effect of housing [F (1, 72) = 4.625, p = 0.0349], a main effect of sex [F (1, 72) = 63.38, p < 0.0001] and a main effect of week [F (6.550, 471.6) = 3.399, p < 0.0001], where aGH rats consumed significantly more water than aSI rats. Finally, Bonferroni post hoc analysis for multiple comparisons indicated that the weight-adjusted water consumption was significantly greater in female rats at weeks 1 (p = 0.0001), 2 (p = 0.0227), 3 (p = 0.0049), 4 (p = 0.0072), 5 (p = 0.0021), 6 (p = 0.0001), 7 (p = 0.0001),8 (p < 0.0001), 9 (p < 0.0001), 10 (p < 0.0001), 11 (p < 0.0001), 12 (p < 0.0001), and 13 (p < 0.0001) compared to male rats, irrespective of the housing condition. Furthermore, aGH male rats consumed significantly more water compared to aSI males during weeks 1 (p = 0.0436), 2 (p = 0.0401), 3 (p = 0.0257), 4 (p < 0.0265), 5 (p = 0.0487), 6 (p = 0.0241), and 8 (p = 0.0268). Detailed statistics are reported in Supplemental Table 6.

Tonic dopamine release kinetics

We measured tonic (1 pulse) dopamine release in the VP of aGH and aSI male and female rats following anxiety-like behavior testing (Fig. 4C–D).

Release (Fig. 4C):

Comparison of maximal dopamine release using a two-way between-subjects ANOVA (housing × sex) revealed no significant effects of housing condition or sex.

Rate of release (Fig. 4D):

Similarly, a two-way between-subjects ANOVA (housing × sex) of rate of dopamine release also did not reveal a significant effect of housing condition or sex.

Overall, housing manipulation during adolescence did not influence tonic dopamine release kinetics in the VP. Detailed statistics are reported in Supplemental Table 7.

Phasic dopamine release kinetics

In addition to baseline release following tonic stimulation, we quantified dopamine kinetics using phasic stimulation (5 pulses at 5, 10, 20, and 100 Hz frequencies) to measure terminal excitability (Fig. 4E–F).

Phasic dopamine release (Fig. 4E):

A three-way mixed ANOVA (stimulation frequency × housing × sex) indicated a main effect of stimulation frequency [F (3, 96) = 69.35, p < 0.0001] but no effects of housing or sex.

Rate of phasic dopamine release (Fig. 4F):

A three-way mixed ANOVA (stimulation frequency × housing × sex) indicated a significant effect of stimulation frequency [F (3, 96) = 133.8, p < 0.0001], with no effects of housing or sex.

Overall, dopamine release parameters increased in response to increasing stimulation frequencies, but this facilitation of dopamine release and rate of dopamine release did not differ by housing condition or sex, suggesting that dopamine release kinetics are tightly regulated in the VP and highly conserved across sex. Detailed statistics are reported in Supplemental Table 7.

Ethanol mediated changes in tonic and phasic dopamine release kinetics in aGH and aSI rats are sex-dependent

In order to assess the impact of aSI on ethanol-induced changes in dopamine transmission in male and female rats, after basal dopamine release was stable, we perfused ethanol (80 and 150 mM cumulatively) over the VP-containing slice from aGH and aSI male and female rats and measured tonic (Fig. 5A–B) and phasic (Fig. 6A–H) dopamine release kinetics (release and rate of release).

Tonic dopamine release kinetics following application of ethanol

Measures of tonic dopamine release and the rate of tonic dopamine release are expressed as a difference score compared to baseline release measures ([Ethanol-DA release - baseline DA release] or [Ethanol-rate of DA release - baseline rate of DA release]) and analyzed using a three-way mixed ANOVA (housing × sex × ethanol concentration).

Tonic dopamine release (Fig. 5A):

A three-way mixed ANOVA indicated a main effect of ethanol concentration [F (1, 17) = 35.29, p < 0.0001] and a main effect of housing [F (1, 17) = 4.762, p = 0.0434], with no effects of sex, or interactions between variables. While ethanol inhibited dopamine release in aGH rats, this inhibitory effect was dampened in aSI rats.

Rate of tonic dopamine release (Fig. 5B):

The three-way mixed ANOVA indicated a significant housing × concentration interaction [F (1, 17) = 7.002, p = 0.0164] and a main effect of ethanol concentration [F (1, 17) = 34.90, p < 0.0001], but no effects of sex or further interactions. Bonferroni post-hoc multiple comparisons indicated that, compared to the 80 mM ethanol concentration, the 150 mM concentration significantly reduced the rate of dopamine release in both aSI (p < 0.0001) and aGH rats (p = 0.0458). Furthermore, at the 150 mM concentration, ethanol-mediated inhibition was significantly greater in aSI rats compared to aGH rats (p = 0.0477; sex collapsed).

Overall, these data showed that aSI dampened the inhibitory effects of ethanol on dopamine release but augmented the ethanol-induced inhibition of the rate of dopamine release selectively at the higher concentration. Detailed statistics are reported in Supplemental Table 8.

Phasic dopamine release kinetics following application of ethanol

Ethanol-induced changes in phasic dopamine release kinetics are shown in Fig. 6. Male and female rats were analyzed separately with a three-way mixed ANOVA (housing × stimulation frequency × concentration of ethanol) comparing phasic dopamine release or rate of phasic dopamine release following the 80 mM and 150 mM concentrations of ethanol.

Phasic dopamine release (Fig. 6A–B):

In females (Fig. 6A), a three-way mixed ANOVA revealed a significant stimulation frequency × ethanol interaction [F (2.98, 29.80) = 3.97, p = 0.0170] and a main effect of stimulation frequency [F (1.58, 15.82) = 46.04, p < 0.0001], Bonferroni post hoc for multiple comparisons indicated significant differences between the 0 mM and 80 mM concentrations of ethanol at 20 Hz stimulation (p = 0.0047), and between the 0 mM and 150 mM concentrations of ethanol at 100 Hz stimulation (p = 0.0145). In males (Fig. 6B), a three-way mixed ANOVA revealed an interaction between the stimulation frequency and ethanol [F (2.91, 26.19) = 3.66, p = 0.026], with a significant main effect of stimulation frequency [F (1.60, 14.39) = 26.12, p < 0.0001] and a main effect of ethanol concentration [F (1.75, 15.71) = 3.81, p = 0.0500]. Bonferroni post-hoc testing indicated significant differences in aGH males at 20 Hz stimulation between the 0 mM and 150 mM concentrations (p = 0.0007) and the 80 mM and 150 mM concentrations (p = 0.0454). At 100 Hz stimulation parameters, aGH males again differed between the 0 mM and 150 mM concentrations (p = 0.0083).

Rate of dopamine release (Fig. 6C–D):

In females (Fig. 6C), the three-way mixed ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of stimulation frequency [F (1.29, 12.94) = 31.63, p < 0.0001], but no effect of ethanol concentration or interaction. A three-way mixed ANOVA in males (Fig. 6D) revealed an interaction between the stimulation frequency and ethanol [F (2.80, 25.16) = 3.47, p = 0.0330], as well as a significant main effect of stimulation frequency [F (1.58, 14.19) = 35.06, p < 0.0001], and ethanol concentration [F (1.56, 14.08) = 4.49, p = 0.0380].

Overall, ethanol generally inhibited phasic dopamine release, with little effect on the rate of dopamine release. However, this inhibition of phasic dopamine release was relegated to aSI females and aGH males, and was observed only at the greatest stimulation parameters. Detailed statistics are reported in Supplemental Table 9.

Discussion

Our data demonstrated that aSI produced an anxiety-like phenotype, augmented preference for ethanol, but did not promote aversion-resistant ethanol consumption. Furthermore, while aSI did not alter pallidal tonic or phasic release and rate of release at baseline, dopamine release parameters in the VP were altered by ethanol in a sex-dependent manner. Particularly, the inhibitory effects of low and high concentrations of ethanol on tonic dopamine release were dampened while the 150 mM ethanol-induced inhibition of rate of tonic release was enhanced in aSI rats. Interestingly, the inhibitory effects of ethanol on phasic dopamine release were observed only in aSI female rats and aGH male rats. Overall, our results suggest that aSI promotes maladaptive addiction-related behavioral phenotypes, such as anxiety-like behavior and aberrant ethanol consumption. The maladaptive ethanol consumption behaviors may be linked to the enhanced efficacy of ethanol to alter dopamine release parameters within the VP.

Previous studies have reported an aSI-associated increase in anxiety-like behavior phenotype in adulthood as measured by the open-field assay [11,44], see 8 and 12 for review. Here, we recapitulate these findings in male rats and add to a growing base of literature suggesting similar effects are observed in female rats [45]. In the current study, male and female aSI rats exhibited lower latency to exit the inner-zone, and a greater latency to re-enter the inner-zone of the open-field, though the former effect was driven largely by aSI female rats, and the latter effect was driven by aSI male rats. Both, latency to exit inner zone and latency to re-enter the inner-zone have been used to assess anxiety-like behaviors in previous reports [46–53]. Interestingly however, unlike previous reports [11,54], we did not observe an aSI-induced reduction in total time in the inner-zone or elevation in total distance travelled in male or female rats. It is important to note that our open field arena was significantly larger than the one used in previous studies [11,54,55] and could have affected the overall behavior of the rats. One study that used a larger arena also did not report differences in inner-zone time in aSI and aGH rats [56]. It is possible that a smaller arena induces additional stress resulting in synergistic effects on maladaptive locomotor behavior. With respect to the EPM, male and female aSI rats in the current study spent significantly less time in the open arms. This effect was not due to differences in overall locomotion, as there were no differences between aSI and aGH rats in the total number of entries in both open and closed arms combined. While our data in male rats are consistent with previous reports [9,57], the female data contradict previous findings [58,59]. This discrepancy may be related to differences in testing procedures, whereby lighting during our EPM was substantially higher in lux compared to one of these reports [58], ~40 lux, in contrast to ~250 lux in the current study. It is possible that the lower lighting did not deter exploration in female rats in the previous studies, and the brighter lighting used in our study dampened exploration allowing an anxiety-like phenotype to emerge.

We further characterized the anxiety-like phenotype induced by aSI using the SAT, which has been demonstrated to accurately reflect more traditional tests of anxiety-like behavior such as the EPM [40]. Here we show that aSI animals spent significantly more time in Zone 1 and exhibited a greater latency to leave Zone 1, the least anxiogenic portion of the assay that is reflective of the closed arm of the EPM. Furthermore, aGH rats spent significantly more time than aSI rats in Zone 4, the most anxiogenic region of this assay and reflective of the open arm of the EPM. Together, our results are consistent with a robust anxiety-like phenotype induced by aSI. None of the previous studies have compared aGH- and aSI-associated behavior on the SAT during adulthood, but our results are congruent with a previous study in female rats with short term early aSI followed by behavioral assessment on the SAT during the late adolescent period [60], and a prior study in male rats that demonstrates that chronic intermittent exposure to ethanol vapor increases anxiety-like behavior as measured by the SAT [61].

Together with the open-field (latency to exit the inner-zone, female aSI rats; latency to re-enter the inner-zone, male aSI rats), the EPM (both sexes), and the SAT (both sexes) data, we confirmed that aSI produced an anxiety-like phenotype. It is important to note that assessing behaviors in preclinical models is complex and often riddled with sex-specific effects and sensitivities to various assays. Discrepancies observed in the current and previous studies highlight the nuance of measuring anxiety-like behavior, and therefore the importance of using multiple assays and measures within each assay. Thus, although we observed sex-differences in all three assays we used, both male and female aSI rats exhibit heightened anxiety-like behaviors in at least one measure in each assay, further suggesting that aSI promotes anxiety-like behaviors irrespective of sex.

We and others have shown an aSI-associated augmentation in ethanol self-administration in male rats [14,15,62,63]. Recent studies also showed this effect in female rats [63,64]. Consistent with these studies, our current data demonstrate greater ethanol preference in male and female aSI rats in a 30-minute and 24-hour period, effects that are likely due to positive reinforcement properties of ethanol; e.g., aSI-mediated conditioned place preference [65] and potentiated acute ethanol-induced elevations in dopamine levels in the NAc [9] following aSI. Interestingly, in the current study, aSI did not promote aversion-resistant ethanol consumption in male or female rats. Others have reported aversion-resistant ethanol consumption in models of stress; however, these studies used more robust stressors such as chronic exposure to predators [66] or early-life footshock [67], as opposed to the mild psychosocial stress exposure used in the current study. One study reported that a week-long aSI exposure beginning PD21 exacerbated ethanol consumption and aversion-resistant drinking selectively in female [64]. It is important to note that in this study [64], ethanol+quinine solution consumption was 50 % of the basal ethanol consumption in both aGH and aSI at the 25 μM quinine concentration. Ethanol consumption reduced significantly in both groups with no remaining group difference with 100 μM quinine adulteration. Moreover, this study began the social isolation paradigm at PD21, a week earlier than the current study. Exposure to stress during early adolescence possibly induces changes in female neurobiology associated with aversion-resistant drinking, whereas stress exposure later in adolescence may induce changes in males. We and others have previously shown that intermittent ethanol exposure during early or late adolescence produces differential neurobiological and behavioral changes in male and female rats [68,69].

Since our results overall suggested that aSI did not alter compulsive or aversion-resistant drinking, it is possible that the aSI associated augmented ethanol preference is likely driven by enhanced valency of rewarding stimuli. While one study has investigated the impact of aSI on reward valency [65], neurochemical studies regarding the effects of aSI have largely been focused in the NAc [9,10,14,17]. The VP is not only sensitive to social stressors [28], but pallidal dopamine release is also important for mediating the effects of ethanol [30]. Pallidal dopamine also plays a modulatory role by inducing both excitatory and inhibitory effects on VP neurons [31,32]. Thus, we probed several dopamine release parameters in the VP in both aSI and aGH male and female rats. These results will be discussed in the sections below.

Previously, we demonstrated an aSI-associated robust facilitation in dopamine release in the NAc core and shell [14,21]. Interestingly, we did not observe any differences in either baseline tonic or phasic dopamine release between aSI and aGH rats in the VP. Similarly, aSI did not affect the rate of tonic or phasic dopamine release in the VP. These data suggest that it is unlikely that aSI altered VTA-VP dopamine projection density or terminal excitability. Although there is evidence that VTA-dopamine neurons projecting to the medial NAc send collaterals to the NAc medial shell and other extra-striatal regions including the VP, a distinct population of lateral NAc-projecting VTA dopamine neurons were confined to striatal areas [70]. Data in our previous study were recorded from the lateral NAc core and shell regions [14,21]. The dopamine terminals we recorded from in the current study may therefore belong to the medial NAc-projecting VTA dopamine neurons.

Chronic stressors often sensitize monoamine responses to ethanol exposure [9,71]. Therefore, we probed for differences between dopamine release parameters in aSI and aGH animals following the perfusion of ethanol onto slices containing the VP. This is the first study examining the effect of ethanol on tonic and phasic dopamine release in VP using ex vivo FSCV. Our data showed that the inhibitory effects of ethanol on dopamine release parameters were concentration-housing- and sex-specific. We first demonstrated that a high concentration of ethanol (150 mM) significantly reduced tonic dopamine release in both aGH and aSI rats compared to a lower concentration of ethanol (80 mM). Our results are consistent with previous studies showing an ethanol-induced depression in dopamine release in the NAc core, particularly at higher concentrations [72–74]. Interestingly, the inhibitory effects of ethanol on tonic dopamine release were greater in aGH rats compared to aSI rats. It is possible that stress exposure blunts dopamine response to ethanol via an enhancement in GABA modulation [75], as ethanol-mediated dopamine release may be driven by GABA receptor activation on cholinergic interneurons [76].

We are the first to report effects of ethanol on evoked phasic dopamine release in any region of the brain in female rats. While we observe similar effects of perfusion of ethanol over the pallidal slice on tonic dopamine release, the results on phasic release are sex-specific and housing-dependent. Specifically, in female rats, ethanol inhibited dopamine release significantly in aSI rats; however, this effect was blunted in male rats exposed to aSI. It is possible that at the terminal level, local circuitry in males and females differentially affects dopamine release. It is important to note that systemic ethanol administration-induced elevations in vivo preparations are region- and sex-dependent; for example, while ethanol-mediated elevations in accumbal dopamine in female rats is greater than in male rats, in the caudate ethanol elevates dopamine in male with no effect in female rats [77]. Ethanol-mediated inhibition of dopamine release is modulated by nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in the NAc [73,78]. Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors are composed of distinct subunits, with the alpha6 subunit-containing nicotinic receptor demonstrating an especially great sensitivity to ethanol [79]. Furthermore, there are sex-differences in the conformation of nicotinic acetylcholine [see [80] for review]. Ethanol interaction with these distinct subunits could potentially drive differential effects on dopamine release. Neural insult during adolescence via aSI or chronic intermittent binge-like exposure to ethanol augments acute ethanol challenge-mediated elevation in extracellular levels of dopamine in the NAc [9,10,81]. It is possible that these aSI-associated sex differences in ethanol-induced modulation of pallidal dopamine are driven by sex-dependent changes in nicotinic acetylcholine receptor expression or function. But it is important to note that to date, there is no literature characterizing the relationship between nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and dopamine in the VP. Thus, further research is necessary to identify the exact mechanism underlying these sex-specific changes.

Similar to its inhibitory effects on dopamine release, superfusion of high-concentration ethanol (150 mM) over pallidal slices reduced the rate of tonic dopamine release in aSI and aGH male and female rats compared to a lower concentration of ethanol (80 mM); however, with an aSI-associated potentiation in this inhibitory effect. Surprisingly, ethanol (80 or 150 mM) did not affect the rate of phasic dopamine release in any group. The rate of dopamine release is highly influenced by calcium influx and calcium channels. Although we do not observe differences in rate of release between aGH and aSI groups at baseline, it is possible ethanol influences calcium influx differentially in adolescent stress naive and exposed rats. Ethanol-mediated reduction in dopamine release occurs via a calcium signaling mechanism in the NAc core; particularly, an augmentation in calcium-concentration blocks the ethanol-induced inhibition in dopamine release [72], which is highly influenced by the rate of release. Similarly, ethanol reduces calcium influx which can lead to an overall reduction in dopamine transmission [82,83]. While the interaction between psychosocial stress and ethanol on calcium channel function has not been examined systematically, our results suggest that psychosocial stress potentiates the ethanol-mediated disruption in calcium signaling.

Summary and conclusions

In conclusion, our results confirmed that aSI generates an anxiety-like phenotype, which may further contribute to aberrant ethanol-drinking behaviors. While dopamine transmission at baseline in the VP was conserved following aSI, subsequent exposure to drugs such as ethanol triggered atypical responses of the pallidal dopamine system. We further demonstrated that while ethanol inhibited overall tonic dopamine transmission (release and rate of release) in the VP in male and female rats, albeit with a blunted effect in aSI rats, it only affected phasic dopamine release, not the rate, in a sex-specific manner. Particularly, ethanol inhibited phasic dopamine release in aGH male rats but not in aGH female rats. Surprisingly the effects of ethanol were reversed in both male and female rats after aSI, such that ethanol inhibited phasic release in aSI female rats and had no effects in aSI male rats. Though we do not understand the underlying mechanisms driving these sex differences fully, these sexually dimorphic results indicate distinct aSI-associated circuit changes within the VP in male and female rats. Since VP is involved in reward valency processing [23,24,33], the ethanol-mediated alterations in VP may contribute to the aSI-associated augmentation in ethanol preference, albeit in a stress- and sex-dependent manner. Early-life stress in humans contributes to the generation of AUD [84,85], and adverse childhood experiences may accelerate the age of first use of alcohol [86,87]. Chronic stressors are specifically indicative of the development of AUD [84,85]. While the mechanism underlying AUD is both highly-studied and elusive, there is little doubt that chronic and adolescent stress exposure influence its pathology. Our data contribute to a growing field of study concerning the impact of stress within the context of AUD. The development of pharmacotherapies to treat AUD arising from such early-life stressors represents a promising goal for future research.
