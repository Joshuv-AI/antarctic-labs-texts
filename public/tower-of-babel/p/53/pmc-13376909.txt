Effectiveness of an educational intervention based on the health belief model on eco-driving behaviors among taxi drivers in Tehran
Authors: Mohammadreza Mokhtari, Farkhondeh Amin Shokravi, Hasan Shahbazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAir pollution in Tehran, mainly caused by vehicular emissions, poses a serious public health threat. Eco-driving practices are considered effective behavioral strategies to reduce fuel consumption and pollutant emissions. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of an educational intervention based on the Health Belief Model in promoting eco-driving behaviors among taxi drivers. A randomized controlled trial was conducted in February 2025 among 80 male yellow line taxi drivers in Tehran, randomly assigned to intervention and control groups. The intervention included two structured training sessions developed according to HBM constructs. Data were collected at baseline, immediately after the intervention, and three months later using a validated questionnaire. Statistical analyses were performed using parametric and non-parametric tests. Compared with controls, the intervention group showed significant improvements in awareness, perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, self-efficacy, and eco-driving behaviors immediately after the intervention and at three-month follow-up (p ≤ 0.001). Cues to action improved significantly only at follow-up, whereas perceived barriers did not show sustained change. HBM-based education effectively enhanced both cognitive constructs and eco-driving behaviors among taxi drivers. While educational interventions can lead to meaningful behavioral improvements, addressing structural and economic barriers through supportive policy measures is essential for the long-term sustainability of eco-driving practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50972-7.Keywords: Health belief model, Educational intervention, Eco-driving, Taxi drivers, Air pollution, TehranSubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Background

Air is one of the most vital elements essential for human survival1. Economic growth and urbanization, as two dominant global trends, are increasingly associated with rising levels of air pollution. This phenomenon, particularly in urban areas, has emerged as a major public health concern and a threat to human well-being2.

Air pollution refers to the presence of one or more pollutants in outdoor or indoor air at concentrations and durations that may harm human health, vegetation, animals, or property, or unreasonably interfere with the enjoyment of life or the use of property3. Today, 9 out of 10 people worldwide breathe air that exceeds safe pollution levels. Extensive research has demonstrated a significant association between air pollution and the exacerbation of cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, as well as certain mental health disorders4,5. According to scientific evidence, air pollution ranks among the top four to five leading causes of mortality worldwide6. In Iran, air pollution is recognized as the third leading cause of death. According to official reports, in 2019, the mortality rate due to air pollution in Iran was 40.74 deaths per 100,000 population7.

Transportation, as a fundamental component of modern urban life, plays a pivotal role in the generation of air pollutants8. The sector’s heavy reliance on fossil fuels has resulted in significant environmental consequences9. Research evidence indicates a direct correlation between the expansion of transportation infrastructure and the allocation of more urban space to vehicle traffic, with increased fuel consumption, vehicle numbers, and pollutant emissions10. Major pollutants emitted from transportation activities include carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen oxides (NOx), sulfur oxides (SOx), particulate matter, unburned hydrocarbons, and heavy metals such as lead11.

Tehran is among the most polluted cities in Iran12. According to data from the Tehran Air Quality Control Company, the transportation sector stands as the predominant source of atmospheric pollutants in the Iranian capital. Specifically, it accounts for 98% of carbon monoxide, 47% of nitrogen oxides, 86% of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and 70% of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) emissions. Evidence further suggests that particulate matter especially PM2.5 has the most substantial impact on Tehran’s air quality, exerting significantly greater harmful effects compared to other pollutants13–15. Numerous studies have indicated that motor vehicles, as a major source of mobile pollutants, play a critical role in contributing to air pollution in Tehran16.

Road transportation is a major contributor to urban air pollution through diverse mechanisms17. These include (i) the direct emission of gaseous pollutants and particulate matter (PM) from vehicle exhaust systems; (ii) the generation of secondary pollutants formed through atmospheric reactions involving primary emissions; and (iii) non-exhaust emissions originating from brake and tire abrasion, roadway surface wear, and the resuspension of road dust. While regulatory frameworks and technological advancements have significantly curtailed tailpipe emissions, non-exhaust emissions continue to evade strict regulation. This is primarily due to the diffuse and heterogeneous nature of their sources, coupled with the technical challenges associated with accurate quantification and control18.

It is widely acknowledged that a significant portion of global health issues can be prevented through low-cost behavioral interventions, emphasizing the critical role of behavior in health promotion19. In response to the growing concern over air pollution, eco-driving has emerged as an effective behavioral intervention aimed at reducing emissions from road transportation20. Eco-driving behavior can be conceptually defined as a set of intentional, learned, and modifiable driving practices designed to optimize fuel efficiency and minimize unnecessary fuel consumption, thereby reducing vehicular emissions. Unlike technological or regulatory approaches that focus on vehicle design or emission standards, eco-driving emphasizes behavioral modification through cognitive, motivational, and skill-based mechanisms at the individual driver level. Such practices include smooth acceleration and deceleration, maintaining steady and appropriate speeds, anticipating traffic conditions, avoiding excessive idling, timely gear shifting, and maintaining proper tire pressure. By adopting these techniques, drivers can significantly reduce fuel consumption and consequently decrease the emission of harmful pollutants such as carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen oxides (NOx), and particulate matter. Empirical evidence suggests that eco-driving may reduce fuel consumption by up to 15%, highlighting its potential as a practical and scalable strategy for mitigating transportation-related air pollution and its associated health consequences.21–25. Furthermore, the implementation of eco-driving strategies offers an efficient and low-cost means of mitigating the adverse health effects associated with air pollution, aligning with broader public health objectives26.

Theoretical frameworks and models serve as essential tools in guiding the design and implementation of health education and promotion programs27. These frameworks assist planners in identifying why individuals may fail to adopt recommended behaviors, how such behaviors can be effectively modified, and which factors should be considered when evaluating program effectiveness28. Among these, the Health Belief Model is particularly recognized for its utility in designing educational interventions aimed at promoting preventive health behaviors29. By providing a structured understanding of the psychological determinants influencing individual health decisions, the HBM enables health education specialists to develop interventions that align with the beliefs, motivations, and needs of the target population30. This approach enhances the likelihood of behavior change and contributes to the broader goal of improving public health outcomes31,32.

HBM is one of the foundational theories in health behavior research, offering a cognitive framework for understanding and predicting preventive health actions. The model posits that individuals are more likely to engage in health-promoting behaviors when they perceive a personal risk and believe that a specific action can mitigate that risk effectively33. HBM comprises several core constructs. Perceived susceptibility refers to an individual’s assessment of their likelihood of experiencing a health problem. Perceived severity involves the perceived seriousness of the consequences associated with the health issue. Perceived benefits denote the individual’s belief in the efficacy of the recommended action to reduce the risk or impact of the condition. Perceived barriers include the individual’s evaluation of the obstacles or costs related to adopting the behavior. Additionally, self-efficacy, introduced in later iterations of the model, reflects the confidence in one’s ability to perform the recommended behavior successfully. Cues to action, both internal and external, can also serve as triggers that initiate health behavior changes34.

By providing a structured approach to understanding health-related decision making, the HBM enables health professionals to design targeted interventions35. These interventions can enhance risk perception, minimize perceived barriers, and bolster individual’s confidence, thereby promoting the adoption of preventive and health-enhancing behaviors in the target population36.

In addition, the selection of the Health Belief Model for the present intervention was not arbitrary, but was grounded in empirical evidence. Prior to designing this intervention, the research team conducted a descriptive–analytical study aimed at identifying the key predictors of air pollution–reducing behaviors among taxi drivers in Tehran using the Health Belief Model. The findings of that study, which have been published in Scientific Reports, demonstrated that several HBM constructs were significant predictors of eco-driving and pollution-reducing behaviors among drivers. Based on these results, the current study was designed as a pilot intervention specifically targeting the influential HBM constructs identified in the prior investigation, ensuring theoretical continuity and evidence-based intervention development37.

The model has been extensively applied in interventions addressing environmental protection behaviors, energy-saving practices, and other risk-related behaviors, where individual perceptions of risk, benefits, barriers, and self-efficacy play a central role in behavior adoption38.However, despite the conceptual relevance of HBM to eco-driving which similarly involves risk perception, cost–benefit evaluation, and behavioral control its application in transportation-related behavioral interventions remains limited39.This gap highlights the novelty of the present study, which extends the application of HBM to eco-driving behavior among professional drivers in a highly polluted metropolitan context.

In the context of eco-driving, the constructs of the Health Belief Model can be directly mapped onto drivers’ cognitive and behavioral processes. Perceived susceptibility and perceived severity reflect drivers’ awareness of their contribution to air pollution and the seriousness of its health and environmental consequences. Perceived benefits refer to drivers’ beliefs that eco-driving practices can effectively reduce fuel consumption and emissions, while perceived barriers include practical constraints such as habitual driving patterns, time pressure, and economic concerns. Self-efficacy represents drivers’ confidence in their ability to consistently perform eco-driving behaviors under real-world traffic conditions, and cues to action include educational messages and reminders that trigger behavior change37,40.95.

Given the critical role of the transportation sector in contributing to urban air pollution41 and the proven effectiveness of eco-driving behaviors in reducing vehicular emissions42, designing interventions that can promote such behaviors is of paramount importance. Despite the existence of various strategies, a gap remains in the application of theory-based educational programs targeting drivers’ behaviors, particularly in metropolitan contexts such as Tehran. Utilizing the Health Belief Model as a framework enables the identification of cognitive and perceptual factors influencing eco-driving practices39. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of an educational intervention grounded in the Health Belief Model on promoting eco-driving behaviors among taxi drivers in Tehran.

Method

This study was designed as a two-arm randomized controlled trial (RCT) with parallel groups, consisting of an intervention group and a control group. The trial was prospectively registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20241002063242N1; registration date: November 29, 2024). The study was conducted in Tehran, Iran, among licensed yellow line taxi drivers. The intervention was implemented in February 2025 and completed within the same month. “The full trial protocol and the pre-specified statistical analysis plan are available in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20241002063242N1).”

The educational intervention was developed and delivered based on the Health Belief Model (HBM). It consisted of two intensive training sessions conducted between February 1 and February 19, 2025. Participants were assessed at three time points: baseline (prior to intervention), immediately after the intervention, and at three-month follow-up.

Study setting and population

The study was conducted in Tehran, Iran, at official taxi stations. To ensure adequate representativeness, stations were purposively selected from the north, south, east, and west of the city, allowing for convenient access to drivers without major disruption to their work.

The study population included licensed male drivers of yellow line taxis. Eligibility criteria were: (i) possession of a valid taxi license, (ii) at least one year of professional driving experience, and (iii) willingness to participate, with informed consent obtained verbally, as written consent was not feasible. Exclusion criteria were: (i) having a medical condition that prevented participation in training sessions, (ii) absence from one or more intervention sessions, or (iii) incomplete or inaccurate completion of the questionnaires.

A total of 80 participants were enrolled in the RCT and randomly assigned to intervention (n = 40) and control (n = 40) groups. The sample size was determined pragmatically, as the trial was designed as a pilot RCT to assess feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of an educational program. Drivers with the lowest eco-driving behavior scores were prioritized to maximize the potential impact of the intervention.

Sampling and sample size

Participants for this pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) were drawn from a prior descriptive-analytical survey conducted among 401 yellow line taxi drivers across Tehran, which ensured proportional representation from the north, south, east, and west regions of the city. From this survey, 80 drivers with the lowest eco-driving behavior scores were purposively selected for the intervention, in order to target those most in need of behavior modification.

The 80 participants were randomly allocated into two equal groups (intervention, n = 40; control, n = 40) using a simple randomization method via a random number table. Participants were distributed across multiple taxi stations in the city, ensuring independent allocation and minimal interaction between groups. Only the principal investigator was aware of group assignments.

As this study was designed as a pilot trial, the sample size was determined pragmatically based on feasibility and resource considerations rather than a formal statistical power calculation. The primary objective was to evaluate the feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of an educational intervention based on the Health Belief Model among taxi drivers, providing essential insights for future large-scale studies.

Oral informed consent was obtained from all participants, as written consent was not feasible in this context. The procedure for verbal consent, as well as assurances of confidentiality and anonymity of data, were reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Randomization and allocation concealment

The random allocation sequence was generated by the principal investigator and concealed from participants and data collectors until interventions were assigned; no other staff involved in recruitment were aware of group assignments.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the educational intervention, no blinding (masking) was implemented. Participants, the investigator delivering the intervention, outcome assessors, and data analysts were all aware of group assignments.

Instrument and measures

A self-developed questionnaire was used to assess Health Belief Model constructs and eco-driving behaviors. The instrument was designed based on a comprehensive review of the literature, existing validated questionnaires, and the specific cultural and professional context of taxi drivers in Tehran. Initial items were drafted to capture the relevant constructs of the HBM, including knowledge, perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, self-efficacy, cues to action, and actual eco-driving behavior. Knowledge items were designed to assess general, health- and environment-oriented awareness relevant to emission reduction and eco-driving, rather than manufacturer-specific technical standards, and were adapted to the heterogeneous and context-specific conditions of the urban taxi fleet in Tehran.

The preliminary questionnaire underwent rigorous content and face validity assessment through expert review and pilot testing with a small sample of taxi drivers to ensure clarity, relevance, and feasibility. Subsequently, construct validity was evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis, and reliability was assessed via internal consistency and test–retest methods. The finalized instrument demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties.

The results of the questionnaire’s development and psychometric evaluation have not yet been published but will be reported in a forthcoming publication.

Educational intervention design

The educational intervention was developed based on the findings of the descriptive–analytical phase of the study and guided by the Health Belief Model. The primary aim was to promote environmentally sustainable driving behaviors among taxi drivers in Tehran. The content and structure of the intervention were tailored to the participants’ needs, identified through the lowest scoring domains in the HBM constructs during the preliminary phase.

Considering the professional and time constraints of taxi drivers, the intervention consisted of two concise, face-to-face sessions, each lasting approximately 30 min. Sessions were conducted at selected taxi stations across Tehran, ensuring minimal disruption to participants’ work schedules. The educational content was structured around three key domains: cognitive, affective, and behavioral, covering core HBM constructs including awareness, perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy.

Session content and structure

Session 1 The first session began with thought-provoking questions to engage participants cognitively. Instruction was delivered using a semi-lecture format combined with interactive dialogue. The session concluded with targeted group discussions, allowing participants to reflect on their role in air pollution, identify barriers and facilitators to sustainable driving, and enhance collective self-efficacy. Key topics included air pollution, its consequences, perceived susceptibility and severity, and the role of drivers in reducing environmental impact.

Session 2 Held the following morning, the second session began with a brief review of the previous session to reinforce memory retention. Additional topics related to sustainable driving behaviors and HBM constructs were covered. Structured group activities and scenario-based exercises allowed participants to explore real-life urban driving challenges (e.g., traffic congestion, frequent stops, vehicle maintenance) and develop practical solutions to reduce fuel consumption and emissions. The researcher facilitated discussions to help participants identify personal barriers and benefits aligned with the HBM framework. The session concluded with a brief feedback exercise, and participants received an educational pamphlet summarizing key concepts and providing researcher contact information for ongoing support.

All participants allocated to the intervention group (n = 40) were carefully monitored to ensure attendance at both sessions. Given their routine work schedules at taxi stations, participants were contacted the day before the second session via phone calls or text messages to confirm their availability and encourage participation. Each small group (4–5 drivers) maintained the same composition across both sessions to ensure continuity of discussion and familiarity among participants. This systematic coordination allowed all participants to attend the second session on the following day, with no absences reported. These procedures were implemented to maintain consistency of exposure to the educational content and to preserve the fidelity of the intervention.

Development of educational materials

Educational content was developed systematically based on a comprehensive literature review of environmentally sustainable driving practices and prior studies using the HBM framework. Messages were adapted for comprehension and cultural relevance, incorporating principles of health literacy and behavior change. Visual aids including illustrations, infographics, and AI-assisted images were designed to enhance engagement and facilitate understanding. The final materials were reviewed by both the research team and external experts, followed by pilot testing with a small sample of taxi drivers to ensure clarity, relevance, and usability. Feedback was incorporated to finalize the materials for use in the intervention.

Field implementation and delivery

Due to logistical constraints, sessions were conducted in small groups of 4–5 drivers or individually where group formation was not feasible. All sessions were delivered by the trained researcher to ensure consistency and adherence to the intervention protocol. Confidentiality of participants’ responses was strictly maintained, and each questionnaire was assigned a unique code. No personal identifiers were collected. Overall, the intervention was designed to maximize engagement, reinforce theoretical constructs of the HBM, and promote practical adoption of environmentally sustainable driving behaviors in a real-world setting.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted in three phases corresponding to the pre-intervention and post-intervention assessments. Initially, pre-intervention data were extracted from the subset of 80 taxi drivers selected based on low baseline behavioral scores. Following the intervention, two post-intervention assessments were carried out: the first immediately after the intervention and the second at three months follow-up.

All questionnaires were administered in person by the trained researcher to ensure accuracy and completeness. Given the varying literacy levels and limited availability of some drivers, a structured interview approach was employed, wherein the researcher read each question aloud and recorded the participants’ responses without any influence or interpretation. This method minimized missing data, ensuring that all 40 questionnaires in each study group were fully completed.

Participant confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the study. No personal identifiers were recorded, and each questionnaire was assigned a unique code. All data were securely stored and used solely for research purposes.

Concomitant care received during the trial for each group

During the trial, participants in the intervention group received only the two-session educational program based on the Health Belief Model, along with an educational pamphlet provided at the end of the second session. No additional training or care was provided. The control group received no intervention and continued their routine activities as usual.

Harms and safety

The educational intervention involved only discussions, group activities, and distribution of educational pamphlets, posing no physical or psychological harms. Participants were free to withdraw at any time, and no adverse events were reported throughout the study.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 27). A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered for all tests.

Due to non-normal distribution of the data, non-parametric tests were used. Specifically, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for between-group comparisons, and the Friedman test was employed for within-group repeated measures analyses. These statistical procedures were selected in accordance with the RCT design and the study objectives.

All randomized participants in both the intervention and control groups were included in the statistical analyses to evaluate the effectiveness of the educational intervention. No missing data were observed; all collected questionnaires were complete and included in the analyses.

No additional, subgroup, or sensitivity analyses were performed.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tarbiat Modares University (Code: IR.MODARES.REC.1403.137) and registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20241002063242N1). Prior to participation, all drivers received clear information about the study objectives, procedures, confidentiality of data, and the voluntary nature of participation. Informed consent was obtained verbally due to participants’ occupational constraints and reluctance to provide written consent.

Questionnaires were anonymized and coded, with no identifying information recorded without permission. Participants were assured that their data would remain confidential and that they could withdraw at any time without consequences. The educational sessions involved only discussions, group activities, and distribution of pamphlets, and thus posed no physical or psychological risks. Only the intervention group received the educational pamphlet containing the researcher’s contact details for follow-up inquiries, while the control group received no materials.

All procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical principles and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Participant flow

A total of 80 eligible taxi drivers were enrolled in the study and randomly assigned to either the intervention group (n = 40) or the control group (n = 40). All participants in the intervention group received the intended educational program, and there were no losses or exclusions in either group throughout the study period. Data from all randomized participants were included in the analyses of the primary and secondary outcomes.

Recruitment and follow-up

The educational intervention was conducted for the intervention group from February 1 to February 19, 2025. The control group did not receive any intervention. Outcome assessments were performed for both groups at two post-intervention time points: immediately after the completion of the intervention and at three months follow-up. The trial was completed as planned, with no early termination.

Harms

As noted in the Methods section, the educational intervention involved only discussions, group activities, and distribution of educational pamphlets, which posed no physical or psychological risks. Consistent with this, no harms or unintended effects were observed or reported in either the intervention or control groups during the trial.

Ancillary analyses

No subgroup or sensitivity analyses were conducted, as the trial was limited to the pre-specified primary and secondary outcomes.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that a Health Belief Model based educational intervention was effective in enhancing multiple constructs of the model and, ultimately, in promoting eco-driving behaviors among taxi drivers in Tehran. At baseline, no significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups across the studied constructs, indicating initial homogeneity of the samples. However, immediately after the intervention and at the three-month follow-up, the intervention group exhibited significant improvements in awareness, perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, cues to action, and self-efficacy. These findings highlight both the immediate and sustained impact of the educational program.

With respect to awareness, scores in the intervention group improved significantly post-intervention and remained elevated at follow-up, suggesting that the tailored educational content successfully enhanced drivers’ knowledge and maintained its effect over time. This outcome is consistent with previous intervention studies, where carefully designed, educational messages were shown to produce enduring knowledge gains.

Improvements were also evident in perceived susceptibility, as participants in the intervention group developed a heightened sense of vulnerability to the adverse consequences of air pollution. The significance of this construct lies in its role in motivating individuals to take preventive actions when they perceive themselves at risk. The durability of this effect at three months underscores the importance of emphasizing tangible, relatable health risks during training. Similarly, perceived severity increased meaningfully in the intervention group, reflecting participants’ deeper recognition of the potential harms associated with air pollution. Sustained changes in this domain suggest that compelling, real-life examples and persuasive educational messages contributed to stronger risk appraisals.

The intervention also succeeded in enhancing perceived benefits, with drivers acknowledging the personal and societal advantages of eco-driving behaviors. This perception was not only strengthened immediately after training but also persisted over time, demonstrating that the intervention provided participants with a clear and motivating rationale for behavioral change.

In contrast, perceived barriers did not show long-term improvement. Although a modest reduction was observed immediately after the intervention, this effect diminished by the three-month follow-up, with scores returning to near-baseline levels. The barriers most frequently cited such as the financial burden of vehicle maintenance, periodic inspections, and fuel choices are inherently structural and economic in nature. Unlike cognitive constructs such as awareness or self-efficacy, perceived barriers often extend beyond the scope of individual-level education and require broader policy measures, infrastructural support, and economic incentives. This finding reinforces the need for multi-level strategies that combine educational interventions with systemic supports, such as subsidies for clean fuels, reduced costs for vehicle inspections, and improved access to maintenance services.

Regarding cues to action, no significant changes were noted immediately post-intervention; however, at three months, a significant improvement emerged in the intervention group. This delayed effect suggests that behavioral cues often require time to manifest, becoming more salient as individuals integrate newly acquired knowledge and motivation into their daily practices.

Perhaps most notably, self-efficacy increased substantially among intervention participants immediately after the program and remained significantly elevated at follow-up. This construct is widely recognized as a strong predictor of health-related behavior change. The observed improvement likely reflects the participatory and skills-based nature of the intervention, which emphasized active learning, practical exercises, and positive feedback. Enhanced self-efficacy provided participants with the confidence needed to adopt and sustain eco-driving practices.

Finally, the intervention produced significant improvements in eco-driving behaviors themselves. Scores rose immediately after the program and remained higher than baseline at three months, confirming both the efficacy and durability of the educational approach. The combined influence of improved awareness, heightened risk perception, recognition of benefits, and strengthened self-efficacy appears to have translated into meaningful behavioral change.

In summary, this study demonstrates that HBM-based education can be a powerful tool for shaping environmental health behaviors. The intervention effectively improved most model constructs and produced sustained changes in eco-driving practices. The exception was perceived barriers, which did not respond to education alone, underscoring the necessity of complementary structural and policy-level interventions. These findings highlight the importance of integrating individual-focused educational strategies with broader systemic measures to achieve long-term behavioral change.

To clarify the effectiveness of the Health Belief Model in behavior change, the findings of this study were compared with those of Nabizadeh et al. (2022), who examined “The effect of an educational intervention based on the Health Belief Model on promoting cardiovascular disease preventive behaviors among oilfield employees.” Similar to their results, the present study demonstrated significant improvements in awareness, perceived susceptibility, perceived benefits, self-efficacy, and related behaviors, supporting the applicability of HBM-based interventions across different occupational groups. Notable differences emerged regarding perceived severity and perceived barriers. While perceived severity improved in this study but not in Nabizadeh’s, the opposite was observed for perceived barriers: the intervention had no sustained effect among taxi drivers, whereas a significant reduction was reported among oilfield workers. These differences likely reflect the nature of barriers encountered structural and economic in drivers versus occupational and environmental in oilfield employees. Overall, both studies affirm the effectiveness of HBM-based education while emphasizing the importance of complementing individual-level interventions with supportive environmental and policy measures to overcome persistent barriers43.

The present findings were also compared with those of Khani Jeyhooni et al. (2018), who investigated “Promotion of skin cancer preventive behaviors among farmers based on the Health Belief Model with a social support component.” Both studies reported significant improvements in awareness, perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, self-efficacy, and sustained behavioral changes, reaffirming the value of HBM-based interventions in health promotion. However, differences emerged in perceived barriers. In the current study, short-term reductions were not sustained, whereas Khani Jeyhooni’s intervention produced a significant and lasting decline. This contrast may reflect the economic and structural nature of barriers among taxi drivers, which are less responsive to education, versus the cultural and environmental barriers faced by farmers, which proved more modifiable. Importantly, the integration of structured social support in Khani Jeyhooni’s study through family involvement, mobile reminders, and group follow-ups likely contributed to the durability of change, highlighting the need to complement educational strategies with social and policy-level support for long-term impact44.

The results of this study were also compared with those of Moridi et al. (2021), who investigated “Promotion of home accident preventive behaviors among mothers of children under five years based on the Health Belief Model.” Both studies demonstrated significant improvements in key HBM constructs including awareness, perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, self-efficacy, and target behaviors. In addition, cues to action improved and were sustained at follow-up in both studies, highlighting the broad applicability of the HBM framework in contexts ranging from household accident prevention to eco-driving behaviors. Despite these similarities, findings on perceived barriers diverged. In the present study, reductions were short-lived and returned to baseline at three months, while Moridi et al. achieved a significant and lasting decrease. This contrast likely reflects the nature of barriers: economic and structural for taxi drivers requiring policy or economic support versus psychological and environmental for mothers, which were more amenable to education, group discussions, and follow-up reminders. The sustained improvements in Moridi’s study also reflect the use of multimedia tools, weekly messages, and telephone follow-ups, underscoring the role of continuous support. In contrast, the present findings emphasize the necessity of policy-level and structural measures to overcome economic barriers and secure long-term behavioral change45.

The present findings were also compared with those of Noman et al. (2024), who investigated “The effect of an HBM-based educational intervention on breast cancer screening behaviors among Yemeni female teachers in Malaysia.” Both studies showed that HBM-driven interventions enhanced awareness, perceived benefits, and self-efficacy, leading to significant behavior change eco-driving behaviors in the current trial and breast self-examination (BSE) and clinical breast examination (CBE) practices in the Noman study. Despite these parallels, some distinctions were evident. In this study, perceived susceptibility and severity of air pollution improved significantly and remained stable, while Noman et al. reported no comparable changes in breast cancer threat perception, likely due to the different immediacy of risks perceived by taxi drivers versus teachers. Perceived barriers also diverged: financial and structural constraints among drivers persisted despite training, whereas Noman et al. observed a reduction in barriers to BSE but continued challenges with clinical screening. Furthermore, cues to action showed contrasting patterns. In the present study, improvement emerged only at the three-month follow-up, while Noman et al. achieved earlier effects through reminders and multimedia tools. Together, these comparisons emphasize that while HBM-based education reliably strengthens awareness, benefits, and self-efficacy, the sustainability of outcomes in constructs such as barriers and threat depends on contextual and structural factors unique to each population46.

The current findings were also compared with those of Puspitasari and Nugraheni (2020), who evaluated an “HBM-based booklet intervention to prevent high-risk pregnancies in Indonesia”. Both studies confirmed that the HBM framework can positively shape cognitive beliefs and promote healthier behaviors. In our trial, education enhanced knowledge, perceived susceptibility and severity, perceived benefits, self-efficacy, and ultimately eco-driving practices. In contrast, the Indonesian study reported stronger gains in perceived susceptibility, perceived benefits, and partial reduction of barriers. Nonetheless, the trajectory of change differed across certain constructs. For instance, perceived severity increased and remained stable at three-month follow-up among drivers, whereas no such effect emerged in the Indonesian sample. This may reflect differences in intervention delivery: the use of a single booklet may have been insufficient to convey risk intensity, a point acknowledged by Puspitasari and Nugraheni themselves, who noted that multi-channel communication could have yielded greater impact. Differences also emerged regarding perceived barriers and cues to action. In this study, economic and structural barriers such as costs of clean fuel or vehicle maintenance remained largely unresolved, while the Indonesian trial addressed primarily psychological or attitudinal obstacles that were more amenable to educational materials. Moreover, cues to action improved only over time among taxi drivers, but showed no significant change in the Indonesian study, likely due to the absence of sustained external reminders. Collectively, these comparisons highlight that although HBM-based education is effective in shaping awareness and motivation, its long-term success depends on both the type of barriers targeted and the diversity of educational tools employed47.

The present findings were compared with Singh et al. (2021), who evaluated an “HBM-based educational intervention among patients with type 2 diabetes”. In both studies, the interventions led to significant improvements in awareness, perceived susceptibility and severity, perceived benefits, and self-efficacy. Taxi drivers in Tehran demonstrated increased knowledge and heightened sensitivity to the risks of air pollution, while diabetic patients in Singh’s study reported greater understanding of the benefits of self-care practices. These results underscore the adaptability of the HBM framework in promoting cognitive and motivational constructs across diverse health-related behaviors, from environmental practices to chronic disease management. Differences emerged regarding perceived barriers and cues to action. In the current study, reductions in barriers were short-lived, with economic and structural obstacles limiting sustained behavioral change, whereas Singh et al. reported significant decreases in cognitive and skill-related barriers. Similarly, cues to action improved gradually among drivers, while in Singh’s study, multimedia tools, videos, and group discussions facilitated more immediate behavioral triggers. Despite these variations, both studies observed enhanced self-efficacy and meaningful behavioral improvements, highlighting that HBM-based interventions can generate consistent positive change across different populations and behavioral domains, provided that the nature of barriers and the choice of educational tools are considered48.

For a more comprehensive analysis, the present results were compared with Damayantie et al. (2025), who examined an “HBM-based educational intervention to improve hypoglycemia prevention skills in patients with diabetes”. Both studies demonstrated significant behavioral improvements: eco-driving practices increased among taxi drivers in Tehran, while the intervention group in Damayantie’s study showed a notable rise in overall prevention skills from 48.11 to 55.89 (P = 0.0001). This alignment suggests that HBM-driven education can effectively enhance health- and environment-related behaviors across different contexts. However, a key distinction lies in the assessment of specific model constructs. In the current study, separate measurement of awareness, perceived susceptibility and severity, perceived benefits, self-efficacy, and cues to action revealed sustained gains at three-month follow-up. In contrast, Damayantie et al. reported only overall skill scores without disaggregating the individual HBM constructs, limiting direct construct-by-construct comparison. Overall, both studies underscore the capacity of HBM-based interventions to promote meaningful behavioral change, while the detailed evaluation in the present research provides a clearer picture of the underlying cognitive and motivational mechanisms driving these outcomes49.

Pinar et al. (2020) investigated the effect of an “HBM-based educational intervention on awareness, beliefs, and behaviors regarding breast cancer screening”. Although the behavioral contexts differed, both their study and the present research demonstrated that HBM-driven education effectively enhanced awareness, perceived susceptibility and severity, perceived benefits, self-efficacy, and target behaviors. A notable difference emerged regarding perceived barriers. In the current study, educational intervention alone did not produce sustained reductions in barriers, whereas Pinar et al. observed a significant decrease. This contrast highlights that the nature and context of barriers play a critical role in determining the effectiveness of HBM-based interventions50.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that an educational intervention grounded in the Health Belief Model was effective in improving multiple cognitive and behavioral constructs among taxi drivers in Tehran, ultimately leading to significant and sustained improvements in eco-driving behaviors. The intervention enhanced drivers’ awareness, perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, cues to action, and self-efficacy, with effects maintained up to three months post-intervention. These findings confirm the utility of the HBM as a robust theoretical framework for guiding behavioral interventions in environmental health promotion. However, perceived barriers remained largely unchanged, underscoring the limitations of educational strategies alone in addressing structural and economic constraints. Overall, the study highlights the potential of theory-driven health education to foster sustainable behavioral change, while simultaneously pointing to the necessity of multi-level approaches when structural barriers are present. These insights emphasize the importance of integrating individual-level educational strategies with supportive policy measures to ensure the long-term sustainability of eco-driving practices.

Limitations

Despite the rigorous methodological design and multi-phase approach of this study including instrument development and psychometric validation, a cross-sectional descriptive-analytical phase, and an interventional phase several important limitations should be acknowledged.

First, all data were based on self-reports collected through structured face-to-face interviews and researcher-administered questionnaires. While this approach minimized literacy-related issues and ensured completeness of responses, it inherently increases the risk of social desirability bias and recall error, which may limit the accuracy of assessing eco-driving behaviors. Additionally, the inability to directly observe participants’ behaviors and the time-intensive nature of interviews represent significant constraints. In addition, although the study included a dedicated section to assess self-reported eco-driving behaviors, not all micro-level driving practices (such as smooth acceleration, smooth braking, or maintaining perfectly steady speeds) were directly measured, as accurate assessment of these behaviors would require in-vehicle monitoring or telematics-based tools that were not feasible within the logistical, ethical, and resource constraints of this field-based study.

Second, the study sample was limited to line taxi drivers in Tehran. Consequently, the generalizability of the findings to other driver populations such as private car owners, intercity drivers, or rideshare drivers (e.g., Snapp or Tap30) as well as to different cultural or geographical contexts, is restricted.

Third, the follow-up period for assessing the sustainability of behavioral changes was limited to three months. While this timeframe allowed for the evaluation of short-term effects, it does not provide insight into the long-term maintenance of eco-driving behaviors, and future studies with extended follow-up are recommended.

Finally, although the intervention focused on individual-level educational strategies, structural and economic barriers such as vehicle maintenance costs, fuel type limitations, and policy constraints could not be addressed within the scope of the study. This highlights the necessity of integrating educational interventions with broader systemic and policy-level support to achieve lasting behavioral change.

Recommendations and implications for future research and practice

The findings of the present study indicate that educational interventions based on the Health Belief Model can effectively enhance cognitive and behavioral constructs related to eco-driving among taxi drivers. However, the persistence of perceived barriers highlights the limitations of educational approaches alone in addressing structural and economic constraints. Accordingly, future interventions should adopt a multi-level strategy that combines individual-level education with supportive policy and infrastructural measures. For instance, facilitating access to cost-effective vehicle maintenance services, promoting the use of cleaner fuels, and providing incentives for regular eco-friendly driving practices may help reduce perceived obstacles and sustain behavioral change over time. Moreover, longitudinal studies with extended follow-up periods are recommended to evaluate the durability of behavioral changes and to identify factors that contribute to long-term maintenance. Investigating the interaction between individual-level determinants (e.g., self-efficacy, awareness) and contextual factors (e.g., socioeconomic conditions, regulatory support) will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying eco-driving behaviors. Integrating mixed-methods approaches, including qualitative assessments of drivers’ experiences and perceptions, may further enhance the design and effectiveness of interventions.
