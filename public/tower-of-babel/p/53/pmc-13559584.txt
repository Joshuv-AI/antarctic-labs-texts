Combining Napsin A expression with circulating tumor cell counts for enhanced identification of pulmonary adenocarcinoma in ground-glass nodules
Authors: Suhong Ruan, Hongjie Yu, Zhenyuan Song, Xiang Zhu, Guangda Yuan, Hongya Xie, Chuanyu Zhang, Tengteng Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCirculating tumor cells (CTCs) have shown potential for early lung cancer diagnosis, but their sensitivity and isolation yield are currently limited. Napsin A is a sensitive and specific marker for adenocarcinoma diagnosis. Therefore, combining CTC detection with Napsin A expression analysis may provide an accurate method for identifying pulmonary adenocarcinoma in ground-glass nodules (GGNs), which could significantly improve patient survival rates. This study aims to evaluate the diagnostic value of detecting Napsin A‑positive CTCs for identifying pulmonary adenocarcinoma among patients with GGNs.MethodsPeripheral blood samples were collected from 91 patients who had been diagnosed with GGNs. CTCs were captured using an efficient nano-enrichment method, and Napsin A expression was detected through immunofluorescent labeling. The diagnostic value of these metrics for pulmonary adenocarcinoma in GGNs was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the area under the curve (AUC).ResultsOur findings demonstrate that the number of CTCs in patients in the malignant group was significantly higher than that in the benign group (P<0.001). ROC analysis reveals an AUC of 0.627 for CTC detection alone, with a sensitivity of 63.64% and specificity of 72.46%. However, when CTC counts were combined with Napsin A-positive expression (Napsin A+), the diagnostic performance markedly improved: the sensitivity and specificity for detecting lung adenocarcinoma among GGNs rose to 90.91% and 88.41%, respectively, and the AUC increased to 0.918, indicating substantially enhanced discriminatory power.ConclusionsThe combination of CTC counting and Napsin A+ expression analysis holds promise for the identification of pulmonary adenocarcinoma in GGNs.

Introduction

Lung cancer, a common malignancy worldwide, often lacks obvious symptoms, so most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage (1,2). Recent research has demonstrated that low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening can effectively detect lung cancer at an early stage and significantly reduce mortality rates associated with this disease (2,3). As LDCT becomes more widely used, the detection rate of indeterminate pulmonary nodules has also increased. Currently, the diagnosis of benign or malignant pulmonary ground-glass nodules (GGNs) primarily depends on computed tomography (CT) characteristics and the experience of radiologists, which have certain limitations. GGN, characterized by a mildly increased density on CT images with visible bronchial and vascular texture, represents a non-specific imaging finding that can be caused by various pathologies, including inflammatory lesions, limited fibrosis, hemorrhage, atypical adenomatous hyperplasia, and adenocarcinoma (4-6). Although the size, shape, and margins of the GGN are significant in identifying benign or malignant lesion, the accuracy is not high (7,8). Furthermore, there are differences in the interpretation of results by clinicians, and no definitive conclusions regarding biopsy or surgery can be drawn based solely on CT findings in patients with GGN.

Numerous studies and clinical observations have consistently demonstrated that the vast majority of patients with pathologically confirmed GGNs exhibit early-stage lung adenocarcinoma. Napsin A, an aspartic protease, has been identified as a specific marker for lung adenocarcinoma, exhibiting no expression in squamous cell carcinoma or small cell carcinoma (9-11). Lee et al. proposed that the expression of Napsin A could potentially serve as an indicator for surgical intervention in cases of lung adenocarcinoma (12). Furthermore, research has validated that Napsin A, which is specifically expressed in lung adenocarcinoma, can be used to distinguish lung adenocarcinoma from metastatic cancer, and its expression is positively correlated with the degree of tumor progression (13).

In recent years, circulating tumor cells (CTCs) have emerged as novel biomarkers that play a pivotal role in the early diagnosis and prognosis of lung cancer (14-16). CTCs refer to tumor cells shed from the primary site and disseminated into the bloodstream, representing a direct determinant of tumor recurrence and metastasis (17-19). Notably, CTC detection has demonstrated promising potential as a liquid biopsy method for diagnosing, prognosticating, and monitoring therapy response in lung cancer patients. These assays serve as complementary tests to traditional imaging techniques and other established biomarkers (20). However, most studies on CTCs primarily focus on screening, diagnosis, and treatment guidance for intermediate/advanced stage lung cancer cases; limited attention has been given to early/intermediate stage lung cancers. The reason may be that accurate detection of CTCs in early-stage lung cancers remains a great challenge, and their low abundance in circulation reduces the sensitivity of diagnosis (21,22).

In this current study, we investigate the utility of two markers, Napsin A and CTCs, for distinguishing pulmonary adenocarcinoma in GGNs, while also analyzing clinical characteristics and diagnostic efficacy. This research may address the existing gap in the early identification of pulmonary adenocarcinoma in GGNs and provide more effective strategies for clinical treatment. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1155/rc).

Methods

Patients and blood samples

A total of 131 patients who underwent lung LDCT examination at the Suzhou hospital between October 2022 and June 2023 were enrolled in this study. The final diagnosis of the pulmonary nodules was determined based on postoperative pathological reports. The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (I) severe cardiac, hepatic, pulmonary, or renal insufficiency or psychiatric disorders; (II) a history of malignant tumors within the past three years; (III) incomplete patient information; (IV) abnormalities in sample collection or handling, such as hemolysis or low cell counts. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of Affiliated Suzhou Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) (No. K-2022-104-K01). Verbal informed consent for publication was obtained from all participants. Two 7.5 mL whole blood samples were collected from each patient using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) vacuum blood collection tubes (CYTOMARK, UK) prior to any treatment. The blood samples were stored at 4 ℃ and processed within 48 hours.

Substrate preparation

The substrate preparation and CTC detection were conducted following the procedures outlined in our previously published research (23). The substrates were prepared as follows: Corning 60-mm Petri dishes were subjected to a 12-minute plasma detergent wash. Subsequently, 1 mL of 3% APTES (3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane) solution, prepared in ethanol, was added to each dish and incubated in the dark for 1 hour. Finally, the substrates underwent six rinses with deionized pure water followed by oven drying at 80 ℃ for 1 hour.

CTC capture and detection

The CTC capture and detection procedures were conducted as follows. Approximately 7.5 mL of blood samples were gently mixed with 1× red blood cell lysis buffer at a ratio of 1:10 and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 200 g for 5 min, and the resulting precipitate was re-suspended in 1% FPBS. Subsequently, the precipitate (cells) was added to the substrate and incubated initially at 37 ℃ for 1 hour, followed by an additional incubation at 4 ℃ for 10 minutes.

After replacing the liquid with a solution of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), incubation continued for another 10 minutes. Next, the PFA was replaced with pre-cooled methanol, and incubation took place at −20 ℃ for a duration of 10 minutes. Finally, the methanol was removed, and the substrate was rinsed 3 times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, 2% skim milk in PBS was added to the substrate for blocking. Then, 2% skim milk containing 1 µL anti-pan-cytokeratin-Cy5 (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA, Cat# ab7753) and 1 µL anti-CD45-FITC antibodies (Abcam, USA, Cat# ab40763) were added. Following overnight incubation at 4 ℃, the substrates were washed three times with PBS. Nuclei were labeled using 4, 6-Diamino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining solution (Abcam, USA, Cat# ab228549). The treated dishes were scanned by the high-throughput capture Cytell Cell Imaging System (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) to capture the images. The cells positive for DAPI (DAPI+) and CK (CK+), but negative for CD45 (CD45−), were identified as CTCs. The number of CTCs was counted by the supporting software. The test was considered positive if the CTC count was ≥3/7.5 mL of blood and negative if the CTC count was 0–2.

Detection of CTCs combined with Napsin A

The detection of CTCs in combination with Napsin A was performed using another 7.5 mL blood sample obtained from the patient, following the same protocol as described above, with the antibody concentration and incubation time remaining unchanged. The only difference was that, after blocking the substrate, 2% skim milk containing 1 µL anti-pan-cytokeratin-Cy5, 1 µL anti-CD45-FITC, and 1 µL anti-Napsin A-PE antibodies (Abcam, USA, Cat# ab319403) was added. After the same overnight incubation at 4 ℃, the plates were washed three times with PBS. DAPI was still used to label the nuclei. Based on meeting the above CTC criteria, if Napsin A is positive (Napsin A+), the cell is regarded as a Napsin A+ CTC. Napsin A+ CTC counts were calculated and recorded using the software accompanying the Cytell system.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 (Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (Boston, MA, USA). Differences between groups were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test and the Kruskal-Wallis H test, with a P value <0.05 considered statistically significant. Correlation analysis was conducted using Pearson correlation analysis, and the diagnostic value of CTC counts and Napsin A expression for GGNs was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Diagnostic performance indices, including the area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and cut-offs, were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software. All experiments for sample detection were conducted as single-time trials.

Results

Description of participants

The flow diagram illustrating this study design is provided in Figure 1. This figure shows the number of patients screened, eligible, and enrolled, as well as the sample detection process. The characteristics of all participants are presented in Table 1. A total of 131 participants were enrolled in the study, including 73 males and 58 females, with ages ranging from 25 to 87 years. Among them, there were 87 patients (66.41%) aged over 60 years. LDCT scans revealed pulmonary nodules in a total of 91 patients, accounting for 69.47% of the entire patient cohort. Of these patients, 48 were females and 43 were males. Pathological examination confirmed malignant tumors in sixty-nine patients, while the others had benign lung diseases. The median age was found to be approximately 62 years, with more than half (60.87%) being over the age of 60 years. The immunohistochemical analysis using TTF-1, Napsin A, and CK7, showed that 63 patients had lung adenocarcinoma while the remaining cases belonged to other types of lung cancer (Table 2).

Analysis of CTCs count and Napsin A expression detection

In this experiment, a total of 7.5 mL of blood was collected from the participants prior to any therapy, and the number of CTCs was quantified using the Cytell Cell Imaging System. The identification criteria for CTCs included positive nuclear staining with DAPI (blue), positive staining with CK (red), and negative staining with CD45 (green). A total of 29 samples exhibiting ≥3 CTCs per 7.5 mL of blood were classified as positive, among which 28 were pathologically confirmed as malignant lesions (Table 3). Fifty-two patients with positive Napsin A expression (Napsin A+) were confirmed as having malignant lesions by pathology, of whom one had squamous cell carcinoma and the others were lung adenocarcinoma. Napsin A+ CTCs exhibited an additional positive staining for Napsin A (yellow) compared to the identification criteria for CTCs alone. Fluorescence microscopy (Leica DMi8, Germany) was employed to visualize and count stained cells. Napsin A+ CTC counts were obtained through data acquisition was performed using accompanying software and subsequently subjected to statistical analysis (Figure 2).

Clinical characteristics associated with CTC and Napsin A+ CTC counts

The relationship between CTC counts and Napsin A+ CTC counts with clinicopathological characteristics was also assessed, including gender, age, and clinical diagnosis. There were no significant differences in the numbers of CTCs or Napsin A expression level among patients based on age or gender (Figure 3A-3D). There was no significant correlation between CTC count and Napsin A+ CTC count with age (r=−0.033, P=0.76; r=0.032, P=0.76), gender (r=−0.040, P=0.71; r=0.075, P=0.48), or smoking history (r=0.148, P=0.16; r=0.063, P=0.55). However, both CTC counts and Napsin A+ CTC counts were significantly higher in the malignant group than in the benign group (P<0.001), as depicted in Figure 3E,3F. Furthermore, lung adenocarcinomas exhibited a notably higher rate of CTC-positive patients compared to other malignancies (Figure 4A, P<0.001).

Comparison of the diagnostic value of CTC and Napsin A+ CTC in GGNs

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves using pathological diagnostic results as the gold standard were plotted to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of the CTC and Napsin A+ CTC in GGNs. The diagnostic performance indices, including the area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and cut-offs, are presented in Table 4. Sensitivity assesses the ability of a biomarker to correctly identify patients who actually have a certain disease, while specificity examines the ability of the biomarker to correctly classify patients as not having the disease. And the AUC of the ROC curve represents the discriminatory power of the curve, with values ranging from 0.5 (no discrimination) to 1.0 (perfect discrimination), along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Based on the ROC curve analysis of CTC counts (Figure 4B), the optimal cutoff value yielded a sensitivity of 63.64% and a specificity of 72.46%. The AUC for CTC detection was calculated as 0.743 (95% CI: 0.638–0.848; P<0.001). Building upon these findings, we explored the combination of CTCs with the tumor biomarker Napsin A to further enhance diagnostic efficiency. First, information of the two predictor variables was integrated using binary logistic regression to compute a new continuous variable—the prediction probability (p). Subsequently, this prediction probability was employed as a new test variable in ROC analysis to assess its diagnostic performance. The results indicated that the AUC of the ROC curve for the Napsin A+ CTCs was 0.918 (95% CI: 0.845–0.990; P<0.001), and the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of this model for pulmonary adenocarcinoma in GGNs were 90.91% and 88.41%, respectively (Figure 4C).

Diagnostic value of Napsin A+ CTC in GGN with different degrees of pathological invasion

The degree of pathological invasion can be classified into atypical adenomatoid hyperplasia (AAH, a precancerous lesion), adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), and invasive adenocarcinoma (IAC). All GGN patients were stratified base on the degree of pathological invasion. Stratification 1 consisted of patients with pre-invasive lesions (AAH and AIS) and those with invasive lesions (MIA and IAC). Stratification 2 included patients with indolent lesions (AAH, AIS, and MIA) and those with IAC. ROC analysis across the different stratification demonstrated that Napsin A+ CTC had high diagnostic efficacy in differentiating pre-invasive from invasive lesions, with an AUC of 0.864 (95% CI: 0.776–0.952) (Figure 5A). Additionally, it showed only moderate diagnostic performance in distinguishing indolent GGNs from IAC with invasive potential (AUC =0.637; 95% CI: 0.519–0.755) (Figure 5B); and increasing the sample size may improve diagnostic performance.

Discussion

Lung cancer is the primary cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide (24-26). Early diagnosis plays a pivotal role in improving the prognosis of cancer patients. With the widespread implementation of LDCT for early lung screening, an increasing number of GGNs are being detected, causing concern among patients (27). Regrettably, there is currently no standardized guidance available for GGN surgery; therefore, preoperative identification of pulmonary adenocarcinoma within GGNs is imperative to assist clinicians in selecting appropriate treatment strategies for individual patients (28).

Currently, CTC detection has demonstrated high accuracy in the assessment of pulmonary nodules, making it a focal point of recent diagnostic research (29). Nevertheless, its low detection rate restricts clinical application (30). Despite the possibility of CTC release into the circulation at an early stage of the tumor development, lung cancer’s widespread epithelial-mesenchymal transition deprives tumor cells of epithelial markers, rendering the CTC detection approach ineffective (31). Therefore, it is crucial to develop strategies that can enhance CTC detection for early diagnosis, recurrence monitoring, and treatment assessment in patients with lung cancer. To investigate the diagnostic effectiveness of CTCs in patients with GGNs, we quantified CTC counts and conducted necessary analyses. The results demonstrated no significant association between CTC counts and age or gender (r=−0.033, P=0.76; r=−0.040, P=0.71), which is consistent with previous studies (32,33). ROC analysis revealed that the AUC for CTC detection was greater than 0.5, suggesting that CTC detection had certain diagnostic efficacy. Furthermore, we observed large differences in the distribution of CTC counts among GGN patients (as shown in Table 3), indicating that CTC detection may assist clinicians in risk assessment and personalized treatment for these patients.

Combination of tumor biomarkers and CTC counts can enhance the sensitivity and specificity of early diagnosis, which has been confirmed by studies (20). In recent years, many predictive models utilizing combinations of different tumor biomarkers have been proposed and investigated (34,35). However, the optimal combination approach for the early diagnosis of malignant tumors is still up for debate. Napsin A, considered as a marker for lung adenocarcinoma, may serve as a surgical signal for the condition (12). Moreover, Napsin A is specifically expressed in primary lung adenocarcinomas, aiding in distinguishing them from metastatic lung carcinomas. Its expression positively correlates with the progression of primary lung adenocarcinomas (13). Therefore, we further examined the clinicopathological information of the patients and found that CTC count and Napsin A expression were statistically different between patients with malignant and benign lesions (P<0.001). Additionally, there were significant variations in CTC counts across different pathological types (P<0.01). These findings are generally consistent with existing literature and further support the feasibility of combining CTCs with tumor biomarkers such as Napsin A (36-38). Consequently, we employed Napsin A+ CTC as our final diagnostic model.

Our study implied that this model performed well in the identification of pulmonary adenocarcinoma in GGNs. The sensitivity and specificity of this diagnostic model were as high as 90.91% and 88.41%, respectively. Nevertheless, the diagnostic sensitivity of CTC detection alone was low (63.64%), which may be attributable to EMT-mediated suppression of EpCAM expression and the low abundance of CTCs in circulation—both factors that can lead to false-negative results. The marked increase in sensitivity of Napsin A+ CTC may be due to the fact that Napsin A expression is not affected by the EMT program and remains stably expressed in tumor cells with impaired epithelial phenotypes. Combined detection of CTCs and Napsin A overcomes the limitations of low sensitivity associated with CTC detection alone. As the first study to apply Napsin A+ CTC in the detection of pulmonary adenocarcinoma in GGNs, its diagnostic performance was satisfactory (with an AUC of 0.918). Previous studies mainly focused on proving that CTC is an effective biomarker in lung cancer diagnosis. Our study also showed that the Napsin A+ CTC approach exhibits superior sensitivity, specificity, and AUC values compared to CTC alone. More importantly, it has a high diagnostic efficacy in differentiating pre-invasive lesions from MIA/IAC, with an AUC of 0.864 (95% CI: 0.776–0.952). These findings demonstrate that the Napsin A+ CTC model might greatly increase the diagnostic effectiveness for early lung cancer in patients with GGNs.

This study had certain limitations. The distribution of the two groups in the sample was imbalanced, with nearly three times as many patients in the malignant group compared to the benign group among all GGNs diagnosed by LDCT. Further validation of the model is necessary using a larger sample size. Second, a minor amount of CTCs were also found in the benign group of patients. According to previous studies, these CTCs observed in benign patients may be attributed to chromosomal variants caused by lung inflammation (39). Future research should investigate the long-term dynamics of CTCs to determine their potential progression into lung cancer or other pulmonary diseases, despite the possibility of false-positive CTCs may be rapidly eliminated by the immune system.

Conclusions

Our study revealed that early‐stage lung cancers manifesting as GGNs exhibit distinct CTC levels across different pathologic subtypes. The Napsin A+ CTC diagnostic model effectively distinguishes pulmonary adenocarcinoma among GGNs, potentially addressing the current gap in early lung cancer detection and enabling more effective clinical management strategies to improve patient prognosis.
