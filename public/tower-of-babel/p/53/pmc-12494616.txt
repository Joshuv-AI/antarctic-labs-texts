Association of appendectomy with disease phenotype and clinical course in Crohn's disease: results from two cohorts
Authors: Y Chen, Y Cheng, T Song, C Zhang, H Peng, Y Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe appendix, as a component of the digestive system, plays a role in intestinal immunity.ObjectiveTo investigate the association between appendectomy history and disease phenotype/progression in Crohn’s disease patients.DesignTwo cohorts from a single center.PatientsPatients with Crohn’s disease diagnosed between 2011 and 2021, including those without surgery and those undergoing their first surgery for Crohn’s disease.MethodsPatients were divided into surgical and non-surgical cohorts, each further split into appendectomy and non-appendectomy groups.ResultsIn the non-surgical cohort, significant phenotypic disparities were observed between appendectomy-only and non-appendectomy groups across Montreal classification parameters, including age (p < 0.001), location (p = 0.03), and behavior (p = 0.01), with reduced perianal lesion prevalence in appendectomy patients (15% (9/60) vs. 35.7% (162/454), p = 0.001). Appendectomy patients exhibited later disease onset (IQR36 vs. 24 years, p < 0.001) and diagnosis (IQR37 vs. 26 years, p < 0.001). In the surgical cohort, significant differences emerged among non-appendectomy, appendectomy-only, and ileocecal resection groups in Montreal classification parameters: age at diagnosis (p = 0.014), location (p < 0.001), and behavior (p = 0.003). Disease progression timelines differed markedly, with later onset (IQR 29 vs. 27 vs. 25 years, p < 0.001), diagnosis (IQR 31 vs. 30 vs. 27 years, p < 0.001), and surgery (IQR 35 vs. 33 vs. 31 years, p < 0.001) observed in appendectomy-only patients. Surgical management varied significantly, including diagnosis-to-surgery intervals (mean 3.4 vs. 2.6 vs. 3.7 years, p < 0.001), perianal lesion (29.3% (123/420) vs. 24.4% (39/160) vs. 35.3% (173/490), p = 0.02), and one-stage surgery (36.2% (152/420) vs. 75.6% (120/160) vs. 66.1% (324/490), p < 0.001).LimitationsRetrospective analysis with potential data biases.ConclusionDespite notable differences in disease phenotype, appendectomy does not seem to influence the clinical course of Crohn's disease. However, it seems to be associated with the lower risk of perianal disease and alleviates the severity of their condition.Keywords: Crohn’s disease, Appendectomy, Surgery, Montreal classification, Postoperative recurrence

Introduction

The incidence and prevalence of Crohn’s disease have been steadily increasing worldwide. This disease typically manifests between the ages of 20 and 40, with a smaller peak occurring between 50 and 60 years of age. It is more prevalent in developed countries than in developing ones, and in urban areas compared to rural regions [1]. In developing Asian countries, the annual incidence of Crohn’s disease is rising in conjunction with economic development and improved living conditions, reaching 0.54 cases per 100,000 people [2].

During human development, the appendix first appears at the junction of the small and large intestines in the 5th week of gestation [3]. Its tubular, closed anatomical structure, narrow lumen, and location at the base of the cecum protect it from fecal flow and pathogenic microorganisms, thereby promoting the accumulation of biofilms within the lumen [4]. The appendix plays an indispensable role in maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis and barrier function [5]. Under conditions of genetic susceptibility, abnormal antigen initiation and dysregulation of the appendiceal immune pathway—possibly in response to specific microbial antigens—may contribute to the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis [3]. Over the past few decades, the role of the appendix in the etiology of inflammatory bowel diseases has been extensively examined in cohort studies [6–10].

Appendectomy may also influence the clinical course of inflammatory bowel disease. Some studies have found that the risk of total colectomy in patients with ulcerative colitis is lower among those who have undergone appendectomy compared to those who have not [11]. Additionally, the need for immunomodulatory and biologic therapies is significantly reduced following appendectomy. Appendectomy induced clinical responses in nearly half of patients with refractory ulcerative colitis, with a substantial proportion achieving endoscopic remission [12]. Appendectomy has consistently been associated with a reduced risk of developing ulcerative colitis, whereas this association is less clear in patients with Crohn’s disease [7]. The risk of developing Crohn’s disease after appendectomy appears to be higher than in non-appendectomy controls [13], but this association may reflect diagnostic bias, such as early ileocecal Crohn’s disease being misdiagnosed as appendicitis and subsequently diagnosed as Crohn’s disease after appendectomy [14]. In Crohn’s disease, fewer studies have examined the relationship between appendectomy and disease clinical course, and the results are inconsistent. Some studies indicate an increased risk of intestinal resection following appendectomy [7, 15], while others find no significant difference in resection rates or medical management [16, 17]. The appendix appears to have a promoting effect in the early stages of Crohn’s disease, but it does not alter the final outcomes for patients [18].

This study aims to investigate the association between appendectomy history and disease phenotype/progression in patients with Crohn’s disease, specifically focusing on the time from diagnosis to the initial use of biologics and the occurrence of postoperative surgical recurrence.

Methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients with Crohn's disease fulfilling the WHO-endorsed diagnostic criteria were retrospectively screened at a tertiary referral center between 2011 and 2021. Patients with Crohn's disease at our centre within the time frame were screened, and patients who had not undergone surgery (n = 514) and those undergoing their first Crohn's surgery at our institution (n = 1070) were included. We excluded patients with a history of intestinal resection at other hospitals (n = 562), those who had undergone temporary stoma diversion or staged stoma reversal for Crohn's disease (n = 164), and those who had received endoscopic stricture dilation (n = 65) (Fig. 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for patients

Study cohorts

Patients were classified into a surgical cohort and a non-surgical cohort based on their history of surgical resection for Crohn’s disease. Each cohort was further subdivided into an appendectomy group and a non-appendectomy group. Additionally, since ileocecal resection entails the removal of the appendix, patients in the appendectomy group from the surgical cohort were further divided into an ileocecal resection group and an appendectomy-only group.

Data collection

We collected comprehensive patient data, which included gender, age at first surgery, disease location, disease behavior, previous medications, age at first symptom onset, age at definitive diagnosis, age at surgery, family history, smoking history, and medication usage. Disease phenotypes were classified according to the Montreal classification system (Table 1). Additionally, postoperative surgical recurrence, defined as a reoperation for recurrent Crohn’s disease, was documented within the surgical cohort.

Statistical analysis

Chi-square tests were employed to evaluate differences in baseline characteristics between groups for categorical variables, while Fisher’s exact test was utilized when counts were low (< 5). When appropriate, the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test was applied for numerical variables. All data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), mean and standard deviation (SD), or percentages (%) of the total cohort. Kaplan-Meier and log-rank tests were used only in the surgical cohort to evaluate time to surgical recurrence. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant; p-values and confidence intervals (CI) were calculated at the 95% confidence level. Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM).

Result

Surgical cohort vs. non-surgical cohort

A total of 1070 patients underwent their first intestinal resection for Crohn's disease, comprising 420 in the non-appendectomy group, 160 in the appendectomy-history group, and 490 in the ileocecal resection group. Additionally, 514 patients were included in the non-surgical cohorts, with 454 in the non-appendectomy group and 60 in the appendectomy group (Fig. 1). Both cohorts demonstrated comparable female representation (34.2% in each group); however, a statistically significant difference was observed in the median age at diagnosis (IQR 29 vs. 27 years, p = 0.02).

Significant disparities were observed between the surgical and non-surgical cohorts in Montreal classification categories: age at diagnosis (p = 0.002), disease location (p < 0.001), and disease behavior (p < 0.001). In contrast, comparable proportions were noted for perianal lesions (31.2% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.41), history of appendectomy (15% vs. 11.7%, p = 0.08), family history of inflammatory bowel disease (0.5% vs. 0.2%, p = 0.70), and extraintestinal manifestations (9.7% vs. 8.2%, p = 0.32). Furthermore, smoking prevalence was significantly lower in the surgical cohort compared to the non-surgical cohort (7.7% vs. 11.9%, p = 0.008), as detailed in Table 2.

Disease phenotype in non-surgical cohort

Within the non-surgical cohort, significant disparities were observed between the appendectomy-only group and non-appendectomy group in Montreal classification categories: age at diagnosis (p < 0.001), disease location (p = 0.03), and disease behavior (p = 0.01), as well as in the prevalence of perianal lesions (15% vs. 35.7%, p = 0.001). In contrast, no significant differences were noted in sex distribution (female: 36.7% vs. 33.9%, p = 0.67), smoking rates (14% vs. 15%, p = 0.85), extraintestinal manifestations (5.0% vs. 8.6%, p = 0.34), or medication regimens. Disease progression in the surgical cohort

Marked variations were also evident in clinical timelines: age of disease onset (IQR 29 vs. 27 vs. 25 years, p < 0.001), age at diagnosis (IQR 31 vs. 30 vs. 27 years, p < 0.001), and age at surgery (IQR 35 vs. 33 vs. 31 years, p < 0.001). Notably, the appendectomy-only group consistently exhibited intermediate proportions across all Montreal classification subtypes compared to the other two groups. Furthermore, distinct patterns emerged in surgical management and complications: diagnosis-to-surgery interval (mean 3.4 vs. 2.6 vs. 3.7 years, p < 0.001), prevalence of perianal lesions (29.3% vs. 24.4% vs. 35.3%, p = 0.02), and rates of one-stage procedures (36.2% vs. 75.6% vs. 66.1%, p < 0.001). These findings highlight the unique transitional characteristics of the appendectomy-only subgroup. Additionally, a log-rank test analysis, which defined postoperative surgical recurrence as the endpoint event, indicated no significant statistical difference in surgical recurrence among the three groups (P = 0.50), as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Discussion

This study found that in the non-surgery cohort, the proportion of patients developing perianal disease was significantly lower following appendectomy; however, the time to accurate diagnosis was noticeably prolonged. Among the surgical patients, the characteristics of the appendectomy-only group were intermediate between those of the non-appendectomy group and the ileocecal resection group. Notably, the appendectomy-only group exhibited the lowest proportion of perianal disease, a finding that aligns with the conclusions drawn from the non-surgery cohort.

Previous studies have demonstrated that appendectomy is linked to greater proximal intestinal involvement, an elevated risk of strictures, and a decreased risk of perianal fistulas, without influencing the overall severity of the disease [16]. Other research has indicated a higher prevalence of penetrating disease phenotypes among patients with a history of appendectomy [19]. Furthermore, the disease course in individuals who have undergone appendectomy generally tends to be milder [20].

In the non-surgery cohort, the proportion of patients with L1 significantly increased following appendectomy. This increase may be attributed to the challenges in accurately diagnosing the disease at the time of the appendectomy, which could influence or delay the correct diagnosis [21]. Furthermore, there was a trend toward increased B3 involvement, potentially due to the bias introduced by the more prevalent penetrating phenotype in ileocecal disease [22]. In contrast, the difference in B2 involvement was more pronounced, indicating a higher incidence of fibrosis following appendectomy. However, the factors driving the progression from inflammation to fibrosis and strictures in Crohn’s disease remain poorly understood, as does the reason why not all patients with intestinal inflammation develop strictures [23]. This observation suggests that the body’s reparative processes may outweigh tissue damage, consistent with the relatively mild disease progression noted after appendectomy [20].

In the surgical cohort, the duration from diagnosis to surgery was significantly shorter in the appendectomy-only group compared to the ileocecal resection group (mean 2.6 vs. 3.7, P < 0.001), contradicting the expectation that ileocecal disease would be more likely to necessitate surgical intervention [24]. However, there were no significant differences between the two groups in the age at surgery (IQR 33 vs. 31 years, P = 0.21). Furthermore, survival analysis regarding disease recurrence revealed no differences between the two groups, indicating that appendectomy does not influence surgical outcomes.

This study included all patients with Crohn's disease treated at our center, utilizing a substantial number of surgical patients to create two cohorts: a surgery cohort and a non-surgery cohort, based on whether they underwent surgery for intestinal complications. The mutual validation between these two cohorts enhances the reliability of the results. In patients diagnosed with Crohn's disease, early appendectomy may prevent the development of perianal disease, thereby improving patient quality of life [25]. Future research could investigate the regulatory role of the appendix in the onset of Crohn's disease, contributing to a deeper understanding of its pathogenesis. This study is a retrospective analysis, and the data may be incomplete, inaccurate, or inconsistent, potentially introducing bias into the study results and affecting the reliability of the conclusions. Since the data are derived from existing cases, selection bias may be present in the sample. The extended timespan over which the cases were collected could diminish the relevance of the data. Furthermore, during postoperative follow-up for surgical recurrence, there may be instances of patient loss to follow-up or missing critical information, which could impact the accuracy of the study results.

Notably, the appendectomy-only group comprises both patients who have undergone a previous appendectomy and those who had their appendix removed during the initial surgery at our hospital. The history of previous appendectomy was obtained from the patients' medical records, supplemented by intraoperative findings. Although there may be recall bias concerning the precise timing of the previous appendectomy, the status of the appendix removal is confirmed. Our study found higher smoking rates in the non-surgical cohort; this may be because surgical patients are strongly encouraged to quit smoking at our center. However, we could not rule out the potential effect of smoking on disease phenotype and surgical indication [26, 27].

Conclusions

In patients with Crohn's disease, appendectomy is associated with a reduced incidence of perianal disease; however, it may delay the diagnosis of the condition and is linked to an increased risk of stricturing and penetrating complications. Importantly, appendectomy does not influence the timing of surgery or the rate of postoperative surgical recurrence. For Crohn's disease patients who require intestinal surgery, the decision to perform a concurrent appendectomy to mitigate the risk of perianal complications should be made collaboratively between the surgeon and the patient, although additional data may be necessary to substantiate this approach. Future research should prioritize investigating the regulatory role of the appendix in the early stages of Crohn's disease onset rather than its impact on disease recurrence.
