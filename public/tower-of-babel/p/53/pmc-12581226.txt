Genome-wide identification and analysis of the abiotic stress responsiveness of the heat shock protein 90 gene family in Medicago sativa L
Authors: Hao Liu, Yuqi Zhang, Xianyang Li, Li Zhao, Xinyue Ma, Fei He, Mingna Li, Xue Wang, Ruicai Long, Junmei Kang, Qingchuan Yang, Lin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeat shock protein 90 (HSP90) is a highly conserved class of cellular chaperone proteins that are widely present in plants and are involved in the response to various biotic and abiotic stresses. The HSP90 gene has been extensively studied across numerous species, and several investigations on alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) have demonstrated that HSP90 gene is implicated in plant-pathogen interactions. However, the HSP90 gene has not been fully identified in alfalfa.ResultsIn this study, the HSP90 genes in alfalfa were identified and characterized throughout the genome. A total of 29 MsHSP90s were identified from the alfalfa “Xinjiangdaye” genome and were categorized into three subgroups, Ⅰ, Ⅱ, and Ⅲ, with 15, 9, and 5 members in each group, respectively. The MsHSP90 family members in each subgroup have similar intron and exon structures, and motif analysis revealed that the MsHSP90 gene family is highly conserved. Analysis of RNA-seq data of MsHSP90 genes in different tissues (roots, elongated stems, pre-elongated stems, leaves, flowers, and nodules) revealed that the expression of three MsHSP90 genes (MsHSP90-4, MsHSP90-14, MsHSP90-18) had tissue specificity and that 17 MsHSP90 genes were expressed in all six tissues, but their expression levels varied significantly. Analysis of MsHSP90 RNA-seq data under three stresses (salt, drought, cold) revealed that four MsHSP90 genes (MsHSP90-10, MsHSP90-11, MsHSP90-16, and MsHSP90-27) respond to all three stresses. In this study, the HSP90 gene family of alfalfa was comprehensively identified and analyzed, and the candidate genes related to adverse stress conditions were identified by analyzing RNA-seq data.ConclusionThe findings of this study provide critical information for further analysis of the HSP90 gene function in alfalfa, laying a foundation for enhancing its stress resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07520-8.Keywords: Medicago sativa L., MsHSP90 gene family, Genome-wide analysis, Tissue-specific expression, Abiotic stress response

Background

Plants are exposed to changing biotic and abiotic stresses during growth [1]. Stresses such as drought, salinity, and low and high temperatures can disrupt cellular homeostasis in plants, affecting normal plant growth [2, 3]. During evolution, plants have developed many regulatory mechanisms to cope with stress [4]. In response to abiotic stresses such as high temperatures, many molecular chaperone proteins are rapidly synthesized in the cell to prevent disruption of intracellular homeostasis [5]. Molecular chaperone proteins can bind to short fragments of protein substrates, stabilize unfolded or partially folded proteins, and prevent these proteins from aggregating and degrading, thus maintaining cellular homeostasis [6, 7].

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) were first discovered in 1962, when Ritossa identified a new protein in Drosophila subjected to heat stress [8]. In later studies, this protein was named HSP [9], and further research showed that in addition to heat stress, other factors, such as cold and hypoxia, also induced the production of HSP in cells [10, 11]. HSPs were first discovered in plants by Barnett in 1979, when heat stress was applied to tobacco and pea tissue culture cells [12]. Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are a highly conserved class of molecular chaperones [13, 14]. On the basis of their approximate molecular weights (MWs), these proteins can be categorized into five major groups: the HSP100, HSP90, HSP70, HSP60, and small HSP (sHSP) families [15]. Each class of HSPs has a specific function [16]. HSP90 is an evolutionarily conserved heat stress protein distributed in the cytoplasm, endoplasmic reticulum, chloroplasts, and mitochondria [17], accounting for 1%–2% of total cellular proteins [18], and its content can increase by 4%–6% when subjected to stress [19]. HSP90 is an ATP-regulated dimerized chaperone that is composed of three highly conserved structural domains: a C-terminal domain of approximately 25 kDa bound to the substrate, a 35 kDa intermediate domain, and a 12 kDa N-terminal domain at the ATP binding site [20]. The dimer is in an open state when it is not bound to ATP. When ATPase is activated, ATP binds to the N-terminal region of HSP90, and the conformation changes. The two separated N-termini temporarily unite to close HSP90, and the substrate proteins are wrapped in the clip at the same time. After the hydrolysis of ATP, the two monomers are separated, and ADP and Pi are released [21, 22].

In recent years, HSP90 has been investigated in many species, as various plant reference genomes have been elucidated [23–25]. Many studies have shown that HSP90 has an important role in plant growth and development and in response to biotic and abiotic stresses [26]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, seven AtHSP90 genes were identified [27]. AtHSP90.5 is essential for chloroplast biogenesis and embryogenesis. Further evidence also suggested that AtHSP90.5 contributes to the catabolism of VIPP1 to form and/or maintain cyst-like membranes [28]. In the absence of heat shock, cytosolic HSP90 exerts a negative regulatory effect on HsfA1. Conversely, upon heat shock, cytosolic HSP90 is transiently inactivated, which may subsequently lead to the activation of HsfA1 [29]. In Oryza sativa, nine OsHSP90 genes were identified [30]. The overexpression of rHSP90 promoted salt tolerance, heat tolerance and drought stress tolerance in rice [31]. The OsHSP90-2 and OsHSP90-4 genes presented increased expression under heat, drought and salt stress [32]. In Glycine max, 12 GmHSP90 genes were identified [33], of which the overexpression of five typical genes (GmHSP90A2, GmHsp90A4, GmHsp90B1, GmHsp90C1.1, and GmHsp90C2.1) reduced the damage caused by abiotic stresses in plants [33].

Alfalfa is one of the most important high-quality legume forages worldwide [34], it has the advantages of high protein content and high fiber content and plays a critical role in the development of animal husbandry. As alfalfa is routinely subjected to a wide range of environmental stresses during cultivation, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying its stress resilience represents a crucial area of research [35]. HSP90 has been characterized and validated in many species [27, 30, 33], and several investigations on alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) have demonstrated that HSP90 gene is implicated in plant-pathogen interactions. However, the HSP90 gene has not been fully identified in alfalfa. The HSP90 family in alfalfa was comprehensively analyzed via genome-wide screening, phylogenetic analysis, gene structure and conserved motif analysis, chromosomal localization and covariance analysis, as well as RNA-seq data analysis of alfalfa HSP90 gene in different tissues and under different stress conditions. The results of this study provide important information for further analysis of the functions of HSP90 and its application in the molecular breeding of alfalfa.

Materials and methods

Identification and analysis of HSP90 genes in Medicago sativa genome

The alfalfa genome was obtained from the Alfalfa Genome Project (https://fgshare.com/projects/whole_genome_sequencing_and_assembly_of_medicago_sativa/66380) [36]. The Arabidopsis thaliana HSP90 protein was used as a BLAST template (TAIR database: https://www.arabidopsis.org/). Hidden Markov modeling (HMM) was used to obtain a profile of the HSP90 domain from the Pfam database (PF00183) to further eliminate redundancy [37]. The theoretical molecular weights (MWs), isoelectric points (pIs) and instability indices of MsHSP90 were calculated via the ExPASy database (http://www.ExPASy.org/). The subcellular localization of the MsHSP90 gene was predicted via the WoLF PSORT website (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/).

Phylogenetic and structural analysis of the HSP90 family in Medicago sativa

Phylogenetic trees were constructed on the basis of the HSP90 protein sequences of Medicago sativa, Arabidopsis thaliana, Glycine max (Glycine max genome database: https://www.soybase.org/), and Oryza sativa (RGAP database: http://rice.uga.edu/) using MEGA11, the maximum likelihood (ML) method and 1000 bootstrap repeats. The online website MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) was used to analyze the MsHSP90 protein motifs, and the number of motifs was set to 10. The conserved structural domains of MsHSP90 were predicted from the NCBI conserved domain database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/). Structural information on the MsHSP90 gene was obtained from the alfalfa genome Gff annotation file. The results of the above analyses were visualized via TBtools-II (v2.110) software [38].

Chromosomal localization, gene duplication events and collinearity analysis

Chromosomal distribution information for the MsHSP90 gene was obtained from a GFF file of the Medicago sativa genome and visualized with TBtools software [38]. The MCScanX tool was used to analyze collinearity information of the HSP90 gene within and between species [39]. Tandem duplication occurs when two or more genes are located within 200 kb of the same chromosome. The results were visualized via TBtools.

Analysis of promoter cis-acting elements

TBtools was used to extract 2000 bp fragments upstream of the MsHSP90 gene promoters, and the cis-acting elements were identified via the online website PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) and visualized via TBtools.

Expression profile analysis of RNA-seq data

RNA-seq data from six alfalfa tissues (roots, elongated stems, pre-elongated stems, leaves, flowers, and nodules) (SRP055547) as well as RNA-seq data related to the response to salt, drought, and cold stress (SRR7091780-SRR7091794 and SRR7160313-SRR7160357) were downloaded from the NCBI database for analysis [40, 41]. TBtools was used to visualize the data.

Plant materials, growth and stress conditions, and RT‒qPCR analysis

The alfalfa variety used in this study was Zhongmu No. 1, which was obtained from the Institute of Animal Science of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Seeds were treated at 4 °C for 3 days and then cultured in a greenhouse for 2 weeks under a 16/8 h light cycle, 70–80% relative humidity and 24 °C/20°C day/night temperature conditions. Salt stress was simulated via NaCl (250 mM) treatment. The root tip samples were collected, 0 h was used as the control group, and 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 h were used as the sampling time points. Mannitol (400 mM) was used to simulate drought stress, and root tip samples were collected at 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 h. Plants subjected to cold stress treatment at 4 °C for 0 h were used as the control group, and 2, 6, 24 and 48 h were selected as the sampling time points for leaf samples. Three replicates were used for each stress treatment, and five seedlings were pooled in each replicate. Untreated control plants were cultured normally.

The primers used in the study were designed via Primer 5.0 software. Total RNA was extracted from all the samples via TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The corresponding cDNA was obtained via an EasyScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit [random primer (N9)]. SYBR Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa, Japan) and a 7500 real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) were used for the RT‒qPCR experiments. Three replicates were analyzed for each sample, and the data were standardized relative to alfalfa actin gene expression. Relative expression levels were calculated via the 2−ΔΔCT method [42], with Actin2 used as the internal reference.

As shown in Table 1, among the 29 MsHSP90 members, the number of amino acids encoding proteins ranged from 698 to 818, the MWs ranged from 80.18 kDa to 94.08 kDa, the pIs ranged from 4.79 to 5.34, and the instability indices varied from 33.67to 48.38. Moreover, the subcellular localization prediction of 29 MsHSP90 genes revealed that 15 MsHSP90 genes were predicted to be expressed in the cytoplasm, 5 MsHSP90 genes were predicted to be expressed in the endoplasmic reticulum, and 9 MsHSP90 genes were predicted to be expressed in chloroplasts or mitochondria.

Phylogenetic analysis of HSP90 genes in Medicago sativa

To gain a deeper understanding of the taxonomic and evolutionary relationships among MsHSP90 genes, we generated a phylogenetic tree with seven Arabidopsis HSP90 protein sequences, 13 soybean HSP90 protein sequences, eight rice HSP90 protein sequences, and 29 alfalfa HSP90 protein sequences (Fig. 2). On the basis of the clustering results as well as the structural domains, the MsHSP90 gene family was divided into three groups, in which groups I, II, and III contained 15, 9, and 5 MsHSP90 genes, respectively.

Analysis of the basic structure and conserved domains of HSP90 family member in Medicago sativa

To characterize the structure of the HSP90 family members in alfalfa, we analyzed 10 conserved motifs of the MsHSP90 protein via the online tool MEME. As shown in Figs. 3B and 4, all MsHSP90 proteins contained 10 motifs except for MsHSP90-26, which did not contain motif 6. These findings indicate that the HSP90 family is highly conserved in alfalfa.

Basic information about the motif of MsHSP90s

To analyze the structure of the MsHSP90 gene, we screened its conserved structural domains at the online site NCBI. As shown in Fig. 3C, each subgroup of MsHSP90 genes has a specific structure. In the first group, each gene contains 3–4 exons, and a conserved structural domain (HSP90 superfamily) was predicted via NCBI. In the second group, the number of exons is 18 to 20, two conserved structural domains are predicted at NCBI (PRK05218 and HtpG), and each gene contains one conserved structural domain. In the third group, the number of exons was 15, and two conserved structural domains (HATPase_Hsp90-like, HSP90) were predicted by NCBI for each gene.

Gene duplication events and collinearity analysis of HSP90 genes in Medicago sativa

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships of the MsHSP90 genes, gene duplication events were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 1, we identified 6 tandem duplication events involving 12 MsHSP90 genes, such as MsHSP90-5 and MsHSP90-6, which are located on chr1.3 (Table S2). In addition, as shown in Figs. 5, 27 segmental duplication events involving 19 MsHSP90 genes (Table S3), such as MsHSP90-1 and MsHSP90-3, which are located on chr1.1 and chr1.2, respectively, were detected. In summary, there were significantly more segmental duplication events than tandem duplication events during the evolution of the MsHSP90 family.

Collinearity analysis of HSP90 genes in Medicago sativa. The red line represents homology between genes

To further elucidate the evolutionary relationships of the HSP90 gene family in different crops, evolutionary relationships and collinearity between alfalfa and three species (Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula, and Glycine max) were predicted (Fig. 6). Twelve MsHSP90 genes were collinear with those of Arabidopsis thaliana, 16 MsHSP90 genes were collinear with those of Medicago truncatula, and 19 MsHSP90 genes were collinear with those of Glycine max. In addition, 12, 16 and 46 collinear gene pairs were detected in Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula and Glycine max, respectively. The alfalfa HSP90 gene is significantly more homologous in soybean than in the other two species.

Collinearity analysis of the MsHSP90 genes with those of Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula and Glycine max. The blue line represents homology between genes

Analysis of cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of HSP90 genes in Medicago sativa

The PlantCARE database was employed for the purpose of identifying the cis-acting elements present within the promoter region of the MsHSP90 genes. Fifteen elements related to growth and development, the hormone response and the stress response were ultimately selected for analysis (Fig. 7), and the complete promoter cis-acting element information is shown in Table S4. Among these elements, an anaerobic induction element (ARE) was present in most MsHSP90 genes (25), and alpha-amylase (A-box) was present in the fewest MsHSP90 genes (1). Analysis revealed that several MsHSP90 gene promoters contained cis-acting elements associated with abiotic stresses, such as drought-responsive (MBS), low-temperature-responsive (LTR), and others. These results suggest that MsHSP90 genes may be involved in growth and development, the hormone response, and the stress response in Medicago sativa.

Expression of HSP90 genes in different tissues of Medicago sativa

To elucidate the expression patterns of the MsHSP90 genes in different tissues, the transcriptomic data of MsHSP90 genes in six distinct tissues (roots, elongated stems, pre-elongated stems, leaves, flowers, and nodules) were obtained from a public database (Table S5). Analysis of tissue transcriptomic data for 29 MsHSP90 genes revealed that 24 of these genes could be expressed in at least one tissue and that 5 genes were not expressed in these 6 tissues (Fig. 8). The expression of three of the MsHSP90s presented tissue specificity, e.g., MsHSP90-4 was expressed only in flowers, and 17 MsHSP90s were expressed in six tissues, but presented significant differences in expression levels, e.g., MsHSP90-22 was expressed in all six tissues but presented significantly higher expression levels in leaves than in other tissues. In addition, 2 and 2 genes were expressed in 3 and 5 tissues, respectively.

Expression of HSP90 genes in Medicago sativa under abiotic stress

To further investigate the role of MsHSP90 in alfalfa under abiotic stress, RNA-seq data of Medicago sativa under three stresses (salt, drought, and cold) were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 9A-C, the expression of 11, 10, and 6 MsHSP90 genes significantly changed under salt, drought, and cold stress, respectively. As shown in Fig. 9D, five genes responded to only one stress, e.g., MsHSP90-21 responded to cold stress only. Five genes responded to two stresses, e.g., MsHSP90-5 responded to cold and salt stresses. Four genes responded to all three stresses (MsHSP90-10, MsHSP90-11, MsHSP90-16, and MsHSP90-27).

To validate the RNA-seq data, four MsHSP90 genes that exhibited responsiveness to all three stresses were selected for RT-qPCR analysis. The experimental primers are presented in Table S7. As shown in Fig. 10, the expression of MsHSP90-10/MsHSP90-11 exhibited a gradual decline under salt stress. In contrast, the expression of MsHSP90-16 initially increased and subsequently decreased, maintaining a level that was consistently higher than the control. Notably, the expression of MsHSP90-27 was significantly elevated relative to the control following stress. In response to drought stress, the expression of MsHSP90-10/MsHSP90-11 exhibited a gradual decline over time. With regard to cold stress, the expression levels of all four MsHSP90 genes reached their highest point at 48 h. These results align with the data obtained from RNA-seq.

Discussion

HSP90 gene family is very widely distributed and has been found in almost all studied eukaryotic species, such as Arabidopsis [27], soybean [32], rice [30], and tobacco [24], but has not been reported in alfalfa. In this study, 29 MsHSP90 genes were identified in the “Xinjiangdaye” genome of alfalfa. The number of HSP90 genes in alfalfa is much greater than that in Arabidopsis, soybean, rice and other crops, possibly because the “Xinjiangdaye” genome used in this study is a tetraploid genome [43]. In animal cells, there are four subgroups based on subcellular localization: cytosolic HSP90 (HSP90-α and HSP90-β), endoplasmic reticulum HSP90 (GRP94), chloroplast HSP90 and mitochondrial HSP90 [22]. In this study, subcellular localization prediction of the MsHSP90 gene revealed that in group I, all members were predicted to be in the cytoplasm and to contain a MEEVD motif at the C-terminus. In group II, all members were predicted to be in chloroplasts or mitochondria. In group III, all members were predicted to be in the endoplasmic reticulum, and the C-terminus contains a KDEL motif (Figure S1). These findings are the same as those of a previous study in Arabidopsis thaliana [27].

In this study, the Arabidopsis, soybean, rice, and alfalfa HSP90 protein sequences were used as templates to construct phylogenetic trees. HSP90 proteins in the same branch may play similar roles. For example, in Arabidopsis, overexpression of AtHSP90.2, AtHSP90.5, and AtHSP90.7 decreased tolerance to salt and drought stress but increased tolerance to high Ca2+ concentrations [44]. In soybean, GmHSP90A2 (Gm16G178800) responds to heat stress and positively regulates heat tolerance by interacting with GmHSP90A1 [45]. As shown in Fig. 2, AtHSP90.2, AtHSP90.5 and AtHSP90.7 belong to groups I, II and III, respectively, and GmHSP90A2 belongs to group I. It can be hypothesized that MsHSP90 genes, which are within the same subgroup as the above HSP90 genes, may have similar functions. Structural domain analysis of MsHSP90 demonstrates that the HSP90 protein exhibits a high degree of conservation in alfalfa, which may be related to the evolutionary and amplification patterns of the HSP90 gene. Tandem and segmental duplications are identified as the predominant mechanisms driving the expansion and diversification of plant gene families [46]. In MsHSP90, there are significantly more segmental duplication events than tandem duplication events, so the amplification and evolution of MsHSP90 mainly depend on segmental duplication events.

Studies of HSP90 genes in other species have demonstrated that they are not only associated with stress signaling; the folding of receptors, transcription factors and kinases; and physiological processes in plants but also contribute to cell survival under stress [26, 47, 48]. In this study, the cis-acting element on the promoter of the MsHSP90 gene not only responds to a variety of biotic or abiotic stresses, but also participates in the growth and development process of alfalfa, as well as in hormone secretion. The RNA-seq data of MsHSP90 in different tissues revealed expression patterns of HSP90 in different tissues, and it can be hypothesized that certain HSP90 genes may play a role in specific tissues. For example, in Arabidopsis thaliana, cytosolic HSP90 genes are expressed mostly in the root apex and stele [49, 50]. An analysis of the RNA-seq data of MsHSP90 under different stresses revealed that 11, 10, and 6 MsHSP90 genes significantly responded to salt, drought, and cold stresses, respectively, and among them, 4 genes responded to all three stresses at the same time. These genes may be key genes for subsequent research on molecular breeding for alfalfa stress tolerance.

Alfalfa is an important forage crop worldwide. However, most of the alfalfa planting areas in China are located in areas with severe salinization [51], so it is very important to breed new alfalfa varieties with good stress tolerance. In this study, several MsHSP90 genes related to stress tolerance were screened. These MsHSP90 members can be studied more intensively, such as via gene editing technology and transgenesis, to cultivate new resistant alfalfa germplasm resources.

Conclusions

In this study, 29 MsHSP90 genes were identified in the alfalfa genome; 25 of these genes were unevenly distributed on eight chromosomes, and four genes were not localized to regular chromosomes. These genes were subdivided into groups Ⅰ, Ⅱ, and Ⅲ, with group Ⅰ containing the largest number of MsHSP90 genes. Segmental duplication is the main mode of expansion of the MsHSP90 gene family in alfalfa. Analysis of abiotic stress RNA-seq data revealed that 11, 10 and 6 MsHSP90s respond to salt, drought and cold stresses, respectively. Among them, four MsHSP90s responded to all four stresses (MsHSP90-10, MsHSP90-11, MsHSP90-16, MsHSP90-27). In summary, we comprehensively characterized the HSP90 gene family in alfalfa. These findings establish a foundational framework for further investigation into the functional roles of the MsHSP90 gene and offer novel perspectives for advancing molecular breeding strategies in alfalfa.
