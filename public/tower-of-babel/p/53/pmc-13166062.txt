Comparing Logistic Regression and Artificial Neural Network Models for Analyzing Medicare Utilization and Costs Among Older Adults
Authors: Jie Chen, Seyeon Jang, Min Qi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Artificial neural networks (ANNs) are increasingly applied in health care outcome prediction, yet their relative benefits compared with traditional methods in health services research remain unclear.Objective:To examine health care utilization and costs among community-dwelling older adults using the Andersen Behavioral Model, and to compare the performance of logistic regression and ANN models.Research Design:Cross-sectional study utilizing linked data from CMS Medicare fee-for-service (FFS) claims and Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (CAHPS) surveys (2018–2022). The sample included 254,748 Medicare beneficiaries aged 65 and older. Outcomes were high Medicare costs (top 25%), 30-day readmissions, and preventable hospitalizations (PQIs). Predictors included socioeconomic factors, chronic conditions, and patient-reported measures. Model performance was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and Brier scores.Results:Chronic conditions, including heart disease and depression, significantly predicted higher Medicare costs. Poor self-rated health, functional limitations, dual eligibility, and lower educational attainment correlated strongly with readmissions and preventable hospitalizations. ANN and logistic regression models demonstrated comparable performance across outcomes, with similar AUC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and Brier scores.Conclusions:Both logistic regression and ANN models effectively predict health care utilization and high-risk outcomes among older adults using structured Medicare data. Logistic regression offers interpretability and robust predictive power, whereas ANN models may provide additional value as healthcare datasets grow increasingly complex and comprehensive.

With an accelerating aging population, Medicare spending is projected to increase at an average annual rate of 7.4% between 2023 and 2032.1 Preventable hospitalizations and 30-day readmissions are major contributors to high health care costs among older adults, who are among the most frequent users of health care services.2,3 These measures also serve as quality indicators and are often tied to value-based payment incentives.4,5

Socioeconomic status (SES) plays a critical role in health care utilization and spending.6,7 Factors such as education and self-reported health influence access to care and health outcomes, and lower SES is consistently associated with higher rates of chronic conditions, reduced access to care, and increased hospitalizations. These dynamics are especially important for older adults, whose complex care needs often require coordinated, patient-centered approaches.6–8

The Andersen Behavioral Model offers a widely used framework for understanding determinants of health care use by organizing predictors into predisposing, enabling, and need-based factors.9 To improve predictive accuracy, studies must integrate strong conceptual models with comprehensive datasets that include administrative claims, SES indicators, and patient-reported measures. However, many existing studies lack such integrated data, limiting their ability to fully capture variation in health care use among older adults.

Recent advances in machine learning have introduced opportunities to improve prediction accuracy for clinical and utilization outcomes.10–13 Artificial neural networks (ANN), in particular, have been widely applied in precision medicine for tasks such as diagnosis, treatment selection, and risk stratification.14–16 However, their use in health services and health policy research remains limited. A key barrier is interpretability, as neural networks function largely as black-box models that offer limited insight into the relationships between predictors and outcomes.11,17,18 In addition, machine learning approaches are highly sensitive to the quality and structure of input data. In many big data and machine learning applications, poorly curated or conceptually weak inputs can lead to the well-known “garbage in, garbage out” problem,19–21 in which even sophisticated algorithms yield misleading results. This highlights the importance of pairing machine learning with well-established theoretical frameworks and comprehensive datasets.

In this study, we link Medicare fee-for-service (FFS) inpatient claims, survey responses, and socioeconomic indicators to examine predictors of health care utilization and spending among older adults. This unique linkage integrates clinical, socioeconomic, and patient-reported variables that are rarely available together, providing a strong empirical foundation for applying the Andersen Behavioral Model in both logistic regression and neural network models. Within this conceptual structure, we compare the predictive performance of logistic regression and ANNs. Our goal is not to develop a highly complex or computationally intensive deep learning model, but rather to evaluate the feasibility and added value of applying a conceptually guided multilayer perceptron (MLP) architecture to structured Medicare data.22,23 By assessing both methods within the same theoretical and empirical framework, we aim to clarify the trade-offs between model complexity and interpretability in health services research. This comparison enhances understanding of the conditions under which machine learning may or may not offer advantages over traditional methods and identifies opportunities for future work that incorporates more flexible architectures, improves uncertainty estimation, and addresses challenges such as class imbalance and population representativeness.

METHODS

Data

The primary dataset for this study is the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Medicare Beneficiary Summary File (MBSF). Using the beneficiary ID, we linked the MBSF with the 2018, 2019, 2021, and 2022 Medicare FFS Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (MCAHPS) Survey.24 Data collection for 2020 was suspended due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The FFS MCAHPS Survey is an annual survey conducted with a subset of community-dwelling Medicare beneficiaries enrolled in FFS. This survey collects information on beneficiaries’ experiences with Medicare and their FFS providers, including SES and self-reported health status. Previous studies have used this dataset to test the association between patient self-perceptions and various outcomes.25,26 Using the beneficiary location ID, we linked the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) as a proxy for the availability of local resources and the social determinants of health affecting each beneficiary.27

The MBSF includes beneficiary enrollment information across various Medicare programs (parts A, B, C, and D). It is further linked to the Chronic Condition and Cost and Use file segments, providing data on the presence of specific chronic conditions, as well as a summary of health care utilization and total annual payments for services. We further linked the MBSF with Medicare FFS inpatient claims data to measure readmission rates and preventable hospitalizations among beneficiaries who had at least one hospitalization during the survey year. Our study focused on community-dwelling Medicare FFS beneficiaries aged 65 and above.

Outcome Measures

The first primary outcome measure is the annual total Medicare payment per individual. We captured this measure using the MBSF Cost and Use file. To maintain consistency with the existing literature, we used the same Medicare payment metric as in the 2021 Alzheimer’s Disease Facts and Figures Special Report on Race, Ethnicity, and Alzheimer’s in America.28 We calculated total Medicare payments per person per year by summing Medicare expenditures across key health care services, including acute inpatient care, other inpatient hospital services, skilled nursing facilities, hospice care, home health services, hospital outpatient care, ambulatory surgery centers, anesthesia, part B drugs, evaluation and management, part B physician services, other procedures, imaging, tests, other part B carrier services, and part D Medicare payments. To account for inflation, all costs were first adjusted to 2023 dollars using the Consumer Price Index.29 We then created a binary classification for high-cost beneficiaries, coding individuals in the top 25% of the cost as high-cost (coded as 1), and all others as not high-cost (coded as 0).

Among those FFS beneficiaries who had any hospitalization, we merged MBSF data with the Medicare FFS Inpatient Claim File to capture the readmission and PQI measures. The second outcome measure is 30-day all-cause hospital readmission, which was calculated using Medicare FFS Inpatient data containing detailed records of hospital admissions and discharges. Readmissions were identified by tracking any subsequent hospitalizations within 30 days of an initial hospital admission (index admission) for a qualifying condition. This measure was dichotomously coded, with 0 indicating no readmission and 1 indicating any readmission within the 30-day period.

PQI measures in this study were constructed using algorithms from AHRQ’s Prevention Quality Indicators,30,31 which are designed to assess hospital admissions that could potentially be avoided with effective outpatient care. We used 2 composite PQI measures: PQI_92 (Chronic Composite), which captures admissions related to chronic conditions such as diabetes (with or without complications), COPD, asthma, hypertension, heart failure, and angina without a procedure, conditions often linked to gaps in outpatient management; and PQI_91 (acute composite), which includes admissions for acute conditions like dehydration, bacterial pneumonia, and urinary tract infection that may be preventable with timely primary care.

Independent Variables

The covariates included in this study are derived from the Andersen Behavioral Model and existing literature on aging and health care utilization.9,32 These independent variables, widely used in the literature, capture demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors that influence Medicare expenditures, readmissions, and health care quality.33–37

Key demographic variables include age, sex, and race and ethnicity. Socioeconomic variables include full 12-month dual eligibility status for Medicare and Medicaid (yes or no), insurance coverage for prescription drugs (none or have), and educational attainment. Health-related covariates include self-reported health status and the presence of major chronic conditions. In addition, we controlled for self-reported ease of obtaining prescription medications and activities of daily living (ADLs) as indicators of functional status. To account for neighborhood-level socioeconomic factors, we included a dummy variable indicating whether the SVI was above or below the median. Finally, year-fixed effects were controlled to adjust for time-based variations in health care policies and utilization trends.

Analysis

We first conducted descriptive analyses to summarize sample characteristics. We then applied logistic regression models to examine 3 binary outcomes: (1) high total annual Medicare costs, defined as the top 25% of the cost distribution; (2) 30-day all-cause readmissions; and (3) preventable hospitalizations, measured using Prevention Quality Indicators (PQIs). All regression models incorporated individual-level survey weights to ensure national representativeness and to account for the complex survey design.

Next, we implemented ANNs to evaluate predictive performance for the same outcomes. ANN models were constructed using the MLP architecture, which is one of the most commonly used neural network approaches for structured data. The MLP was selected based on its accessibility, interpretability, and established use in prior health services research. The MLP architecture consisted of 3 main components:38 an input layer representing predictor variables (including predisposing, enabling, and need factors guided by the Andersen Behavioral Model), one or more hidden layers with nonlinear activation, and a single-node output layer for binary classification. Sigmoid activation functions were used in both the hidden and output layers.

The model was trained using backpropagation with the Adam optimizer39 to minimize binary cross-entropy loss. To enhance generalizability and reduce overfitting, we applied 5-fold cross-validation40 and early stopping based on the trajectory of validation loss.41 We conducted iterative hyperparameter tuning to optimize model performance.42,43 Specifically, we varied the number of hidden nodes, the learning rate, and the regularization strength through weight decay. Each configuration was trained with multiple random initializations to evaluate stability. Model selection was based on validation performance, including the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and overall accuracy.44 We evaluated both one hidden layer and 2 hidden layer architectures and found that performance was similar. Results from the one-layer model are presented in the main text, and results for the two-layer model are provided in the Appendix, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/MLR/D140.

The dataset was randomly split into 70% training and 30% testing subsets to evaluate model generalizability. All models used sigmoid activation functions in the hidden and output layers. Performance was evaluated using standard diagnostic metrics,45 including the area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and the Brier score.

Sensitivity Analysis

We conducted sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our findings. First, we evaluated alternative model specifications by incorporating additional covariates, including need factors (eg, self-reported mental health status) and enabling factors (eg, low-income subsidy eligibility, English proficiency, and ease of accessing care). Results remained consistent across these specifications and are available upon request. Second, we examined the performance of a 2-layer neural network to assess whether a deeper architecture improved predictive accuracy. Findings were similar to the 1-layer model and are reported in the Appendix, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/MLR/D140.

All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA 18 MP and SAS. Neural network models were implemented using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0. The SPSS MLP architecture was selected to ensure transparency, reproducibility, and alignment with common practices in applied health services research.

RESULTS

Our study included 254,748 Medicare FFS beneficiaries aged 65 and older to analyze total Medicare payments. Among them, 35,212 beneficiaries who were hospitalized during the survey year were included in the analyses of PQIs and all-cause 30-day readmissions.

Sample characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The average total Medicare payment per beneficiary per year was $11,064. Among those with at least one inpatient admission, 14% experienced 30-day all-cause readmission. The rate of preventable hospitalizations was 12% for chronic conditions and 6% for acute conditions. The majority of survey participants were White, and 50% were younger than 75. Approximately 62% had hypertension, 14% had depression, 21% self-reported fair or poor health, and 29% reported having at least one ADL limitation.

ANN Results for High Medicare Total Costs

The same covariates used in logistic regressions were included in the ANN analysis. Several factors emerged as key predictors of high total Medicare costs, including heart conditions, asthma, hyperlipidemia, Medicaid dual eligibility, depression, hypertension, ease of accessing medications, ADL limitations, and ADRD. The final ANN model followed a 23-7-1 architecture [ie, 23 input neurons, 7 hidden layer neurons (auto-generated), and 1 output neuron] trained on data from 254,748 beneficiaries using a feed-forward backpropagation algorithm.

One hidden layer, associated with higher costs (weighted sum=0.559), included depression (weight=0.694), dual eligibility (weight=0.509), ADRD (weight=0.238), and ease of accessing needed medications (weight=0.192). Another hidden layer, associated with lower cost likelihood (weighted sum=-0.653), included the absence of heart conditions (weight=-0.644), hyperlipidemia (weight=-0.361), asthma (weight=-0.222), and ADL limitations (weight=-0.516). The AUC for this model was 0.781, indicating good discriminatory ability (Fig. 1A).

The ANN model for readmissions used a 23-11-1 structure. The AUC was 0.670, suggesting fair classification performance. A hidden layer associated with lower readmission risk (weighted sum=−0.625) included the absence of heart conditions (weight=−0.669), depression (weight=−0.489), hypertension (weight=−0.576), and poor/fair self-reported health (weight=−0.228) (Fig. 1B).

ANN Results for PQI_91

For PQI_91, the model used a 23-8-1 architecture. A hidden layer associated with a higher likelihood of preventable hospitalizations (weighted sum=0.287) included heart disease (weight=0.375), diabetes (weight=0.440), depression (weight=0.338), and poor/fair health (weight=0.244). ANN Results for PQI_92

The PQI_92 model followed a 23-10-1 architecture. The hidden layer associated with a higher risk of any PQI_92 hospitalization (weighted sum=0.672) included heart disease (weight=0.480), diabetes (weight=0.442), hyperlipidemia (weight=0.551), and poor/fair health (weight=0.289). The AUC was 0.767, indicating good classification accuracy (Fig. 1D).

DISCUSSION

We examined hospital readmissions, preventable hospitalizations, and high medical costs among Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries. A key finding was that need-based factors, particularly depression and heart disease, were significant predictors of both high costs and hospital readmissions across all models. Heart disease is commonly linked to modifiable lifestyle and behavioral risk factors, and emerging evidence shows strong connections between lifestyle patterns and both cognitive and behavioral health in later life.46 Depression reflects psychological well-being and has been consistently associated with adverse health outcomes among older adults.47,48 These findings reinforce the central role of clinical and psychosocial need in shaping health care use and spending.

Grounded in the Andersen Behavioral Model, our analysis showed that MLP-based neural networks performed comparably to logistic regression across outcomes. This highlights the continued robustness of traditional regression methods when applied to structured, conceptually informed data, and demonstrates the feasibility of integrating neural networks into theory-driven health services research. Several factors help explain similar performance. Many predictors in our dataset, including demographic, socioeconomic, and health status measures, exhibit mostly linear or monotonic relationships with outcomes. Logistic regression is well-suited to this structure and remains a preferred tool in policy-focused research because it provides interpretability and transparency.

Although neural networks did not substantially outperform regression models in this setting, they offer strengths that merit attention. Neural networks can model nonlinear associations and higher-order interactions that may be difficult to specify in traditional econometric frameworks. Our focus on the MLP architecture, implemented in an accessible software environment, was intentional. MLPs are widely used, appropriate for structured tabular data, and sufficiently transparent for integration into a conceptual model such as the Andersen framework. Embedding the neural network within a theory-driven structure ensured meaningful variable selection and reduced the risk of arbitrary or data-driven model construction.

It is important to emphasize that this study was designed as a feasibility and comparative analysis rather than a comprehensive benchmarking of machine learning algorithms. Our goal was to assess how a widely available neural network model performs relative to logistic regression in a transparent and replicable analytic framework grounded in an established theoretical model. This approach illustrates how machine learning methods can complement, rather than replace, traditional models in health services research.

Methodologically, we implemented iterative hyperparameter tuning, including adjustments to learning rate, hidden layer size, and regularization strength. Five-fold cross-validation and early stopping were applied to reduce overfitting and improve generalizability. We also evaluated a 2-layer architecture and found that performance was similar to the primary model. Although MLPs are useful for structured, cross-sectional data, we acknowledge that more advanced architectures, such as convolutional networks, recurrent networks, and transformer-based models, offer additional capabilities, including attention mechanisms and improved modeling of longitudinal or unstructured data. These approaches may be appropriate for future research involving clinical text, imaging, or sequential electronic health records.

A related limitation is the absence of statistical uncertainty measures from neural networks. Unlike regression models, neural networks do not naturally produce confidence intervals or standard errors, which can limit interpretability in clinical or policy settings. Our focus was on predictive performance and model behavior, but future work could incorporate approaches such as bootstrapping or Bayesian neural networks to characterize uncertainty around predicted probabilities and feature importance.

Class imbalance represents another important limitation. Outcomes such as 30-day readmission were relatively rare, which can affect model calibration and sensitivity. Although our models showed reasonable discrimination, we did not apply resampling or class-weighting techniques in this analysis. Future studies should consider strategies, such as synthetic minority oversampling (SMOTE) or class-weighted loss functions, and alternative metrics, such as precision-recall curves, to better capture predictive performance under imbalance.

We also acknowledge limitations related to population representation. Although our dataset included higher proportions of Black and Hispanic respondents than many clinical datasets, these groups remain underrepresented relative to the national Medicare population. Under-representation may affect model performance for these subgroups and reflect broader structural inequities, including the digital divide. Lower socioeconomic status, rural residence, and limited digital literacy can influence engagement with health care systems and the availability of high-quality data, which in turn affects the generalizability of predictive modeling.49 Ensuring that machine learning tools do not reinforce disparities will require more comprehensive data in model development.50 These challenges also highlight the importance of culturally informed approaches such as SUNSHINE across cultures, which emphasizes system resilience, and the recognition of diverse cultural contexts in aging and health services research.50

Despite these limitations, this study contributes several important insights. First, we demonstrate how a neural network approach can be integrated into a structured, theory-driven analysis guided by the Andersen Behavioral Model. Second, we use a large, linked dataset combining Medicare claims, patient-reported measures, and socioeconomic indicators, enabling a richer understanding of predictors associated with health care use and spending. Third, by directly comparing logistic regression and neural networks within a common conceptual and analytic framework, we provide clarity on the conditions under which machine learning does and does not offer added value in structured health services data.

CONCLUSIONS

Our findings suggest that when applied to structured health services data and guided by an established conceptual framework, traditional logistic regression can perform comparably to ANNs. This highlights the continued importance of theory-driven and interpretable modeling approaches for informing health care policy and clinical decision-making. Although neural networks offer flexibility for capturing nonlinear relationships and complex interactions, their performance advantages may be limited in settings where predictor-outcome relationships are well characterized and primarily linear.

As health care data become increasingly large, diverse, and multidimensional, machine learning methods hold considerable promise for advancing prediction, personalization, and system-level insight. Realizing this potential will require careful attention to transparency, reproducibility, model uncertainty, and equitable representation in training data. Future work should explore the use of more advanced neural network architectures, uncertainty estimation methods, and strategies to address class imbalance, particularly for outcomes that are rare or unevenly distributed. With thoughtful design and integration into theory-informed frameworks, neural networks and related methods can complement traditional approaches, enhance our understanding of health care systems, and support strategies aimed at improving outcomes for diverse and aging populations.
