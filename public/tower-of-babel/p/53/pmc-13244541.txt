Evaluation of an Emergency Department Over-the-Counter Analgesic Medication Starter Pack Initiative on 30-day Revisits
Authors: Sophia Sheikh, Caterina Alacevich, Magda Montague, Claire Layton, Morgan Henson, Ramzi Salloum, Phyllis Hendry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:An Over-The-Counter (OTC) Starter Pack Initiative was implemented in the emergency department (ED) of a safety-net institution. Select OTC analgesic medications were dispensed free to patients experiencing financial/healthcare access barriers.Study Objective:We compared 30-day all-cause ED revisit rates in program participants before and after program participation.Methods:An institutional review board (IRB) approved retrospective observational study of ED visits made by program participants from 01/01 to 10/21/2021 was conducted. Demographics, number of ED visits, ICD-10 codes, and pain scores were collected. A modified patient fixed-effects Poisson regression estimated the relative risk of 30-day all-cause ED revisit after receiving assistance (“OTC-assisted”) compared with the preprogram “usual care” risk.Results:There were 149 unique participants with both “usual care” and “OTC-program assisted” visits. Among these participants, the 30-day revisit rate was lower after program assistance (34%) compared to their revisit rate following a usual care visit (52%). OTC-Starter Pack program assistance was associated with a 20% reduction in the relative risk of revisiting the ED within 30 days (RR: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.71, 0.910) (p = 0.000). A cost saving analysis suggests that the program was associated with a per-patient saving of $53.21, on average, in avoided ED costs, net of the cost of the starter pack.Conclusions:The OTC-Starter Pack Program was associated with reduced 30-day revisit rates among patients with financial or healthcare access barriers and suggested cost savings within our study population.Keywords: pain, emergency department recidivism, nonopioid, financial barriers

Introduction

Research over the past two decades has consistently reported a marked rise in emergency–department (ED) visits, an uptick in ED recidivism, and an expanding population of high–utilizer patients (1, 2). Data from the National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey showed a 9% increase in ED visits in the United States from 2014 to 2022 (3, 4). Moreover, as many as 8% of the overall ED population may revisit the ED on several occasions— many of these visits being potentially avoidable – accounting for 20.5% of all annual ED visits (5–7). The combined load of these trends is estimated to cost the U.S. health–care system billions, with avoidable ED visits alone responsible for $4.4 billion annually by some estimates (8).

Pain is one of the most common reasons for seeking care in the ED (as high as 78% of visits according to some) and is a contributing factor for ED high-utilization and recidivism (9, 10). Other reported factors include older age, higher comorbidity burden, and lower socioeconomic status (11–13). Additionally, relatively low prescription fill rates have been reported after an ED visit or hospitalization (19–22%) (14, 15). Costs of medications, lack of transportation, long pharmacy wait times, and geographic distance from pharmacies can all impact patient medication fulfillment (16–18). Literature suggests patients who do not fill their post–ED prescription, regardless of the underlying reason—whether due to financial or transportation constraints, are more likely to return for a subsequent ED visit, as medication nonadherence often leads to unresolved or recurring health issues (14, 19). Therefore, interventions seeking to improve ED pain management practices, focusing on populations with lower socioeconomic status or limited access to health-care, and addressing obstacles that prevent patients from filling prescriptions may help decrease repeat ED visits.

We implemented an over-the-counter (OTC) Analgesic Starter Pack program within the ED of a safety-net hospital within a large academic health center to address this multifaceted issue. The aim of the program was to promote opioid alternative multimodal practices through patient education and dispensing of free select OTC medications to patients facing financial or other healthcare barriers potentially limiting their access to nonopioid analgesic options when managing pain outside the ED. Our focus on OTC medications was driven by a national push in response to the opioid epidemic to use multimodal approaches focused on opioid alternatives when managing pain. It is estimated that over 70% of Americans use OTC analgesics, although their use is more common in females and non-Hispanic whites, and higher socioeconomic populations (20). The objective of this analysis was to estimate the association between the OTC-Starter Pack program and 30-day ED revisit rates.

Methods

Program Details

The OTC Analgesic Starter pack program launched in April 2021 as part of an ED opioid stewardship quality improvement initiative (21). The OTC Analgesic Starter pack program was implemented in an urban safety-net hospital system, with a Level 1 adult and pediatric trauma center, serving a predominantly socioeconomically disadvantaged population. The institution has 695 beds, with approximately 23,685 admissions and approximately 90,000 ED visits annually. The average ED patient is 47 years old, 52% self-report as African American, 94% as non-Hispanic, 44% are on Medicare or Medicaid, 33% are on commercial insurance, and 23% are self-pay or receive city healthcare assistance. Up to a 7-day supply of acetaminophen, ibuprofen, diclofenac 1% topical gel, and lidocaine 4% patch were available as part of the program. The item(s) were ordered at discharge by the treating ED clinician and dispensed in the ED on a one-time basis free of charge to the patient. Education and informational brochures on oral and topical over-the-counter analgesic options were also provided to patients (22). An analysis was conducted to determine the association between the program with 30-day ED return rates in program participants. The study was approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board.

Study Design and Data Collection Procedure

The study employed a retrospective observational cohort design and was not planned a priori. The Integrated Data Repository (IDR) at University of Florida (UF) Health Jacksonville was utilized to identify patients who participated in the program during the study period. The IDR is a large-scale database that collects and organizes information from across UF Health’s clinical and research enterprises. This clinical data warehouse aggregates data from the university’s various clinical and administrative information systems, including the EpicCare electronic health record (EHR) system. The IDR was queried for adult patients participating in the OTC Analgesic Starter pack program from 01/01/2021 to 9/30/2021. Data associated with ED visits made by program participants from 01/01/2021 to 10/31/2021 (study period plus an additional 30-day window to allow capture of all 30-day revisits) were collected and analyzed. To allow a comparison of 30-day ED recidivism rates before and after program participation, only participants with at least one ED visit for pain prior to program participation (see Supplemental Materials for list of ICD 10 codes) were eligible for inclusion in this analysis.

Data Variables

Demographics, the number of ED visits in the prior 12 months, ICD-10 codes (refer to Supplemental Materials), current medications, Emergency Severity Index (ESI) acuity level 1–5 (lower ESI level corresponds to higher medical acuity), Area Deprivation Index, Charlson Comorbidity Index (grouped as 0, 1, 2 + or missing based on the distribution), ED disposition, and ED triage and discharge pain scores were collected (to calculate change in ED pain score) for all ED visits (23, 24). The Area Deprivation Index (ADI) is a validated tool measuring neighborhood disadvantage and considers factors related to income, education, employment, and housing quality (25). A higher ADI percentile indicates higher disadvantage. Pain intensity scores were measured using the Numeric Pain Rating Scale (26). Pain scores were collected as higher ED pain scores have been associated with increased 30-day recidivism (27, 28). Program medication costs were determined using the hospital’s central pharmacy records.

Data Analysis

Program participant ED visits were grouped and analyzed following the schematic depicted in Figure 1. An ED visit that occurred prior to program participation between January 1-September 30, 2021, with a disposition of discharge followed by a return visit within 30 days for any reason were termed “usual-care” visits and revisits, respectively. Visits in which program participation occurred, as well as those that occurred after program participation during the study period were defined as “OTC-assisted” visits. This approach was taken because it was possible that 1) participants may have had OTC starter pack medications left over from the visit in which it was prescribed and 2) every program participant received education and informational brochures on the OTC medications. Research shows that patient education improves medication adherence and helps reduce overall healthcare use (15, 29). All visits post program participation were labelled as “OTC-assisted,” as it would have been difficult to account for the potential impact patient education may have had on these subsequent visits.

Classification of emergency department visit types. OTC = over-the-counter.

Participants without “usual care” visits were not included in the analysis as they lacked comparator data (i.e., baseline or preprogram participation ED revisit data). A simple comparison of ED revisit rates between patients receiving and not receiving program assistance would have led to biased estimates due to program participation endogeneity, as the program targeted patients with financial and socioenvironmental barriers. Such a comparison would have failed to consider endogenous differences between participating and nonparticipating patients, such as socioeconomic status, education, or health literacy, which are risk factors for ED visit frequency independent of program participation and are not captured in EHR data (12, 13, 28, 30).

Poisson Regression Model (Visit-Level Data)

Descriptive statistics of patient characteristics were reported at the patient level. Descriptive statistics of visit-specific factors were estimated at the visit level. Differences in proportions were statistically tested using Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney) tests. A modified patient fixed-effects Poisson regression with cluster-robust standard errors was performed to evaluate the association between OTC program participation and the relative risk of a 30-day ED revisit. The within-patient Poisson regression compared participants’ 30-day all-cause ED revisit risk before program assistance (“usual care”) with their risk after receiving program assistance (“OTC-assisted”). A fixed effects analysis was chosen to mitigate bias from unobserved time-invariant participant characteristics. Visit-specific Charlson Comorbidity index (0, 1, 2 +, missing), visit-specific ESI acuity level (1–2, 3, 4–5), and triage pain score (0–10) were included in the analysis. Standard errors were clustered at the patient level. Analyses were performed in Stata versions 15 and 19.

Cost Savings Analysis

To estimate the potential per-patient cost savings provided by the program, first, we determined the averted cost by multiplying the change in the probability of an ED revisit (Δ p) with the average cost of an ED visit at the institution (Cost of ED Visit). From this total, we then deducted the average per-patient cost of the starter pack (Cost of OTC Starter Pack) for program participants.

Results

There were 334 unique program participants, but only 149 had both “usual care” and “OTC-assisted” index visits (i.e., could serve as their own control) and thus were included in the analysis. The remaining 185 program participants did not have ED visits for pain prior to program participation and were excluded from the analysis.

Table 2 describes the characteristics of the “usual care” and “OTC-assisted” index visits. The mean change in pain score was −1.24 under “usual care” compared to −1.43 in “OTC-assisted” visits. The mean all-cause 30-day revisit rate was higher after a “usual-care” visit (70%) compared to after an “OTC-assisted” visit (54%). The “usual care” index visits, on average, had a higher proportion of ED visits with ESI acuity levels of 1 or 2 (corresponding to higher acuity) compared to “OTC-assisted” visits. The rate of opioid medications prescribed was higher in “usual care” than in “OTC-assisted” visits, 3.5% vs. 2.3%, respectively, although not statistically significantly different (p = 0.35).

Regression Model

Table 3 reports the results of a modified within-patient fixed-effects Poisson regression estimation with cluster-robust standard errors. The 30-day all-cause revisit rate was lower after program assistance (34%) compared to the revisit rate following a usual care visit (or prior to program participation) (52%). Under “usual care,” the adjusted predicted probability of 30-day ED revisit was 73.6% (95% CI: [70.4, 76.8]). After program participation, the adjusted predicted probability of a 30-day revisit dropped to about 59% (95% CI: 54.3–63.8). Thus, the intervention was associated with a 14.6 percentage points-lower revisit probability. In terms of relative risk, the OTC-Starter Pack program assistance was associated with a 20% lower risk of revisiting the ED within 30 days (RR: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.71–0.91) (p = 0.000). A sensitivity analysis excluding comorbidity as a regressor was also performed and gave the same results: RR: 0.81 (SE 0.05), 95% CI: [0.72, 0.92], (p = 0.001).

Cost Savings Analysis

The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality estimate the average ED visit cost for a low-income metropolitan area to be about $500 per visit (31). The average cost of an ED visit at the study institution during the study period was $400 per visit. The average cost of an OTC Analgesic Medication Starter Pack item per visit was $2.90, and per patient was $5.19. The intervention was associated with a 14.6 percentage points-lower revisit probability. Combining the reduction in revisit probability with the cost of an ED visit ($400) and subtracting the cost of the starter pack, a cost saving analysis suggests that each patient who receives the OTC starter pack is expected to save $53.21, on average, in avoided ED costs, net of the cost of the starter pack.

Discussion

The objective of this analysis was to determine the association between an emergency department-based OTC Analgesic Medication Starter Pack Initiative on subsequent 30-day ED revisits in patients with financial or healthcare access barriers. Within our cohort, we identified program participation was associated with a substantially lower (20%) risk of returning to the ED for pain within 30 days. Additionally, the program may lead to cost savings when comparing the average ED cost to the average cost of the analgesic medication starter pack.

There are several potential explanations for our findings. As this initiative was focused on providing analgesic medications to patients facing financial or transportation barriers to local pharmacies, it is reasonable to assume interventions that improve access to analgesic medications would have a positive impact on patient care and ED recidivism for pain. Although we were unable to find published reports of other ED or hospital programs providing free analgesic medications to patients at discharge for comparison, there are reports in the literature describing conflicting outcomes of programs providing free nonanalgesic medications (predominantly antibiotics, take-home naloxone); some associated with reduced ED returns, while others showed no difference or higher ED recidivism (32–36). This variability in program outcomes could be due to heterogeneity in study populations, such as differences in patient demographics, comorbidities, and social determinants of health, as well as differences in study methodology. Another important consideration was that our cohort served as their own control or comparison group to limit the influence of endogenous differences between participating and nonparticipating patients, like socioeconomic status, education, or health literacy, which are risk factors for ED recidivism independent from program participation (12, 13, 28, 30).

While providing free medications to patients experiencing medication access barriers was the main feature of this program, participants also received education and informational brochures on the oral or topical over-the-counter analgesic medications provided to them. These materials came bundled with the analgesic medication starter pack and were reviewed with patients by the ED clinical team at the time of discharge. Several studies support patient education as a means for improving medication compliance, patient outcomes, and reducing healthcare utilization (15, 29, 37). Standard ED discharge materials typically include information regarding the patient’s diagnosis and prescriptions but tend to be superficial in nature and focused on patient safety, ED return precautions, and post ED visit follow-up guidance. In contrast, the educational materials provided as part of this initiative focused on strategies for managing pain at home using the prescribed OTC analgesic medications.

When examining index visit types, we found a greater reduction in the mean change in pain intensity scores during “OTC-assisted” index visits compared to “usual-care” index visits. However, neither mean change in pain scores met the thresholds proposed in the literature as to what change is considered clinically significant when using the numeric pain rating scale (NPRS) (i.e., the minimum clinically important difference). Variable thresholds have been reported, including a reduction in score by 1.65 points, 2 points, and by 30%; however, these studies suffered from many factors that limit generalizability, including study populations of patients with only chronic pain, cancer pain, or an oversampling of male patients (38–41).

Higher ED pain scores have also been associated with increased ED recidivism (27, 28). Thus, the greater reduction in pain scores observed during the “OTC-assisted” index visit may have contributed to the lower 30-day ED revisit rates in this group. Additionally, “OTC-assisted” index visits had a higher proportion of visits in which an opioid alternative analgesic was either administered in the ED or prescribed at discharge. This pattern likely reflects clinician awareness of the program; perhaps with the intention to prescribe the starter pack at ED discharge, they were more likely to administer these medications during the ED encounter itself and provide prescriptions, along with the starter packs.

Limitations

There are several limitations to note regarding our analysis. First, the OTC Starter Pack program addressed some but not all factors contributing to ED recidivism. However, the program was associated with a significant reduction in ED recidivism despite its limited scope. Second, our dataset is affected by limitations imposed by an observational study design and retrospective data collection from the electronic health record with a small sample size, namely potential for incomplete/inconsistent documentation, selection bias, and reduced capacity to measure all time-varying confounding variables. Third, the OTC Analgesic Starter Pack program was part of a larger initiative (PAMI ED-ALT) to reduce opioid prescribing in the ED and to promote the use of opioid alternative options (21). PAMI ED-ALT began in January 2021 and consisted of clinician and ED staff education and ED pain management order panels within the electronic health record. Unlike the OTC Analgesic Starter pack program, which was limited to patients with financial or transportation barriers, the ED pain management order panels were available throughout the study period (i.e., available during both usual-care and OTC-assisted visits). Thus, it is unlikely that the order panels would have influenced our study results. If anything, the impact of the parallel PAMI ED-ALT program might have reduced ED recidivism already in “usual care” visits, hence reducing the estimated effect size of the OTC program outcome. Additionally, our analysis included common ICD-10 codes associated with pain. However, certain pain conditions may have been inadvertently excluded, while their inclusion could have potentially impacted the study outcomes. As data was collected retrospectively and we were unable to determine whether pain was the driving factor for the return visit or if the return visit was for the same pain condition as the index visit, we included all 30-day ED return visits for any reason. Lastly, our findings may not be generalizable as our program was implemented in only one ED setting.

Conclusions

Within our study cohort, we demonstrated that a free ED-based nonopioid analgesic medication program implemented in a safety-net hospital system was associated with a reduction in the relative risk of 30-day ED returns. Additionally, the program was associated with cost savings when comparing the average institutional costs of an ED visit to the average cost of the analgesic medication starter pack. These findings require validation within a larger, more heterogeneous patient population.
