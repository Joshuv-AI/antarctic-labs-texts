Design and construction of nanoengineered multifunctional microneedles with sustained-release properties for targeted microenvironment remodeling in diabetic bone regeneration
Authors: Heyi Gong, Sihan Ma, Boyuan Wang, Yue Liu, Xiao Han, Yu Wang, Duo Sun, Yadi Su, Junqi Wang, Jinghui Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic periodontitis associated with diabetes mellitus (CPDM) presents significant challenges due to an impaired tissue microenvironment characterized by persistent oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and dysfunction of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). Nanotechnology-driven Met@ZIF-8@PDA microneedle (MN) therapeutic platform has been developed that integrates multiple functions and allows for precise modulation. The zeolite imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8), possessing an ultra-high specific surface area, serves as an effective drug carrier for loading metformin (Met). Subsequently, biomimetic modification using polydopamine (PDA) enhances both biocompatibility and antioxidant properties. The degradable microneedles integrated with nanoparticles facilitate targeted delivery within periodontal pockets while enabling the sustained release of Met and Zn2+ for up to 168 hours. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated the remodeling of the damaged microenvironment through a triple synergistic mechanism: first, the phenolic hydroxyl structure of PDA directly scavenges excess reactive oxygen species (ROS); second, PDA and Met collaboratively enhance the M2/M1 macrophage ratio; third, localized drug delivery via microneedles aids in rescuing osteogenic differentiation of impaired MSCs. Mechanistically, this therapeutic paradigm shift from 'passive repair' to 'active regulation' arises from precise modulation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. In conclusion, the remarkable drug delivery capabilities of ZIF-8, when combined with the biomimetic functional modifications of PDAs and the targeted delivery advantages offered by microneedles, pave the way for advancements in CPDM therapy.

Introduction

Periodontitis, a prevalent chronic inflammatory disease, significantly impacts oral health and is characterized by bone loss as well as the loosening and eventual loss of teeth [1]. It is crucial to recognize that periodontitis is linked to various systemic diseases, including diabetes mellitus [[2], [3], [4]]. In the diabetic microenvironment, the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) within periodontal tissues leads to a substantial production of pro-inflammatory factors [5]. Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and C-reactive protein, can disrupt the intricate balance between bone formation and bone resorption. Hyperactivation of osteoclasts results in the resorption of bone tissue at a rate that surpasses the repair and rebuilding capacity of osteoblasts. This imbalance leads to inflammatory resorption of the alveolar bone, ultimately resulting in tooth loss [6,7]. Therefore, the management of periodontitis in diabetic patients should emphasize the reduction of ROS, the optimization of the immune microenvironment, and the enhancement of tissue repair processes.

Metformin (Met) is a widely recognized first-line medication employed globally for the management of type 2 diabetes. In addition to its hypoglycemic effects, it possesses significant antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [8,9]. Moreover, there has been a growing interest within the scientific community regarding the osteogenic effects of metformin in recent years. Reports indicate that metformin enhances the expression of osteogenic genes through various signaling pathways, including AMPK, Wnt, ERK1/2, and AKT, among others [[10], [11], [12]]. Furthermore, metformin may hold considerable potential in improving the macrophage-mediated immune microenvironment for bone repair by modulating interactions between macrophages and bone marrow stem cells (BMSCs). However, achieving locally effective concentrations through the administration of metformin alone poses significant challenges. An overdose may lead to adverse effects such as lactic acidosis and hepatotoxicity [13]. Additionally, drug delivery efficiency is significantly compromised due to impaired microcirculation in diabetic periodontal tissues [14]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to establish a localized delivery system aimed at enhancing drug bioavailability.

Zeolite imidazoline framework-8 (ZIF-8) is a subclass of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) with high water stability and high porosity, making it an excellent delivery platform for metformin [15]. In addition, studies have reported that ZIF-8 facilitates the sustained release of zinc ions, which play a significant role in osteogenesis, anti-inflammation, and even the protection of mitochondria from excessive ROS [10,16]. Polydopamine (PDA) coatings represent a widely utilized approach for modifying the surfaces of materials. The incorporation of PDA onto the surface of ZIF-8 effectively prevents the rapid release of encapsulated drugs while simultaneously enhancing the biocompatibility of the carrier [17,18]. Nanodelivery systems based on ZIF-8@PDA have been employed in various applications, including wastewater treatment, tumor therapy, pest management, infected wound healing, and the removal of oral biofilm [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. Moreover, the catechol group of polydopamine endows it with exceptional antioxidant properties. It can also facilitate the polarization of macrophages towards an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and enhance the secretion of various anti-inflammatory factors, including IL-10 and transforming growth factor-β(TGF-β). These factors play a pivotal role in inhibiting excessive inflammatory responses and promoting tissue repair [24,25].

Microneedles (MNs) have emerged as an exceptional drug delivery system applicable in various fields, including hypertrophic scar treatment [26], wound healing [27], and oral diseases [28]. They are capable of penetrating the gingival tissue, facilitating minimally invasive drug delivery while prolonging its release. This innovative approach effectively addresses the inherent complexities of the oral environment, such as salivary flushing and muscle activity [29]. Methacrylate gelatin (GelMA) is a widely utilized photo-crosslinked hydrogel recognized for its exceptional biocompatibility and ability to facilitate the sustained release of nanoparticles, making it an ideal material for drug delivery applications [30].

In this study, we developed a multifunctional integrated microneedle system assisted by nanotechnology (Met@ZIF-8@PDA MN) through the incorporation of Met@ZIF-8@PDA nanoparticles with GelMA. This innovative system facilitates macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, regulates local excess ROS, and remodels the inflammatory microenvironment, thereby restoring the osteogenic function of damaged stem cells. We conducted a comprehensive investigation through both in vitro and in vivo experiments that focused on its biocompatibility, mechanical properties, antioxidant efficacy, immunomodulatory effects, and bone regeneration capacity. Additionally, we explored the immune environment remodeling mechanism using transcriptome sequencing as well as western blotting techniques. In conclusion, we present a drug delivery system specifically designed to address the complexities of pathological microenvironments. Materials and methods

Synthesis and characterization of Met@ZIF@PDA nanoparticles (NPs)

First, 300 mg of 2-methylimidazole was dissolved in 10 mL of methanol. Subsequently, 40 mg of metformin was added to the resulting solution and stirred for 20 min. Next, 150 mg of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O was dissolved in 5 mL deionized water (DI water) and combined with the aforementioned mixture. After stirring at room temperature for 60 min, the solution was centrifuged at a speed of 10,000 rpm for 5 min and washed three times to obtain Met@ZIF NPs. Five milligrams of Met@ZIF powder were then dissolved in 30 mL of Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.5). To this solution, an additional 5 mg of dopamine hydrochloride (DA) was incorporated, and the mixture was thoroughly stirred in a dark environment for 2 h. The Met@ZIF@PDA nanoparticles were subsequently collected through centrifugation at a speed of 10,000 rpm for 5 min and washed. Blank ZIF@PDA was prepared similarly without the addition of drugs.

The characterization of Met@ZIF-8@PDA was conducted using a variety of analytical techniques, including scanning electron microscope (SEM), transmission electron microscope (TEM), zeta potential, X-ray powder diffraction (XRD), dynamic light scattering (DLS), thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA), and fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR).

Synthesis and characterization of GelMA

GelMA was synthesized following previously established protocols with appropriate modifications [31]. Briefly, 5 g of gelatin was added to 100 mL of phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at 50 °C while stirring at a rate of 800 rpm. Once the gelatin had completely dissolved, methacrylic anhydride (5 mL) was introduced into the solution. After a reaction period of 6 h, the solution was ultimately dialyzed against deionized water using a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff (MwCO) of 3500 Da for 7 days. Following this, the solution was centrifuged and the resulting supernatant was then lyophilized at −80 °C to yield the final product. It was characterized by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy.

Different concentrations of sterile Met@ZIF-8@PDA NPs (0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2 % w/v) were dispersed into sterile GelMA solution under stirring overnight at 50 °C in the dark. The final concentration of nanoparticles was established at 1.0 mg mL−1, determined through the cell viability assay (Fig. S1). Then the GelMA solution, including the drug and LAP, was heated until fully dissolved before being poured into each polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold. Subsequently, the molds were centrifuged at 4000 rpm and repeated 3 times. Crosslinking was achieved by irradiating with UV light for 10 min. Afterward, a 20 % (w/v) poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) solution was introduced to fabricate the base. Finally, the Met@ZIF-8@PDA MN was gently removed from the mold.

The morphology of the microneedles was observed using a digital camera, an optical microscope, and SEM. The penetration performance was evaluated through a compression test and skin penetration experiments. The swelling and degradation properties of the microneedles were assessed in a simulated physiological environment (PBS, pH 7.4, 37 °C) to replicate the in vivo microenvironment. The microneedles were initially weighed to obtain the first weight (W0) and subsequently incubated in PBS. At each scheduled time points (0,2,4,6,8,10, and 12 h), any excess PBS was gently blotted off the microneedle using filter paper, after which the weight following swelling (Ws) was recorded. The swelling rate of the microneedles was calculated using the following formula: Swelling rate (%) = (Ws – W0)/W0 × 100 %. After pre-weighing (W0), the microneedles were collected and dried to a constant weight (Wt)at scheduled time points (24 h, 48 h, 72 h, 96 h, 120 h, 144 h, and 168 h). The degradation rate was calculated using the following formula: Degradation rate (%) = (W0-Wt)/W0 × 100 %. The release kinetics of zinc ions and metformin were examined at scheduled time points. Each microneedle was immersed in 5 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C. The release of Zn2+ was quantified using an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Additionally, the concentration of metformin released into the supernatant was determined at an absorption wavelength of 233 nm.

Cell culture

Rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (rBMSCs) were isolated from the tibial and femoral bone marrow of Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats. The rBMSCs were cultured in α-MEM medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (PS) and maintained in a humidified environment at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. Mouse macrophage cell line RAW264.7 cells were provided by the laboratory of the School of Stomatology, Jilin University, and were cultured in DMEM medium enriched with 10 % FBS under the same humidified conditions.

Biocompatibility evaluation

Cytotoxicity was assessed using both the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay and the live/dead staining assay. Additionally, a hemocompatibility assay was conducted following previous references. [32] The MNs were inserted into the gingival for 14 days. Following this treatment duration, rats were sacrificed under anesthesia. The major organs were subsequently collected for pathological examination.

In vitro osteogenic evaluation

To compare the hyperglycemic microenvironment with that of normal conditions, rBMSCs were pre-cultured in α-MEM medium supplemented with additional glucose to achieve a concentration of 35 mmol L−1 [16]. RBMSCs were cultured with various microneedles. The culture medium was then replaced every three days with an osteogenic induction medium, consisting of complete α-MEM supplemented with β-glycerophosphate and ascorbic acid. Cell samples were stained using the BCIP/NBT ALP kit, while alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was assessed utilizing commercial ALP assay kits. Furthermore, alizarin red S (ARS) staining was conducted. To quantify mineralization levels, wells received an addition of 10 % cetylpyridinium chloride. The supernatant was collected and measured at a wavelength of 562 nm using an enzyme marker. To assess gene expression levels and protein expression levels for typical osteogenic markers, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assays and immunofluorescence (IF) staining analyses were performed accordingly. The primer sequences of osteogenic differentiation-related genes are shown in Table S1. After 7 and 14 days of osteogenic differentiation induction, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde and permeabilized using 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 30 min. Subsequently, they were blocked with PBS containing 1 % BSA for an additional 30 min. Primary antibodies against RUNX2, COL-1, and OCN were then applied at a dilution of 1:200 and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following this incubation, the cells were washed with PBS, and Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgG, diluted to 1:500) was introduced. After a 2-h incubation period, the secondary antibody was removed. The cytoskeleton was subsequently stained using rhodamine-labeled phalloidin, while nuclei were stained with DAPI. Finally, the cells were observed and photographed using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Antioxidant capacity of microneedles

The antioxidant efficacy of various microneedles was assessed through 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2- phenyl-4,4,5,5,-tetramethylimidazolin-1-oxyl-3-oxide radical (PTIO•) scavenging experiments, following previously established methodologies [33,34]. MNs were introduced into each radical solution and incubated for 120 min in the absence of light. Subsequently, absorbance measurements were taken at 515 nm and 557 nm. The scavenging effect was calculated using the following formula:scavengingactivity=[Ablank−AMN]/Ablank×100%where Ablank and AMN represent the absorbance values for the control and MN treated groups, respectively.

RAW264.7 cells were cultured with different MNs. Following an incubation period of 24 h, 200 μM H2O2 was added and allowed to incubate for 24 h. After introducing either DCFH-DA (10 μM) or live/dead staining solution, the mixture was kept in the dark for 30 min before examining the cells under a fluorescence microscope.

In vitro assessment of immunomodulatory properties

To assess the impact of microneedle systems on macrophage polarization, RAW 264.7 macrophages were pre-treated with 100 ng mL−1 of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 20 h to mimic an inflammatory environment. Then, the cells were treated with media containing various microneedles for 24 h. For flow cytometry analysis, the cells were labeled with antibodies against CD86 and CD206. The gene expression of IL-6, Arginase-1 (Arg-1), IL-10, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) in macrophages was assessed using qRT-PCR. The primer sequences are shown in Table S2. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining was performed to confirm the levels of markers associated with macrophage polarization. The cells were fixed using paraformaldehyde and subsequently permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for a duration of 30 min. Following this, the cells were blocked with PBS containing 1 % BSA for an additional 30 min. After blocking, primary antibodies targeting CD86, CD206, Arg-1, iNOS, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and TGF-β were diluted to a ratio of 1:200 and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Post-incubation, the cells were washed with PBS before applying fluorescent secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG diluted to 1:500). Next, the nuclei were stained with DAPI. Finally, observations were performed using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The culture supernatants were collected for measuring concentrations of IL-10 and TNF-α using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits.

Osteogenesis induced by the macrophage in vitro

We collected the macrophage medium co-cultured with the material and subsequently mixed it with the osteogenic induction medium to investigate the impact of macrophages on the osteogenic differentiation potential of rBMSCs. It was evaluated using ALP, ARS, qRT-PCR, and IF.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and western blot

RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with LPS and subsequently treated with or without Met@ZIF-8@PDA microneedles, followed by RNA sequencing analysis. Protein levels of STAT3 and phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) in RAW264.7 cells were assessed using western blot analysis. Harvest the target cells and lyse them in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer, supplemented with a protease inhibitor and a phosphatase inhibitor. Total protein concentration was determined with a BCA assay kit. Different proteins were separated by 10 % SDS-PAGE, transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used included STAT3 antibody (1:1000) and P-STAT3 antibody (1:1000). Following incubation with secondary antibodies, target proteins were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) method and quantified using Image J software.

In vivo study

In this study, the animal experiments were approved by the School of Basic Medical Sciences of Jilin University (License No.: 2025396). A total of 36 female SD rats were induced with diabetes through an intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ) at a dosage of 60 mg kg−1. Orthodontic ligature wire was put into the sub-gingivally around the maxillary first molar to establish a model of periodontitis. Subsequently, various microneedle patches were applied to the periodontal defects. The animal was sacrificed after four weeks of treatment. Tissue samples were collected for micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis, histological evaluation, and immunofluorescence studies. Micro-CT scanning was performed at a resolution of 36 μm, with the voltage set at 90 kV and the current at 88 μA during the scanning process. Image reconstruction and quantitative analysis were conducted using CTan software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical graphs were generated by GraphPad Prism 9 and Origin software. All quantitative data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Differences between groups were assessed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Characterization of Met@ZIF-8 and Met @ZIF-8@PDA

We first determined the morphological structures of nanoparticles by SEM (Fig. 1A) and TEM (Fig. 1B). Both ZIF-8 and Met@ZIF-8 exhibit classical regular dodecahedral morphologies with sharp edges. The average size of Met@ZIF-8 is larger than that of ZIF-8 nanoparticles, which aligns with findings from previous studies [16]. After modifying the PDA coating, the particles became rounded and spherical. Meanwhile, we found that Met@ZIF-8@PDA NPs were darker in color [17]. Particle size distribution plots (Fig. 1C) indicate that the average particle size of ZIF-8 NPs was 91.00 ± 18.25 nm, while that of Met@ZIF-8 NPs was 148.75 ± 19.48 nm, and for Met@ZIF-8@PDA NPs, it was measured at 294.17 ± 27.69 nm. DLS measurements (Fig. S2) revealed polydispersity index (PDI) values of 0.09 ± 0.04, 0.10 ± 0.04, and 0.18 ± 0.07 for ZIF-8, Met@ZIF-8, and Met@ZIF-8@PDA, respectively. The zeta potentials of Met@ZIF-8 (26.30 ± 0.50 mV) and ZIF-8 (28.13 ± 1.70 mV) were not significantly different. However, following the modification with PDA, the zeta potential decreased (Fig. 1D). The observed decrease in potential may be attributed to the masking effect of the PDA coating on the positive charge of ZIF-8. The results of XRD (Fig. 1E) showed that the crystal structure of ZIF-8 and Met@ZIF-8 was similar, while the peak intensity of Met@ZIF-8@PDA NPs was weakened. Similar to previous findings, the crystallinity of ZIF-8 was also affected by the PDA coating [18]. Next, we verified the synthesis of the above materials by FTIR (Fig. 1F). Results showed that the absorption peaks of metformin near 3371 and 3310 cm−1 are attributed to N-H stretching vibration, and 1635 and 1571 cm−1 are attributed to C=N stretching. The absorption peaks of ZIF-8 and Met@ZIF-8 in the range of 1350 - 1500 cm−1 are attributed to ring stretching of the imidazole ring. The absorption peak of Met@ZIF-8@PDA at 1586 cm-1 is attributed to C=N stretching. Similarly, it also showed absorption peaks in the range of 1350–1500 cm−1. The peaks in the range of 500–800 cm−1 are attributed to the C-H bending of the aromatic ring. TGA (Fig. 1G) curves indicate that Met@ZIF-8 and Met@ZIF-8@PDA exhibited better thermal stability than ZIF-8. All the results validate our successful synthesis of ZIF-8, Met@ZIF-8, and Met@ZIF-8@PDA nanoparticles.Fig. 1Characterization of nanoparticles. Characterization of microneedles

The 1H NMR spectra (Fig. S3) confirm that GelMA has been successfully synthesized, exhibiting a degree of methacrylic anhydride (MA) substitution of 24 % [35]. Observations of microneedles were conducted as illustrated in Fig. 2A–C and Figure S4. The microneedle patch consists of a 15 × 15 array featuring quadrangular pyramids, each measuring 250 μm in width and 600 μm in height, with a needle spacing of 500 μm. Fig. 2D illustrates that each needle is capable of withstanding a compressive force of 0.6 N, which is adequate for penetrating the oral mucosa [36]. To investigate the penetration capability of microneedles, Met@ZIF-8@PDA MN was applied to rat skin to observe the arrangement of the MN patches (Fig. S5-6). The microneedles achieved swelling equilibrium after 6 h, as illustrated in Fig. S7. Initially, the microneedles exhibited a rapid degradation rate, which was followed by a gradual decline (Fig. S8). This behavior is consistent with the release of zinc ions and metformin. A swift release of Zn2+ was observed within the first 24 h, after which the release rate of Zn2+ gradually decreased. By 168 h, the concentration of released Zn2+ reached 1.531 μg/mL (Fig. S9). Biocompatibility assessment of microneedles

Biosafety is an essential prerequisite for the application of biomaterials [37]. Therefore, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the biocompatibility of rBMSCs and RAW264.7 co-cultured with different microneedles in vitro. The outcomes of the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 2E and Fig. S11) reinforced that all groups demonstrated satisfactory cell viability. In addition, rBMSCs and RAW264.7 observed in each group exhibited normal and healthy morphology following staining the cells with calcein-AM/PI (Fig. 2F and Fig. S12). In a hemolysis test, the supernatant from the positive control group remained red and transparent, exhibiting a hemolysis ratio (HR) close to 100 %. In contrast, the supernatants from all four MN groups resembled those from the negative control group. The HRs for MN, Met MN, ZIF-8@PDA MN, and Met@ZIF-8@PDA MN were recorded at 0.96 %, 1.56 %, 1.54 %, and 1.87 % respectively (Fig. 2G). It has been reported that an HR of less than 5 % is acceptable [32]. Finally, we assessed the biocompatibility of these microneedles in vivo by H&E staining assay. As shown in Fig. 2H, no significant inflammatory lesions or tissue damage were observed across all groups. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that these microneedles possess commendable biocompatibility and are suitable for subsequent functional evaluations.

In vitro osteogenic performance evaluation

Building on the promising results, we further investigated the bone formation capacity of the functional microneedles under diabetic conditions. The rBMSCs in the control group were cultured under normal glucose (NG) conditions, while the cells co-cultured with various microneedles were subjected to high-glucose conditions (HG). A co-culture system was established using a Transwell device, with microneedles positioned in the upper chamber and rBMSCs placed in the lower chamber (Fig. 3A). As an initial indicator of osteogenic differentiation, assays for ALP staining and activity were performed. In Fig. 3B, D, it is clear that the Met@ZIF-8@PDA MN group significantly mitigated the inhibitory effects of high glucose on bone formation, nearly restoring rBMSCs to a state comparable to those cultured in a normal medium. After a period of 14 to 21 days of inducing osteogenesis, the ARS staining revealed a significant increase in calcium nodule density within the Met@ZIF-8@PDA MN group compared to other experimental groups (Fig. 3C). To further validate the osteogenic differentiation potential, qRT-PCR was utilized to assess gene expression at specific time points. Fig. 3F–J and Fig. S13 represent the relative gene expression levels of ALP, osteocalcin (OCN), collagen type I (COL-1), bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2), and Runt-associated transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) at 7 and 14 days, respectively. Studies have shown that RUNX2 is a marker for the early stages of osteogenic differentiation, promoting the maturation of bone matrix as well as proliferation and differentiation. COL-1 is a key component of the bone matrix, promoting osteoblast adhesion and differentiation [38]. OCN is a crucial marker in the late stage of osteogenic differentiation. Besides, it is a matrix protein throughout the mineralization process [39]. They were significantly elevated in the Met@ZIF-8@PDA MN group. Moreover, the expression levels of corresponding proteins were evaluated using immunofluorescence staining. The highest levels of osteogenesis-related protein expression were observed in the Met@ZIF-8@PDA group on day 7 (Figure S14-19) and day 14 (Figure S20-25).

In conclusion, the Met@ZIF-8@PDA group exhibited superior osteogenic effects. It has been demonstrated that metformin mitigates oxidative stress and inflammation under hyperglycemic conditions while activating the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway to regulate cellular autophagy, thereby enhancing the functionality of rBMSCs in a diabetic condition [40]. ZIF-8, recognized as an exceptional drug delivery carrier, facilitates a controlled release of drugs while enhancing their bioavailability [41]. Furthermore, zinc ions play a multifaceted role in the processes of bone repair and regeneration [16].

In vitro antioxidant capacity evaluation

Chronic inflammation of periodontal tissues results in elevated levels of ROS and a compromised antioxidant defense system. The literature indicates that the synergistic interactions between ROS and the advanced glycation end products/receptors (AGE/RAGE) pathways in high-glucose environments may further aggravate periodontal damage in diabetic patients [42]. The antioxidant activity of various MNs was evaluated using DPPH and PTIO• scavenging experiments. The VC group served as a positive control. Fig. 4A illustrates the redox mechanism involved in free radical scavenging by DPPH. Fig. 4B demonstrates that the DPPH scavenge rate of the MN, Met MN, ZIF-8@PDA MN, and Met@ZIF-8@PDA groups was recorded at 20.88 %, 45.32 %, 74.69 %, and 96.81 %, respectively. The DPPH solution exhibited a purple color with a characteristic peak at 515 nm, which can be utilized to monitor its reduction. Upon introducing the PDA-containing treatment, it was observed that absorption peaks decreased (Fig. 4C) and the DPPH solution underwent significant decolorization (Fig. 4B). As illustrated in Fig. S26, the Met@ZIF-8@PDA MNs exhibited significantly enhanced PTIO• scavenging activity compared to the other groups. In vitro immunomodulatory property evaluation

Studies have shown that under diabetic conditions, macrophages are unable to transition from the M1 to M2 phenotype. This impairment leads to the production of numerous pro-inflammatory factors, ultimately, which are strongly associated with failures in bone regeneration (Fig. 5A) [44]. Conversely, treatment with Met@ZIF-8@PDA MNs effectively reversed this inflammatory condition, resulting in an increase of M2 macrophages to 18.16 %. Furthermore, the proportion of M1 macrophages was recorded at 14.76 %, which closely resembled that observed in control groups (Fig. 5B). In contrast, treatment with Met@ZIF-8@PDA MNs revealed a markedly different picture, where more M1 macrophages transitioned into M2 phenotypes. Metformin facilitates the conversion of macrophages from M1 to M2 phenotypes and diminishes ROS production through an AMPK-dependent manner, has been confirmed by previous research [45]. Zinc-containing biomaterials promote the phenotypic switching of macrophages and stimulate the secretion of anti-inflammatory factors. Furthermore, zinc ions facilitate the polarization of regulatory T cells, thereby enhancing the inflammatory microenvironment [46]. The degradation products of PDA can mitigate LPS-induced ROS and inhibit the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway [47]. Similar findings have corroborated the synergistic anti-inflammatory effects of polydopamine and metformin [10].

Mechanism of the immunomodulatory performance of Met@ZIF-8@PDA MNs

To conduct a comprehensive investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying immunomodulatory effects, RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with LPS and treated with or without Met@ZIF-8@PDA microneedles for RNA-Seq. The results from the principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrate significant differences among the groups, thereby highlighting the robustness of the sequencing data (Fig. S47). As illustrated in the volcano plot (|log2FC | >1, P < 0.05), following treatment with Met@ZIF-8@PDA MNs, a total of 254 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (Fig. 7A). Among these, 152 genes demonstrated upregulation, while 102 genes exhibited downregulation. Fig. 7B illustrates a heatmap depicting the expression patterns of DEGs. The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Fig. 7C) reveals significant involvement of pathways related to the cell cycle, DNA replication, cell division, and DNA repair. These factors are essential for the process of tissue regeneration [49,50]. It also indicates that DEGs are predominantly associated with immune responses and inflammatory processes, which is consistent with the observed treatment outcomes (Fig. 5). Fig. 7D and Fig. S48 present heatmaps illustrating DEGs related to inflammatory and immune responses, respectively. Gene expression levels of inflammatory chemokines, including CXCL2, CXCL3, CCL4, and CCL5, as well as inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin IL-6, TNFRSF8, TNFRSF9, and TNFRSF13B, were significantly lower in the treated group compared to the LPS group [51,52]. The enrichment analysis performed by the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) confirmed a significant enrichment of signaling pathways associated with the cell cycle and DNA replication, aligning with the findings from the GO analysis. Furthermore, several critical signaling pathways related to inflammation and macrophage polarization were also significantly enriched, including TNF, JAK-STAT, and IL-17 signaling pathways (Fig. 7E) [53,54]. Recent literature has highlighted the critical role of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway activation in contributing to inflammation and the subsequent activation of osteoclasts [55,56]. The findings are also in alignment with the spatial transcriptome results observed in human gingiva under both healthy conditions and periodontitis [57]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the therapeutic mechanism of Met@ZIF-8@PDA MNs may involve immunomodulation via the JAK-STAT pathway, as confirmed by Western blot analysis. As illustrated in Fig. 7F, G and Fig. S49, Met@ZIF-8@PDA MNs effectively inhibited the LPS-induced activation of JAK-STAT signaling pathways. The pSTAT3 was significantly reduced, thereby exerting notable anti-inflammatory effects. In addition, the specific roles and synergistic effects of other potential signaling pathways require further validation in future studies.

In vivo assessment of treatment efficacy

A photograph of the rat periodontitis model is presented in Fig. S50. Fig. 8A demonstrates the treatment schedule in vivo studies. The blank control group received no treatment. The osteogenic effects of our system were subsequently assessed using micro-CT analysis (Fig. 8B). As a crucial parameter, the vertical distance between the alveolar bone crest (ABC) and the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) was measured and quantified using high-resolution 3D reconstruction techniques. Fig. 8D showed that the CPDM group exhibited the greatest distance between ABC and CEJ (1.22 ± 0.02 mm) among all groups, indicating significant destruction of the alveolar bone. In comparison to these conditions, blank MN (1.06 ± 0.08 mm) has no significant effect on bone recovery. However, both Met MNs (0.60 ± 0.08 mm) and ZIF-8@PDA MNs (0.63 ± 0.17 mm) groups demonstrated recovery in alveolar bone height. Notably, the Met@ZIF-8@PDA MN group (0.40 ± 0.05 mm) exhibited a pronounced reduction in alveolar bone destruction due to its exceptional immune regulation and pro-osteogenesis efficacy. Additional bone parameters, including bone volume per tissue volume (BV/TV), trabecular separation (Tb. Sp), and trabecular thickness (Tb. Th), were analyzed to further validate our previous findings. In comparison to the CPDM group, the Met@ZIF-8@PDA MN group demonstrated a significant enhancement in BV/TV and Tb. Th values while simultaneously reducing the Tb. Sp value. This indicates that our design is highly effective in mitigating bone loss following treatment (Fig. 8C and Fig. S51-52). Based on the images stained with H&E and Masson, there was a notable loss of collagen accompanied by a significant presence of inflammatory cells in the CPDM group (Fig. 8E–G). In contrast, our Met@ZIF-8@PDA MNs exhibited an outstanding ability to rectify these abnormalities and facilitate collagen deposition. Furthermore, compared to the control group, we observed a significant reduction in the proportion of M1 macrophages within the Met@ZIF-8@PDA MN group, accompanied by a notable increase in M2 macrophages (Fig. S53). This trend is consistent with the down-regulation of TNF-α and up-regulation of IL-10 (Fig. S54). These findings confirm that the microneedle system exerts immunomodulatory effects through macrophage polarization. Traditional invasive treatments—such as subgingival scaling and periodontal flap surgery—exhibit a generally low compliance rate due to their complex procedures and postoperative discomfort [58]. In contrast, microneedle therapy presents a minimally invasive alternative to conventional periodontal treatments, facilitating effective intervention while minimizing patient discomfort and substantially reducing the risk of infection [59]. In conventional periodontal treatment, local drug delivery methods such as gels are prone to dilution by saliva. This often results in inadequate local drug concentrations and mucosal injury. Oral administration may lead to gastrointestinal reactions and dysbacteriosis, among other systemic side effects. In contrast, microneedle therapy offers considerable advantages. The length of microneedles can be precisely controlled, enabling them to penetrate solely the gingival while delivering drugs directly to the periodontal pocket. This characteristic not only substantially reduces drug loss and diffusion but also effectively increases local drug concentration, thereby ensuring treatment efficacy. Furthermore, the degradation products of microneedles are biocompatible, making this method safer than oral or injectable administration [29].

In the future, for patients with diabetes and periodontitis, the development of a multi-modal, functionally integrated microneedle system that combines hypoglycemic effects with antioxidant, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory properties, and bone regeneration-promoting functions may emerge as a predominant research direction. Such systems could integrate phototherapy, electrotherapy, magnetotherapy, and drug delivery technologies to achieve synergistic therapeutic outcomes. Furthermore, exploring the combined application of emerging technologies such as stem cell therapy and gene therapy alongside microneedles is anticipated to pave new avenues for managing periodontitis. The advancement of smart microneedles and sensors also warrants attention. These devices can accurately monitor the condition of periodontitis in real time by detecting biomarkers, including inflammatory factors, ROS, and bacterial metabolites. This capability provides healthcare professionals with more precise information regarding patient status while facilitating the formulation of personalized treatment strategies. Simultaneously, these devices can offer feedback on treatment efficacy and adjust parameters in real time, supporting remote monitoring efforts and comprehensive health management.

Conclusion

The management of CPDM has long been recognized as a significant clinical challenge. We have developed an innovative multifunctional microneedle patch enhanced by nanotherapeutics. The outcomes of both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrate that Met@ZIF-8@PDA MNs not only exhibit excellent biocompatibility and mechanical properties but also possess a robust immunomodulatory effect. This microneedle system effectively mitigates oxidative stress and diminishes inflammatory responses by inhibiting the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, thereby transforming the immune microenvironment into one that is highly favorable for bone regeneration. This antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenesis-promoting microneedle-based delivery system adeptly addresses the intricate pathophysiology associated with CPDM and presents a promising therapeutic strategy for its treatment and management. We believe that this type of medical microneedle, characterized by straightforward preparation, integrated functionalities, outstanding properties, and precise controllability, will establish a new trend in clinical applications.
