Associations between body composition and glycemic control: an exploratory analysis of bioelectrical impedance parameters in adolescents with type 1 diabetes
Authors: Arianna Torre, Bruno Bombaci, Mattia Papa, Sara Aramnejad, Mariarosaria La Rocca, Alessandra Di Pisa, Angela Alibrandi, Fortunato Lombardo, Giuseppina Salzano, Stefano Passanisi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between body composition and glycemic control in adolescents with type 1 diabetes (T1D) remains insufficiently explored. Clarifying the role of specific body compartments may provide insights into metabolic risk and inform personalized management strategies in this population. Our study aimed to describe body composition in adolescents with T1D and to evaluate the associations between anthropometric and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) derived parameters and markers of glycemic control.MethodsIn this exploratory cross-sectional study, 133 adolescents regularly followed at our Pediatric Diabetes Center between May and December 2024 were consecutively recruited. Inclusion criteria comprised a confirmed diagnosis of T1D, complete pubertal development, and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) use. Anthropometric measurements and body composition parameters were obtained using BIA. Glycemic control was evaluated through HbA1c, mean 12-month HbA1c, and CGM-derived metrics, including time in range (TIR), time in tight range (TITR).ResultsVisceral fat was positively correlated with both point-in-time HbA1c (r = 0.210, p = 0.016) and mean 12-month HbA1c (r = 0.216, p = 0.013), while no associations were observed with other BIA-derived parameters. TITR was independently associated with male sex (β = 0.244; p = 0.049), regular physical activity (β = 0.195; p = 0.041), use of automated insulin delivery systems (β = 0.405; p < 0.001), and BMI z-score (β = − 0.310, p = 0.016). TIR was inversely associated with basal metabolic rate (β = − 0.262, p = 0.012).ConclusionsIn adolescents with T1D, visceral adiposity showed weak but significant associations with both point-in-time and 12-month HbA1c levels. These findings are hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted cautiously given the modest effect sizes and study design. Further longitudinal studies using imaging-based methods are needed to clarify the clinical relevance of these associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02197-x.

Background

Visceral fat excess, a condition closely linked to obesity, has long been recognized as a major contributor to metabolic disturbances leading to insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes [1]. Beyond its metabolic effects, visceral adiposity represents a strong and independent predictor of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Its pathogenic role is largely mediated by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and adipokines, which promote endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and impaired insulin signaling [2].

Although the relevance of visceral adiposity has been extensively characterized in type 2 diabetes, its impact on individuals with type 1 diabetes (T1D) remains poorly understood. Recent advances in diabetes management, including continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) and automated insulin delivery (AID) systems, have markedly improved glycemic outcomes in young people with T1D [3]. However, these technological and therapeutic improvements have also shifted the metabolic phenotype of this population. The intensification of automated insulin delivery required to maintain glucose stability may lead to increased total insulin exposure, potentially contributing to higher rates of overweight and obesity among adolescents with T1D [4, 5]. This epidemiological shift raises important questions regarding the contribution of fat distribution, rather than total body mass, to glycemic variability and long-term metabolic health.

Body composition analysis provides valuable insights into the complex interplay between adipose tissue, lean mass, and metabolic control. In this context, bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) represents a practical, noninvasive method for estimating body compartments, offering a feasible approach for use in pediatric clinical settings [6]. Understanding the associations between BIA-derived parameters and glycemic metrics may therefore help identify early markers of metabolic imbalance in youth with T1D.

The present cross-sectional study was designed to describe body composition in a cohort of adolescents with T1D and to explore the relationships between anthropometric and bioimpedance-derived parameters and glycemic control.

Methods

This exploratory cross-sectional single center study consecutively recruited adolescents with T1D regularly followed at our Pediatric Diabetes Centre between May and December 2024. All eligible individuals during the study period were considered for enrollment. Participants were required to have a diagnosis of T1D according to the International Society for Pediatric and Adolescent Diabetes criteria [7], to be in complete pubertal development as defined by Tanner staging, and to use a glucose sensor for at least 70% of time. Exclusion criteria were the presence of uncontrolled comorbidities, any change in insulin delivery method within the three months prior to enrollment, and being in partial remission phase [8]. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Local Ethics Committee (no. 114/25). Written informed consent was obtained from participants and their parents or legal guardians. At enrolment, demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, biological sex, diabetes duration, comorbidities, weight, height, BMI expressed as z-score, waist and hip circumferences, as well as waist-to-height and waist-to-hip ratios. Adherence to regular physical activity was defined as participation in at least two sessions per week, each lasting no less than 60 min. Glycemic control was evaluated using both glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), measured at the time of enrollment and averaged over the previous twelve months, and continuous glucose monitoring metrics derived from the preceding 14 days. These included time in range (TIR, 70–180 mg/dL), time in tight range (TITR, 70–140 mg/dL), time above range (TAR > 180 mg/dL), time below range (TBR < 70 mg/dL), coefficient of variation (CV), glucose management indicator (GMI), mean sensor glucose, and its standard deviation. Ambulatory glucose profile data were retrieved from specific web-cloud platforms (i.e., Carelink System®, Libreview®, Dexcom Clarity®, Glooko® platforms). Data on total daily insulin dose and its distribution between basal and prandial percentages, were also recorded. Body composition was assessed using BIA. A vectorial Bluetooth bioimpedance analyzer (BioSmart™, Eupraxia s.r.l., Reggio Emilia, Italy) was used. This device employs a standardized tetrapolar configuration, in which four surface electrodes are placed to the dorsum of the hands and feet, and a low-level alternating current is applied to measure resistance and reactance. These impedance parameters were then used to estimate fat free mass (FFM), fat mass (FM), visceral fat (VF), basal metabolic rate (BMR), total body water (TBW), intracellular water (ICW), and extracellular water (ECW). The VF parameter provided by the device represents a proprietary model-derived estimate rather than a direct anatomical measurement. Considering the biometric characteristics of the cohort, BIA-derived VF values were normalized to height cubed to account for differences in body size, as well as variations related to sex and age, minimizing their influence on visceral fat estimation [9]. Measurements were performed under standardized conditions, including avoidance of recent vigorous physical activity immediately before assessment and routine outpatient fasting-state conditions.

Statistical analysis

No formal a priori sample size or power calculation was performed, as this was an exploratory, hypothesis-generating study. The sample size reflects the number of eligible adolescents followed at our center during the study period and was considered sufficient to explore associations, although the study may be underpowered to detect small effect sizes. Numerical data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were reported as absolute numbers and percentages. Most numerical variables showed approximately normal distribution according to the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and were therefore analyzed using parametric methods. Associations between glycemic control indicators and auxological or BIA-derived parameters were explored using Pearson correlation analysis. For those variables without a normal distribution, Spearman test was applied. To identify exploratory associations with TITR, multivariable linear regression models were applied, testing age, sex, diabetes duration, physical activity, use of AID therapy, BMI, and all auxological and BIA-derived parameters as covariates. Given the strong intercorrelation among several BIA–derived parameters, all variables were first explored in univariable analyses. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and only variables with acceptable collinearity (VIF < 5), along with clinical relevance, were retained in multivariable models. This approach was adopted to reduce overfitting and avoid redundancy among closely related body composition metrics. Additional univariable and multivariable regression analyses were conducted to evaluate exploratory associations with BMR. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

A total of 133 adolescents (mean age 16.4 ± 2.1 years; diabetes duration 7.7 ± 3.9 years) were included. The cohort was exclusively of Caucasian ethnicity, reflecting the local population served by our center. Females accounted for 50.4% of the cohort, and 51.9% reported regular physical activity. Most participants (69.2%) were treated with AID systems. The remaining practiced multiple daily injections. Detailed participants’ clinical and demographic data are reported in Table 1.

Overall, glycemic outcomes were favorable, as reflected by key CGM-derived metrics. TIR was 73.2 ± 13.0%, and TITR was 51.0 ± 10.7%, indicating that participants spent a substantial proportion of time within recommended glucose ranges. Point-in-time HbA1c was 6.8 ± 0.8%, and the 12-month mean HbA1c was 6.8 ± 0.7%, consistent with these CGM measures.

Anthropometric evaluation indicated a mean body weight of 64.1 ± 12.3 kg and a mean BMI z-score of 0.70 ± 0.98. According to BIA, mean body composition parameters were as follows: FFM 80.2 ± 9.3%, FM 19.1 ± 8.1%, MM 42.8 ± 9.5%, TBW 58.7 ± 8.9%. The mean VF/height3 value was 3.2 ± 7.8.

Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated significant associations between metabolic and anthropometric variables and glycemic control indicators. Specifically, HbA1c values were positively correlated with visceral fat (r = 0.210; p = 0.016). A similar association was observed with the 12-month HbA1c, which was also directly correlated with visceral fat (r = 0.216; p = 0.013) (Fig. 1). Because visceral fat values showed substantial dispersion, Spearman correlation analyses were also performed as sensitivity analyses, which showed comparable results (r = 0.221; p = 0.011) (Supplemental File 1). When stratified for sex, the above-mentioned associations were present only for males (Supplemental File 2). By contrast, neither baseline nor 12-month HbA1c values demonstrated significant correlations with other BIA-derived body composition parameters, including FFM, MM, and TBW, nor with anthropometric surrogates of visceral adiposity, such as waist-to-height and waist-to-hip ratios. In the sensitivity analysis adjusted for age, sex, diabetes duration, BMI, and AID use, VF was not independently associated with 12-month mean HbA1c (β = 0.016; p = 0.137) (Supplemental File 3).

Analysis of other glycemic metrics showed a significant positive correlation between CV and FFM (r = 0.181, p = 0.047). Mean daily blood glucose concentration was positively correlated to waist-to-hip ratio (r = 0.183, p = 0.049). No significant correlations were observed between any glycemic metric and FM (Table 2.).

In the multiple linear regression analysis, male sex (β = 0.244; p = 0.049), regular physical activity (β = 0.195; p = 0.041), use of AID (β = 0.405; p < 0.001), and BMI z-score (β = – 0.310, p = 0.016) were independently associated with TITR (Table 3).

A separate multiple linear regression analysis showed that age (β = 0.275; p < 0.001) and male sex (β = 0.625; p < 0.001) were positively associated, whereas TIR (β = – 0.262, p = 0.012) was inversely associated with BMR.

Discussion

In our exploratory cross-sectional cohort, visceral adiposity was weakly associated with both point-in-time and 12-month HbA1c values. While these associations support a link between fat distribution and glycemic control, the modest correlation coefficients and the attenuation found in the sensitivity analysis indicate limited effect size and raise questions regarding their clinical relevance at the individual level. These findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than indicative of a direct or determinant role of visceral fat on glycemia. The observed association may be biologically plausible, as visceral adipose tissue has been linked to insulin resistance and inflammatory pathways in previous studies [10]. Conversely, chronic hyperglycemia may further promote visceral fat accumulation through alterations in lipid metabolism, creating a bidirectional relationship between glycemic stability and visceral adiposity [11]. Because elevated HbA1c is a recognized marker of long-term diabetes complications [12–14], the observed association between visceral adiposity and HbA1c may warrant further investigation in studies incorporating cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes. However, the present design does not allow mechanistic interpretation and the reported findings should not be interpreted as evidence of increased cardiovascular risk. Ultrasound-measured visceral fat has previously been reported to correlate directly with the Framingham risk score and vascular age, with multivariate analyses identifying it as an independent predictor of cardiovascular risk [15]. While our study did not directly assess cardiovascular outcomes, these findings provide indirect support for the established link between visceral fat and cardiovascular risk, highlighting the value of assessing body composition beyond traditional anthropometric measures.

We also observed a significant positive correlation between the CV and FFM. This finding should be interpreted cautiously. Although skeletal muscle is the primary site of insulin-mediated glucose uptake [16, 17], the absence of consistent associations between FFM and other glycemic metrics limits the strength of any mechanistic interpretation. This result may therefore reflect residual confounding or chance rather than a clinically meaningful relationship.

In our cohort, TITR was associated by both physical activity and BMI. Regular exercise is a well-established component of T1D management, associated with improved HbA1c, enhanced insulin sensitivity, and reduced cardiovascular risk [18]. Our data extend this evidence by showing that adolescents engaging in regular physical activity, approximately 48% of the cohort, also exhibited improvements in CGM-derived metrics. Higher BMI values were also associated with lower percentages of TITR. This suggests that overweight and obesity may hinder the achievement of glycemic targets. Interestingly, no other BIA-derived body composition parameters were associated with TITR. This may reflect the overall favorable glycemic control and reduced variability of the cohort, limiting the ability to detect clinically meaningful associations. Additionally, the widespread use of AID therapy in our cohort, the variable most strongly associated with TITR in our analysis, may have attenuated observable associations between body composition parameters and TITR. AID systems are considered the standard of care for children and adolescents with T1D, regardless of age or disease duration, by optimizing glycemic control and enhancing quality of life [19, 20]. Evidence from previous studies has consistently demonstrated the superiority of AID technology over earlier treatment modalities in achieving glycemic targets and reducing the risk of long-term complications [19, 21–23].

Another notable finding was the significant association between TIR and BMR, the minimum energy expenditure required to sustain vital functions at rest [24]. Evidence from adults with type 2 diabetes indicates that elevated BMR is linked to increased visceral adiposity, higher fasting plasma glucose, and altered HOMA-IR [25]. Our findings may support the possibility of an association between glycemic stability and energy expenditure. Chronic hyperglycemia may activate alternative metabolic pathways, increase oxidative stress and promote a pro-inflammatory environment, which in turn elevates energy expenditure [26].

Some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, its cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal inferences, limiting the strength of the associations detected by the statistical analyses. Our analysis did not include specific biochemical or imaging markers of cardiovascular risk, which could have strengthened the interpretation of the observed association between visceral adiposity and glycemic target. Although BIA provides a practical and noninvasive approach to assess body composition, it remains less accurate than gold-standard techniques such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry or magnetic resonance imaging. Furthermore, because BIA-derived visceral fat is a surrogate estimate generated through proprietary algorithms, measurement error may have attenuated weak associations. Additional limitations include the use of multiple CGM systems, which may have introduced measurement variability, and the absence of a healthy control group. Finally, although cohort homogeneity reduces confounding related to differences in clinical management, it also limits generalizability. The predominance of adolescents achieving glycemic targets (mean TIR > 70%) and limited ethnic diversity mean that these findings may not be representative of the broader population of adolescents with T1D.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this exploratory cross-sectional study identified weak associations between visceral adiposity and HbA1c in adolescents with T1D. The restricted glycemic variability of this well-controlled cohort may have limited the detection of stronger relationships. Given the modest effect sizes, restricted glycemic variability, and the use of a BIA-derived surrogate marker, these findings should primarily inform future longitudinal and imaging-based studies rather than current clinical practice.
