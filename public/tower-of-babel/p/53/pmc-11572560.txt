The Efficacy of Modified Ilizarov Distraction‐Tension Device on Limb Wound Difficult to Cover with Skin Flaps
Authors: Yueliang Zhu, Sunwen Pan, Yongqing Xu, Jian Shi, Zhen Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionClosure of complex limb wounds poses challenges and requires innovative approaches. This research aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a modified distraction‐tension device using Ilizarov external fixation for wound closure in challenging cases.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 43 patients with extremity wounds that were difficult to cover with skin flaps between January 2019 and December 2022. Tension‐relieving traction was applied using the Ilizarovexternal fixator apparatus, tailored to individual wound characteristics. Three types of wire–pin connections were used in this study. The distraction begins on the third postoperative day, with a speed of 0.5mm/d. Clinical wound healing scores were evaluated at 5 and 30 days postoperatively. Complications were documented following the Paley classification system.ResultsTraction using modified Ilizarovexternal fixation promoted a significant reduction in wound size. The mean traction period was 11.2 ± 7.3 days, and the mean healing duration was 17.0 ± 3.7 days. The clinical wound healing score improved from 3.7 ± 2.9 at 5 days to 1.7 ± 0.7 at 30 days postoperatively (p < 0.05). Complications were minimal, with no significant obstacles or sequelae observed. Direct closure healing was achieved in 21 cases, skin graft healing in 13 cases, and suture healing in 9 cases. No recurrences were reported. Using Paley's classified complications, there were 17 problems, 9 obstacles, and 0 sequelae.ConclusionThe Ilizarov tension‐relieving traction shows promise in facilitating wound closure that is challenging to manage with skin flaps. The modified three types of pin–skin connection configuration could satisfy various types of wound closure.Keywords: Complex Limb Wounds, Ilizarov Tension‐Relieving Traction, Modified Ilizarov external fixation, Skin Flaps, Wound Closure Effectiveness

Introduction

The management of limb wounds encompasses a range of conventional techniques that prove successful in most cases. However, a subset of wounds presents as challenging scenarios that defy resolution despite the implementation of various established treatment modalities. 1 , 2 Large soft tissue defects or chronic nonhealing wounds pose considerable difficulties in achieving successful closure. 3 Usually, flap surgery is the first choice for wound closure. However, there are situations where flap technique could not be difficult to use, such as chronic wounds in a scarred limb with many surgeries before. In this case, the local flap would be impossible because there are no flap donor sites within the scarred area. Free flap transfer would also be difficult because there are hardly normal recipient vessels for the anastomoses. To reconstruct the flap circulation, we have to do additional vessel transfer or use the cross‐limb technique, and both methods are time‐consuming and technique‐requiring. 4 , 5 The patients who have already experienced a failed flap surgery seem to prefer another solution if it exists. And for those with diabetes and long‐term smoking, the free flap may be a highly risky surgery. Therefore, it is still necessary to find solutions that could replace flap surgery for wound closures. There are a few devices that were invented and used for this purpose, but they are primarily designed for the acute closure of fresh wounds. Reports on the direct application of tension and compression techniques for managing soft tissue defects remain limited and fragmented within the existing literature and mainly for fresh wounds. 6 , 7 , 8 Ilizarov designed his apparatus to close fresh wounds that could not be directly sutured. 9 There is limited literature on chronic wound closure research.

In the past years, we have continually explored and made various attempts to close wounds on the base of the Ilizarov apparatus. The configuration of the apparatus was continually modified according to the needs of the clinic. The ideal configuration should be stable, flexible, and user‐friendly with a reliable skin‐frame connection. It was expected to accomplish the closure of wounds with various depth, length, and width using only classical Ilizarov components. Considering these considerations, we modified the original Ilizarov wound closure system to three basic configurations of skin‐frame connection. We conducted a retrospective case series analysis, scrutinizing the clinical data of 43 patients with limb wounds that proved arduous to cover using skin flaps.

The primary objectives of this study were to (i) comprehensively evaluate the efficacy of a novel distraction‐tension device incorporating Ilizarov external fixation in the management of complex limb wounds, with a comparative analysis against established conventional techniques; (ii)meticulously assess the device's impact on wound dimension reduction, expedited healing processes, improved clinical evaluation metrics, and the mitigation of adverse events; (iii) determine the device's appropriateness for specific anatomical regions by conducting detailed anatomical scrutiny, particularly focusing on amputation stumps and the intricate plantar surfaces of the foot, thus elucidating its potential for tailored applications in addressing diverse anatomical complexities.

Methods

General Information

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) wounds located in the extremities; (2) ineligibility for skin flap coverage surgery; (3) previous failed skin flap surgery; (4) refusal of skin flap surgery.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) amputation during the treatment process; (2) concurrent diabetes or immune system diseases; (3) removal of external fixation due to inability to tolerate it.

A total of 43 patients with limb wounds that were difficult to cover with skin flaps were included in the study, including 25 males and 18 females, with an age range of 9–75 years (mean age: 47.8 ± 11.6 years) between January 2019 and December 2022. The causes of injury were as follows: 19 cases of traffic accidents, 11 cases of heavy object crush injuries, 7 cases of machinery crush injuries, and 6 cases of iatrogenic skin flap harvesting. Additionally, 7 cases had tibia and fibula fractures, 4 cases had femur fractures, 3 cases had forearm fractures, and 2 cases had hand fractures. The wound locations were as follows: 33 cases in the lower extremities (7 cases in the thigh, 17 cases in the lower leg, and 9 cases in the foot and ankle) and 10 cases in the upper extremities (2 cases in the arm, 7 cases in the forearm, and 1 case in the hand). The reasons for the difficulty in covering the wounds with skin flaps were as follows: 10 cases due to previously failed skin flaps, 10 cases due to lack of adjacent flap donor sites in scarred limbs, 7 cases of composite defects involving 2–5 digits and soft tissues, 8 cases with existing external fixation around the wounds, which increased difficulty for flap transfer, and 8 cases with multiple contributing factors. The size of the skin defects ranged from 1.2 cm × 1.0 cm to 32.3 cm × 18.1 cm. The duration of the wounds ranged from 2 to 9 months (mean duration: 4.5 ± 1.7 months). All patients provided informed consent. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the 920th Hospital of the Chinese People's Liberation Army Joint Logistics Support Force (NO.920IEC/AF/61/2021‐01.1).

Treatment Methods

Distraction‐Tension Device

This device was assembled with Ilizarov external fixation components and developed on the “Device for Assisting Wound Auto‐Recovery Growth” (Patent No.: ZL201620663808.2, China). The main material of this device is stainless steel, manufactured by the Beijing Institute of External Skeletal Fixation Technology. Although this device's appearance varies significantly, it consists of three basic components: base, distraction rods, and wire–skin connection. The base serves to stabilize the entire structure by fixing the ring via half‐pins or K‐wires to the bone. The distraction device consists of a threaded rod and posts connected to the base. By adjusting the nut on the rods, distraction and compression forces can be applied, generating the dynamic force for wound closure. The wire–skin connection was connected to the distraction rods. Refer to Figure 1 for details.

Modified Ilizarov distraction‐tension device for wound closure. The base usually involves two rings or half‐rings. The rings are located in the proximal and distal ends of the wound and are connected by threaded rods. The rings were fixed on the bone via half‐pins or K‐wires. Distraction‐tension devices are threaded rods through posts that were connected to the rings. Turning the nut (green arrows) on the three rods could produce distraction or compression forces (blue arrows) via the movements of the threaded rods. Wire–pin connection is of three types, discussed in the following. (1) Parallel pin–skin connection: The K‐wires are inset subcutaneously and parallelly to the wound length. This configuration is indicated for long wounds. (2) Hook–pin connections: The k‐wire ends were bent and hooked to the skin and subcutaneous tissue. The configuration is indicated for short and deep wounds with strong skins, such as the skin of the plantar foot. (3) Olive wire–skin connection. It consists of olive wires and washers of different sizes. Usually, a rubber penicillin vial or a gauze is inset between the washers and skin as a cushion. This configuration is very flexible and could be used in various wounds.

Surgical Techniques

Following the administration of general anesthesia, the surgical intervention involved the excision of necrotic and infected tissues. The wound was meticulously irrigated using a combination of hydrogen peroxide and normal saline. The base of the device was then meticulously installed, considering the anatomical location and form of the wound in the affected limb. First, an appropriate site adjacent to the incision for the installation of a suitable base must be located. Employ a Kirschner wire or a 4mm half‐pin to penetrate the tissue, subsequently establishing a connection with a ring or a half‐ring fixation frame. Subsequently, based on the configuration of the wound, introduce K‐wires using one of the following techniques: a parallel pin–skin connection, a hook–pin connection, or an olive wire–skin connection, as depicted in Figure 1. Following this step, establish a linkage between the wires and the base. Subsequently, rotate the nut by one to two turns to assess the magnitude of the resultant force. In situations where patients had an existing external fixation device and were concurrently undergoing fracture fixation, bone transport, or deformity correction procedures, the preexisting treatment plan was continued, and the device was constructed directly using the existing filters. Proper disinfection of the pin tract was accomplished using 75% ethanol, followed by meticulous wound dressing with a gauze soaked in normal saline.

Postoperative Management

Postoperatively, the patient was started on a regimen aimed at reducing postoperative swelling, managing pain, and preventing deep vein thrombosis in the lower extremities. Early mobilization exercises were encouraged while ensuring meticulous maintenance of the cleanliness of the needle tract. Any exudate or bloodstains were promptly addressed by gentle cleaning using cotton swabs soaked in normal saline. Pin tracts exhibiting a thin yellowish dry bone scab were left undressed, without the application of additional dressings or disinfectants. On the day following the surgical procedure, a gradual adjustment of tension on the threaded rod was initiated, using the distraction and compression nuts. The recommended compression rate was typically set at 0.5 mm/day and administered in three increments. However, the rate could be adjusted according to the specific characteristics of the wound. Daily assessments of the skin perfusion surrounding the wound were conducted to identify and prevent the cut of the wire pins and disconnection of the wire and skin. If there was an inclination of the cut, the distraction rate was reduced accordingly. In the event of cut or failed wire–skin connections, reconnection was performed using healthy soft tissues adjacent to the wound. This procedure was usually performed in the wards.

Observational Parameters

The evaluation of wound healing was performed using the Clinical Healing Score, 10 assessed at 5 and 30 days postoperatively. The scoring criteria were as follows: 1 point for the absence of soft tissue reaction at the wound site, 2 points for mild edema or erythema, 3 points for inflammatory reactions exceeding 1 cm along the suture line accompanied by pain or redness, 4 points for the formation of hematomas or abscesses, and 5 points for wound dehiscence, skin necrosis, or the inability to achieve primary closure. Complications were recorded following the Paley classification, which categorizes complications as problems (mild complications), obstacles, or sequelae. 11 Problems do not require additional surgical intervention, obstacles necessitate supplementary surgical procedures for resolution, and sequelae denote complications that persist despite the completion of treatment.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using the SPSS 17.0 software package (IBM, USA). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous data were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and paired sample t‐tests were employed for comparisons. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General Results

All patients were followed up for a duration ranging from 3 to 11 months (mean ± SD: 4.5 ± 1.7 months). The duration of reduction and traction after wound surgery ranged from 6 to 23 days (mean ± SD: 11.2 ± 7.3 days). The mean time required for wound healing was 17.0 ± 3.7 days, with a range of 15–47 days. At the 5‐day postoperative assessment, the clinical wound healing score was 3.7 ± 2.9, which significantly improved to 1.7 ± 0.7 at the 30‐day evaluation (t = 6.923, p < 0.001). Notably, all patients exhibited a reduction in wound exudate and observed favorable granulation tissue growth.

Complications

The recorded complications, according to the Paley classification, included 17 problems, 9 obstacles, and no sequelae. Among the problems, 11 cases involved pin tract infections, which resolved spontaneously after the removal or replacement of the wires and pins. Nine cases presented with pin loosening or skin cut, which was effectively addressed through bedside reconnection as described before.

Functional Results and Typical Cases

Of this series, 21 demonstrated direct clinical wound closure, 8 cases achieved healing following skin grafting, and 14 cases healed through suturing. Throughout the follow‐up period, no instances of wound recurrence were observed. Illustrative cases are depicted in Figures 2, 3, 4.

A male patient, 35 years old. Three months following the completion of tibial open bone defect reconstruction and bone grafting. The tension‐induced ossification zone in the anterior aspect of the proximal tibia exhibited signs of bone infection. After meticulous wound debridement and dressing changes, a residual bone and soft tissue defect measuring 5 cm× 3 cm persisted. The surrounding soft tissues presented with fibrotic scarring, posing challenges for both adjacent skin flap transfer and free flap coverage (attributable to the limited availability of suitable vascular anastomosis sites). To address the medial aspect of the defect, a 3.0 cm× 1.5 cm osteotomy was performed, and a gradual traction system employing an olive wire–skin connection was employed to progressively mobilize the bone and skin from the inner to the outer aspect (A). The traction pins were retained with additional pressure. Radiographic analysis before traction (B) delineated the osteotomy line represented by green dotted lines; yellow denotes the olive wires, and the arrows indicate the direction of traction. After three months, traction was discontinued, and the traction bone segment achieved osseous union with the tibial cortex on the outer aspect (C). Traction was administered gradually, at a rate of 0.5 mm/day, administered thrice daily, resulting in wound closure after one month (D). Subsequently, the external fixation was removed, and during the four‐month postoperative follow‐up, the skin exhibited complete healing without any signs of recurrence (E).

A male patient, 27 years old, presented with bilateral calcaneal comminuted fractures following a fall from a height. Open reduction and internal fixation with plates were performed, but the incision became infected. Bilateral fibular artery perforator propeller flaps were utilized for wound coverage; however, the wound became infected, leading to distal flap necrosis. The wound remained exposed (A,B). After one month of repeated debridement and dressing changes, a gradual traction device was implemented with a smaller wound size, using a hook–skin connection. Postoperatively, traction was gradually applied by rotating the nut three times a day, one side at a time, at a rate of 0.5 mm/day (C,D). After 10 days of traction, wound edges were approximated, and fixation was continued for an additional 15 days until wound closure was achieved in the right foot, leading to the removal of external fixation. The left side underwent sutured mattress closure. At the three‐month follow‐up, bilateral calcaneal bones exhibited deformity during the healing process, but the wounds healed without recurrence (E,F).

A female patient, 61 years old, presented with bilateral lower limb injuries resulting from the forceful impact of a mudslide. The distal tibia and fibula exhibited an open fracture, accompanied by extensive soft tissue degloving injuries and defects. Notably, both tendons and bones were exposed, as evidenced by the marked regions (A, highlighted in green). Following meticulous wound debridement on two occasions, a traction device was employed, utilizing an olive wire–skin connection system to facilitate bidirectional skin traction (B). The traction protocol was maintained at a consistent rate, with daily dressing changes. After three weeks, a reduction in wound size was observed, concomitant with the robust development of healthy granulation tissue. Notably, exuberant granulation tissue formation was evident on the previously exposed surface of the tibia (C), necessitating the implementation of a skin graft to achieve definitive wound closure. Subsequently, the patient underwent bilateral ankle joint fusion six months postoperatively. At the one‐year follow‐up, the patient demonstrated improved ambulatory function with the aid of an assistive device. However, it is important to note that a residual posterior tilt deformity of the right tibial plateau persisted (D).

Discussion

The investigation into a modified distraction‐tension device employing Ilizarov external fixation for complex limb wounds unveiled notable reductions in wound dimensions, expedited healing timelines, and enhanced clinical wound healing assessments. Minimal complications were observed, underscoring the device's efficacy in fostering granulation tissue growth and advancing wound closure processes.

Limitations of Conventional Methods

Diverse subspecialties employ distinct therapeutic approaches for such complex wounds. Plastic surgery prioritizes the utilization of skin flaps for effective wound coverage, emphasizing meticulous planning of flap donor and recipient sites, including innovative concepts such as chimeric flaps for large and complex wound coverage. 12 , 13 , 14 Burn surgery emphasizes advanced dressing techniques 15 and spearheads the research in wound regeneration. 16 Negative pressure wound therapy is a widely adopted method across various specialties, facilitating granulation tissue growth and wound healing. 17 , 18 However, in certain instances, conventional methods employed by various specialties and techniques may prove insufficient in addressing specific wound types. Moreover, the installation of Vacuum Sealing Drainage can present challenges, particularly in cases where patients already have external fixation devices in place. Consequently, there exists a need to develop alternative approaches that address the limitations associated with existing methods.

Methodological Advantages and Applicability of the Modified Device

This study's methodology offers a valuable solution for the following scenarios. First, it is particularly well‐suited for chronic wounds accompanied by extensive surrounding scar tissue. Such cases present challenges for both local and free flap procedures. Second, it provides an effective option for patients who have experienced previous flap failures. Third, this technique serves as an alternative for patients who decline flap surgery due to concerns regarding additional donor site scarring. Fourth, it is a suitable option for individuals at high risk of flap transfer failure, such as elderly patients, those with diabetes, long‐term smokers, or individuals with peripheral arterial disease. Lastly, it is particularly advantageous for treating small, deep wounds where the use of flaps may be excessive and unnecessary.

Anatomical Considerations and Site‐Specific Applications

The method is most applicable to specific anatomical sites, including amputation stumps and the plantar surfaces of the foot. The skin in these areas offers robust needle–skin contact, facilitating effective traction. Additionally, this technique proves beneficial for wounds presenting with bone defects or open fractures, as the needle–bone contact provides enhanced stability and strength compared with needle–skin contact alone. Moreover, it is especially advantageous for cases where patients already have external fixation devices in place, particularly Ilizarov external fixators. Using this straightforward and user‐friendly device, the need for additional base installation is obviated. This approach enables the utilization of local resources and facilitates a tailored treatment strategy based on the specific requirements of each case. The application of tensional forces during wound healing has been found to promote the regeneration process in addition to the closure achieved through compression and distraction at the wound edges. 19 Our research group has observed accelerated granulation tissue growth in patients treated with the tensional distraction method, which undoubtedly contributes to enhanced wound healing. It is worth noting that even in scar tissue, cellular regeneration occurs during the slow stretching process, indicating that the stretching of the skin does not result in a decrease in thickness but rather an increase in surface area through the generation of new skin cells. 20

Comparison with Traditional Techniques and Advantages of the Modified Device

In cases involving wounds in the lower extremities, the most intricate approach historically involved the use of cross‐leg free flaps, which entailed harvesting a flap from the contralateral leg and anastomosing the vascular pedicles of the flap with those of the recipient site. 21 , 22 This technique is associated with considerable technical complexity, and patients experience significant difficulties in their daily lives due to forced immobilization of both lower limbs for a period exceeding 4 weeks. The device used in this study could be an optimal choice for such patients, considering the simplicity of the technique and the easy availability of the Ilizarov apparatus.

In comparison with the aforementioned skin flap remedial techniques, the device and method employed in this study offer several advantages. They are easily mastered by orthopedic surgeons, cost‐effective, and allow for direct removal at the bedside or in outpatient settings after wound healing. The skin expansion technique through injection requires pre‐implantation of expanders and regular water injection over several months (in this study, the duration of traction was 6–23 days). 23 , 24 , 25 Moreover, this technique is primarily familiar to plastic and burn surgeons rather than orthopedic surgeons.

Biocompatibility and Component Analysis of the Modified Device

Stahl's skin traction device connects to the skin by adhering to it. 26 The adhesive nature of the traction device makes it prone to detachment, requiring multiple replacements during use, and may lead to soft tissue allergies and other complications. 27 Our device's components are made of medical‐grade stainless steel, ensuring biocompatibility and fewer allergies. Skin traction belts from Hojmed can also reduce wound size. However, silicone tapes and traditional traction belts have difficulties controlling the elasticity of the silicone tube, and they are prone to detachment and wound infection. 28 , 29 Additionally, they require additional plaster or support devices to fix the surrounding skin to ensure therapeutic efficacy. In contrast, our device's metal components have minimal elasticity issues and complications. Furthermore, it provides direct fixation of the surrounding skin and bone, resulting in a more stable and reliable closure. A stable and rigid fixation was helpful for granulation regeneration, especially suitable for ischemic limb wounds, because microcirculation reconstruction is simultaneously finished. 30

Surgical Considerations and Complication Management

Surgical considerations and prevention of complications using the modified distraction‐tension device include ensuring the stability of the external fixation base, securely fastening the wire–skin connection, and applying distraction along the tangent of the limb cross‐section. Soft tissue cutting is the main local complication of this technique. To reduce cutting incidents, attention should be given to selecting the appropriate wire–skin connection based on wound characteristics and adjusting the compression rate. In certain cases, with adherent scar tissue, periosteotomy can be performed to achieve closure.

Limitations and Strengths

The study shows cases of innovation in addressing intricate limb wounds through the implementation of a modified distraction‐tension device using Ilizarov external fixation. It presents a bespoke solution for challenging wound closures, with a specific focus on distinct anatomical sites and diverse wound presentations, enriching practical insights within clinical contexts. The retrospective nature of the study potentially introduces biases, constraining generalizability. With a sample size of 43 patients, the study's statistical robustness may be limited. Larger‐scale investigations are essential to corroborate the device's efficacy across heterogeneous populations. Moreover, the study's short‐term follow‐up duration may overlook enduring outcomes and latent complications, underscoring the necessity for prolonged observations in future research endeavors.

Conclusion

The modified Ilizarov tension‐relieving traction method, combined with three types of pin–skin connections, has shown promising results in facilitating wound closure for challenging cases. The approach resulted in significant reductions in wound size, improved clinical healing scores, and minimal complications. This innovative technique holds the potential for managing complex limb wounds that are difficult to address with conventional skin flaps. Indeed, to establish the efficacy of this device, further validation through larger sample sizes and long‐term follow‐up of additional cases is warranted. While the preliminary findings presented in this study demonstrate promising outcomes, a robust evaluation involving a greater number of patients would provide stronger evidence regarding the effectiveness and reliability of this method.
