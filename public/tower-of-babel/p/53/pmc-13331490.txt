Juvenile ankylosing spondylitis unmasked by severe aortic regurgitation and subaortic bump
Authors: Manjit Mahendran, Jothir Surya Teja Yallanti, Sourabh Agstam, Sivasubramanian Ramakrishnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEnthesitis-related arthritis is a subtype of juvenile idiopathic arthritis that may precede or later progress to ankylosing spondylitis in adulthood. We describe a case of enthesitis-related arthritis diagnosed based on characteristic echocardiographic findings of aortitis, subaortic bump and severe aortic regurgitation.

INTRODUCTION

Enthesitis-related arthritis is a type of juvenile idiopathic arthritis that often precedes or evolves into ankylosing spondylitis in adults. We report here a case of enthesitis-related arthritis diagnosed based on characteristic echocardiographic findings and severe aortic regurgitation (AR).

CASE PRESENTATION

A 5-year-old boy presented to the cardiology outpatient department with complaints of easy fatiguability, exertional palpitations, back pain, and poor weight gain. Physical examination revealed mild bilateral knee swelling. Cardiovascular examination revealed prominent carotid pulsation and an outward and downward shifted apical impulse. On auscultation, a grade 3/4, early diastolic murmur was heard over the left 3rd parasternal area, occupying almost the whole diastole. The electrocardiogram showed a normal sinus rhythm with prominent “R” in V5 and V6, prominent “S” in V1, and “q” waves in V5 and V6, suggesting left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy (volume overload). V1 and V2 had T-wave inversion suggestive of persistent juvenile T-wave inversion. A 2D transthoracic echocardiogram (2D TTE) showed a tricuspid aortic valve with nodular thickening and inward rolling of the non-coronary cusp and right coronary cusp, resulting in left coronary cusp (LCC) prolapse and a significant coaptation gap [Figures 1-3]. There was also evidence of aortitis in the form of thickening of the posterior wall of the aorta. A subaortic bump was noted at the aortomitral continuity. Color Doppler showed severe AR and a holodiastolic flow reversal in the descending aorta (thoracic and abdominal aorta). The regurgitation jet through the coaptation gap is shown in Figure 4. The LV was dilated, LV end systolic internal diameter was 33 mm (Z score + 9.0), and LV end diastolic internal diameter was 44 mm (Z + 6.1). Aortic annulus diameter was 19 mm (Z + 8.3), aortic sinus diameter was 24 mm (Z + 7.1), and ascending aorta diameter was 22 mm (Z + 7.0). The anterior mitral leaflet was thickened, and there was no mitral regurgitation. The morphological features of the aortic and mitral valves were not suggestive of rheumatic heart disease. There was global LV hypokinesia with mild LV systolic dysfunction with an ejection fraction (EF) of 50%.

Blood investigations revealed a hemoglobin level of 6.7g/dL, suggestive of severe anemia; a markedly elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 56 mm/h, and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 39.2 mg/L, suggestive of chronic inflammation. Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCAs) were negative. There was no family history of arthritis or psoriasis.

A differential diagnosis, including enthesitis-related arthritis, reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and syphilitic aortitis, was considered. The HLA-B27 test performed during the evaluation was reported as positive. In the background of chronic inflammatory back pain and arthritis, elevated CRP, age <16 years, positive HLA-B27, and characteristic echocardiographic findings of aortitis, subaortic bump, and AR, a diagnosis of enthesitis-related arthritis was made according to the International League of Associations for Rheumatology revised classification criteria.[1]

The patient was started on weight-based doses of losartan and furosemide. He also received naproxen for a short duration for arthritis. At the 2-year follow-up, the child was asymptomatic with adequate weight gain, no palpitations, and no joint pains. Echocardiography showed severe AR, and the left ventricular ejection fraction had improved to 65%. The patient is currently on medical therapy with losartan and furosemide. He will be followed up with echocardiographic monitoring for worsening LV function, AR severity, and aortic root dilatation. If these parameters worsen, a surgical valve repair/replacement will be considered.

DISCUSSION

Enthesitis-related arthritis is a type of juvenile idiopathic arthritis that often precedes or evolves into ankylosing spondylitis in adults. It is characterized by sacroiliitis, enthesitis, inflammatory bowel disease, and cardiac manifestations such as AR, aortitis, atrioventricular block, or bundle branch blocks. Echocardiographic findings of the aorta in adult-defined ankylosing spondylitis include aortic root wall thickening (>2.2 mm), increased aortic root diameter, increased length and height of aortomitral junction (>7.7 mm and > 3.2 mm, respectively), increased aortic root stiffness, and increased mitral and aortic valve thickness.[2] In our patient, the posterior wall thickness was 4 mm, the subaortic aortomitral junction was 9 mm long and 4 mm thick. AR is seen in chronic ankylosing spondylitis patients in an age-dependent manner, with a prevalence increasing from 20% in the sixth decade to 30% in the seventh decade and 56% in the eighth decade of life.[3] In enthesitis-related arthritis, an increase in aortic root diameter is well documented, but AR is rarely reported.[4] AR is mainly reported in the long-term follow-up of patients with enthesitis-related arthritis who progress to ankylosing spondylitis in adulthood. The mechanism of AR in ankylosing spondylitis is as follows: aortic root dilatation leads to thickening and shortening of the aortic cusps, which, in turn, cause inward rolling, resulting in cusp prolapse and a coaptation gap, ultimately leading to AR.[5] Mitral regurgitation is rarely seen in patients with ankylosing spondylitis. Aortitis is a characteristic feature of ankylosing spondylitis, caused by adventitial scarring and intimal proliferation.[67] The inflammatory path may extend below the aortic annulus, to the aortomitral continuity, and sometimes into the interventricular septum. The thickened aortomitral junction progresses to a subaortic fibrous ridge, which is seen in echocardiography as a “subaortic bump.” Thus, aortitis and aortic root dilatation due to chronic inflammation result in a characteristic “subaortic bump”, cusp prolapse, and severe AR as seen in this case of enthesitis-related arthritis.
