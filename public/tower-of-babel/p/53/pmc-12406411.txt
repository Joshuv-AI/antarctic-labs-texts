Vitamin B levels in older adults with pre-frailty and frailty: the impact of MTHFR and TCN2 polymorphisms and their association with global DNA methylation and physical performance
Authors: Pitaksin Chitta, Busadee Pratumvinit, Witchayaporn Kaewboonruang, Atchara Dawangpa, Saliltip Khamrangsee, Prasert Assantachai, Rujapope Sutiwisesak, Wongsathit Wongloet, Tewin Tencomnao, Chanachai Sae-Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty syndrome poses significant challenges in older populations. Understanding the genetic and biochemical factors associated with frailty is essential for effective management strategies.MethodsIn this study, Thai older adults (≥ 60 years, n = 170) were assessed for physical parameters, levels of B vitamins, creatinine, and homocysteine. The polymorphisms of methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) (677 C > T) and Transcobalamin II (TCN2) 776G > C were identified. Global DNA methylation (global DNAm) was assessed using a colorimetric assay.ResultsParticipants were categorised into robust (n = 61), pre-frail (n = 62), and frail (n = 47) groups by Fried criteria and Kihon checklist. The physical parameters, including chair stand, functional reach, gait speed, and handgrip strength, showed highly significant differences among the groups (p < 0.01). Significant differences in folate and vitamin B6 concentrations were observed between MTHFR and TCN2 genotypes, respectively. In addition, global DNAm levels were significantly lower in pre-frail individuals, particularly among those carrying the MTHFR C677T genotype, compared to both robust and frail groups. Notably, lower global DNAm was associated with a higher likelihood of being classified as pre-frail rather than frail, and a lower likelihood of being pre-frail compared to robust individuals. Moreover, correlation analyses revealed significant associations among physical parameters, clinical characteristics, and global DNAm.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated the interplay between genetic variants, micronutrient status, and epigenetic modifications in the context of frailty among older adults. These findings highlight the potential of epigenetic and metabolic markers in identifying early frailty, though longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to further clarify causal pathways.Trial registrationThis study was duly registered with the Thai Clinical Trial under the identifier TCTR20240626002 (date of registration: 21/06/2024).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01004-0.

Introduction

Frailty, one of age-related syndromes is a multifaceted condition influenced by a myriad of factors, including genetics, biology, physical health, mental well-being, social interactions, and environmental circumstances [1]. In addition, frailty is recognized as becoming increasingly prevalent with advancing age and as carrying a risk for adverse health consequences such as incident age-related diseases, falls, hospitalization, and mortality [2–4]. To specify frailty, Fried et al. proposed a standardized definition, which entails the simultaneous presence of three to five criteria as follows: self-reported exhaustion, diminished physical activity, slow walking speed, weakened handgrip strength, and unintentional weight loss [5]. Furthermore, there has been vigorous pursuit of biological parameters associated with clinical frailty. Various studies revealed that plasma IL-6 levels, total cholesterol and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol were observed to be strongly associated with frailty syndrome [6–8].

As for most methylation reactions within mammalian cells, the key methyl donor for the epigenome is S-adenosyl methionine (SAM), which is generated during the methionine cycle via the one-carbon metabolism [9]. Folate, vitamin B12, choline, betaine and methionine act as methyl donors in this pathway, while vitamin B2 and vitamin B6 are cofactors for one-carbon transfers [10]. One-carbon metabolism is a biochemical pathway that provides methyl groups essential for various biological methylation reactions, including those involving proteins, phospholipids, and nucleic acids. Additionally, methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR), an essential enzyme, facilitates the conversion of 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate to 5-methyltetrahydrofolate, which is the primary methyl donor for the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine via methionine synthase and vitamin B12 [11, 12]. While, transcobalamin II (TCN2), an important protein, is required for the transport of cobalamin from the circulating into the cells [13]. Numerous studies have highlighted the association between alterations in water-soluble B vitamins and ageing [14, 15]. Plasma homocysteine concentration has been found to exhibit a positive correlation with age, demonstrating cross-sectional correlations with folate, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, creatinine, cysteine, and urea [16, 17]. Moreover, previous studies demonstrated that elevated concentrations of homocysteine, folate, and vitamin B12 were also associated with variants of MTHFR polymorphism (677 C > T). The mutation of the MTHFR gene, which causes the C677T polymorphism, is located at exon 4, leading to the conversion of valine to alanine at codon 222 [18]. This heterozygous polymorphism results in reduced activity of the MTHFR enzyme. Homozygous mutated (T677T) individuals exhibit higher homocysteine levels, while heterozygous mutated (C677T) individuals have mildly elevated homocysteine levels compared to normal, non-mutated controls [18, 19]. Besides, the variants of TCN2 polymorphism (776G > C) have been reported as the potential influencers of the cellular expression and plasma concentration of TCN2 protein, as well as cobalamin availability [13, 20]. It has been reported that individuals with heterozygous G776C for the TCN2 polymorphism exhibit higher homocysteine concentrations compared to those who are homozygous (G776G and C776C) [21, 22].

DNA methylation (DNAm) is an epigenetic modification in which a DNA methyltransferase catalyses the transfer of a methyl group from SAM to the 5-carbon of cytosine to form 5-methylcytosine (5-mC), and in which SAM is converted to S-adenosylhomocysteine [23]. Various studies have shown that the differential DNAm was also associated with changes in water-soluble B vitamin levels in the plasma, including vitamin B6, folate, vitamin B12, and homocysteine [9, 24–26]. Moreover, aberrations in DNAm are associated with various diseases [27, 28], emphasising the importance of nutrition in influencing epigenetic modifications linked to health and diseases, including frailty, obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, osteoporosis, dementia, depression, and cancer. Kmiołek et al. revealed that age influenced global DNAm, telomere length, as well as the level of total oxidant stress in frailty syndrome patients [29].

Understanding the associations among genetic variations in water-soluble B vitamins, global DNAm, and their impact on frailty syndrome could pave the way for novel interventions aimed at modulating these processes, potentially mitigating the adverse effects of ageing and improving the health span of the older population. Hence, our study aimed to demonstrate the correlation between physical examination outcomes, MTHFR and TCN2 polymorphisms in water-soluble B vitamins, and global DNAm among the older Thai adults.

Methods

Participants

Before enrolment, all participants provided their written informed consent for collecting blood and clinical data, and for genetic analysis. Ethical approval for this study (COA no. Si 213/2021) was granted by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand. The study was conducted across 26 communities in Bangkok Noi district, Bangkok, Thailand, with a mobile health screening unit established to serve the community through coordination with community leaders. Eligible participants were required to be aged ≥ 60 years, community-dwelling, and have normal to mild cognitive function, as assessed using the Thai Mental State Examination with a score of ≥ 18 [30]. Exclusion criteria included individuals with active malignancy, disability, dementia or serious mental illness, as well as those with a life expectancy of less than six months or enrolled in a palliative care programme. Moreover, participants aged 60 years and older were classified as older adults in this study. This age threshold aligns with the national standard used in Thailand [31].

Frailty assessment, physical performance, and neurodegeneration screening

In this cross-sectional study, we used the Fried criteria [5] and the Kihon checklist [32] to assess and classify frailty status among older participants. The criteria included (1) unintentional weight loss exceeding 5% in the past year, calculated from self-reported past weight and current weight (normal < 5%); (2) handgrip strength, determined by the best of two measurements with the preferred hand using Takei Tkk5401 digital hand grip gauge (Takei, Japan) (normal for female > 18.5 kg and male > 23.6 kg); (3) slow gait speed, measured by the minimum of two trials over 4 m (normal < 4.17 s/4meters); (4) low physical activity levels, expressed as weekly energy expenditure via global physical activity questionnaire (GPAQ) (normal for female > 367 scores and male > 600 scores) [33]; and (5) exhaustion, evaluated via two questions from the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) (normal = 0/2 score) [34]. To provide a more comprehensive assessment of frailty status, we utilised the Kihon Checklist, which evaluates seven subdomains: instrumental activities of daily living disability, physical inactivity, malnutrition, oral hypofunction, socialisation, cognitive function, and depression. These criteria are well-suited for detecting frailty syndrome in Asian populations, as they provide a comprehensive evaluation of physical, mental, social, and functional domains, yielding a total score ranging from 0 (indicating no frailty) to 25 (indicating high frailty). Based on established cut-off points [32], participants were categorised as follows: robust (scores 0–3), indicating minimal to no frailty; vulnerable (scores ≥ 4), which was further subdivided into pre-frail (scores 4–8) and frail (scores 9–25). In addition to the examination of Fried criteria and Kihon Checklist, we further measured sit and reach, functional reach, back scratch, and chair stand tests. They were also screened for detecting depression in the older adults using Thai Geriatric Depression Scale (TGDS) [35, 36]. A TGDS score of 13 or higher was used to indicate depression.

Blood collection and blood chemical analysis

By strictly classifying frailty using both the Fried phenotype and the Kihon Checklist, only 520 participants met the consistent criteria. Of these, 24 individuals were subsequently excluded due to age (over 80 years), and an additional 145 were excluded due to insufficient blood samples or inadequate DNA concentrations. From the remaining eligible participants, all 47 individuals classified as frail were included. Consequently, participants classified as robust and pre-frail were randomly selected and age-matched to the frail group (Fig. S1). Blood samples were collected in a fasted state from 170 older participants, comprising males (n = 37) and females (n = 133), in BD Vacutainer tubes with lithium heparin. Buffy coat and plasma were then isolated using density gradient centrifugation and stored at -80˚C until analysis. The plasma vitamin (folate and B12) concentrations, active B12, creatinine, homocysteine, glucose, direct low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (direct LDL-CHOL), lactate dehydrogenase, triglyceride, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) levels, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were assessed at Siriraj Hospital through the central clinical pathology laboratory using the chemiluminescence method on cobas e801 and cobas c702 (Roche, Germany), whereas complete blood cell counts were performed using electrical resistance, hydrodynamic focusing, and SLS-haemoglobin methods on Sysmex XN-3000 or XN-10 (Sysmex Corporation, Japan). Vitamin B6 was evaluated through a biochemistry laboratory conducted using a high-performance liquid chromatography method. In addition, eGFR was calculated using (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) equation [37].

DNA extraction and DNA amplification

Genomic DNA was extracted from the buffy coat using QIAamp Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and quality of the DNA were accurately determined by DeNovix fluorometer (DeNovix, USA) and stored at -20 ˚C before future use.

The primer pairs of MTHFR and TCN2 genes were designed to detect MTHFR 677 C > T (rs1801133) and TCN2 776G > C (rs1801198) polymorphisms (Table S1). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was carried out in a 24 µl reaction volume containing 1 µl of DNA template, 12 µl GoTaq® green master mix solution (Promega Corporation, USA), 1 µl of each gene-specific primer and adjusted to a total volume with PCR grade water. PCR was set up on the Bio-Rad PCR thermal cycler (Bio-Rad, USA) and PCR conditions were 95 ˚C for 10 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 ˚C for 45 s, 60 ˚C for 40 s and 72 ˚C for 30 s with a final extension at 72 ˚C for 5 min. The PCR product was qualified using gel electrophoresis before submitting it to Macrogen Inc., Sequencing Center (South Korea) for Sanger sequencing.

Evaluation of global DNA methylation

We performed global DNAm using the Abcam global DNA methylation assay kit (5-mC, colorimetric) (Abcam, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 ng of genomic DNA and six concentration points of standard controls were treated with the binding solution and incubated at 37 ˚C for 60 min. The plate was washed with a buffer. DNA samples and standard controls, then, were incubated with anti-5-mC monoclonal and detection antibodies. After applying enhancer and developer solutions, the absorbance was read at 450 nm using FLUOstar® Omega microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, Germany). A standard curve was generated from the absorbance values of the standard to calculate the DNAm percentage in the entire DNA samples.

Statistical analyses

To determine the appropriate descriptive statistics, we assessed the normality of continuous variables using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are reported as mean ± SD, and non-normally distributed variables as median with the interquartile range (IQR). While categorical variables were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons, while one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was used for multiple groups. Post-hoc analyses were performed using Tukey’s HSD test for ANOVA or Dunn’s test for Kruskal-Wallis, with the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method used to correct for multiple comparisons. Correlation between the vitamin concentrations, clinical measurements, and percentage of global DNAm was conducted using Spearman’s rank correlation test. Univariate logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the associations between frailty status and vitamin concentrations, creatinine, homocysteine, and global DNAm. Comparisons were conducted between frail and robust, frail and pre-frail, and pre-frail and robust groups, using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses and graph presentations were conducted using RStudio (Version: 2023.06.0-421).

Results

In this study, we randomly selected 170 older adults (> 60 years) who were divided into three groups: robust (n = 61), pre-frail (n = 62) and frail (n = 47), according to the Fried criteria concurrent with the Kihon checklist. The percentage of males and females was similar in each group. No statistical difference in age was observed among participants with robust, pre-frail, and frail. Additionally, the blood examinations were also not significantly different among the groups. In physical examinations, gait speed and handgrip strength tests, which were the components of Fried criteria, as well as the chair stand and functional reach tests, showed a highly significant difference among groups (p < 0.01). However, some participants were excluded from blood glucose testing as they had already consumed breakfast before blood collection. Correlation between clinical characteristics, global DNAm levels, and physical examination findings

We identified statistically significant cross-sectional relationships between clinical characteristics, physical examination outcomes, and global DNAm using the Spearman test (Fig. 1). Plasma vitamin B6 concentrations were significantly positively correlated with vitamin B12, active B12, and folate concentrations (p < 0.001). Conversely, the homocysteine concentrations exhibited a significant negative correlation with these vitamins. Additionally, significant positive correlations were observed between creatinine and homocysteine concentrations (p < 0.001) as well as global DNAm (p < 0.01). Notably, we also observed correlations between clinical characteristics and physical examination results. Handgrip strength was significantly positively correlated with creatinine levels (p < 0.001). Furthermore, vitamin B6 exhibited a significant correlation with the chair stand test (p = 0.048). Conversely, we also found negative correlations between the percentage of weight loss and creatinine concentration (p = 0.016). Additionally, the sit-and-reach test negatively correlated with creatinine and homocysteine concentrations (p < 0.01).

Distribution of MTHFR and TCN2 polymorphisms

Due to one-carbon metabolism genes influencing DNAm levels, we analysed the distribution of MTHFR and TCN2 genotypes in this study population. A total of 170 participants were identified with homozygous (CC), heterozygous (CT), or homozygous (TT) variants of MTHFR 677 C > T genotype. Notably, the CC variant of MTHFR emerged as the predominant genotype (70.0%), with frequencies of 54.1% in males and 74.4% in females. In frailty subgroup analysis, we observed CC frequencies of 77.1% in robust, 64.5% in pre-frail, and 68.1% in frail groups. The allele frequencies for C and T ranged from 0.8 to 0.9 and 0.1 to 0.2, respectively. Regarding the TCN2 776 C > G genotype, the heterozygous CG genotype was the most prevalent (50.6%), with frequencies of 51.4% in males and 50.4% in females. A similar distribution of TCN2 genotype was observed in the frailty subgroup analysis. The allele frequencies for C and G ranged from 0.4 to 0.5 and from 0.6 to 0.6, respectively. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences in genotype or allele distributions of either MTHFR or TCN2 in the study population and across gender or frailty status groups (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

To determine the strength of association of global DNAm levels with frailty status in 170 participants, univariate logistic regression analyses were performed. The results revealed a statistically significant association between global DNAm levels and the odds of being pre-frail when compared to both robust and frail groups (Fig. 3f). Specifically, individuals with lower global DNAm levels exhibited significantly higher odds of being pre-frail compared to frail individuals (OR = 5.6; CI = 1.6–22.4; p = 0.011). Additionally, individuals with lower global DNAm levels had significantly lower odds of being pre-frail compared to robust individuals (OR = 0.2; CI = 0.1–0.7; p = 0.014). Additionally, we examined the associations between frailty status and B vitamin concentrations, creatinine, and homocysteine. Nevertheless, no statistically significant findings were observed (Fig. S4).

Discussion

The present cross-sectional study investigated the interplay among genetic variants (MTHFR C677T and TCN2 776G > C), B vitamins, homocysteine, creatinine, global DNAm, and frailty status in a cohort of 170 older Thai adults. Our findings show significant differences in key physical parameters (e.g., gait speed, handgrip strength, chair stand, functional reach) across robust, pre-frail, and frail groups. We observed significant differences in folate concentrations between CC and CT of MTHFR genotypes and in vitamin B6 concentrations between CC and CG of TCN2 genotypes. Notably, global DNAm levels were significantly lower in pre-frail individuals compared to both robust and frail groups, and lower global DNAm was associated with significantly higher odds of being pre-frail compared to frail and lower odds compared to robust. Furthermore, we identified significant correlations among physical parameters, clinical characteristics (e.g., creatinine, homocysteine, B vitamins), and global DNAm.

Understanding the mechanisms influencing the quality of ageing remains one of the most challenging research topics. Various lines of evidence have shown how characterising frailty, indicative of vulnerability to adverse health outcomes, can help unravel the molecular mechanisms underlying the functional decline in older individuals. This contributes to a deeper characterisation and improved understanding of the ageing process. The significant influence of genetic variants in both nuclear and mitochondrial DNA on inter-individual susceptibility to functional decline and vulnerability to diseases in the older adults has been well-established [38, 39]. Epigenetic alterations across genetic factors and vitamin levels may elucidate the inter-individual variability in frailty status. Studies have consistently shown that older individuals exhibit lower levels of global DNAm across their epigenomes [40]. For instance, global DNA hypomethylation in leukocytes has been observed in older individuals compared to younger populations [41]. This association between ageing and global DNA hypomethylation has also been demonstrated in animal models, where significant declines in global 5-mC levels were observed with advancing chronological age in both the blood and brain of owl monkeys (Aotus nancymaae) [42]. Therefore, the exploration of nutrients and vitamins associated with the methyl donor diet becomes particularly interesting for the older adults. Supplementation of nutrients such as folate, vitamin B12, and other B vitamins can potentially increase DNAm levels through one-carbon metabolism, which involves folate and homocysteine cycles. These vitamins are essential for facilitating the conversion of homocysteine, an intermediate amino acid, into methionine via vitamin B12-dependent remethylation. Methionine is subsequently converted into SAM, the primary methyl donor for DNAm. The proper functioning of this interconnected pathway ensures that SAM availability is sufficient to maintain DNAm, which is a critical epigenetic mechanism regulating gene expression. Disruptions in one-carbon metabolism due to insufficient levels of B vitamins may lead to abnormal DNAm patterns, contributing to cellular dysfunction, increased inflammation, and accelerated biological ageing, all of which are associated with frailty [43]. Research has shown that supplementation with folate (400 µg) and B12 (500 µg) in individuals aged 60 and older for two years resulted in improved physical health without significant side effects [25, 44]. Vitamin B12 transport and absorption may contribute to decreased availability of vitamin B12 and an increased risk of frailty syndrome [38]. Therefore, micronutrient malnutrition could potentially contribute to the pathogenesis of frailty through various biological pathways.

Ageing is accompanied by physiological and pathological changes that often alter the vitamin requirements of older individuals, necessitating increased intake of specific nutrients. Vitamins B1, B2, B6, and B12 are pivotal for a multitude of metabolic functions in the body, encompassing energy generation, neurotransmitter production, and red blood cell formation [45]. Age-related changes in the absorption and utilisation of these vitamins can increase the risk of deficiencies, highlighting the importance of adequate intake to maintain optimal physiological function.

Genetic polymorphisms, such as MTHFR C677T and TCN2 C776G, play significant roles in one-carbon metabolism, influencing B vitamin levels, homocysteine, and DNAm [12, 46]. Matteini et al. revealed a correlation between various genetic variations affecting the vitamin B12 transport mechanism and frailty. Their study emphasised a probable correlation between genetic factors involved in vitamin B12 metabolism rather than serum vitamin B12 levels [38]. Our findings emphasised other findings [47–49], revealing homozygous CC of MTHFR and heterozygous GC of TCN2 were the dominant genotypes in the Asian population. Additionally, we investigated the influence of MTHFR 677 genotype variations on B vitamins, creatinine, and homocysteine concentrations. No statistically significant differences were observed in a sub-analysis of older participants who were not taking supplements containing B vitamins. This observation suggests that MTHFR genotype variations may not significantly influence the concentrations of these essential nutrients and metabolic indicators among the older adults, possibly due to their underlying health conditions. Therefore, we explored deeper into the influence of MTHFR polymorphisms on frailty status within non-supplement B vitamin participants. Our analysis highlighted that robust individuals with the MTHFR homozygous CC genotype exhibited significantly higher levels of folate compared to the robust group with the heterozygous CT genotype. This finding is consistent with a previous population-based cohort study, Hustad et al. examined the impact of the MTHFR genotype on vitamins in the Norwegian Colorectal Cancer Prevention cohort, revealing significant differences in total folate levels between the CC, CT, and TT variants [50].

Regarding the TCN2 gene, it plays a crucial role as a carrier protein for the transportation of vitamin B12 from the terminal ileum to the epithelial cells and facilitating its cellular uptake through receptor-mediated endocytosis [51]. Variants in the TCN2 genotypes may result in diminished availability of vitamin B12, which potentially contributes to the development of frailty syndrome [38]. Our results revealed only significant differences in vitamin B6 levels among 130 older participants (who were not taking B vitamin supplements). Upon further examination of the influence of TCN2 polymorphism on frailty syndrome within participants not taking B vitamin supplements, our results showed no significant difference in the levels of folate, vitamin B12, including active B12, creatinine, and homocysteine among robust, pre-frail, and frail groups with TCN2 variants. Concomitantly, TCN2 C776G genotype did not influence plasma vitamin B12, homocysteine, or creatinine levels of healthy older adults [13, 21, 52, 53]. However, we found that TCN2 polymorphisms and plasma levels of vitamin B6 were interactive modulators of the metabolism of homocysteine and creatinine, which is an important intermediate in the transfer of one-carbon units among individuals classified across robust, pre-frail, and frail groups. Further study could investigate the longitudinal impact of these genetic variations on frailty progression and associated health outcomes in larger and more diverse older populations.

The association between ageing and DNAm changes is well-documented in the literature, reflecting the dynamic nature of epigenetic regulation over the lifespan [54]. However, few studies have investigated the global DNAm and frailty in older adults. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a correlation among MTHFR and TCN2 genotypes, and global DNAm among the older individuals classified as frail, pre-frail, and robust. Global DNAm levels varied among frailty groups, with pre-frail individuals exhibiting the lowest %5-mC, significantly differing from both robust and frail groups. The intriguing non-linear pattern suggests that the loss of global DNAm in our cohort is specifically associated with pre-frailty, indicating that the transition from robustness to frailty may involve a distinct phase of heightened epigenetic vulnerability rather than a simple linear decline with age. This may reflect insufficient healthcare access among the pre-frail group compared to the frail group, who often receive closer medical attention. Interestingly, frail individuals had higher %5-mC levels despite carrying genotypes associated with reduced enzymatic activity, a finding consistent with studies suggesting compensatory increases in DNAm in response to physiological stress [55]. We further analysed to observe the differences in %5-mC levels in MTHFR and TCN2 genotypes among older participants. Notably, the pre-frail group carrying heterozygosity CT of MTHFR exhibited lower %5-mC levels compared to the frail group carrying homozygosity CC. From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the importance of early identification and intervention during the pre-frailty phase. Monitoring global DNAm, alongside genotyping for key one-carbon metabolism genes, could enable personalised strategies to preserve methylation homeostasis and delay frailty progression. Nutritional optimisation, inflammation control, and lifestyle modifications tailored to an individual’s genetic and epigenetic profile might be especially effective during this critical window.

Moreover, our analysis revealed significant correlations among B vitamin levels, homocysteine, and creatinine, consistent with previous studies [56–58]. From the cross-sectional analysis of homocysteine levels and vitamin blood levels, and intake in older participants in the Framingham Study, homocysteine showed an inverse association with folate, vitamin B12, and vitamin B6, as well as age [59]. Deficient B vitamin status appears to be a key driver of high homocysteine in many older adults. Deficiencies in B vitamins, prevalent among older adults [60, 61], can disrupt cellular one-carbon metabolism, leading to homocysteine accumulation [62, 63]. This, in turn, may be associated with an increased risk of frailty [64]. Supporting this notion, Wong et al. found an association between elevated homocysteine and frailty, as measured by the FRAIL scale, in a study of Australian men aged 70–88 years [65]. Additionally, Sanchez et al. also observed that homocysteine levels correlated with frailty defined by Fried’s criteria in a Spanish population aged 65–98 years [66]. Furthermore, the homocysteine-to-creatinine ratio (Hcy: Cre) reflects the rate of homocysteine metabolism relative to muscle mass. Deficiencies in B vitamins, essential for this process, can lead to an elevated Hcy: Cre ratio [67]. Our study uniquely highlights a negative correlation between homocysteine and creatinine levels and performance on the sit-and-reach test, a linear flexibility assessment that measures the flexibility of the hamstrings and lower back.

Additionally, a positive correlation was found among global DNAm, creatinine, and handgrip strength. According to a study from Bednarska-Makaruk et al., multivariate regression analysis demonstrated a significant positive association between DNAm and creatinine [68]. Previous studies have shown that muscle mass and physical activity influence creatinine levels [69, 70]. Vitamin B6 intake was also associated with improved performance in physical tests, such as chair rise and handgrip strength, particularly among individuals with low physical activity levels [71]. Therefore, our findings, along with these pieces of evidence, suggest that global DNAm, vitamin B6, and creatinine levels could collectively serve as biological markers for muscle strength, particularly handgrip strength.

The current study possesses several strengths. Firstly, participants underwent an assessment of frailty status using both Fried’s phenotype and the Kihon checklist. All evaluations were conducted by trained medical staff through interviews and assessments. Furthermore, additional physical performance measures such as sit-and-reach, functional reach, and balance were included. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, we were unable to assess clinical samples other than blood samples, although blood samples are a feasible approach for epigenetic studies on muscle strength. Secondly, due to the relatively small sample size, the study may have had limited statistical power to detect subtle associations, particularly in subgroup analyses. Thirdly, a key limitation of our study is its cross-sectional design, which precludes inferences about temporality and causality. The observed associations may reflect reverse causation or residual confounding, and therefore should be interpreted with caution. Lastly, the prevalence of MTHFR T677T in our population is very low. This hinders a complete understanding of the correlation between all genotypes of MTHFR C677T with vitamin levels, global DNAm, and physical performance. Further research should investigate the longitudinal impacts of genetic and epigenetic factors on frailty and their interplay with vitamin status in larger, more diverse populations.

Conclusions

Our study highlights associations between physical examinations, MTHFR and TCN2 genotypes, levels of B vitamins, creatinine, homocysteine, and global DNAm in older individuals with frailty syndrome. These findings suggest that frailty progression is influenced by TCN2 polymorphism related to the lower vitamin B6 level. Moreover, global DNAm levels were significantly lower in pre-frail individuals, particularly those carrying the MTHFR C677T genotype, compared to both robust and frail groups. The positive correlation between creatinine levels, hand grip strength, and global DNAm may reflect the combined influence of muscle mass and cellular health. Our findings offer new insights into the interplay among global DNAm, MTHFR, and TCN2 genotypes, as well as frailty status in older Thai adults. However, caution is warranted when generalising these results to other populations. Epigenetic profiles and genetic polymorphisms can exhibit significant variability among different ethnicities, lifestyles, and environmental exposures, potentially impacting the reported relationships. Consequently, further research, especially via longitudinal studies and mechanistic analyses in varied populations, is necessary to confirm these findings, clarify the biological pathways connecting global DNAm and genetic factors to frailty, and evaluate the generalisability of these associations. To explore the generalisability of our findings, subsequent research should replicate these analyses in independent, multi-ethnic populations and perform meta-analyses to quantify between-study heterogeneity. It will be important to standardise definitions of frailty and DNAm assays across research. It will also be important to test predictive models (discrimination and calibration) across groups of people with varied ages, ancestries, and socioeconomic backgrounds. Stratified analyses and tests for interaction (by age, sex, comorbidity, and nutritional status), together with transportability methods and longitudinal designs, will help determine whether the observed global DNAm–frailty relationships are consistent and potentially causal across settings.
