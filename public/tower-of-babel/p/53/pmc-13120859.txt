A selection model for umbilical cord blood units with high isolation yield of total nucleated and CD34+ cells
Authors: Vuong M Pham, Hung V Nguyen, Qui T Dang, Son K Nguyen, Mi H V Ngo, An P N Tra, Huyen T T Truong, Hung M Pham, Dien V Mai, Oanh H Le, Lan T Huynh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Background: Cord blood is an important hematopoietic stem cell source for treating over 80 FDA-approved diseases, driving demand for HSC-containing-umbilical cord blood (UCB) unit cryopreservation for future use. Total nucleated cell (TNC) and CD34+ cell amounts are key determinants of transplant success. Predicting processing outcomes is clinically valuable, particularly for public stem cell banks where storing high-quality UCB units is a priority. This study aimed to establish selection models for UCB units with high isolation yields of these critical cellular determinants. Methods: We first performed univariate analysis and multiple linear regression on 3338 UCB units processed at MekoStem Stem Cell Bank (Vietnam) from 2019 to 2023 to explore correlations between potential variables (maternal age, mode of delivery, baby blood type, birth weight, gestational age, infant sex, blood volume, procurement time, and time to processing after collection) and TNC and CD34+ cell counts. Subsequently, Bayesian Model Averaging and exhaustive search were applied to develop predictive models. The developed models were then validated using a dataset of 660 UCB units processed in 2024. Results: Results identified an optimal selection model incorporating birth weight, delivery mode, blood volume, and gestational age for predicting high isolation yields of both TNC and CD34+ cells. Reference values for these variables were birth weight >3200 g, vaginal delivery mode, blood volume >79.22 mL, and gestational age >39 weeks or ≤39 weeks for TNC or CD34+ cell models, respectively. Conclusions: The models proposed in this study demonstrated robust predictive performance in external validation and can be applied to any type of stem cell bank.Keywords: TNC, CD34+, cord blood, hematopoietic stem cells, stem cell banking, predictive model

Total nucleated cells (TNC) and CD34+ cell amounts are critical factors affecting hematopoietic stem cell transplantation outcomes. Predicting umbilical cord blood processing outcomes is therefore clinically valuable, particularly for public stem cell banks where storing high-quality units is a priority. We successfully identified an optimal selection model incorporating birth weight, delivery mode, blood volume, and gestational age for predicting high isolation yields of both TNC and CD34+ cells. This model demonstrated robust predictive performance in external validation and is applicable to any type of stem cell bank.

Introduction

Previously considered as medical waste, umbilical cord blood (UCB) was then identified as an alternative source of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) by Hal Broxmeyer and colleagues.1 UCB offers several advantages over other stem cell sources, including abundant availability, minimal ethical considerations, ease of collection, and non-invasive procurement.2–4 Furthermore, HSCs isolated from UCBs demonstrate high proliferative capacity.5,6 Clinically, compared to other sources of HSC, HLA matching is not strictly required in transplantation of UCB.7,8

The first cord blood transplant was performed in 1988 as an effort to treat Fanconi anemia for a 5-year-old boy using a UCB unit donated by his younger sister.1 Since then, the FDA has approved HSC transplantation for a range of diseases, primarily focusing on both malignant and non-malignant blood and immune disorders. Currently, more than 35 000 transplants using autologous, related, and unrelated cord blood have been performed globally for treating various blood diseases.9 Beyond FDA-approved indications, many clinical trials of UCB applications on other conditions such as glioblastoma multiforme, Alzheimer’s disease, and diabetic foot ulcer are under investigation.10

Although accumulating evidence indicated that not all CD34+ cells are true HSCs,11,12 total nucleated cell (TNC) and CD34+ cell counts are widely considered as factors affecting the clinical outcomes of HSC transplantation.13,14 Despite the advantages mentioned above, the most critical limitation of UCB transplantation is the low concentration of HSCs in collected samples. Compared to bone marrow and mobilized peripheral blood, UCB contains the lowest concentration of CD34+ cells, resulting in slower engraftment.6,8 Consequently, the biological properties and cryopreservation of its cellular content should also be studied intensively. Organizations such as the Foundation for the Accreditation of Cellular Therapy (FACT) have established international standards for cord blood banking and for administration with fixed thresholds for these cellular contents. Generally, cellular retention requirements for cord blood stored for autologous or related administration are less stringent. Therefore, private or family stem cell banks continue to play a role as biological insurance providers for future family or individual needs. In contrast, given that the processing and cryopreservation of UCB units are high-cost procedures, public banks prioritize banking high-quality UCB-derived stem cell products after processing for retaining the high clinical outcome for future application. Recent studies reveal that hybrid stem cell banks are receiving increased attention from customers, markets, and policymakers due to their combined benefits from both private and public systems.15,16

Various factors influence the isolation yield of TNC and CD34+ amount. We temporarily categorize these factors into 3 groups: (1) maternal factors, including maternal age and mode of delivery; (2) neonatal factors, including baby blood type, birth weight, gestational age, infant sex, and blood volume; (3) technical factors, including procurement time (hour and month), and time to processing after collection.

In this study, we extensively analyzed a large number of private UCB units collected and processed at MekoStem Stem Cell Bank (Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam) within 2019-2023 to explore correlations and associations between these factors and post-processing TNC (TNC POST) and CD34+ cells. Additionally, based on multivariate analyses, we developed and validated a predictive model for high TNC and CD34+ cell yields. The primary goal of this study is to identify critical factors affecting TNC and CD34+ cells that influence downstream clinical applications. These results can inform optimal conditions for screening, collecting, and processing UCB units to achieve high-quality stem cell product storage. Therefore, our findings are applicable to any mode of stem cell banking.

Materials and methods

CBU selection and collection

This retrospective was conducted at MekoStem Stem Cell Bank using data from UCB units processed from 2019 to 2024. Specifically, data from UCB units within 2019-2023 were analyzed to examine correlations and associations among variables and to develop a predictive model. The dataset of UCB units collected and processed in 2024 was used for validating the predictive model. All parents consented to the use of this information for research purposes, provided no identity was disclosed or shared with any third parties.

Umbilical cord blood units were collected using either in utero or ex utero collection procedures from multiple hospitals throughout Vietnam by a well-trained procurement team. Following delivery of baby regardless of delivery mode (vaginal or caesarean section), the umbilical cord was clamped and sterilized well with povidone 10% (Mekophar). Then, a sterile 16-gauge needle attached to a UCB collecting bag containing 22 mL of citrate-phosphate-dextrose-adenine (CPDA-1) as an anticoagulant (JMS Singapore PTE Ltd) was inserted into the umbilical vein. Upon completion of procurement, the bags were sealed, labeled with a sample code, put into a styrofoam box with cold ice gel packs, and transported to our stem cell bank for processing.

CBU processing

The collected cord blood bags were transferred to a clean room (Grade B GMP-WHO classification) for processing within 48 h after collection using red blood cell depletion and plasma reduction methods. The collection bags were opened under a class II biosafety cabinet. A volume of 1.5 mL of cord blood from the collection bag was withdrawn for ABO-Rh typing and hematological analysis (CELL-DYN 1800 or CELL-DYN Emerald 22, Abbott). Subsequently, 6% hydroxyethyl starch (HES) (Nipro) was added into the collection bag in a ratio of 1:5 (HES: blood volume). The Process/Transfer/Freezing bag system (Thermogenesis) was then connected to the collection bag.

The blood bag was then shaken gently for 15 min (IKA Works GmbH & Co KG) followed by a centrifugation at 50 g for 10 min at 10 °C (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The plasma and HSC-containing buffy coat fraction after centrifugation were transferred into the transfer bag 1. A volume of 0.5 mL of the remaining RBC in the collection bag was withdrawn for ABO-Rh group typing and hematological analysis.

The Process/Transfer/Freezing bag system was then disconnected from the collection bag and underwent a centrifugation at 400 g for 10 min at 10 °C (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then, plasma fraction was transferred into transfer bag 2. Then, the transfer bag 2 was disconnected from the Process/Transfer/Freezing bag system. A volume of 1.0 mL of HSC containing buffy coat fraction in transfer bag 1 was withdrawn for CD34+ quantification and quality control. A cryoprotectant mixture containing DMSO (Protide Pharmaceuticals) and dextran (Protide Pharmaceuticals) in a 1:1 ratio was introduced into the freezing bag by an infusion pump (Terumo) at a rate of 24 mL/h to give the final cryoprotectant concentration of 10%. The bag was then cryopreserved using a controlled-rate freezing machine (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stored in the vapor phase of liquid nitrogen.

TNC count

Total nucleated cell analyses were performed twice before processing (TNC PRE) and after the processing (TNC POST) by an automated hematology analyzer (CELL-DYN 1800 or CELL-DYN Emerald 22, Abbott). The TNC count of each sample was obtained by multiplying the absolute WBC counts by sample volumes.

CD34+ cell count and viability

The amount and viability of the CD34+ fraction in the cord blood samples after processing were analyzed using FACS Canto II with the Enumeration kit (BD Biosciences). The cells were co-stained with phycoerythrin (PE)- and fluorescein (FITC)-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD34 and CD45 antigens, respectively. Dead cells were excluded from quantification by co-staining with 7-AAD. The BD FACSCanto software (version 3.0.4894.41215) was used for the analysis.

Blood group typing

The Beth-Vincent test was used for ABO/RH blood group typing of UCB samples by blood grouping antibodies (Spectrum) according to the instructions of the manufacturer. The test was performed twice with double check before and during processing.

Maternal, neonatal, and technical information collection

Maternal data, including maternal age, were collected at the time donors registered for the service of stem cell storage. Mode of delivery was also considered as maternal data and was collected at the hospital after the delivery together with procurement time and procurement month. Neonatal data, including gestational age, infant sex, and birth weight were collected at the hospital after the babies were born while blood type and blood volume were gathered at the processing lab. Time to process after collection is considered as technical information and was collected at the processing lab. All data collected in this study was controlled strictly, no identity was disclosed or shared with any third parties.

Characterization of UCB units collected and processed between 2019 and 2023.

The number of male-derived UCB units was 1817 and accounted for 54.43% of the total. Most UCB units were procured during daytime (73.24%) with 1368 units (40.98%) collected in the morning (6:00-11:59) and 1077 units (32.26%) in the afternoon (12:00-17:59). January and December were 2 months having the highest numbers of procured UCB units (10.25% and 9.83%, respectively), while the lowest numbers of procured UCB units were recorded in June and July (6.53% and 6.86%, respectively). The baby’s blood type was consistent with the distribution of blood groups of Vietnamese people.17 Particularly, the percentage of blood types from highest to lowest was O (44.52%), B (30.65%), A (19.80%), and AB (5.03%). Most UCB units had Rh+ blood type (99.79%).

Most babies had a gestational age ranging from 38.0 to 39.9 weeks old (75.64%), while babies with gestational ages <36.0 weeks, 36.0-37.9 weeks, and ≥ 40 weeks accounted for 2.34%, 12.25%, and 9.83%, respectively. Birth weight ranged from 1400 to 4800 g, with the highest percentage in the (3000; 4000] g group (65.04%) and followed by (2000; 3000] g group (32.06%), > 4000 g group (1.94%), and ≤2000 g group (0.96%). Blood volume ranged from 24.56 to 210.49 mL with an average of 82.14 ± 0.50 mL. TNC prior to processing (TNC PRE) ranged from 44.70 to 3745.00 (×106) with an average of 949.49 ± 7.65 × 106 cells.

Maternal age ranged from 15.87 to 47.18 years old, with the majority at (30.00; 35.00] years old (35.95%). The majority of UCB units were collected from cesarean deliveries (2190 units, 65.61%) while the number of vaginal deliveries was 1148 (34.39%). Time to processing after collection was mainly within (12.00; 24.00] h (37.12%), while 29.81% within (24.00; 36.00], 26.36% within (36.00; 48.00] h, and 6.11% at or earlier than 12.00 h after collection.

After processing, TNC POST was ranged from 42.50 to 3638.00 × 106 cells with an average of 865.34 ± 6.95 × 106 cells. Compared to TNC PRE, the processing results showed a high TNC recovery (>90%, data not shown). In addition, the range of CD34+ amounts was 0.10-28.60 × 106 cells with an average of 3.24 ± 0.05 × 106 cells.

Univariate analysis of variables on TNC POST and CD34+ cells

In this study, neonatal factors included baby blood type, birth weight, infant sex, gestational age, and blood volume. Maternal factors comprised maternal age and modes of delivery. Technical factors included time to processing after collection, procurement month, and procurement time. The correlation of these neonatal, maternal, and technical factors with TNC POST and CD34+ cell amount was assessed.

The results showed that both TNC POST and CD34+ cells had significant correlation with birth weight (P < .0001, r = 3.867; P < .0001, r = 0.2911) (Figure 1C and K), TNC PRE amount (P < .0001, r = 0.9963; P < .0001, r = 0.7328) (Figure 1D and L), and blood volume (P < .0001, r = 0.7622; P < .0001, r = 0.5848) (Figure 1E and M). Maternal age, time to processing after collection, procurement month, and procurement time did not show a positive correlation with either TNC POST or CD34+ amount (Figure 1A, F, G, H, I, N, O, and P). While gestational age showed a positive correlation with TNC POST (P < .0001, r = 0.1992) (Figure 1B), it did not significantly correlate with CD34+ amount (P = .7346, r = 0.0059) (Figure 1J).

To further analyze the correlation of maternal age, birth weight, gestational age, time to processing after collection, and procurement time with TNC POST and CD34+ cell counts, these factors were divided into subgroups. The comparison between subgroups was performed using ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Particularly, birth weight was divided into 4 subgroups including ≤2000 g, (2000; 3000] g, (3000; 4000] g, and >4000 g. The results showed that increases in birth weight resulted in significantly greater TNC POST (Figure 2C). In terms of CD34+ cells, significant differences between subgroups were also found except when comparing the ≤2000 g versus (2000; 3000] g subgroups (Figure 2K).

Gestational age was divided into 4 groups: <36 weeks, [36; 37.9] week, [38; 39.9] weeks, and ≥ 40 weeks. The results showed that UCB units collected from infants with gestational age less than 36 weeks had the lowest TNC POST amount. Interestingly, increasing gestational age resulted in a statistically significant increase in TNC POST (P < .05 in <36 vs [36; 37.9] and [38; 39.9] vs ≥ 40 weeks groups, P < .0001 for all other pairwise comparisons) (Figure 2B). However, no significant differences between gestational age groups were found for CD34+ counts (Figure 2J). In addition, both TNC POST and CD34+ cell counts decreased in UCB units collected from pregnant women older than 25 years old (Figure 2A and I). However, among the maternal age subgroups ≤25, (25.00; 30.00], (30.00; 35.00], >35.00 years old), a significant difference was found only between the ≤25 and (30.00; 35.00] years old in terms of TNC POST (Figure 2A). Furthermore, higher yields of TNC POST were found in UCB units processed later than 12 h after collection; however, this difference was not statistically significant (2D). Similar observation was also found in CD34+ cell counts, where samples processed at or before 12 h after collection had the lowest yield. Among the processing time subgroups (≤12, (12; 24], (24; 36], (36; 48] h), the (12; 24] h group had the highest yield of CD34+ and was significantly higher than that in ≤12 and (24; 36] groups (Figure 2L), suggesting that achieving high TNC and CD34+ cell yields is not benefitted from processing samples soon within 12 h after collection. These findings indicated that both TNC POST and CD34+ cells are not dependent only on a single factor such as maternal age or birth weight but are influenced by multiple variables.

Since most Vietnamese people have Rh+ blood type, analyses in this study focused only on ABO blood groups. Blood group did not show strong influence on TNC POST and CD34+ cell counts. UCB units with AB blood group had higher amounts of both TNC POST and CD34+ cells, however, significant differences between groups were not found (Figure 2E and M). Furthermore, while female infants had a remarkably higher amount of TNC post compared to male infants (P < .0001) (Figure 2F), the amount of CD34+ was insignificantly higher in female infants (Figure 2N). This result suggested that the ratio of CD34+/TNC in male infants is higher than that in female infants. Furthermore, the amount of TNC POST and CD34+ cells was higher in the vaginal delivery group compared to the cesarean delivery group; however, statistically significant differences were found only for TNC POST (P = .0013) (Figure 2G and O). Additionally, UCB units procured during daytime hours [6:00-17:59] showed insignificantly higher TNC POST and CD34+ cell counts compared to those procured at another time (Figure 2H and P).

Developing predictive models of TNC POST and CD34+ cells

Given that the quality of UCB units is influenced by multiple related variables, we performed multivariate analyses using all variables collected for univariate analyses. TNC POST and CD34+ cells were considered as dependent variables, while all others were considered as independent variables.

A normality test was first applied to assess whether the datasets of TNC and CD34+ cell counts followed normal distribution or not. The results performed by D’Agostino & Pearson test, Anderson–Darling test, Shapiro–Wilk test, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test revealed that both the dataset of TNC and CD34+ cells did not follow normal distribution (Figure S1). As a result, multivariate analyses were then performed with natural logarithm transformation applied to dependent variables including TNC-POST and CD34+ cells. After logarithm transformation, the right-skewness of both TNC-POST and CD34+ datasets was significantly improved, making the model assumptions closer to normality (Supplementary Material).

Multiple linear regression was first performed to explore the relationship between all independent and dependent variables (TNC POST and CD34+ cell amounts). The results showed that time to processing after collection, birth weight, blood volume, gestational age, vaginal delivery mode, and O blood type had a positive impact on both TNC POST and CD34+ cell amount. In addition, while infant sex and maternal age showed influence only on the TNC POST amount, procurement month and procurement time showed impact only on the CD34+ amount (Tables S1 and S2). Then, to reduce uncertainty and improve the prediction accuracy, Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA) was applied to our datasets.18 In terms of TNC POST, 3 models with the highest posterior probability were identified (Table S3). Based on Posterior Inclusion Probability (PIP) values, adjusted R2, and posterior probability (post prob), Model 1 in Table S3 was selected and listed in Table 2. This model identified 7 variables as the most important factors influencing TNC POST: time to processing after collection, infant sex, birth weight, blood volume, gestational age, maternal age, and delivery mode were identified as the most important factors influencing the TNC POST with PIP 100, R2 0.620, and post prob 0.806.

We then developed simpler predictive models of TNC POST and CD34+ cell counts with fewer variables included by performing an exhaustive search. All candidate models for TNC POST and CD34+ cell counts were, respectively, listed in Tables S5 and S6. Based on AIC and adjusted R2, the best models in Tables S5 and S6 were selected and presented in Table 2 (TNC POST) and Table 3 (CD34+). The results showed that blood volume was included in all models. In addition, adjusted R2 of models containing blood volume alone did not remarkably decrease in comparison with BMA models (0.544 vs 0.620 in TNC POST model and 0.336 vs 0.365 in CD34+ model). These results revealed that blood volume was a critical factor influencing the isolation yield of TNC POST and CD34+ amount.

Validation of the predictive models for UCB with a high isolation yield of TNC POST and CD34+

All selection models in Tables 2 and 3 were validated by using the dataset of UCB units collected and processed within 2024 (N = 660). Like the training dataset, a normality test was first applied to TNC and CD34+ cell counts of the validated dataset. The results showed that these dependent variables did not follow a normal distribution. However, after logarithm transformation, right-skewness of the TNC-POST and CD34+ cell counts improved significantly making the model assumptions closer to the desired normal distribution (Figure S2). Then, multiple linear regression was applied on each model listed in Tables 2 and 3. Adjusted R2 of each model on the validated dataset was listed in Table 4 (TNC POST) and Table 5 (CD34+). The difference in adjusted R2 between the training and validated dataset was less than 10% in both TNC POST and CD34+ models, indicating that the model was stable and not substantially overfitted. Furthermore, the results imply that the predictors included in these models were likely to be genuinely associated with the outcome, rather than reflecting random variation in the training dataset. However, the results showed that 2 independent variables, including time to processing after collection and maternal age, did not retain their statistical significance in TNC POST models 1 and 2. Similarly, variable O blood type in CD34+ model 1 did not present statistical significance in the validated dataset. These results suggested that time to processing after collection, maternal age, and O blood type were not robust and stable across datasets. This might be explained by the difference in sample size and distributional shift between cohorts.19,20

To further validate these developed models, we dichotomized each independent variable at the median value derived from the training dataset. Based on these cutoffs, subjects in the validated dataset were divided into 2 groups for each model as described in Table S7. The mean values of TNC POST and CD34+ cells between 2 groups of each model were then compared by Student’s t-test. The results showed that the number of subjects collected from the validated dataset in both TNC POST and CD34+ models was in contrast with the number of variables included in these models. Particularly, in terms of TNC POST, only 2 subjects based on the cutoffs of independent variable were collected for group A of model 3. No subjects met the selective criteria of group A of model 1 and model 2 of TNC POST. In addition, in all models, group B had a higher number of subjects than group A, suggesting that high-quality UCB units account for a small percentage in the whole cohort. Even though the models including blood volume alone were validated successfully by both 2 validation approaches, we did not consider it as a robust model because of the analysis revealing that blood volume is not the sole variable impacting the isolation yield of TNC POST and CD34+ amount. Thus, models 3-6 (Table 2) and models 2-4 (Table 3) could be identified as potential selection models of “good” UCB units for high isolation yield of TNC POST and CD34+, respectively. Among these models, we would consider the model that includes birth weight, delivery mode, blood volume, and gestational age to be the most appropriate selective model for both TNC POST and CD34+ cells. The reference value of 3 former variables, respectively, were > 3200 g, vaginal delivery model, and > 79.22 mL. As for the gestational age, it is noted that its reference value was opposite between TNC POST (>39 weeks old) and CD34+ (≤39 weeks old).

Discussion

Cord blood is considered a potential source of HSCs thanks to its advantages over other sources. Currently, the US FDA has approved HSC use for treating more than 80 diseases. Furthermore, an increasing number of clinical trials using HSCs for other diseases are being performed globally with promising outcomes, suggesting the probability of future approval for additional clinical applications of HSCs. Consequently, the demand for storing HSC-containing bag derived from cord blood in stem cell banks for future use has increased. In contrast to family stem cell banks that operate based on client demand to store stem cell-containing samples, public and hybrid banks typically establish higher criteria of processed cord blood units for banking.16,21

However, the low percentage of HSCs is the biggest downside of UCB compared to other sources.22,23 Therefore, efforts to retain or expand these therapeutic cells during processing or after isolation, respectively, are needed. While ex vivo expansion of HSCs remains challenging,24,25 investigators focus on the former approach by selecting “good” UCB units for processing or optimizing the procurement, processing, and thawing of samples. Accumulating evidence indicated that not all CD34+ cells are true HSCs, and conversely, long-term repopulating HSCs can exist in a CD34− fraction.11,12,26 However, the counts of TNC and CD34+ cells are widely considered quality indicators of UCB units due to their critical influence on clinical outcomes of HSC transplantation.27,28 Various factors influence the isolation yields of TNC (TNC POST) and CD34+ cells.

In this study, we aimed to develop selection models for UCB units with high isolation yields of TNC POST and CD34+ cells. Our approach included: (1) performing univariate analyses to explore the correlation between each variable and TNC POST and CD34+ cell counts; (2) developing selection models based on multiple linear regression results; and (3) validating the models. Variables analyzed in this study were categorized into 3 groups: maternal, neonatal, and technical groups. Results revealed that birth weight and blood volume were common factors showing a positive correlation with both TNC POST and CD34+ cell counts. We also found that blood volume collected during cesarean section was higher compared to vaginal deliveries (data not shown). This observation was in accordance with previous reports,29–31 with possible explanations proposed by Bassiouny et al. and Mancinelli et al. In cesarean surgery, blood clot formation in the cord vein is reduced because the placenta is removed from the maternal body more quickly than in vaginal birth.29 In addition, under the influence of gravity, placing the infant above the placenta before clamping the umbilical cord during cesarean section facilitates downflow of blood into the placental cord and compartment.31 Nonetheless, TNC POST and CD34+ cell counts were higher in the vaginal delivery group compared to the cesarean delivery group, suggesting that in addition to blood volume, other factors importantly affect the isolation yields of these cells. In contrast, maternal age, time to processing after collection, procurement month, and procurement time did not significantly correlate with either TNC POST or CD34+ cell counts. In addition, while showing positive correlation with TNC POST counts, gestational age did not significantly correlate with CD34+ cell counts. However, subgroup comparisons revealed differences in TNC POST and/or CD34+ cell counts within specific ranges. Remarkably, UCB units collected from pregnant women older than 25 years old had lower TNC POST and CD34+ cell counts than those from younger women. Additionally, higher yields of TNC POST and CD34+ cells were found in UCB units processed later than 12 h after collection. Female infants had significantly higher TNC POST counts compared to male infants. This result was consistent with previously published data.32 However, CD34+ cell counts were insignificantly higher in male infants, suggesting that the CD34+ cells/TNC ratio is higher in male infants. The results also revealed that both TNC POST and CD34+ cell counts were higher in UCB units collected during daytime (6:00-18:00), however, no significant difference compared to nighttime collection was found. This observation may be explained by tight circadian regulation of HSC and progenitor cell trafficking.33,34 However, it should be emphasized that the isolation yields of TNCs and CD34+ cells were influenced by multiple variables rather than by the effect of any single factor.

Analyses showed that technical factors did not strongly impact isolation yields of TNC POST and CD34+ cells; however, a critical point that should be noted is the stability of technician skills, which can be demonstrated by TNC and MNC recovery rates. All samples analyzed in this study met the accepted TNC recovery criteria set by our facility and showed minimal differences between samples (data not shown). This consistency enhanced the prediction accuracy of our developed selection models. In the selection model development, natural logarithm transformation and BMA modeling were applied to the training dataset to improve prediction accuracy. Subset models were then developed by performing an exhaustive search on the selected BMA model. Seven selection models of TNC POST and 5 models of CD34+ cells were selected. External validation is a crucial step in assessing whether a model maintains its predictive performance in new cohorts and settings. In our study, although all models demonstrated comparable adjusted R2 values, some variables did not retain their statistical significance in both TNC POST and CD34+ cell models. Models 3-6 (Table 2) and models 2-4 (Table 3) were potential selection models for UCB units with high isolation yield of TNC POST and CD34+ cells, respectively. The model that includes birth weight, delivery mode, blood volume, and gestational age met our objective of developing a versatile model that can be used for both TNC POST and CD34+ cell prediction. The reference values of these variables were: birth weight >3200 g, vaginal delivery mode, blood volume >79.22 mL, and gestational age >39 weeks (TNC POST) or ≤39 weeks (CD34+ cells). Regarding gestational age, it should be noted that it remarkably influences neonatal morbidity. Previous studies showed that neonatal intensive care unit admission and neonatal morbidity were higher in early-term infants. Compared to birth at 39-41 weeks of gestation, birth at 35-37 weeks was independently associated with higher frequencies of clinically relevant adverse perinatal outcomes, including increased risk for intrapartum/neonatal mortality, respiratory morbidity, infectious complications, and neurologic morbidities, while morbidities between 38 and 39 weeks were not significantly different.35–37 Therefore, the most appropriate reference value for gestational age in the selection models should be 38-39 weeks.

Our selection model suggested in this study consisted of blood volume; however, previous studies highlighted that volume was not the best predictor of CD34+ CBU content.32,38 The differences arose from the different methodological approaches among the studies but, more importantly, were not conflicting. Specifically, TNC (after processing) was included in the studies by Fumarola et al.32 and Mazzocchetti et al.38 as an independent variable in multivariate analyses to predict the CD34+ cell content. Results showed that compared with blood volume, TNC was a stronger predictor of CD34+ cell isolation yield. However, our aim was to build models that can be applied at the end of cord blood procurement when the decision to proceed to processing can be made before any assays are performed. Thus, although we recognized that TNC PRE positively correlated with both TNC POST and CD34+ cell counts, neither TNC PRE (before processing) nor TNC POST (after processing) was included in multivariate analyses in our study. This selection model, in combination with optimized procurement and HSC expansion techniques, will facilitate the development of potential stem cell applications in the future. More importantly, our results supplement existing stem cell banking practices and provide practical applications for any type of stem cell bank. It is acknowledged that not all variables influencing the isolation outcome of cord blood TNC and CD34+ were included in this study. Further studies investigating additional variables are therefore needed.
