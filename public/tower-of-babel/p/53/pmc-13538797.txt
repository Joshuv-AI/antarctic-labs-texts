Continuous Glucose Monitoring and Hypoglycaemia Risk During Paediatric DKA Treatment in the PICU: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Minsun Kim, Esther Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHypoglycaemia is a frequent complication during diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) treatment. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) provides real‐time glucose trend information; however, its clinical utility during paediatric DKA management in the intensive care unit (ICU) remains uncertain. We investigated whether CGM insertion during DKA treatment in the paediatric ICU (PICU) is associated with reduced hypoglycaemia and a lower burden of point‐of‐care (POC) glucose measurements.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective single‐centre cohort study of paediatric patients (< 20 years) admitted to the PICU with DKA between December 2013 and December 2025 who received continuous intravenous insulin infusion. Patients were categorised based on CGM insertion during their PICU stay. The primary outcome was hypoglycaemia. Secondary outcomes included frequency of POC glucose measurements, ICU length of stay and time to DKA resolution.ResultsSeventy‐three patients were included; 31 (42.5%) underwent CGM insertion. The incidence of hypoglycaemia was significantly lower in the CGM group (glucose < 70 mg/dL: 6.5% vs. 47.6%, p < 0.001; < 60 mg/dL: 0% vs. 21.4%, p = 0.008). In multivariable analysis, CGM insertion was independently associated with lower hypoglycaemia risk (adjusted OR 0.059, 95% CI 0.011–0.323; p = 0.001). The CGM group also had a significantly lower frequency of POC glucose measurements than the control group.ConclusionAmongst paediatric patients with DKA treated in the PICU, CGM insertion was independently associated with reduced hypoglycaemia and a lower POC testing burden. Prospective studies are warranted to determine whether CGM‐guided DKA management improves clinical outcomes in critically ill children.Keywords: continuous glucose monitoring, diabetic ketoacidosis, paediatric intensive care unit

Introduction

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is still one of the main causes of diabetes‐related morbidity in children, and it needs close monitoring in the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) [1, 2]. To treat DKA, metabolic correction must be performed promptly and thoroughly, with continuous insulin infusion and fluid therapy [1, 2]. But such intensive metabolic management may lead to iatrogenic hypoglycaemia [2, 3, 4]. Although the incidence of iatrogenic hypoglycaemia during DKA treatment has not been extensively investigated, previous studies have reported hypoglycaemia rates ranging from approximately 13.1% to 35% [5, 6].

Hypoglycaemia is linked to increased inpatient mortality among critically ill patients as well as those admitted to general wards [7, 8]. Among hospitalised patients with diabetes, each day complicated by hypoglycaemia has been associated with an approximately 2.5‐day increase in length of stay (LOS) [9]. Moreover, severe or unrecognized hypoglycaemia can lead to neurological injury, cognitive impairment, seizures and coma [10]. Furthermore, concern for and occurrence of iatrogenic hypoglycaemia in DKA patients leads to slowing or cessation of an intravenous insulin infusion, which ultimately prolongs the duration of DKA therapy [11].

Current management of DKA primarily depends on intermittent bedside capillary point‐of‐care (POC) glucose measurements to guide insulin and fluid therapy [2, 12]. However, this method offers limited real‐time insight into glucose dynamics and may delay the detection of impending hypoglycaemia during the acute management of DKA [13]. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), which provides interstitial glucose values, including the direction and rate of change of glucose, has been proposed as the preferred method to minimise the risk of hypoglycaemia in both outpatient and inpatient settings [14, 15, 16].

Despite these potential advantages, current clinical practise does not recommend routine CGM use in the treatment of DKA, owing to insufficient evidence regarding its accuracy and safety in this context [2]. Therefore, we investigated whether CGM insertion during paediatric DKA treatment in the PICU is associated with improved safety, as reflected by reduced hypoglycaemia and a lower burden of POC glucose testing.

Methods

Study Design

A retrospective chart review was conducted to collect data from the medical records of paediatric patients aged < 20 years admitted to the PICU with DKA between December 2013 and December 2025 at Jeonbuk National University Children's Hospital (Jeonju, South Korea). Patients were excluded if their ICU LOS exceeded 10 days or if they did not receive continuous intravenous insulin infusion therapy. Repeat admissions for DKA were included and analysed as independent clinical events.

A total of seventy‐eight patients were admitted to the PICU with a diagnosis of DKA during the study period. We excluded patients with an ICU LOS exceeding 10 days (one patient) and four patients who did not receive continuous intravenous insulin infusion. Consequently, 73 patients were included in the final analysis. Patients were categorised into two groups according to whether they had a CGM inserted during their PICU stay. None of the patients were wearing a CGM device at the time of admission. As part of routine clinical practise at our institution, when patients with diabetes, including those presenting with DKA, were admitted to the PICU, the attending physician informed the guardian about the availability and potential clinical utility of CGM as an adjunct monitoring tool. A CGM device was inserted only when the guardian provided informed consent. CGM was applied solely as part of routine clinical care and was not inserted for research purposes. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Jeonbuk National University Hospital (IRB No. 2022‐10‐026). No informed consent was required because the data were collected through a retrospective chart review and contained no personally identifiable information.

Definition of Diagnostic Criteria and Outcome Measures

The diagnosis of DKA was based on the 2022 International Society for Pediatric and Adolescent Diabetes guidelines [2]. The severity of DKA was classified according to the level of acidosis upon admission: mild (pH < 7.3 or bicarbonate < 18 mmol/L), moderate (pH < 7.2 or bicarbonate < 10 mmol/L), or severe (pH < 7.1 or bicarbonate < 5 mmol/L) [2].

Clinical characteristics included age, sex, height, body weight, body mass index (BMI), type of diabetes, new‐onset diabetes, Glasgow coma scale, insurance level, and intensivist coverage. Height, body weight, and BMI were converted into standard deviation scores (SDS) using the 2017 Korean National Growth Charts [17]. The insurance levels that applied to medical expense coverage were National Health Insurance and Medical Aid. Intensivist coverage was defined as the presence of a board‐certified paediatric intensivist in the ICU at the time of patient admission.

Laboratory data were collected with initial blood gas analysis, including pH, total CO2, bicarbonate, base excess, anion gap and lactate levels. In addition, the serum electrolyte level, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, white blood cell count, haematocrit, platelet count, C‐reactive protein, HbA1c, serum C‐peptide level and urine ketone levels were included. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was computed utilizing the revised Schwartz equation [18].

The primary outcomes were the incidence of hypoglycaemia, defined as the percentage of patients who encountered at least one hypoglycaemic event, and the total count of hypoglycaemic events during PICU management. For the primary analysis, hypoglycaemic events were counted throughout the PICU stay regardless of the timing of CGM insertion. Secondary outcomes included the frequency of POC glucose measurements, PICU LOS, overall hospital LOS, duration of insulin infusion, time to resolution of DKA, hospital mortality rates and treatment‐related parameters (initial insulin infusion rate, mean insulin infusion rate, and POC glucose at initiation of dextrose‐containing fluids).

Hypoglycaemic events were defined as blood glucose levels below 70 mg/dL (< 3.9 mmol/L) detected by capillary POC glucose monitoring. An additional assessment was conducted, defining hypoglycaemia as a glucose level < 60 mg/dL (< 3.3 mmol/L). The frequency of POC glucose measurements was calculated as the total number of POC glucose measurements during the ICU stay divided by the ICU LOS for each patient. The time to DKA resolution was determined by measuring the time between the patient's arrival in the emergency department and the resolution of ketoacidosis, defined based on the following criteria from the American Diabetes Association Consensus Statement on Hyperglycemic Crises—plasma glucose < 200 mg/dL (< 11.1 mmol/L) and two of the following: plasma bicarbonate ≥ 15 mEq/L, venous pH > 7.3 and anion gap ≤ 12 mEq/L [19] All patients were managed according to the same standardized institutional DKA treatment protocol, regardless of whether a CGM device was inserted. CGM was used for observational purposes only. The CGM group did not receive any protocol modifications or additional interventions compared with the control group.

Capillary POC Blood Glucose Measurements and CGM

POC glucose measurements were performed every 1–2 h during continuous intravenous insulin infusion using a capillary blood sampling method with a bedside glucose meter (Accu‐Chek Inform II; Roche Diagnostics, USA).

A CGM sensor wire was placed on the lower abdomen under aseptic conditions. Four different CGM systems were used: the Medtronic Guardian Sensor 3 (N = 13), the Dexcom G6 (N = 12), the Abbott FreeStyle Libre 1 (N = 5) and the CareSens Air (N = 1). The CGM reading range for the Medtronic Guardian Sensor 3 and Dexcom G6 is 40–400 mg/dL (2.2–22.2 mmol/L), and for the Abbott FreeStyle Libre 1 and CareSens Air, it is 40–500 mg/dL (2.2–27.8 mmol/L) [20, 21].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R Studio Version 2025.09.2 (Build 418). To compare the groups, we used the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. The data were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and as total number and percentages for categorical variables. We used a negative binomial regression model to estimate incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals for comparing hypoglycaemia episodes rates between the CGM and control groups, with ICU LOS included as an offset variable. To identify independent factors associated with hypoglycaemia (< 70 mg/dL), multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted. The primary multivariable model was developed a priori based on clinical significance and includes CGM insertion, admission body weight, DKA severity, and HbA1c. We reported adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Sensitivity analyses were conducted by further controlling for diabetes type and intensivist coverage to evaluate the robustness of the basic findings. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The incidence of hypoglycaemia < 70 mg/dL was significantly lower in the CGM group than in the control group (6.5% vs. 47.6%, p < 0.001). Likewise, hypoglycaemia < 60 mg/dL was significantly less frequent in the CGM group, occurring in 21.4% of patients in the control group and in none of the patients in the CGM group (p = 0.008).

The total number of hypoglycaemic events was substantially lower in the CGM group than in the control group. For glucose < 70 mg/dL, 70 episodes occurred in the control group compared with only three in the CGM group (p < 0.001). For glucose < 60 mg/dL, 20 episodes were recorded in the control group, whereas none occurred in the CGM group (p = 0.006).

In rate‐based analyses accounting for ICU LOS, CGM insertion was associated with a significantly lower rate of hypoglycaemic events. The IRR for hypoglycaemia < 70 mg/dL was 0.058 (95% CI 0.018–0.185; p < 0.001). For hypoglycaemia < 60 mg/dL, no episodes were observed in the CGM group, corresponding to an estimated IRR approaching zero (95% CI 0.000–0.200; p = 0.003) (Table S1).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of paediatric patients with DKA treated in the PICU, CGM insertion was associated with a significantly lower risk of treatment‐related hypoglycaemia and a reduced burden of POC glucose measurements. Importantly, the association between CGM insertion and hypoglycaemia remained robust after multivariable adjustment and sensitivity analyses. Notably, hypoglycaemic events were less frequent in the CGM group. To our knowledge, this study provides the first clinical evidence to demonstrate an independent association between CGM insertion and reduced hypoglycaemia risk during paediatric DKA management.

Aggressive metabolic correction in DKA creates a narrow therapeutic window and predisposes patients to iatrogenic hypoglycaemia, which has been associated with adverse neurological outcomes [4, 7, 22, 23]. The incidence of iatrogenic hypoglycaemia in DKA treatment has not been well studied, but a 35% incidence of hypoglycaemia was described with standard care of intravenous insulin [5]. Strategies that facilitate early detection and prevention of hypoglycaemia may enhance patient safety in critically ill paediatric patients with DKA. In current clinical practise, DKA management relies primarily on intermittent POC glucose measurements, which may be insufficient to capture rapid glycemic excursions [2, 13]. In contrast, CGM provides interstitial glucose values along with the direction and magnitude of glucose trends [13, 15]. Although CGM is widely used in outpatient diabetes care and is increasingly used in hospitalised patients, evidence regarding its role during DKA treatment in critically ill paediatric patients remains limited. To date, studies evaluating CGM in DKA have primarily focused on its performance and accuracy [24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Reported overall mean absolute relative difference (MARD) values during DKA management have ranged from approximately 10.8% to 14.5%, supporting the feasibility and reliability of CGM use in this setting [24, 25, 27, 29]. However, these studies have largely focused on CGM accuracy rather than clinical outcomes, including hypoglycaemia. In our cohort, among 73 total hypoglycaemic events, only 3 occurred in the CGM group, representing a statistically significant reduction compared with the control group. Notably, of the three hypoglycaemic events observed in the CGM group, only one occurred after CGM insertion, further supporting an association between active CGM monitoring and fewer hypoglycaemic events. In rate‐based analyses adjusted for ICU LOS, the CGM was associated with a markedly lower incidence of hypoglycaemia (IRR = 0.058), reinforcing the consistency of this association across analytic approaches.

Risk factors for hypoglycaemia during DKA treatment have included high‐dose insulin therapy, delayed initiation of dextrose‐containing fluids, the post‐transition period following the switch from intravenous to subcutaneous insulin, and impaired renal function [2, 3, 32]. To our knowledge, no previous reports have demonstrated a modifiable factor independently associated with a reduction in hypoglycaemia during DKA treatment. In our study, CGM insertion was independently associated with a substantially lower risk of hypoglycaemia in multivariable logistic regression analysis (adjusted OR 0.059, 95% CI 0.011–0.323; p = 0.001). In our study, CGM was used solely for observational purposes and did not alter the standardized DKA treatment protocol. These findings suggest that improved glucose visibility may be associated with safer glucose management during intensive metabolic correction. It is plausible that clinicians used glucose trend information to adjust insulin infusion rates or administer dextrose in advance, thereby facilitating anticipatory rather than reactive decision‐making.

The CGM also may offer several advantages over POC glucose measurements in patients with DKA, including improved patient comfort by avoiding repeated fingersticks and pain, sleep interruptions, and reduced workload for the healthcare team [33, 34, 35]. We observed a statistically significant reduction in the total frequency of POC glucose measurements in the CGM group. For Daily number of POC glucose measurements by ICU day analysis, this reduction became evident starting from ICU day 2, coinciding with the median time to CGM insertion in our study. These findings imply that CGM potentially can reduce nursing workload and patient discomfort. Although we did not perform a formal cost analysis, the reduction in nursing time and resources associated with frequent POC testing could help offset the CGM device costs.

By enabling continuous visualization of glucose trends, CGM facilitates earlier detection of impending hypoglycaemia and timely intervention. This shifts the paradigm from ‘reactive’ treatment—responding to a low POC glucose value—to ‘proactive’ management based on glucose trends. Our findings support the potential clinical utility of CGM in critical care settings, warranting further validation through controlled trials.

Our study had some limitations. First, the retrospective single‐centre design limits causal inference and generalizability. Second, CGM insertion timing and device selection were not standardized, and multiple device types were used. Third, although CGM was intended for observational purposes, unmeasured changes in clinician behaviour due to real‐time device visibility cannot be fully excluded. Finally, the relatively small sample size may have limited statistical power.

Despite these limitations, the observed associations between CGM insertion, reduced risk of hypoglycaemia, and decreased burden of invasive monitoring support the potential role of CGM as a safety‐enhancing monitoring modality during DKA treatment and provide a strong rationale for prospective interventional trials evaluating CGM‐guided DKA management on patient outcomes, patient experience and resource use.

Conclusion

In paediatric patients with DKA treated in the PICU, CGM insertion was associated with a reduced risk of hypoglycaemia and a lower burden of invasive monitoring. These findings suggest that continuous metabolic monitoring may be associated with improved safety during intensive DKA management. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to determine whether CGM‐guided DKA management strategies improve clinical outcomes in critically ill children.
