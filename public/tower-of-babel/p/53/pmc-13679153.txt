A risk management intervention in Australian hospital food service departments: an evaluation using the RE-AIM framework
Authors: Jodi Oakman, Jasmine Love, Joanne Marcucci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe RE-AIM (reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, maintenance) framework was used to undertake a process evaluation of a workplace intervention using the A Participative Hazard Identification and Risk Management (APHIRM) Toolkit. The aim of the intervention was to reduce work-related physical and psychosocial hazards associated with musculoskeletal disorders and stress related mental health in hospital food services employees. This study aimed to evaluate the process undertaken to support implementation of the APHIRM toolkit.MethodQualitative and quantitative data collected from individual participants, research team observations and organisations were used to report on the five dimensions of the RE-AIM framework and explore the implementation process of the APHIRM toolkit (the intervention).ResultsOrganisations reported a range of strategies to improve reach of the intervention, with variability across different work groups. Common challenges reported related to senior management support, communication and competing organisational priorities. A key strength of the APHIRM toolkit is the prioritisation of physical and psychosocial hazards and the opportunities for dialogue between workers and management and supervisors that do not normally exist.ConclusionsThis process evaluation of the intervention offered valuable insights which can be used to improve the APHIRM toolkit’s resources, tools, and training. It highlighted ongoing challenges in addressing psychosocial hazards and the important role of the toolkit in providing workers with an opportunity to be involved in developing tailored risk controls, through the use of a highly participative approach. For the hospital food service employees involved, this participatory approach was novel. Findings from this evaluation will inform modification of guidance and training provided within the APHIRM toolkit.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28638-w.

Background

Workplace interventions to address complex work-related health problems such as musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) and stress related mental health problems (MHPs) are often challenged by implementation issues which can impact their effectiveness [1–3]. Despite the significant cost and societal burden of workplace MSDs and MHPs [4] and the substantial overlap in the hazards associated with their aetiology, risk management approaches have been slow to adopt more comprehensive approaches [5–7].

Barriers to more comprehensive and arguably more effective risk management of MSDs have been documented and include a widespread assumption that physical disorders such as MSDs must be caused by physical hazards, and psychosocial hazards are associated with MHPs [8–10]. This mind body dualism is unhelpful and a key barrier to adoption of more comprehensive approaches where physical and psychosocial hazards are identified and addressed together along with their subsequent outcomes, MSDs and MHP [11].

Identification of relevant hazards is a key step in effective risk management programs [5]. Although many tools exist for MSD and MHP prevention, workplace practitioners do not always have access to these, nor do they feel confident in using them. Tools often focus on a subset of hazards, and only a few incorporate both physical and psychosocial hazards. A recent review which identified comprehensive tools for the purpose of identifying physical and psychosocial hazards for MSD prevention, found that only three tools met the definition of comprehensive, that is they identified and assessed a range of hazards and provided support including training for the full MSD risk management process [12].

To address this significant research to practice gap, A Participative Hazard Identification and Risk Management (APHIRM) toolkit was developed to support workplaces in the identification of workplace physical and psychosocial hazards and their related outcomes, MSD and MHP [13]. The toolkit is designed to support participative development of risk controls to address the identified hazards and subsequently reduce MSD and MHP risk and is based on principles of implementation science. The APHIRM toolkit includes a variety of online risk management tools and associated resources to facilitate hazard identification, risk assessment, implementation of risk control actions and evaluation of their effectiveness via a routinely repeated cycle of procedures.

The APHIRM toolkit requires active participation from workers because levels of physical and psychosocial hazards are difficult or impossible for others to observe accurately. When a questionnaire such as the one used in the APHIRM toolkit, is completed by individual workers, information about those hazards and the sources of risk can be elicited using participative ergonomics procedures [14]. Workers can be asked about the prioritised hazards via formal focus groups and individual online feedback, or less formally via discussions at ‘toolbox’ meetings [13]. Suggestions for risk control actions can also be obtained in these ways. If the questionnaire is completed jointly by a small group of workers, the discussion required to rate each hazard can also encompass job-specific causes and potential risk control options. Work place risk management for MSD and stress related MHP is more effective in reducing risk when workers are actively involved in the process [15, 16].

Implementation in real world settings is complex and understanding of what works and what does not is an important part of intervention research [17]. Process evaluation provides an opportunity to explore the barriers and enablers to the effectiveness of intervention in depth [18, 19]. The use of an established framework provides a stronger basis for analysis than would otherwise be possible. The RE-AIM framework is a widely used process evaluation model which assesses implementation at both the individual and the staff and setting level. At the individual level Reach assesses who from the targeted population participated in the intervention and Effectiveness assesses the impact of the intervention on key outcomes, including any negative consequences. At the staff and setting level Adoption assesses the extent to which the workplace is willing to engage in and adopt the intervention and Implementation assesses how well the intervention was delivered as intended. Maintenance is assessed at both the individual level - whether outcomes are sustained long-term; and at the staff/setting level – the degree to which the intervention becomes a part of ongoing routine practice [20, 21]. This evaluation used both qualitative and quantitative data, with the aim to undertake a process evaluation of APHIRM toolkit implementation using the RE-AIM framework; understand barriers to implementation; and integrate the findings into future translation guidance to support uptake of the APHIRM toolkit beyond the current project.

Methods

Study design and context

This study reports on the independent evaluation of the implementation of a risk management intervention (the APHIRM toolkit, described in detail below) in food services departments across eight health services in Victoria, Australia using the RE-AIM framework. Health services were recruited by a private consultant who led the intervention. Six health services were located in metropolitan Melbourne and two in regional Victoria. The health services elected to focus on their food services areas who are responsible for preparation and delivery of patient meals across the hospital setting and have not previously received significant attention in work place prevention programs [22].

Intervention - the APHIRM toolkit

The APHIRM Toolkit is an evidence-based online tool designed to guide users through the five stages of the risk management cycle [13]. A risk management team (RMT) is responsible for implementing the toolkit, ideally led by an OHS manager and a general manager, one or two workers from the targeted area, a health and safety representative and a union representative. The APHIRM toolkit is a comprehensive intervention which supports identification of physical and psychosocial hazards associated with increased risk of MSDs and MHPs.

The five stages of the APHIRM toolkit cycle are summarised in Table 1. Workers complete an anonymous survey reporting their individual exposures to range of physical and psychosocial hazards and for outcomes, the frequency and severity of musculoskeletal pain and a stress measure. An online algorithm uses this survey data to identify and report the ‘top ten’ hazards – i.e., the five most strongly linked to musculoskeletal pain and the five most strongly linked to stress. Workplace conditions related to these hazards are identified through worker feedback and the RMT uses this feedback to develop and implement action plans to eliminate or reduce risks associated with MSD and MHP. The process involves consultation with the workgroup to identify what is causing these problems and discuss potential solutions, acknowledging their expertise in understanding their work. Extensive guidance and online training modules within the toolkit support the leaders of RMTs to implement APHIRM. An extensive guide to support development of risk controls in accordance with the hierarchy of risk controls is provided. Regular repetition (e.g., annually) of this cycle is encouraged and enables evaluation of previous action plans and continual improvement [8, 13].

Recruitment & data collection

Implementation of the APHIRM toolkit took place between August 2023 and May 2025. Semi-structured interviews with RMT leads and members were conducted to evaluate the implementation of the toolkit. RMT leads were first contacted in March/April 2024 via email with an invitation to participate in the evaluation of the APHIRM implementation. RMT leads participated in an initial interview via Zoom in April/May 2024 after workers had completed the anonymous survey and provided survey feedback (Stage 1 and 2). In July 2024, RMT leads were contacted to arrange time for research staff to visit the food service to conduct interviews with the RMT members in person. Site visits took place between September and December 2024, when development / implementation of action plans were in progress. RMT leads were contacted in March 2025 with an invitation to participate in a second interview. Interviews took place over Zoom between March-May 2025 prior to the second survey. RMT lead interviews were approximately 40 min and RMT member interviews, 20 min. The recruitment timeline is displayed in Fig. 1. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Ethics approval was provided by the La Trobe Human Research Ethics Committee (HEC24065).

APHIRM toolkit measures

Hazard exposure survey items

The APHIRM toolkit survey originally included 52 work-related hazard items, both biomechanical and psychosocial [13]. Biomechanical hazard items were developed in earlier stages of this research program, encompassing hazards arising from whole-body ‘manual handling’ tasks, repetitive hand-finger work and static postures. Psychosocial hazard items were initially drawn from a variety of sources including COPSOQ [23] and the Work Organisation Assessment Questionnaire [24], with wording adapted when necessary to suit the variable English literacy levels of Australia’s multicultural workforce, particularly among blue-collar workers. Recent expansion of the toolkit to enable MHP risk management entailed addition of a scale to measure Stress (described above) and the following five hazard items: whole-body and hand-finger vibration; unpleasant arguments/conflicts; aggression/violence; bullying; and sexual harassment. For all items, respondents rated their exposures on 5-point Likert scales of frequency or degree.

Primary outcomes

Musculoskeletal pain (MSP) score

Survey respondents rated frequency (0 ‘Never’ to 4 ‘Almost always’) and severity (1 ‘Mild’ to 3 ‘Severe’) of pain over the last six months for each of five body regions: neck/shoulders; hands/fingers; arms, middle/lower back; and hips/bottom/legs/feet. Frequency was multiplied by severity for each body region, then summed to generate a score out of 60.

Stress score

Occupational stress was measured using 11 survey items derived from the Stress, Cognitive Stress, and Burnout scales in the Health and Wellbeing domain of the Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire (COPSOQ, 23) and one item on sleep difficulties from the General Well Being Questionnaire [25]. Each of the 12 items was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 Not at all to 4 A large part of the time); ratings were summed to create a combined stress score out of 48.

Qualitative data

RMT lead and member interviews

Semi-structured interviews with members of the RMT explored each of the five dimensions of the RE-AIM framework: Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance. In the initial interview with the RMT leads, which took place after the anonymous baseline survey and survey feedback. Topics covered in the interview included reasons for engaging with the APHIRM toolkit, level of management support, communications strategies and survey participation of the workgroup including any barriers to participation. The interviews with the general RMT members were undertaken during the phase of action plan development and implementation of risk controls. Interview topics included: their experience with being involved in the RMT meetings, overall engagement of the workgroup and perceived benefits or negatives of the intervention. The final interview with RMT leads just prior to the second survey included questions on the development and implementation of action plans and any challenges encountered, perceived benefits of the toolkit, barriers to implementation and intentions to continue using the APHIRM toolkit. The complete list of questions for the semi-structured interviews is in Supplementary File 1.

Qualitative data analysis

Interviews were audio recorded, transcribed and uploaded to NVivo Software for coding [26]. Data were analysed deductively using the RE-AIM framework. The process undertaken was: (1) A coding framework based on the five RE-AIM domains described by Glasgow, Harden [20] was developed; (2) Four transcripts (two coded by each researcher) were reviewed by a third researcher, with subcodes within each RE-AIM domain refined through discussion; (3) The remaining transcripts were coded using the agreed coding framework, and additional subcodes added where required; (4) Subcodes were then organised into thematic categories within each of the five RE-AIM domains; (5) Thematic categories were reviewed and refined. The data collected to address each domain are presented in Table 2. The final interview occurred prior to the second survey, therefore the intention to continue using the APHIRM toolkit was assessed (rather than actual continued use). Timing of the final interview precluded the assessment of sustained change. Health services were allocated a letter to ensure confidentiality (e.g., Org A, Org B …).

Results

Results are presented under each of the RE-AIM domains with supporting quantitative and qualitative data.

Reach

Strategies to boost workgroup awareness and participation

The RMT leads used a range of approaches to inform the workgroup about the APHIRM toolkit and the implementation process. Strategies included flyers in prominent locations and engaging in person with staff. RMT leads wanted to provide sufficient information for workers to understand the purpose and the importance of engagement with the implementation of APHIRM. To facilitate survey completion, flyers with QR codes were posted in key locations. Some organisations ran sessions during work time for staff to attend and complete the survey. Running specific sessions enabled RMT leads to explain the purpose of the survey and answer any questions or clarify wording, which was important particularly when English was not the primary language. Some sessions were held at shift change to maximise participation rates. Some organisations used a paper survey to overcome barriers such as low ICT skills. Workgroup participation

Satisfaction with survey participation rates was mixed among the RMT leads. Response rates ranged from 44 to 100% of the workgroup (see Table 4). Barriers to participation included English as a second language, awareness or understanding of the intervention, inadequate communication and access to workers due to shift scheduling. For example, Org B noted higher response rates in the production kitchen (80%) which operates Monday to Friday, with less shift work compared to the finishing kitchen (64%) with a rotating roster schedule. A key facilitator to survey completion was having good management support.

Both Org A and D reported that although they achieved good participation in the survey, they received limited data on the second stage of the toolkit. Stage 2 involves the workgroup providing information on the causes and solutions to the identified hazards. Both organisations collected feedback individually from workers via the toolkit app, citing the nature of shift work as a challenge to run a group session to collect feedback. Org A noted that the group format was more likely to result in the collection of meaningful feedback and would address challenges with any language barriers.

Adoption

RMT Lead engagement

The RMT leads were positive about the focus of the intervention on addressing psychosocial hazards in addition to physical hazards. The process of consulting the workgroup and seeking their input on improving health and safety in their workplace was reported as valuable. The staged approach and online resources made APHIRM a straightforward tool to adopt. Support from the other food services involved in the project was considered a great resource. However, the RMT leads found the process was time- and resource- intensive. High workloads and staff shortages resulted in limited time to fully engage with all the resources contained in the toolkit. One organisation, distributed the baseline survey seven workgroups but the team could not then run the next steps of the process due to insufficient resourcing.

RMT member engagement

RMT members were positive about the increased profile on health and safety that the project facilitated. However, engagement with the intervention was reported as mixed. Although most RMT members reported participating in meetings, low awareness about the APHIRM toolkit and the intervention in general was observed across several sites. Some team members reported encouraging staff to complete the survey while others were unable to recall information about the survey.

Workgroup engagement

Feedback from the workgroups demonstrated mixed levels of engagement. RMT members noted that some staff would raise issues or ask for updates on progress. However, workgroup awareness was a common barrier to their engagement. In addition to shift work (as noted earlier under ‘reach’), the ‘time oriented’ nature of food services raised a barrier to participation, with difficulties in finding time to provide information to staff, organise their participation and deliver feedback on progress and outcomes. Staff reported concerns in taking time to complete the survey which impacted their ability complete their work. Slow progress through the stages of the toolkit was a further barrier, resulting in lost interest and concerns about the potential implementation of changes.

Management engagement

Although most RMT leads described senior management as generally supportive, engagement in the implementation of APHIRM varied. Some senior managers had very little understanding of the intervention, some were described as supportive but hands-off, while others were more engaged. Engagement from middle managers / supervisors was reported as critical to the successful implementation of APHIRM. Supervisors interact with staff regularly and are critical for encouraging staff to engage with the process through participation in the and RMT meetings. While some RMT leads reported having good support from middle managers, others experienced a lack of forward planning and communication from middle management which was difficult for RMT leads and staff. Information sessions held at project commencement on the APHIRM toolkit, and the implementation process were described as helpful for getting initial support from middle managers / supervisors. Staff changes during the project interrupted progress in some organisations.

Perceived benefits of the toolkit

RMT leads reported that the APHIRM toolkit provided a structured framework to risk management, with greater depth than other approaches, along with a prioritisation of which hazards to focus on. The implementation project created a network of other contacts who were also implementing the APHIRM toolkit, and this provided a valuable opportunity to share ideas and gain insights into the approaches used by other food services departments. The RMT leads reported that using the APHIRM toolkit helped with identifying what hazards are and how to report them, it also extended understanding of hazards beyond easily seen issues such as broken equipment. Overall RMT leads were satisfied with the implementation of APHIRM. They reported some good outcomes had been achieved and the experience was worthwhile. Within each of the organisations engagement of different workgroups varied.

The APHIRM toolkit provided staff with an opportunity to voice their concerns, share their ideas and be heard. RMT team members reported that the intervention had prompted management to be more responsive to concerns raised. RMT members were interviewed during the development and implementation of action plans. The timing of these interviews meant they could comment on the impact of actions already implemented. For example, one Food Services Assistant (Org A) reported a reduction in meal tray size, and changes to the process of loading trays on trolleys (i.e., heavier trays at waist height) which was reducing the physical effort required by workers. A manager from Org D, described the introduction of weekly huddles to provide opportunities for staff to raise concerns. During these interviews many actions were not implemented, hence overall satisfaction amongst the RMT members with the APHIRM toolkit was mixed. Although most were optimistic that positive changes would occur, some raised concerns about whether change would be sustained.

Implementation

RMT meetings and development of action plans

A key barrier to APHIRM toolkit implementation was scheduling and attendance at RMT meetings. Insufficient notice and forward planning to manage staff absence from the kitchen to attend meetings was commonly reported. RMT members reported receiving notifications via email; however, most kitchen staff do not regularly check their emails. Some RMT members considered that a broader range of staff were needed, e.g., representatives of different job roles or different contract types to assist with developing the action plans.

In the RMT meetings, possible solutions for each hazard were discussed, sometimes using the risk control guide to assist. However, in the early stages it was difficult to shift discussions from problems to solutions. Some hazards identified through the survey were no longer an issue by the time the RMT met, or a new issue had arisen. Action plans required consideration of multiple stakeholders, and therefore representation of different types of staff on the RMT was important. Psychosocial hazards were reported as more challenging for RMT leads to address and were described as more difficult to explain than physical hazards.

Implementation of action plans

At the time of the final interview with the RMT leads, most sites had implemented at least some action from their action plans, with others at various stages of progress. As described above scheduling meetings was challenging which contributed to delays in the development and implementation of action plans, particularly for actions that were complex and required further development for progress. Most sites identified funding as a key barrier to implementation of some actions including upgrades to or replacement of equipment. Organisational processes often required additional planning and testing before budgets were approved to upgrade/replace equipment. Staff training was important for implementation of new equipment and processes. As an example, in Org A, staff were not informed about a change tray sizes to reduce the weight, so incidents of food spillage were reported. In response, an action was created to improve the communication and training for new processes and equipment.

Maintenance

Most RMT leads were unsure about whether their organisation would continue to use the APHIRM toolkit. Although some were keen to maintain the use of the toolkit, the time and resources involved were a major barrier. The RMT meetings provided opportunities for obtaining in-depth information about the sources of workplace hazards and potential solutions from the perspective of workers and managers. However, with scheduling issues a challenge for RMT leads, they suggested additional administrative supported would have reduced the time pressure and improved feasibility for ongoing use of the APHRIM toolkit.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the process of APHIRM toolkit implementation using the RE-AIM framework domains of reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation and maintenance [21]. The use of a clearly defined framework provided an opportunity to collect data on the implementation process which can be used to improve guidance for future workplace interventions. Despite the significance of the MSD and MHP problem in workplaces, real world implementation and evaluations are rarely reported. Real world interventions are challenging, and the complexity of workplaces, personnel changes and competing organisational deliverables often result in projects being abandoned without reporting to improve future implementation approaches. Addressing research to practice gaps requires evaluation and reporting of both effective and ineffective strategies implemented to reduce risks associated with MSDs and MHPs.

Consistent with previous research prior to commencement intervention, strong senior and middle management support was required to improve the implementation process [26]. Organisations with strong support were more likely to report better progress. Early engagement improves managers understanding of the process and their engagement in the risk control process, which is important particularly for those controls which may require financial commitments. In addition, management and supervisor support is required to facilitate staffing availability, which is integral to participative approaches such as APHIRM for survey completion and to provide feedback and different stages of the implementation. A further consideration, not examined in this evaluation but relevant given the variability of the intervention process identified between the organisations, is the concept of Stage of Change [27], proposed to support change process in workplaces. The intervention examined in this study, the implementation of the APHIRM toolkit, identifies hazards at different organisational levels in the workplace, which then require the development and implementation of suitable risk controls. The use of the SOC model could support evaluation of the readiness to change a target behaviour, and aligning interventions with participants’ level of readiness to take action and/or change their behaviour, by focusing on factors most relevant to their current stage of change [28]. That is, workplace interventions can be tailored to suit the organisations stage of change and improve the likelihood of adoption and effectiveness [29].

Education of employees and their managers is critical to ensure understanding of the role of physical and psychosocial hazards in MSD aetiology. This is important as the survey covers a diverse range of hazards and understanding the relevancy of questions on psychosocial hazards to improve completion rates. Managers and supervisors need knowledge on MSD and MSP aetiology so assist them with guiding their workgroups and address questions, but also to understand the different types of risk control measures that might be required, particularly in relation to psychosocial hazards.

The intervention timelines were flexible to accommodate the different needs of participating organisation. On the one hand this is a strength, but the extended period of engagement required did result in some fatigue with the project. In organisations with multiple competing demands, a shorter duration of intervention may facilitate improved engagement particularly at the risk control implementation stage of the process.

The communication channels that the APHIRM toolkit intervention provided through its participative approach were clearly supported, and enabled dialogue between managers/supervisors and staff which was not otherwise possible. The presentation of the toolkit results and the opportunities to provide feedback on what was causing the issues experienced by the workers facilitated this communication process. Enabling processes such as these is a critical component for the creation of sustainable interventions [30]. Although, question around the sustainability of the toolkit usage could not be established the dialogue opportunities for staff with their supervisors and managed are an outcome that might be sustained outside of the formal APHIRM toolkit.

In relation to the changes in MSP and stress scores, reductions were observed in two of the four organisations. Perhaps Reduction in MSP and stress scores were observed in two of the four organisations, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the short follow-up period. As the APHIRM toolkit is designed to develop and implement risk controls to address hazards, reductions in hazard exposure are likely to be more reflective of immediate change due to the intervention than changes in MSP and stress.For future evaluations we propose that hazards measures are considered the primary outcome measure and MSP and stress as secondary [31]. A hazard focussed approach is supported by international experts in musculoskeletal disorders to reduce all pain likely to be worsened by work [32] and is consistent contemporary thinking on the need for multi-level interventions in workplaces [33, 34]. Hazards can be conceptualised as lead indicators and need to be reduced before a reduction in pain or stress levels is likely to be reported.

A vast array of implementation science frameworks now exist with the aim to improve understanding of how and why implementations succeed or fail [35]. Categorisation of the various frameworks has been reported and include process models, determinate frameworks, classic theories, implementation theories and evaluation frameworks [35]. Using this categorisation, RE-AIM is considered an evaluation framework and was selected as the best fit for the current study. However, substantial overlap exists between different frameworks, with some authors proposing the use of more than one is needed to ensure a comprehensive evaluation. RE-AIM was selected as it enabled a categorisation of the intervention into areas of interest to the funder of the project, and inform potential improvements to support future implementation of the APHIRM toolkit [20]. One area of the framework that was challenging to examine was maintenance given the project timelines. However, it is still useful to consider how maintenance might be maintained or opportunities to improve sustainability of the intervention. Adopting a pragmatic approach to the use of implementation frameworks and careful consideration of context is key [36] and the RE-AIM framework provide a useful structure to support the current evaluation.

Recommendations for future evaluations

Some useful recommendations arise from this study which can be considered in future evaluations. The inclusion of senior managers in the evaluation process is proposed to understand how they assess and approve the implementation process but also in the development of potential risk controls developed as part of the APHIRM toolkit process. Embedding feedback loops into the evaluation so it can be used to support improved implementation is a further consideration.

Recommendations for future implementation of the APHIRM toolkit

Future interventions would benefit from dedicated personnel to support the implementation process of the APHIRM toolkit. However, many of the steps within the APHIRM toolkit process should be undertaken as part of standard risk management, not in addition to current occupational health and safety requirements. Based on the findings from this evaluation it is clear that participant burden was considered high, and consideration of how to reduce this is needed. Ongoing training at each stage of the toolkit process could assist with increasing engagement.

In this case organisations from the same sector and same workgroups were implementing APHIRM simultaneously so a community of practice was developed throughout the intervention period. Development of a community of practice to provide support for organisations implementing the APHIRM toolkit may help with implementation.

Limitations

Workplace interventions are challenging to implement. The research team was not responsible for the intervention of the APHIRM toolkit. Its role was to evaluate the implementation process. The implementation was undertaken pragmatically to manage the many constraints that confront the health care sector with time frames frequently extended to maximise engagement and ensure completion of the intervention. Excessive work demands were often cited as a barrier to participation at all stages of the interventions. Information on the plan and prepare stage, which included informing senior management and establishment of the risk management team (Table 1) was not clearly documented, which is a limitation for the current evaluation given some of the variability with the processes that was discovered across the different organisations. In addition, a feedback loop throughout the evaluation was not embedded in the process which could have been used to provide some interim feedback. Staff turnover impacted intervention progress. Training for RMT leads was provided at the beginning of the intervention by the private provider. However, newly engaged RMT leads and RMT team members did not receive the same training which was a limitation to progress. The anonymous nature of the baseline and follow up survey mean that causal inferences cannot be inferred.

Conclusions

Despite many challenges with the intervention reported here, valuable insights were provided from this process evaluation which can be incorporated into the resources, tools and training provided within the APHRIM toolkit. The APHIRM toolkit provided opportunities for workers to identify and then contribute to the development of risk controls that were specific to their own workgroup needs. For food services employees in hospitals this was a novel experience, as they were not usually a primary focus of prevention activities and this process enabled a mechanism for them to engage with their supervisors and managers. Findings from this evaluation will inform modification of guidance and training provided with the freely available APHIRM toolkit which is designed to support risk management of MSDs and MHPs in workplaces.
