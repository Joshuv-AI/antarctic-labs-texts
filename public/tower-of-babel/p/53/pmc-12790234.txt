Thrombolysis of Acute ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction With Concurrent Polytrauma
Authors: Matthew Y. Lim, Kieran Macleod, Kapil Kumar, Ashlee Jeffries, John Floridis, Daniel Y. Ellis, Karen Teo, John F. Beltrame, Peter J. Psaltis, Jessica A. Marathe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTrauma-related myocardial infarction (MI) is rare. In regional and rural settings, ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) may necessitate systemic thrombolysis despite concurrent relative contraindications.Case SummaryA 47-year-old woman experienced an anterior STEMI immediately following a motor vehicle accident in a regional area. Given definitive care was at least 3 hours away, multidisciplinary consultation was undertaken, and administration of systemic thrombolysis was recommended. Successful coronary reperfusion was achieved, but the patient developed internal iliac artery bleeding requiring embolization. Coronary angiography revealed a mild left anterior descending artery stenosis. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging confirmed MI with evidence of microvascular obstruction, late gadolinium enhancement, and myocardial edema in the territory of the left anterior descending artery.DiscussionThis is to our knowledge the only reported case of systemic thrombolysis for trauma-related MI in the past 30 years.Take-Home MessageMultidisciplinary collaboration in regional and rural settings is important to ensure optimal outcomes where relative contraindications to thrombolysis may exist.

History of Presentation

A 47-year-old woman was transferred to a regional emergency department in southern Australia after a motor vehicle accident in which her car was struck by a truck at 60 km/h (38 miles/h). She was hemodynamically stable, with no visible injuries other than superficial bruising and skin tears, but was disoriented, nauseated, and reported pelvic pain.

Urgent whole-body computed tomography (CT) performed as per trauma protocol demonstrated right sacral ala, bilateral pubic rami, and lumbar-5 transverse process fractures, without active hemorrhage. Her electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated T-wave inversion in leads V3-V4 and aVL (Figure 1A), prompting measurement of troponin-T, which was elevated at 53 ng/L. Repeat ECG 30 minutes later showed evolving pseudonormalization, with biphasic anterior T waves. Despite the absence of chest pain, the rapidly evolving pattern on ECG, suggestive of acute anterior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), prompted discussion with the on-call cardiologist at the nearest quaternary metropolitan hospital. Given an estimated transfer time of at least 3 hours to a center capable of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), it was recommended to proceed with systemic thrombolysis after consulting with orthopedic, radiology, and retrieval specialists, particularly given the absence of radiological evidence of hemorrhage. Tenecteplase (35 mg) was administered, and emergency retrieval to the quaternary hospital was concurrently arranged. Full-dose thrombolysis was considered appropriate given the large territory of myocardium at risk, the ECG showing >5 mm of ST-segment elevation,1 and lack of demonstrable bleeding at the time of initial assessment.

Shortly after thrombolysis, the patient's ECG improved, with >50% resolution of ST-segment elevation (Figure 1C), but she developed acute lower back pain, and during helicopter retrieval reported new photophobia and phonophobia. This was accompanied by new intermittent rotational nystagmus and deconjugate gaze, prompting concern for neurological complications. She concurrently became hemodynamically unstable.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history included migraine with aura and stable anorexia nervosa. She had none of the traditional cardiovascular risk factors and no family history of cardiovascular disease.

Differential Diagnosis

Multiple etiologies were considered at the time of her ECG changes, including spontaneous coronary artery dissection or Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (TTC). However, systemic thrombolysis was still administered before patient transfer on the basis that the most likely cause was still acute atherothrombosis and that access to invasive coronary angiography and revascularization would be delayed.

Investigations

Repeat whole-body CT shortly after arrival at the quaternary center demonstrated active left internal iliac artery bleeding and a left vertebral artery dissection with associated left occipital and bilateral cerebellar lobe infarcts; these were thought to be trauma induced and subsequently aggravated by the thrombolytic therapy. Invasive coronary angiography was delayed given these findings, which was considered acceptable given ST-segment elevation resolution.

The following day, the patient's high-sensitivity troponin-T peaked at 5,460 ng/L and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide at 2,368 ng/L. This, along with an unchanged ECG, made TTC less likely. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed moderate left ventricular (LV) dysfunction (LV ejection fraction: 35%-40%) with severe anterior segment hypokinesis, also not typical for TTC (Videos 1 and 2).

Invasive coronary angiography was performed on the day after presentation and showed normal antegrade flow in all coronary arteries, with a mild luminal stenosis (∼20%) evident in the midsegment of the left anterior descending artery (LAD) (Figure 2). Neither intracoronary imaging nor percutaneous intervention was performed during this procedure. A few days later, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed an LV ejection fraction of 55% and LAD-territory regional wall motion abnormalities with subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement, microvascular obstruction, and myocardial edema, consistent with subacute anterior MI (Figure 3). The patient's lipid profile revealed total cholesterol 4.5 mmol/L (81.1 mg/dL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol 2.4 mmol/L (43.2 mg/dL), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol 1.0 mmol/L (18.0 mg/dL), and triglycerides 2.4 mmol/L (43.2 mg/dL), with an elevated lipoprotein (a) of 254 nmol/L (101.6 mg/dL) and glycated hemoglobin of 4.7%.

Management

The patient was hospitalized for a total of 22 days. Tranexamic acid and adult-dose cryoprecipitate were administered during the acute bleed, and the bleeding internal iliac vessel was successfully embolized using Gelfoam injection; postprocedural tranexamic acid infusion was not required. After embolization, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit. A single blood transfusion was required for anemia, and her pelvic fractures were managed conservatively. Her neurological status also stabilized.

Given the lack of obstructive coronary disease, there was no apparent role for PCI, and the patient was managed conservatively despite the cause of the MI not definitively established. The patient remained on single antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 100 mg daily) and prophylactic heparin (5,000 IU twice daily) for 5 days until the risk of bleeding was felt to have reduced sufficiently; she was then commenced on dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 100 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg daily), high-dose atorvastatin, and an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (perindopril). Beta-blockers were initially avoided owing to concerns regarding hemodynamic instability. Repeat TTE before discharge showed partial recovery of LV function, with her LV ejection fraction improving to 45% to 50%.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient's recovery was rapid, and she regained independent mobility within weeks. Unfortunately however, her visual field defect persisted, suggestive of permanent ischemic injury.

Follow-up TTE at 3 months demonstrated a sustained LV ejection fraction of 45% to 50%, with persisting LAD-territory regional wall motion abnormalities. To guide future management, a coronary CT angiogram was performed at 3 months, which revealed a moderate to severe noncalcified stenosis in the mid-LAD (Figure 4) at the site of the previously mild luminal stenosis. This was confirmed on invasive coronary angiography (Figure 5), with optical coherence tomography revealing fibroatheroma in the midvessel with a minimal luminal area of 1.53 mm2 (Figure 6) and corresponding fractional flow reserve of 0.87. These findings were thought to support the hypothesis that the initial MI was rupture or erosion of a nonobstructive atherosclerotic plaque, with subsequent accelerated plaque growth in the months after the MI despite guideline-recommended secondary prevention therapy. At the time of last follow-up (8 months post-MI), the patient's ECG showed persistent, albeit improved, anterolateral T-wave inversion (Figure 1D). She has not experienced any angina or heart failure symptoms and leads an active lifestyle with regular physical exertion. Secondary prevention has been a continuing focus. Her low-density lipoprotein cholesterol was reduced to 1.9 mmol/L (34.2 mg/dL) after 6 months of treatment with atorvastatin 80 mg/ezetimibe 10 mg, hence she was commenced on evolocumab 140 mg twice weekly.

Discussion

We conducted a literature review encompassing case reports from the past 30 years. A search of OVID Medline ALL, OVID Embase, and Cochrane Library up to and including December 3, 2024, using the terms “trauma” and “myocardial infarction” yielded 3,504 initial articles. Included in the review were cases that involved MI from coronary artery pathologies; cases resulting primarily from cardiac contusion or ventricular/pericardial injuries were excluded. Ultimately, 123 relevant cases from 120 articles were reviewed (Supplemental Table 1).

Of the 123 included patients, the majority were young (mean: 38.5 years), male (91.9%), and without traditional cardiac risk factors (64.2%). The most frequently reported pathology was coronary artery dissection (47.2%); plaque rupture or erosion was uncommon (4.1%). Motor vehicle accidents were the most common mechanism of injury (52.0%), followed by sporting injuries (17.9%). STEMI was the predominant clinical presentation (87.0%), with the LAD being the most common culprit vessel (65.9%); multivessel injury (2.4%) occurred in only 3 patients.

There was a high rate of serious complications noted (41.3%), defined as death (8.9%), cardiac arrest (6.5%), shock of any description (5.7%), heart failure (13.0%), new-onset atrial fibrillation (2.4%), pericardial effusion (2.4%), and heart block (2.4%). Treatment in most cases was by coronary stenting (33.3%), followed by conservative management (23.6%) and coronary bypass surgery (11.4%) (Supplemental Table 1). A single case2 was identified in which intracoronary thrombolysis was administered, however we found no cases describing use of systemic thrombolysis. Thus, the current case is to our knowledge the only example in the past 30 years documenting the use of systemic thrombolysis in trauma-related MI.

Australia, alongside many other countries, faces challenges with health care access in rural and remote areas, with vast distances between rural practices and centers at which PCI can be performed.3,4 Further discussion and consensus opinion is needed regarding the optimal approach to STEMI management in such cases, highlighting the importance of case reports and case series to share experiences between clinicians, as there is a lack of established guidelines for managing complicated STEMI in this context.3,5,6

The long-term implications of trauma-induced plaque rupture/erosion remain poorly understood. This case demonstrates the accelerated progression of nonobstructive plaque to an obstructive, albeit asymptomatic, lesion within months despite guideline-recommended therapy. We suspect that this may have occurred via heightened coronary and systemic inflammation secondary to the constellation of trauma, acute MI, stroke, and bleeding. Conclusions

This case is a unique example of trauma-induced STEMI without prior obstructive atherosclerosis. It illustrates the complex, often dynamic decision-making process required in resource-limited settings, highlighting the dilemma of administering systemic thrombolysis in patients where hemorrhagic risk must be weighed against the need for timely myocardial reperfusion. We present this case as a cautionary example reinforcing the need for individualized, multidisciplinary, and evidence-informed decisions in similar scenarios.
