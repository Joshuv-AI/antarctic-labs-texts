Knowledge improvement on premarital HIV counseling with PAUSE© flipchart versus conventional method: a cluster randomised controlled trial
Authors: Aznida Firzah Abdul Aziz, Magaletchumi Chelladorai, Chai Eng Tan, Leelavathi Muthupalaniappen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPremarital HIV testing was made mandatory for Muslim couples in Malaysia as an effort to curb the transmission of HIV. The PAUSE© flipchart is a Malay language counselling tool to facilitate consistent and effective pre-test counselling for premarital couples. This study evaluates the effectiveness of the PAUSE© flipchart versus conventional methods in enhancing HIV knowledge at public primary healthcare clinics in Johor Bahru.MethodsA parallel cluster randomised controlled trial was conducted among adult Malay-literate clients who registered for mandatory premarital HIV testing. Cluster randomisation was used to select 8 public primary healthcare clinics from Johor Bharu District to be allocated into intervention and control groups. Clients in the intervention group (n = 90) received counselling using the PAUSE© flipchart, whereas clients in the control group (n = 90) received routine verbal counselling. HIV knowledge was assessed using the Malay HIV Knowledge Questionnaire before and after counselling. Understandability of the PAUSE© flipchart was evaluated using the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool for Printable Materials (PEMAT-P) among clients in the intervention group.ResultsBoth groups showed significant increases in HIV knowledge post-counselling (p < 0.001). The intervention group had a mean score increase of 6.99 (95% CI [6.01, 7.97]), significantly higher than the control group’s 3.99 (95% CI [2.89, 5.09]). Post-counselling scores were higher in the intervention group [mean 19.99 (SD = 1.26) vs. 16.59 (SD = 4.41), p < 0.001]. The PAUSE© flipchart demonstrated high understandability with a mean score of 95.92 (SD:7.15) among clients.ConclusionThe PAUSE© flipchart is a highly acceptable, user-friendly, and more effective alternative to conventional premarital HIV counselling, warranting adoption in primary care to standardize and strengthen client education.Trial registrationThis trial was retrospectively registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry on 26 June 2024 (ACTRN12624000788572).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03469-1.Keywords: HIV Infections, Health Education, Counselling, Patient Education

Background

The global HIV/AIDS epidemic continues to pose a significant public health challenge despite advances in medical treatment and prevention strategies. As of 2023, approximately 38 million people worldwide were living with HIV, underscoring the ongoing need for effective prevention and education efforts [1]. The World Health Organisation (WHO) advocates for voluntary screening programs and counselling in nations with elevated HIV prevalence [2]. Over the decades, HIV testing in the public sphere has transitioned from being concentrated within high-risk populations to encompassing a wider voluntary counselling and testing (VCT) demographic. In the initial phase, following the guidance of the WHO, VCT was exclusively a voluntary initiative, allowing individuals to access health facilities for status screening at their discretion. Subsequently, several countries revised the VCT program, instituting mandatory pre-marital HIV screening for couples prior to marriage. This initiative aligns with the 2012 World Health Organisation’s guidance on couples HIV testing and counselling, which includes antiretroviral therapy for treatment and prevention in serodiscordant couples. This is advised for all couples to identify, enhance uptake, and ensure compliance with antiretroviral treatment (ART) [3].

In Malaysia, the epidemic persists with notable progress in certain areas, yet critical gaps remain, particularly in public awareness and education about HIV/AIDS [4]. By 2022, an estimated 86,142 Malaysians were living with HIV (PLHIV), with 81% aware of their status. The most affected populations include people who inject drugs, followed by female sex workers, transgender individuals, men who have sex with men as well as heterosexual transmission.

In Malaysia, the mode of HIV transmission has shifted over the years, with sexual transmission now accounting for the majority of new infections. In response, premarital HIV counselling and testing (PHCT) was introduced as a key public health strategy, consistent with approaches adopted in many other countries [5]. As Malaysian law recognises only heterosexual marriage, prevention efforts have primarily focused on heterosexual transmission, particularly among Muslim couples intending to marry. PHCT was first implemented in Johor in 2001 and later expanded nationwide under the mandate of the State and Central Religious Affairs Departments [6]. It is now compulsory for all prospective Muslim couples, who represent the majority population, with the aim of curbing HIV transmission within marriage and facilitating early intervention for those diagnosed. In contrast, non-Muslim couples are encouraged to undergo testing on a voluntary basis, as there is currently no legal requirement for them to do so.

The Malaysian Ministry of Health’s inclusion of PHCT as a standard practice reflects a proactive approach in reducing HIV transmission and promoting health awareness among couples at a critical juncture in their lives [7, 8]. Effective pre-test counselling in the premarital context aims to inform couples about their HIV status, educate them on prevention methods, and reduce stigma associated with HIV. Pre-test counselling is essential as part of the process of obtaining informed consent prior to HIV testing [9]. Pre-test counselling also provides an opportunity to improve public awareness regarding HIV and AIDS particularly in populations with lower health literacy. It also helps the client to be prepared for possible outcomes of the test. When pre-test counselling has been done well, it facilitates post-test counselling especially when the test result is positive. Only PHCT conducted at public primary healthcare clinics is valid and accepted by the state religious departments processing applications for marriage.

The quality and delivery of premarital HIV counselling in public primary healthcare clinics vary considerably. Counselling is often conducted by healthcare providers with different levels of training—ranging from medical officers to staff nurses—without a standardised counselling aid. Consequently, the effectiveness of these sessions depends heavily on individual providers’ knowledge, communication skills, and familiarity with current HIV/AIDS guidelines, leading to inconsistencies in content, depth, and quality. Similar issues have been reported internationally, such as in Flanders, Belgium, where a lack of standardised procedures, insufficient training, and language barriers were associated with inconsistent HIV counselling practices [10]. Other studies have also noted that inadequate facilities compromising privacy, limited training, and overburdened counsellors with scarce resources can undermine the effectiveness of HIV education and prevention counselling [11].

In the Malaysian context, counselling duration can range from brief to prolonged sessions depending on the counsellor’s approach, with language differences and low health literacy further limiting comprehension. Such variability risks leaving some clients without adequate or accurate information, reducing the potential impact of premarital counselling as a preventive strategy.

This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a standardized, expert-developed counselling tool—in improving HIV-related knowledge among premarital counselling clients in Malaysia. Knowledge was selected as the primary outcome because it represents the foundation upon which preventive behaviours are built. Improved HIV knowledge has been associated with increased uptake of voluntary testing, greater adherence to preventive practices, and reductions in risky sexual behaviour, all of which contribute to lowering HIV transmission rates. By ensuring that all clients receive clear, accurate, and comprehensive information regardless of provider variability, the counseling guide has the potential to strengthen the preventive function of premarital HIV counselling and contribute to better public health outcomes.

In 2023, the PAUSE© flipchart was created by Zainal et al. as an effort to standardize premarital HIV counselling delivery [12, 13]. This flipchart is a paper-based tool which presents structured patient education content for counselling clients who require HIV screening as a pre-requisite for marriage. A paper-based format was chosen for this tool because of lower cost for production and easy portability within different rooms in the health clinic. This tool included an overview of HIV and AIDS, mode of transmission, the HIV testing process, benefits and risks of HIV testing, implications of both positive and negative tests, prevention, and confidentiality of test results [13]. The role of the PAUSE© flipchart was to serve as a guide to help the counsellors deliver a structured counselling session to the clients.

The PAUSE© flipchart underwent content validation by a panel of experts, including infectious disease specialists, family medicine specialists with a special interest in HIV and communicable diseases, family medicine specialists involved in managing HIV patients, and trained HIV counsellors. Additionally, face validation was conducted among clients of mixed races and healthcare workers to ensure its broad applicability and acceptance. The flipchart demonstrated excellent understandability, scoring above 70% on the PEMAT-P questionnaire, indicating that clients found it easy to comprehend. Preliminary evaluations have shown that the PAUSE© flipchart was effective in facilitating premarital pre-test counselling, with a statistically significant improvement in overall post-counselling HIV knowledge scores. This tool represents a significant advancement in standardizing and enhancing the quality of premarital HIV counselling in Malaysia. Hence standardized counselling can be delivered consistently by any at public primary healthcare clinic provider assigned to the Premarital Counselling clinic, whether they are a medical officer, medical/physician assistant, community health nurse, or counsellor based at the public primary healthcare centre (Klinik Kesihatan). However, the pilot study was a single-centre study with a small sample size, and the use of convenience sampling limited the ability to generalize the study results.

This cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT) aimed to assess the efficacy of the PAUSE© flipchart in comparison to traditional counselling approaches for enhancing HIV knowledge among clients at designated public primary healthcare clinics in Johor Bahru district. The main purpose was to evaluate the enhancement of HIV knowledge scores following structured counselling, whereas the secondary objectives involved assessing the comprehensibility of the PAUSE© flipchart among clients.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a parallel cluster randomised controlled trial across public primary healthcare clinics in the Johor Bahru District of Malaysia. To prevent cross-contamination between participants receiving different counselling interventions, we utilised clustering at the clinic level. Clinics located within the same sub-district were allocated to the same study arm. All counsellors were based on-site at their respective clinics and did not provide outreach services to neighbouring facilities.

Participants

The study population consisted of individuals attending mandatory premarital HIV testing services at the selected sites. To be eligible, participants had to be at least 18 years old and proficient in Malay. We excluded individuals with a history of premarital HIV counselling or testing, healthcare professionals, and those with visual or auditory impairments.

Sample size

We calculated the required sample size using data from Zainal et al. [13] and established methods for deriving mean and variance from median values [14]. To detect a mean difference of 0.9 in HIV knowledge scores (𝛼= 0.05, 80% power), 82 participants were needed per arm. After accounting for a potential 10% non-response rate, we set the final recruitment target at 180 participants (90 per group).

Randomisation and sampling

A two-stage randomisation strategy was employed. Initially, we used simple random sampling via a computer-generated sequence to select 8 out of 16 available clinics, which were then allocated 1:1 to either the intervention (n = 4) or control (n = 4) group. Clinics were grouped by sub-district to further ensure no overlap between study arms.

Secondly, we recruited individual participants using systematic sampling. We screened chronological client lists and approached every second eligible individual until reaching a quota of 22–23 participants per clinic. If a client declined, the next eligible person was invited to maintain the sampling interval.

Intervention and control

In the intervention arm, participants underwent structured counselling using the PAUSE© flipchart. This 16-page validated tool focuses on HIV transmission, testing, and prevention, featuring infographics for clients and cues for the provider [13]. One healthcare worker per clinic delivered the sessions (approx. 15–20 min) following a 3-hour training workshop. The control arm received standard, non-structured verbal counselling as per routine clinic practice, typically lasting 5–10 min.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was HIV knowledge, measured using a 21-item Malay version of the HIV Knowledge Questionnaire [13]. This tool, originally developed by Nyamathi et al., [17] evaluates both cognitive and transmission-related knowledge with scores from 0 to 21. For the intervention group, we also assessed the PAUSE© flipchart’s clarity using the PEMAT-P understandability subscale, where scores above 70% represent good understandability [15, 16]. All participants completed the knowledge assessment immediately before and after their counselling sessions. Refer to Fig. 1 for the study overview.

Blinding

Given the nature of the counselling interventions, blinding was not possible. However, the use of self-administered questionnaires immediately following the intervention helped mitigate potential measurement bias.

Statistical analysis

Analysis was performed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) [18]. We used descriptive statistics to summarise participant demographics. Depending on the results of Kolmogorov–Smirnov normality testing, continuous data are reported as mean (SD) or median (IQR). We applied independent t-tests for between-group comparisons of post-counselling scores and paired t-tests for within-group changes. The threshold for statistical significance was p < 0.05.

The PAUSE© Flipchart demonstrated a high level of understandability among clients, with a mean score of 95.92 (SD 7.15). Scores ranged from a minimum of 75.0 to a maximum of 100.0, indicating that the majority of clients found the flipchart highly comprehensible. The narrow interquartile range further suggests consistent ratings across the sample.

Discussion

This cluster randomised controlled trial evaluated the effectiveness of the PAUSE© flipchart in delivering premarital HIV pre-test counselling compared to the non-structured counselling in improving HIV knowledge among premarital clients in public primary healthcare clinics in Johor Baru District. Participants receiving the intervention achieved significantly higher cognitive and transmission-domain scores, supporting the use of structured, standardised tools to enhance the quality and consistency in counselling. Although sessions using the flipchart required longer duration (15–20 min versus 5–10 min), this likely reflects more comprehensive engagement and improved clarity. Consistent with prior evidence, the use of visual aids and structured messaging enhances patient-provider communication and health literacy [20].

The lack of statistically significant difference in transmission-specific knowledge between groups may be attributable to a ceiling effect, given the high baseline awareness among participants. This finding aligns with studies from Selangor reporting moderate to high HIV knowledge levels [21], as well as systematic reviews indicating that educational interventions often yield limited incremental gains in specific domains or behaviours when baseline understanding is already high [22]. Nevertheless, the high PEMAT-P understandability scores observed in this study (median 100, IQR 6.0), consistent with earlier findings [13], indicate that the PAUSE© flipchart is highly comprehensible and appropriate for use in primary care settings.

This study adds to the existing literature by providing trial-based evidence from a real-world primary care setting that a structured, visual counselling tool can improve HIV knowledge beyond conventional counsellor-dependent approaches, while maintaining high levels of client understandability. Importantly, it demonstrates the feasibility of integrating such tools into routine premarital screening services within the public healthcare system.

Several limitations should be considered. Baseline imbalances in gender and educational status between groups may have influenced findings, as bivariable analysis could not adjust for these differences. The higher educational attainment in the control group would be expected to favour knowledge acquisition, potentially underestimating the true intervention effect.

Future studies with larger sample sizes should enable multivariable analyses to address confounding. While stratified randomisation may improve balance, its feasibility may be constrained in the present context, due to the relatively homogenous population undergoing mandatory premarital screening among Muslim couples. In addition, the study was conducted in a single geographic area, which may limit generalisability over other Malaysian settings with different sociodemographic, cultural and health literacy profiles. Knowledge was assessed immediately post-counselling, and longer-term retention was not evaluated due to resource and logistical constraints. Clients who came for premarital counselling are generally younger and not regular attendees for long-term follow-up at the public primary health centres. Furthermore, although self-administered questionnaires were used, the involvement of counsellors in their distribution may have introduced subtle bias; independent outcome assessment could strengthen future studies.

The findings have important public health implications for primary care and HIV prevention in Malaysia. The PAUSE© flipchart provides a practical approach to standardising premarital HIV counselling, reducing variability in provider-dependent practices and ensuring consistent delivery of accurate information. By improving client understanding, the intervention contributes to strengthening health literacy, a key determinant of informed decision-making and engagement with preventive services. In the context of ongoing workforce constraints in public primary healthcare, structured tools such as PAUSE© can facilitate task-sharing with allied health personnel while maintaining quality of care. Importantly, the scalability and low-cost nature of the flipchart align with national priorities outlined in Malaysia’s Health White Paper [23], which emphasises strengthening primary care and preventive services, as well as the National Strategic Plan for Ending AIDS [24], which highlights the need for effective HIV education and testing strategies. Integrating structured, evidence-based counselling tools into routine practice may therefore support more consistent and equitable delivery of HIV-related services, contributing to broader efforts to improve population health outcomes and reduce HIV transmission at the national level. Future research should also evaluate the feasibility, adoption, and sustainability of this intervention at scale using implementation frameworks such as RE-AIM or CFIR.

Conclusion

The study found that the PAUSE© flipchart can be used for pre-marital counselling, as it is acceptable, and easily understood from its high PEMAT-P scores. The study also proved that the PAUSE© flipchart is more effective than the conventional method in improving knowledge of HIV for pre-marital counselling.
