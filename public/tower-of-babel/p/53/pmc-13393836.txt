Dynamic patterns of anemia and cognitive function: a longitudinal cohort study based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study
Authors: Junjie Cui, Qiucheng Zhang, Xianjun Lv, Liubing Zhao, Wei Xu, Liang Wang, Shuai Pei, Wei Zhang, Mengting Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnemia is associated with cognitive decline, but the influence of its temporal patterns on cognitive trajectories remains unknown. This study investigated the longitudinal association between dynamic anemia patterns (persistent, intermittent, never) and cognitive function in middle-aged and older Chinese adults.MethodsWe analyzed data from 6,364 participants aged ≥ 45 years in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (2011–2020). Based on 2011 and 2015 hemoglobin measurements, participants were categorized as never, intermittent, or persistent anemia. Linear mixed-effects models assessed associations with cognitive function. Sensitivity analyses included multiple imputation, varying anemia thresholds, and inverse probability weighting.ResultsBased on hemoglobin levels in 2011 and 2015, 4,769 (74.9%) participants had never anemia, 1,201 (18.9%) had intermittent anemia, and 394 (6.2%) had persistent anemia. In linear mixed-effects models adjusted for age and sex, both intermittent anemia (β = -0.31, 95% CI: -0.56 to -0.06, P = 0.016) and persistent anemia (β = -0.47, 95% CI: -0.88 to -0.06, P = 0.025) were associated with lower baseline cognitive scores compared to the never anemia group. However, only persistent anemia was associated with a faster rate of cognitive decline over time (time × persistent anemia: β = -0.057, 95% CI: -0.100 to -0.014, P = 0.009), whereas the interaction for intermittent anemia was not significant (time × intermittent anemia: β = 2.18e-5, 95% CI: -0.025 to 0.025, P = 0.999). Sensitivity analyses, including multiple imputation and inverse probability weighting, and analyses using different diagnostic thresholds produced directionally consistent results, with more lenient thresholds attenuating the effects; the WHO threshold demonstrated the strongest discriminatory power. Transition pattern analysis revealed that, compared to the persistently normal group, the persistent anemia and incident anemia groups maintained lower cognitive levels throughout follow-up, whereas anemia remission was associated with significant, though incomplete, cognitive improvement.ConclusionsPersistent anemia is associated with the most rapid cognitive decline, while intermittent anemia is associated with lower baseline cognition. Improvement in anemia status may confer cognitive benefits, highlighting the importance of dynamic anemia monitoring and timely intervention in cognitive health management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27247-x.Keywords: Anemia Patterns, Cognitive Function, Longitudinal Study, CHARLS, Linear Mixed-Effects Models

Introduction

Cognitive decline and dementia present major public health challenges for aging societies globally [1, 2]. As China’s population ages at an accelerating rate, the rising prevalence of cognitive impairment imposes a substantial burden on families and society [3]. Identifying modifiable risk factors for cognitive decline is therefore paramount for promoting healthy aging and reducing this burden.

Anemia, a common hematologic disorder in older adults, affects approximately 17% of this population, with incidence increasing with age [4]. Evidence consistently links anemia to cognitive decline, mild cognitive impairment, and a higher risk of dementia [5–8]. Proposed biological mechanisms include cerebral hypoxia [9], oxidative stress damage [10], impaired neurotransmitter synthesis [11] and shared pathophysiological processes such as chronic inflammation [12].

Recent research, including a study by Xiong using China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) data, indicates a bidirectional relationship between anemia and cognitive function [13]. Most existing studies, however, rely on single-time-point assessments of anemia status. This approach cannot capture the complex relationship between the dynamic progression of anemia and cognitive trajectories, thereby neglecting a crucial temporal dimension [5, 7, 13]. The simplified dichotomization of anemia status may also obscure the differential cognitive effects of distinct anemia patterns.

To address this gap, we introduce the concept of dynamic anemia patterns. Persistent anemia likely exerts a significant and sustained impact on cognitive function, potentially through chronic cerebral hypoxia and other mechanisms. Intermittent anemia, in contrast, is a more transient state often resulting from fluctuations in nutritional status [14], intermittent chronic inflammation [15], or adherence to treatment regimens [16].

Although less recognized, the potential cognitive impact of intermittent anemia merits investigation as a possible marker of physiological instability.

Leveraging the longitudinal CHARLS database, this study systematically investigates the association between dynamic anemia patterns and cognitive function from a temporal perspective. We focus primarily on persistent anemia, hypothesized to have the most significant long-term cognitive effect, while also exploring the impact of intermittent anemia for comparison. These findings should clarify how different anemia patterns influence cognitive health, providing a foundation for future research and interventions that account for anemia’s dynamic nature.

Methods

Study design and data source

This population-based longitudinal cohort study used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative survey designed to systematically collect comprehensive health, socioeconomic, and other information from Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. We analyzed publicly available data from five waves: 2011 (baseline), 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. CHARLS employs a multistage stratified probability-proportional-to-size sampling strategy, encompassing 150 counties and districts across 28 provinces to ensure strong national representativeness. The study protocol received approval from the Ethical Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11014), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Study population

Participant selection is illustrated in Fig. 1. Of the 17,708 participants in the CHARLS 2011 baseline survey 648 were excluded because of missing age data or age below 45 years, leaving 17,060 eligible participants. We further excluded 10,004 participants with missing or abnormal hemoglobin values in either 2011 or 2015, including 9,816 with missing hemoglobin data and 188 with values outside the plausible physiological range of 6–20 g/dL, resulting in 7,056 participants. We then excluded 692 participants with missing cognitive assessment in 2011 or 2015, including 504 missing in 2011, 264 missing in 2015, and 76 missing at both waves. After applying these criteria, 6,364 participants were included in the primary analysis (Fig. 1). These participants were followed across all five waves, providing longitudinal data to examine the association between dynamic anemia patterns and cognitive function.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for the study sample

Variable definitions and measurements

Definition of anemia status and grouping

Hemoglobin concentration was measured via standardized venous blood tests performed in laboratories designated by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Anemia was defined using World Health Organization criteria as a hemoglobin level below 13 g/dL for men and below 12 g/dL for women. According to their hemoglobin measurements from 2011 to 2015, participants were classified into three groups: (1) Never anemia, for those not meeting the anemia criteria at either time point; (2) Intermittent anemia, for those meeting the criteria at only one of the two time points; and (3) Persistent anemia, for those meeting the criteria at both time points.

Cognitive function assessment

Cognitive function was assessed using the CHARLS cognitive module, an instrument adapted from the U.S. Health and Retirement Study cognitive tests and validated for reliability in the Chinese population [17]. This module evaluates two domains: episodic memory, measured by immediate and delayed recall of a 10-word list (score range 0–10), and executive function/mental status, assessed via the serial-7 subtraction test (0–5 points), date orientation (0–4 points), day-of-the-week orientation (0–1 point), and a figure drawing test (0–1 point), yielding a combined score of 0–11. The global cognitive function score, ranging from 0 to 21, was calculated as the sum of these two domain scores, where higher values correspond to better cognitive performance.

Covariates

Baseline age and sex were included as covariates in the primary longitudinal model. In addition, baseline characteristics used for descriptive comparisons across anemia-pattern groups included education level, marital status, rural residence, smoking status, drinking status, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, stroke, heart disease, and depressive symptom score.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1). Two-sided tests were used, with the significance level set at α = 0.05.

Description of baseline characteristics

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Group comparisons of baseline characteristics were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables.

Primary statistical analysis

Linear mixed-effects models were used to examine the longitudinal association between dynamic anemia patterns and cognitive function. The dependent variable was the global cognitive score. Time was coded as years since baseline (2011), corresponding to 0, 2, 4, 7, and 9 years for the 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020 survey waves, respectively. The primary model was specified as follows: cog ~ pattern × time + age2011 + sex + (1 | id). Never anemia was the reference group in this model. The interaction term between anemia pattern and time was used to assess whether the rate of cognitive change differed across anemia-pattern groups. A random intercept for each participant was included to account for within-subject correlation across repeated cognitive measurements. Model estimates are reported as regression coefficients (β), standard errors, P values, and 95% confidence intervals. P values were obtained using the Satterthwaite approximation implemented in the lmerTest package, and confidence intervals were calculated using the Wald method.

Sensitivity analyses

Three sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary findings and to evaluate the potential influence of missing data, the definition of anemia, and attrition. First, missing covariate data were handled using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE), a method recommended for incomplete covariates under the missing-at-random assumption that accommodates different variable types; five imputed datasets were generated and pooled using Rubin’s rules to account for between-imputation uncertainty [18].

Second, we examined the sensitivity of our results to the operational definition of anemia by repeating the primary models with alternative hemoglobin cutoffs, using three alternative uniform hemoglobin thresholds of 12, 13, and 14 g/dL for both sexes. For each threshold, anemia status in 2011 and 2015 was redefined, participants were reclassified and the same linear mixed-effects model structure was refitted. Third, to mitigate potential selection bias from loss to follow-up, we applied inverse probability weighting (IPW) by using logistic regression to estimate each participant’s probability of remaining under observation at each follow-up wave, conditional on baseline characteristics and health-related covariates; these stabilized weights were then incorporated into the linear mixed-effects models, an approach aligned with marginal structural modeling for addressing informative censoring [19, 20].

Subgroup and stratified analyses

Stratified analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modification by sex and age group. Specifically, we examined the associations of anemia patterns with cognitive function among female participants with intermittent anemia, male participants with persistent anemia, participants aged 60–69 years with persistent anemia, and participants aged ≥70 years with persistent anemia. For each stratum, we summarized the baseline difference and slope difference relative to the reference group. Detailed stratified results are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Exploratory analysis

Participants were further categorized into four transition patterns based on their anemia status at baseline (2011) and first follow-up (2015): persistent normal, incident anemia, anemia remission, and persistent anemia. We compared cognitive trajectories across these patterns using linear mixed-effects models. This exploratory analysis investigated the potential influence of anemia status changes on cognitive function to inform clinical intervention.

Public health impact assessment and intervention simulation

To evaluate the potential public health impact of anemia-associated cognitive impairment, we calculated the population attributable risk fraction (PARF) and conducted scenario-based simulations to assess the relative effect of several hypothetical intervention strategies. The PARF was calculated using the standard formula: ​PARF = [∑ Pi (RRi − 1)] / [1 + ∑ Pi (RRi − 1)], where Pi denotes the population proportion in each exposure group and RRi​ represents the corresponding relative risk of cognitive impairment. Three intervention strategies were considered: early screening, targeted treatment, and comprehensive intervention. For each scenario, we specified screening coverage, treatment coverage, follow-up coverage, treatment efficacy, follow-up efficacy, assumed relative risks for intermittent and persistent anemia, and relative cost parameters. The cost-effectiveness ratio (CER) was calculated as the total intervention cost divided by the relative reduction in PARF compared with the status quo. All simulation parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Results

Characteristics of the study cohort

The initial study population comprised 17,708 participants from the CHARLS 2011 baseline survey. After applying the selection criteria, the final analytic cohort included 6,364 individuals aged 45 years or older. By design, all included participants had available hemoglobin and cognitive assessments in both 2011 and 2015. The wave-specific counts for available cognitive assessments were 5,872 in 2013, 5,068 in 2018, and 5,293 in 2020. Relative to the final cohort (N= 6,364), missing cognitive outcome data therefore occurred for 492 participants (7.7%) in 2013, 1,296 (20.4%) in 2018, and 1,071 (16.8%) in 2020.

Baseline characteristics and distribution of anemia patterns

Of the 6,364 participants, 4,769 (74.9%) were classified as never having anemia, 1,201 (18.9%) as having intermittent anemia, and 394 (6.2%) as having persistent anemia. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Participants with intermittent or persistent anemia were older than those who never had anemia (mean age: 59.73 ± 8.93 and 61.13 ± 9.92 vs. 58.19 ± 8.20 years; P < 0.001). Hemoglobin levels demonstrated a clear gradient across these patterns (14.83 ± 1.54, 13.13 ± 1.68, and 11.24 ± 1.11 g/dL, respectively; P < 0.001), alongside progressively lower baseline global cognitive scores (11.13 ± 4.17, 10.39 ± 4.30, and 9.96 ± 4.41; P < 0.001). The proportion of male participants decreased across the groups (48.8%, 42.3%, and 35.0%; P < 0.001). Behavioral factors also varied, with a lower prevalence of both smoking (41.6%, 34.7%, 26.9%; P < 0.001) and drinking (35.4%, 28.7%, 24.4%; P < 0.001) from the never to the persistent anemia category. The prevalence of comorbidities including hypertension (28.5%, 25.1%, 18.3%; P < 0.001), dyslipidemia (12.1%, 9.1%, 5.4%; P < 0.001), and heart disease (13.5%, 10.4%, 8.9%; P = 0.001) differed significantly across patterns, whereas diabetes (P = 0.961), stroke (P = 0.792), and marital status (P = 0.082) did not. Depressive symptom scores were higher in the intermittent anemia group (2.08 ± 1.10) than in the never anemia group(1.96 ± 1.06) and the persistent anemia group (2.03 ± 1.08) (P = 0.003).

Linear mixed-effects models applied to longitudinal data systematically revealed differential associations between dynamic anemia patterns and cognitive function. These associations manifested not only as differences in baseline cognitive levels but also, more critically, as heterogeneous rates of cognitive change over time. This analysis provides key evidence from a dynamic perspective for understanding how anemia affects cognitive health (Fig. 2).

Cognitive function trajectories by anemia pattern (2011–2020)

Intermittent anemia: an independent and significant risk factor for cognitive impairment

Compared with the never anemia group, the intermittent anemia group showed a lower baseline global cognitive score, but no evidence of a faster rate of cognitive decline over time. Their baseline global cognitive score was significantly lower (β = -0.31, 95% CI: -0.56 to -0.06, P = 0.016). However, the interaction between intermittent anemia and time was not statistically significant (β = 2.18e-5, 95% CI: -0.025 to 0.025, P = 0.999).

Persistent anemia: Lower Baseline Cognition and Accelerated Decline

Compared with the never anemia group, persistent anemia was associated with both lower baseline global cognitive scores and a faster rate of cognitive decline over time. The baseline global cognitive score was significantly lower in the persistent anemia group than in the never anemia group (β = -0.47, 95% CI: -0.88 to -0.06, P = 0.025). The interaction between persistent anemia and time was also statistically significant (β = -0.057, 95% CI: -0.100 to -0.014, P = 0.009), indicating a faster annual decline in cognition relative to the never anemia group. Persistent anemia may exert a sustained adverse effect on cognition through several mechanisms. Long-term anemia may reduce cerebral oxygen delivery and impair neuronal energy metabolism. Although compensatory responses such as increased cardiac output and elevated erythrocyte 2,3-diphosphoglycerate may partially offset this effect in the early stage [21, 22], such adaptations may not be sufficient to prevent progressive cognitive deterioration under sustained anemic conditions [16]. Association effects between anemia patterns and cognitive function

Domain-specific effects and stratified effects

Cognitive domain subgroup analysis implied heterogeneity in the relationship between anemia patterns and cognitive functioning. For episodic memory, both intermittent anemia (β = -0.075, 95% CI: -0.133 to -0.018, P = 0.010) and persistent anemia (β = -0.135, 95% CI: -0.230 to -0.041, P = 0.005) were associated with lower scores. For executive function, intermittent anemia was associated with lower scores (β = -0.165, 95% CI: -0.320 to -0.010, a = 0.037), whereas the association for persistent anemia did not reach statistical significance (β = -0.094, 95% CI: -0.348 to 0.159, P = 0.465). This discrepancy may reflect differential vulnerability of cognitive domains to hypoxic stress, as brain regions involved in memory, particularly the hippocampus, may be especially sensitive to reduced oxygen supply [23]. Stratified analyses also suggested heterogeneity by sex and age group. Women with intermittent anemia showed a baseline cognitive deficit without evidence of accelerated decline, whereas men with persistent anemia showed a faster rate of cognitive decline. Among participants aged 60–69 years, persistent anemia was associated mainly with a baseline cognitive deficit, whereas among those aged ≥70 years, persistent anemia was associated with both a baseline cognitive deficit and a faster rate of decline. Detailed stratified results are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Sensitivity analyses

Multiple imputation and inverse probability weighting analyses

To evaluate the potential impact of missing covariate data and attrition bias, we conducted multiple imputation using chained equations (MICE) and fitted the primary model with inverse probability weights (IPW). Following these adjustments, the estimates for both intermittent and persistent anemia remained nearly identical in direction and magnitude, and all significant associations were retained (detailed results are available upon request). These results suggest that our primary findings are not substantially influenced by missing data or loss to follow-up.

We assessed the sensitivity of our findings to the anemia definition by repeating the primary analysis with three uniform hemoglobin thresholds (12, 13, and 14 g/dL for both sexes) in place of the sex‑specific WHO criteria; these results are shown in Table 3; Fig. 4.

Sensitivity analysis results using different hemoglobin thresholds for defining anemia

Effect estimates under different anemia diagnostic thresholds

Using the strictest threshold (12 g/dL), the association between intermittent anemia and baseline cognition was stronger (β=–0.51, 95% CI:–0.80 to–0.22, P < 0.001) than in the primary analysis, whereas persistent anemia showed a marginally non‑significant baseline deficit (β=–0.44, 95% CI:–0.92 to 0.04, P = 0.076). The interaction term for persistent anemia × time approached significance (β=–0.05, 95% CI:–0.10 to 0.003, P = 0.066), while the intermittent anemia × time interaction remained non‑significant.

At the 13 g/dL threshold—which approximates the WHO cutoff for men but is higher for women—the baseline effect of intermittent anemia was attenuated and only borderline significant (β=–0.24, 95% CI:–0.47 to 0.002, P = 0.052). Persistent anemia remained significantly associated with lower baseline cognition (β=–0.38, 95% CI:–0.68 to–0.08, P = 0.012), but neither interaction term reached significance.

Under the most lenient threshold (14 g/dL), the baseline effect of intermittent anemia disappeared completely (β=–0.14, 95% CI:–0.40 to 0.11, P = 0.282), while persistent anemia continued to show a significant baseline deficit (β=–0.35, 95% CI:–0.62 to–0.08, P = 0.012). As before, the time‑interaction terms were not significant.

Summary of sensitivity analyses

Overall, sensitivity analyses confirm that the associations between dynamic anemia patterns and cognitive function remain robust under various approaches to handling missing data. The threshold analysis shows a clear gradient: as the hemoglobin cutoff is relaxed, the estimated effect of intermittent anemia on baseline cognition attenuates and ultimately vanishes, whereas the effect of persistent anemia on baseline cognition persists even at higher thresholds. Notably, the accelerated cognitive decline linked to persistent anemia (the time interaction) was detectable only under the strictest threshold (12 g/dL) and in the primary analysis using WHO sex‑specific criteria, indicating that the WHO definition most effectively captures the cumulative risk posed by persistent anemia on cognitive decline. These results emphasize the necessity of employing a clinically meaningful, sex‑specific definition to identify individuals at genuine risk of cognitive impairment.

Exploratory analysis: transitions in anemia status and cognitive trajectories

Objective and methods of analysis

To investigate the potential impact of anemia status changes on cognitive function from a dynamic perspective, we conducted this exploratory analysis. Using hemoglobin measurements from the 2011 baseline and the 2015 follow-up, we categorized participants into four mutually exclusive transition patterns: persistently normal, incident anemia, anemia remission, and persistent anemia. By comparing the cognitive trajectories of these four groups from 2011 to 2020, we aimed to address two clinical questions: whether anemia remission is associated with improved cognitive trajectory, and how the cognitive risk of incident anemia compares to that of persistent anemia.

Distribution of anemia transition patterns

As shown in Fig. 5, among the 6,364 participants included in this analysis, the majority (74.9%) maintained normal hemoglobin levels throughout the observation period, forming the persistently normal group. A further 12.9% transitioned from normal to anemic status, constituting the incident anemia group, while 5.9% experienced remission of baseline anemia and were classified as the anemia remission group. The persistent anemia group represented the smallest proportion at 6.2%, a distribution consistent with the primary analysis. This stratification provides a clear sample size basis for the present exploratory analysis.

Distribution of anemia transition patterns

Comparison of cognitive trajectories across transition patterns

As shown in Fig. 6, cognitive trajectories differed substantially across the anemia transition patterns. The persistently normal group maintained a relatively stable trajectory without significant cognitive decline during follow-up. In contrast, the incident anemia group, which progressed from normal hemoglobin levels to anemia, had consistently lower adjusted cognitive scores although its rate of decline did not differ significantly. This indicates that the onset of anemia results in substantial cognitive impairment, similar to the long-term impact of persistent anemia. The anemia remission group, however, showed an intermediate trajectory, with a rate of decline that did not significantly differ, suggesting that improved anemia status may confer a protective effect on cognitive function, even if it does not completely halt the decline. These findings underscore the importance of dynamic anemia patterns, revealing that only persistent anemia was associated with a statistically significant acceleration whereas anemia remission may attenuate some of these adverse effects.

Cognitive function trajectories by anemia transition pattern

Conclusion of the exploratory analysis

This exploratory analysis yields preliminary but highly suggestive evidence for an association between dynamic changes in anemia status and cognitive trajectories:

The anemia remission group showed a comparatively more favorable trajectory than the persistent anemia group and remained closer to the persistently normal group over follow-up, suggesting that correcting anemia may help slow or partially reverse associated cognitive decline.

The incident anemia group showed less favorable cognitive scores than the persistently normal group throughout follow-up, highlighting the potential cognitive risk associated with new-onset anemia.

These findings reinforce the conclusion that anemia management should account for its dynamic progression rather than relying solely on a static diagnosis.

Discussion

Utilizing the nationally representative CHARLS longitudinal cohort, this study provides the first systematic investigation into the association between dynamic anemia patterns and cognitive function trajectories among middle-aged and older adults in China. It advances beyond the traditional limitation of treating anemia as a static dichotomous variable by introducing the concept of “dynamic anemia patterns.” The primary findings indicate that persistent anemia is an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment, an effect that remains robust under the standard WHO diagnostic threshold. Although also significant, intermittent anemia plays a less prominent role in cognitive decline, particularly when an individual’s anemia status improves.

Interpretation of main findings and comparison with previous studies

The most pivotal finding of this study is the significant association of persistent anemia with both lower baseline cognitive function and an accelerated rate of cognitive decline. This result indicates that the impact of anemia on cognitive health extends beyond severe, long-term states, as sustained anemia itself constitutes an important cognitive risk. These effects likely stem from chronic cerebral hypoxia, where persistent anemia causes prolonged oxygen deprivation, impairing neuronal energy metabolism and promoting neurodegeneration [24]. Our study therefore identifies persistent anemia as a key risk factor for cognitive impairment, particularly evident in the elderly population aged ≥ 70 years.

In contrast, intermittent anemia exerted a less dramatic effect on cognitive function, with its impact more closely tied to anemia fluctuations and underlying pathophysiological factors such as chronic inflammation and nutritional deficiencies [25–27]. These findings align with previous studies (e.g., Xiong et al. [13]) but extend it by demonstrating that the fluctuating nature of anemia exacerbates cognitive decline, rather than the mere presence of anemia. Our results consequently underscore the need to account for dynamic anemia patterns in both future research and clinical practice.

Mechanistic insights

This study confirms that dynamic anemia patterns, particularly persistent anemia, are associated with cognitive decline, with chronic cerebral hypoxia serving as the core pathophysiological basis. By reducing the blood’s oxygen-carrying capacity, anemia subjects the brain to prolonged hypoxia. This state directly impairs neuronal energy metabolism and can trigger a cascade of subsequent neurodegenerative processes [8], ultimately manifesting as cognitive impairment. Beyond hypoxia, iron metabolism itself plays an indispensable role in normal brain function, as iron deficiency has been shown to damage the striatal dopaminergic-opioid system, leading to neurological injury and irreversible cognitive and behavioral deficits [16]. Furthermore, hemoglobin is present in non-erythroid brain cells, including neurons and glial cells [28], where it serves physiological functions in maintaining mitochondrial activity [10, 29] and mediating the clearance of toxic proteins [30]. Consequently, anemia may impair cognition not only by limiting oxygen delivery but also by directly disrupting these critical intracerebral processes.

Our exploratory analysis offers evidence for the reversibility of anemia status. The anemia remission group exhibited a more favorable cognitive trajectory, suggesting that timely identification and correction of anemia may help protect against cognitive decline. This finding holds significant clinical implications, as it identifies a potentially modifiable window for intervention.

Clinical and public health implications

The findings of this study have clear implications for clinical practice and public health policy:

The clinical monitoring paradigm should shift from a static to a dynamic model, moving beyond reliance on single-point tests to establish systems for tracking hemoglobin levels over time in middle-aged and older adults, especially the very elderly.

Patients with “intermittent anemia” constitute a high-risk group for cognitive decline and should be explicitly identified and prioritized for management, while elderly anemia patients aged ≥ 70 years represent a core target for cognitive function monitoring.

Since individuals with incident anemia already exhibit cognitive risk comparable to those with persistent anemia, early screening and intervention are crucial; the correlation between anemia remission and improved cognitive trajectory further justifies active treatment to preserve cognitive health.

Dynamic anemia monitoring should be integrated into national basic public health service programs for middle-aged and older adult health management, and public health education must be enhanced to raise awareness of the cognitive risks associated with anemia volatility.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strengths of this study are its prospective design within a large, nationally representative longitudinal cohort and its innovative definition of dynamic anemia patterns. The robustness and clinical relevance of the findings are further supported by systematic sensitivity analyses and comprehensive exploratory assessments. Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, measuring hemoglobin at only two time points may not adequately capture the complex fluctuations in anemia status. Second, the absence of data on the specific etiology of anemia, such as iron deficiency or chronic disease, prevented an examination of potential differential effects by cause. Third, despite adjustment for multiple important confounders, residual or unmeasured confounding remains possible. Finally, although the cognitive function assessment is widely validated, it may be less sensitive to subtle changes than specialized neuropsychological tests.

Future research directions

Based on the findings and limitations of this study, future research should prioritize more frequent hemoglobin measurements to better characterize anemia fluctuation patterns. Subsequent work could also employ neuroimaging to visualize how these dynamic anemia patterns affect brain structure and function. Furthermore, the co-management of anemia with chronic conditions warrants investigation. Patients with chronic kidney disease, heart failure, or autoimmune diseases, for example, face a substantially elevated risk of both anemia and cognitive impairment [31–36]. Future studies must therefore differentiate the heterogeneous effects of dynamic anemia on cognition across specific disease contexts, such as chronic kidney disease and heart failure, to inform more precise, individualized interventions.

Conclusion

The findings of this study align with extensive observational evidence that anemia is a critical risk factor for cognitive impairment and dementia [37]. Our research extends this understanding by demonstrating that the temporal pattern of anemia is central to assessing risk. Specifically, persistent anemia presents a greater risk for cognitive decline in China’s middle-aged and older population than does intermittent anemia. This association between persistent anemia and cognitive decline remains robust when applying the standard WHO diagnostic threshold for anemia. Notably, we also observed that improvement in anemia status, particularly remission, may confer a protective effect on cognitive function. These results refine the relationship between anemia and cognitive health by identifying sustained anemia as a principal threat. They thereby offer a novel scientific rationale for early prevention and precise intervention strategies, underscoring the management of persistent anemia as a key component of healthy aging.
