Enhanced convolutional neural networks by using adaptive conditional dropout with dynamic regularization

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep convolutional networks have high capacity and thus tend to overfit, especially when trained on restricted or imbalanced data, which compromises their applicability to safety applications. To address this, we propose Conditional Dropout with Dynamic Regularization (CDDR), a simple phase aware regularization method that requires no architectural changes. In CDDR, dropout is completely disabled during the first four epochs to encourage dropout free feature learning. From the fifth epoch onward, the dropout rate is adaptively determined depending on the derivative of the first order of the training loss, and then its value is fixed for the remainder of training. Meanwhile, the L2 weight decay strength is also updated according to training dynamics. Additionally, during training the CDDR method uses the conditional dropout technique, and optionally at the time of inference, Monte Carlo Dropout is applied in order to approximate predictive uncertainty. When applied to VGG-16 and ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Tiny ImageNet, CDDR surpass DropBlock, standard Dropout, and SpatialDropout, by achieving up to + 3.3% on CIFAR-10, + 9.27% on CIFAR-100, and + 3.94% on Tiny ImageNet while reducing the train test gap by 58.8% on CIFAR-100. These results show the effectiveness of CDDR in enhancing current CNN structures, especially in applications where data scarcity and class imbalance occur.Keywords: Conditional dropout, Overfitting, Image classification, Bayesian learningSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Recent advances in object detection and image classification were made possible by deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs). They performed well in medical diagnostics by learning multilevel structured feature representations. However, as networks become deeper and more complex, this is increasing the likelihood of overfitting. At that point, the model doesn’t learn general patterns; but begin to memorize the same training samples rather than learning generalizable patterns from unseen data. Conventional regularization methods, such as L2 weight decay and early stopping, give some adaptability to changes in the learning dynamics of deep networks. Dropout, which randomly imposes zero values on neurons during training, has been shown to effectively reduce coadaptation among neurons in dense layers, but it is less effective in convolutional layers, since nearby features needs a strong statistical correlation1,2. Techniques, such as SpatialDropout and DropBlock, can enhance unstructured dropout via the dropping of whole channels or adjacent regions of the feature map. However, they are still based on static masking methods. Advanced methods such as learnable and guided dropout schedules can have high computational complexity or require more tuning3. In order to solve these issues, we propose CDDR as a simple and efficient training phase framework. Not only does it update the dropout rate, but also sets the L2 weight dynamically during training. This is done through control over real time training without any changes in structures of the network. In the first four epochs, the CDDR completely deactivates dropout to prevent disruption to the learning of fundamental characteristics. Beginning with the epoch five onward, the model gradually and adaptively determines the dropout rate based on changes in recent loss, while simultaneously adjusting the weight decay strength to achieve a better balance between learning and regularization. A Bayesian interpretation is adopted, and uncertainty is optionally estimated at inference time via Monte Carlo Dropout, making the model robust to high-risk operations. CDDR outperforms non-dynamic dropout strategies in generalization performance when it is applied to standard CNN pipelines with VGG-16 and ResNet-18 architectures. The results show that relying on dynamic information in the regularization process help achieve a better balance between the early learning flexibility and better performance later. This makes CDDR suitable and effective when dealing with limited data or if there is an imbalance between classes. Our adaptive dropout with dynamic regularization has two distinctive stages: the first stage refers to ‘no‐dropout’ period to stabilize the feature learning (epochs 1–4), while the second stage is about the adaptive dropout phase (epoch 5 and beyond), where the dropout rate is regulated through derivatives of loss and then fixed for the remainder of training. Related work

When the model is larger than what the training data can support in deep neural network, overfitting occurs. The model then begins to retain noise and random details instead of learning general knowledge for unseen data. To solve this issue, many regularization techniques have been introduced for use in feedforward and convolutional networks. One of the simplest is L1/L2 weight penalties, which reduce weight values and smooth the process of training through the addition of an extra limit to the loss function based on the weight values3–5. Weight decay is often used as an equivalent to L2 optimization, weights are gradually reduced during the optimization process. On the other hand, early stopping is used to terminate model training when validation performance starts to decline, preventing the model from continuing towards overfitted solutions6,7. There are many advanced techniques that help make training more stable and improve accuracy, such as entropy regularization in Bayesian networks8. Dropout9 is the one of the most powerful stochastic regularizes. In dropout, different neurons are blocked at each update, which breaks co-adaptations and promotes redundancy of learned features. Later, other studies demonstrated that the dropout can be shown as a practical approximation of the Bayesian average, which helps improve the estimation of uncertainty in model prediction10. Building on this idea, techniques like Gaussian and adaptive dropout emerged, which aim to adapt the noise level to different network layers or to the learning processes themselves11,12. However, in convolutional layers, where adjacent values are correlated, unstructured dropout may not be enough and sometimes allows the information to pass through without real interruption. In order to address this problem, the Spatial Dropout feature was proposed, where the entire feature map is disabled at once instead of removing individual units. This increases the efficiency of breaking down channel dependencies, thereby improving the generalizability of the model13. DropBlock removes the adjacent patches from the feature maps, yielding an improvement in the efficiency of deep CNNs14. The problem with these two techniques is that they use fixed masking probabilities that do not change during training, which may not be suitable for all training phases or schedules. Therefore, the recent studies have focused on or on learning that can be combined with improved training processes and model behavior. While these methods are more general, they may require additional architectural modules, increasing optimization costs, and they often rely on task designed schedules that can limit their generalizability.

The proposed CDDR method builds on these recent developments by introducing a two-phase mechanism. This is achieved by integrating the policy of loss dependent dropout and applying it across different stages. This allows the model to adapt to the training process with greater flexibility and effectiveness. During the early stage of feature formation, the primary function is to completely suppress dropout. This is done by using the sigmoid function of recent loss derivatives, in which the dropout probability is determined adaptively in later epochs, and is strongly related to the L2 weight decay, all within a Bayesian interpretation of dropout. Unlike previous models, CDDR does not depend on expensive super learning techniques or the addition of new components to the body of the network. Especially in the cases where the data become limited or classes are not balanced. This simplicity helps to improve accuracy and reduce overfitting.

Data augmentation and CutMix process.

The process of preprocessing relies on a series of updates to improve the diversity of features while also preserving the content of the image. Input images x are first subjected to basic spatial augmentation operations such as random cropping (32 × 32 regions from 36 × 36 images) and horizontal flipping with a 50% probability to obtain improved samples . These operations help the model learn to reduce sensitivity to translation and reflection, while retaining essential visual properties. The samples then enter a CutMix phase, where pairs of augmented images are randomly combined by replacing rectangular regions between them. This process blends both image content and corresponding labels proportionally to the area of exchanged regions, creating hybrid samples that retain the designation. Most importantly, CutMix is applied only to the preaugmented images, which ensures that all generated samples retain natural feature structures and good spatial coherence. This two-phase design creates geometrically aligned but highly diverse training samples. This makes it easier for the model to learn to recognize objects from partial visual information. This in turn combines features across different visual concepts and gain resistance to blocking, while maintaining accurate predictions even in partial information. We obtain more diverse samples compared to using traditional sequential processing, by the separating of spatial augmentation from semantic mixing. This prevents the addition of too large noise or unwanted artifacts that may negatively affect feature learning19.

Theoretical concept of conditional dropout

Dropout is a method that zeros out a portion of the input units during the forward and backward propagation process to prevent overfitting. The probability value represents the chance of dropping a neuron (setting it to zero)20. In the proposed model, the maximum dropout probability is set to 0.5, and this value represents the level of the regularization strength of dropout used. Increasing or decreasing this value would respectively decrease or increase the strength of dropout effect in the model. Implementation details and integration into CNN pipelines

To make sure the results can reproduced, we present the method we used in the experiments. CDDR is used as a simple training control method, where the dropout rate is updated only once per epoch based on how the training loss changes. In this way, CDDR can operate independently of the model’s architecture, optimizer, and backpropagation method used. In order to ensure the stability of training with dynamic regularization, the CDDR method includes two clear mechanisms. First, we compute the loss difference:

To keep dropout stable, the loss change is first limited in this range [− 0.2, 0.2] before being passed to the sigmoid function. Then, dropout is disabled at the beginning of training. This allows momentum optimizers to stabilize the weights and fine tuning the training behavior before the adaptive regularization is activated to prevent any abnormal behavior during training. The dropout rate is normalized so it can be compared across all cases. It is always kept between 0 and 1, which prevents dropout from becoming too small or turning off during training. This helps keep training stable and avoids the sigmoid output becoming too large or too small.

The model is defined using standard PyTorch dropout layers, while the CDDR controller adjusts the attribute values during training. This controller is created with the model itself and dynamically adjusts the model’s global dropout probability, without affecting the computation or gradient calculation mechanism. This allows CDDR to be directly integrated into CNN training path without requiring any changes or modifications to the optimizer loop.

The CDDR does not add a significant computational overhead during training, as it is simply modifying the dropout probability in existing layers, without adding new layers, extra parameters, or any sampling procedures. Therefore, the forward and backward passes remain at the same level of complexity as in standard CNN training with dropout. Optionally, a Bayesian method can be used only in the inference phase via the Monte Carlo Dropout technique. In this case, multiple random forward passes (T=30) are used for uncertainty estimation.

Experimentation

We applied our experiments to three different datasets Tiny ImageNet, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, to test how well the proposed CDDR method performs and compare it against standard regularization methods such as Standard Dropout, SpatialDropout, and the recently proposed DropBlock. We also ran ablation studies on CIFAR-100 to see how each part affects the proposed method. The performance of CDDR on different network architectures is compared in the experiments with (VGG16, ResNet18, ResNet50, and EfficientNet-B0), to confirm that it works well and generalizes across different model designs. We performed all experiments in Python 3 using PyTorch 1.13.1 and performed on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. For CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, random cropping with 4-pixel padding and random horizontal flipping was used as an augmentation technique. For Tiny ImageNet, images were resized to 64 × 64, and augmentation included random resized cropping and color jittering. Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) was applied to train the models, using a momentum 0.9 and rate learning rate 0.1. The 1 × 10⁻4 was the value that set for weight decay, where 128 was the value of the batch size. We trained all models for 300 epochs on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, and Tiny ImageNet. The adaptive dropout scaling hyperparameter (α) was set to 0.7. The proposed method was compared using classification accuracy on the test sets, and in addition we evaluated overfitting control using training–testing accuracy gap36.

Model architectures and setup

CDDR was applied to the VGG16, ResNet-18, ResNet-50, and EfficientNet-B0 models. The models’ weights are trainable, but dropout is disabled during early stages of learning. In the CDDR method, we start to apply conditional dropout from the fifth epoch, where the dropout rate is modified dynamically based on training progress, while continuing to use L2 regularization. Both layer stability and nonlinearity were preserved by adopting batch normalization and ReLU activation throughout the layers38–40. For all datasets, input images were normalized and the same data augmentations (random cropping, flipping, color jittering) were used. CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 classification

CIFAR-100 and CIFAR-10 datasets have 60.000 RGB images of size 32x32. 50.000 images are used for training, and10.000 for testing. The primary difference between these datasets is that CIFAR-100 contains 100 different classes, which makes learning on CIFAR-100 is more challenging while CIFAR-10 contains only ten different classes. On CIFAR-10, the CDDR with ResNet-18 acquires a test accuracy of 95.45%, slightly better than Standard Dropout (91.48%), SpatialDropout (91.98%), and DropBlock (92.15%). On CIFAR-100, CDDR achieved 79.08%, which outperforms Standard Dropout (69.81%), DropBlock (70.03%), and SpatialDropout (69.71%).

Tiny ImageNet classification

There are 200 classes in Tiny ImageNet, each consisting of 64 × 64 RGB images, with each class containing 500 training and 50 validation images. This dataset is relatively challenging because of the high number of classes and the small images sizes, which makes learning more difficult.

The CDDR model showed consistent improvements in Top-1 accuracy compared to the other models on Tiny ImageNet. Models based on traditional dropout were faster to reach convergence, but they plateaued early, while CDDR continued improving due to its phased regularization and adaptive dropout.

Results

The CDDR method achieved the best classification accuracy by reducing overfitting. It shows the smallest gap between training and test accuracy. In terms of accuracy, CDDR achieved + 3.3% improvement on the CIFAR-10 dataset when compared to DropBlock, + 3.36% improvement on Tiny ImageNet, indicating its robustness on effectiveness even with more complex datasets. We also measured the overfitting control by comparing the difference between training and testing accuracy at convergence. The results show that CDDR can decrease the gap between training and testing accuracy by up to 58.8%, when compared to conventional dropout. This shows an improvement in the model’s capability to generalize.

Specifically at certain rates, the results suggest that applying a conditional method to dropout regularization can be really useful to improve performance of the model without reducing its learning ability44,45.

The design of CDDR determines its efficiency. Data loss probability is updated based on only the loss value and does not affect or alter the model’s backward or forward or computations. The overall training time stays consistent across different architectures of CDDR, because we only modify the dropout probability in the existing layers without adding any new forward or backward runs. These results demonstrate that CDDR does not add a remarkable overhead to training time because it updates the scalar dropout parameters only once per epoch, which does not require any additional forward or backward calculations.

In general, CDDR adds an increase in training time, that ranges from 1 to 5.5% per epoch. This gain is more remarkable in deep models like ResNet-50 and, it is the result of dropout updates and additional control. This has less impact in smaller models such as EfficientNet-B0. As CDDR scales the scalar dropout probability of existing layers once per epoch, it does not modify the computational graph, parameter count, or FLOP complexity of the underlying network. These results show that CDDR performs better with low and predictable computational cost, making it appropriate for large scale learning.

Conclusion

Conventional dropout methods are not always effective in training, and do little to explain how a model learns. As a result, the deep neural networks generally suffer to generalize when dealing with new data. We present CDDR to solve this problem. CDDR is composed of the combination of conditional dropout, which is dynamically determined, and Bayesian prediction. By means of this method, model learning is improved and the training process is kept more stable. We apply our method to Tiny ImageNet, CIFAR-100 and CIFAR-10 by employing different network models. The results that we obtained from our experiments indicate that the proposed method, CDDR, performs remarkably better when compared with well-known methods such as standard dropout, spatial dropout, DropBlock, DropConnect, and A DropConnect.

It achieved an improvement in accuracy of 3.3% on CIFAR-10 and 9.27% on CIFAR-100, along with an approximately 58.8% decrease in overfitting when we compared it with the baseline methods. These results show the efficacy of CDDR in enhancing the classification performance while keeping high flexibility across different network architectures. The proposed method also shows a promising direction for developing regularization methods in deep learning, which serving both theoretical and practical needs in complex image classification tasks. However, CDDR introduces additional other hyperparameters for dynamic scaling and Bayesian integration, which require fine tuning to achieve the best generalization across different datasets and architectures. Future study may focus on automating selection of the hyperparameter, and generalizing CDDR to transformer architectures for wide usage.
