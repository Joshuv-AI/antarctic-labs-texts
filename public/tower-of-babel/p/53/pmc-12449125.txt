Supporting older-adult behavioral health: building the first state Center of Excellence for Behavioral Health and Aging

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe behavioral health (BH) needs of older adults are unique, increasing, and, too often, poorly understood.MethodsOregon established the first state-level center of excellence in the United States focused on the BH of older adults via a state–university–community partnership. Oregon's Center of Excellence for Behavioral Health and Aging (OCEBHA) was conceptualized by the state health authority and initially funded using a block grant from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration.ResultsOCEBHA seeks to expand the capacity of health and social programs and providers to deliver BH services for older adults with serious mental illness and substance use disorders through translational research, workforce development, and policy innovation.ConclusionThis review article describes the United States’ and Oregon's BH and aging landscape, highlighting the disconnects between research evidence, clinical treatment/intervention, and policy implementation. It outlines the rationale for establishing centers like OCEBHA, which was designed to bridge these gaps. By detailing OCEBHA's structure and focus areas—translational research, workforce development, and policy innovation—the article shows how this model can help align evidence-based practices with service delivery and policy. It also offers a roadmap for other states seeking to strengthen support for older adults with BH needs.

Behavioral health and aging landscape

For far too long, policymakers, health and human service professionals, and researchers have overlooked the behavioral health (BH) needs of older adults.1 Behavioral health describes the promotion of mental well-being, and the prevention and treatment of mental health and substance use disorders (SUDs). The BH needs of older adults are unique, increasing, and too often poorly understood by society, policymakers, clinicians, care partners, and older adults themselves.2,3 In response to this need, Oregon has established the Oregon Center of Excellence for Behavioral Health and Aging (OCEBHA; ocebha.org), a state–university–community agency partnership in Oregon. A center of excellence in health care is an entity that provides leadership, best practices, research, support, and training for a specific focus area. The goal of a center of excellence is to drive innovation, improve performance, and ensure consistent quality and standards for optimal outcomes. Initial funding of OCEBHA used pass-through funds from Oregon's block grant from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA). This funding source required OCEBHA to focus specifically on serious mental illness (SMI) and SUDs among the older-adult population. The primary goal of OCEBHA is to expand the capacity of health and social programs and providers to deliver resources and services to older adults with BH needs, particularly those with SMI and SUDs in Oregon. OCEBHA is the first state-level center with this ambitious goal.

The combination of population aging and major shortages in the BH workforce in the United States4,5 has created a pressing need to better address the multiple, complex BH needs of older adults. This article aims to (1) describe the current BH and aging research, treatment/intervention, and policy landscape in Oregon and the United States; (2) describe the gaps between BH research, treatment/intervention, and policy; (3) provide the rationale for establishing OCEBHA; (4) explain how centers like OCEBHA can bridge the gaps between BH research, treatment, and policy; and (5) offer a roadmap to other states interested in targeting services for older adults with BH issues.

Background

Older adults experience a high prevalence of SUDs, SMI, and other BH conditions such as anxiety and depression.6,7 The SAMHSA defines SMI as the presence of a mental, behavioral, or emotional disorder that substantially interferes with or limits 1 or more major life activities.8 Serious mental illnesses include conditions such as bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, and schizophrenia. Of older adults, 1.9% are estimated to have SMI,8 and it is estimated that up to 28% of older adults worldwide experience depression.9

While the estimated prevalence of SUDs (including alcohol use disorder and drug use disorder) among older adults is approximately 9%,8 some estimates suggest that as many as 67% of older adults misuse alcohol and this likely depends on thresholds set by researchers.6 Depression prevalence rates are increasing among older adults,10 and the impact of SMI and SUDs on older adults is often severe as older adults are less likely to be diagnosed and often have more complex needs due to comorbidities.11,12 Death by suicide as a percentage of all-cause mortality is higher among older adults than other age cohorts.13 There has also been a marked increase in SUDs among members of the Baby Boomer generation.14,15 Some have referred to increased substance use among older adults as the “hidden” or “invisible” epidemic.16,17 Better recognition of BH diagnoses, as differentiated from signs of normal aging, as well as a willingness among older adults to discuss BH needs, may partly account for these increases in diagnoses.

The increase in BH conditions among older adults is particularly concerning given that SUDs and SMI both lead to early and accelerated disability and cognitive decline.18–23 Individuals with SMI have a 10- to 25-year shorter life expectancy compared with the general population.24 Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of death among the general population; however, the mortality rate for CVD is twice as high among people with SMI.25,26 Further exacerbating poor physical health outcomes among people with SMI is the concept of “diagnostic overshadowing”—that is, the misattribution of physical symptoms to mental illness.27

Negative attitudes and stereotypes about aging and older adults in the United States exacerbate the stigma associated with SMI and SUDs.28 The effects of stigma include reduced engagement with health and social systems and worse behavioral and physical health outcomes.29 A 2015 study found that 40% of rural older adults cited stigma (what would others think) as a barrier to seeking mental health services.30 Further exacerbating care and health outcomes, BH providers may exhibit stigma and bias toward older adults.28 Structural barriers also contribute to people eschewing BH or aging services professions, as they are not viewed as prestigious or as highly compensated as other health care professions.31–33

The lack of culturally relevant services and outreach strategies contribute to disparities in access to and quality of care for racially and ethnically diverse older adults.34 Black and Hispanic populations are 3–4 times more likely to have a diagnosis of schizophrenia compared with their White counterparts.35 Black and White Americans have similar rates of alcohol use disorder and cannabis use disorder between 30 and 60 years; however, higher rates are observed for Black or African Americans compared with their White counterparts in advanced age.36 More research is needed about the BH of older immigrant populations and other groups. Examples include research on the BH needs of Black or African Americans who experience challenges in accessing and completion of SUD services37,38 and Asian Americans whose BH conditions are underreported and under-identified.39,40

Subpopulations of older adults, including those with low incomes, who live in rural communities, and residents of long-term care settings have higher rates of mental illness.41–43 The SMI rates among assisted-living (AL) and nursing facilities residents are high and have grown substantially in recent decades by 54% and 77%, respectively.44 This is likely due to the functional cognition deficits among those living with SMI and SUDs combined with increased age-related declines due to medical comorbidity.45–47

Comprehensive care for older adults with BH conditions is impeded by the workforce shortage of generalist BH clinicians48 and severe shortage of clinicians trained to work with older adults.1,49 Over 122 million Americans reside in areas classified as Mental Health Provider Shortage Areas (HPSAs).48 An HPSA is a geographic area designated by the US Health Resources and Services Administration as having a shortage of mental health care providers. The lack of older adult trained mental health providers is even more stark. By 2030, it is projected there will be fewer than 25% of the needed BH providers who specialize in geriatrics.1 This lack of trained professionals, in turn, leads to a lack of awareness, screening, and proper assessment of the unique BH needs of older adults.2 Less than half of older adults report that their health care provider asks about their mental health.50 Years lived with disability due to neurocognitive disorders, SMI, and SUDs are significant contributors to the skyrocketing cost of health care for older adults.51 These trends emphasize the need for a workforce trained in the specific and unique needs of older adults, along with implementation of multidisciplinary team care, with comprehensive assessment, clinical management, intensive outreach, and coordination of mental, physical and social health services.

National initiatives in BH and aging

The multiple intersections between mental health, brain health, and physical health suggest a need to respond with coordinated policies and programs to support older adults.52–54 Lifestyle medicine, positive psychology, and psychiatry can offer guidance to provide new approaches to ensuring brain health, psychological, emotional, and cognitive resilience, and emphasizing strengths of aging acquired as one grows older, like successful emotion regulation, coping, and wisdom.55 However, systems-level approaches are needed to address the increased needs of this population. Age-friendly health systems

Beginning in 2025, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) requires inpatient hospitals participating in Medicare's Hospital Inpatient Quality Reporting program to implement the Age-Friendly Hospital Measure including the “4Ms” framework, which stands for “What Matters, Medication, Mentation, and Mobility.”56 The 4Ms, developed by the John A. Hartford Foundation and Institute for Healthcare Improvement, provide a framework for health systems to better care for and treat older adults.57

Geriatrics Workforce Enhancement Program

The Health Resources and Services Administration’s Geriatrics Workforce Enhancement Program (GWEP) develops, implements, and evaluates training and educational programming for the health care workforce with a focus on tribal, underserved, and rural communities. Since 2019, the GWEP has offered the opportunity for primary care delivery sites to create Age-Friendly Health Systems (AFHS) that highlight the 4Ms of age-friendly care to older adult patients and their loved ones.57,58 The GWEPs across the United States partner with primary care clinics, residential care facilities, and long-term care communities to develop skills, protocols, and outcomes tracking to become recognized AFHS care sites. The GWEP’s “Mentation” education focuses on BH, SUDs, and dementia. Geriatrics Workforce Enhancement Program awardees have incorporated AFHS training into numerous rural and underserved settings.59 A key goal of the 2024–2029 GWEP cohort is to improve health outcomes for older adults by integrating geriatrics with primary care and other appropriate specialties using the AFHS framework.60 Oregon received a GWEP award in 2024.

E4 Center of Excellence for Behavioral Health Disparities in Aging at Rush University

The E4 Center of Excellence for Behavioral Health Disparities in Aging at Rush University (e4center.org) was funded by SAMHSA to “develop and disseminate training and technical assistance for healthcare practitioners on issues related to addressing BH disparities” for older adults.61 The mission of the E4 Center is to engage, empower, and educate health care providers and community-based organizations in BH equity for older adults and their families. The E4 Center achieves this mission through the provision of education, implementation resources, and technical assistance regarding mental health, substance use, and their intersection with physical health. The E4 Center also developed an adaptation of the 4Ms of an AFHS for BH clinicians, in response to the need for an implementable framework with limited training.62,63

Center of Excellence for Behavioral Health in Nursing Facilities

To address the BH needs of older adults residing in long-term services and supports (LTSS) settings, the Center of Excellence for Behavioral Health in Nursing Facilities (COE-NF; nursinghomebehavioralhealth.org) was established to provide technical assistance and workforce development to facilities that support individuals with co-occurring disorders, SUDs, and SMI.64 This Center is funded through a SAMHSA grant and is a prime example of federal support to address the BH needs of older adults. The mission of the COE-NF is specific to nursing facilities and has a national, rather than a local state, focus.

Multisector Plans on Aging

Seven states recently adopted Multisector Plans on Aging (MPAs),65 which provide a blueprint for multiple sectors to work with state and local governments to develop public–private partnerships to strategically address the needs of an increasingly aged society.66 Federal legislation introduced in 2024 would provide federal support to states for implementing MPAs; however, at the time of writing this article, this legislation has not advanced.67 Multisector Plans on Aging can positively impact the BH needs of older adults, but there is a need to ensure that BH is explicitly included within MPAs.

Rationale for OCEBHA: gaps in research, treatment, and policy

Oregon: the behavioral health and aging landscape

Oregon's older-adult population has high BH needs. Oregon has the seventh highest rate in the United States of excessive drinking among older adults.68 Oregon also has the fourth highest rate of death by suicide among adults 65 years of age and older in the United States (27.3 vs 17.7 per 100 000)69 (see Table 2). These age-related BH needs are compounded by disparities across race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and geography. Older veterans living in rural regions of Oregon are at particularly high risk of death by suicide.70 The highest suicide rates occur among males aged 85 years and older and 21% of LGBTQIA+ older adults have experienced suicidal ideation in the past year.71,72

Oregon's long-term care settings also have a high prevalence of older residents living with a diagnosis of SMI (12%), depression (46%), and anxiety (26%).73 Meanwhile, nearly 50% of all Oregon nursing facility stays involve a BH diagnosis.74 The use of psychotropic medications by AL residents in the prior week is higher than in the general population: 20% of AL residents received an antipsychotic medication and 36% of residents received an antidepressant. This is likely related to Oregon's historical shift away from nursing homes to home- and community-based services (HCBS) including AL.75

The State of Oregon has a long and rich history of innovation within care, services, and supports, especially with Medicaid-funded care for older adults. In the 1980s, the Oregon Health Plan began a bold experiment to expand Medicaid coverage to all.76,77 By 2023, Oregon had achieved an insured rate of 97% of all Oregonians.78 Oregon was also the first state to innovate with Medicaid-delivered LTSS to provide care through Medicaid outside of nursing facilities.79 Over 40 years later, Oregon continues to be a leader nationally in the use of HCBS, with approximately 83% of all LTSS Medicaid spending on HCBS.80 To address the treatment gaps, the state recently expanded access to Medicaid-funded SUD services through a 1115 Demonstration Waiver that enhanced residential treatment services as well as funded prevention, outreach, and recovery supports.81 The supports offered under this Demonstration Waiver were not, however, specifically targeted to the gaps in treatment and support for older adults. The lack of specific inclusion of older adults is also the case with other BH initiatives such as Oregon's opioid settlement fund.82

Building on successes and key partnerships

Many of Oregon's policy successes have been driven by advocates and policy entrepreneurs who pressed to put older-adult BH on the state policy agenda.83 In 2015, a workgroup composed of representatives of aging services and mental health agencies, health care providers, quality-improvement groups, and advocacy organizations identified gaps and recommended changes to the Oregon legislature. The direct outcome of this policy entrepreneurship was the establishment of the Older Adult Behavioral Health Initiative (OABHI).75,84

As a state-level program, the OABHI (oregonbhi.org) has sought to meet the needs of older adults and people living with physical disabilities of all ages by improving timely access to care from qualified providers who collaborate to provide coordinated, quality, and culturally responsive BH and wellness services. The OABHI is composed of 24 Older Adult Behavioral Health Specialists (OABHSs) who work in Community Mental Health Programs (CMHPs) and other nonprofit agencies across Oregon. The number of OABHSs is based on the county's population of adults aged 65 years and older, and each county has access to at least 1 OABHS. Some of the 36 Oregon counties share specialists.

The OABHSs achieve their goals through 3 primary job functions: (1) workforce development and health and wellness promotion, (2) complex care consultations (CCCs) for BH needs, and (3) coordination with community partners who work in aging services, BH services, or primary care. The CCCs promote a multidisciplinary team approach to ensure that older adults and people living with disabilities receive appropriate and timely care.84 An evaluation of OABHI reported that thousands of trainings have been provided through the OABHI since 2015,84,85 with 367 trainings from 2021–2023 alone, and the OABHS conducting over 2400 CCCs between 2021 and 2023.86

Establishing an Oregon Center of Excellence for Behavioral Health and Aging

While there is significant interest in BH in Oregon87 and nationwide88,89 among policymakers, advocates, and providers, there is no comprehensive plan to assess and address the BH needs of older adults in Oregon or nationally. This is particularly troublesome as Oregon recently has ranked near the bottom of all US states for mental health (47th) due to a high prevalence of mental health illness, along with a lack of access to BH services across the state.90 Access to necessary mental health treatment facilities has specifically been documented as lacking in Oregon.91 The lack of access to necessary treatment options is compounded by significant shortages in the BH workforce.92 These major gaps must be filled through a multifaceted approach that includes training, translational research, and policy innovation if greater access to adequate BH services and supports is to be achieved for older adults. OCEBHA represents the next step in the state–university–community agency partnership to bridge gaps for the population needing BH support.96 OCEBHA builds on prior work in Oregon and links with national efforts to promote the BH services and care for the aging population.60,97,98

OCEBHA was established in 2024 through a competitive contract from the Oregon Health Authority using SAMHSA block grant funds. This is a unique funding approach and OCEBHA is the only state-level Center of Excellence focused on BH and aging in the United States. OCEBHA consults with and draws inspiration from multiple sources, including the Engage, Educate, and Empower for Equity: E4 Center of Excellence for Behavioral Health Disparities in Aging at Rush University.99

OCEBHA aims to improve the well-being of Oregon's older adults by expanding the capacity of programs and providers to deliver resources and services to older adults with BH needs, particularly those with SMI and SUDs. While OCEBHA's initial focus is on Oregon, its long-term goals include engaging in national efforts to promote workforce training, applied research, and policy analysis.

OCEBHA uses a collaborative approach to support evidence-based BH policy and practice and improve the quality of BH services for older adults, addressing inequities, improving patient and care partner experiences, and enhancing the capacity of community organizations. We built on Portland State University's tradition of community-engaged research and reviewed models of state, university, and community partnerships to guide our approach. For example, the Academy Health's State–University Partnership Learning Network provides examples of successful partnerships between state agency staff, community partners, and researchers who collaborate on policy-relevant research and develop best practices.96,100 OCEBHA is guided by a local steering committee of key community partners, including individuals with lived experience, and a national advisory board of experts in workforce, aging, mental health, substance use, and public policy. The OCEBHA team developed an internal equity framework specific to aging and BH, which is used to guide all work of the Center.

Bridging gaps

During the initial year of operation, OCEBHA achieved several accomplishments related to the Center's focus on workforce development and engagement of key partners within the BH and aging community.

Workforce development and training

The workforce development and training work of OCEBHA focuses on creating opportunities for professionals who work with older adults to learn, network, and advance care in Oregon. In 2024, OCEBHA coordinated and facilitated its first annual in-person BH and aging conference, which brought nearly 200 attendees from across the state for 2 days of presentations, discussions, collaboration, and resource-sharing. The conference also served as the first meeting for OCEBHA's annual Leadership Academy, a cohort-based program of 20 professionals representing careers such as clinical social work, palliative care nursing, and project management who come together for expert-facilitated discussions to develop into leaders in their organizations and create invaluable connections across sectors.

Community asset map

In 2024, OCEBHA developed a map of community assets for older adults. The map provides a geographic representation of assets in Oregon, including in health care, social services, emergency resources, community-based resources, and advocacy. The geographic representation has helped to identify resource deserts (areas with few or limited resources for older adults). The availability of the searchable map on the OCEBHA website allows older adults, caregivers, and other community partners to locate supports in their area. Each resource includes a pop-up window that identifies the resource's services, address, contact information, and hours of service. The accompanying web page also includes a list of specific types of providers on the map with detailed descriptions and links to directories maintained by those providers themselves.

Older-adult BH workforce readiness assessment tool

Effective BH services for older adults combines support for mental well-being and integration with primary health care and addresses social determinants of health. In support of OCEBHA's goal of expanding the capacity of local agencies, OCEBHA developed its Workforce Readiness Assessment Tool to provide a framework that can be used by Federally Qualified Health Clinics, BH providers, and primary care providers (PCPs) to improve and maintain the ability of their workforce to offer high-quality BH services for older adults. The tool has 2 purposes: first, to document the provider's readiness to provide quality BH services for older adults, and second, to help providers develop and implement a plan for ensuring their workforce is prepared to meet the BH needs of older adults as this need continues to grow. Concepts in this tool are based on evidence-based sources on workflow design, including the American Medical Association's guidance on Behavioral Health Integration,101 the 4M's Behavioral Health Checklist from Rush University,62 Trauma-Informed Care,102 Zero Suicide in Health Systems,103,104 and implementation science.105,106 The tool's concepts are grouped into 5 key domains: (1) leadership, (2) training, (3) infrastructure, (4) care and treatment, and (5) quality improvement. Each domain includes indicators of workforce readiness, which agencies can address together or separately depending on their capacity and needs. Monitoring these indicators over time will help maintain quality BH services for older adults. Health systems and agencies that might want to utilize this tool include Area Agencies on Aging (AAA), CMHPs, PCPs, and others that offer services and supports for older adults with BH needs.

Other key partnerships

The Oregon GWEP sought out partnership with OCEBHA to combine workforce education resources and work collaboratively to successfully increase older-adult–focused training opportunities across Oregon. In addition to the partnerships that OCEBHA has with the OABHI and Oregon GWEP, several other partnerships are in development. These include partnerships with Oregon's network of AAA, collaboration with other universities, and work with organizational members of OCEBHA's steering committee. The development of these partnerships is vital to carrying out OCEBHA's mission, vision, and plans to support the BH needs of older adults in Oregon.

A roadmap for other states

To build on the success of the initial year of operation, OCEBHA will develop its work related to policy innovation and systems change as well as translational research. These efforts will be both Oregon-focused as well as offer opportunities for state-to-state partnerships and national collaborations. OCEBHA can serve as a model for other states as they develop their work to support older-adult BH.

Policy innovation and systems change

OCEBHA is developing multiple resources for policy innovation and systems change in BH and aging. Some of the ways the Center will achieve this will be through tracking policies and engaging policymakers on issues related to BH and aging. The Center will host policy discussions and forums on timely BH and aging topics and develop and disseminate policy recommendations for state and national audiences. OCEBHA will raise awareness about BH needs of older adults through its work, including advocating for AFHS and for ensuring the BH needs of older adults are adequately captured within MPAs. Community engagement initiatives through the Center can amplify the voices of older-adult populations and other stakeholders to inform actionable policy solutions.

Enhancing the workforce pipeline

The need to develop the BH and aging care workforce will continue to be paramount given demographic projections. Expanding the pipeline will include partnerships with university programs that train clinicians, such as schools of social work or nursing to ensure the specific and unique BH needs, particularly SMI and substance use among older adults, are included in their curriculums. Exposure to aging content often comes too late, if ever, in the educational experiences of students, and integration of knowledge on the BH needs of older adults is crucial for optimal training of the next generation of aging-focused health care professionals. OCEBHA will also further develop and expand its Leadership Academy, which seeks to establish a cadre of leaders committed to working in BH and aging.

Translational research

OCEBHA seeks to become a nationally recognized center for community-engaged BH and aging research. To accomplish this aim, OCEBHA will develop and support a peer and lived-experience research advisory group, conduct and publish OCEBHA research, disseminate newly published research, find and apply for funding mechanisms for research, and form and support a research collaborative focused on BH and aging to implement rapid translational research. A future research activity of OCEBHA will be to evaluate the impact of the novel workforce readiness assessment on clinic readiness to deliver BH care and outcomes among older adults.

Future research will need to address the underlying mechanisms of treatment response variability among older adults. A social-determinants-of-health lens, widely recognized in public health, can inform intervention development. Because optimal care of older adults with mental health disorders is both patient-focused and family-centered, research into enhancing the well-being of family care partners is needed.

Conclusion

A clear need exists to focus greater attention on the BH needs of older adults in the United States and globally. States can continue to take the lead in providing the leadership needed to address the specific BH needs of older adults. OCEBHA provides 1 example of a way to achieve this goal through sustained focus on workforce development, advocacy, and research on BH services for older adults, serving as a model for the nation. Other states could take a similar approach to leveraging innovative funding sources and partnerships to establish state-level Centers of Excellence for BH and Aging. Further, as a leader in the BH and aging space, OCEBHA can advocate for policies to better meet the needs of an aging state and nation. This advocacy will be based on scientific evidence, best practices, and lessons learned through the work of the Center.
