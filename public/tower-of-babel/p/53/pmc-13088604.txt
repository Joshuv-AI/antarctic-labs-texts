Latent profiles and network analysis of adolescent depression and cognitive impairment
Authors: Xintong Ma, Wei Li, Yuanyuan Liu, Xue Kang, Qi Xin, Junru Li, Kaiyue Wang, Yuke Zhang, Yanlong Li, Qingyuan Yu, Fei Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescence is a critical period for rapid emotional and cognitive development. Depression and cognitive impairment frequently co-occur in this population, yet their comorbidity patterns and symptom-level interactions remain insufficiently explored.MethodsA total of 2,244 students (mean age = 16.8 ± 0.84 years; 1,218 males, 1,026 females) from a high school in Heilongjiang Province, China, were recruited. Depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment were assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) and the Perceived Deficits Questionnaire–Depression (PDQ-D). Latent profile analysis (LPA) was applied to identify subgroups, followed by network analysis to examine central symptoms (expected influence, EI), bridge symptoms (bridge expected influence, BEI), and network differences (NCT).ResultsThe optimal LPA model identified three comorbidity subgroups: low, moderate, and high. NCT revealed significant differences in network structure and global strength between the low–moderate (S = 1.514, P < 0.001) and moderate–high groups (S = 1.324, P < 0.001), whereas no differences were found between the low–high groups. Somatic symptoms were the most central nodes across networks. Prospective memory impairment and interpersonal problems were stable bridges, while inhibition/organization deficits uniquely bridged domains in the moderate subgroup.ConclusionsAdolescent Depression and Cognitive Impairment can be classified into low, moderate, and high comorbidity subgroups. Somatic symptoms emerged as the central symptom, while prospective memory impairment and interpersonal problems were identified as key bridge symptoms, suggesting potential intervention targets for early screening and stratified treatment.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-07946-w.Keywords: Adolescent, Depression, Cognitive impairment, Latent profile analysis, Network analysis, Comorbidity

Introduction

Adolescence (10–24 years) is a critical stage of life characterized by rapid emotional, cognitive, and social development [1]. In recent years, there has been a growing global focus on mental health issues in this age group. Large-scale surveys in China report that the point prevalence of depressive disorders in children and adolescents is around 2.0%, and increases to approximately 6–7% in those aged 15–16 years [2]. Globally, the annual prevalence of major depressive disorder (MDD) in adolescents is estimated to be about 8%, with lifetime prevalence reaching nearly 20% by late adolescence [3]. Depression accounts for approximately 13% of the disease burden among adolescents, ranking among the top five causes of disease burden in this population [4, 5]. Meanwhile, cognitive difficulties including problems with attention, memory, and executive functioning are increasingly recognized as common challenges during adolescence [2, 3]. A national survey in the U.S. showed that nearly 40% of high school students reported having obvious problems such as inattention, memory decline, or difficulty in making decisions [6].Depression and cognitive impairment are both urgent public health concerns for youth.

Importantly, depression and cognitive impairment frequently co-occur in adolescence and may exacerbate each other, forming a vicious cycle of dysfunction [7]. Previous meta-analytic evidence has also confirmed that depressive states can impair executive functions, working memory, and attentional control, leading to poorer academic and social functioning among adolescence [8]. Cognitive deficits may continue to exist even after depressive symptoms have remitted, indicating that such impairments could be relatively stable risk factors for recurrence [9].During adolescence, concentration difficulties and decision-making problems are themselves diagnostic symptoms of depression, and persistent cognitive inefficiencies may heighten psychological stress, thereby worsening depressive symptoms [10]. During adolescence, this interactive relationship highlights the necessity of conducting relevant research, namely, delving into the internal structure of the comorbidity of depression and cognitive impairment.

Existing research has demonstrated robust correlations between the core dimensions of depressive symptoms, including low mood, anhedonia, anxiety, and somatic complaints, and impairments spanning multiple cognitive domains, namely attention, memory, executive function, and language [11]. Recently, Jones et al. endeavored to model symptom relationships within and across mental disorders, proposing that the co-occurrence of depression and cognitive impairment can be conceptualized as a “symptom network” in which certain bridge symptoms link depressive and cognitive domains and transmit psychopathological influence between them [12]. Building on this network perspective, recent studies have begun mapping specific pathways that connect depressive symptoms with cognitive dysfunction. Hoorelbeke et al. (2022) applied network analysis in remitted depressed individuals and found that residual depressive symptoms cluster with self-reported executive function deficits. Notably, within this network working memory and shifting difficulties emerged as key bridging nodes linking the depression and executive functioning clusters [13]. Likewise, in an adolescent cohort, Roberts et al. (2023) identified attention dysregulation as a highly central factor linking cognitive impairments with anxiety and low mood. Notably, after controlling for attentional control problems, the previously observed associations between affective symptoms and broad cognitive deficits largely disappeared - suggesting that attention dysregulation may drive the co-occurrence of cognitive and emotional problems in youths [14]. Zhao et al. (2025) investigated the gender-specific patterns of the depression-cognitive symptom network in depression patients. Their findings highlighted the importance of targeted interventions on gender-specific bridge symptoms for this group [15].Although the above studies have demonstrated the comorbidity of depression and cognitive deficits in the child and adolescent population, there are still few fine - grained investigations into the symptom levels of depression and cognitive deficits among community adolescents.

However, as emphasized by Fried and Nesse (2015), even under the same diagnosis, symptoms may vary substantially among individuals [16]. Even when the overall levels of cognitive deficits or depression are similar, individuals can present with completely different symptom patterns and outcomes. For example, many depressed adolescents experience cognitive difficulties (such as memory and attention problems), and their performance on cognitive tasks is often worse than that of their non-depressed peers [3]. Nevertheless, it is conceivable that not all adolescents experience both depression and cognitive impairment simultaneously, and symptom presentations may differ across subgroups. Identifying subgroups with shared symptom characteristics may help provide more targeted treatment for these populations.

Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) is a person-centered method used to identify potential subgroups with similar response patterns across multiple indicators (e.g., diagnostic criteria) [17]. LPA has been widely applied to adolescent populations to characterize symptom heterogeneity and comorbidity patterns. Herman et al. (2007) used LPA to empirically derive subtypes of depressed adolescents, identified different classes, and emphasized the heterogeneity of adolescent depression [18]. Paskewitz et al. (2024) applied LPA to identify multiple latent profiles of executive function in adolescents, including a subgroup with specific inhibitory deficits, underscoring the heterogeneity of cognitive functioning [19]. Given the significant comorbidity of depression and cognitive deficits in adolescents, it is worth investigating whether there is a heterogeneous comorbid pattern between the two. Therefore, applying LPA to the measurement of depressive symptoms and cognitive functioning can help identify potential subgroups of adolescents, uncover diverse comorbidity patterns between depression and cognition, and provide valuable references for more targeted prevention and treatment.

The present study aims to apply LPA and network analysis to explore the heterogeneity and underlying mechanisms of co-occurring depression and cognitive impairment among adolescents. Specifically, the study seeks to: (1) identify latent subgroups characterized by distinct profiles of depressive and cognitive symptoms; and (2) use network analysis to pinpoint core and bridge symptoms that may serve as intervention targets, ultimately providing a theoretical foundation for more precise and effective clinical strategies.

Methods

Participants

This study used convenience sampling to conduct a psychological survey among high-school students in Heilongjiang Province, northern China in December 2024. A total of 2,244 paper-based questionnaires were validly collected, with participants aged between 15 and 19 years. The sample included 1,218 males and 1,026 females. Recruitment was carried out through posters placed within the school to invite voluntary participation. The research team organized in-person questionnaire completion sessions in designated classrooms, where participants provided written informed consent and completed the questionnaires anonymously and independently under the supervision of teachers and researchers. No personal identifiers (e.g., names or student IDs) were collected, which helped reduce concerns about evaluation and minimized potential social desirability bias.

Measurement tools

Demographic characteristics

Demographic characteristics including gender, age, grade, residence, parental education and occupation, household economic status, and family structure were collected using a self-report questionnaire. These variables were analyzed using descriptive statistics and group comparisons across latent profiles.

Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D)

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the CES-D scale, originally developed by the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health in 1977 [20]. The scale consists of 20 items across four dimensions: depressive affect (8 items), positive affect (4 items), somatic symptoms (6 items), and interpersonal problems (2 items). Responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (less than 1 day per week) to 4 (5–7 days). The total score ranges from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. A total score ≤ 35 suggests no depressive symptoms, 36–39 indicates possible depressive symptoms, and ≥ 40 is classified as clinically significant depression. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.88.

Perceived Deficits Questionnaire for Depression (PDQ-D)

Cognitive difficulties were evaluated using the Perceived Deficits Questionnaire for Depression (PDQ-D). The scale comprises 20 items across four domains: attention, retrospective memory, prospective memory, and planning/organization. The PDQ-D has demonstrated cross-cultural validity [21] and good reliability in Chinese populations [22]. Participants rated each item based on their experiences in the past seven days using a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 4 (very often). Each subscale contains five items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 80. A score of ≥ 21 is indicative of cognitive dysfunction. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.96.

Statistical analyses

Latent Profile Analysis (LPA)

LPA was performed using Mplus 8.3 to identify latent subgroups based on the CES-D and PDQ-D subscale scores. Model selection was based on comparison across one- to five-class solutions using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted BIC (aBIC), where lower values indicated better model fit [23]. Additional indices included the Lo-Mendell-Rubin Test (LMRT) and Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT), with P-values < 0.05 suggesting significant improvement in fit [24]. Entropy values ≥ 0.80 were considered to indicate good class separation [25]. In addition to model fit indices, class size and interpretability were also considered in determining the optimal solution. Previous methodological studies suggest that when the proportion of the smallest category is relatively low, the model may be unstable; therefore, categories comprising less than 10% of the total sample should be interpreted with caution [26, 27].

Network estimation

According to the results of LPA, the participants were divided into different comorbid subgroups. Separate symptom networks were constructed for each group using R version 4.2.1. Symptom dimensions were modeled as nodes, with regularized partial correlations forming the edges. Network estimation was conducted using a graphical Gaussian model with EBIC and LASSO regularization via the “qgraph” and “bootnet” packages [28, 29]. Edge color and thickness reflected the valence and strength of associations, respectively.

Centrality and stability

Expected Influence (EI) was computed to assess the centrality of each node, identifying symptoms with greater connectivity and influence [28]. Bridge Expected Influence (BEI) was calculated using the “networktools” package to detect bridging symptoms linking depression and cognition domains [12]. Node predictability was estimated using the “mgm” package, capturing the variance in each node explained by adjacent nodes [30]. Stability and accuracy were evaluated using 1,000 bootstrap iterations via “bootnet“ [29].

Traditional centrality indices such as closeness and betweenness were not reported due to instability in psychological networks [31, 32]. Instead, EI was prioritized as a reliable indicator of nodal influence [33, 34].

Network comparison

Network differences between the comorbidity groups were tested using the Network Comparison Test (NCT) implemented in the “Network Comparison Test” R package. A total of 1,000 permutations were used to assess differences in global strength and individual edge weights between networks [35].

Its LMR test result was non - significant, and the smallest class had just 108 participants (< 5%).Overall, considering model fit indices, statistical evidence, and practical interpretability, the three - class model was the optimal solution.

Network analysis of depression and cognition across different classes

Figure 2A-C illustrates the depression-cognition networks for the low, moderate, and high comorbidity groups. Of the 28 possible edges, network density was 78.6% (22/28) in the low comorbidity group and 75.0% (21/28) in both the moderate and high groups. The strongest connections were observed between CESD1 (depressed mood) and CESD3 (somatic symptoms; 0.523) in the low group, PDQD2 (retrospective memory) and PDQD3 (prospective memory; 0.526) in the moderate group, and CESD1(depressed mood) and CESD3 (somatic symptoms;0.614) in the high group (Table S1). All networks demonstrated good stability, with EI and BEI values exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.25 (low group: 0.749, 0.672; moderate group: 0.750, 0.750; high group: 0.362, 0.750). Moreover, bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for edge weights were narrow, indicating high accuracy and reliability of the estimates (Figs. S2, S3).

Green nodes represent depressive symptoms (CES-D), and blue nodes represent cognitive symptoms (PDQ-D). In the low comorbidity network, the nodes with the highest EI centrality were PDQD3 (prospective memory), CESD3 (Somatic symptoms), and PDQD4 (inhibition/organization) (Fig. 3A). In the moderate comorbidity network, CESD1 (depressed mood), CESD3 (Somatic symptoms), and PDQD2 (retrospective memory) showed the highest EI centrality (Fig. 3B). In the high comorbidity network, CESD3 (Somatic symptoms), CESD1 (depressed mood), and PDQD3 (prospective memory) were most central (Fig. 3C).

As shown in Fig. 3D–G and Supplementary Table 2, Bridge Expected Influence (BEI) values varied across latent profiles. In the low comorbidity group, PDQD3 (prospective memory) had the highest BEI value (0.359), suggesting a potential bridging role between depressive and cognitive symptoms. In the moderate comorbidity group, PDQD4 (inhibition/organization; BEI = 0.209) and CESD4 (interpersonal problems; BEI = 0.119) showed the highest BEI values, highlighting their relative importance as cross-domain connectors. In the high comorbidity group, PDQD3 (prospective memory; BEI = 0.122) and CESD4 (interpersonal problems; BEI = 0.148) again ranked highest, underscoring their consistent role as bridge symptoms across profiles. Although no BEI Z-scores exceeded the conventional threshold of 1, the relative ranking of BEI values provided meaningful indications of potential bridging symptoms.

The Network Comparison Test (NCT) revealed significant differences between the low and moderate comorbidity groups in terms of global strength (low: 4.57 vs. moderate: 3.05; S = 1.514, P < 0.001) and overall network structure (M = 0.298, P < 0.001). Post-hoc analyses identified 19 edges with significant group differences, including 5 CESD internal connections (e.g., CESD1–CESD3, CESD2–CESD3), 4 PDQD internal connections (e.g., PDQD2–PDQD3, PDQD1–PDQD4), and 10 cross-domain edges between CESD and PDQD (e.g., CESD3–PDQD1, CESD4–PDQD3). Full results are presented in Supplementary Table 3. In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the low and high comorbidity groups in either network structure (M = 0.355, P = 0.095) or global strength (low: 4.57 vs. high:4.38; S = 0.190, P = 0.469).However, a distinct pattern emerged when comparing the moderate and high comorbidity groups. Their network structures differed significantly (M = 0.363, P = 0.003), and the high comorbidity group exhibited significantly greater global strength than the moderate group (moderate:3.05 vs.high: 4.38; S = 1.324, P < 0.001). Nineteen significantly different edges were identified, including 4 CESD internal connections, 4 PDQD internal connections, and 11 cross-domain edges between CESD and PDQD (see Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

This study identified three comorbidity patterns of adolescent depression and cognitive impairment (low, 33.4%; moderate, 49.5%; high, 17.1%), indicating a stratified rather than homogeneous construct. Somatic symptoms consistently emerged as the central symptom, while subgroup-specific central nodes were prospective memory /organization in the low group, depressive mood/retrospective memory in the moderate group, and depressive mood/prospective memory in the high group. BEI analysis further showed that cognitive symptoms commonly served as cross-domain bridges, with prospective memory in the low group, inhibition/organization and interpersonal problems in the moderate group, and prospective memory and interpersonal problems in the high group. These findings underscore the heterogeneity of depression-cognition comorbidity and provide empirical evidence for stratified assessment and targeted intervention.

Most existing studies have focused on depression alone, exploring heterogeneity in its subgroups. For example, Herman et al. (2007) used LPA to identify low- and high-level subtypes among depressed adolescents [18]. Yu et al. applied LPA in adolescents with depressive mood and identified four subgroups that differed significantly in symptom characteristics and social functioning, emphasizing the importance of classification-based evaluation and treatment [36]. Piqueras et al. examined broader emotional problems in adolescents at risk of emotional and behavioral difficulties with low resilience and identified four profiles: “emotionally healthy,” “moderate-risk,” “high-risk,” and “severe-risk” [37]. Given the comorbidity between adolescent depression and cognitive deficits [6, 7], our study focused on the comorbidity patterns of depression and cognitive impairment. By identifying three subgroups low, moderate, and high comorbidity our findings not only enrich understanding of the heterogeneity of adolescent depression but also refine the characterization of depression-cognition comorbidity, providing novel evidence for the unique profiles of this comorbidity.

The three comorbidity patterns of adolescent depression - cognitive impairment identified in this study lay a stratified foundation for subsequent network analysis and provide a new entry point for further revealing the core and bridging mechanisms in different subtypes. Across all subgroups, somatic symptoms consistently emerged as the most central node, underscoring the persistent role of somatic manifestations in sustaining depression-cognition comorbidity networks. This finding aligns with prior research showing that adolescents with depression frequently report somatic complaints such as headache, fatigue, and sleep disturbances, which exacerbate functional impairment and contribute to chronicity [38]. Large-scale studies further demonstrate that greater somatic symptom burden is significantly associated with increased depression risk, greater symptom severity, and comorbid conditions [39]. In parallel, network research has highlighted fatigue and energy loss as central symptoms in adult somatic symptom disorder networks, sustaining and amplifying depressive syndromes [40]. Collectively, these findings reinforce the centrality of somatic symptoms across populations and lend strong support to their critical role in adolescent depression-cognition comorbidity. Subgroup-specific variations add further nuance: in the low comorbidity group, prospective memory impairment was identified as central, suggesting that cognitive dysfunction may serve as an early warning signal even under mild overall symptom severity [41]. By contrast, depressed mood was central in the moderate and high comorbidity groups, indicating that as severity escalates, emotional dysregulation becomes increasingly pivotal in maintaining the depression-cognition network. This is consistent with prior evidence showing that depressed mood typically occupies the central position in networks among individuals with more severe depressive symptoms [42].

Bridging symptoms shed further light on the interconnections between depression and cognitive impairment. Across groups, prospective memory impairment and interpersonal problems consistently served as bridging nodes, reinforcing their role as stable connectors between emotional and cognitive domains. Prior research supports these findings: prospective memory deficits, particularly in remembering planned tasks, are common in depression and linked to daily functional difficulties, helplessness, and persistent depressive affect [41], while interpersonal problems contribute to stress, rumination, and cognitive disruption. Kupferberg and Hasler (2023) further noted that social cognitive biases in depression exacerbate interpersonal conflicts, reinforcing depressive symptoms [43]. A subgroup-specific pattern was also observed, as inhibition/organization deficits emerged as a bridging symptom only in the moderate comorbidity group. This suggests that executive dysfunction, particularly in inhibitory control and working memory updating, may characterize this subgroup and represent a critical stage in the progression of depression–cognition comorbidity [44]. Such impairments can prolong negative information in working memory, fueling rumination and self-blame, which in adolescents may manifest as disorganization, intrusive thoughts, and heightened vulnerability. In low comorbidity groups these effects may be subtle, whereas in high comorbidity groups they may be overshadowed by stronger symptom interconnections. These results align with cognitive subtype research indicating that “cognitively impaired” depressed patients exhibit both more severe mood symptoms and greater executive dysfunction compared with “cognitively preserved” subtypes [45]. Overall, the bridging role of prospective memory, interpersonal problems, and inhibition/organization highlights both stable and subgroup-specific mechanisms of comorbidity, pointing to interpersonal and executive dysfunction as promising intervention targets.

In addition, pairwise comparisons of global network strength across the three subgroups revealed a noteworthy pattern: both the low and high comorbidity groups exhibited significantly higher global network strength than the moderate comorbidity group, whereas no significant difference was observed between the low and high comorbidity groups. Previous studies have suggested that cognitive impairment constitutes a relatively stable feature of depressive symptomatology, and that its association with depression does not necessarily follow a simple linear pattern as symptom severity increases [46]. This perspective may help explain the relatively strong and stable coupling between depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment observed in both the low and high comorbidity groups.In the present study, adolescents in the low comorbidity group had a mean depression score of only 27.14, indicating minimal symptom severity and suggesting that this subgroup largely represents a psychologically healthy population [20]. Under such conditions, the observed association between depression and cognitive impairment is likely to reflect a normative and relatively stable coupling between emotional and cognitive functioning. By contrast, the moderate comorbidity group exhibited a marked reduction in depression–cognition connectivity. One possible explanation is that, as depressive symptoms increase to a moderate level, adolescents may activate psychological defense and resilience-related mechanisms [47, 48]. Factors such as social support, mindfulness, and adaptive emotion regulation strategies may become more salient at this stage, thereby buffering or compensating for cross-domain symptom connectivity [48–50].In the high comorbidity group, however, depressive symptom severity was substantially elevated (mean score = 39.56), approaching commonly used thresholds for clinical depression [20]. At this level of symptom burden, protective psychological factors may no longer be sufficient to regulate or buffer the association between depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment, allowing the intrinsic coupling between depression and cognitive dysfunction to re-emerge. Taken together, based on the present findings, the moderate comorbidity stage may represent a particularly plastic phase of symptom network reorganization, in which interference factors that attenuate depression–cognition coupling could serve as feasible targets for future preventive interventions.

Clinically, this study provides several implications. First, the identification of three comorbidity profiles emphasizes the need for stratified assessment: universal mental health education for low comorbidity youth, and more targeted interventions for those with moderate or high comorbidity [36]. Second, the consistent centrality of somatic symptoms highlights the importance of monitoring physical complaints (e.g., headache, fatigue, sleep problems) and integrating symptom management into school-based health programs such as sleep hygiene, physical activity, and mindfulness training [38]. Third, the centrality of prospective memory impairment in the low comorbidity group suggests its potential as an early risk signal, warranting the inclusion of prospective memory assessments and training (e.g., reminders, structured planning, cognitive modules) [41]. Fourth, the prominence of depressed mood in moderate and high comorbidity groups indicates that mood symptoms drive comorbidity at higher severity, underscoring the need for interventions prioritizing mood regulation, such as CBT, emotion regulation training, and group support [42]. Fifth, the bridging roles of prospective memory and nterpersonal problems suggest combining cognitive and interpersonal interventions, such as memory support training and social skills programs, to disrupt maladaptive links between cognitive deficits and depressive affect [42, 51]. Finally, the subgroup-specific role of inhibition/organization deficits in the moderate group highlights the value of executive function rehabilitation, including task management training, attention control, or digital cognitive remediation tools [44].

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited from a single province in China using convenience sampling, which may restrict generalizability. Future work should employ multi-site, nationally representative, or cross-cultural samples to validate the identified profiles. Second, depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment were measured with self-report scales (CES-D, PDQ-D). Although these instruments are reliable, they rely on subjective reporting and may be influenced by social desirability; incorporating clinician ratings and neuropsychological tasks would provide a more comprehensive assessment. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether moderate comorbidity represents a transitional stage toward high comorbidity. Fourth, although BEI analysis identified symptoms with relatively higher bridging values, none exceeded the conventional threshold of 1. Replication with larger samples and intervention studies targeting candidate bridge symptoms (e.g., prospective memory, interpersonal problems, inhibition/organization deficits) are warranted. Finally, potential confounders such as sleep, stress, and family environment were not assessed but may influence both depression and cognition. Including these factors in future models will enrich understanding of comorbidity mechanisms.
