Effectiveness of a Multipronged Intervention to Reduce Gestational Diabetes Mellitus Incidence in High-Risk Pregnant Women in Puducherry: A Mixed Method Cluster Randomised Controlled Trial Protocol Behavioural Regulation, Individual Guidance, and Healthy Transformation for MOM
Authors: Mohammed K. Musthafa, Venkatachalam Jayaseelan, Murali Subbaiah, Nivedita Nanda, Anandalaksmi Varadharajalu, Kalaiselvy Arikrishnan, Mahadevan Duraiswamy, Premarajan K. Cheriyarath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) affects up to 31% of pregnancies in South Asia, posing significant risks to maternal and neonatal health. Despite their potential, Indian primary health centres (PHCs) are underutilised in GDM prevention. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a multipronged intervention compared to standard care in reducing GDM incidence among high-risk pregnant women and to explore barriers to adherence.Methods:This is a mixed-methods, two-arm, parallel-group cluster randomised controlled trial involving 12 PHCs randomised (1:1) to intervention or control arms. The intervention includes personalised dietary counselling (Medical Nutrition Therapy with millet-based, low-glycaemic index meals), structured physical activity guidance, educational booklets, WhatsApp-based reminders, and PHC staff training. Control PHCs will provide routine antenatal care. A total of 1200 participants (600 per arm) will be recruited. The primary outcome is GDM incidence, assessed via OGTT at 23–25 and 37 weeks using IADPSG criteria. Secondary outcomes include adherence to intervention, changes in metabolic biomarkers, and maternal and neonatal health indicators. Qualitative data from in-depth interviews and focus group discussions will undergo deductive content analysis.Results:This protocol outlines planned analyses. Both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses will be conducted. A conceptual framework will be developed to represent qualitative findings.Conclusion:This trial implements a culturally tailored, scalable intervention integrated into India’s primary healthcare system. Findings may guide national GDM prevention strategies, support early intervention, and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes in resource-constrained settings.

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), defined as glucose intolerance first identified during pregnancy, affects 17.7% of pregnancies globally and up to 31.7% in Southeast Asia.[1] In India, the GDM prevalence ranges from 13.4% in rural to 17.8% in urban areas, with studies from Puducherry reporting 27%.[23] Key risk factors include maternal age > 25 years, body mass index (BMI) >23 kg/m², family history of diabetes, polycystic ovary syndrome, and history of macrosomia/GDM.[4] GDM increases risks of preterm birth, caesarean delivery, macrosomia, Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) admission, and long-term cardiometabolic complications in both mother and child.[56]

In South India, particularly Puducherry, rice-based, carbohydrate- rich diets and cultural myths hinder dietary modification. Millet-based meals, rich in complex carbohydrates and low in glycaemic index, are a culturally acceptable alternative.[5]

Lifestyle interventions, including dietary counselling and physical activity, have reduced GDM incidence in trials.[7] However, Indian primary health centres (PHCs) face time constraints, limited nutrition training, and low adherence to Medical Nutrition Therapy (MNT) due to cost and planning barriers.[8910] Digital reminders show promise but are rarely integrated with PHC-based care.[11]

This cluster randomised controlled trial integrates early individualised MNT, structured physical activity, digital reminders, and PHC staff training. GDM screening will be conducted at 13, 25, and 35 weeks of gestation using the World Health Organization (WHO) 2013 criteria, which apply IADPSG diagnostic thresholds across all gestational ages. Although early screening for GDM at 13 weeks is not specifically recommended by the IADPSG criteria, this approach is supported by WHO 2013 guidance and will be accompanied by longitudinal biomarker assessment. The trial aims to (1) compare GDM incidence between intervention and control groups, (2) assess adherence to MNT and physical activity, (3) explore barriers and facilitators to adherence, and (4) evaluate associations between biomarkers and GDM outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design: A mixed-method interventional study

Quantitative part: Two-arm, parallel-group cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT) with a 1:1 allocation ratio, conducted in 12 out of 27 PHCs in Puducherry. The cluster design accounts for facility-level variations in antenatal care and enhances scalability by minimising the contamination. Participants and PHC staff cannot be blinded due to the behavioural intervention, but outcome assessors (laboratory technicians) and data analysts are blinded to minimise outcome ascertainment bias. The trial adheres to SPIRIT 2013 guidelines.[12]

Qualitative part: Descriptive type using focus group discussions and in-depth interviews

Study setting

The trial is conducted in Puducherry, a Union Territory in southern India with urban and rural populations. PHCs serve 30,000–50,000 people, registering 50–100 pregnant women monthly, and provide comprehensive Antenatal Care (ANC) clinics, supported by medical officers and primary health care providers like Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs). The 12 selected PHCs reflect Puducherry’s diverse socio-economic and dietary contexts, where lifestyle transitions drive high GDM prevalence.[313] ANC encompassing diagnostics and curative and nutritional counselling in a weekly antenatal clinic and maternity benefits schemes is implemented in Puducherry.

Study population

High-risk pregnant mothers, registered at antenatal clinics of selected PHCs in Puducherry

Inclusion Criteria: Pregnant women aged >18 years, <13 weeks of gestation, and at high risk for GDM per CDC-aligned criteria.[4] Euglycemic women with ≥1 risk factor: Age >25 years, pre-pregnancy BMI ≥23 kg/m², history of GDM/miscarriage/high birth weight baby (>4 kg)/congenital anomalies, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) symptoms, or family history of diabetes (first degree relative)/GDM, and participants continued to reside in Puducherry until delivery.

Exclusion criteria: Steroid therapy, history of preterm birth/repeated miscarriage, severe systemic diseases: chronic heart disease, kidney disease, rheumatic arthritis, severe anaemia, hyperemesis gravidarum, and assisted conception.

Adherence to diet and physical activity is monitored at 25 and 35 weeks using the Pregnancy Dietary Adherence Questionnaire (PDAQ, South Indian context), Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ), and 24-hour dietary recall, reinforced by ASHAs. Participants with danger signs (e.g., severe bleeding, convulsions) may abstain from physical activity but continue MNT, with data included in analyses. In case of any unforeseen risk, prompt referral will be made to JIPMER’ for further management.

Group 2 (Control arm): Receives standard ANC as per national guidelines,[14] including routine check-ups and one-time brief counselling on nutritional and physical activity. Experimental GDM interventions are prohibited.

Participant timeline

Participants are enrolled at <13 weeks gestation. Both groups undergo OGTT at 23–25 and 35–37 weeks, with biomarker assessments at 13 and 35 weeks. The intervention group receives counselling at <13, 25, and 35 weeks, with weekly WhatsApp reminders between 13–25 and 25–35 weeks. Adherence is evaluated at 25 and 35 weeks. Standard ANC visits align with national guidelines.[14]

Sample size

Quantitative part

Using OpenEpi v3.01, the sample size assumes a GDM incidence of 13.9% (intervention) versus 21.6% (control), based on prior studies.[7] With 80% power, 0.05 alpha, and a design effect of 1.5 for cluster randomisation, 796 participants are required. Adjusting for 10% loss to follow-up yields 1194, rounded to 1200 (600/arm). Twelve PHCs (6/arm) ensure approximately 100 participants per PHC, accounting for intra-cluster correlation.[12]

Qualitative part

Four to six FGDs and six to eight in-depth interviews will be conducted until data saturation is achieved.

Sampling technique

Quantitative part: Cluster random sampling is used to select 12 PHCs from the list of 27 PHCs. All the participants from the clusters meeting the study criteria will be included in the study until achieving the sample size.

Qualitative part

Participants will be selected through purposive sampling, focussing on individuals who are vocal and capable of sharing rich and relevant perspectives on their experiences with the intervention and factors that facilitated or hindered adherence.

Study procedure

Quantitative part

Recruitment

Eligible women are identified from PHC pregnancy registers at <13 weeks of gestation. ASHAs mobilise high-risk women via community outreach, home visits. Consecutive sampling targets 100 participants per PHC. PHC staff are trained to screen for GDM risk factors during routine visits. For qualitative components, purposive sampling recruits participants for four FGDs and four individual interviews based on PDAQ scores.

Randomisation

Sequence Generation: Randomisation allocates 12 PHCs (6 intervention, 6 control) using a random number sequence generated by a non-study team faculty from the Department of Preventive and Social Medicine.

Allocation Concealment: The faculty informs the researcher of PHC allocation only before site visits.

Implementation: The researcher enrols participants, with group assignment determined by PHC randomisation.

Blinding

Participants and PHC staff are unblinded due to the behavioural nature of the intervention. Outcome assessors (laboratory technicians for OGTT) and data analysts are blinded to group allocation. Unblinding is not planned as adverse events are managed via standard referral protocols.

Data collection methods

Data are collected at PHCs by the researcher, supported by trained staff and ASHAs.

Baseline: Socio-demographic, behavioural (PDAQ, GPAQ, 24-hour dietary recall), anthropometric, comorbidity, and obstetric history data are collected at <13 weeks, followed by a 75g OGTT to exclude overt diabetes. Women diagnosed before 13 weeks, including those requiring pharmacotherapy, will be excluded and referred for appropriate care since the primary objective is to estimate GDM incidence.

Follow-up: OGTT will be performed at 23–25 weeks to assess GDM incidence and will be repeated at 35–37 weeks for those testing negative, while women diagnosed at 23–25 weeks will be reassessed to evaluate glycaemic control. Biomarkers and glucose/lipid profiles are collected at 13 and 35 weeks via blood assays (duplicate measurements). Maternal/neonatal outcomes are recorded at delivery from PHC/JIPMER records explained in the flowchart Figure 1.

Qualitative data

The study will include four FGDs and six in-depth interviews or IDIs with pregnant women and their family members. Each session will begin with an introduction by the interviewer or moderator, a brief explanation of the study’s purpose, and collection of basic demographic details. The interviews will be facilitated by semi-structured, pre-tested interview guide. During FGDs, an assistant moderator will take detailed notes to capture key observations, while the moderator will manage the group dynamics. A sociogram will be drawn by the note-taker to visually track how conversations flow among participants. Each session will last around 60–90 minutes and will continue until data saturation. All interviews will be audio-recorded with prior consent. To ensure data quality, debriefing sessions, participant feedback on key findings, and extended engagement will be used. Peer review and expert validation of transcripts will further support reliability.

Statistical methods

Quantitative part

Quantitative analyses use STATA v17. Continuous variables (e.g., biomarker levels, gestational weight gain) are summarised as mean (SD) or median (IQR) based on normality. Categorical variables (e.g., GDM incidence) are reported as frequencies/percentages. Both ITT and PP analyses will be performed, with ITT including all randomised participants and PP including only those completing the intervention per protocol. GDM incidence will be compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests (P < 0.05). Risk ratios with 95% CI will assess intervention effects. Gestational weight gain and biomarker levels will be analysed using unpaired t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, depending on normality. Logistic regression will identify factors associated with GDM, adjusting for confounders (e.g., age, BMI, family history), with model fit assessed via R². Subgroup analyses will explore GDM incidence variations by socio-demographic factors (e.g., urban/rural residence, education) and adherence levels (high vs. low PDAQ scores). Missing data will be assessed and reported. If <10% and Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), values will be ignored. For >10% missing data, multiple imputation or mean substitution will be employed. Qualitative data from FGDs and IDIs will undergo hybrid coding and manual thematic analysis by two coders, with disagreements resolved via discussion or a third reviewer.[15]

Qualitative part

Audio recordings will be transcribed verbatim format within 3 to 7 days of each session and then translated into English. For accuracy, the English version will be back-translated into the local language with a minimum of 80% agreement. Manual thematic content analysis will be performed with a deductive approach. Themes and codes will be developed from interview content, grouped into categories and sub-categories. Participant statements will serve as the primary units of analysis. A conceptual framework will also be created to illustrate the interpretation of qualitative findings. The findings will be presented in line with the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) checklist to ensure transparency and rigours.[16]

Necessary permission was also obtained from the Puducherry State Government.

The study is classified as minimal risk according to the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) ethical guidelines. Written informed consent will be obtained in Tamil from all participants for both study participation and blood sample collection. Participation is entirely voluntary, and participants will have the right to withdraw at any stage without any consequences. Data confidentiality will be strictly maintained using de-identified records. Any adverse events arising during the study will be promptly referred to JIPMER for appropriate clinical management. Any modifications to the study protocol will be submitted to the ethics committee for re-approval.

DISCUSSION

GDM is a rising concern in India, with Puducherry reporting particularly high prevalence rates. Current national strategies predominantly focus on post-diagnosis management, leaving a critical gap in early preventive measures. This study addresses that need by implementing a culturally sensitive, multipronged intervention aimed at preventing GDM among high-risk pregnant women in their first trimester. Conducted within 12 government PHCs in Puducherry, the trial leverages the existing public health infrastructure to deliver an integrated package that includes individualised dietary counselling with millet-based, low-glycaemic index meals, physical activity guidance, weekly WhatsApp reminders, and PHC staff training.

The methodology is rigorous, utilising a cluster randomised controlled design to minimise contamination and enhance real-world applicability.[17] A mixed-methods approach adds depth, with qualitative interviews and FGDs capturing the behavioural and contextual nuances influencing adherence.[18] Notably, this study incorporates longitudinal monitoring of metabolic biomarkers such as adiponectin, leptin, IL-6, fetuin A, and insulin providing valuable insight into physiological changes underlying GDM risk.

The novelty of the intervention lies in its early initiation during the first trimester, its alignment with local dietary practices, and the use of mobile-based reinforcement to support behavioural change.[19] It goes beyond the conventional one-time counselling model by ensuring repeated engagement and monitoring. The inclusion of culturally appropriate dietary counselling with millet-based meals tailored to regional preferences, the involvement of family members in counselling sessions, and the decentralisation of service delivery through PHC teams and ASHA workers are key differentiators that enhance feasibility and acceptability.

This study has several strengths [Table 1]. It is embedded in the public health system, ensuring scalability and relevance for national programs. The intervention is comprehensive, preventive, and rooted in community realities, while the use of validated tools and blinded outcome assessment strengthens the internal validity. The inclusion of both quantitative and qualitative methods enables a deeper understanding of not just effectiveness but also implementation dynamics. Furthermore, biomarker monitoring adds an objective biological dimension rarely seen in public health interventions at the primary care level.

Strengths and limitations of the study

However, the study also has limitations. Adherence to dietary and physical activity recommendations may vary due to socio-cultural beliefs, limited health literacy, and economic constraints.[2021] As is common with behavioural interventions, blinding of participants and healthcare providers is not feasible, which could introduce performance bias. A projected 10% loss to follow-up may slightly reduce the study’s statistical power. Additionally, while the intervention is well suited to South Indian contexts, its generalisability to other cultural or geographic settings may be limited without contextual adaptation.

If successful, the study is expected to demonstrate reduced incidence of GDM, improved maternal health behaviours, and better understanding of dietary and metabolic influences on pregnancy outcomes. The findings could inform revisions in national guidelines to include early Medical Nutrition Therapy and third-trimester screening, offering a scalable and sustainable model for GDM prevention in other low-resource settings across India and similar countries.

Protocol deviations

Protocol violations (e.g., non-adherence to MNT/physical activity, incorrect PHC randomisation, participant withdrawal) are documented in proforma and reported to the principal investigator. Minor deviations (e.g., missing a counselling session) are addressed through ASHA reinforcement and included in intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses. Major violations (e.g., refusal of OGTT) may lead to exclusion from per-protocol (PP) analyses but are retained in ITT analyses. Violations are reviewed monthly during field visits to ensure protocol fidelity, with retraining provided if needed.

Data management

Data are recorded on hard-copy proforma and entered into RedCap. Dietary recall data are standardised and processed via DietSoft. Anthropometric measurements use calibrated instruments. Blood samples are stored at -80°C for ELISA every 80–90 samples. Proforma and consent forms are stored in locked files. Data are encrypted with unique identifiers, retained for 3 years post-study, and accessible only to the research team.[12]

Monitoring

No Data Monitoring Committee is established as the behavioural intervention poses minimal risk. The principal investigator conducts monthly field visits and quarterly 5% proforma cross-verification to ensure data quality. No interim analyses or stopping rules are planned.

Harms

Adverse events (e.g., severe bleeding, convulsions) are monitored during PHC visits and reported by ASHAs. Affected participants are referred to a tertiary care centre, with costs covered by standard care. No serious adverse events are anticipated from the intervention.[1214]

Auditing

Monthly field visits and quarterly proforma cross-verification ensure protocol adherence. The Institutional Ethics Committee may conduct independent audits. Findings are addressed with retraining or protocol adjustments.

Dissemination

Results will be published in four open-access journals: (1) GDM incidence, (2) MNT adherence barriers/facilitators, (3) biomarker changes, and (4) first-trimester GDM prevalence/risk factors. Findings will be shared with participants via PHC meetings and ASHA outreach. Data will be accessible via CTRI and journal repositories, with no restrictions on de-identified datasets.
