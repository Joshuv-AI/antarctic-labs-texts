Racial and ethnic disparities in COVID-19 vaccination: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shabnam Iezadi, Pirhossein Kolivand, Peyman Namdar, Peyman Saberian, Kamal Gholipour, Samad Azari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to systematically review and synthesize the evidence on the relationship between race/ethnicity and COVID-19 vaccine uptake in the general population.MethodsA comprehensive search of relevant electronic databases was conducted in May 2024 and updated in December 2024. Original research studies comparing COVID-19 vaccine uptake or vaccination rates in racial and ethnic minority populations with those of non-Hispanic White populations were eligible for inclusion. A descriptive synthesis of findings was presented in both narrative and tabular formats. Bayesian random-effects meta-analysis was performed to estimate pooled adjusted relative risk ratios (RRs) and their 95% credible intervals (CrIs) for COVID-19 vaccine uptake across racial and ethnic groups. Posterior distributions were also examined to explore patterns of disparities.ResultsA total of 30 studies (21 cross-sectional and 9 cohort) were included in the systematic review, of which 10 studies were eligible for meta-analysis. Most studies were conducted in the United States and the United Kingdom. The majority of the studies reported that racial and ethnic minority groups, including Black, Hispanic, and Other/Mixed racial groups, had lower COVID-19 vaccination rates compared to White populations. Meta-analysis results showed that Black individuals were 13% less likely to be vaccinated (RR: 0.87, 95% CrI: 0.81–0.93), and those of Mixed race were 7% less likely (RR: 0.93, 95% CrI: 0.90–0.97), compared to White individuals. For single-dose uptake, Black individuals were 16% less likely to be vaccinated than White individuals (RR: 0.84, 95% CrI: 0.76–0.92), representing the lowest pooled RR among all groups. Posterior distributions confirmed consistent disparities in vaccine uptake for Black and Mixed race populations across dose categories.ConclusionMost of the studies reported that racial and ethnic minority groups had lower rates of COVID-19 vaccination compared to White populations. Bayesian effect estimates reinforced these disparities for Black and Mixed race minority groups. While no significant differences were found for Asian, Hispanic, and Other race groups, wide credible intervals in some subgroups suggest the need for further research to explore potential contextual and regional variations not captured in the studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01740-y.Keywords: COVID-19 vaccine, Race, Ethnicity, Disparity, Health equity

Background

Vaccination stands as one of the most effective public health interventions worldwide, serving as a cornerstone in combating the spread of infectious diseases. Throughout the COVID-19 pandemic, arguably the most formidable health crisis of the last century, vaccines have emerged as pivotal tools [1]. Demonstrated to be safe and efficacious, vaccines played a crucial role in averting severe or fatal cases of COVID-19, and in April 2021, became available to adults in the United States (USA) and many other high-income countries [2]. COVID-19 vaccines have saved over 19 million lives across 185 countries, thereby reducing global fatalities from the virus by 63% during the first year of vaccination [3].

Yet, like many other COVID-19-related challenges, disparities in vaccine uptake among various racial and ethnic groups became apparent during the pandemic. This disparity in vaccination rates highlights a pressing social justice issue, potentially exacerbating the accumulation of susceptible individuals within communities. Despite being at higher risk of adverse COVID-19-related health outcomes, such as hospitalization and mortality, individuals from minority racial and ethnic backgrounds encountered inequities in accessing vaccines, particularly during the early phases of vaccine distribution. Several studies have underscored disparities in access to COVID-19 vaccines among minority ethnic groups [4–6]. Additionally, evidence has demonstrated lower vaccine coverage in areas with a higher concentration of minority racial and ethnic minority populations [7, 8]. Nevertheless, the consistency of findings across various studies remains unclear.

Understanding the correlation between individuals' racial and ethnic backgrounds and vaccination uptake can provide crucial insights into health disparities, thereby revealing significant potential for service improvement and enhancing vaccination coverage [9]. This understanding can also shed light on the underlying structural and systemic factors contributing to vaccine inequities, such as access barriers, healthcare disparities, and historical mistrust. Consequently, gaining insight into the influence of race and ethnicity as determinants of COVID-19 vaccine coverage is a critical step in mitigating disparities in vaccine uptake during future pandemics.

While several systematic reviews have explored individuals' attitudes and perceptions toward vaccination [10–13], barriers to vaccine accessibility and distributions [14, 15], and strategies for improving vaccine uptake in the context of COVID-19 [15–17], comprehensive evidence on the association between race/ethnicity and vaccine uptake remains limited. On the other hand, current systematic reviews on the vaccine uptake have focused on specific population including healthcare workers [18], and pregnant women [19]. Further research and targeted interventions are needed to address these disparities and ensure equitable vaccine coverage for all communities.

In this study, we aimed to systematically review the evidence on the relationship between individuals' race/ethnicity and COVID-19 vaccine uptake in the general populations.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guideline [20].

Data sources and search strategy

The relevant electronic databases, including Web of Science, Scopus, and Medline (via PubMed), were searched using a comprehensive search strategy, in May 2024 and then updated in late December 2024. To provide a comprehensive picture of COVID-19 vaccination uptake, we also searched grey literature through searching google scholar and reviewed the citation and reference lists of the relevant papers as well. The search strategy was developed in advance applying key terms from relevant literature in health disparities and COVID-19 vaccination. Through a pilot search, before applying the primary search strategy for the systematic search of all databases, each domain of the topic was first searched separately and then in combination through PubMed, and then any necessary changes were made on the key-terms. Search strategies are provided in the Supplementary Information File.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Population: Studies reporting COVID-19 vaccine uptake or rates by race/ethnicity in general population settings.

Exposure: The primary exposure of interest was belonging to a specific racial or ethnic group for which race and ethnicity information was available.

Comparisons: Direct comparisons of vaccination disparities across racial/ethnic groups.

Outcome: Outcome measures included COVID-19 vaccine uptake and vaccination rates.

Study Design: Peer-reviewed, English-language original research papers, including observational studies (retrospective/prospective cohorts, cross-sectional studies).

Exclusion criteria

Studies focusing on subgroups with distinct vaccination protocols (e.g., healthcare workers, individuals with disabilities, immunocompromised individuals, etc.).

Studies focusing on specific vaccination strategies.

Non-empirical publications, including short communications, commentaries, editorials, perspectives, government reports, and statistical bulletins.

Data extraction and management

After pooling the search data in EndNote X8 and removing duplicate results, two investigators independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of the studies to identify relevant papers. They then meticulously examined the full texts of the relevant papers, applying the eligibility criteria and focusing on the aims of the systematic review. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were discussed and resolved by the lead investigator.

Next, all relevant data were extracted from the selected papers and compiled into a pre-developed data extraction sheet (see the Supplementary Information File). The data extraction sheet was designed based on the research objectives, included information on the general characteristics of the studies, research designs, data sources, outcome measures, data analysis methods, study results, and limitations. Before being used in the systematic review, the extraction sheet was tested in a pilot examination of three papers by two investigators, and adjustments were made as needed.

Quality appraisal of the studies

To ensure the internal validity of the review’s findings and avoid issues of false precision, two investigators independently evaluated the quality of eligible studies using standardized critical appraisal tools from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for observational studies [21]. They utilized the relevant checklists for cross-sectional and cohort studies, which contained 8 and 11 questions, respectively. Each checklist item was answered with “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable.” A “Yes” response received a score of 1, while “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable” received a score of 0. The quality score for each study was calculated and reported as a percentage. The final score for each study was determined by consensus between the two evaluators. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with the other authors.

Data analysis

Descriptive synthesis

We conducted a descriptive synthesis of the results, presenting the findings in both narrative and tabular formats.

Bayesian meta-analysis

We conducted a Bayesian meta-analysis to estimate the relative likelihood of COVID-19 vaccine uptake across racial and ethnic groups, using non-Hispanic White individuals as the reference category. Five racial and ethnic minority groups were included: Black, Hispanic, Asian, Other, and Mixed race. The “Other” and “Mixed race” categories encompassed individuals not classified as Black, Hispanic, or Asian in the original studies, based on how race and ethnicity were reported. These categories were selected due to consistent reporting across the majority of included studies. To ensure comparability, only studies that reported disparities in vaccine uptake using these five categories were included in the meta-analysis. Studies using non-comparable classifications were excluded from the meta-analysis but summarized narratively in the systematic review.

The natural logarithm of the RRs was then calculated, and these log-transformed values were used as the dependent variable in the Bayesian meta-analysis.

Bayesian random-effects models were fitted using the brms package in R (version 4.5.1, 2025-06–13 ucrt). The primary outcome was the log-transformed RR, and standard errors were incorporated using the se() function. A random intercept by study was included to account for between-study heterogeneity. For the overall model, we specified a normal(0, 1) prior for the intercept and a half-Cauchy(0, 0.5) prior for the standard deviation of the random effects.

We used four Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains, each with 8,000 iterations (including 4,000 warm-up iterations). To improve convergence and reduce divergent transitions, we set adapt_delta = 0.999 and max_treedepth = 20. Model convergence and adequacy were assessed through standard diagnostics, including R-hat values, effective sample sizes, and divergence checks.

Posterior samples for the pooled log RR and between-study heterogeneity (τ) were extracted and exponentiated to obtain RRs on the original scale. Median posterior estimates and 95% credible intervals (CrIs) were reported using the tidybayes package. A posterior density plot was generated using ggplot2, with the 95% CrI shaded and a vertical line at RR = 1 to indicate the null effect.

Subgroup analyses by vaccine dose

We conducted Bayesian subgroup meta-analyses stratified by vaccine dose. Vaccine uptake was categorized into three mutually exclusive groups: at least one dose (reference), single dose, and two doses.

For Black, Asian, and Other racial groups, we fitted Bayesian random-effects meta-regression models. Weakly informative priors were used: a normal(0, 0.5) prior for regression coefficients and an exponential(2) prior for the between-study standard deviation. These models were run using four MCMC chains with 12,000 iterations each (4,000 warm-up), with adapt_delta = 0.999 and max_treedepth = 15.

For the Hispanic and Mixed race groups, we used a Bayesian meta-analysis model with a Student-t likelihood to accommodate potential outliers and small-study effects. The model included vaccine dose as a fixed effect and modeled random intercepts by study. We applied QR decomposition to enhance numerical stability. Conservative priors were specified: normal(0, 0.3) for fixed effects and exponential(2) for the random effects standard deviation. The degrees of freedom (ν) for the Student-t distribution were estimated from a gamma(2, 0.1) prior, allowing for heavier tails and robust inference. These models used four parallel chains with 6,000 iterations (2,000 warm-up) and adapt_delta = 0.999.

Assessment of publication bias

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses using frequentist methods. Funnel plot asymmetry was visually assessed, and Egger’s regression test was performed to detect associations between effect size and standard error. Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill method was also applied to estimate the number of potentially missing studies and assess their impact on the pooled estimates. These analyses were conducted using the metafor package in R and served as supplementary diagnostics to the Bayesian models.

Results

Our primary search strategy yielded 6,224 results. After removing duplicates, we screened 3,848 papers based on their titles and abstracts, and selected 56 papers for full-text review (see Fig. 1). Ultimately 30 papers, including 21 cross-sectional studies and 9 cohort studies, met the eligibility criteria [23–52]. The majority of the studies were conducted in USA (23 studies) and the United Kingdom (UK) (5 studies), with one study conducted in both the UK and the USA. Additionally, one study was from Belgium, and none were from developing countries.

PRISMA flow diagram for study selection

The quality appraisal of the included studies showed average scores of 84.62% for cross-sectional studies and 93.93% for cohort studies. In the majority of the papers, a major concern was the classification of individuals who received “at least one dose” as vaccinated, which does not account for differences between single-dose and multi-dose recipients. Moreover, in cross-sectional studies, another main limitation was the reliance on self-reported outcome measures, making vaccine uptake data susceptible to recall and social desirability biases (see the Supplementary Information File).

Statistical associations between race/ethnicity and COVID-19 vaccination

A total of 24 articles assessed the correlations between race/ethnicity and COVID-19 vaccine uptake [25–32, 34–39, 41–45, 47, 48, 50–52]. Of these, 19 studies found that Black and/or Hispanic groups were less likely to be vaccinated compared to White populations [25, 26, 28–32, 34–37, 41–45, 50–52]. However, three studies reported the opposite, finding that Black and Hispanic groups were more likely to receive the vaccine than White individuals [27, 38, 47]. Moreover, one study reported lower vaccine coverage for Black populations and higher for Hispanics compared to Whites [52]. Table 1 demonstrates a summary of the results of included studies.

Results regarding Asian populations in USA were inconsistent. Seven studies indicated that Asians had higher odds of vaccination compared to Whites [32, 38, 41–43, 47, 52], while four studies found lower odds of vaccination for Asians compared to White individuals [28, 30, 31, 34].

Ten studies reported lower odds of vaccination for individuals from other/unknown or mixed racial and ethnic groups compared to White individuals [28–31, 34, 35, 37, 43, 44, 51]. Additionally, one study found that first-generation and second-generation non-natives were more likely to be unvaccinated compared to native populations [39]. Conversely, one study reported higher odds of vaccination for other/unknown or mixed racial and ethnic groups compared to White populations [27]. One study highlighted a complex pattern of racial and ethnic disparities among minority groups: non-Hispanic Black participants were less likely to be vaccinated compared to both Mexican Hispanic and non-Mexican Hispanic participants. In contrast, Mexican Hispanic participants were less likely to be vaccinated compared to non-Mexican Hispanic participants [50].

Four studies reported heterogeneous results regarding racial and ethnic disparities in vaccination rates [28, 40, 41, 45]. For example, one study from USA found that during the first year of vaccination, Asian or Pacific Islander, Black, and Hispanic individuals had lower vaccination rates compared to White individuals. However, in the second year, the vaccination rates for Asian or Pacific Islander, Black, and Hispanic groups were higher than those for White individuals [28]. Another study from USA revealed that initially, Black and Hispanic individuals were less likely to receive the vaccine compared to non-Hispanic White and other ethnic groups. Over time, however, a greater cumulative proportion of Black and Hispanic individuals received the vaccine [40]. A third study highlighted regional differences in vaccination rates among school children. While White students in Manhattan were vaccinated at higher rates than Black and Hispanic students, in Staten Island, the Bronx, and Queens, White students had the lowest vaccination rates of any racial or ethnic group [41]. Additionally, a study comparing focusing on both USA and UK found that in the USA, Black individuals were less likely to be vaccinated than White individuals. In contrast, in UK, non-White participants were more likely to be vaccinated than their White counterparts [45].

Descriptive variations in COVID-19 vaccination among racial groups

In comparing vaccination rates across different racial and ethnic groups, four studies observed that Black individuals had lower vaccination rates than White populations [23, 24, 46, 49], and three studies noted lower vaccination rates among Hispanic groups compared to White groups [24, 46, 49]. However, two studies indicated that vaccination rates among Asian populations were similar to or even higher than those of White individuals [46, 49]. Nevertheless, these comparisons were made without the use of statistical tests, relying solely on descriptive data.

Two studies using descriptive analysis revealed a different pattern of racial and ethnic disparities in COVID-19 vaccination. One survey study found that foreign-born respondents reported higher COVID-19 vaccination rates than USA-born participants [46]. Another study highlighted that White and Black populations were vaccinated at rates significantly lower than would be expected based on their age distributions. In contrast, Hispanic and Native populations were vaccinated at rates closely aligned with expectations, while Asian American and Pacific Islander populations were vaccinated at rates much higher than anticipated [33].

Results of the meta-analysis

A total of 10 studies (11 observations) from the USA and UK were included in the meta-analysis, selected based on the similarity of data points and outcome measures [26, 29, 31, 34–36, 41, 42, 44, 45, 51]. One study reported separate results for the USA and UK, which were treated as distinct observations in the analysis. All reported pooled RRs refers to adjusted relative risk ratios. Funnel plots examining publication bias are provided in the Supplementary Information File. Due to the high level of heterogeneity among the included studies, random-effects models were used to estimate pooled effect sizes.

Black vs. whites populations

The Bayesian meta-analysis identified significant disparities in COVID-19 vaccine uptake, with Black individuals being less likely to be vaccinated than White individuals. The overall pooled estimate showed a 13% lower likelihood of vaccination among Black populations (RR: 0.87, 95% CrI: 0.81–0.93).

Subgroup analyses by vaccine dose revealed a 13% lower likelihood of receiving at least one dose (RR: 0.87, 95% CrI: 0.78–0.96), a 16% lower likelihood for receiving a single dose (RR: 0.84, 95% CrI: 0.76–0.92), and a non-significant 3% lower likelihood for receiving two doses (RR: 0.97, 95% CrI: 0.85–1.12). These findings suggest that disparities were more pronounced during the initial stages of vaccination (see Fig. 2).

Points represent study-level adjusted risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals. The vertical dashed line at RR = 1.00 denotes no difference in uptake between racial groups. τ (tau) represents the between−study heterogeneity (posterior median with 95% CrI)

Between-study heterogeneity was modest (τ ≈ 0.09, 95% CrI: 0.06–0.18). Posterior density plots indicated a low probability of RRs greater than or equal to 1, particularly for the single-dose category, reinforcing the evidence of lower vaccine uptake among Black individuals.

Funnel plot inspection showed modest asymmetry, suggesting possible small-study effects. However, Egger’s regression test did not detect significant asymmetry (z = –1.37, p = 0.17). The trim-and-fill method estimated three potentially missing studies, but their imputation did not meaningfully change the direction or magnitude of the pooled estimates (see Figs. 6 and 7 in Supplementary Information File).

Hispanic vs. white populations

The Bayesian meta-analysis of five studies from USA revealed no significant difference in COVID-19 vaccine uptake between Hispanic and White populations (RR: 1.00, 95% CrI: 0.87–1.14). Subgroup analyses by vaccine dose indicated a 3% lower uptake for at least one dose (RR: 0.97, 95% CrI: 0.71–1.33) and a 5% lower uptake for a single dose (RR: 0.95, 95% CrI: 0.63–1.47), though these differences were not statistically significant (see Fig. 3).

The vertical dashed line at RR = 1.00 denotes no difference in uptake between racial groups. τ (tau) represents the between−study heterogeneity (posterior median with 95% CrI)

Between-study heterogeneity was low to moderate (τ = 0.12, 95% CrI: 0.06–0.32), indicating reasonably consistent findings across studies. However, the wide credible intervals for some subgroups—especially single-dose uptake—highlight the need for further research to explore potential contextual or regional disparities not captured in this analysis.

Posterior density plots supported these findings. Across all dose categories—at least one dose, single dose, and two doses (RR = 1.06, 95% CrI: 0.77–1.42)—the distributions were centered near the null value (RR = 1.0), with substantial overlap of credible intervals above and below 1.0. These patterns suggest no statistically significant disparity in vaccine uptake between Hispanic and White populations.

The posterior distribution for between-study heterogeneity (τ = 0.15, 95% CrI: 0.07–0.53) further supports the interpretation of low to moderate heterogeneity, with uncertainty reflected in the width of some credible intervals—particularly for the single-dose category. While the overall findings indicate comparable vaccination rates, the range of uncertainty underscores the importance of additional studies to clarify potential variability in specific contexts.

Funnel plot inspection revealed a roughly symmetric distribution of effect sizes around the pooled estimate, with no clear visual evidence of publication bias. Egger’s regression test supported this conclusion (z = –0.89, p = 0.37). The trim-and-fill analysis suggested one potentially missing study, but the imputed estimate remained statistically non-significant and consistent with the Bayesian result, indicating minimal impact on the overall conclusion (see Figs. 8 and 9 in Supplementary Information File).

Asian vs. white populations

A total of 10 observations were included in the Bayesian meta-analysis examining differences in COVID-19 vaccine uptake between Asian and White populations. The overall pooled estimate showed no statistically significant difference in vaccine uptake between Asian and White populations (RR: 1.05, 95% CrI: 0.90–1.22) (see Fig. 4).

Bayesian subgroup analyses also revealed no statistically significant differences across any vaccine dose category. For “at least one dose” (RR: 1.03, 95% CrI: 0.85–1.26) and the “single dose” categories (RR: 0.96, 95% CrI: 0.79–1.18) the pooled RRs were centered near the null value, suggesting a slightly lower, but statistically non-significant, uptake among Asian individuals. The “two doses” subgroup yielded a pooled RR of 1.30 (95% CrI: 0.97–1.70), indicating a possible higher uptake in Asian populations, though the credible interval included the null.

Posterior density plots illustrate these patterns, with wide distributions centered around RR = 1.00 for the first two categories and a rightward shift for the two-dose subgroup. While the latter hints at increased uptake, the uncertainty around the estimate limits strong conclusions. The posterior distribution of between-study heterogeneity (τ) indicated moderate variability across studies (τ = 0.19, 95% CrI: 0.12–0.35), suggesting some differences in effect estimates across contexts.

Assessment of publication bias showed no significant concerns. Egger’s regression test indicated no evidence of funnel plot asymmetry (z = –0.08, p = 0.93). The trim-and-fill analysis estimated three potentially missing studies on the right side of the funnel plot—representing studies with more favorable outcomes for Asian populations. However, imputing these studies yielded a slightly increased but still statistically non-significant pooled RR, suggesting that the main conclusions remain robust even after accounting for potential publication bias (see Figs. 10 and 11 in Supplementary Information File).

Other race vs. white populations

The Bayesian meta-analysis of nine studies comparing Other Race populations to White individuals revealed no statistically significant difference in COVID-19 vaccine uptake, with a pooled estimate indicating a 7% lower likelihood of vaccination among those categorized as Other Race (RR: 0.93, 95% CrI: 0.81–1.07).

Subgroup analyses by vaccine dose showed similar patterns, with non-significant differences across two categories—9% lower likelihoods for at least one dose (RR: 0.91, 95% CrI: 0.76–1.07), 4% lower likelihood for a single dose (RR: 0.86, 95% CrI: 0.70–1.05) —while a slightly higher likelihood for two doses (RR: 1.11, 95% CrI: 0.87–1.39). Moderate between-study heterogeneity was observed (τ = 0.17, 95% CrI: 0.10–0.33), possibly reflecting variation in study design, regional contexts, or population characteristics (see Fig. 5).

Mixed race vs. white populations

The Bayesian meta-analysis, based on five studies and six observations from USA and UK, showed that individuals identifying as Mixed Race were 7% less likely to be vaccinated than White individuals (RR: 0.93, 95% CrI: 0.90–0.97), which were statistically significant. This disparity was consistent across vaccine dose categories, with an 8% lower uptake for at least one dose (RR: 0.92, 95% CrI: 0.85–1.00) and a 7% lower uptake for a single dose (RR: 0.93, 95% CrI: 0.89–1.00). Although the upper bounds of the credible intervals approached the null value (RR = 1.00), the pooled estimates remained below 1.00, suggesting modest but consistent disparities (see Fig. 6).

Discussion

This systematic review examined racial and ethnic disparities in COVID-19 vaccination uptake. The majority of studies reported lower vaccination rates among racial and ethnic minority groups compared to White individuals. In particular, Black individuals were found to have up to 70% lower odds of receiving the COVID-19 vaccine than their White counterparts [25–32, 34–45, 47, 48, 50–52].

However, findings were not consistent across all studies. A few reported higher odds of vaccine uptake among Asian or Hispanic populations in specific regions [27, 38, 41–43, 46, 47]. These reverse findings may reflect contextual factors and differences in participant characteristics. For example, in the study by Xu et al., participants were children and young adults, with vaccination decisions made by parents. Racial and ethnic differences in parental attitudes and hesitancy may therefore explain the observed disparities [27]. Similarly, Elbel et al. studied a university student population and found higher vaccination rates among Asian students, which may be attributed to differences in neighborhood characteristics, access to resources, and cultural or social preferences influencing vaccination decisions [41].

Despite some variation across studies, our meta-analysis confirmed that Black and Mixed race populations were significantly less likely to receive the COVID-19 vaccine compared to White populations, by 13% and 7%, respectively. The lower likelihood of uptake for a single dose compared to two doses (RR: 0.84, 95% CrI: 0.76–0.92 vs. RR: 0.97, 95% CrI: 0.85–1.12) suggests that disparities were more severe during the initial phase of vaccine rollout. While no significant difference was observed between Hispanic and White populations overall, the wide credible intervals, particularly for single-dose uptake (RR: 0.95, 95% CrI: 0.63–1.47), indicate the possibility of localized or subgroup disparities. These may reflect barriers such as limited healthcare access, language difficulties, or medical mistrust that disproportionately affect certain Hispanic communities, even if not evident at the national level.

Ensuring the efficient and equitable administration of vaccines, particularly to those at highest risk for infection and severe health outcomes, is crucial as soon as effective vaccines become available [53]. Previous research has highlighted the link between race, ethnicity, socioeconomic characteristics, and adverse health outcomes during the COVID-19 pandemic [54]. Our study contributes to this understanding by demonstrating similar disparities in COVID-19 vaccine uptake.

Despite being at higher risk for death, hospitalization, and infection from COVID-19, Black populations had lower vaccination rates than White individuals during the first year of vaccine distribution [54]. If these disparities are not addressed, they may lead to severe health outcomes for vulnerable populations in future pandemics. To mitigate these health disparities, it is imperative to first identify the root causes and determinants of these inequities. Additionally, ongoing monitoring of vaccination coverage and targeted initiatives to promote equity in vaccination are essential, especially for populations disproportionately affected by COVID-19.

Our study also highlights a persistent gap in understanding the underlying factors contributing to racial and ethnic disparities in COVID-19 vaccination coverage. While the meta-analysis incorporated adjusted effect sizes that accounted for socioeconomic, demographic, and structural variables reported in individual studies, fully conceptualizing the mechanisms driving these disparities remains challenging. Existing research has identified several key contributors, including age, socioeconomic status, and structural barriers.

Socioeconomic disadvantages, such as lack of health insurance, lower income and education levels, unemployment, and financial hardship, were consistently associated with lower vaccine uptake among Black and Hispanic populations compared to White individuals. Demographic factors, including younger age and larger household size, also played a role in these disparities [25, 29, 35, 45]. In addition, structural barriers, such as limited access to healthcare, perceived health system inequities, and experiences of discrimination, further contributed to reduced vaccination rates [26, 41].

These factors often intersect, creating compounded barriers for individuals facing multiple disadvantages, such as minority status combined with low socioeconomic position or language barriers, intensifying disparities in vaccine uptake. Although adjustments for these factors reduced disparities in some studies, significant differences remained, indicating that conventional covariates only partly explain inequities [31, 34, 36, 51]. Future research should apply an intersectional approach to better understand how overlapping social and structural determinants drive vaccination disparities and inform more targeted interventions.

Inequity in COVID-19 vaccine coverage can be discussed from two perspectives: population perspectives and health system perspectives. From a population perspective, vaccination disparities are largely driven by attitudes toward vaccines. Evidence shows that vaccine hesitancy among ethnic minorities—fueled by mistrust in the healthcare system and peer influences—significantly contributes to lower coverage [55]. Communities with higher social vulnerability also show greater reluctance and lower vaccination rates [14, 55]. To address this, raising awareness about vaccine safety and efficacy and building trust through genuine community engagement with healthcare and government institutions are essential for improving uptake and strengthening resilience for future pandemics.

However, attitudes toward vaccination and health system do not always align with actual uptake, and discrepancies often reflect systemic barriers [31, 56]. Health system shortcomings, such as limited access, long distances to vaccination sites, language barriers, and inadequate prioritization, can hinder vaccine uptake among racial and ethnic minorities [14]. At the national level, unequal vaccine distribution has also contributed to disparities, with studies showing that areas with higher Black and Hispanic populations received fewer doses during the initial rollout.

To address vaccination disparities, equity-focused strategies are essential. These include targeted outreach, culturally and linguistically tailored education, and accessible vaccination sites—especially in underserved areas. Mobile clinics and extended hours can improve access for communities with limited healthcare infrastructure. Engaging trusted local leaders in community-led initiatives can counter misinformation and build vaccine confidence. Policymakers should also invest in disaggregated data collection to uncover hidden inequities and guide equitable distribution efforts, aligning with broader goals of inclusive and trusted pandemic response efforts [57, 58].

In some settings, mandates and incentives have also been employed to increase vaccine uptake. However, their effectiveness in reducing disparities may depend on how they are implemented and the level of trust within the target communities [59]. Ultimately, addressing both population-level and health system-level barriers through community-informed, context-sensitive approaches is essential to achieving more equitable vaccine coverage and improving health outcomes across all racial and ethnic groups.

While this review quantifies racial and ethnic disparities in COVID-19 vaccine uptake, these patterns must be understood within the broader context of systemic marginalization and structural racism. Vaccine inequities are not simply the result of individual choices, but are shaped by long-standing institutional and socio-political forces. In countries without such systemic oppression, disparities may be less pronounced or take different forms, which may partly explain the heterogeneity observed. As most included studies were from the USA, a nation with a well-documented history of racialized health inequities, our pooled estimates primarily reflect this context and may not generalize to other settings with different health systems and social dynamics.

Limitations

We acknowledge that our study has a few limitations. First, our review includes only English language papers. Hence some studies published in local journals in non-English language regions may have been missed. Second, while included studies have examined different vaccine doses, we summarized the results of all studies together to report the overall disparities in vaccine coverage during the COVID-19. Nevertheless, in the meta-analysis we performed subgroup analysis based on the number of vaccine doses. Third, self-reported information in cross-sectional studies may have been impacted by recall and social desirability biases [42]. Fourth, cross sectional studies have just provided descriptive comparison of the COVID-19 vaccine rates among different race groups. To provide an objective and accurate overview of the disparities, we presented the findings from descriptive studies separately from those that conducted statistical analyses to assess associations between racial and ethnic groups and COVID-19 vaccination rates in the descriptive synthesis section. Fifth, limited racial and ethnic details in the medical and demographic documentaries and lack of a uniform definition of race and ethnicity worldwide might have led to some level of discrepancies among studies [54]. To address this issue, we included only those racial and ethnic groups that were consistently and similarly defined across the individual studies. However, these categories do not encompass all racial and ethnic identities and may overlap with groups not included in the meta-analysis. For example, the "Other" and "Mixed race" categories were included when explicitly labeled as such in the source studies, although they may encompass heterogeneous subgroups. Despite this limitation, providing a descriptive synthesis of all reported racial and ethnic groups in the systematic review helps mitigate this issue and offers a more comprehensive understanding of vaccine disparities across diverse populations. Sixth, heterogeneity analysis revealed a statistically significant and high level of variation among the studies, which was expected given the context-specific nature of the topic. As the studies included in the meta-analysis were conducted in USA and UK, relied on administrative or registry data, and employed robust study designs, the primary sources of heterogeneity likely stem from differences in regional and local health policies, cultural attitudes toward vaccination, different study timelines, and variations in health system structures. Factors such as public trust in vaccines, social determinants of health, vaccine rollout strategies, and access to vaccination services may have contributed to this variability [52, 60]. To address this heterogeneity, we applied a random-effects model within the Bayesian meta-analysis framework and specified stronger priors to improve model fit. In addition, we provided a narrative synthesis of individual study findings to complement the quantitative results. Nonetheless, the pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution due to the high degree of heterogeneity observed. Seventh limitation is the geographic focus, with nearly all included studies conducted in USA and UK. This limits generalizability to other global contexts, especially low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where vaccine uptake is influenced by different structural, social, and healthcare factors. Future research should explore racial and ethnic disparities in vaccine uptake across diverse regions to guide equitable, context-specific interventions. Finally, although we used standardized JBI tools to assess study quality, these primarily focus on internal validity and may not fully capture variation in analytic methods. While most studies adjusted for basic sociodemographic factors, few outlined a clear approach to covariate selection, and structural or contextual variables were rarely included. This raises the possibility of residual confounding or overadjustment, especially in studies with extreme effect sizes. Despite high quality ratings, pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution given the inconsistencies in adjustment practices and limited transparency in statistical modeling.

Despite these limitations, our study offers comprehensive insights into racial and ethnic disparities in COVID-19 vaccine uptake and highlights important policy considerations for mitigating health inequities in future public health crises. Although the substantial heterogeneity limits the interpretability of bias detection tests and calls for cautious interpretation of pooled estimates, synthesizing the available evidence remains valuable. The meta-analytic findings complement the descriptive results and collectively contribute to a more nuanced and actionable understanding of vaccine equity during the pandemic.

Conclusion

Racial and ethnic minority groups, particularly Black populations, have been disproportionately affected by the COVID-19 pandemic and appear to have experienced lower vaccine uptake compared to White individuals. This systematic review highlights the persistence of these disparities and underscores the need for multifaceted interventions that address both population-level and health system barriers.

Targeted strategies are essential to identify and overcome the unique challenges faced by marginalized communities. Improving vaccine equity requires not only expanding access but also building trust through culturally responsive outreach and systemic reforms. Future research should explore the explanatory factors driving these disparities and expand investigations to diverse global settings, particularly LMICs, to inform context-specific policies.
