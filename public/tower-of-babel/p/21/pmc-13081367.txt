Effects of Tabata training combined with iron supplementation on proprioceptive acuity in young women with iron deficiency anemia: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mohamed Achraf Harrabi, Rahma Ayed, Mouna Turki, Wissal Boughattas, Thouraya Fendri, Fatma Chaari, Ines Mezghani, Choumous Kallel, Haithem Rebai, Fatma Ayadi, Sonia Sahli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProprioception is fundamental for accurate movements, balance, and postural control, ensuring stability and orientation in both static and dynamic tasks. Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) may impair proprioceptive acuity, thereby affecting motor control. As physical training is known to improve proprioception in both healthy and clinical populations, combining exercise with iron supplementation may offer additional benefits. This study aimed to explore the effects of 8-week Tabata training based on plyometric exercises combined with iron supplementation on proprioceptive acuity in young women with IDA.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial included young women with IDA (18–25 years) who were randomly allocated to a Tabata training group (TG; n = 18) or an untrained control group (UTG; n = 20). Both groups received iron supplementation (494.5 mg ferrous sulfate), while the TG additionally completed an eight-week Tabata-based plyometric training program. Hemoglobin (Hb) and serum ferritin levels were assessed, as well as, fatigue, attentional capacity, and proprioceptive acuity were evaluated using the Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI), an online simple reaction time test, and the weight discrimination test, respectively, at baseline and 72 h after the final intervention session.ResultsThe results of this study demonstrated significantly higher improvements post intervention in most proprioceptive acuity increments [0.4 kg (P = 0.003), 0.28 kg (P = 0.023). 0.11 kg (P < 0.001)], Hb levels (P = 0.03), as well as in general and physical fatigue (P < 0.001) and reduced motivation (P = 0.003) scores in TG compared to UTG.ConclusionsThe findings of this study revealed greater improvements in proprioceptive acuity in the TG compared to the UTG, highlighting the additional benefits of our combined training program compared to iron supplementation therapy alone, particularly on Hb concentrations, general and physical fatigue and motivation scores.Trial registrationThis study was registered retrospectively on pan African clinical trials registry (ID: PACTR202507672075403; Date of Registration: 02/07/2025).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-026-01614-y.Keywords: Proprioception, Dietary Iron, Exercise Therapy, Anemia, Iron Deficiency, Fatigue

Background

Proprioception represents the perception of the body and joints’ position, orientation and movement, as well as the sense of effort and force [1, 2]. It is essential for the execution of precise and coordinated movements, and maintenance of postural stability and motor control during static and dynamic activities [3]. It significantly impacts motor learning and rehabilitation processes [4]. A decline in proprioceptive function can impair perception of body positioning, which can impact joint biomechanics and neuromuscular control of the limbs, leading to balance issues and increased risk of falls [5, 6]. Proprioception may be affected by a wide range of factors. In this sense, iron deficiency anemia (IDA), defined as a low concentration of hemoglobin (Hb) in the blood that is insufficient to meet the individual’s physiological needs due to iron deficiency, is associated with proprioceptive acuity impairment [7]. Due to menstrual losses and restrictive diets aimed at weight control, women of reproductive age are at high risk of IDA [8]. In this context, nutritional status and hormonal fluctuations during menstrual phases, particularly menstruation, could exacerbate IDA and potentially further impair proprioception, since Hb and ferritin concentrations are linked to proprioceptive acuity [7]. Fatigue may represent a key mechanistic link between IDA and proprioceptive impairment. IDA has been shown to reduce physical capacity and muscle endurance due to insufficient muscle oxygenation, leading to increased muscle fatigue, which can alter muscle mechanoreceptor sensitivity, motor unit activation, and sensory-motor feedback processes involved in proprioceptive control [7]. Consequently, fatigue-related neuromuscular alterations may contribute to impaired proprioceptive acuity in this population. Moreover, a recent study found that iron supplementation with 160 mg elemental iron for 8 weeks could increase hemoglobin and ferritin concentrations, leading to improved iron stores, which may influence neural processes, enhancing attentional capacity and proprioceptive acuity [9]. However, while iron supplementation represents the standard treatment for IDA, its effects on sensory-motor function appear to be only partial.

On the hand, proprioception has been demonstrated to correlate with fatigue [7]. It is known that physical activity is an effective tool for improving cardiorespiratory fitness [10], and consequently delaying and reducing the sensation of fatigue [11]. Therefore, we can hypothesize that the combination of physical activity and iron supplementation could offer additional beneficial effects on proprioception in young women with IDA, compared to the standard treatment by iron supplementation alone. However, the practice of regular physical activity comes up against several barriers such as a perceived lack of time, and access to appropriate equipment and facilities [12]. The reduced time [12] and the related pleasure [13, 14] of certain interval training protocols, such as high-intensity interval training (HIIT) and sprint interval training (SIT), make them attractive from a public health point of view and reinforce the idea that these protocols can easily be adopted and maintained across diverse populations. Despite this reduction in time commitment, it has been widely demonstrated that interval training protocols yield comparable enhancements in cardiorespiratory fitness [15, 16], health outcomes [17, 18] and exercise performance [19] for both healthy and pathological populations, to traditional exercise methods (e.g., moderate or vigorous intensity continuous training).

The Tabata protocol presents a unique interval training approach consisting of 8 cycles of 20 s high intensity cycling at 170% of peak oxygen uptake (VO2max), interspaced with 10 s rests, that usually necessitates specialized laboratory equipment. However, a modified version of this protocol, incorporating whole-body exercises, has been shown to efficiently improve cardiorespiratory fitness and muscle endurance in women [20], delaying the onset of fatigue with no specialized equipment needed, eliminating barriers related to access to facilities [21].

In parallel, plyometric training performed over 6 to 8 weeks has consistently been shown to improve proprioceptive abilities across diverse populations. By enhancing neuromuscular activation and sensory feedback, plyometric exercises contribute to improved body awareness, motor control, and injury or fall prevention [22–24]. These findings suggest that combining plyometric exercises with a time-efficient interval training format may be particularly effective for enhancing proprioceptive function.

IDA is associated with impaired proprioceptive acuity, increased fatigue, and altered neuromuscular control [7], all of which may compromise postural stability and motor performance in young women [25]. While iron supplementation is essential for restoring hematological status [9, 26], the potential benefits of combining supplementation with structured physical training on sensory-motor outcomes remain unclear. Although iron supplementation and physical training have each been shown to independently influence fatigue, neuromuscular function, and proprioception, their combined effects on proprioceptive acuity in patients with IDA remain largely unexplored. Addressing this gap is particularly relevant given the need for accessible, time-efficient, and equipment-free interventions that can be easily implemented in this population.

Based on the above-mentioned considerations, we hypothesized that an 8-week iron supplementation program combined with a Tabata training protocol based on plyometric exercises would induce greater improvements in proprioceptive acuity in young women with IDA compared to iron supplementation alone. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study investigated the effects of a physical training program on proprioception in this population. Thus, the aim of the present study was to explore the effect of an 8-week Tabata training based on plyometric exercises versus simple iron supplementation on proprioceptive acuity in young women with IDA.

Methods

Study design

This study was a randomized controlled trial conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines and registered with the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (PACTR; ID: PACTR202507672075403). The trial was retrospectively registered due to administrative delays. Registration was initiated on 05 November 2023, prior to participant enrollment (trial start: 13 November 2023), and formally approved on 02 July 2025. The study protocol, including eligibility criteria, outcomes, and analysis plan, was defined a priori, and no changes were made after trial initiation.

Participants and experimental procedures

A total of 180 young women, aged between 18 and 25 years, volunteered for this study. All participants were recruited from the same regional accommodation and catering unit managed by the Tunisian vocational training agency. This ensured consistency in daily routines, including diet, sleep patterns, and physical activity. The daily dietary plans were supervised by a qualified dietitian/nutritionist to standardize nutrient intake. Although the power analysis indicated that 14 participants per group would be sufficient, a total of 180 volunteers were initially recruited, as not all were expected to have IDA. Only 53 participants met the strict eligibility criteria for confirmed IDA and were included in the final sample. Recruiting a large number of volunteers ensured that the final groups included an adequate number of participants with confirmed anemia, maintaining the study’s statistical power and validity.

Participants were eligible if they were aged 18–25 years and diagnosed with IDA, defined according to World Health Organization criteria as Hb < 12 g/dL and serum ferritin < 15 µg/L. Participants also had to provide informed consent. Women were excluded if they smoked, were pregnant, or had severe anemia (Hb < 8 g/dL). Other exclusion criteria included a history of chronic inflammation (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis or gout), malabsorption syndromes, neurological disorders, or other significant medical conditions (cardiovascular, renal, hepatic, or systemic diseases). Women with a family history of thalassemia or sickle cell anemia, known hypersensitivity to iron supplements, or active internal bleeding (e.g., hemorrhoids, peptic ulcers) were also excluded. All participants underwent a comprehensive clinical evaluation by a physician to ensure that these operational criteria were met, guaranteeing consistency and reproducibility of participant selection. Only participants who completed the full intervention program and the final evaluation session were included in the analysis (per-protocol approach). Those who did not complete the intervention or missed scheduled assessments were excluded.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups: (i) a control group that received iron supplementation only without any physical training (untrained group, UTG), or (ii) an experimental group that followed Tabata training alongside iron supplementation (trained group, TG). The randomization sequence was generated by an independent external researcher using simple randomization. Group assignments were placed in sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes prepared by the same independent researcher, ensuring that allocation concealment was fully maintained until interventions were assigned. Participants were evaluated at two time points: (i) pre-intervention (24 h before starting the intervention) and (ii) post-intervention (72 h after completing the 8-week program). Examiners conducting outcome assessments were blinded to group assignments. All collected data were coded and anonymized so that data analysts were also blinded to group allocation. The study was therefore double-blind at the level of assessors and data analysts, while participants were aware of their own group assignment due to the nature of the intervention. This ensured that both outcome assessment and analysis were conducted independently of knowledge of group assignments. All these measurements were taken in separate testing rooms in the participants’ accommodation center. In addition, complete blood count (CBC), C-reactive protein (CRP), and serum ferritin level were assessed in the biochemistry laboratory department of the university hospital Habib Bourguiba. Ethical approval and informed consent procedures are detailed in the Declarations section.

Laboratory analysis

Blood samples were collected through antecubital venous sampling following a fasting period of 12 h. All venous blood samples were collected under standardized conditions, in the morning between 07:00 and 09:00 a.m., and during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle (between day 15 and day 28) to minimize circadian and hormonal variability, which can affect ferritin and Hb results. A CBC was conducted utilizing a Sysmex® XN-1000 hematology analyzer. Serum ferritin (reagent reference: 03737551190) was assessed using a Roche Cobas® e 601. The determination of CRP (reagent reference: 04956842190) was carried out on a Roche Cobas® c 501. All samples were analyzed in the morning on the same day as collection.

Proprioceptive acuity assessment

The weight discrimination test, which evaluates the proprioceptive system’s ability to detect changes in weight, was employed to assess proprioceptive acuity. This psychophysical paradigm is widely used in experimental and clinical research to examine the sensitivity of the proprioceptive system and to detect subtle differences in sensorimotor perception across tasks, populations, or experimental conditions [7, 9, 27, 28]. This test was conducted using a leg-extension chair equipped with a lever system for free-weight loading, with the participants’ knees positioned at a 90°angle. The weight of the unloaded lever was considered as the standard (2.5 kg). A collection of metal metric weights (0.11, 0.28, 0.4, and 0.5 kg) was utilized to progressively increase the standard lever weight. These comparison weights represented increments of 4% (2.61 kg), 11% (2.78 kg), 16% (2.9 kg), and 20% (3 kg) relative to the standard weight. The task required participants to perform simple lifting movements with their dominant leg (the leg they prefer for kicking a ball) to compare the weights. Each participant underwent a series of 10 familiarization trials, consisting of 5 easier trials with 0.5 kg increments and 5 more challenging trials with 0.28 kg increments. The formal testing session started once the participants reported felt comfortable with the task. They were blindfolded, and their ears were blocked to prevent any auditory cues. The discrimination protocol followed a 2-alternative forced-choice design, where each trial included two weight presentations: the standard (2.5 kg) versus the comparison weight (for instance, the standard weight plus a 0.4 kg increment). After tapping their knees, participants were instructed to lift each weight by extending and then flexing their knees, and to indicate which weight they perceived as heavier (the first or the second weight). The order of presentation (standard versus comparison) was pseudorandomized, with both alternatives having an equal probability within a set of 14 trials. The score for each weight increment was determined by calculating the percentage of correct responses (number of correct responses ÷ 14) × 100. Consistent with typical discrimination paradigms, four consecutive blocks were conducted, each presenting increasing levels of difficulty (from 0.5 kg to 0.11 kg increments), resulting in a total of four blocks comprising 14 trials each. Test–retest reliability analyses performed in the present study indicated good to excellent reliability across the different weight increments. Specifically, reliability was good for the smallest increments (ICC = 0.77 for 0.11 kg and ICC = 0.83 for 0.28 kg) and excellent for the larger increments (ICC = 0.95 for 0.4 kg and ICC = 0.94 for 0.5 kg).

Attentional capacity assessment

The attentional capacity of participants was assessed through an online Simple Reaction Time (SRT) test (http://humanbenchmark.com/tests/reactiontime/index.php). This assessment involves measuring the duration it takes for a participant to respond to a visual stimulus with a single action. The SRT test is a validated tool for assessing basic attentional processing speed and visuomotor responsiveness in sports science contexts [29]. Participants were instructed to click on the screen as quickly as possible, using their dominant hand, immediately upon the stimulus changing to green. Subsequently, a value was presented in ms, reflecting the response time. The SRT score is calculated as the average of five consecutive trials. The SRT demonstrated excellent test–retest reliability in our sample, with a high intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.98, indicating that this measure was stable across repeated testing sessions.

Fatigue assessment

The evaluation of fatigue was conducted via the French version of the Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI) (Supplementary Material 1) [30]. This inventory is recognized for its validity, reliability, and practicality as a self-administered questionnaire specifically for women suffering from anemia [31]. It comprises 20 questions aimed at measuring fatigue across various dimensions. The inventory includes 5 categories (with 4 questions each) that address general fatigue, physical fatigue, mental fatigue, diminished activity, and motivation. The score for each category is derived from the total of the scores associated with the questions within that category. Elevated scores reflect increased levels of fatigue.

Intervention program

In both groups, each participant consumed 247.25 mg of dried ferrous sulfate tablets, which correspond to 80 mg of elemental iron taken twice daily (totaling 160 mg of elemental iron per day). The tablets were administered in the morning on an empty stomach, at least one hour before breakfast, and in the evening before sleep, at least one hour after the last meal, throughout the entire 8-week program duration. All participants were explicitly instructed to refrain from engaging in any additional physical activity or structured exercise outside the prescribed intervention during the study period.

The UTG received oral iron supplementation throughout the 8-week period without participating in any physical training. In addition to iron supplementation, participants in the TG engaged in a highly low-volume HIIT regimen, known as the “Tabata Protocol,” for 8 weeks. Following the FITT principle, the program was carefully structured to ensure clarity and reproducibility. The Frequency of training was four sessions per week (Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, and Friday), scheduled between 5 and 7 pm to fit participants’ daily routines. The Intensity was supramaximal, with participants performing high-intensity intervals, executing the maximum number of repetitions during each 20-second exercise interval and reporting their perceived exertion immediately after each interval. Regarding Time, each training day consisted of a 4-minute main Tabata set, composed of eight cycles of 20 s of exercise alternated with 10 s of rest, preceded by a warm-up that involved ascending and descending five flights of stairs, and followed by a cool-down period including relaxation and stretching exercises. The Type of exercises included four bodyweight movements, including squat jump, split jump, skater jump, and burpees, with only one type of exercise performed per training day. These exercises were chosen for their simplicity, accessibility, and effectiveness, requiring no specialized equipment. Participants were required to attend at least 80% of all training days, corresponding to a minimum of 26 days, and those missing three consecutive training days were excluded from the analysis. All training sessions were conducted in a gym located within the accommodation center and were supervised by a physician and led by a coach with 10 years of experience.

Perceived exertion assessment

Rated perceived exertion (RPE) was evaluated using the Borg scale [32], which is a validated and reliable tool for assessing perceived exercise intensity, correlating consistently with physiological markers of effort [33]. The Borg RPE scale includes 15 distinct RPE rating levels. This scale ranges from 6, indicating “very very easy,” to 20, denoting “very very difficult.” The RPE assessment was performed by displaying a visual cue card during the 10-second rest period that followed each exercise interval, along with the question: “What level of effort are you exerting at this moment?“. Borg scale was specifically developed to correspond with the typical breathing and heart rate of an adult [32].

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated, a priori, using G*Power (version 3.1.9.2; University of Kiel, Kiel, Germany). The effect size (f) of 0.535 was calculated from the partial η² reported in a previous study [9]. The computation used an alpha level of 0.05, statistical power of 0.95, a correlation of 0.50 between repeated measures, and a non-sphericity correction (ε) of 1. The estimated total sample size required was 14 participants. Although participant dropouts occurred during the study (TG: n = 8; UTG: n = 7), a total of 38 participants were included in the final analyses (TG = 18; UTG = 20). Based on this final sample size, the achieved statistical power exceeded 0.95, confirming that the study remained adequately powered despite attrition. Therefore, no additional sample size adjustment was required to account for dropouts.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analyses of the results were performed using IBM SPSS® Statistics 25. To evaluate normality and homogeneity of variance, the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test were utilized, respectively. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. For participant characteristics comparisons, T-tests were used. Attentional capacity, proprioceptive acuity, fatigue scores, and Hb and serum ferritin concentrations were examined through two-way ANOVAs [group (TG vs. UTG) × session (pre- vs. post-intervention)], with repeated measures. Additionally, the effect sizes were calculated using partial eta-square (ηp2). Test–retest reliability of proprioceptive acuity and attentional capacity was assessed using a two-way mixed-effects intraclass correlation coefficient with absolute agreement (ICC (3,1)). ICC values were interpreted as follows: values < 0.50 indicate poor reliability, values between 0.50 and 0.75 indicate moderate reliability, values between 0.75 and 0.90 indicate good reliability, and values > 0.90 indicate excellent reliability [34]. The statistical significance threshold was set at P = 0.05. The partial eta-square values were classified as follows: small effect (0.01 < ηp2 < 0.06), medium effect (0.06 < ηp2 < 0.14), and large effect (ηp2 > 0.14) [35]. Effect size values of Cohen’s d and Hedges’ g were interpreted as follows: very small effect (0.01–0.19), Small effect (0.20–0.49), medium effect (0.50–0.79), large effect (0.80–1.19), very large effect (1.20–1.99), Huge / extremely large effect (≥ 2.00) [35, 36].

Results

Participants and characteristics

The recruitment session started on November 1 and ended on November 11, 2023. The participants in this study were followed throughout the intervention period (from November 13, 2023, to January 8, 2024) and were evaluated (final session) on January 11, 2024. Figure 1 illustrates the flow of participants in this study. The research included 38 young women diagnosed with IDA. Data from 18 participants in the TG (age, 22.28 ± 2.03 years; height, 1.64 ± 0.04 m; weight, 59.02 ± 5.72 kg; and body mass index (BMI), 21.83 ± 1.37) and 20 participants in the UTG (n = 20; age, 22.15 ± 2.16 years; height, 1.62 ± 0.07 m; weight, 58.69 ± 8.54 kg; and BMI, 22.29 ± 2.4) were included in the final analyses. All analyses were conducted according to the original group assignments. There were no statistically significant differences in age, height, weight, or BMI between the two groups.

Concerning the 0.4 kg increment, a significant effect of group (F = 8.51; P = 0.006; ηp2 = 0.19) and session (F = 6.7; P = 0.014; ηp2 = 0.16) factors on proprioceptive acuity values was revealed. These values were significantly higher post-intervention compared to pre-intervention only for the TG (P = 0.011). Concerning the group effect, during the pre-intervention session, there were no significant differences in proprioceptive acuity values between the two groups. However, post-intervention, these values were significantly (P = 0.003) higher in the TG compared to UTG (Table 1).

Regarding the 0.28 increment, significant effect of group (F = 4.44; P = 0.042; ηp2 = 0.11) and session (F = 10.73; P = 0.002; ηp2 = 0.23) factors was found. These values were significantly higher post-intervention compared to pre-intervention in the TG only (P = 0.013). Concerning the group effect, in pre-intervention session, there were no significant differences in proprioceptive acuity values between groups. However, post-intervention, these values were significantly (P = 0.023) higher in the TG compared to UTG (Table 1).

Concerning the 0.11 kg increment, significant effect of group (F = 4.36; P = 0.044; ηp2 = 0.11) and session (F = 93.41; P < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.72) factors, and significant group × session interaction (F = 9.81; P = 0.003; ηp2 = 0.21) were revealed. These values were significantly (P < 0.001) higher post-intervention compared to pre-intervention for both groups. Concerning the group effect, during pre-intervention session, there was no significant difference in proprioceptive acuity values between groups. However, post-intervention, these values were significantly (P < 0.001) higher in the TG compared to UTG (Table 1).

Hb level

Concerning Hb levels, a significant effect of the session (F = 291.19; p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.89) was found. These levels were significantly higher (P < 0.001) post-intervention compared to pre-intervention for both groups. There was no significant difference in Hb values between groups in pre-intervention. However, post-intervention Hb values were significantly (P < 0.05) higher in the TG compared to UTG (Table 1).

Serum ferritin level

For serum ferritin levels, a significant effect of the session (F = 190.99; P < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.84) was revealed. Bonferroni test showed that these values were significantly higher (P < 0.001) post-intervention compared to baseline for both groups. For the group effect, there was no significant difference in ferritin concentrations between groups in either session (Table 1).

Attentional capacity

ANOVA showed significant effect of the session (F = 175.24; P < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.83) for SRT values. Bonferroni test showed that these values were significantly lower (P < 0.001) post-intervention compared to baseline for both groups. There was no significant difference in SRT values between groups in either session (Table 1).

Fatigue

For general fatigue, ANOVA revealed a significant effect of group (F = 6.74; P = 0.014; ηp2 = 0.16) and session (F = 433.2; P < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.92) factors, as well as significant group × session interaction (F = 8.94; P = 0.005; ηp2 = 0.2) on general fatigue scores. These scores were significantly (P < 0.001) lower post-intervention compared to pre-intervention for both groups. There was no significant difference in general fatigue scores between groups during the pre-intervention session. However, post-intervention, these scores were significantly (P < 0.001) lower in the TG compared to the UTG (Table 1).

Concerning physical fatigue, ANOVA indicated a significant effect of the session factor (F = 267.24; P < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.88) and a significant between group × session interaction (F = 10.6; P = 0.002; ηp2 = 0.23) on physical fatigue scores. Post-intervention, physical fatigue scores were significantly lower for both groups compared to pre-intervention (P < 0.001). While there was no significant difference in physical fatigue scores between groups before the intervention, post-intervention scores were significantly lower in the TG compared to UTG (P < 0.001) (Table 1).

For mental fatigue, ANOVA revealed a significant effect of the session factor (F = 260.08; P < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.88). Mental fatigue scores were significantly lower for both groups post-intervention, compared to pre-intervention (P < 0.001). There was no significant difference in general fatigue scores between the two groups during both sessions (Table 1).

Regarding reduced activity, ANOVA revealed significant effect of the session factor (F = 164.02; P < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.82). These scores were significantly lower post-intervention compared to pre-intervention for both groups (P < 0.001). There was no significant difference between the two groups during both sessions (Table 1).

For reduced motivation, ANOVA revealed a significant effect of the session factor (F = 97.03; P < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.73) and significant group × session interaction (F = 7.39; P = 0.01; ηp2 = 0.17). These scores were significantly lower post-intervention compared to pre-intervention for both groups (P < 0.001). No significant difference between groups was found at baseline. However, post-intervention, these scores were significantly lower in the TG compared to UTG (P = 0.003) (Table 1).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to explore the effect of an 8-week Tabata training based on plyometric exercises versus simple iron supplementation on proprioceptive acuity in young women with IDA. This study revealed that proprioceptive acuity improved significantly only in the most challenging increment discrimination condition following 8 weeks of iron supplementation. Nevertheless, the enhancements were greater and covered most of the increments in the weight discrimination protocol when iron supplementation was combined with Tabata training based on plyometric exercises. This study also demonstrated improvements in Hb and serum ferritin concentrations, attentional capacity and all fatigue scores, regardless of the intervention type. These post-intervention improvements were significantly better in the TG compared to UTG. Moreover, the magnitude of the within-group effect sizes indicates that both interventions led to meaningful improvements over time. The between-group effect sizes observed post-intervention further highlight that the TG experienced greater improvements than the UTG, suggesting that adding Tabata-based plyometric training to iron supplementation provided additional benefits in sensorimotor control. These patterns across both within- and between-group comparisons support the practical relevance of the observed changes in young women with IDA.

The proprioceptive improvements in the UTG after 8 weeks of iron supplementation alone are consistent with a recent study [9]. These improvements in proprioception may be linked to increased iron bioavailability, which probably influenced neural processes such as neurotransmitter synthesis and homeostasis (e.g., dopamine), myelination, synaptogenesis, and nerve impulses conduction. The enhanced attentional capacity observed in UTG after 8 weeks of iron treatment supports this hypothesis, as iron supplementation has been shown to reverse nerve conduction alterations and improve attention in patients with IDA [9, 37].

The observed improvements in proprioceptive acuity following iron therapy may also be partially attributed to reduced levels of fatigue. A significant association between general, physical, and mental fatigue and impaired proprioception in young women with IDA has been reported [7]. Furthermore, fatigue may impair the sensitivity of muscle mechanoreceptors, particularly those responsive to tension. This may result in reduced responsiveness to mechanical stimuli, ultimately affecting proprioceptive accuracy [38, 39].

Interestingly, these improvements were observed with both interventions but were greater with combined physical training and iron supplementation than with iron supplementation alone. However, our results showed no additional improvement in attentional capacity with Tabata training combined with iron supplementation compared to iron supplementation alone. This confirms the beneficial effect of improving iron stores through supplementation on neurological functions.

The available literature examining proprioception in the context of IDA remains limited. To the best of our knowledge, only one study has investigated the effect of iron supplementation on proprioception [9], and no studies have examined the effects of physical exercise combined with iron supplementation on proprioceptive outcomes. Therefore, the present study contributes novel insights by addressing this gap and extending current knowledge in this area.

It is evident that proprioceptive decline in women with IDA was associated with high levels of physical and general fatigue induced by low Hb and serum ferritin levels [7]. To reduce fatigue, interventions based on physical exercise were implemented to improve physical capacity and delay fatigue. In this regard, the training type proposed in this study may induce central and peripheral adaptations, including improvements in aerobic capacity and anaerobic performance [40–42]. These adaptations may enhance oxidative energy production, reduce reliance on anaerobic metabolism during high-intensity exercise, and thereby contribute to improved exercise tolerance and a potential delay in the onset of exhaustion [43].

Furthermore, plyometric exercises may further enhance proprioceptive acuity compared with iron supplementation alone. Indeed, plyometric training imposes substantial demands on the neuromuscular system, as the highly dynamic jump-landing tasks involved require rapid force production, precise inter-muscular coordination, and enhanced sensorimotor control [44, 45]. Additionally, the incorporation of unilateral and bilateral jump/landing exercises in different directions can provide adequate training stimuli for the proprioception [46]. Such effects on the neuromuscular system could further elucidate the improvements in proprioceptive acuity observed in the TG compared to the UTG.

Moreover, Hb concentrations post-intervention were significantly higher in the TG compared to UTG. This result may explain the greater improvement in proprioceptive acuity observed in trained women, given the association between Hb concentration and proprioception [7]. It also highlights the beneficial effect of this type of physical training on erythropoiesis when iron intake is adequate. Improved hematopoietic microenvironment via increased osteoblast activity, hormonal and cytokine modulation regulating hematopoietic stem cell function, erythropoietin receptor affinity changes, and elevated transferrin receptor expression may explain these findings [47].

The present findings highlight several strengths and practical implications. This study is among the first to examine the combined effects of short-term, high-intensity plyometric training and iron supplementation on proprioception, fatigue, and hematological outcomes in women with IDA. The sensitive proprioceptive assessment and inclusion of fatigue and attentional measures provide a comprehensive evaluation of functional improvements beyond hematological changes. Practically, these results support recommending that women with IDA, in addition to receiving iron supplementation, should reduce sedentary behavior and incorporate tolerable, enjoyable, and structured exercise programs, such as Tabata-based plyometric training, into their routine. This combined approach may enhance proprioception, reduce fatigue, improve adherence to treatment, and optimize functional recovery, daily activities, injury prevention, and overall quality of life. These recommendations may be particularly relevant for clinicians, rehabilitation specialists, and public health practitioners.

This study has some limitations that should be considered in future research. First, using an objective tool to monitor exercise intensity, such as a heart rate monitor, would strengthen the accuracy of the training protocol. In addition, due to ethical and legal constraints, our study did not include control groups receiving neither iron supplementation (or placebo) nor training, or undergoing training alone (or training with placebo). Such groups would have allowed clearer separation of intervention effects and better consideration of the influence of participants’ awareness on subjective outcomes like fatigue. Although dietary intake was partially controlled through standardized meals consumed in identical portions (breakfast, lunch, and dinner) at the same canteen under the supervision of a qualified dietitian/nutritionist, precise quantitative assessment of nutrient intake was not performed. Similarly, sleep quality was not objectively measured despite comparable daily schedules ensured by recruitment from the same accommodation center. Future studies should include detailed dietary and sleep assessments to further minimize these potential confounders. Moreover, the relatively short duration of the 8-week intervention and the absence of long-term follow-up limit the ability to determine whether the observed effects are maintained over time and should be addressed in future studies. Finally, investigating the mechanisms through which this form of physical exercise stimulates erythropoiesis, as well as the potential neural and neuromuscular adaptations in women with IDA, could offer valuable insights to support our proposed explanations.

Conclusion

In young women with IDA, participants who completed an 8-week Tabata-based plyometric training program combined with iron supplementation demonstrated greater improvements in proprioceptive acuity than those who received iron supplementation alone. These changes were observed alongside higher Hb concentrations and lower general and physical fatigue scores in the TG. Taken together, these findings appear to be associated with more favorable proprioceptive and fatigue-related outcomes compared with iron supplementation alone.
