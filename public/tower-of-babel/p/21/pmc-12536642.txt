Validation of the modified BODE index using the activity domain of St George's Respiratory Questionnaire
Authors: Jong Hun Park, Ha Jin Lee, Chin Kook Rhee, Yong Bum Park, Jae Ha Lee, Hyoung Kyu Yoon, Cheon Woong Choi, Seong Yong Lim, Kwang Ha Yoo, Joon Young Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe traditional BODE (Body-mass Index, Obstruction, Dyspnoea and Exercise) index can be challenging in routine practice. This study introduced the modified BODE (mBODE) index that replaces the exercise component with the activity domain of the St. George's Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ). Our study aimed to evaluate its relationship with baseline clinical characteristics and its predictive performance for exacerbations and mortality compared to the original index.MethodsData were extracted from the Korean COPD Subgroup Study cohort. The activity domain of the SGRQ was divided into quartiles, replacing the exercise component of the BODE index to define the mBODE index. Clinical outcomes were compared between quartiles of the BODE and mBODE indices. We analysed the associations of both scores with 1-year exacerbations and all-cause mortality.ResultsThis study enrolled 1765 individuals, and both scores were associated with moderate-to-severe and severe exacerbations. In the analysis of future exacerbation frequency, the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and log-likelihood values indicated that the mBODE index had better predictive performance than the BODE index in both models. In the analysis of the presence of future exacerbation, the AIC showed that the mBODE index had superior predictive performance compared to the BODE index. Furthermore, both indices predicted all-cause mortality in individuals with COPD (BODE: adjusted hazard ratio (aHR=2.53, p<0.001; mBODE: aHR=2.00, p<0.01).ConclusionsThe mBODE index reflected the clinical characteristics and showed association with exacerbations and mortality comparable to those of the traditional BODE index.

Introduction

COPD is characterised by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow obstruction [1]. It is caused by airway inflammation and tissue damage resulting from interactions between genetic and environmental factors. The damaged tissue undergoes bronchial remodeling, leading to thickening of the bronchial wall during recovery that exacerbates the progression of COPD [2]. According to the World Health Organization, COPD is the third leading cause of death worldwide, causing 3.23 million deaths in 2019 [3]. Owing to factors such as air pollution, COPD is expected to become the leading cause of death by 2050, highlighting its growing clinical importance [4, 5].

The Body-mass index, Obstruction, Dyspnoea and Exercise (BODE) index, a scoring system developed to evaluate clinical outcomes in individuals with COPD, is calculated based on the scores for four parameters: body mass index (BMI), airflow obstruction (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)), dyspnoea (modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnoea scale) and exercise capacity (6-min walk distance (6MWD)) [6]. Celli et al. [6] introduced the BODE index, which can assess mortality risk more accurately than methods that rely solely on measuring FEV1. Subsequent studies confirmed that the BODE index can be used not only to predict mortality risk but also to predict risk of hospitalisation due to COPD [7, 8].

However, there are limitations when evaluating exercise capacity with the 6MWD via the BODE index. The 6MWD can differ according to patient compliance, temporary health conditions and sex. The BODE index cannot properly evaluate individuals with severe comorbidities, including musculoskeletal, cardiovascular and psychiatric diseases. Additionally, a lack of healthcare resources can prevent performance of the 6MWD test [9]. To overcome these limitations, previous studies have attempted to use alternative measures of exercise capacity to modify the BODE index; however, these measurements were of limited clinical use [10–12].

In this study, we replaced the exercise capacity component of the BODE index with the activity domain of the St. George's Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) to develop the modified BODE (mBODE) index. Our study aimed to investigate the correlation between the mBODE index and baseline clinical characteristics and to evaluate its predictive performance for exacerbations and mortality, in comparison to the traditional BODE index.

Methods

Study population and data collection

The data for this study were from the Korean COPD Subgroup Study (KOCOSS) cohort, a multicentre prospective observational COPD cohort study in Korea [13, 14]. Data collection began in April 2012, involving 55 medical institutions nationwide, and we extracted the data in March 2023. The participants included in this study were aged ≥40 years, with a post-bronchodilator FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio <0.70. Clinical data were collected using case report forms completed by physicians or trained nurses. We also merged the dataset with mortality data obtained from Statistics Korea.

Clinical parameters were assessed at each visit, including demographic details such as age, sex, BMI and smoking habits. Symptoms and exercise capacity at baseline were evaluated using the mMRC dyspnoea scale, COPD assessment test (CAT), SGRQ and 6MWD test. Mental health status, i.e., depression and anxiety, was evaluated using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), respectively. A BDI score ≥14 indicated depression [15], while a BAI score ≥8 indicated anxiety [16]. Radiological findings including the presence of emphysema or bronchiectasis on chest computed tomography (CT) were assessed by a radiologist. Lung function parameters, including FEV1, FVC, the FEV1/FVC ratio, diffusion capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and residual volume to total lung capacity ratio (RV/TLC), were also measured at baseline.

BODE and mBODE measurement

The BODE index is calculated by summing the scores of the four components – BMI, airflow obstruction (post-bronchodilator FEV1), dyspnoea (mMRC dyspnoea scale) and exercise capacity (6MWD) – following the method outlined by Celli et al. [6]. The mBODE index was calculated by replacing the 6MWD with the activity domain of the SGRQ. The activity domain scores were divided into four quartiles based on their distribution in the study population, with cut-off values at 53.1, 30.2 and 14.9. These quartiles were assigned scores as follows: 3 points for the top 25%, 2 points for the 25th to 50th percentiles, 1 point for the 50th to 75th percentiles, and 0 points for the 75th to 100th percentiles (supplementary table S1). The mBODE index was then calculated by replacing the 6MWD in the BODE index with the SGRQ activity domain score.

Definition of exacerbation

The presence and frequency of exacerbations were measured prospectively over 1 year. Exacerbation was defined as acute worsening of respiratory symptoms that required additional therapy, including systemic corticosteroid or antibiotics [17]. A moderate exacerbation was defined as an exacerbation requiring outpatient treatment, whereas a severe exacerbation was defined as an exacerbation requiring an emergency room visit or hospitalisation [18].

Statistical analysis

Individuals were categorised into quartiles based on their BODE and mBODE index scores to assess differences in clinical characteristics among the groups. Continuous data from individuals in each group were analysed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), while categorial data were analysed using chi-square tests.

Negative binomial regression models were constructed to compare the frequencies of moderate-to-severe and severe exacerbations according to the BODE and mBODE scores over the 1-year follow-up period. Adjustment variables included individual age, sex, smoking history and the presence of exacerbation in the year before follow-up. The predictive performance of each index for moderate-to-severe and severe exacerbations was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and log-likelihood values. We evaluated the calibration of the negative binomial model by calculating Probability Integral Transform (PIT) values and examining their distribution using histograms. Multivariate logistic regression models were constructed to compare the rates of moderate-to-severe and severe exacerbations in the 1-year follow-up period. Adjustment variables included individual age, sex, smoking history and the presence of exacerbation in the year before follow-up. To assess model fit, AIC values were calculated, and area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curves were used to determine predictive performance. Calibration of the logistic regression models was assessed through calibration curves and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Furthermore, we performed survival analysis to examine the differences in all-cause mortality across quartiles of the BODE and mBODE scores. Log-rank tests were used to identify significant differences between quartiles, and Cox regression models were used to calculate adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs), with age, sex and smoking history included as covariates.

Results

Individual characteristics according to the BODE and mBODE scores

Of the 2623 individuals included in this study, BODE scores were calculated for 1765 individuals and mBODE scores were calculated for 638 individuals. Supplementary figure S1 shows the distribution of individuals across the quartiles of both indices. Supplementary table S2 and table 1 show the differences in clinical characteristics according to the quartiles of the BODE and mBODE indices. Dyspnoea, health-related quality of life and exercise capacity were consistently worse in individuals with higher BODE and mBODE scores. Individuals with higher scores for both indices were more likely to have a history of asthma, more frequently suffered from depression and anxiety, and showed more evidence of emphysema or bronchiectasis on chest CT. In the analysis of pulmonary function test results, FEV1, FVC and the FEV1/FVC ratio were lower in individuals with higher BODE or mBODE indices. Moreover, higher BODE and mBODE indices were associated with lower DLCO and RV/TLC, which represent the levels of air-trapping. The proportion of individuals with a history of exacerbation during the year before baseline also increased with both indices.

Associations of BODE and mBODE scores with exacerbation

We analysed exacerbation frequency using negative binomial regression models (table 2). A higher BODE index was significantly associated with higher rates of moderate-to-severe (incidence rate ratio (IRR)=1.27, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.21–1.35, p<0.001) and severe (IRR=1.48, 95% CI 1.31–1.68, p<0.001) exacerbation. Correspondingly, a higher mBODE index was also significantly associated with higher rates of moderate-to-severe (IRR=1.31, 95% CI 1.20–1.44, p<0.001) and severe (IRR=1.47, 95% CI 1.23–1.77, p<0.001) exacerbation.

Discussion

This study analysed clinical characteristics, exacerbation risk and mortality in individuals with COPD according to the BODE and mBODE indices. Individuals with higher scores on both indices had worse dyspnoea, lower quality of life and mental health, reduced lung function, and an increased history of exacerbations at baseline. Over the 1-year follow-up period, both exacerbation frequency and incidence increased with the index scores. Both indices demonstrated a stronger predictive performance with severe exacerbations than moderate-to-severe exacerbations as evidenced by higher area under the curve values and increased miscalibration in predicting moderate-to-severe events. Notably, the mBODE index demonstrated predictive performance for exacerbation risk and frequency, comparable to that of the classic BODE index, suggesting that it may serve as a practical alternative in clinical settings where conducting the 6MWT is challenging. Furthermore, both indices consistently predicted all-cause mortality.

The BODE index integrates multiple domains to assess the outcomes of individuals with COPD. Introduced by Celli et al. [6], the BODE index addresses the limitation of relying solely on FEV1 by incorporating additional factors that influence COPD progression and mortality risk. This multidimensional approach enables the BODE index to capture both respiratory and non-respiratory impacts, reflecting the systemic nature of COPD and overall disease burden. Studies have shown that the BODE index can predict exacerbation frequency and hospitalisation risk [19–21]. Furthermore, a higher index value is associated with a poorer quality of life and greater healthcare resource utilisation [21–23]. The predictive value of the BODE index across a range of COPD outcomes has established it as a valuable clinical tool aiding risk stratification and tailored management of individuals with COPD.

The classic BODE index was associated with clinical outcomes in individuals with COPD by integrating BMI, post-bronchodilator FEV1, the mMRC dyspnoea scale and the 6MWD. However, performing the 6MWD requires sufficient space, specific resources (such as supplemental oxygen and medications) and the presence of medical staff to address potential emergencies [24]. Absolute contraindications to the 6MWD include unstable angina or myocardial infarction within the previous month, while relative contraindications include a resting heart rate exceeding 120 bpm, a systolic blood pressure over 180 mmHg and a diastolic blood pressure over 100 mmHg. Individuals with these risk factors may be at increased risk of arrhythmias or cardiovascular collapse during the test. Moreover, given that most individuals with COPD are elderly and have a high prevalence of musculoskeletal diseases, such as osteoarthritis, performing the 6MWD can be challenging, potentially leading to unreliable results [25].

Considering these limitations of the 6MWD, this study introduced the mBODE index, which replaces the 6MWD component with the SGRQ activity domain score, a patient-reported outcome (PRO) measure. PROs gather information directly from individuals, including descriptions of their symptoms, satisfaction with care, and the physical, mental and social impacts of disease or treatment [26]. Since PROs do not require physical activity, there are no specific contraindications, and the risk of adverse effects is minimal [27]. By replacing the 6MWD with a PRO, the mBODE index enables assessment in a broader range of clinical settings, including local clinics lacking specialised facilities. Although alternative functional measures such as the one-minute sit-to-stand test may require fewer resources than the 6MWD, and the SGRQ can also be time-consuming, the SGRQ offers notable advantages: it is patient-centred, requires no physical exertion (thus avoiding limitations associated with orthopaedic or neurological conditions) and reduces the need for close healthcare supervision to mitigate risks such as trauma.

However, PROs have the limitation of relying on individuals’ subjective evaluations, which can be influenced by various factors. Psychological states like anxiety and depression may lead individuals to over- or underestimate symptoms, and environmental factors at the time of testing can affect responses, potentially creating inconsistencies in results obtained from the same individual over time [28]. Furthermore, individuals with cognitive impairment may have difficulty recognising and expressing their symptoms accurately [29].

In our analysis, the mBODE index was superior compared to the traditional BODE index in predicting both exacerbation risk and frequency. These findings suggest that the mBODE index is not only an efficient alternative but also a more precise tool for identifying high-risk individuals, particularly those prone to frequent exacerbation. Given that early identification of exacerbation risk is crucial in COPD management, the improved predictive performance of the mBODE index could be beneficial for tailoring interventions and optimising resource allocation in individuals with COPD [17].

This study has several strengths. First, it is based on nationwide COPD cohort data, with a sufficiently large number of individuals enrolled across the country. This broad sampling enhances the validity of the results and facilitates the application of the findings to the general population. Moreover, the mBODE index not only simplifies the assessment process but also shows improved predictive performance for exacerbation risk over the BODE index. This makes the mBODE index suitable for use in a wider range of healthcare settings, including primary care hospitals where resources may be limited. Furthermore, the mBODE index had comparable predictive performance in predicting mortality, which is one of the most important outcomes of the classic BODE index.

There are several limitations to this study. First, as most individuals enrolled in the KOCOSS cohort were treated in tertiary hospitals, our data may not represent the entire COPD population. Therefore, the generalisability of the mBODE index may be limited, especially in primary hospitals. Second, since all individuals were South Korean, the mBODE index must be validated in different racial or national populations. Third, the cohort included high proportions of smokers and males; while adjustments were made for sex and smoking history, the characteristics of female and nonsmoker populations may differ from those of the general COPD population. Fourth, although our model demonstrates promise, external validation in independent cohorts should be performed in future studies. Fifth, the quartile cut-off values used for the SGRQ activity domain scores and both BODE indices have not been validated, warranting further investigation of their appropriateness. Sixth, the calibration of the negative binomial regression model represents a limitation of this study. Despite evaluating predictive performance through PIT histograms, persistent discrepancies were observed between the predicted probability distribution and the observed data. These biases in predictive calibration indicate potential challenges in generalising our findings. Seventh, this study used annual mortality data to perform survival analysis, which may not fully capture the exact time-to-event nature of mortality. Specifically, the breaks in the survival curves reflect the cumulative number of deaths recorded at the end of each year rather than the precise timing of deaths within the year. While this approach provides a practical and standardised way to evaluate survival in our cohort, it may cause potential biases in estimating hazard ratios and survival probabilities. Finally, as exacerbation data were collected over a 1-year follow-up period, future studies should assess long-term outcomes.

Conclusions

In this study, we found that the mBODE index, which measures exercise capacity using the SGRQ activity domain score, more accurately predicted the presence and frequency of 1-year exacerbations in individuals with COPD than the classic BODE index. The mBODE index consistently predicted future all-cause mortality, aligning with the predictive reliability established for the BODE index in previous studies. Given its accessibility, the mBODE index could serve as a practical tool for assessing exacerbation risk and mortality in individuals with COPD in clinical settings. Future studies should investigate the usefulness of the mBODE index over extended follow-up periods.
