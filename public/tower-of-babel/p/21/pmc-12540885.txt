Real-world safety assessment of tamsulosin based on adverse event analysis from the FAERS database
Authors: Zufa Zhang, Long Lv, Fengze Jiang, Sheng Guan, Danni He, Hongxuan Song, Weibing Sun, Feng Tian, Sixiong Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTamsulosin, an alpha-1-adrenergic receptor antagonist, is a commonly used drug in urology. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the safety of tamsulosin during its use by examining the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database. This study was a retrospective pharmacovigilance analysis using FAERS data from the first quarter of 2004 to the second quarter of 2024. Four disproportionate analyses were used to detect safety signals. Bonferroni correction was applied to control for type I error due to multiple testing at the Preferred Term (PT) level. Further evoked time-series analysis and subgroup analysis were performed to assess the stability of the results of the associated adverse events (AEs). A total of 10,071 reports listing tamsulosin as the primary suspected drug were identified. The time-to-onset analysis revealed a polarized distribution, with a concentration of AEs occurring during the early phase of treatment. Subgroup analysis demonstrated gender-specific differences in AEs reporting patterns, particularly between men with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and women with ureteral stones. Age-related differences were also noted, with increased reporting of certain AEs in middle-aged and elderly populations. In addition to well-known AEs, several unexpected and rarely reported signals were detected. Most findings were expected, but new signals of previously unreported hyperhidrosis, cystitis, panic reaction, and painful erection were reported. These signals should be interpreted cautiously and require further validation in clinical settings. Introduction

Tamsulosin is an alpha-1-adrenergic receptor antagonist that was approved for marketing by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 19971. Tamsulosin is primarily used for the management of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), often associated with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)2,3. As the world ages, the incidence of BPH is also on the rise, leading to increased demand for tamsulosin1. Studies indicate that approximately 50–60% of men over 50 experience symptoms related to BPH, with prevalence reaching up to 80% in men over 704,5. In practice, both men and women may take tamsulosin, with a common reason for women being the treatment of lower ureteral stones6. Tamsulosin is among the most commonly prescribed medications for these patients.

While tamsulosin is generally considered safe, it can still cause adverse effects (AEs) like dizziness, hypotension, fatigue, and sexual dysfunction. These effects are infrequently reported in central databases and are primarily identified during clinical trials7. In recent years, studies on tamsulosin and its AEs, especially regarding the safety and efficacy of long-term use, have gradually increased8. However, few studies have systematically reported tamsulosin-related AEs. Recent large observational studies have also evaluated the safety of tamsulosin. A population-based cohort study using insurance claims data found an increased risk of orthostatic hypotension and related falls in older men treated with tamsulosin9. In addition, analyses of electronic health records (EHRs) have raised concerns about potential cognitive effects, including the association between long-term tamsulosin use and increased risk of dementia10. These large datasets provide valuable background information and external validation of adverse event signals detected in pharmacovigilance databases such as the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS).

FAERS has been monitoring and documenting drugs since 2004 and is a highly effective and accessible resource for clinicians and researchers11. FAERS includes verified AEs reports from diverse sources, such as medical and non-medical professionals12. Due to limited centralized reporting of tamsulosin AEs, we mined the FAERS database and performed secondary analyses to evaluate AEs associated with tamsulosin use from the first quarter of 2004 to the second quarter of 2024.

Methods

Data source

The FAERS database is a key database designed to support the FDA’s postmarket surveillance program for drugs and therapeutic biologics. It is updated quarterly and includes seven datasets of demographic and administrative information (DEMO), drug information (DRUG), adverse drug reaction information (REAC), patient outcome information (OUCT), reporting source (RPSR), start and end date of drug therapy (THER), and indication for drug administration (INDI)13. We conducted a retrospective pharmacovigilance analysis using FAERS, and the data are available on the official website(https://www.fda.gov/drugs).

Drug selection

Drugs reported in FAERS were categorized into 4 patterns: PS (primary suspect), SS (second suspect), C (concomitant), and I (interaction). To improve accuracy, only drugs with tamsulosin as the PS were chosen as the role codes for AEs14. We employed preferred terminology (PT) for coding, and then mapped to the corresponding highest system organ class (SOC) level in MedDRA (version 26.0) to determine that AEs reports associated with tamsulosin were identified at both the SOC and PT levels15. To address discrepancies in FDA-recorded drug names, we analyzed AEs associated with tamsulosin (trade name Flomax), using it as the MeSH for search name expansion, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1. In addition, we selected the most recent FDA_DT with the same CASEID or a higher PRIMARYID when CASEID and FDA_DT were the same to identify and remove duplicate reports. It is worth stating that there is a one-to-many relationship between the number of individual patients and AEs.

Study design

This study conducted a retrospective pharmacovigilance analysis using FAERS data from the first quarter of 2004 to the second quarter of 2024. Figure 1 illustrates the workflow of the study. A total of 21,433,114 spontaneous reports were retrieved from the FAERS database. After excluding duplicate entries and restricting to reports where a single drug was marked as the PS, 17,956,653 PS reports remained. Among them, we identified 10,071 case reports in which tamsulosin was listed as the PS drug. These reports were used for subsequent signal detection and disproportionality analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical methods such as proportional reporting ratio (PRR), reporting ratio of ratios (ROR), Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), and empirical Bayesian geometric mean (EBGM) were used to detect safety signals16. A positive algorithm for any one of the algorithms was defined as a positive result. We referenced the FDA and several classic pharmacovigilance study methods, and the denominators for all imbalance analysis methods were based on the total number of entries for all AEs contained in all PS drug reports in the FAERS database17,18. Supplementary Tables 2 and 3 provide the 2 × 2 list of columns and detailed formulas for these disproportionality analysis methods and thresholds for positive signals. Beyond the threshold, larger values indicated stronger signal values. In signal detection at the PT tier, to control for the risk of Type I error associated with multiple testing, we performed Bonferroni correction for all adverse event signal results presented to further control for the risk of false positives. However, considering its conservative nature, we also retained the labelling of some of the signals that were significant only before correction as a clinical exploratory tip. The onset of tamsulosin was examined using median, interquartile spacing (IQR), and Weber distributions. Subgroup stratification analyses were performed for the population over 55 years of age, for the most common indications (men with BPH and women with ureteral stones), and different gender groups.

Microsoft EXCEL 2010 was used to process the data, and R Language 4.4.1 was used for data visualization and statistical calculations.

Results

Clinical baseline characteristics

The clinical characteristics of tamsulosin-associated AEs are shown in Table 1. The sample size was larger in men, and the number of AEs was significantly higher than that of women. The number of AEs following tamsulosin use was concentrated in middle-aged and older adults > 55 years of age. In terms of reporting countries, the United States (41.5%) reported the highest number of AEs, followed by the United Kingdom (6.3%), Japan (3.4%), France (3.2%), and Canada (2.9%). Nearly one-third of the outcomes were other serious outcomes (30.8%), followed by hospitalization, death, and disability events, which occurred in 1659 (16.5%), 302 (3.0%), and 243 (2.4%) cases, respectively. BPH was the most commonly reported indication (n = 4154, 41.3%), followed by Dysuria (n = 200, 2.0%), Pollakiuria (n = 179, 1.8%), Nephrolithiasis (n = 177, 1.8%), and Urinary retention (n = 149, 1.5%). Figure 2A and Supplementary Table 4 show the annual distribution of reports of tamsulosin-related AEs. In 2004, the lowest number of reports was reported, while the number of reports peaked in 2009. The number of reports increased from 2018 to 2020, followed by a gradual downward trend.

PT level signal detection

We used four algorithms to obtain the number of AEs reports at the PT level and then ranked and listed the top 20 PTs by number of reports (Table 3). To control the risk of false positives arising from multiple comparisons, we further applied Bonferroni multiple correction to all PT-level results, adjusting the significance threshold to α = 0.05/2416 ≈ 0.0000207. At this threshold, most of the detected signals were highly consistent across all four algorithms. Notably, only ‘Headache’ was no longer significant after Bonferroni correction (adjusted P = 1), suggesting a weak association. Supplementary Table 6 demonstrates the positive PT signals of tamsulosin-associated AEs obtained according to the ROR algorithm and ranks them in order of the number of reports. The FDA released an updated explanatory document on tamsulosin on 2019.01.23 (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2019/020579s033lbl.pdf). Accordingly, we found dizziness, syncope, hypotension, falls, loss of consciousness, postural hypotension, malaise, iris disorders, erectile dysfunction, and blurred vision to be common AEs that had been documented. Surprisingly, there were also unmentioned rare conditions such as hyperhidrosis (n = 36; ROR 1.56, PRR 1.56, EBGM 1.47, IC0.55), cystitis (n = 11; ROR 2.12, PRR 2.12, EBGM 1.89, IC0.91), panic reaction (n = 5; ROR 10.11, PRR 10.1, EBGM 5.05, IC2.34), and painful erection (n = 3; ROR 4.85, PRR 4.85, EBGM 3.41, IC1.77).

Time to onset (TTO) of Tamsulosin-associated AEs and Weibull distribution analysis

TTO was defined as the interval between EVENT_DT (date of occurrence of an adverse event) and START_DT (date of initiation of tamsulosin use)19. The results of TTO and Weibull distribution are shown in Supplementary Tables 5 and Supplementary Fig. 1. After exclusion of false reports, a total of 2,362 AEs reported time to onset. The median time to onset was 35 days (IQR 4-362 days). As shown in Fig. 2B, the results showed a polarization of tamsulosin AEs. The time-to-onset data revealed that 46.74% of AEs occurred within the first month of medication use. However, the longest cases may also cover more than one year (n = 592,25.06%). The remaining TTOs were between one month and one year. To assess whether the risk of tamsulosin-associated AEs changed over time, we fitted a Weibull distribution model using time-to-onset data. The estimated shape parameter (β) was 0.44, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.43 to 0.45, indicating an early failure pattern (β < 1), where the hazard decreases over time.

Analysis of gender differences and age stratification

To analyze whether gender influences the AEs of tamsulosin, we identified the top 20 PTs with a disproportionate number of AEs between males and females using the ROR algorithm at the PT level, and these are presented in Fig. 3. In addition, Supplementary Tables 7 and 8 show the top 50 most common AEs for males and females under each of the four algorithms, respectively. Of these, the results for males appear to have remained relatively consistent compared with the overall population. Vertigo, drug failure, and hypotension were also common AEs at the PT level in the overall population. Because tamsulosin has a limited indication for women, the AEs showed uniqueness. This includes exposure during pregnancy, dosing errors, and unapproved indications.

Tamsulosin risk signaling in patients with BPH

Since tamsulosin is a commonly used drug in patients with BPH, we limited the indications and performed various analyses in subgroups of patients with BPH. As shown in Fig. 4A, we counted the top 20 AEs at the PT level and correlated them with the SOC level. We found that AEs risk signals were involved at 7 system-organ levels, with the top 5 being general disease and medication-site conditions, neurologic disorders, renal and urologic disorders, and vascular disorders. As shown in Fig. 4B, we calculated the top 20 positive PTs of AEs by the ROR algorithm, among which drug inefficacy, dizziness, nocturia, dysuria, and hypotension were the positive AEs of tamsulosin in patients with BPH. As shown in Fig. 4C, we showed the distribution of AEs in patients with BPH at the societal level, and the results showed that general disease and site of administration conditions, neurological diseases, and renal and urologic diseases were the most common SOCs, while congenital, familial, and hereditary diseases were the least common SOCs.

AEs in female patients with ureteral stones

However, to reduce bias due to different clinical indications and purposes of use, we further analysed women taking tamsulosin for a short time for colic due to ureteral stones. The results showed that at the PT level, the most common AEs included ‘off-target use’ (ROR: 10.28), ‘medication error’ (ROR: 13.97), and ‘urethral pain’ (ROR: 10.08), indicating that the adverse event profile of female short-term users was significantly different from that of male long-term users of BPH. At the SOC level, ‘general disease and site-of-administration reactions’, ‘injuries, toxicities and handling complications’, and ‘urinary tract disorders’ were the most common. The distribution patterns differed from male BPH patients. This further validates the importance of stratification of indications (Supplementary Tables 10–11).

Discussion

Most previous studies on tamsulosin have focused on clinical trials and meta-analyses, with limited attention given to recent real-world studies6,20,21. This study is the first to collect and evaluate pharmacovigilance data on post-marketing tamsulosin use, utilizing a large sample of real-world data. The aim was to identify new and significant adverse reactions, providing a foundation for the rational clinical use of the drug.

We found that 74% of reported AEs occurred within the first month of tamsulosin use. This early concentration of AEs is consistent with findings from randomized clinical trials and cohort studies22,23, which have reported that most side effects associated with alpha-blockers occur during the initial phase of treatment. However, it is also important to consider potential biases inherent in spontaneous reporting systems. Thus, while our findings align with controlled data in terms of timing, they should be interpreted cautiously in the context of real-world pharmacovigilance. Additionally, certain urinary tract-related AEs (e.g., urinary retention) reported in our analysis may reflect the natural course of BPH or drug inefficacy rather than a direct adverse effect of tamsulosin. With regard to the signaling of cataracts, we believe that caution is needed in interpreting their causality, especially since most of the reported AEs occurred within the first month. These events may not necessarily share a mechanistic link with tamsulosin but could be coincidental or related to underlying comorbidities.

In the subgroup analysis of patients with BPH, some AEs such as nocturia, pollakiuria, and urinary retention were also listed as treatment indications. This apparent contradiction likely reflects the complexity of real-world pharmacovigilance data rather than true paradoxes. First, these events may indicate treatment failure or lack of efficacy, where the symptoms persist or worsen despite therapy, and are therefore reported as AEs. Second, the natural progression of BPH or related urological conditions can result in fluctuating symptoms that may be reported post-treatment regardless of causality. Third, the FAERS database relies on spontaneous reporting without standardized adjudication. Reporters may unintentionally enter the same symptom both as an indication and an adverse event, particularly if the timing or perceived cause is unclear. Therefore, caution is needed when interpreting these overlapping events.

Numerous randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have confirmed that tamsulosin is generally well-tolerated and effective in relieving LUTS associated with BPH24,25. However, several known AEs such as ejaculatory disorders, including anejaculation and retrograde ejaculation, have been frequently reported in clinical trials26,27. Our findings from the FAERS database also captured these known AEs, further validating the consistency between real-world data and controlled trials. According to clinical guidelines from the European Association of Urology (EAU) and American Urological Association (AUA), ejaculatory disorders, anejaculation, and retrograde ejaculation were considered a class effect of alpha-blockers, particularly tamsulosin3,28. While our analysis revealed some novel signals, the consistency with guideline-recognized AEs reinforces the reliability of our FAERS-based findings.

For the significantly higher number of men than women who developed AEs with the drug, this may be attributed to the main indications for tamsulosin, which are more common in men and indirectly contribute to the increasing opportunities for tamsulosin use29,30. According to the disproportionality analysis, the most common and important signals of SOC levels were urinary disorders, gastrointestinal disorders, neurological disorders, respiratory disorders, thoracic and mediastinal disorders, vascular disorders, heart disorders, and ear and labyrinthine disorders. Female patients with ureteral stones were also analysed specifically in this study. The results showed a significant difference in the spectrum of AEs from men with BPH, with particularly prominent signals in terms of dosing errors and off-indication use. This suggests that AEs are highly correlated with therapeutic goals and medication settings.

It is important to emphasize that the FAERS database captures suspected, not confirmed, adverse drug reactions. The reported events do not necessarily establish causality but serve as hypotheses-generating signals that require further validation through controlled clinical trials or mechanistic studies. While we briefly mentioned possible pharmacological explanations referenced in the literature, we acknowledge that definitive mechanisms cannot be confirmed within the scope of this study. Therefore, all mechanistic interpretations should be treated as speculative and interpreted with caution.

The time of induction of AEs regarding tamsulosin showed polarization. This phenomenon may be related to the initial mechanism of action of the drug and the adaptive response of the patient. The initial action of tamsulosin improves urinary symptoms and lowers blood pressure primarily by relaxing smooth muscle31. Although Tamsulosin may have a low frequency of side effects during long-term use, some chronic or delayed-onset adverse reactions can still have an impact on a patient’s health8,32. Prolonged use of the drug may trigger several side effects related to drug accumulation, drug resistance, or sustained potency33. These timing patterns may reflect pharmacodynamic effects, but must be interpreted cautiously in the absence of time-to-onset verification.

Our analysis aligns with recent real-world evidence highlighting tamsulosin’s safety concerns. Large cohort studies have reported associations between tamsulosin use and increased risk of falls due to orthostatic hypotension, particularly in elderly men34. Additionally, population-based research suggests possible links between alpha-1 blockers and cognitive decline, warranting further investigation35. While FAERS signals cannot confirm causality, these findings collectively underscore the need for careful monitoring and integrated data approaches to evaluate tamsulosin’s safety.

Analysis of the FAERS database revealed the presence of these adverse reactions to tamsulosin, which contributes to a comprehensive assessment of the drug safety of tamsulosin. The emergence of these new signals suggests the need for more extensive studies to verify whether these signals are causally related, especially through prospective clinical studies or randomized controlled trials for further confirmation. This study has several limitations inherent to spontaneous reporting systems such as FAERS36. First, the data may be subject to reporting bias and notoriety bias, which can distort the apparent frequency of AEs. Second, the database lacks information on total drug exposure, patient baseline risk, and confounding factors, making it impossible to estimate true incidence rates or establish causal relationships. Finally, the findings in this study should be interpreted as preliminary signals for hypothesis generation rather than confirmed adverse drug reactions. Therefore, this requires a more cautious interpretation of the results of our analysis, and while these signals are of interest, it is also necessary that future prospective studies will more accurately assess the safety risks associated with tamsulosin.

Conclusions

Our comprehensive and systematic pharmacovigilance analysis of the FAERS database identified several common and rare AEs of tamsulosin use and their associated timing. Most findings were consistent with known adverse reactions. However, this study also identified preliminary signals of potentially unreported AEs. These signals should be interpreted cautiously and require further validation in clinical settings.
