Determination of the surface properties and isomer pair separation powers of benzoin gums using inverse gas chromatography
Authors: Ayse Erdogan Cakar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surface energy analysis of red benzoin (RB) and white benzoin (WB), and the separation power of their isomers, was performed using inverse gas chromatography (IGC). The selectivity coefficients (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{A}$$\end{document}) of the structural isomers of RB and WB at infinite dilution were determined to be between 373.2 and 398.2 K for butyl acetate, butyl alcohol, amyl alcohol, and xylene isomers. IGC measurements were performed at infinite dilution to determine the specific adsorption enthalpies of the probe molecules. Based on the IGC measurements, the acidity constants (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{A}$$\end{document}) and basicity constants (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{D}$$\end{document}) of RB and WB were determined using both the classical Gutmann approach and the Hamieh model. Benzoin gums were found to exhibit acidic characteristics.

Introduction

Benzoin is a natural resin obtained from the Styrax tree belonging to the Styracaceae family, insoluble in water, pleasantly scented, biologically active, and biodegradable1,2. Styrax is a perennial plant belonging to the Styracaceae family, growing in subtropical areas such as the Mediterranean, Asia, North and South America. Styrax benzoin is a secretion produced by the tree when its bark is injured. There are over 150 species of Styrax, and each of the resins secreted is distinct from all members of the Styracaceae family. Styrax is self- or cross-pollinating. Therefore, the chemical composition of stem wood or resin varies. Also known by various names such as Benzoin, Jawi, Benjamin, Benjoin, and Benzoebaum, Styrax benzoin comes in two species: Siam and Sumatra benzoin3,4. These types arise from variations in the benzoic acid content of benzoin resin. Siam benzoin has a milky white to reddish yellow color and a vanilla-like odor, while Sumatra benzoin has a dull red to gray-brown color and a willow-like odor due to its higher cinnamic acid and cinnamate content3. Sumatra benzoin is extracted from Styrax trees found in Indonesia, particularly in Sumatra, and dried after collection. The resin is initially harvested as a yellowish-white color. It flows out of the yellowish bark wound. When the resin continues to flow and is collected a second time, some of it is white, and some is dark brown due to oxidation. The remaining resin is dark or slightly reddish5,6. Sumatra benzoin is mostly used as incense. Siam benzoin is used primarily as a varnish in the pharmaceutical industry and in woodworking. Styrax benzoin resins are obtained by pounding the bark of approximately 20-year-old trees. Initially yellow, the resin hardens and thickens, turning reddish brown. Styrax benzoin contains approximately 18% benzoic acid, 17 − 10% methyl cinnamate, 14 − 8% cinnamyl cinnamate, 7 − 4% cinnamic acid, 4 − 2% benzyl cinnamate, and small amounts of vanillin and phenylpropyl alcohol. However, it may also contain high-molecular-mass compounds such as p-coumaryl alcohol, coniferyl alcohol esters of free benzoic acid and cinnamic acid, and pinoresinol3–5,7. Benzoin is used in the treatment of skin diseases and infectious diseases, as a disinfectant, perfume, incense, soap, toilet fragrance, and in the cosmetics industry, as a flavoring in alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages, thanks to its pleasant odor and strong antioxidant, antiviral and antifungicide properties3,4.

In IGC, considering an extension of conventional gas chromatography, this difference arises from the inversion of phases. In conventional gas chromatography, the phase within the column is fixed. The sample is characterized by observing the retention time and peak area of the injected volatile component, whose properties are unknown. However, in IGC, the sample to be characterized is loaded into the column, and known volatile probes such as heptane and methyl acetate are injected into the column to investigate what happens at the interface between the sample and the gas phase8–10. The reason for choosing polar and non-polar probes is to obtain a broader understanding of the surfaces8.

IGC is divided into two types, infinite dilution and finite concentration, depending on the amount of probe injected. In infinite dilution, only a small amount of probe, just enough for the detector to detect, is sent to the detector. In finite concentration, only a small amount of probe is sent to the detector for analysis9–11. Dispersive surface energy, nanoroughness, and acid-base properties are determined using infinitely dilute concentrations. This allows for the characterization of surface area distributions with different energies and surface heterogeneity12. The surface of any material can be subjected to non-polar (dispersive), polar (specific), and surface interactions such as acid-base interactions13,14.

This model describes the temperature-dependent variation of the molecular surface area, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:a\left(T\right)$$\end{document}, for n-alkanes (Figs. 3, 4).

In contrast, the Dorris-Gray and Schultz approaches assume purely distributional interactions for n-alkanes and neglect the effect of energetic heterogeneity and interaction coupling. One reason for this difference is that the Dorris-Gray method can lead to systematic deviations because it does not take thermal expansion and structural effects into account. Consequently, the Hamieh model may predict a larger contribution of the dispersive component to the total surface interaction energy, which may result in higher calculated values.

The classical linear correlation exhibits several intrinsic limitations when applied to real solid surfaces. Numerous studies have reported substantial deviations from linearity when a comprehensive set of polar probe molecules is considered. In practice, satisfactory regression coefficients are often achieved only after excluding certain solvents that engage in strong dipolar, hydrogen-bonding, or multipolar interactions, such as alcohols, ketones, and nitro derivatives. This selective omission of probes, which is frequently neither explicitly reported nor adequately justified, highlights the restricted applicability of the classical two-parameter model and underscores its inability to fully account for the complexity of surface–probe interactions.

To address these limitations, Hamieh developed a new equation expressed as follows28,29:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{-\varDelta\:H}_{A}^{S}={K}_{A\:}\times\:DN+{K}_{D}\times\:{AN}^{*}-K\times\:(DN\times\:{AN}^{*})+{K}_{2A}\times\:(DN{)}^{2}+{K}_{2D}\times\:({AN}^{*}{)}^{2}\:$$\end{document}

In this formulation, (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{A}$$\end{document}) and (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{D}$$\end{document}) represent the conventional acid and base interaction constants, respectively. The coefficient (K) accounts for the amphoteric coupling between electron-donor and electron-acceptor interaction pathways, whereas (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{2A}$$\end{document}) and (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{2D}$$\end{document}) are second-order terms introduced to capture nonlinear effects, such as polarization and curvature, associated with the acceptor and donor contributions, respectively. Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the surface properties of RB and WB and to determine their isomer separation power. Firstly, determining the surface properties of benzoin resins using IGC enables detailed characterization of natural polymers. The surface properties of benzoin gums were evaluated using IGC experiments performed at infinite dilution. For this purpose, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{S}^{D}$$\end{document} values were calculated for both substances using the Dorris-Gray, Hamieh, and Schultz methods based on the results of the IGC experiments. When the results of these methods were compared for both substances, it was found that the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}$$\end{document} values varied in the order Hamieh > Schultz > Dorris-Gray. The results obtained are consistent with the assumptions underlying the Hamieh model regarding temperature-dependent molecular surface effects. In all three methods calculated, it was determined that the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{S}^{D}$$\end{document} values decreased as the temperature increased for both benzoin gums. Negative adsorption enthalpy values indicate that the adsorption of all solvents by the studied benzoin gums is exothermic. According to the Hamieh model, both RB and WB surfaces exhibit Lewis acidic character. However, the parameters obtained for the RB dataset appear to be more physically consistent, and the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{A}/{K}_{D}$$\end{document} ratio of 14.64 suggests that the surface has dominant Lewis acid centers. However, additional probe molecules are required to better validate this interpretation, as the Hamieh model was constructed using only six probe molecules to estimate the five adjustable parameters. Consequently, the excellent fit obtained for RB (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}$$\end{document} = 1.0000) may partially reflect the limited degrees of freedom available in the regression. Although the adsorption energies are higher in the WB dataset, the basicity parameter could not be reliably defined due to the negative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{D}$$\end{document} value. Therefore, the RB dataset appears to be more consistent because both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{A}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{D}$$\end{document} retain physically meaningful positive values. The differences between the Hamieh, Schultz, and Dorris-Gray approaches do not imply that one model is superior to the others because each method underlies different theoretical assumptions. Consequently, the surface energy values obtained from these approaches should be considered as information providing different perspectives on the surface energetic properties of benzoin gums. Finally, the isomer separation performance of the benzoin gums was investigated. The results show that both benzoin gums exhibit effective separation, but RB is more effective at separating acetate and alcohol isomers, while separating xylene isomers equally effectively.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and instrumentations

All the chemicals used in this study are given in Table 11. The benzoin gums were sourced from a local market in Bahrain. To eliminate extra moisture, the benzoin gums were stored in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 24 h before being prepared as the stationary phase. Benzoin gums were dissolved in ethanol and Chromosorb W column filler was added slowly with continuous stirring and continued until the ethanol evaporated. Finally, the stationary phase was obtained in powder form, and it was kept in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 48 h to completely remove the solvent from this phase. After the combustion test with some of these products, the amounts of benzoin coated on Chromosorb W(AW-DMCS-treated, 80/100 mesh) were found to be 19.8% for WB and 19.7% for RB. 0.5 m stainless steel columns were loaded with about 0.6 g of the prepared stationary phase. The packed column was installed in the gas chromatograph, and conditioning was done there for 24 h at 90 °C with helium (He) as the carrier gas. A 1 µL Hamilton syringe was used to pass the probes over the stationary phase, and their retention behavior was studied. The air peak was also obtained using a 10 µL Hamilton syringe.
