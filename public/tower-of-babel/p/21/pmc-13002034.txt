Smartphone applications as assistive technology for the visually impaired individuals: A hospital-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Suraj Singh Senjam, N Mounica, Vivek Gupta, Praveen Vashist, Radhika Tandon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Using smartphone-accessible applications among visually challenged people can enhance independence management of many daily activities.Purpose:To assess the use of accessible applications among patients with visual disabilities (PVDs) attending a vision rehabilitation clinic of a tertiary eye center.Methods:PVDs, aged 18 years and above, were recruited consecutively in the clinic from January to March 2023. Information on the use of smartphones and apps was collected using semi-structured interviews. Apps were categorized into special (accessible) and mainstream. Visual acuities (VAs), both distance and near, were also recorded.Results:In total, 115 PVDs, with a mean age of 26.3 years (SD + 12.8), were interviewed. Among them, 28 respondents (24.6%) were dependent on family members for daily activities solutions. Of the 98 mobile users, 87 (88.8%) used smartphones, and 35 (40.2%) reported using one or more special apps. While InstaReader (20%) and KiBo (16%) were the most used apps by participants with VA <1/60 blindness, Visor app was used by 14.7% participants with VA <6/18 to ≥1/60. Communication, reading, setting reminders, and entertainment were the primary activities performed with special apps. Multivariable analysis showed that participants with a higher education (OR = 15.57; 15.1 for graduate and above, P ≤ 0.05) and being unmarried (OR = 9.92, P = 0.01) were predictors for the use of special apps.Conclusion:Many individuals with visual impairment own a smartphone but do not use special applications for their daily solutions despite evidence of their benefits. Promoting use of such special apps is essential to enhance their independence and daily functioning.

Visual disability causes hindrances in executing daily activities, social inclusion, employment opportunities, and digital learning, thereby affecting the quality of life.[123] In total, there are 43 million people with blindness and 295 million people with moderate-to-severe visual impairment, while the majority of them belong to low and lower to middle-income countries.[4] Assistive technology helps people with visual disabilities to cope with their daily activities and challenges posed at their workplace, home, and social life.[5] These technologies not only bridge the accessibility gap but also contribute significantly to the inclusion and empowerment of visually disabled individuals in various aspects of modern civic life. One such continuously advancing assistive technology is smartphones and their third-party accessible applications that are specially designed for people with visual impairment and built-in screen readers like Talkback for Android phone.[6]

In this era of technological advancement, a smartphone is a portable multimedia that helps in performing various tasks in daily activities, related to education and job, entertainment, and inclusion and participation into a society, thereby enhancing the quality of life of the disabled.[7] The availability of smartphones has grown significantly in the last few years in low-to-middle-income countries (LMICs) due to cost reductions.[8] Literature has also reported the usage of accessible applications in smartphones among the visually impaired for shopping, education, and navigation purposes.[91011] People with severe low vision or blind can use smartphones without relying on vision with the help of various accessible apps and built-in accessible features like screen readers. For example, built-in screen readers like Talkback for Android and VoiceOver for Apple iOS enable users to identify the contents with fingers just scrolling on the smartphone icons.[1213]

Article 25 of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities emphasizes that persons with visual disabilities should not be ignored in getting access to healthcare while accessing their healthcare needs.[14] Appropriate digital literacy is one strategy that can enhance healthcare access to them. These accessible apps help in acquiring information about various global issues, including the COVID-19 pandemic and its trends, and help in executing many routine activities independently.[615161718] Furthermore, smartphones are easy to carry, designed universally, and not associated with social stigma.[1920]

Despite these useful features and user-friendliness of smartphones, the majority of individuals with visual disabilities are still using basic/feature phones, particularly in LMICs.[6] Further, there is limited study available on the use of accessible application among individuals with visual disabilities. It is worth investigating what accessible apps are being used and for what purposes in these population. Therefore, this study aims to assess the use of accessible applications and its purposes among visually challenged patients attending vision rehabilitation clinics of tertiary eye care centers. Such a study will help in the generation of awareness regarding smartphone-accessible apps as assistive technology and how they can be used in daily life.

Methods

Study design and setting

In this hospital-based cross-sectional study, patients were recruited consecutively from the low vision rehabilitation clinic of our tertiary eye center situated in Delhi. People with visual disabilities (low vision and blindness) are referred to from various ocular subspecialties of the outpatient department for vision rehabilitation services. Patients aged 18 years and above with visual disabilities (best corrected visual acuity <6/18 in the better eye), who visited for the first time, were included in the study after obtaining their informed consent. We also enrolled participants who use a mobile. We excluded follow-up patients or those who had previous visits. Patients registered during the 3 months (January to March 2023) in the clinic were included in the study.

Study definitions

Mainstream apps: These are conventional smartphone apps used by both sighted and unsighted individuals, for example WhatsApp. Such apps are designed for the public, not particularly for groups with specific health conditions.

Special apps: These are apps being developed and designed for a group of people with specific health condition. For example, a special app for a person with low vision is WeZoom or Visor. These apps provide visual assistance to low-vision people. Similarly, there are visual substitution apps like InstaReader or Kibo for a person with total blindness. Users access these visual substitute apps with hand gestures, not relying on vision. To accommodate both types of apps, we use accessible apps for visual disabilities which are further classified into visual assistance apps for persons with best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) binocular <6/18 up to 1/60 and visual substitution apps for person BCVA less than 1/60. These special third-party special apps help users with inaccessible content to accessible (accessible apps). The authors have used this term in a few published research studies.[2122]

Study tool and data collection

Details on demography, educational status, cause of low vision or blindness, and usage of mobile phones, including smartphones along with various special (accessible) applications and their purposes of usage, were obtained on a semi-structured questionnaire. This semi-structured questionnaire was pretested and standardized by experts working in low-vision rehabilitation [Annexure 1]. All information was self-reported and collected by conducting a face-to-face interview with a team of two trained staff. The interview was conducted before any type of vision rehabilitation services, including necessary training provided. During the interview, the team informed each participant about the special apps and gave examples of those special apps both blind and low vision. Although the questionnaire was in English language, it was administered verbally in a one-to-one setting using local vernacular language. Applications in smartphones were categorized as mainstream apps and special (accessible) apps for visual impairment. The dataset included detailed insights into the array of special applications employed by participants, each serving distinct purposes for their daily solution.

Visual assessment

The detailed vision assessment was performed at the low-vision evaluation clinic located in the outpatient block of the center. BCVA of each participant was measured in this clinic. Additionally, in the visual rehabilitation clinic (VRC) of the center, each participant underwent a test for the best corrected binocular vision with pinhole both near and distance using a modified Snellen ‘E’ chart with two optotypes (6/18 as small and 6/60 as large), and a near vision ‘N’ notation chart. In brief, the following steps were performed.

Test with the four small ‘E’s (6/18) at 6 meters distance

Participants who correctly identified at least three out of four small ‘E’s were classified as ‘without low vision.’ For those unable to meet this criterion, testing continued with larger ‘E’s (6/60) presented in four different directions to assess visual acuity.

Test with the large Es (6/60) at 3 and 1 meters

After the initial assessment with the modified Snellen ‘E’ chart, for participants who could not correctly identify at least three out of four large ‘E’s at 3 meters, further testing was performed at 1 meter distance. Patients who could not see 1/60 were further tested for hand movement and perception of light. Based on the results of BCVA for distance, the participants were categorized into two groups: those who had BCVA distances less than 6/18 up to 1/60 (Category 1) and those with less than 1/60 (Category 2). This categorization was made to differentiate participants according to their severity of vision impairment and to align them with type of apps most suitable for their productive use. Participants in cat-1 were expected to benefit primarily from visual assistance apps (which enhance remaining vision through magnification, contrasts), whereas participants in cat-2, with profound vison loss, were expected to benefit more from visual substitution apps. For example, participants with Cat-2 vision do not benefit with visual assistance apps like Wisor.

Near Vision assessment

We used Times New Roman font as the standard font for testing near vision (best corrected), which was recorded as N notation. The near vision acuity performance was recorded as the smallest print that can be read by participants, and the distance was specified in centimeters. Like distance BCVA, participants were divided into BCVA near with better than N 18 and worse than N 24 at 35 cm.

Data analysis

Given the use of applications, participants were divided into two groups: participants who used special apps, that is, smartphones with accessible apps for visual loss, and the other group without accessible apps. Further, these two groups were compared in terms of demographic details and the factors associated with the use of special apps. Relative frequency of the use of special apps for daily solutions among participants was elicited. The data were entered into the EpiData 3.1 version and were analyzed descriptively. Since all participants belonged to vulnerable groups, the confidentiality of data was maintained throughout the study periods. The data were encrypted and stored in password-protected devices. The analysis was done using the STATA 15.1 version (StataCorp. 2015. Stata Statistical Software: Release 15. College Station, Tx: StataCorp LP). We dichotomized the continuous variable generated in the study to assess any association between dependent and independent variables. Pearson Chi-square and Fisher’s exact test were used to test the association between the use of special apps for vision loss and independent factors. A P value at <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Multivariable regression analysis was used to identify the predictors of special applications use.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institute board ethics committee (Ref. No.: IEC- 176/05.03.2021). Each participant provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

A total of 115 patients with visual disabilities were recruited in the study. None of the eligible patients declined for the interview. The mean age of the participants was 26.3 years (SD 12.8). More than half of the participants were 18–25 years old (53%). Approximately, 80% participants were males, and 42.6% were graduates or above in their education. Further, 76.5% of respondents were unmarried, and 95.7% had a family monthly income of less than Rupees 30,000/- [Table 1]. Given daily living activities, around two-thirds of them (65%) executed their daily activities by themselves. Further, approximately 42.6% of them reported that they did not have a visual disability certificate.

Discussion

The burgeoning integration of computer technology into smartphones has led to a surge in the advancement of human–smartphone interaction. Many new interface designs and applications made smartphones more inclusive and user-friendly even for persons with visual disabilities.[23] The present study investigated the use of smartphones, with or without special apps for vision loss, and predictors of smartphone use in study participants at a tertiary eye care in Delhi.

Given the demographic characteristics, the present study showed that many of the participants were unmarried. One potential reason could be that more than half of the participants belong to the younger age groups in our study. Moreover, it is our experience in the clinic that many people with visual disabilities tend to avoid marriage or get married later in life. In India, a scheme named the ‘ADIP (Assistance to Persons with Disabilities) scheme,’ under The Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, provides free smartphones to students with severe vision loss or blindness.[24] Distributing smartphones to these visually challenged students is a part of promoting digital literacy and digital India – a program to transform India into a digitally empowered society.[25] Perhaps this could additional factors why most young people possess a smartphone and use special apps.

Th study indicates that males compared to females have higher access to vision rehabilitation services. This reflects that low vision rehabilitation-seeking care is active among male participants compared to females. Interpretation of this finding should be approached with caution because it does not reflect the relative proportion of each sex in real sense. To understand these data, further studies can be planned with recruitment from the outpatient’s department, not from a low-vision-specific clinic or through population-based studies where all spectra of patients, including with or without low vision, are represented.

Similar findings were seen in studies conducted elsewhere. For instance, a low-vision rehabilitation clinic-based study in Bangladesh and Saudi Arabia revealed that 64.0% and 65% of low-vision patients were respectively males.[2026] Potential reasons for this finding could be lack of escort for female patients, social stigma in which parents do not want to disclose the disabilities of their daughter, financial constraints, preoccupation with household works of the parents, and parents ignoring their daughter to get a check in the rehabilitation clinic. However, to know the reasons, further study is warranted to rule out why females seek less vision rehabilitation care.

Further, one-third of participants depend on family members, parents, or peers to execute their daily living activities. They need to be provided with a proper vision rehabilitation service, including adaptive training and assistive devices on how to perform daily activities alone with seldom services from others. The present study also revealed that nearly half of them did not have visual disability certificates at the time of visiting our clinic. Since disability certificates are crucial to avail of the relevant government benefits and schemes, they must be felicitated to get disability certificates on time. Further study to investigate the reasons for not having certificates among these groups is crucial. However, to the best of our knowledge, the reasons can be that the majority of patients at the study hospital were referred from other eye care facilities for further evaluation and management, so the referring eye centers could not advise a disability certificate. The study site (Hospital) is a tertiary and referral center, so the final decisions are being made, including complicated cases, thereby patients being advised for disability certification from the hospital itself.

The result also shows that more than 95% of them have a monthly family income of less than 361 USD. Given the poor monthly family income of many participants, availing of government schemes under the government of India, including financial assistance under disability pension scheme is of paramount importance for them.

In our study, one of the interesting findings is that most of the participants (87%) own a smartphone, although only a few of them use special accessible apps to enhance their daily work. This reflects that there is a reasonably good digital inclusion among them. The use of special apps is much higher among male participants with younger age groups and who completed a higher education. A study conducted by Crossland MD et al.[27] reported that around 81% of the study participants used a smartphone, while in Abraham CH et al.[28] study in a low-middle-income country, the smartphones use rate was found to be 46.9%. In a study by Malkin AG et al.,[29] they reported that 90% of the low-vision patients in their study possessed a smartphone, but only 6% of them used visual assistive apps. In our study, 75.5% possess a smartphone, but only 40% used special assistive apps. A study conducted by Busaeed et al.[30] found that higher education status, graduation and above, was associated with the use of assistive devices and special apps. A similar finding is also reported in a study conducted among 259 participants by Griffin-Shirley et al.[31]

The present study also revealed that the participants with poor monthly income families have a greater number of smartphones compared to those with a higher income family. This could be due to the free distribution of smartphones under the Assistance to Disabled Persons (ADIP) scheme of the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, the Government of India.[24] Under this, a few selected assistive devices, including smartphones, were distributed for free, especially to younger age groups, such as students or scholars.

The findings showed that the most frequent daily activities performed with the help of smartphones among participants included listening to music (91.5%), followed by entertainment (88.5%), messaging and texting (88.5%), and listening to news (80%). For this, the participants used various accessible applications, InstaReader being the most common and followed by KiBo and Visor. These apps are either freely available (InstaReader) or offered at a minimal cost (approximately Rs. 85/- for Kibo), making them accessible and affordable options for individuals with visual disabilities. Such findings also highlight good digital literacy among study participants. In a study conducted by Bhakhri et al.,[32] the most frequent activities executed using smartphones among low-vision participants were texting (100%), Internet browsing (27%), and sending an email (36%). Another study conducted by Griffin-Shirley et al.[31] showed that the most common special accessible apps used were BeMyEyes, ColorID, CamFind, KNFB Reader, and BrailleTouch for activities like object identification, reading and writing, GPS navigation, and news.

A similar study conducted by Robinson et al.[33] reported that the commonly used accessibility apps were Zoom Magnification (84.8%) and Large Text (81.8%). Further, accessible apps such as InstaReader, Kibo, Visor, Envision AI, Be My Eyes, MANI, and Eloquence are common applications used by study total blind participants.

Several limitations can be mentioned; first, since recruitment was done among participants accessing services at an urban hospital, the generalizability of the findings may be affected, for example, predominantly male participants, a higher proportion of educational level (43% of them are graduate and above), a greater percentage of possessing disability certificates, and apps being used for online shopping. These factors may not accurately represent the broader community, including rural residents. Therefore, future community-based study is recommended to ensure representative. Second, our study did not rule out the reasons for not using special apps. A study is warranted to investigate it in the future. Third, we did not enquire about the use of other smart devices such as iPads and Tablets, although we expect that a few participants may use these devices for similar activities. We excluded these bigger size devices because generally, people with vision loss pose a challenge to carry them to the hospital.

Conclusion

Smartphones have become a valuable and irreplaceable assistive device for visually impaired individuals due to advances in special accessible apps. Such special apps help individuals with low vision and blindness to execute their daily living activities of various kinds. Despite these multiple benefits with special applications, many individuals with visual disabilities, especially from LMICs, cannot access it for their daily solutions. Awareness regarding various innovative special apps in smartphones needs to be promoted or educated not only among end users and public but also in eye care providers.
