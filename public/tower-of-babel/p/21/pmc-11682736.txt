Biofunctionalized gelatin hydrogels support development and maturation of iPSC-derived cortical organoids
Authors: Andrew Kjar, Mia R. Haschert, José C. Zepeda, A. Joey Simmons, Alexis Yates, Daniel Chavarria, Melanie Fernandez, Gabriella Robertson, Adam M. Abdulrahman, Hyosung Kim, Nicole T. Marguerite, Rachel K. Moen, Lauren E. Drake, Corinne W. Curry, Brian J. O’Grady, Vivian Gama, Ken S. Lau, Brad Grueter, Jonathan M. Brunger, Ethan S. Lippmann
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYHuman neural organoid models have become an important tool for studying neurobiology. However, improving the representativeness of neural cell populations in such organoids remains a major effort. In this work, we compared Matrigel, a commercially available matrix, to a neural cadherin (N-cadherin) peptide-functionalized gelatin methacryloyl hydrogel (termed GelMA-Cad) for culturing cortical neural organoids. We determined that peptide presentation can tune cell fate and diversity in gelatin-based matrices during differentiation. Of particular note, cortical organoids cultured in GelMA-Cad hydrogels mapped more closely to human fetal populations and produced neurons with more spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents relative to Matrigel. These results provide compelling evidence that matrix-tethered signaling peptides can influence neural organoid differentiation, opening an avenue to control stem cell fate. Moreover, outcomes from this work showcase the technical utility of GelMA-Cad as a simple and defined hydrogel alternative to Matrigel for neural organoid culture.

INTRODUCTION

Three-dimensional (3D) stem cell-derived models have become an indispensable tool in cell biology. Remarkably, stem cell aggregates can recapitulate some of the detailed spatial and transcriptomic elements of organogenesis when provided with a minimal set of differentiation cues.1,2 These concepts have been applied extensively to the generation of neural organoids, as reviewed elsewhere.3–7 Neural organoids are complex cellular aggregates derived from pluripotent stem cells, which can self-pattern over long-term differentiation protocols.8 Early pioneering studies generated “unguided” neural organoids in Matrigel,9 a tumor-derived extracellular matrix (ECM) with an undefined composition that is generally used for neural cell cultures. Neural organoids generated in Matrigel harbor diverse groups of excitatory neurons and astroglia, resembling different brain regions in a batch-dependent manner, and stochastically produce non-neural cells including choroid plexus epithelium and mesenchymal cells. “Guided” protocols, which induce neural fate via transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) signaling inhibition, were then developed, demonstrating more reliable, region-specific cell fates.1,10,11 However, the relative proportions of cells generated by these methods and their organization within the organoid are not fully representative of mature (or fetal) in vivo populations, and cellular health within these models also remains controversial.12,13 These concerns broadly limit the utility of organoids for disease and developmental modeling, and thus improving the representativeness and health of neural cell populations in neural organoids remains a major research effort.

As demonstrated in these early studies, neural organoid cell fates can be controlled by regulating extracellular cues, including soluble moieties (small molecules, growth factors, etc.). Small molecules that activate or repress canonical signaling pathways (Wnt, BMP, etc.) can direct human neural organoids toward different regional identities and molecular fates.14,15 In contrast, the effects of the surrounding ECM, which can provide both biophysical and biochemical cues, have been generally less studied with neural organoids,16 although they are relevant for brain development in vivo.17 Many synthetic matrices have been developed and applied to non-neural organoid systems.18 In contrast, most neural organoid studies have relied on embedding in Matrigel, which offers limited control over mechanical or biochemical properties and exhibits batch-to-batch inconsistencies. Efforts have been made to augment neural organoid differentiation in Matrigel through additional engineering or supplementation of the matrix. For example, the incorporation of poly(lactide-co-glycolide) copolymer microfilaments in Matrigel droplets assisted with neuroectoderm formation and cortical development in unguided neural organoids.19 Modifying the stiffness of Matrigel with increasing amounts of alginate—an inert biopolymer derived from algae—can also influence the morphology of neural organoids and neuronal differentiation.20 However, these approaches do not avoid the challenges associated with Matrigel, and thus, synthetic matrices have been proposed.18 To move neural organoid cultures beyond Matrigel, a synthetic matrix must convincingly demonstrate outcomes at least as good as Matrigel, such as the long-term patterning and maturation of neuronal populations, while offering other engineering advantages, such as ease of manipulation and modular control over biochemical and mechanical properties.

Synthetic extracellular matrices have been developed with conjugated peptide epitopes to direct cell signaling,21 but these concepts have not been applied to neural organoids. We previously developed hydrogels consisting of gelatin functionalized with a peptide derived from the extracellular epitope of type 1 cadherins, including neural cadherin (N-cadherin).22 Gelatin represents a base ECM that is easy to manipulate and chemically modify, is widely available, and has previously displayed favorable results in both in vitro and in vivo neural microenvironments.23–25 Since N-cadherin plays important roles in cortical development,26 we hypothesized that these matrices could support cortical organoid differentiation. In extensive work, we cultured cortical organoids in these functionalized gelatin matrices for up to 4 months and comprehensively characterized the resulting cellular diversity as a function of physical properties and N-cadherin peptide presentation, with rigorous comparisons to cortical organoids embedded in Matrigel. Overall, we demonstrate that fate trajectories can be tuned by the presence of the N-cadherin peptide on the gelatin backbone, with subtler effects imparted by physical properties of the hydrogel. Excitingly, relative to Matrigel controls generated in parallel, cortical organoids differentiated in GelMA-Cad (N-cadherin peptide-functionalized gelatin methacryloyl) hydrogels produce cell populations that more closely map to the developing human brain. Moreover, cortical organoids differentiated in GelMA-Cad hydrogels yield neurons with more spontaneous excitatory post-synaptic currents (sEPSCs) and action potentials compared to Matrigel, indicating enhanced maturation. We anticipate that GelMA-Cad hydrogels can serve as immediate alternatives to Matrigel for cortical organoid models, and the concept of manipulating and directing differentiation with matrix-tethered bioactive peptides will likely be applicable to other neural organoid platforms.

RESULTS

Matrix properties instruct early cortical organoid morphology

To optimize a matrix for cortical organoid growth, a range of functionalized gelatin hydrogels were generated by sequential covalent addition of a methacryloyl group and a N-cadherin peptide group (GelMA and GelMA-Cad, respectively; Figure 1A). The N-cadherin-derived peptide was chosen because N-cadherin is necessary for neuron development in vivo, and the same peptide has been shown to support neural cultures in vitro.22,27,28 Conjugations were confirmed by 1H-nuclear magnetic resonance (1H-NMR) and chemical methods (Figures 1B and S1). We first sought to select an N-cadherin peptide density of interest by diluting GelMA-Cad in GelMA and examining organoid phenotypes. Early experiments showed that while the area, circularity, and thickness of beta-III tubulin (βIIIT) were unaffected by peptide density, neurite extensions were most likely to form in hydrogels composed of a 1:2 ratio of GelMA-Cad:GelMA (referred to hereafter simply as GelMA-Cad), demonstrating the bioactivity of the hydrogel matrix (Figure S2). This peptide concentration was selected for use in all subsequent experiments, with non-conjugated GelMA as a control.

We next screened parameter combinations to understand which conditions produced well crosslinked hydrogels, based on visual observation (Table S1). Two different concentrations of lithium phenyl-2-4-6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) crosslinking agent that produced stable hydrogels were selected for investigation, which were termed high crosslinker (HC) and low crosslinker (LC). In a combinatorial approach, the two crosslinking concentrations and N-cadherin peptide presence (with and without) were investigated in parallel, giving four total hydrogel formulations for the remainder of our studies. In mechanical tests, we did not measure differences in local Young’s modulus across hydrogel conditions using atomic force microscopy (Figure 1C). We then characterized bulk storage and loss moduli by rheology, noting a significant reduction in both as an effect of crosslinker concentration (Figures 1D–1F). Scanning electron microscopy of lyophilized crosslinked gels suggested that neither crosslinker concentration nor peptide presence significantly influenced pore size, although we note that pore sizes can be directly affected by a freeze-dry cycle, and our measurements are unlikely to correspond exactly to hydrated pore sizes29 (Figures 1G and 1H). Concordantly, the permeability of 3 kDa fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran was of a similar magnitude for all four hydrogels, supportive of similar nutrient diffusion properties (Figure 1I). Taken together, our mechanical and physical characterizations suggested that mechanical properties were largely independent of peptide presence, giving confidence that potential differences in embedded organoids would be due to biochemical composition. This motivated us to proceed to detailed differentiation studies.

Cortical organoids were embedded in each of the hydrogel conditions in well plates and allowed to differentiate in static culture until day 30, with parallel organoids generated in Matrigel for comparison. Notable morphological differences were apparent; at day 10, GelMA- and GelMA-Cad-embedded organoids remained highly compacted, as opposed to the extensive budding that occurs in Matrigel30,31 (Figure 2A). At day 30 in both GelMA and GelMA-Cad, the resulting organoids were significantly smaller than those embedded in Matrigel but of comparable circularity (Figures 2B and 2C). Consistent with previous reports22,27 and our earlier observations (Figure S2E), the N-cadherin peptide promoted neurite extensions, confirming its biological activity mostly independent of crosslinker concentration (Figures 2D and 2E).

Matrix properties can potentially alter the trajectory and spatial arrangement of developing cells30; for example, a matrix with an organizing filamentous center was reported to produce larger neural rosettes.19 Thus, whole-mount imaging of zonal occludin 1 (ZO-1) staining was used to assess the formation of neural rosettes in day 10 organoids (Figure 2F). Morphologically, polarized ZO-1+ regions were generally smaller and more frequent in organoids embedded in gelatin-based hydrogels (Figures 2G and 2H). Whole-mount βIIIT staining was also used to understand early neuron distribution in the organoids at day 30, as MAP2+ neuron population thickness has been associated with stiffer matrices.20 Indeed, when normalized to the organoid size, the higher crosslinker concentration was associated with an increased βIIIT area, although the differences were relatively subtle (Figures 2I–2K). Overall, across all described morphological measurements, GelMA-CadHC-embedded organoids displayed features most similar to Matrigel-embedded organoids.

To further understand the effect of matrix composition on cellular trajectory, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was used to profile day 21 organoids. As expected, the majority of cells at this time point were classified as radial glia (SOX2+), with smaller populations of intermediate progenitors and neurons (Figures 2L and 2M). Upon direct comparison between radial glia from each condition, the strongest differentially expressed gene signal was the upregulation of ribosome-associated transcripts in Matrigel (Figure 2N). Ribosomal transcript upregulation may indicate increased proliferation in Matrigel-embedded organoids, consistent with the increased gross size (Figures 2A and 2B) and larger rosette structures (Figures 2F and 2G). Interestingly, cells reminiscent of neural placode (SIX1+, ISL1+), neural crest (FOXD3+), or mesenchyme (COL1A1+, TWIST1+) were also present in the Matrigel-derived organoids while being largely absent in organoids embedded in gelatin-based matrices, suggesting an increase in the specification of neural plate cells (Figure 2M).

Taken together, these results indicate that gelatin-based hydrogels are compatible with early cortical organoid differentiation and can impart morphological and early fate differences relative to Matrigel.

GelMA-Cad promotes development of cortical neurons

To build on the early differentiation datasets, we next cultured organoids embedded in Matrigel, GelMA, and GelMA-Cad under free-floating conditions for long-term experimentation. Embedding was accomplished using a custom 3D-printed rig to control crosslinking parameters and facilitate the transfer of embedded organoids to culture plates (Figure 3A). Organoids were then initially assayed at 60 days using scRNA-seq and immunolabeling. scRNA-seq captured 17,170 high-quality single cells, which were annotated based on canonical markers (Figures 3B and 3C). Populations of radial glia, intermediate progenitor cells, upper-layer excitatory neurons, and deep-layer excitatory neurons comprised the majority of sequenced cells, while inhibitory neurons and choroid plexus epithelial cells comprised a small minority of detected cells. At this time point, matrix conditions were found to influence later-born neuron numbers and distributions. For example, upper-layer excitatory neurons (SATB2+) were slightly increased in GelMA- or GelMA-Cad-embedded organoids as compared to Matrigel (comprising ~3% of cells in GelMA-Cad as compared to <1% in Matrigel). Supporting the scRNA-seq results, an increased thickness of SATB2+ cells was measured in whole-mount images of organoids cultured in GelMA-Cad as compared to Matrigel (Figures 3D and 3E). Graph-based trajectory analysis was then used to score cells, with the origin node located at the radial glia cluster. When compared across the excitatory neuron clusters, Matrigel-embedded neurons scored significantly lower in pseudotime than all GelMA or GelMA-Cad conditions (p < 0.0001 for all pairwise comparisons), consistent with the enhanced maturation of neurons produced in GelMA or GelMA-Cad conditions (Figures 3F and 3G).

Based on these initial population characterizations, we performed a more comprehensive analysis of the excitatory neuron transcriptomes as a function of hydrogel composition (Figures 3H and S3). Compared to GelMA or GelMA-Cad, excitatory neurons generated in Matrigel were enriched for the KEGG pathways “HIF-1 signaling pathway,” “glycolysis,” and “carbon metabolism,” which have been previously noted as metrics for cellular stress12 (Figure 3H). In contrast, top transcripts enriched in GelMA-Cad organoids code for neural transcription factors (SOX4, NPAS4), proteins involved in synapse regulation (MEF2C), neurite outgrowth (DOK5, ID2), or neural markers (FOS, SLA), suggestive of neural maturation. Overall, day 60 analyses suggested that cortical neuron generation is readily supported by GelMA-Cad, with an enrichment of upper-layer neuron phenotypes.

During long-term culture, organoid cell fates diverge based on hydrogel composition

Sixty days does not typically provide sufficient maturation time for the development of diverse cell phenotypes in neural organoids.9,32 Thus, we undertook characterization at 3- and 4-month time points. By 4 months, hydrogels with LC concentrations were degraded, while those with HC concentrations remained intact around the organoids (Figure 4A). To construct an atlas of cell types in Matrigel-, GelMA-, and GelMA-Cad-embedded organoids at late stages, 8–10 organoids were pooled per condition and digested out of the hydrogel for scRNA-seq. In total, 137,844 cells passed quality control and were retained in the final dataset. Cells were annotated based on canonical marker genes and by integration with the human neural organoid atlas,14 revealing striking differences in the resulting cell populations (Figures 4B–4D). For example, organoids grown in GelMA produced large numbers of mesenchymal cells (COL1A1, COL4A1) and choroid plexus epithelial cells (TTR, CLIC6). Bright-field images of organoids embedded in GelMAHC revealed the expansion of cells into the surrounding hydrogel, a potential source of the mesenchymal cell populations (Figure 4A). In contrast, organoids grown in GelMA-Cad produced robust neural and glial populations irrespective of crosslinking condition, emphasizing the importance of the N-cadherin peptide as a cue for directing and maintaining neural fate. This was further assessed by using the scRNA-seq data to score cells for neural maturation,33 axon guidance,34 glutamatergic synapse formation,34 GABAergic synapse formation,34 and single-cell entropy35 (a proposed measure of maturity). Cells generated in GelMA-Cad or GelMA scored comparably to Matrigel (means within one standard deviation in all but one case) (Figure S4A), demonstrating the support of established neuronal trajectories in GelMA-Cad.

Crosslinker concentration was instructive to the emergence of sparse non-neural lineages—for example, single-cell analyses suggested that organoids in GelMAHC or GelMA-CadHC were more likely to harbor choroid plexus epithelial cells. This difference was concordant with the detection of small populations of choroid plexus cells at high-crosslinking conditions at day 60 (Figures 3B and 3C). In GelMA-CadHC organoids, full-mount imaging revealed groups of tightly associated TTR+ border cells in 2/5 organoids, reminiscent of structures in the choroid plexus (Figure 4E). In contrast, these structures were not detected in GelMA-CadLC organoids (0/10 organoids). However, the emergence of choroid plexus epithelial cells was stochastic, as in previous work.30 In an independent batch of organoids where imaging was conducted on thin sections, we detected TTR+ cells in Matrigel controls (3/4 organoids) and only one GelMAHC organoid (1/4 organoids) (data not shown).

Importantly, the sequencing data indicated greater numbers of deep-layer and upper-layer excitatory neurons formed in GelMA-Cad hydrogels. In GelMA-CadLC, upper-layer and deep-layer neurons collectively represented 27% of the detected cells at day 120 (15% and 12%, respectively) (Figures 4B and 4C). In contrast, at day 120, upper-layer and deep-layer neurons comprised <1% cells detected in Matrigel or GelMAHC and only 4% in GelMALC. To determine if the increase of upper-layer neurons was cell-line specific, cortical organoids were also generated from the KOLF2.1J iPSC line.36 On day 90, robust numbers of SATB2+ neurons were detected in GelMA-CadLC and GelMA-CadHC (21% and 10%, respectively) (Figure 4F), indicating the consistency of our findings.

After unbiased profiling of cell fates by scRNA-seq, immunofluorescent imaging of cryosectioned slices was used to confirm select cell identities. SATB2+ and CTIP2+ neurons were readily detected in GelMA-Cad-embedded organoids (Figures 4G and S4B). Staining for glial lineages (GFAP+) revealed elongated and branched morphology in day 90 organoids (Figures 4G and S4C). We also confirmed large areas of Reelin (RELN) positivity (Figures 4G and S4D). This population is of interest because cells in the outermost layers of the developing cortex play important roles in developing polarity and guiding cell migration, and it is well established that cell-deposited or cell-tethered extracellular cues, including RELN and N-cadherin, are involved in this process.26,37 We did not measure statistically significant differences in cell numbers between Matrigel and GelMA-CadHC in these sections (Figures S4E–S4I). However, significant heterogeneity in the proportions of these populations was evident depending on the orientation of the specific stained section within the organoid, a known problem with this experimental approach.38

To further benchmark cell phenotypes to embryonic development, we integrated our GelMA-CadHC- and Matrigel-derived transcriptomic datasets across all time points with a published fetal dataset12 (Figure 4H). Cluster designations from the fetal dataset were transferred to our data by majority representation per cluster, revealing increasing diversity through the course of the differentiation trajectory (Figure 4I). The fetal database included an “outlier” cluster designation, characterized by the expression of ECM-related genes and a downregulation of neuron-related transcripts (Tables S2 and S3); upon mapping, early-stage organoid-derived progenitors mapped to this cluster, consistent with previous reports.2 Strikingly, late-stage Matrigel-embedded organoids also contained large proportions of cells mapping to this phenotype, while GelMA-Cad-embedded organoids contained much smaller proportions of outlier cells, further supporting the utility of this hydrogel for enhancing neural fate trajectories.

Integration with the primary atlas also allowed us to make comparisons of cellular stress across all time points. While we noted that that there was significant reduction of cell stress in GelMA-Cad compared to Matrigel at day 60, these differences were not present at other time points (Figure S5A). As orthogonal confirmation, cleaved caspase staining of thin organoid sections revealed no significant differences between conditions at day 90 (Figure S5B).

Electrophysiological properties in differentiating cortical organoids are defined by hydrogel composition

We next sought to determine if similar differences in functional maturation could be detected in the cortical organoids. To evaluate electrophysiological activity in organoids, whole-cell patch-clamp electrophysiology was used to measure action potential generation and sEPSCs, two membrane and synaptic network properties that are positively correlated with neuronal maturation.39 sEPSCs were first recorded from neurons in Matrigel-embedded or GelMA-Cad-embedded organoids at day 90 of differentiation (Figure 5A). sEPSCs were detected in a higher percentage of neurons recorded from GelMA-Cad-embedded organoids than Matrigel-embedded organoids (Figure 5B). Additionally, neurons recorded from GelMA-Cad-embedded organoids displayed a significantly higher frequency of sEPSCs compared to Matrigel-embedded organoids (Figure 5C), while the low frequency of sEPSCs in Matrigel-embedded organoids was consistent with previous findings.40 The average sEPSC amplitudes between the groups were indistinguishable (Figure 5D). The frequency of sEPSCs in GelMA-Cad-derived neurons remained higher than Matrigel-derived neurons at day 120 of differentiation (Figures S6A and S6B). To investigate membrane excitability, we tested current-voltage relationships in whole-cell patch-clamp experiments. We injected increasing amounts of current starting from a holding potential of −70 mV (Figure 5E). Recordings revealed that neurons in GelMA-Cad-embedded organoids were able to generate a significantly greater number of action potentials relative to neurons from Matrigel-embedded organoids (Figure 5F). This trend was not sustained at day 120 of differentiation, where Matrigel and GelMA-Cad conditions were indistinguishable (Figures S6D and S6E). Taken together, these findings suggest an accelerated maturation of synaptic networks and an early maturation of membrane properties in cortical organoids embedded in GelMA-Cad hydrogels.

GelMA-Cad hydrogels generate radial glia diversity that more closely represents human embryonic development

The deep characterization of human radial glia molecular phenotypes in vivo has only recently begun to emerge due to limited tissue availability.41–43 Thus, recent publications in the cortical organoid field have placed substantial emphasis on methods that give rise to distinct radial glia populations, which may be responsible for producing molecularly diverse neurons in cortical organoids.11,44 Since we computationally noted differences in cellular diversity as a function of ECM properties and composition, we sought to connect these differences to radial glia signatures. In day 90 and 120 scRNA-seq data, top markers for each unbiased radial glia cluster were computed and annotated using Gene Ontology terms (Figure 6A; Tables S4 and S5). Seven clusters of biological interest were identified—radial glia expressing markers of apoptosis, cilia, neurogenesis, hypoxia, and ribosome biogenesis and two clusters expressing markers of response to ECM. At day 120, ciliated and neurogenic radial glia comprised 40% of radial glia in GelMA-CadLC and 52% of radial glia in GelMA-CadHC, whereas these radial glia populations were under-represented in GelMA- or Matrigel-embedded organoids (8%–18%; Figure 6B). When compared to a primary fetal tissue atlas, ciliated radial glia shared the most marker genes with gestational week 22 truncated radial glia, whereas neurogenic radial glia shared the most marker genes with gestational week 18 outer radial glia/astrocytes (Figure 6C). Three top transcripts enriched in the neurogenic radial glia cluster were HOPX (the classical outer radial glia marker), FABP5, and GFAP. Upon staining of thin sections, we confirmed the presence of triple-positive cells; again, due to challenges with thin-section imaging, we did not observe differences in abundance between conditions (Figures 6D, S7A, and S7B).

ECM-related genes were upregulated in two clusters of radial glia. Cluster 1 ECM-responding radial glia were detected in GelMA-Cad and Matrigel samples and shared the most marker genes with gestational week 18 outer radial glia/astrocytes. Under trajectory analysis, cluster 1 ECM-responding radial glia were the nearest neighbors to the glioblast/astrocytic cluster, further suggesting their annotation as early astrocytic progenitors (Figures 6E and S7C). In contrast, cluster 2 ECM-responding radial glia were highly enriched in GelMA (23% of radial glia in GelMALC and 33% of radial glia GelMAHC at day 120). This phenotype was also consistently detected in Matrigel controls (11% at day 90 and 12% at day 120) but comparably lower in both GelMA-Cad conditions (Figure 6B). Cluster 2 ECM-responding radial glia did not map well to any radial glial phenotypes in the fetal atlas, suggesting that these cell types are a deviation from typical cortical development. Interestingly, the proportion of cluster 2 ECM-responding radial glia was highly correlated with the proportions of total detected mesenchymal cells (R2 = 0.75), although trajectory analysis did not detect intermediate cell fates that could be reproducibly mapped (Figure 6F).

Lastly, all organoids harbored a population of radial glia expressing genes related to ribosome biogenesis, which mapped to the earliest gestational data (week 6) and were closely connected to unspecified neuron populations in the trajectory analysis, suggestive of their role in producing newborn neurons (Figure 6E). Overall, cortical organoids embedded in GelMA-Cad produced the smallest proportions of improper cell types while having the greatest representation of developmentally relevant cell types.

DISCUSSION

We report two primary advancements in this body of work. First, we demonstrate that the bioactive N-cadherin peptide mimetic, when conjugated to a gelatin ECM, can profoundly impact the fate trajectories of embedded cortical organoids. This outcome has wide-ranging implications for using other peptides that can activate diverse signaling pathways for the local manipulation of differentiation trajectories in organoids. Second, we demonstrate that optimized GelMA-Cad hydrogels support cortical organoid development, with notable differences relative to Matrigel, such as enhanced development of synaptic networks. These differences, along with the well-defined composition of GelMA-Cad and its ease of use, may motivate the replacement of Matrigel in differentiation protocols.

We selected the N-cadherin peptide motif as a cortical organoid differentiation cue of interest for several reasons. First, we have previous experience with N-cadherin-functionalized hydrogels, where we demonstrated that GelMA-Cad can support glutamatergic neurons.22 Moreover, others have shown that N-cadherin-functionalized 2D substrates can improve neural differentiation efficiency from pluripotent stem cells.45 Forced expression of N-cadherin has also been shown to improve neural differentiation in 3D aggregates of mouse induced pluripotent stem cells46 and monolayer carcinoma cells.47 Mechanistically, N-cadherin has a variety of known roles in neuron development and physiology by which it might support neural organoid differentiation workflows.26,28,48 N-cadherin is known to promote robust neurite extensions mediated through homophilic binding27; we observed strong neurite outgrowth in organoids developed for 20 days embedded in GelMA-Cad versus GelMA hydrogels. We also observed strong reinforcement of neural fate in GelMA-Cad as opposed to GelMA and Matrigel, which produced more mesenchymal-like cells. Overall, the differences in long-term fate trajectories between GelMA and Matrigel versus GelMA-Cad revealed the importance of this simple matrix-tethered cue and generally suggest an untapped strategy where a rich design space of peptide-functionalized hydrogels could be used to direct neural patterning in organoids, either in cortical systems or other regional differentiation paradigms.

Upper-layer neurons are of particular interest for various disease states, including Alzheimer’s disease, in part because they are selectively vulnerable to tauopathies49; the robust development of organoid models that harbor these cells will enable better direct interrogation of these cellular phenotypes in disease contexts. Thus, we focused on validating that these populations formed robustly within GelMA-Cad-embedded organoids. Indeed, organoids cultured in GelMA-CadLC produced between 10% and 20% SATB2+ neurons as estimated by scRNA-seq, which was significantly better than our Matrigel-embedded organoids generated in parallel. Interestingly, at 2 months, GelMA-Cad embedding produced SATB2+ neuron layer thicknesses more similar to sliced organoid cultures (~200-μm layer thicknesses)50 while avoiding the need for this more complex subculture method. Beyond upper-layer neuron populations, cortical organoids generated in GelMA-Cad were capable of supporting other developmentally relevant populations typically represented in previous protocols, marking GelMA-Cad as a compelling immediate alternative to Matrigel. For example, layer one RELN+ cells were robustly present in both sectioned slices and scRNA-seq datasets across time points. RELN is known to be indispensable to neuronal development and patterning,51 thus making it crucial to validate its presence in GelMA-Cad-derived organoids. Similarly, we detected a comparable presence of GFAP+ astrocytic cells in organoids embedded in Matrigel and GelMA-Cad. Astrocytes have received significant recent attention for their role in disease progression52 and are also known to mediate developmental processes,53 making their presence similarly crucial for future disease-focused studies that use GelMA-Cad as an organoid generation platform instead of Matrigel. Taken together, organoids embedded in GelMA-Cad could produce all major cell types of interest that differentiate through conventional Matrigel protocols.

In comparing our outcomes to other protocols utilizing Matrigel embedding, we noted several studies that could produce larger populations of upper-layer neurons (closer to 50%).1,2 One key difference is that these protocols employ an inhibitor of Wnt signaling in combination with a TGF-β signaling antagonist to promote neuroectoderm specification. Moreover, the duration and timing of TGF-β signaling inhibition is distinct from those employed in our studies. The necessity of Wnt signaling inhibition in cortical organoid differentiation and its role in fate specification generally remain uncertain, and these issues are part of larger calls to standardize organoid differentiation protocols for more reliable comparisons between studies. The use of a defined ECM advances standardization goals and motivates future testing of the GelMA-Cad hydrogel across these protocol variations.

Other efforts have also been made to move beyond Matrigel completely, but full characterization and standardization have not been achieved. For example, decellularized porcine brain ECM has been used for neural organoid culture,54,55 but this approach suffers from the same challenges as Matrigel, namely undefined composition and lack of control over mechanical properties. Neural organoids have also been differentiated in agarose to study the effects of constrained morphology30; however, long-term patterning and resultant neural diversity were not assessed. A hyaluronic-acid-based hydrogel with laminin peptide motifs has been developed to offer better control over tissue mechanics during neural organoid encapsulation, but outcomes were assessed at time points less than 3 weeks, excluding any long-term effects.56 As our results indicate, material properties remain important even over extended culture, and thus our efforts to comprehensively profile neural organoid differentiation over the first 4 months of culture is an important contribution to the full characterization of well-defined, engineered matrices.

To compare the effects of GelMA-Cad, GelMA, and Matrigel hydrogel compositions on organoid differentiation without additional stimuli, we chose to culture organoids under static conditions for all experiments. Flow conditions have been previously shown to improve neural organoid fate development9,55,57 and significantly impact cellular organization.58 However, GelMA-Cad hydrogels were readily capable of supporting robust neuron populations without dynamic flow or other strategies thought to improve material exchange between organoids and the bulk fluid. Because such mixing or flow strategies are not necessarily needed for organoids cultured in GelMA-Cad hydrogels, this technology platform also offers a simplified workflow for long-term culture. Further studies should investigate interactive effects of our biomaterials with fluid flow platforms, such as shaking platforms, stirred bioreactors, or in-well flow systems.59

Limitations of the study

Overall, we demonstrate that GelMA-Cad is a suitable hydrogel candidate for neural organoid differentiation that avoids the disadvantages of Matrigel, including the uncontrolled biochemical components and inaccessibility for further chemical modification,18,60 while also providing several unique advantages. However, gelatin is also naturally derived and can exhibit variability depending on the species source and preparation method, which can impact hydrogel characteristics.61 A continued transition of the field toward a fully defined matrix, to eliminate any concerns of batch-to-batch variability, remains a worthy goal. In addition, while scRNA-seq was effective at probing molecular and cellular differences, it remains unclear if GelMA-Cad alters organoid structural organization at late time points relative to Matrigel. While uniform dissociation of organoids for sequencing analyses is a matured technology, imaging of intact cortical organoids after clearing and antibody labeling is less developed in the literature. Once protocols become validated and high-fidelity antibodies are identified that maintain activity on cleared samples, such studies will be pursued to follow up on our initial work. We further note that while our scRNA-seq strategy was effective at detecting divergent cell phenotypes arising due to matrix properties, the pooling strategy utilized limited detailed characterizations of reproducibility from sequencing results alone. Lastly, electrophysiological measurements were well powered but low throughput, making the characterization of network-scale phenomena an unexplored potential feature of matrix embedding.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODELS AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Cell culture

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were maintained on growth factor reduced Matrigel (Corning). These studies utilized the CC362 and KOLF2.1J36 lines – unless otherwise noted, all experiments were performed using CC3 iPSCs. CC3 iPSCs were maintained in E8 basal media, while KOLF2.1J iPSCs were maintained in StemFlex (Gibco). Upon reaching 60–80% confluence, iPSCs were passaged using Versene (Gibco), and were not used beyond passage 40.

Organoid formation

Neural organoids were generated using the STEMdiff Cerebral Organoid Kit, with modifications. At day 0, iPSCs were aggregated in an ultra-low attachment 96-well plate (Corning/Nexcelcom), 9000 cells/well. Media was changed on days 2, 5, and 7. From days 2–7, cerebral organoid media was supplemented with dual-SMAD inhibitors SB43152 (Tocris 16-141-0) and LDN193189 dihydrochloride (Tocris 60-531-0). After embedding (see organoid embedding), organoids were kept in STEMdiff Cerebral Organoid Kit reagents (expansion media, days 7–10; maturation media, day 10 onwards) with no further modifications (Figure 1A).

METHOD DETAILS

GelMA synthesis

As in previous work,22 type A porcine skin gelatin (Sigma) was suspended in diH2O to make a 10% w/v solution and stirred at 60°C until dissolved (approx. 2 h). Methacrylic anhydride (Sigma) was then slowly added to the solution (0.6 mL/g gelatin) and allowed react for 3 h at 50°C, covered. The resulting solution was then centrifuged at 3220xg for 3 min, and the resultant supernatant was removed and separately mixed with additional prewarmed UltraPure water (Thermo Fisher) to quench the reaction. Dialysis in diH2O using 12 kDa dialysis tubing (Fisher) for 10 days was used to purify the reacted product. At the end of the dialysis, the pH was adjusted to 7.4 by adding 1M sodium bicarbonate. The final solution was then frozen down and lyophilized on a Labconco lyophilizer before storage at −20°C.

GelMA-Cad synthesis

GelMA-Cad was synthesized as previously reported by our group.22 A 10% w/v solution of GelMA was first prepared in Triethanolamine (Sigma) and brought to 37°C. The pH was then adjusted to 8 using 1M sodium bicarbonate. The Neural cadherin peptide (Ac-HAVDIGGGC, Genscript) was added to the GelMA to form a 0.1% w/v solution. The reaction was then allowed to proceed at 37°C overnight (~18 h). Afterward, the resulting solution was further diluted in MilliQ Ultrapure H2O and subjected to tangential flow filtration (Sartorius, 10k MWCO) for 6 h to remove unbound peptide. The resulting solution was then frozen down and lyophilized before storage at −20°C.

Hydrogel preparation

For methacryloyl hydrogels (GelMA and GelMA-Cad), 10% w/v suspensions were made in STEMdiff Cerebral Organoid Kit Basal Media 2, sterile filtered at 55°C, and then cooled to 37°C. Lithium phenyl-2- 4-6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) was measured to a 10 mg/mL stock concentration in STEMdiff Cerebral Organoid Kit Basal Media 2, and sterile filtered at 37°C. For experiments with reduced LAP concentration, stock 10 mg/mL LAP solution was diluted in additional STEMdiff Cerebral Organoid Kit Basal Media 2. To make hydrogel precursor solution, methacryloyl gelatin solution and LAP solution were mixed at a ratio of 1000:50 v/v.

Hydrogel characterization

Hydrogel identities were routinely verified through 1H-NMR using a Bruker 400 MHz NMR spectrometer at room temperature, after preparation in deuterium oxide. Spectra were calibrated to the deuterium oxide peak at 4.7 ppm. GelMA identity was confirmed by an increased peak at 1.9 ppm representing methyl protons of methacryloyl groups, a new proton peak at 5.6–5.7 ppm representing acrylic protons of methacryloyl groups, and a decrease at 3.0 ppm representing the previously free lysine signal. GelMA degree of substitution was quantified using a 2,4,6-trinitrobenzene-sulfonic acid (TNBS) assay, as previously described,68 and found to be 85% by comparison to an L-alanine standard curve and unmodified gelatin. A FITC-tagged N-cadherin peptide (FITC-HAVDIGGGC, Genscript) was conjugated to confirm the peptide conjugation strategy and estimate degree of functionalization. As previously described,69 FITC-conjugated samples were compared to non-fluorescently tagged GelMA-Cad controls for normalization.

For atomic force microscopy, hydrogel discs were prepared from hydrogel precursor solution (see hydrogel preparation) diluted in PBS. 35 μL of hydrogel precursor solution were then added to a RainX treated glass slides and covered with a 15 mm diameter coverslip (Carolina) to form disks. The discs were then exposed to 16 s of 25 mW/cm2 UV light to induce polymerization. A Bruker Dimension Icon Atomic Force Microscope was then used to measure hydrogel stiffness. Bruker SAA-SPH-5UM probes containing a 5 μm polystyrene bead were used to measure two distinct 5 × 5 μm areas of each hydrogel. Five hydrogel disc replicates of each sample were utilized (512 measurements per replicate). For each individual force curve, a first order baseline correction was performed, and the Hertzian model was used to calculate Young’s modulus. Each probe was calibrated at the manufacturer, with secondary calibration by thermal tune (no-touch) in fluid at the beginning of each imaging session. For additional tool calibration, polyacrylamide hydrogels (1kPa standard) were prepared and measured prior to GelMA or GelMA-Cad samples.

For scanning electron microscopy, hydrogel precursor solutions were crosslinked on double-sided carbon tape, attached to a Ted Pella pin mount, and frozen at −80°C overnight. The following day, the samples were transferred to a Labconco lyophilizer overnight and then stored at room temperature until used. The top surfaces of the gels were leveled using a scalpel. After gold sputtering, the samples were observed using a scanning electron microscope (Zeiss Merlin) at an accelerating voltage of 2 kV.

For rheological characterization, hydrogel precursor solutions were crosslinked directly onto an aluminum 8 mm parallel plate rheometer. A strain sweep step was performed to verify the linear region of strain, and then a frequency sweep step (0.1–100 Hz) was performed at 1% strain and 37°C. Hydrogels were likely to break at higher frequencies (>5 Hz); thus, storage and loss moduli were averaged between the 0.1 and 1 Hz measurements for each technical replicate.

To characterize the diffusion coefficient, hydrogels were loaded in custom made microfluidic chips and UV cross-linked. A solution of 100 μg/mL of 3 kDa FITC-dextran (Sigma-Aldrich) was prepared and pipetted into each microfluidic device containing the different hydrogels. Samples were then imaged on a Leica DMi8 epifluorescent microscope for a total duration of 10 min. The permeability coefficient was calculated utilizing the formula below70,71: P=1ΔtVmSAvΔImΔI

Here, Δt is the time difference between two images, Vm is the hydrogel volume, SAv stands for the surface area of the hydrogel, ΔI=Iv,1−Im,1 is the difference between the average fluorescence intensity between the central channel containing the FITC dextran and the hydrogel channel, and ΔIm=Im,2−Im,1 is the difference in average fluorescence intensity over time.

Organoid embedding

For UV-crosslinked culture conditions, day 7 organoids were collected from the 96-well plate using a multichannel pipette, and then transferred to a 50 mL conical. Excess media was aspirated after the organoids were allowed to settle. Hydrogel and respective crosslinking agent (see hydrogel preparation) were mixed with organoids and then crosslinked. For UV-crosslinking in glass-bottom 24-well plates (Corning), approximately 250 μL of hydrogel precursor solution and ~6 organoids per well were plated and then illuminated with UV from the top of the well. For UV-crosslinking in suspension, organoids were drawn up a 2.75 mm diameter glass capillary, and then UV-crosslinked. Crosslinking was facilitated with a UV Curing System (Thor Labs, CS2010) for 16 s at 25 mW/cm2. Capillaries were rotated 180° once during crosslinking to achieve even illumination (as UV light does not penetrate well through the organoids). A custom 3D-printed rig was used to hold the capillary during UV-crosslinking. The embedded organoids were then expelled into media (wells of a 6- or 12-well plate).

Live organoid imaging

Organoids were routinely checked under a brightfield microscope to assess size and shape. For live confocal imaging, calcein (AAT Bioquest) was diluted in 10 μL of sterile DMSO and added to media at a 1:1000 dilution. Organoids were allowed to incubate for 1 h before changing the media to remove residual calcein. Live confocal images were acquired with a Nikon spinning disk microscope.

Organoid fixing, immunostaining, and imaging

Collagenase type IV (Gibco) was diluted to 0.5 units/μL in Hank’s Buffered Saline Solution (HBSS, Gibco) to create a collagenase solution. To recover organoids from hydrogels, media on the organoids was aspirated, and organoids were washed with HBSS. Then, collagenase solution was added, and the organoids were incubated under standard tissue culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). When the hydrogel was sufficiently digested but the organoid was still intact (45–90 min), the collagenase solution was aspirated, and organoids were washed once with DPBS. For some experiments, organoids were fixed within the hydrogel, so the collagenase digestion step was omitted.

Organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and gently shaken on a table-top shaker for 15 min at room temperature. Next, organoids were washed three times with DPBS, 10 min each at room temperature with gentle shaking. Organoids were then incubated at 4°C in blocking buffer (5% v/v goat serum, Life Technologies; 0.3% v/v Triton X-100, Sigma) overnight.

Organoids were transferred to an imaging 96 well plate (Greiner, uClear). Then, organoids were incubated in primary antibodies (diluted in blocking buffer, see Antibodies) for 72 h at 4°C with gentle agitation. After three 10-min washes at room temperature with DPBS, organoids were incubated in secondary antibodies for an additional 72 h at 4°C with gentle agitation. After three 10-min washes at room temperature with DPBS, organoids were placed in a refractive index matching solution72 (60% v/v glycerol and 2.5 M fructose in diH2O). Confocal images were acquired with a Nikon spinning disk microscope.

D21 organoid preparation for scRNA-seq

The Worthington Papain dissociation kit was used to prepare organoids for scRNA-seq based on previous work.1 Papain, DNAse, and ovomucoid inhibitor powders were re-suspended in Earle’s media according to manufacturer specification. DNAse and papain solutions were gently mixed at a ratio of 1:10, and 1 μL of collagenase solution (100 units/μL in HBSS) was added. 5–8 organoids were pooled per well, and organoids were recovered from the hydrogels using collagenase as previously outlined (see organoid fixing, immunostaining, and imaging). After washing the recovered organoids, the media was exchanged for DNAse/papain/collagenase solution. The organoids were then incubated at standard tissue culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) for 1 h. Afterward, the organoids were dissociated by gently pipetting with a 1 mL pipette. The organoids were then returned to standard tissue culture conditions for an additional 10 min. Using a 10 mL pipette, the digested tissue was then homogenized by pipetting up and down ten times. After letting the debris settle, the cell solution (without debris) was transferred to an ovomucoid inhibitor solution (5 mL Earle’s media, 3 mL ovomucoid inhibitor) and spun down for 7 min at 300xg to pellet the cells. The supernatant was decanted, and the pellet was resuspended in cold 0.02% BSA in PBS. The cells were then pelleted and washed in 0.02% BSA on ice twice more before finally suspended to a final concentration of 1000 cells/μL. 5000 cells were encapsulated using the PIPseq method63 (PIPseq T2 kit v3 chemistry). The PIPseq manufacturer’s guide was then followed for library preparation. Reads were generated at a depth of 40M reads/2000 cell sample, from an Illumina NovaSeq6000 (S4) PE150 sequencing run.

D60 organoid preparation for scRNA-seq

Single-cell suspensions at day 60 were generated as described earlier. At the final step, the cell density was titered to 1000 cells/μL. 5000 cells were encapsulated using the PIPseq method (PIPseq T2 kit v4 chemistry). The PIPseq manufacturer’s guide was then followed for library preparation. Reads were generated at a depth of 125M reads/2000 cell sample, from an Illumina NovaSeq6000 (S4) PE150 sequencing run.

D90 and D120 organoid preparation for scRNA-seq

Single-cell suspensions at later time points were generated as described earlier. At the final step, the cell density was titered to 4000 cells/μL, and 40,000 cells were encapsulated using the PIPseq method (PIPseq T20 kit v4 chemistry). The PIPseq manufacturer’s guide was then followed for library preparation. Reads were generated at a depth of 400M reads per sample, from an Illumina NovaSeq6000 (S4) PE150 sequencing run.

Day 90 organoids generated from the KOLF2.1J iPSC line were also prepared similarly, titered to 1000 cells/μL (to encapsulate 5,000 cells) and detected using the PIPseq T2 kit v4PLUS chemistry. Reads were generated at a depth of 40M reads per sample, from an Illumina NovaSeq6000 (S4) PE150 sequencing run.

D90 cryosectioning and immunofluorescent labeling

Organoids were first digested out of the remaining matrices using collagenase, as previously described (see organoid fixing, immunostaining, and imaging). Organoids were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15–20 min at 4°C. After three washes in PBS, 30% sucrose was added dropwise to generate a sucrose gradient. Organoids were incubated at 4°C to allow equilibration and full penetration. A 7.5% gelatin/10% sucrose solution was prepared and warmed to 37°C; organoids were then embedded in cryo-molds surrounded completely by the gelatin/sucrose solution. After polymerization at 4°C for 15–20 min (to allow setting of the gelatin), the embedded organoids were flash frozen (1–2 min) in an isopentane dry ice bath between −50°C and −30°C. After flash freezing, samples were stored at −80°C, sectioned on a cryostat, and mounted on slides.

For immunofluorescent labeling of sectioned slides, slides were first allowed to thaw to room temperature and then washed three times with PBS. Tissues were then permeabilized for 10 min with 0.2% Tween 20 at room temperature. After three PBS washes, tissues were then blocked using 5% BSA in PBS at room temperature for 2 h. Primary antibodies with 1% BSA in PBS were applied overnight at 4°C. The next day, secondary antibodies with 1% BSA in PBS were applied for 3 h at room temperature, followed by three PBS washes. Finally, Prolong Gold with DAPI (Thermo Fisher) was applied, coverslips were mounted, and nail polish was used to seal the slide. Slides were imaged on a Leica DM8 epifluorescence microscope.

Electrophysiological characterization

Whole-cell recordings were performed after transferring organoids to artificial cerebral spinal fluid (aCSF, in mM: 119 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.3 MgCl2-6H2O, 2.5 CaCl2-2H2O, 1.0 NaH2PO4-H2O, 26.2 NaHCO3, and 11 glucose; 290–295 mOsm) at room temperature and patched with 4–6 MΩ recording pipettes (P1000 Micropipette Puller) filled with a potassium-gluconate based internal solution (in mM: 135 K-gluconate, 5 NaCl, 2 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 0.2 EGTA, 2.5 Mg2-ATP, 0.2 Na2-GTP). Spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) were collected while holding cells at −70 mV in the voltage-clamp configuration. For current injections, cells were held at a membrane potential of −70 mV, then injected with progressively increasing levels of depolarizing current in 25 pA steps over a period of 0.5 s per sweep. Inclusion criteria for cells were an access resistance (RA) of <20 mOhms, and a stable holding current of < −100 pA at −70 mV. Recordings were low-pass filtered at 2 kHz, and digitized at 20 kHz using a DigiData 1550A (Molecular Devices). Data collected from electrophysiology experiments were initially analyzed in Clampfit 10.7 and then graphed using GraphPad Prism 10.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Sample sizes and blinding

No statistical methods were used to determine sample sizes, nor were investigators blinded to experimental conditions.

D21 scRNA-seq data analysis

For day 21 organoids, reads were aligned using the PIPseeker pipeline, at a resolution of 5, then read into Seurat64 (v4) in R. Cells with 1800–6000 RNA features were retained, and counts were normalized and scaled. Principal component analysis (PCA) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) were used to visualize the data, while the Seurat - implemented Louvain algorithm was used to cluster cells. Cells were annotated by correlation to a previously published fetal database and canonical marker gene expression.12,73 Cells were annotated with canonical markers genes as follows: Radial glia (SOX2+, HOPX+), Cycling radial glia (TOP2A+), Intermediate progenitors/neurons (EOMES+, or MAPT+, MAP2+), Neural placode (SIX1+, ISL1+)/Neural crest (FOXD3+), and Mesenchymal cells (COL1A1+, TWIST1+).

To compare non-cycling radial glia, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were calculated for each pairwise matrix comparison (GelMA v Matrigel, GelMA-Cad v Matrigel, LC v HC, and GelMA-Cad v GelMA) using default parameters in Seurat. Pathway analysis of each DEG list was conducted using clusterProfiler.65 To visualize the pathways and genes, sub-comparison DEG values were calculated (ex. GelMALC v Matrigel and GelMAHC v Matrigel), and the average log2 fold change of each comparison was plotted against one another on a scatterplot.

D60 scRNA-seq data analysis

For day 60 organoids, reads were aligned using the PIPseeker pipeline, at a resolution of 5, then read into Seurat64 (v4) in R. Cells with 1000–4000 RNA features were retained, and counts were normalized and scaled. Granular filtering for metabolic stress was applied to screen out metabolically stressed cells, as this has been previously shown to improve data analysis of cortical organoids.13 PCA and UMAP were used to visualize the data, while the Seurat-implemented Louvain algorithm was used to cluster cells. Cells were annotated by correlation to a previously published fetal database and canonical marker gene expression.12,73 Cells were annotated with canonical markers genes as follows: Radial glia (SOX2+, HOPX+), Intermediate progenitors/cycling cells (EOMES+, TOP2A+), RELN+ Inhibitory neurons, DLX5+ Inhibitory neurons, Non-specified neurons (MAPT+, MAP2+), Deep layer neurons (BCL11B+, FEFZF2+), Upper layer neurons (SATB2+, CUX2+), and Choroid plexus epithelial cells (TTR+, CLIC6+).

To compare excitatory neurons, DEGs were calculated for each pairwise matrix comparison (GelMA v Matrigel, GelMA-Cad v Matrigel, LC v HC, and GelMA-Cad v GelMA) using default parameters in Seurat. Pathway analysis of each DEG list was conducted using ClusterProfiler.65 To visualize the pathways and genes, sub-comparison DEG values were calculated (ex. GelMALC v Matrigel and GelMAHC v Matrigel), and the average log2 fold change of each comparison was plotted against one another on a scatterplot. Finally, the cells were arranged in pseudotime by importing the dataset into Monocle66 (v3) and setting the starting node at the radial glia cell cluster.

D90 and D120 scRNA-seq data analysis

Reads were aligned using the PIPseeker pipeline, at a resolution of 5, then read into Scanpy.74 Cells with between 2000 and 8000 features and less than 5 percent mitochondrial reads were kept, and then processed based on the top 2000 most variable genes. Samples were integrated using the Harmony algorithm,75 as samples were sequenced in three batches. After scaling and normalization, PCA and t-stochastic network embedding (t-SNE) were employed to provide low dimensional embedding. Cells were first annotated with canonical markers genes as follows: Glioblast/Astrocytes (GFAP+), Radial glia (SOX2+, HOPX+, CLU+), Intermediate progenitors/cycling cells (EOMES+, TOP2A+), RELN+ Inhibitory neurons, DLX5+ Inhibitory neurons, Non-specified neurons (MAPT+, MAP2+), Deep layer neurons (BCL11B+, FEFZF2+), Upper layer neurons (SATB2+, CUX2+), Choroid plexus epithelial cells (TTR+, CLIC6+), and Mesenchymal cells (COL1A1+, COL4A1+). Cells were also annotated by direct integration with the human neural organoid atlas using the Scanpy ingest function.14

Cell proportions were extracted per condition. To understand the relative contributions of peptide presence and crosslinker concentration on cell proportions, cell proportional change was defined as Proportionalchangecell_type_A=Xcell_type_AYcell_type_A

where X and Y are the proportion of cell type A in pooled samples, with X>Y. Samples were pooled with regard to peptide presence or with regard to crosslinker concentration. Radial glia were further interrogated by calculating differentially expressed genes (DEGs) per cluster and running over-representation analysis on DEGs using online gProfiler tools. Top driver gene ontology terms for biological processes and cellular components were then used to annotate the radial glia clusters.

To score cell maturity and pathway activation, the Scanpy dataframe was imported to Seurat, and then scored for KEGG pathways: axon guidance – hsa04360, glutamatergic synapse – hsa04724, GABAergic synapse – hsa04727. Moreover, cells were scored for neural maturation and single-cell entropy, based on previous methods.33,35

Cells derived from KOLF2.1J organoids were analyzed in Seurat as previously described (see D60 scRNA-seq data analysis). Cells were annotated by the same strategy as CC3-derived late-stage organoids.

Comparison to fetal atlas

To compare organoid cell phenotypes to primary tissue, cells derived from GelMA-CadHC and Matrigel from all time points (D21, D60, D90, D120) were integrated and dimensionally reduced with a previously published fetal atlas.12 Canonical correlation analysis, PCA, UMAP, and Leiden clustering were used to visualize and cluster cells. Organoid-derived cells were annotated from the primary atlas, by transferring the majority primary cluster annotation. Clusters with no primary cells were labeled as such. Cell stress metrics (glycolysis and ER stress) were computed using the granular clustering method implemented in the Gruffi package.13

To further compare radial glia, DEGs per primary radial glia cluster were computed in both the fetal atlas and organoid-derived cells. Afterward, the number of overlapping marker genes per organoid-derived and fetal atlas-derived clusters were compared.

Connectivity trajectory analysis

To compute connectivity between clusters, scRNA-seq data were split into respective timepoints (day 90 and day 120) and extracellular matrices (GelMA, GelMA-Cad, Matrigel). Each data subset was then re-normalized and embedded using PCA and UMAP, based on the first 50 principal components. Based on the annotation from the bulk dataset, cell populations containing greater than 100 cells were then extracted and subsampled down to 100 cells each. For each pairwise comparison of cell populations, a connectivity metric was calculated, as defined below, based on previous work.76

Iteratively, the k nearest neighbors were computed for each cell, with k being set at each decile of number of cells. For each k nearest neighbors, an adjacency matrix was constructed, and the normalized median number of connected neighbors (by any path length) were then computed. A smooth function was then fit to model normalized median number of connected neighbors based on number of k nearest neighbors. The connectivity metric was then defined as the integral across the smoothed function. The connectivity metric was computed at each time point for each matrix, and then averaged across samples. By this construction, high connectivity metrics indicate high connectedness between cells, and therefore, greater likelihood of intermediate fates. A non-directed graph representation was used, filtering out lower value connections, after scaling of connection values.

Organoid image processing

Images were processed in ImageJ-FIJI (v1.53t) or CellProfiler (v4.2.6). For area and circularity measurements, organoids were hand-traced. To obtain neurite lengths, the plugin Neurite-J 1.1 was used.77 Neural rosette structures were quantified using a thresholding method, based on the ZO-1 stain. For SATB2 layer thickness measurements, images were contrast adjusted and then hand traced. Eight thickness measurements approximately spaced at 45-degree increments (technical replicates) were taken of each image, and then averaged into one value (biological replicate). RELN+ and GFAP+ areas were calculated based on a global threshold. The DAPI+ area was used to normalize RELN+ and GFAP+ area measurements. FABP5+/HOPX+ areas were calculated based on the overlap of positive areas from global thresholds of each channel. To segment cells into types based on SATB2 and CTIP2 labeling, CellProfiler was used. Nuclei were segmented using the IdentifyPrimaryObjects function, based on the DAPI channel; CTIP2 and SATB2 signals per nuclei were then computed to categorize cells as positive or negative for said markers.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSES

Measurements of hydrogel properties (Young’s modulus, storage modulus, loss modulus, and permeability) were analyzed with a two-way ANOVA in R, modeled on crosslinker concentration and peptide presence. Measurements from immunofluorescent images were analyzed with a one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post-hoc in R. sEPSC frequency was analyzed with a Student’s t-test in GraphPad Prism. Action potential generation rates were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s post-hoc test in GraphPad Prism.
