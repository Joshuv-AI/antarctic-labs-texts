Provision of acute oncology services in the UK: data from the Society for Acute Medicine Benchmarking Audit 2022 (SAMBA22)
Authors: Sanat Kulkarni, Thomas Knight, Tim Cooksley, Ernie Marshall, Neil Patel, Radha Selvaratnam, Catherine Atkin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Acute oncology services (AOS) manage acute cancer-related presentations alongside acute medical teams. This study assessed AOS provision against national peer review measures and the burden of acute cancer-related admissions. The 2022 Society for Acute Medicine Benchmarking Audit surveyed UK hospitals, collecting hospital-level and patient-level data for all medical admissions over a 24-h period. Logistic regression models were constructed to identify differences in patient outcomes for cancer-related admissions. Most hospitals (n=120 or 91.6%) reported having an AOS. There was heterogeneity in AOS provision, with many failing to meet peer-review measures. Of the 7,116 patients, 542 (7.6%) were cancer-related admissions. Cancer-related admissions had greater clinical acuity (p<0.05), length of stay (p<0.001) and 14-day mortality (adjusted odds ratio (OR)=3.54, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.41–5.22, p<0.001) compared with other medical admissions. Increasing availability of AOS with integration of ambulatory pathways are vital next steps to improving care for acute cancer-related admissions.

Introduction

The rising burden of cancer in the UK is placing greater pressure on acute care services.1, 2 Currently, up to a quarter of new cancer diagnoses are made following acute admission,3, 4 whereas 15% of acute medical admissions occur as a result of cancer-related issues.5, 6 It follows that greater incidence and survivorship will translate into increased acute cancer-related admissions.6, 7 Coupled with a drive toward increasingly specialist oncology treatments, this poses a significant challenge to acute care teams.8

Acute oncological presentations can be broadly classified into three types of presentation: (1) patients with a new or suspected new diagnosis of cancer; (2) patients presenting with complications or toxicity from cancer treatment; and (3) patients presenting with symptoms and complications of the cancer itself, such as progression of disease.8, 9 Following concerns over the care of inpatients receiving cancer treatment,10 the National Chemotherapy Advisory Group 2009 report recommended the establishment of an acute oncology service (AOS) in all hospitals with an emergency department (ED),11, 12 tasked with managing the above acute oncological presentations. AOS are typically multidisciplinary teams, comprising oncologists, physicians and acute oncology clinical nurse specialists.8, 13 The fundamental role of the AOS is the integration of specialist oncology input and co-ordination across acute care pathways, promoting education of healthcare professionals and facilitating guideline development.5, 8, 14 AOS has a key role at all stages in the patient pathway (Fig 1).15Fig 1Simplified patient pathways for acute oncology admissions, adapted from the Acute Oncology Measures report.15 AMU = acute medical unit; AOS = acute oncology service; ED = emergency department; GP = general practice; SDEC = same-day emergency care.

Acute medical teams manage 40% of non-elective cancer admissions,6 within services such as the acute medical unit (AMU) and same-day emergency care (SDEC), previously referred to as ambulatory emergency care (AEC). Given that many acute healthcare providers lack specialist oncology training, the National Cancer Action Team's (NCAT) 2017 peer-review measures recommend that treatment protocols for acute oncology presentations should be accessible in all acute care areas, such as ED and AMU.15, 16 Further measures include having an electronic patient alert or flagging system for those who have received chemotherapy or radiotherapy over the previous 6 weeks, which should be viewable when first assessed in ED or AMU.15, 16

Early evidence suggests that acute cancer-related admissions have a significantly longer length of hospital stay compared with other admissions.6 Further studies demonstrate that AOS can significantly reduce length of stay, improve patient satisfaction and be cost-saving.3, 9, 17 Despite its increasing importance, there is minimal literature detailing the current provision of AOS across the UK. There is thought to be marked heterogeneity in the structure, availability of services and patient pathways across different AOS, hypothesised to be related to hospital size, availability of on-site oncology services and service demand.13, 14 Understanding current service provision, in relation to existing recommendations and peer-review measures, can identify key areas for improving acute oncology care. In addition, national-level data on the burden of acute oncology admissions are lacking; understanding the nature of these admissions, patient pathways and their associated clinical outcomes is vital in guiding future service planning.

The annual Society for Acute Medicine Benchmarking Audit (SAMBA) utilises a 24-h ‘day of care’ survey to evaluate acute medical care in the UK. The 2022 audit (SAMBA22) was used to assess the provision of AOS in acute medicine across the UK.18

Methods

Study design and population

SAMBA22 is a national, cross-sectional study, which took place on 23 June 2022.18 Participation in SAMBA was open to all UK hospitals accepting unplanned admissions to acute and general internal medicine; community hospitals and those without unplanned medical attendances were excluded. Participation in SAMBA22 was voluntary, with registration via the Society for Acute Medicine website. Local registration and approvals were obtained by individual sites, including Caldicott Guardian approval. The full protocol for SAMBA22 is available online.19

Data collection and outcomes

Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the University of Birmingham.20, 21 A unit-level organisational survey was completed, including size of hospital (total number of inpatient beds), alongside patient-level data for all medical admissions over a single 24-hour period. Additional hospital-level organisational questions concerning acute oncology were designed to assess the availability of AOS, and to reflect the NCAT peer review measures, focusing on acute oncology pathways and available SDEC-specific guidance (supplementary material S1).

Patient-level data included patient age and gender, Clinical Frailty Score (CFS), presence of an Advanced Care Plan (ACP) and community DNACPR decision, assessment locations within the acute care pathway and acuity, assessed by National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2) and presence of acute kidney injury (AKI). Data were also collected regarding patients’ clinical outcomes at 14 days, including length of stay, intensive care unit (ICU) admission and all-cause mortality. Outcomes were recorded at 14 days to allow evaluation of 14-day readmission rates for acute medical admissions within the primary audit.19 At a patient level, it was recorded whether the acute admission was cancer related and, if so, the type of acute oncology presentation the patient attended with.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using the statistical package STATA 16 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA). Given that data were not normally distributed, descriptive statistics, Kruskal–Wallis and chi-squared tests were used to compare the provision of AOS against NCAT peer-review measures and between different units. These tests were subsequently used to identify differences in patient characteristics, pathways and outcomes between cancer and non-cancer-related admissions. Further binary logistic regression models were constructed to identify differences in clinical outcomes for cancer-related admissions (compared with other admissions), adjusted for age, gender, CFS, AKI and a NEWS2 of 3 or more on admission. These variables were prospectively identified as potential confounders based on previous analyses from SAMBA 2021.22 Adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) are presented for the logistic regression outcomes. Although p<0.05 was considered statistically significant, we recommend interpreting these p-values with consideration of the number of tests conducted and subsequently greater risk of type 1 error. We encourage readers to consider the magnitude of differences between groups; p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons have also been included with the supplementary material.

Ethical approval

Health Research Authority (HRA) approval was obtained for secondary longitudinal data analysis of non-identifiable data (21/HRA/4196). All data were anonymised at the point of data collection; no patient-identifiable data were transferred from participating sites.

Results

Results were divided into unit-level results, assessing the provision of AOS at a unit level and compliance with national peer-review measures, and patient-level results, describing the characteristics and clinical outcomes of cancer-related admissions in comparison to other acute medical admissions.

Within SAMBA22, patient-level data were submitted by 152 units from 149 hospitals, with 147 units also submitting unit-level data. In total, 131 hospitals (89.1%) responded to the unit-level questions concerning AOS. Of the 7,239 unplanned admissions included within SAMBA22, 7,116 (97.6%) had data regarding whether the admission was related to a known or suspected cancer diagnosis, with 65 (0.9%) recorded as ‘unknown’ and the remainder not documented.

Unit-level results

Availability of AOS: Of the 131 responding units, 120 (91.6%, or 81.6% of all units) reported presence of an AOS. In addition, 38.2% of respondents were centres with dedicated inpatient oncology services, and 31.3% of units were a tertiary cancer referral centre/dedicated cancer unit.

AOS pathways and national peer-review measures: Of the 128 units with an ED, 10 (7.8%) did not have an AOS. Most units (56.9%) did not have access to either AOS ‘hot clinics’ or malignancy of unknown origin (MUO) clinics for referral from AMU (Fig 2A). Considering the NCAT peer-eview measures, most units had a febrile neutropenia pathway (82.4%), and 42.3% had an alert system embedded within their patient records for those receiving oncology treatment. Referrals to AOS: Of the 120 hospitals with an AOS, 46 (38.3%) ran a 7-day service, including 23 (19.2%) that provided a continuous service (Fig 3A). Almost all units (92.5%) accepted inpatient referrals to AOS, but only 35.8% permitted direct admission to the AOS, thereby bypassing both the ED and AMU. Most AOS units received referrals for all oncological emergencies (77.3%) and complications of cancer (73.1%), but only 47.5% received referrals for all new suspected diagnoses of cancer. Factors associated with hospital size: There were no significant associations between the size of hospital comparing those with: an AOS (p=0.921), access to AOS clinics (p=0.817), a febrile neutropenia pathway (p=0.315), an acute medicine lead for oncology (p=0.517), an embedded oncology treatment alert system (p=0.722) or SDEC-specific oncology guidance in any category (p=0.596) (supplementary material S2).

For the 120 hospitals with an AOS, there was no significant difference in hospital size associated with running a 24/7 (p=0.157) or 7-days-a-week service (p=0.227), accepting inpatient referrals to AOS (p=0.138) or allowing direct AOS admissions (p=0.868). There was no significant difference between which types of acute oncology presentation were referred to AOS and the size of hospital (p>0.05 for all). However, smaller hospitals were more likely to advise patients to call the AOS hotline to attend ED rather than a potential combination of either ED, AMU,or a dedicated oncology ward (p=0.007) (supplementary material S2).

Factors associated with specialist units or acute medicine leads for oncology: Tertiary cancer referral or specialist units were not significantly more likely to have an AOS (p=0.545) but were more likely to run a continuous (32.5% versus 13.8%, p=0.017) or 7-day-a-week AOS service (55.0% versus 30.0%, p=0.008) (supplementary material S3). Hospitals with an acute medicine lead for oncology or acute oncology were more likely to have SDEC-specific guidelines across all categories, although this only reached significance for guidance for complications of cancer treatment (25.9% versus 9.1%, p=0.042) (supplementary material S4).

Patient-level results

Cancer-related admissions: Of the 7,116 patients included, 542 (7.6%) were admitted because of a known or suspected cancer diagnosis (henceforth considered a ‘cancer-related’ admission or presentation). This was related to cancer progression in 252 (46.5%) of these admissions, with complications of cancer treatment in 166 (30.6%), and as a result of a new or suspected cancer diagnosis in 164 (30.3%). Furthermore, 56 (10.3%) cancer-related admissions were for those on 2 week-wait (2WW) pathways.

Discussion

This study evaluated the provision of AOS within acute medicine services nationally, in relation to existing peer-review measures. Although most acute medicine services had access to AOS, 10 (7.8%) units with an ED did not have an AOS. In addition, 18.6% of acute cancer-related admissions were to a unit without an AOS, highlighting the need to either increase their availability or improve signposting to existing services (where clinically appropriate and geographically feasible). Other nationally agreed peer-review measures were unmet, in particular incorporation of a flag or alert system for those patients receiving cancer treatments; this can directly alert AOS teams to facilitate more rapid review and improve patient safety.9

Most units lacked specific guidance for acute oncology presentations in the ambulatory setting and acute oncology patients were less likely to be seen in or discharged from SDEC compared with other medical admissions. Acute ambulatory care in SDEC settings has been shown to safely and effectively manage common acute oncology presentations, such as low-risk febrile neutropenia, toxicities from immune checkpoint inhibitors and malignant hypercalcaemia, thereby reducing admissions.23, 24, 25, 26 Although the limited utilisation of such pathways observed in our data could relate to the higher acuity of acute oncology patients, a lack of local ambulatory-specific guidelines, access to AOS ‘hot clinics’ and experience of managing such presentations in SDEC settings could also be contributory factors, as could availability of the required side-room facilities in these settings. However, most cancer-related admissions had a low NEWS2 score, no AKI and higher frailty, suggesting a potential missed opportunity for ambulatory management and admission avoidance.

Implementation of existing national guidelines, which can be integrated with local pathways, is an important next step in the implementation of ambulatory acute oncology care.27 This can help ensure that opportunities to facilitate care without admission to an inpatient bed area are maximised. The presence of an acute physician lead for acute oncology, encouragingly reported at one in five hospitals, had greater ambulatory guideline provision, highlighting the importance of fostering co-ordination between acute medicine and oncology.14 National guidance for the management of acute oncology presentations could be a route for improvement, building on resources such as the Royal College of Physicians toolkit28 and the UK Acute Oncology Nursing Society's initial management guidelines.29 Furthermore, as SDEC pathways are implemented, providing additional acute oncology training to acute medical teams, and improving liaison with AOS teams are essential next steps.

There was heterogeneity in the availability and pathways of AOS across the UK. In contrast to previous reports,13 variability within our study was largely unexplained by hospital size or access to on-site oncology services, although specialist units were more likely to offer a continuous service. Alternatively, these differences could be explained by other hospital-level factors outside the scope of this study, such as local demands and resource availability14 or nearby alternative services. Our results suggest that fewer than half of AOS received referrals for acute admissions with new diagnoses of cancer. However, this patient group poses challenges because oncologists are often not involved until a cancer diagnosis is confirmed.3 There is evidence that a referral system for this cohort of patients is feasible, improving length of stay while being cost-saving,3 highlighting an area of improvement for most AOS in the UK.

Almost one in 13 acute medical admissions were cancer related in our study, compared with approximately one in 20 in a previous single-centre study.6 In contrast to Berger et al.,6 most admissions were as a result of cancer progression. This could reflect improved outpatient diagnostic pathways reducing new diagnoses in emergency admissions, or better prevention of treatment complications. As cancer survivorship improves and oncology treatments reduce in toxicity, a need arises to improve pathways and integrate palliative care services for the increasing volume of patients presenting with progressive disease. Furthermore, our data show that one in 10 cancer-related admissions are patients already on a 2WW pathway; this could reflect the increasing numbers of 2WW referrals, increasing failure to meet the 2WW time target30 or patients’ concerns to expedite their assessment. Additional longitudinal data are necessary to determine whether this is rising further.

However, this study has some limitations. First, SAMBA22 provides only a snapshot of admissions over 24 h and there might be variability in patterns of presentation over time. There are additional factors that might affect outcomes and length of stay, such as multiple health conditions and reason for admission that were beyond the scope of the study. We did not distinguish between solid tumours and haematological malignancies, which would allow a more complete understanding of the nature of acute oncology admissions.

Cancer-related admissions included those with a ‘suspected’ cancer diagnosis; however, this could overestimate the number of admissions caused by underlying malignancy. Malignancy is often included within the differential diagnosis of acute medical admissions, and ‘suspected’ cancer can include a proportion of these patients, overestimating the number of acute cancer-related admissions. In future studies, using alternative measures, such as malignancy as the most likely diagnosis or referrals onto suspected urgent cancer pathways might provide more accurate estimates.

The data within SAMBA are collected by acute medicine teams; patients admitted directly to acute oncology admissions units were not included within SAMBA, therefore providing an underestimate of the overall number of unplanned hospital admissions that are cancer related. Data collected directly from AOS could provide some insights into this patient group. Although our dataset had minimal missing data overall, 26% of CFS scores were missing, which might have affected our findings concerning frailty. Moreover, we recognise the increased risk of type 1 error given the number of statistical tests conducted in this study; further research within AOS can help determine whether these are ‘true’ differences.

Finally, response rate should be considered. There is a small risk of responder bias within the data, because units with an AOS might have been more likely to respond to the questions regarding provision of these services. Participation in SAMBA is voluntary and there might be systematic differences in those hospitals that did not participate. Those who do participate represent approximately two-thirds of UK acute medical services, including over 75% of services within England, and hospital size of participants is comparable to national datasets, suggesting the generalisability of these results.

This study focuses on the integration of AOS within AMU/SDEC services and, therefore, centres around medical admissions rather than ED presentations. Although this integrated model is favoured in the UK, alternative models exist worldwide. For instance, units in the USA and Australia have established cancer-dedicated EDs, urgent care centres14 and ambulatory assessment units to treat patients with undifferentiated cancer, with the latter demonstrating reduced admission rates and improved patient satisfaction.25 However, widespread implementation of such models is dependent on patient volume and geographical factors.14 Further research into the effectiveness of such models and potential integration with existing AOS in the UK is necessary.

Future directions of AOS in the UK

Our results suggest that ambulatory pathways are underutilised for acute cancer-related admissions and highlight a potential important area of development for AOS in the UK. Implementing ambulatory pathways and cancer assessment units will facilitate empirical comparative studies to identify optimal models of care for acute oncology patients. Perhaps more pressingly, improving compliance with national peer review through regular audits is an important next step and could mitigate the identified discrepancies in patient outcomes. Similarly, future reassessment of the burden and outcomes of acute oncology admissions on a national level can provide an indication of trends in acute cancer care in the UK and guide future service planning. However, the shift toward integrated care systems (ICS) within the NHS has placed the responsibility for AOS on ‘place-based partnerships’. Moving forward, effective AOS delivery will require Cancer Alliances to take a leadership role in acute cancer care underpinned by a robust partnership at the ICS level between place-based urgent & emergency care and cancer services.

Conclusion

Despite recognition of the need for improvements in outcomes for acute cancer-related admissions, there remains considerable variability in provision of AOS across the UK. Increasing access and availability of these services with integration of ambulatory pathways are vital next steps to improving provision of acute oncology care.

Summary

What is known?

AOS can significantly improve patient outcomes and cost-efficiency for acute cancer-related admissions.

What is the question?

What is the provision of AOS across the UK, particularly in relation to nationally agreed peer-review measures, and is provision influenced by hospital size? What is the nature and clinical outcomes of acute cancer-related admissions and do these differ compared with other acute medical admissions?

What was found?

Although most hospitals have an AOS, there is marked heterogeneity in availability and pathways (unexplained by hospital size), with many failing to meet the assessed peer-review measures. Acute cancer-related admissions (7.6% of all acute medical admissions) had greater clinical acuity, longer length of stay and greater 14-day mortality compared with other medical admissions.

What is the implication for practice now?

Improving access to, and availability of, AOS, monitoring compliance with peer review measures and implementation of ambulatory acute oncology pathways could offer ways to improve the clinical outcomes of acute cancer-related admissions.
