Records of mantle geodynamics and atmospheric escape in Archean quartz
Authors: Félix Vayrac, Guillaume Avice, Xinmu J. Zhang, Rita Parai, Pascal Philippot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Early planetary degassing and atmospheric escape are two major, yet unconstrained, processes that shaped early Earth. Modeling predicts that the atmospheric 20Ne/22Ne ratio is sensitive to solar-Ne mantle degassing over geological time. Until the Great Oxidation Event, atmospheric escape progressively depletes the atmosphere in Xe, leaving an isotopic imprint. However, the quantity of xenon in the ancient atmosphere remains largely unknown. In this study, we analyzed noble gases in Archean hydrothermal quartz fluid inclusions and show that a modern atmospheric 20Ne/22Ne ratio was almost reached 2.7 Ga ago, implying intense mantle degassing during the first 1.7 billion years of Earth’s history, three orders of magnitude higher than today. Furthermore, we determine an Archean atmospheric Xe/Kr ratio, 2.3 times higher than today, consistent with models of Xe depletion over time through atmospheric escape.

INTRODUCTION

Atmospheric escape and early degassing of volatile elements are two major processes which shaped early Earth. The atmosphere of the primitive Earth had a chemical composition totally different from the modern one and evolved over geological time to reach the actual composition (1). A radical change in the history of the atmosphere is the Great Oxidation Event, which occurred ~2.3 billion years ago (2), corresponding to a rise in the partial pressure of dioxygen, but the causes of this change remain debated. Hydrogen escape could offer a possible explanation for the oxidation of the atmosphere (3, 4), but the timescale and extent of this escape remain difficult to assess and depend on various parameters such as the luminosity of the young Sun or the availability of hydrogen.

Our understanding of the past Earth’s mantle geodynamics is also limited. The Earth’s interior was hotter in the past, with mantle potential temperatures up to 250°C higher than the modern one, during the Archean (5). Nevertheless, the degassing history of the Earth’s mantle remains debated. Thermal evolution models (6, 7) postulate that the heat flow was higher during the Archean, inducing a higher degassing rate than today. However, studies of Hadean zircons show evidence of a lower heat flow in the past (8). Other studies (9, 10) revealed that the flux was lower or similar to that of today due to a possible slowdown of the plate tectonic regime. This change in the tectonic regime can be explained by higher mantle temperature, increasing the depth of partial melting at the ridges and, thus, inducing an increase in the thickness of the oceanic crust (oceanic lithosphere harder to subduct).

Temporal changes of geodynamics and atmospheric escape potentially led to variations in the elemental abundance and isotopic compositions of volatile elements contained in the Earth’s atmosphere, which has important implications for the habitability of our planet (1, 11). Noble gases are powerful tracers of these processes, especially due to their chemical inertness. For example, argon is a useful tracer for the degassing history of the Earth’s mantle by following the evolution of the 40Ar/36Ar ratio of the atmosphere over time (12), since its evolution is more sensitive to mantle degassing than continental crust growth (13). The 40Ar/36Ar ratio of the paleo-atmosphere can be retrieved by measuring the argon composition hosted in previously irradiated hydrothermal quartz. Nevertheless, during sample analysis, the measured ratio must be corrected for 40Ar* excesses, either due to in situ β-decay of 40K or to the incorporation of “parentless” 40Ar (from fluid-rock interactions), often leading to large uncertainties for the corrected paleo-atmospheric 40Ar/36Ar ratio. The slight depletion of radiogenic 129Xe in Archean samples [barite, hydrothermal quartz, and organic matter (14–17)] relative to the modern atmosphere has also been used to track the degassing history of Earth’s mantle (14, 17). Nevertheless, this depletion can sometimes be blurred by mantle contributions (18), and subsequent data on a Barberton Greenstone Belt sample did not reveal any 129Xe depletion (19), showing that a clear record of mantle degassing throughout time remains elusive.

Neon can also be used as a tracer of mantle degassing. It has been previously proposed that the isotopic composition of modern atmospheric neon results from the isotopic fractionation of solar-type neon during hydrodynamic escape of the early atmosphere (20). However, this process seems unable to explain the high atmospheric 36Ar/22Ne ratio [18.7; (21)] compared to what it should be for a residual atmosphere, after hydrodynamic escape (~1.6) (22, 23) but cannot be excluded (24). Furthermore, the fact that the atmospheric 15N/14N ratio did not change significantly (within few permil) since the Archean suggests that no atmospheric escape of di-nitrogen (m = 28) occurred (25). The mass of neon being relatively close to that of di-nitrogen, this suggests that not much neon has been escaping from Earth’s atmosphere over geological eons. Rather, the modern atmospheric 20Ne/22Ne ratio results from progressive addition of solar-type Ne degassed from Earth’s mantle (26), after the last giant impact, to an originally chondritic atmosphere delivered by meteoritic bodies at the end of Earth’s accretion, with an isotopic composition close to the Ne-A component trapped in carbonaceous chondrites [20Ne/22Ne = 8.2 ± 0.4; (27)] (13, 22, 23, 28). The degassing of solar-type neon from the mantle into an original chondritic atmosphere appears to be consistent with both the isotopic and elemental composition of the modern atmosphere (22, 23, 28). The origin of the solar-type Ne is still debated (29) but may correspond to an entrapment by dissolution of nebular neon in the magma ocean of the proto-Earth (26, 30–35) or to a contribution from dust enriched in solar wind–derived neon (36, 37). See the recent review on this topic (38). In the remaining parts of the manuscript, we use the term “solar-type” neon with a 20Ne/22Ne ratio of 13.36 ± 0.18 (39), considering the first hypothesis of nebular Ne dissolved in the magma ocean as the initial source of mantle neon. In the scenario of progressive mixing between cosmochemical sources, the evolution of the atmospheric 20Ne/22Ne ratio mainly depends on the degassing history of solar-type Ne from Earth’s mantle, since the contribution of degassed nucleogenic neon in the atmosphere is negligible (40). This means that following the evolution of the atmospheric 20Ne/22Ne ratio provides a previously unidentified way to assess the ancient Earth’s geodynamics. For example, a model predicts a 20Ne/22Ne ratio of 9.747 ± 0.005 (1σ) during the Archean, 3.3 Ga ago, ~1% lower than the modern atmosphere (13). Recently, a model suggested that a model of giant impact could not completely erase the starting atmosphere (less than 10%) and that later impacts could not deliver enough neon to dominate the inventory of the modern atmosphere (41). This highlights the need for paleo-atmospheric Ne data, which is still not constrained.

Atmospheric xenon also underwent substantial changes since the Archean. Previous studies on fluid inclusions trapped in Archean barite and hydrothermal quartz and on atmospheric gases trapped in Archean kerogen (14–16, 19, 42–44) revealed xenon isotopic spectra intermediate between the modern atmosphere and a starting composition [“U-Xe” (20)], which is consistent with a mixture between chondritic (78%) and cometary Xe (22%) (45). The Archean atmosphere is mass-fractionated relative to the modern atmosphere, with a relative enrichment in light Xe isotopes. This mass-dependent fractionation seems to be specific to Xe (14, 43, 44).

To explain this isotopic fractionation, a recent model proposes that Xe+ ions are dragged into an ionized escaping hydrogen flux, along magnetic field lines (46), with the preferential escape of light Xe isotopes. Such a process requires high-energy extreme ultraviolet (EUV) emitted from the young Sun and the presence of a hydrogen-rich (1% total hydrogen) Archean atmosphere relative to the modern one. Thus, the evolution of the xenon isotopic composition can be used as a tracer of hydrogen atmospheric escape. Another feature of the xenon composition in the modern Earth’s atmosphere is its elemental depletion of 4 to 20 times relative to chondritic noble gases. Both the mass-dependent fractionation and the elemental depletion account for the “missing Xe problem” (21). The model (46) resolved both features of the “missing Xe problem,” requiring escape of Xe in short bursts, influenced by solar EUV, hydrogen amount, and magnetic field variability. It also implies an evolution of the Xe/Kr elemental ratio over time, with depletion of the amount of atmospheric Xe with time.

An alternative scenario has been proposed (47), in which heavy Xe isotopes were preferentially trapped in deep silicates during the magma-ocean phase and the residual light Xe isotopes in the atmosphere were lost because of impacts and/or hydrodynamic escape. The heavy Xe isotopes were then released into the Archean atmosphere through continental erosion and recycling of the Hadean felsic crust, leading to an atmosphere elementally depleted in xenon but enriched in heavy isotopes, implying an already low Xe/Kr ratio during the Archean, contrary to the atmospheric escape model (46).

A recent study attempted to assess the 130Xe/84Kr ratio of the Archean atmosphere 3.3 Ga ago by measuring noble gases contained in fluid inclusions trapped in hydrothermal quartz from the Barberton Greenstone Belt (19). The Archean ratio appears to be up to five times higher than the modern one but higher than the one predicted at 3.3 Ga ago, in agreement with the loss of ionized Xe in episodic bursts and not in a continuous way (19, 46). Nevertheless, the calculated Xe/Kr ratio does not display a pure Archean atmospheric ratio but reflects a mixture of Archean atmospheric noble gases with contributions from crustal gases. A study also reported high Xe/Kr ratios measured in fluid inclusions contained in Archean barites (48) although most values were lower than the estimate provided before (19). In this study, a wide range of Xe/Kr ratios was obtained, demonstrating that a tight constraint on the elemental ratio of the Archean atmosphere remains elusive.

Here, we present results from analyses of Ne and other noble gases contained in fluid inclusions trapped in hydrothermal quartz from the Barberton Greenstone Belt (BGB) in South Africa (3.3 Ga ago) and from Fortescue Group in Australia (2.7 Ga ago). By reconstructing the evolution of the atmospheric 20Ne/22Ne ratio, we trace the progressive degassing of the Earth’s mantle, and by reconstructing the evolution of the abundance of atmospheric Xe, we trace progressive atmospheric loss.

DISCUSSION

Contributions from crustal noble gases

Neon released from Barberton and Fortescue Group samples presents high 21Ne/22Ne ratios and low 20Ne/22Ne ratios, compatible with a contribution from nucleogenic neon produced in Earth’s crust (57). Nucleogenic neon isotopes (20Ne*, 21Ne*, and 22Ne*) are produced by the interaction of alpha and neutron particles, produced during the spontaneous fission of uranium and thorium, on target elements such as O, F, Na, and Mg (57). As alpha particles travel only a few tens of microns (58), the crustal 21Ne*/22Ne* ratio mainly depends on the O/F ratio of the lithologies surrounding U-Th–rich minerals (59). Crushing steps on Pi03-44-2020a, Pi03-44-2020b, and Pi03-29 from the Fortescue group analyzed in this study align on the same line toward a unique nucleogenic endmember (Fig. 2). Using the empirical relationship between the nucleogenic 21Ne*/22Ne* ratio and the O/F ratio (59), the nucleogenic 21Ne*/22Ne* ratio of 1.58 ± 0.20 (at 20Ne/22Ne = 0) obtained from this line corresponds to an O/F ratio of 355. For Barberton samples, the data show more scatter with some crushing steps plotting outside of the error envelope of the mixing line, suggesting that the nucleogenic endmember may not be unique. Nevertheless, for a 21Ne*/22Ne* ratio of 2.14 ± 0.25 (at 20Ne/22Ne = 0), the corresponding O/F ratio is 475. Typical modern crustal fluids (51, 58) show a 21Ne*/22Ne* ratio seven to eight times lower than the one expected for an average crust composition with an O/F ratio of 752 (59). These differences have been explained by the limited travel range of alpha particles within solid mineral matrice (10 to 40 μm). The calculated O/F ratio would thus represent the ratio of the lithologies surrounding U-Th–rich minerals and is thus not representative of the average crust (51). In comparison, samples from BGB and Fortescue Group measured in this study and samples from other Archean terranes display a higher extrapolated 21Ne*/22Ne* ratio, up to 3.3 ± 0.2 (52, 60), in agreement with the ratio of 3.51 given for an average crust composition (58, 59).

High 40Ar/36Ar ratios are commonly measured in gases released from fluid inclusions trapped in hydrothermal quartz (14, 44, 61, 62). In this study, most of the 40Ar/36Ar ratios gradually increases as crushing proceeds (Fig. 4). These excesses cannot be explained by an in situ production of 40Ar* from 40K decay, even during billion years. The produced 40Ar* would be seven orders of magnitude less abundant than the 40Ar excess detected in the samples (14). This excess rather corresponds to incorporation of “parentless” radiogenic 40Ar in fluid inclusions. This parentless argon likely comes from the interaction between the fluid and crustal rocks in which 40K had decayed (63, 64).

Archean atmospheric Ne and implications for Earth’s degassing history

One goal of the present study was to determine the isotopic composition of Archean atmospheric neon to put constraints on the history of degassing of solar-type neon from Earth’s mantle into the atmosphere, which presumably led to an increase in the 20Ne/22Ne ratio with time (13, 22, 23). The clear mixing relationship (Fig. 2) revealed by the step-crushing experiment indicates that, contrary to previous studies with more scattered datasets (65, 66), the isotopic composition of paleo-atmospheric neon trapped in hydrothermal quartz can be estimated. To use the mixing correlation, the 21Ne/22Ne ratio of the paleo-atmospheric component must be assumed. The progressive mixture between chondritic Ne and solar-type Ne in the atmosphere leads to a small variation of the 21Ne/22Ne ratio of the Earth’s atmosphere over time. Thus, a range of possible values for the 21Ne/22Ne ratio are used (hatched area in Fig. 2), considering the combined error bar of the production line and the mixing range between chondritic neon [0.0240 ± 0.0030, Ne-A; (27)] and solar-nebula neon [0.03236; (23, 39)]. This range does not consider the possible variation of the atmospheric 21Ne/22Ne ratio due to the degassing of nucleogenic neon produced in the mantle, but this nucleogenic contribution is negligible in the atmosphere: 4.0 × 10−4%, 1.4%, and 8.6 × 10−4%, for 20Ne, 21Ne, and 22Ne, respectively (40). The intersection between the nucleogenic production line (for BGB and Fortescue Group) and the mixing array between chondritic Ne and solar-type Ne (hatched range, Fig. 2) allow to compute Archean atmospheric 20Ne/22Ne ratios between 9.808 and 9.830 (the 1σ upper limit) at 3.3 Ga ago and between 9.762 and 9.798 at 2.7 Ga ago at 21Ne/22Ne = 0.029 (Fig. 2). These extrapolated ratios were estimated using a 21Ne/22Ne ratio of 0.029 and thus represent minimum values (Fig. 2).

The paleo-atmospheric 20Ne/22Ne ratio (9.780 ± 0.018) extrapolated from Fortescue data 2.7 Ga ago, considering a 21Ne/22Ne ratio of 0.029 (green range), is 0.29 ± 0.19% lower than the 20Ne/22Ne ratio of modern atmospheric neon and is identical within uncertainties to the value predicted by the model (13) for the 2.7 Ga ago Archean atmosphere (9.782 ± 0.003 at 1σ, blue star). If lower 21Ne/22Ne ratios are considered (dashed area Fig. 2), the nucleogenic mixing line covers the 20Ne/22Ne ratio of both the model and the modern atmosphere.

Nevertheless, the range of possible 20Ne/22Ne ratios defined by the Barberton data for the Archean atmosphere 3.3 Ga ago (blue range) is higher than the ratio of 9.747 ± 0.005 (1σ) predicted by the model (13) (blue diamond), even considering the uncertainties. One would expect a lower 20Ne/22Ne ratio at 3.3 Ga ago than at 2.7 Ga ago because mantle degassing would increase the atmospheric 20Ne/22Ne ratio. The delivery of chondritic neon by impacts of volatile-rich meteoritic material (67) on the early Earth between 3.3 and 2.7 Ga ago could lead to a decrease in the atmospheric 20Ne/22Ne ratio. However, this would correspond to a minimum of about 3.5 billion tons of meteoritic material delivered per year during 600 million years, which seems implausible regarding the total mass of extraterrestrial material potentially delivered during this period (Supplementary Text) (68). The fact that the nucleogenic production line defined by the BGB samples plots above the one of Fortescue Group can rather be explained by the contribution of a mantle-derived component in the BGB samples (18, 48), with a high 20Ne/22Ne ratio, blurring the initial paleo-atmospheric signal. The scatter in the neon data observed in Fig. 2 for the BGB samples and previously attributed to variations in the nucleogenic end-member composition, is also consistent with a mixture between three components (paleo-atmosphere, crust, and mantle), leading to 20Ne/22Ne ratios higher than the Air. This potential mantle contribution and the analytical uncertainties of the dataset do not allow to firmly determine whether the atmospheric 20Ne/22Ne ratio varied between 3.3 and 2.7 Ga ago. As a mantle contribution in the analyzed samples cannot be completely ruled out, the 20Ne/22Ne ratio of the Archean atmosphere 2.7 Ga ago rather represents a maximum ratio.

An almost-modern atmospheric isotopic ratio of 9.780 ± 0.018 2.7 Ga ago implies an intense degassing of solar-type Ne from the mantle during the first 1.7 Gyr of Earth’s history. Assuming a mantle with a solar composition [20Ne/22Ne = 13.36 ± 0.18; (39)] and an initial chondritic atmosphere [20Ne/22Ne = 8.2 to 8.9; (27, 69)], a minimum time-averaged degassing rate (during the first 1.7 Gyr) of 3.44 × 104 mol 22Ne/year (20Ne/22Ne = 8.9) and 5.34 × 104 mol 22Ne/year (20Ne/22Ne = 8.2) is required to reach a 20Ne/22Ne ratio of 9.780 2.7 Ga ago (Supplementary Text). This value can be compared to the current mantle degassing rate of 72 ± 10 mol 22Ne/year, estimated using the upper-mantle 3He/22Ne ratio [5.8 to 6.7, depending on the mantle 20Ne/22Ne ratio of 13.0 or 13.5 (70)], and the degassing rate of mantle 3He from mid-ocean ridges [450 ± 50 mol/year (71)]. The calculated average degassing rate during the first 1.7 Gyr of the Earth’s history is almost three orders of magnitude higher than the modern one. A significantly higher neon degassing rate compared to the modern one is in agreement with higher mantle potential temperatures during the Archean (5), up to 1600°C, and also consistent with other degassing rates estimated using other noble gases (13, 14, 17, 72). For example, similarly to the Ne rate calculated in this study, the degassing rate of 36Ar was three orders of magnitude higher during the first 1.7 Gyr of Earth’s history [up to ~2.2 × 106 mol 36Ar/year; (13)], relative to the modern one [~9 × 102 mol 36Ar/year; (70, 71)].

Paleo-atmospheric Xe and loss of Xe from the Archean atmosphere

The second goal of this study was to determine accurately the 130Xe/84Kr ratio of the Archean atmosphere, 2.7 and 3.3 Ga ago, to estimate the quantity of xenon in the Archean atmosphere and thus to put further constraints on the mechanism of xenon escape and/or retention within the deep Earth (25, 46, 47).

For most crushing steps, ratios are more than two times greater than the ratio of the modern atmosphere (Fig. 5). As seen in Fig. 1, while 20Ne/36Ar and 84Kr/36Ar ratios are between those of the modern atmosphere and of noble gases dissolved in seawater, the 130Xe/36Ar ratios are higher. The same observation can be made for the 130Xe/84Kr ratios, higher than those of noble gases dissolved in seawater (and Air), while the 22Ne/84Kr ratios for each crushing steps, except for sample PI03-44-2020a,b, are between the modern atmosphere and noble gases dissolved in seawater (Fig. 5). This enrichment in Xe relative to Kr and compared to the modern atmosphere suggests that the Archean atmosphere was enriched in Xe [in agreement with previous findings (19, 48)]. Some of the crushing steps release noble gases with ratios close to those proposed for the Archean atmosphere [0.037; (19)], but are generally lower, implying a possible overestimation of this ratio. Here, krypton in the atmosphere is assumed to be constant since the Archean, in agreement with a constant isotopic composition over Earth’s history (44). Nevertheless, abundance ratios for individual crushing steps show significant scatter (Fig. 5). Note that the 130Xe/84Kr ratio tends to increase when crushing proceeds, pointing toward a hydrothermal end-member with a high Xe/Kr ratio (48). The elemental composition of noble gases in fluid inclusions never displays a pure paleo-atmospheric composition but likely reflects the influence of several processes able to fractionate elemental ratios, such as solubility, salinity, and temperature (73) and interactions between the fluid and crustal rocks, modifying the starting composition (49, 50). Furthermore, considering the total abundance of noble gas contained in fluid inclusions (reflecting a mixture between atmospheric and crustal gases), ratios are significantly lower than the value given for the Archean atmosphere [130Xe/84Kr = 0.037 ± 0.003; (19)]. Such a result reflects an atmospheric composition mixed with crustal gases and does not display a pure abundance ratio of the Archean atmosphere. Each sample from BGB and Fortescue Group has its own abundance ratio for total gas released from the samples, even for duplicate samples (Fig. 5). These variations reflect contributions from crustal noble gases and imply that the composition must be corrected for these contributions in order to determine the 130Xe/84Kr ratio of the Archean atmosphere.

The 130Xe/84Kr ratio of the atmospheric component trapped in the BGB samples is in agreement with a mixture between Air-derived and seawater-derived noble gases with a 130Xe/84Kr ratio around 2.3 times (130Xe/84Kr = 0.012 ± 0.002; average of the corrected Xe/Kr ratios) higher than the modern one [130Xe/84Kr = 0.005 (21)]. The computed Xe/Ne ratio of the Archean atmosphere is around four times higher than the modern ratio (fig. S1). The difference between these two excess factors (2.3 for the 130Xe/84Kr ratio and of 4 for the 130Xe/22Ne ratio), considering that the Kr composition remained relatively constant since the Archean, induces a neon-depleted Archean atmosphere. Nevertheless, the exact value of the Ne/Kr ratio remains difficult to assess due to the fact that the corrected ratios display higher variation compared to the computed 130Xe/84Kr ratios.

A 2.3 times Xe-enriched Archean atmosphere is consistent with the evolution model of the 84Kr/130Xe ratio (19), which predicts a 84Kr/130Xe ratio of 82.9 ± 5 3.3 Ga ago (85.2 ± 11.1 for this study) for a progressive atmospheric escape rather than a burst one, for a constant fractionation factor. Moreover, the calculated 84Kr/130Xe ratio of this study is higher than the one measured previously (19), representing a pure atmospheric abundance ratio. A similar atmospheric 130Xe/84Kr ratios 3.3 and 2.7 Ga ago (defined only for sample Pi03-44-2020a,b) are in agreement with a possible stagnation of the isotopic composition of atmospheric xenon (isotopic plateau) between 3.3 and 2.7 Ga ago (15, 44, 48). The conjoint plateau for the elemental and isotopic composition of xenon could reflect a pause in atmospheric escape for 600 million years, which could be explained by a variation in the parameters controlling escape (46): lack of hydrogen in the atmosphere at that time, inefficiency of the xenon transport mechanism at high altitude, changes in the strength of the magnetic field, and/or variation in the Sun’s luminosity, driving the xenon ionization process. Nevertheless, this observation is based on only one sample from the Fortescue group. Additional measurements will allow determining if the quantity of xenon in the Archean atmosphere stayed constant from 3.3 to 2.7 Ga ago.

An enrichment in xenon leading to a 130Xe/84Kr ratio more than twice that of the modern atmosphere 3.3 Ga ago is consistent with the scenario of Xe loss to space (46), implying that the atmospheric 130Xe/84Kr ratio decreased with time. It is not in agreement with the scenario of an entrapment of Xe in the deep silicate and subsequent outgassing (47), since such a scenario would imply a progressive increase in the 130Xe/84Kr ratio of Earth’s atmosphere over time, due to the release of Xe originally trapped in the solid Earth, to reach the modern ratio.

The computed Archean 20Ne/22Ne ratio and 130Xe/84Kr ratio provide constraints on the degassing history of the Earth’s mantle and on the evolution of the Xe content in the Earth’s atmosphere, respectively. The slightly lower atmospheric 20Ne/22Ne ratio 2.7 Ga ago (9.780 ± 0.018 at 21Ne/22Ne = 0.029) compared to the modern atmosphere is consistent with the model of solar-type Ne degassing from the mantle in a chondritic atmosphere (13), implying intense mantle activity with a high degassing rate of solar-type Ne during the first billion years of Earth’s history. This ratio provides a geochemical constraint for future models/research of the joint evolution of Earth’s mantle and atmosphere. Noble gas data allow to determine a pure paleo-atmospheric 130Xe/84Kr ratio, without crustal contribution, of up to 2.3 ± 0.2 times the modern ratio, consistent with a model of Xe atmospheric escape and therefore with a depletion over time of this ratio to reach the modern one. Similarly to its isotopic composition (15, 44, 48), the abundance of atmospheric xenon seems to show a possible stagnation between 3.3 and 2.7 Ga ago, highlighting a potential pause of xenon atmospheric escape during this period, which could be induced by a variation in the parameters triggering the escape: hydrogen content, strength of the magnetic field, solar luminosity, etc. (46). Future noble gas measurements on samples with a wider range of ages will provide further constraints on the evolution of these two ratios, especially to investigate the possible stagnation of the Xe/Kr ratio and how xenon isotopic fractionation and xenon depletion evolved conjointly.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Samples characteristics and geological settings

Samples analyzed in this study consist of high purity fluid inclusion-rich macrocrystalline quartz, allowing the measurements of high quantities of gas. These samples are well studied, and previous studies demonstrated the presence of paleo-atmospheric signals, notably demonstrating the long-term evolution of the Xe isotopic composition (14, 44).

The Barberton samples measured in this study (BARB3: BARB3-96-3A, BMGA3-5-2021, BMGA3-9, BARB3-91-3A, BMGA3-13-2021A, and BARB3105-1A) come from cores drilled in the BGB in South Africa, in the Kromberg Formation, as part of the ICDP project “Peering into the Cradle of Life” (74). Samples consist of macrocrystalline quartz veins cross-cutting cherts and/or mafic lithologies (75). The quartz veins result from early hydrothermal activity (76), with an age of 3.30 ± 0.05 Ga ago for the time of entrapment of argon in fluid inclusions contained in the BMGA3-9 sample (14).

Samples Pi03-29, Pi03-17-2020 and Pi03-44-2020a (and its duplicate Pi03-44-2020b) (Fortescue Group, Hamersley Basin, Australia) are from the Mount-Roe Basalt Formation (2.775 to 2.763 Ga ago), Boongal Formation (2.745 to 2.73 Ga ago), and from the Maddina Basalt Formation (2.718 to 2.715 Ga ago), respectively, dated using the U-Pb dating method (77). These samples consist of quartz ± carbonate ± chlorite precipitated in millimeter- to centimeter-scale vacuoles formed in pillow-basalts (fig. S4). Such quartz deposits have been reported in older rocks from the Dresser Formation (Pilbara Craton, Western Australia) (3.49 Ga ago) and have been interpreted as resulting from hydrothermal circulations which took place soon after the eruption of the basalts (78–80).

Extraction, purification, and measurements

Noble gases were released using a step-by-step crushing method. Hand-picked millimeter-sized grains of quartz and chert, beforehand cleaned in acetone in an ultrasonic bath, were placed under vacuum in a stainless-steel tube and then crushed using an iron ball lifted up and down by a magnet moved by hand outside vacuum. One crush corresponds to several strokes of the ball onto the quartz grains, and the number of strokes increases for each subsequent crush. Crushing ceases when low amounts of gas are released. This method is used to progressively release gases trapped in fluid inclusions, as described elsewhere (48, 65, 66).

After each crushing step, reactive species (H2O, N2, CO2, hydrocarbons, etc.) were removed from the gas phase using three different getters operated sequentially at 500°C during 5 min and cooled down during 10 min. The first getter consists in Zr-V-Cr pellets and the other two are made of titanium sponge. Once reactive species were entirely removed, noble gases were adsorbed onto activated charcoal and cooled down to 20 K by a cryogenic head. The charcoal was then heated sequentially to release noble gases: Ne at 77 K, Ar at 165 K, Kr at 200 K, and Xe at 340 K. Noble gases were analyzed in the following order: Ne, Kr, Xe, and Ar; Ar being stored in a dedicated volume of the preparation system during Kr and Xe measurements. Once the purification and the separation of noble gases were complete, the noble gases were introduced into a static noble gas mass spectrometer (Noblesse, High Resolution 3F6M, NuInstruments). To limit contributions from 40Ar++ and 44CO2++ signals to 20Ne and 22Ne signals, respectively, two cold fingers plunged in liquid nitrogen were used: one on the last vacuum section of the purification line and the other one directly on the source of the mass spectrometer. In addition, to correct for the potential signal interferences, a double ionization ratio was measured (81).

During the analytical campaign, air standards were regularly processed in the purification line the same way as the samples and introduced into the mass spectrometer to determine the sensitivity, reproducibility, and mass discrimination of the instrument. After new samples were loaded and before their measurements, analytical blanks were determined by opening the crushers to the purification line, without moving the ball to not crush the sample. Blank analyses carried out previously with a hundred strokes applied to an empty crusher as the one used in this study did not increase significantly the amount of blank (66). All data have been corrected for blank contribution and mass discrimination in the instrument. Blank amounts were less than 7% of the quantity of gas released from the samples: 3.43 × 10−13 ccSTP for 22Ne, 8.32 × 10−11 ccSTP for 36Ar, 3.53 × 10−14 ccSTP for 84Kr, and 4.15 × 10−15 ccSTP for 130Xe.
