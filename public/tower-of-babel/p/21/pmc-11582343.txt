Microenvironmental host–microbe interactions in chronic inflammatory skin diseases
Authors: Lene Bay, Gregor Borut Jemec, Hans Christian Ring
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeveral microbiome studies have recently demonstrated microbial dysbiosis in various chronic inflammatory skin diseases, and it is considered an important role in the pathogenesis. Although the role of skin dysbiosis in inflammatory skin diseases is debatable, the local microenvironment is considered essential concerning compositional changes and functional alterations of the skin microbiota. Indeed, various local nutrients (e.g., lipids), pH values, water, oxygen, and antimicrobial peptides may affect the level of skin dysbiosis in these skin diseases. In particular, in atopic dermatitis and hidradenitis suppurativa, significant changes in skin dysbiosis have been associated with local aberrant host immune changes. In this review, the potential pathogenic crosstalk between the host and the microbiota is reviewed in relation to the physical, chemical, and biological microenvironments of various chronic inflammatory skin diseases.Keywords: Skin microenvironment, host‐microbe interactions, inflammatory skin diesases

INTRODUCTION

The topographical microenvironment influences the diversity of the microbiota (1, 2). The microenvironment sets the conditions for colonization and thus shapes the microbial composition and functionality (3, 4). The type and abundance of residing microorganisms depend on broad biological determinants of life in the particular skin location. These environmental conditions are often general factors involved in bacterial growth, for example, the availability of nutrients, pH, water, and oxygen. For instance, the presence of sweat glands that evaporate water and acidify the skin to facilitate thermoregulation favors bacteria that thrive in a humid acidic environment, such as Staphylococcus spp. and Corynebacterium spp. (5) For the development of microbial populations, the skin's chemical environment is crucial (6) and the microorganisms respond to even minor changes in the microenvironment (7). Chemical or biological factors are propagated to the microbiota through simple changes in their living conditions (8). For instance, a weakened skin barrier allows pathogenic bacteria to colonize as seen in numerous inflammatory diseases.

Various biochemical microenvironments ensure that microbial cells are protected from antibiotics, antimicrobial peptides, and immune cells through local hypoxia, reduced metabolic activity, and production of bacterial toxins (7). The superantigens produced by Staphylococcus aureus, for instance, stimulate T cells non‐specifically to release cytokines (9) and thereby exacerbate immune responses. Thus, the consequence is highly decisive in the homeostatic maintenance of skin health (10). Bacteria have a highly developed stress response – a dynamic and adaptable system – that ensures they survive adverse and fluctuating conditions in their immediate surroundings (7, 11). Bacterial stress responses are regulated at the transcriptional, translational, and post‐translational levels and microenvironmental variations thereby cause changes in bacterial gene expression, protein functionality, and metabolic activity (11). Even more than the microbial community composition, the skin's molecule composition varies across body sites (6). This implies that insignificant microbial molecular alterations as well as community compositions may predict pathological changes in the host (8, 10). Changes in the composition and abundance of the microbiota make the physiological state of the microorganisms and the efficacy of immune cells interesting (7). Factors such as bacterial metabolites (12) and host immune responses influence the chemical microenvironment (7) and thereby on microbial growth and metabolism. For instance, polyketide, a group of secondary metabolites formed by microbial condensation of small fatty acids, acts as natural antimicrobials (13). Polyketide lipids are released by bacteria during infection and interact with components of the host's immune response (13) and play important roles in the communication, defense, virulence, and protection of organisms. The situation most likely involves both systemic and local host–microbial interactions and microbe–microbe crosstalk (10).

Factors influencing skin health can be divided into endogenous and exogenous factors (10). Endogenous factors can further be divided into local and systemic host factors, while the exogenous factors cover lifestyle and environmental factors (12) (Table 1). The exogenous factors depend mainly on the habitat and habits of the individual, as urban or rural living often differ in environmental pollution and nutrition composition. Medical exposure is linked to the health status of the individual, its susceptibility, and genetic dispositions that correspondingly dictate the hormone level and metabolism. The metabolites are further reliant on the availabilities of local elements and molecules. All these factors are naturally interconnected and worth a detailed study. Here, we will, however, focus mainly on the local endogenous factors affecting the microenvironment in the skin. We will further outline the overall details of the physicochemical properties, the host–microbial dynamics, and the influence of the local microenvironment in specific inflammatory skin disorders, that is, atopic dermatitis, hidradenitis suppurativa, acne vulgaris, and psoriasis.

MICROENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS

The skin is a stratified tissue that consists of the epidermis, dermis, and subcutis, of which the epidermis exhibits important functions in protecting against microorganisms, regulating heat, and enabling sensations. Human skin is divided into anatomical regions and habitats, that is, dry, moist, and sebaceous skin habitats (1, 3, 14), which are correlated to the variation in the content of moisture and lipids. Specific bacteria are adapted to thrive in certain environments, such as the lipophilic Cutibacterium acnes that dominate sebaceous skin habitats (3). Within habitats, variation is present in the microenvironment (10), which is defined by its local physical, chemical, and biological components (Fig. 1).

Physical properties

Topographically, both macro‐ and microscopical aspects, such as ridges and cavities as well as glands and hair follicles, define the microenvironment (15). Furthermore, the anatomy of the skin and its functional properties are important factors in characterizing the microenvironment. The physical properties of skin are subject to extreme variability within and between individuals (16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21). The variability within body sites, habitats, and microenvironment and their correlation to the microbiota is further supported by tissue tropism – the ability of a given pathogen to infect a specific tissue or region. Tissue tropism has usually been perceived as solely dependent on the interaction between bacterial surface components and host tissue (22). Yet, the nutritional environment at the site of infection also influences tissue tropism of some bacterial pathogens (13, 22). Staphylococcus aureus, a common resident in the skin and the nasal cavity, causes severe infections when entering the soft tissue or the bloodstream and is often associated with serious infections of the skin. Despite significant progress in understanding the factors involved in tissue tropism, there are still many factors unaccounted for. A great deal of research needs to be done on the fine‐scale differences in host tissue chemistry, metabolism, waste production, and local immune responses (22). Most probably these variations make the difference between health and disease.

The skin barrier protects the tissue from dehydration and hinders the colonization of pathogens. Epidermal lipids, a mixture of cholesterol, ceramides, and free fatty acids of keratinocyte origin, fill the spaces between cells and provide a lipid barrier against water and electrolyte movement (23). The intercellular barrier is maintained by tight junctions that control the paracellular pathway by preventing unhindered passage of molecules and water solutes but also form channels for ions (24, 25). The transcellular barrier consists of corneocytes (3) forming a passively shielding physical barrier. Active protection occurs when the corneocytes are continually shed from the outer layers of the stratum corneum, dragging the attached microorganisms with them (26). The high content of keratin combined with a lack of nutrients limits the microbial density of the superficial skin (26); nevertheless, particularly Staphylococcus spp. are highly adapted to these harsh conditions.

The infundibulum of the hair follicle presents the transition between the interfollicular epidermis with its characteristic stratum corneum to the follicular epithelium as defined by the outer root sheet (27, 28). The infundibulum extends from the skin's surface to the sebaceous exit duct (28), which debouches into the inner funnel of the hair follicle (27). Potentially, terminal hair follicle funnels harbor 10 times more bacteria than vellus hair follicles due to the difference in reservoir volume (8) leading to differences in microbial abundance according to skin habitats and sexes (15, 17, 29, 30, 31, 32). The hair follicle infundibulum is often plugged with dry sebum and shed corneocyte residues (33, 34), which serves as nutrition for microorganisms, but the sebum properties and variations in oxygen tension (35) also affect the microbiota. The hair follicle infundibulum is mainly colonized by C. acnes, while other skin commensals, such as Staphylococcus, Corynebacterium, Acinetobacter, and Prevotella spp. are more rarely found (35). This is in stark contrast to the dry skin surface where Staphylococcus epidermidis is the dominant species.

Glands directly influence the local microenvironment of the skin (3, 36) and the microorganisms that it harbors. Eccrine sweat glands are distributed over the entire surface of the skin and together perspire 1–10 L of salty sweat every day (37). Sweat consists mostly of water but contains also minerals and metabolites (37). Eccrine glands secrete immunoglobulin A (IgA) to the skin surface and the nasal mucosa, where it plays an important role in defense against microorganisms and thereby impacts microbial density (38, 39). Simultaneously, other components in sweat have the opposite effect. Particularly, S. epidermidis exhibits a strong preference for sweat as a nutrient. The bacteria utilize urea and amino acids as well as glucose, lactic acid, and amino acids as nitrogen and carbon sources, respectively (40). Apocrine glands – located in the transition from the deep dermis to the subcutis with an exit duct into hair follicles or the skin surface (41) – provide an anaerobic environment for bacterial growth (42). The opaque, odorless, substance secreted from apocrine glands (41), primarily found in body locations such as the axillae and groin (37), contain lipids, peptides, and hormones (42). The microbiota resident in glands and follicles degrade these compounds thereby producing the malodor (42). Sebaceous glands release sebum into the infundibula of hair follicles through the sebaceous ducts and to the skin's surface. These lipids are important nutrients to commensal bacteria. Most of the microbe‐associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) molecules released by the microorganisms are retained by the epidermal lipid barrier (43). In diseases with skin barrier defects, the integrity of the laminar membrane is impaired, which invites infections.

Chemical components

The chemical composition of skin is 70% water, 25% proteins, and 3% lipids. The remaining 2% consists of inorganic elements (See Fig. 1). Water enables the absorption of nutrients and disposal of metabolites (44). The blood flow distributes the ions and elements and nutrients are transferred by diffusion with the interstitial fluid through the dermal‐epidermal junctions (44).

The skin is generally oxic, with niches of decreased oxygen saturation. The epidermis is mostly aerobic but locally modestly hypoxic since oxygen is delivered indirectly through dermal blood vessels or by atmospheric oxygenation. Dermis is well oxygenated, but areas of hair follicles are purely anaerobic (45). This is due to oxygen consumption by aerobic respiration involving oxidative phosphorylation provided by proliferating hair follicles matrix cells. This results in oxygen depletion favoring anaerobic respiration performed by hair follicle stem cells (46). The uneven access to oxygen influences the community composition within hair follicles and in the subepidermal skin, such as the deep epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis (47). Nitric oxide, which is produced by keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and immune cells, is involved in wound healing, immune responses, and the production of cytokines (48). Through nitrification, nitric oxide is converted into nitrite or nitrate by anaerobic bacteria (49, 50), but nitrite and nitrate can also be recycled by ultraviolet radiation and thereby act as a physiological source of nitric oxide (51). Nitric oxide further inhibits lipid peroxidation and protects against ultraviolet rays and reactive oxygen species (ROS)‐induced apoptosis in skin (51). ROS refers to a group of unstable, oxygen‐containing molecules with differing reactivities that are formed by redox reactions or electronic excitation (50). ROS is elevated during infection, facilitates the clearance of pathogens, and contributes to signaling cascades during inflammation (52).

Free amino acids, urea, and other components of the natural moisturizing factor (NMF) increase water penetration and reduce the transepidermal water loss (53). NMF makes up for 20–30% of the dry weight of stratum corneum and acts as a humectant of the corneocytes that help maintain skin plasticity and retain the physical barrier function (54).

The lipids of the skin are involved in the skin barrier function by preventing of transepidermal water loss (TEWL) and the entrance of microorganisms (55). Phospholipids are mainly located in the bilayer of the cell membrane and between cells in the cornified envelope (55). During the maturation of keratinocytes moving toward the outermost epidermis, phospholipids are enzymatically degraded into glucosylceramides, free fatty acids, and glycerol (55). The low level of phospholipids in the corneocytes gives the stratum corneum a significant lipid profile (55). Human sebum consists of glycerides (47%), free fatty acids (10%), wax esters (26%), cholesterol (5%), and squalene (12%) (55, 56). Cutibacterium spp. hydrolyses triglycerides and releases free fatty acids, while Malassezia spp. and Corynebacterium spp. merely benefit from lipids of the sebum (5, 35). The lipid‐rich substance of apocrine glands also contains sugar, proteins, and ammonia (37). These nutrients favor Corynebacterium spp.

The physiological pH is ~5.6 at the surface and ~6.0 in deeper skin layers (57, 58). The pH gradient is critical in controlling enzymatic activities but is affected by the production of sweat and sebum and the microbial metabolism, which again are influenced by systemic endogenous factors: age, sex, habitat, and genetic predisposition (46) but also exogenous lifestyle factors: detergents, cosmetics, and topical antibiotics (58). A thin, delicate, and slightly acidic (4.4–6 pH) film covering the stratum corneum is referred to as the acid mantle (59). Skin pH is essential for maintaining the epidermal barrier and prevents microbial colonization. Moreover, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), such as dermcidin secreted by eccrine glands, are most effective against for instance S. aureus at acidic pH (59). AMPs are multifunctional peptides, produced by human cells, such as epithelial cells and neutrophils, as well as microorganisms. AMPs are fundamentally responsible for eliminating pathogens. The naturally acidic and salty skin only attracts a distinct microbiota (60, 61). Resident bacteria are preserved at an acidic pH, while an alkaline environment promotes the dispersal of commensals and the growth of pathogens, such as S. aureus and Candida albicans. An increased pH also causes an impaired barrier function with elaborated transepidermal water loss (59). A variety of inflammatory skin diseases result from altered biological functions and the many chemical factors in the microenvironment likely affect the microbial community composition and functionality and thereby the host–microbial interaction.

Biological factors

Besides a physical and a chemical barrier, the skin contains a biological barrier comprising the microbial and immunological barrier (12). The innate or the non‐specific immune system recruits immune cells by producing chemical mediators, such as cytokines. In the skin exists a complex battery of different immune cells with various functions. The immune system in the skin includes the skin‐associated lymphoid tissue (SALT), which encompasses dendritic cells (DCs), mast cells, B and T lymphocytes, and keratinocytes. In certain pathogenic events, these cells may facilitate a cascade of local immune responses. Indeed, SALTs play an essential role in the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases such as autoimmune and hypersensitivity disorders which are diseases that are primarily orchestrated by T lymphocytes. A crucial factor in chronic inflammatory skin diseases appears to be keratinocytes. In particular, the production of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) (defensins and cathelicidins) is pivotal as they kill pathogens, activate immune cells, or activate cytokine profiles. Keratinocytes secrete important cytokines such as interleukin (IL) IL‐1, IL‐6, IL‐10, IL‐17, IL‐22, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α), which also appear to play a potential role in the pathogenesis of various chronic inflammatory skin diseases. Dying or damaged cells, for example, keratinocytes, release damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which activate, signal, and steer inflammation. Neutrophils and macrophages are activated by DAMPs and serve several antimicrobial functions, including cytokine secretion, phagocytosis, and degradation (62).

The microbial barrier consists of commensal microorganisms that share the biotope but compete for survival. To secure the habitat, coagulase‐negative Staphylococcus (CoNS) secrete strain‐specific, highly potent AMPs (2) that synergistically work with the human AMP LL‐37 and selectively kill S. aureus (63). Correspondingly, C. acnes produce the tripeptide antibiotic, cutimycin, which limits the colonization of S. aureus (12). There is, however, a great deal of uncertainty about how these interactions unite and affect the community's composition and functionality (12).

Skin homeostasis – the maintenance of the first‐line defense (64) – depends on the delicate balance between the microbiota and the immune system (8). Microorganisms modulate the host response and affect the barrier function through crosstalk with keratinocytes, adipocytes, and immune cells (65). Skin commensals activate and educate immune cells and promote the production of antimicrobial peptides from keratinocytes (63) leading to a protective immune barrier. For instance, S. epidermidis activates CD8+ T cells to induce re‐epithelization after skin injury and thereby have a positive effect on wound healing (66). It also secretes sphingomyelinase that promotes the host in producing ceramides to maintain the epithelial barrier (67). Conversely, S. aureus produces toxins and proteases that induce inflammation through innate and adaptive immunity (10). The modified pH and oxygen or the presence of toxins produced by pathogens protects against AMPs and the less effective immune cells affected by the changed microenvironment (7).

In common skin diseases, host–microbial interactions impact the skin barrier disruption and exacerbate the disease severity (68). Skin disorders are associated with factors that alter the commensal community (69). Consequently, host–microbial interactions change along with the local microenvironmental alteration, which potentially affects the pathogenesis of the disease (10).

INFLAMMATORY SKIN CONDITIONS

Aberrant immune responses may influence the progression of chronic inflammatory skin disorders (69). Moreover, environment, lifestyle, and diet most likely contribute to improper engagement of the immune system. Additionally, altered microbial diversity, due to changes in the local microenvironment is thought to impact immune tolerance mechanisms (69, 70). A disturbance in the balance of the host–microbial interaction advances disruption of the skin homeostasis that likely contributes to the inflammatory loops in the epithelial‐immune microenvironment as seen in the lesional skin of individuals suffering from inflammatory skin diseases (70, 71). A variety of pathogenic mechanisms are involved in these inflammatory loops, including epithelial cells, immune cells, skin barrier, and microbiota (70). This dysregulation is associated with the expression of common inflammatory genes but also with a unique functional pattern in each of the skin disorders (72).

Atopic dermatitis

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is clinically presented by inflamed itchy dry patches of the skin. Factors such as genetics, lifestyle, and an aberrant immune system appear to contribute to the pathogenesis. Topical steroids and moisturizers are the mainstay treatment for mild‐to‐moderate atopic dermatitis. In moderate‐to‐severe AD, immunosuppressive agents play an important role in achieving disease control.

The distinct and characteristic profile of the microbiota in AD lesional skin – with increased colonization of S. aureus correlated to disease severity – has been associated with elevated expression of type 2 inflammation pathways genes, for example, IL 4R, C‐C motif chemokine receptor type 4 (CCR4), and C‐C motif chemokine ligand 22 (CCL22) (71). Activations of these Th2 pathways are particular characteristics in the type 2‐high AD endotype with severe disease (73). Nevertheless, being a multifactional, heterogeneous disease, Th2 responses are significant in many of the AD endotypes (74). In AD lesions, the concentrated infiltration of monocytes, lymphocytes, and eosinophils produces a large quantity ROS, such as NO and O2− (75), which readily react with other molecules. Overproduction of ROS exacerbates inflammation (75) and results in oxidative stress – a condition of imbalance between the ROS and antioxidants – that causes cell damage (76). The establishment of chronic inflammation directed by ROS further leads to disorientation and degradation of collagen fibers, which decreases fibroblast interaction (77).

A hallmark of AD is the skin barrier dysfunction. This impairment of the skin barrier is associated with increased TEWL, depleted stratum corneum hydration (78) and lipid alterations (23). General structural patterns, such as MAMPs exhibited by pathogens, are usually recognized and responded to by the innate immune system. In diseases with skin barrier defects, such as AD, the impaired membrane integrity leads to increased penetration of MAMPs and toxins, but also microorganisms, which promotes inflammation (43).

The pH in AD skin is increased to a natural or even basic range, which results in a cascade of changes that contribute to the characteristic phenotype of AD (79). These conditions in AD skin favor the opportunistic pathogen, S. aureus, which displays numerous virulence factors involved in disease progression. For instance, the production of Staphylococcus enterotoxins is strongly correlated to AD severity (80). The contemporaneous proliferation of S. aureus in AD skin (81) is also correlated to an increase in polymorphonuclear leucocytes, which in return are influenced by the inflamed microenvironment in AD skin (65). While neutrophils prevent early S. aureus colonization (82), the interaction between keratinocytes and neutrophil extracellular traps enhances colonization in vitro (83) by oxidative stress induction and epidermal barrier gene downregulation (84). In terms of fungi, the Malassezia spp. – omnipresent in healthy skin – is considered to play a pathogenic role in AD (85). In 80% of AD patients with eczema in the face and neck region, serum‐specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels to Malassezia furfur were increased indicating widespread Malassezia sensitization in these patients (86). Malassezia spp. triggers AD through an allergic reaction (87) by inducing the production of specific IgE antibodies and the release of proinflammatory cytokines (85). Auto‐reactive T cells – induced by Malassezia spp. – cross‐react with similar proteins in humans, which contribute to inflammation (85). In the less acidic environment – such as the AD skin with a pH of 6.0 – more Malassezia allergens are released, which penetrate the disrupted skin barrier and further contribute to inflammation (85).

The AD microbiome has only a few distinct differences associated with habitat (71). Seemingly, the disease dictates a definite cutaneous community across habitats that is less affected by local physiology (88). This is particularly the case in the superficial epidermis, while in the dermis, AD microbiotas are more alike (89), signifying a general AD microenvironment in the deeper skin layers. This implies a relationship between the cutaneous community composition and the microenvironment (71). Understanding the microbiome's role in AD and its interaction with skin cells will provide important clues about mechanisms and biomarkers, which could provide new knowledge regarding patients' endotypes and improve personalized treatment (71). A broadened understanding within bioactives targeting the epidermal microenvironment has led to novel biotherapeutics in AD treatment (65).

Hidradenitis suppurativa

Hidradenitis Suppurativa is Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory skin disease characterized by recurrent inflamed nodules, tunnels and scarring involving predominantly the intertriginous regions. Although the pathogenesis of HS remains enigmatic, proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‐17 and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) as well as a dysbiotic microbiome are thought to play a role. Mild‐to‐moderate HS is treated with long courses of broad‐spectrum antibiotics. In moderate‐to‐severe HS, biologics such as adalimumab (TNFα inhibitor) and secukinumab (IL‐17 inhibitor) are often the treatment of choice.

Although studies on the skin microbiome in HS are limited, several studies have demonstrated a consistent picture, with similar microbiome characteristics (90, 91, 92). Overall, the studies point to a core HS microbiome dominated by anaerobic gram‐negative bacteria (e.g., Prevotella spp. and Porphyromonas spp). These species have been speculated to contribute to the HS pathogenesis through various pathogenic events such as the formation of biofilms or by upregulation of AMPs, which may catalyze inflammatory cascades (93, 94). Interestingly, several studies have investigated AMPs in HS. The studies point to a consistent overexpression of hBD‐2, S100A7, S100A8, and S100A9 at both mRNA and protein levels, and a decreased expression of hBD‐1 (94). This potential dysregulation of AMPs in both lesional – and non‐lesional HS skin may play an important role in the pathogenesis of HS. The exact etiology behind the reduced levels of hBD‐1 in the pathogenesis of HS is unclear. However, the decreased level of hBD‐1 may play an important role in the pathogenesis of HS as hBD‐1 has antimicrobial activities predominantly against Gram‐negative bacteria which have been found abundant in both inflamed nodules and tunnels in HS. Hypothetically, the deficiency of hBD‐1 may lead to secondary colonization of Gram‐negative anaerobic bacteria such as Prevotella spp. and Porophymonas spp. and potentially increase the pathogenic properties of these species in HS (95).

Moreover, Prevotella spp. primarily activates T helper 17 (Th17) immunes responses and has been demonstrated to increase production of IL 23 and IL‐1 (96, 97, 98). Both interleukins have been speculated to play a role in the pathogenesis of HS. Another factor that may support that Prevotella spp. could constitute the culprit in HS is the evident efficacy of secukinumab and stelara (IL 12/23 inhibitor) (99, 100).

Concerning a few 16S RNA sequencing studies on HS lesions, additional predictive metagenomic approaches such as PICRUSt (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States) have been performed. Interestingly, two studies demonstrated that predictions of certain amino acid metabolism pathways correspond between tunnels and early HS lesions. Specifically, the studies suggested that both early and chronic HS lesions were linked to an increase in biosynthesis and isoleucine degradation. A reduction in the local cutaneous pH value could result from the conversion of isoleucine to isovaleric acid. A second factor in the potential decline in cutaneous pH levels could be the increased production of ketone bodies. It has previously been proposed that disruption of the cutaneous pH value contributes to the pathophysiology of HS (101).

Acne vulgaris

Acne is a chronic inflammatory skin disease, which is characterized by comdedones, pustules, inflamed nodules – and papules. The pathogenesis is unknown but may involve androgenic hormones, bacteria, and genetic factors. The treatment depends on the severity but in moderate‐to‐severe acne systemic tetracyclines or isotretinoin may be considered.

Several potential interrelated processes, including hormonal influences on sebum secretion, follicular hyperkeratinization, and inflammation brought on by C. acnes colonization, are frequently linked to the pathophysiology of acne vulgaris (102). C. acnes is a rod‐shaped commensal bacterium in the pilosebaceous unit that is Gram‐positive, anaerobic, and microaerophilic. Although the amount of C. acnes is similar between acne sufferers and healthy controls and is not in itself connected with the severity of the disease, the strain populations vary in both groups (102).

In this regard, certain subtypes (ribotypes) of acne vulgaris have been suggested to play distinct roles in the pathogenesis of acne vulgaris. Overall, these ribotypes have been associated with upregulated levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as IFN‐α, TNF‐α, IL‐12, and IL‐23 (103, 104). Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that causing CD4+ T cells to secrete IL‐17A and IFN‐γ, it has been shown that C. acnes can initiate the TH17 and TH1 response pathways, which are active in acne lesions (105). The infundibulum and sebaceous duct in the pilosebaceous unit are locations where the TH17 immune response can influence keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation. It can also encourage neutrophil infiltration by releasing chemoattractants from these cells. The upregulation of certain proinflammatory (e.g., TNF‐α and IL‐17) cytokines in acne is indeed supported by the efficacy of biologic therapy in severe acne (106).

Psoriasis

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease manifested by reddish scaly lesions. Although numerous factors may contribute to the pathogenesis of psoriasis, several studies suggest excessive stimulation of the innate and adaptive immune systems as a pivotal component in the development of psoriasis. Inflammation cascades may be initiated and maintained by interactions between T lymphocytes, dendritic cells, neutrophils, and cytokines such as IL‐23, IL‐17, and TNF‐α. This may lead to the rapid proliferation of keratinocytes and the infiltration of immune cells into the skin lesions (70).

Although the contribution of the skin microbiome in the pathogenesis is unclear, various studies have attempted to characterize the microbiome in psoriasis lesions (81). A recent study found a significant reduction in microbiome alpha‐diversity and beta‐diversity in psoriasis‐affected skin. This skin dysbiosis was demonstrated by reduced abundance of Cutibacterium, Burkholderia, and Lactobacilli spp., along with an increased abundance of Corynebacterium kroppenstedii, Corynebacterium simulans, Neisseria, and Finegoldia spp. in psoriasis skin compared to healthy skin (107). Among these species, S. aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes are often stressed as important species that may interact with the innate immune system. In particular, the enterotoxins secreted by S. aureus (e.g., Staphylococcal enterotoxin B), have been associated with flares in psoriasis (69). Colonization of S. aureus in psoriasis has also been speculated to affect an aberrant innate immune response, including the stimulation of AMPs and the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL‐12 and IL‐23 (108).

CONCLUDING REMARKS

This review demonstrates the pathogenic complexity of the potential crosstalk between a cutaneous dysbiotic microbiota and the immune system. Indeed, in chronic inflammatory skin diseases an increasing body of literature points to the pivotal role of the interplay between the microbiota, immune cells (e.g., AMPs), and nutrients.
