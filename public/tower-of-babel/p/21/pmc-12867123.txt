“As you know, parents’ love differs from others.” Understanding needs and strengths shaping mental health among unaccompanied Afghan minors in resettlement in the U.S.
Authors: Ngozi V. Enelamah, Rochelle L. Frounfelker, Audrey Montgomery, Farhad Sharifi, Said Hasib Arwal, Caroline Dilts, Theresa S. Betancourt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Globally, unaccompanied minors face more significant risks for poorer mental health and other psychosocial issues while also grappling with mental health, language, and acculturation challenges. The United States admitted 1550 unaccompanied Afghan minors (UAMs) as humanitarian parolees among some 76,000 Afghans evacuated following the US withdrawal and Taliban takeover in 2021. Most UAMs had lived all their lives in Afghanistan’s protracted conflict. Now resettled in the U.S., it is critical to understand their experiences to inform services, prevent common mental health problems, and strengthen pathways for their development. This study examined the recently arrived UAMs’ needs and challenges in resettlement.The study gathered data from free listing interviews and focus groups with UAMs and their caregivers (n = 29). Guided by a transactional ecological model, thematic content analysis was used to identify and categorize problem clusters and common themes outlined by UAMs and caregivers. UAMs were primarily concerned about being separated from their families and expressed sadness, longing, and grief. Other problems include language barriers and mental health symptoms that affect functioning across their ecological levels of development. Caregivers emphasized challenges related to adjusting to the American cultural context.Findings support the pressing need to design and provide culturally relevant services to reduce mental health problems, strengthen family relationships, and engender positive future outcomes. The results have implications for the provision of equitable mental health services for resettled Afghan youth.

Introduction

Children and youth constitute over 40 % of the people displaced globally because of conflicts and natural disasters (Scharf et al., 2021; UNHCR, 2024a; UNICEF, 2025; Wheaton et al., 2020). Forced displacement exposes children to increased adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and risks of emotional and behavioral problems (Betancourt, 2015; Save the Children, 2023). Children under 18 who migrate without their biological parents are categorized as unaccompanied and separated children (UASC), unaccompanied minors (UMs), or unaccompanied children (UC) (Kellenberger et al., 2004; UNHCR, 1997). Compared to other forced migrant children traveling with their parents, the absence of the protective care of biological parents is associated with increased health disparities, such as childhood illnesses, injury, trauma, child labor and poverty, challenges adapting to a new culture, and a predisposition to mental health problems (Mitra and Hodes, 2019; O’Higgins et al., 2018; Sierau et al., 2019; UNHCR, 1997; von Werthern et al., 2019). Unaccompanied minors are also vulnerable to abuse and trafficking due to debt, familial poverty, ignorance of labor laws, social exclusion, mental health issues, or marginalization (Mitra and Hodes, 2019; Young et al., 2024) (see Fig. 1).

Approximately 124,000 individuals and families were forced to migrate from Afghanistan to other countries following the withdrawal of Western forces in August 2021 (Lawry, 2022; Stitt et al., 2021). Of those paroled to the United States (U.S.), approximately 1550 children migrated without a biological parent or legal guardian (KIND, 2024; Stitt et al., 2021). These unaccompanied Afghan minors (UAMs) had lived most of their lives in the context of Afghanistan’s conflict, which has lasted over three decades (UNHCR, 2024a, 2024b; UNICEF, 2019). Before evacuations, the conflict resulted in trauma and increased vulnerability for many Afghans (Andisha and Lueger-Schuster, 2024; Panter-Brick et al., 2009, 2015). During flight and protracted resettlement processes, Afghan refugees experienced numerous stressors precipitating depression, anxiety, discrimination, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), including low levels of service utilization (Alemi et al., 2014; Alemi and Stempel, 2018).

Studies of Afghan refugees in the U.S. have focused on adults, reporting post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), acculturation stressors from language barriers, social isolation, prolonged documentation procedures, structural, and socioeconomic barriers to accessing healthcare (Alemi et al., 2015; Hamrah et al., 2021; Lipson et al., 1995; Stempel and Alemi, 2021). A baseline assessment of need for Afghan refugees in the U.S. (n = 30, age range 19–75, 50 % female) found that over 50 % met the criteria for a current diagnosis of PTSD, and this incidence increased with age (Samad et al., 1996). In a sample of U.S. Afghan refugees (n = 29, ages 16–70 years), participants reported lack of social support, employment stressors, and mental health sequelae as significant community and resettlement stressors (Lipson, 1991). Barriers such as lack of trust, communication tools, mental health issues, and challenges navigating the systems were reported by participants in a study of recently resettled Afghan refugees (ages 18–55 years) in the U.S. (Reihani et al., 2021), while highlighting public assistance, education, and provider rapport and continuity as facilitators to improving access to healthcare.

A cross-sectional sample of special immigrant visa holders (SIVH) was analyzed, comprising 81.8 % Afghan adults and 82.3 % Afghan children paroled to the US between 2014 and 2016. Children aged 6–17 years constituted 40.7 % of the Afghan child sample, with intestinal parasites and high lead level being the flagged results of interest (Kumar et al., 2020). A recent study of newly arrived Afghan parents highlighted their shift in parenting perspective from a focus on safety to experiences of acculturation stress resulting from balancing cultural norms, social isolation, and appreciation of an improved health system (Rosenberg et al., 2022).

A review providing guidance for Afghan refugee families in the U.S. noted that Afghan youth mental health mirrors their parents’ traumatic stress and other disorders, including experiences of food insecurity. Findings stress the need for context- and culture-specific interpretations of educational achievement, simpler terms, and assessments that consider noncognitive factors during interactions or treatment (Hodges et al., 2024). An earlier study of Afghan youth refugees (12–24 years) in the U.S. found that household socioeconomic status, war experience, and other regional differences across two ethnic and language groups had statistically differential effects, particularly for those (45 %) who met the criteria for lifetime depression (Mghir and Raskin, 1999). Educators working with recently arrived Afghan refugee youth in the U.S. highlight needs such as assisting them to bridge gaps in culture, language skills, communication, and relationship building (Lee et al., 2024).

There are limited studies focused on Afghan youth who are unaccompanied minors (UAMs). A study in the United Kingdom (UK) of UAMs’ (n = 222, ages 13–18 years, 100 % male) sleep problems as a more culturally appropriate assessment of PTSD observed higher rates of PTSD than in the general population, associated with cumulative trauma and semi-independent placement type (Bronstein et al., 2012; Bronstein and Montgomery, 2013). Another study of recently aged-out UAMs (n = 31, 18–24 years) in the UK, where Afghan youth were the majority (39 %) of young migrants, noted deterioration in skills and psychological wellbeing from factors such as unstable immigration status, mental health issues, finance, and social capital among barriers to their socioeconomic wellbeing (Gladwell, 2021). A phenomenological study of UAMs’ (n = 13, then, 18–24 years) integration in Sweden highlighted UAMs’ understanding of the need to learn the host country language and social codes, the role of art, finding meaning, and fulfilling their potential (M. Hosseini and Punzi, 2021, 2023). A U.S. qualitative study on recently paroled Afghan youth (17–24 years, 56 % unaccompanied) examined past and current sources of distress and the youth’s coping mechanisms, noting a preponderance of adverse mental health symptoms resulting from uncertainty, worry, and the extent of systemic barriers to attaining housing and legal status (Z. Hosseini et al., 2025). Recently paroled adults also recount anguish over family separation, isolation, and past trauma (Nylen et al., 2025).

Overall, reports indicate that the Afghan population is one of the fastest-growing groups of immigrants to the U.S. (Alemi and Stempel, 2018; Lee et al., 2024; Montalvo and Batalova, 2024). However, there is limited empirical evidence on the experiences of recently paroled UAMs, who have a short and exclusive immigration status (Z. Hosseini et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2024) and myriad uncertainties to reckon with, particularly in the changing face of America’s immigration policies. It is therefore imperative to gain insight into the specific needs of this subset of newcomer Afghans. This qualitative study aimed to examine the experiences, needs, protective factors, and strengths shaping the mental health of recently paroled UAMs resettled in the U.S. and support the development of culturally relevant mental health screening tools and preventive services. In this paper, we examine the problems and challenges faced by the paroled UAM who arrived in the U.S. since 2021.

Unaccompanied Afghan minors (UAMs) background

The UNHCR defines an unaccompanied minor as a child not being cared for by an adult who is legally or customarily required to do so (UNHCR, 1997). A child in this context is defined as “a person who is under the age of eighteen, unless, under the law applicable to the child, the majority [age] is attained earlier” (The United Nations, 1989) and “unaccompanied” implies someone who is “separated from both parents” or previous legal or customary caregivers (UNHCR, 1997). According to American law, a person under eighteen who enters the country unattended, without legal status, or whose parent or legal guardian is not present is considered an unaccompanied child or minor (HHS Office of Refugee Resettlement, 2024b). Separated children who may be living with another family member other than their parents are categorized differently from unaccompanied minors and unaccompanied refugee children (Kellenberger et al., 2004; Moutsou et al., 2023).

Arrival, oversight, and placement in the U.S.

The U.S. Department of Homeland Security transfers custody of UMs (in two distinct groups namely the unaccompanied children (UC) and the unaccompanied refugee minors (URM)) to the Health and Human Services Office of Refugee Resettlement (HHS ORR) (Alto et al., 2022; HHS Office of Refugee Resettlement, 2024a) where an officer interviews applicants (even if evacuated on demand) to determine their resettlement eligibility. The applicant undergoes multiple security checks, screenings, and medical exams, and ORR provides them with food, shelter, healthcare, educational, and recreational activities. Through its agencies, the ORR places the minor with a vetted sponsor (or parent [if for reunification], relative, group home, or foster parent) whose records have been checked or protects them if there is an indication of family violence, neglect, or abuse.

The Operation Allies Welcome (OAW) facilitated the humanitarian parole of approximately 76,000 Afghans, including 1550 UAMs, to the U.S. in 2021(Lawry, 2022; Montalvo and Batalova, 2024; Stitt et al., 2021). Unaccompanied minors with a U.S. tie (designated as Attached Afghan Minors) were released to the relative’s care. UAMs without U.S. ties remained in ORR’s Unaccompanied Children Program or assigned to long-term foster care, a vetted sponsor, or, if eligible, the ORR’s Unaccompanied Refugee Minor program (HHS Office of Refugee Resettlement, n.d., 2024a).

The recently paroled UAMs have grown in a context where youth face significant distress, where nine youngsters either died or suffered significant injuries daily in Afghanistan’s conflict zones (UNICEF, 2019). Reports note that less than 25 % of the population completes the first nine years of education, and less than a fourth of all children (3.7 million), including girls (60 %), do not attend school (Stitt et al., 2021). Overall, the UAMs arrived in the U.S. from Afghanistan’s collectivist and patriarchal cultural context (Lee et al., 2024; Stitt et al., 2021), in addition to being part of a generation of youth who, since the U.S. intervention in 2001, have experienced improved education, health, social media access, and more progressive sociocultural contexts before their forced migration (M. Hosseini and Punzi, 2021).

To better understand the needs and bridge the gap in research among this population of Afghans, this study collected data from UAMs, their caregivers, and service providers (reported elsewhere). The study was consciously and reflexively conducted, using the lens of the bioecological model of child development (Bronfenbrenner and Morris, 2007), transactional model (Sameroff, 2009) and thematic analysis, “a method of identifying, analyzing, and reporting patterns or themes within data” (Braun and Clarke, 2006; Clarke and Braun, 2018) to interpret the findings. In addition, we adapted the three domains of the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) 32-item checklist (Tong et al., 2007), akin to other accepted qualitative research reporting standards (O’Brien et al., 2014; Sinha et al., 2024), to structure the findings.

Methods

Domain 1: research team positionality

Bearing in mind that our identities and biases as researchers inevitably influence the research process, the project created a diverse group comprising an Afghan community advisory board (CAB) and an academic institution Afghan and non-Afghan research team. Seven academic research staff members included the senior author, TB, a Caucasian, whose research examines the developmental and psychosocial consequences of concentrated adversity on war-affected children and group mental health interventions with demonstrated effectiveness. RF is a White social epidemiologist from North America with advanced training in cultural psychiatry and refugee mental health. NE is a Black immigrant woman researcher at a higher institution in New England, focusing on children and adolescents in forced migration. AM is a Caucasian social worker working directly alongside newly arrived communities within refugee resettlement and healthcare settings. FS is a male social worker and recent Afghan evacuee, initially stationed at Camp Atterbury, and internally displaced populations in Afghanistan. Furthermore, SH is a male Afghan researcher and cultural adviser who was a graduate student at the time of the study. Both Afghan-origin researchers are trilingual, fluent in English, Dari, and Pashto, and facilitate engagement and sensitivity to the needs and preferences of the different ethnicities of the UAM/other participants. All researchers are trained in cultural sensitivity/competence and engaged in regular meetings to plan, debrief, triangulate, and ensure that the observer effect does not affect data collection.

Domain 2: theoretical framework

To situate the dynamic changes in the UAMs’ experience of forced migration, this study combined perspectives from the bioecological model of child development and the transactional model. (Bronfenbrenner, 1997; Bronfenbrenner and Morris, 2007; Lynch and Cicchetti, 1998; Sameroff, 2009). Bronfenbrenner’s bioecological model of child development emphasized that human development occurs through progressive and complex reciprocal interactions between a child and others, as well as systems across layers of their environment, using a process-person-context pattern. Sameroff (2009), through the transactional model, highlighted the transactional nature of these interactions, “an ongoing process of mutual and emergent effects within relationships” and postulated that the systems or ecological levels of influence (hypothesized by Bronfenbrenner) are not static, but rather dynamic, bidirectional, and interdependent. The transactional model illustrates children’s transactional interactions and relationships in various social settings (Sameroff and Mackenzie, 2003). The transactional model also considers the mechanisms and processes of the systems that facilitate interaction with the child. Thus, our adapted ecological-transactional model posits that the child and the layers of their ecology (micro, meso-, macro, and chronosystems) continuously influence each other, thus distinctively shaping the child’s experience. In this study, the ecological-transactional model will guide the examination and interpretation of how services received by UAM or interactions with caregivers, case managers, immigration lawyers, and government policies or laws are perceived, organized, and what set of behaviors in these systems shape UAM experiences.

Planning

The researchers formed two community advisory boards (CABs) – one for adults and one for youths - drawn from existing and newly arrived Afghan communities, government staff, and programs/resettlement agencies serving minors in the research team’s institution’s area. Both the adult and youth CABs met three times each during the study period (March–November) to support the research through interpretations and meaning-making. The research team established a relationship with other external stakeholders and resettlement agencies that assisted with recruiting research participants. The research instruments were translated and back-translated into Pashto and Dari, the two most widely spoken languages in Afghanistan (Lee et al., 2024; Mghir and Raskin, 1999; Stitt et al., 2021), and reviewed by the CAB. The RAs’ deep understanding of the cultural and linguistic context helped ensure that the materials were not only accurate but also approachable and sensitive to participants’ circumstances. Researchers were careful to make the language and process feel welcoming and non-threatening, and to avoid any legalistic or formal wording that might cause fear, especially given the vulnerable status of many participants. While the tools were not formally field-tested in a pilot study, the back-and-forth translation process itself served as a form of internal validation to ensure clarity, cultural relevance, and emotional safety.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Boston College Institutional Review Board (IRB). Guardians, foster parents, or the overseeing agency were required to provide verbal consent for the UAM under 18, including assent by the UAM before participation.

Sampling and recruitment

The sampling frame consisted of the population of UAMs (n = 1550) who were paroled along with other Afghan nationals in 2021; their caregivers; and service providers from across the country. The selection criteria for UAM include those aged 10–22 (as some UAM had aged out since arrival) and their caregivers assigned through ORR-supported programs. The service providers were eligible to participate if they served unaccompanied minors. The study targeted 24 service providers and 100 UAMs and caregivers, respectively. The researchers employed a purposive convenience sampling strategy (a strategy to reach readily identifiable and available individuals easily) (Patton, 1990) through the service providers/agencies to recruit UAMs and their caregivers. Recruitment materials included email, word of mouth, and telephone calls. Initially, the team connected with UAM-specific service providers nationwide, tailoring the approach to the context and needs of that service community. For example, some preferred recruiting youth through flyers, while others requested support from case managers to identify and share information about the research project.

Due to the UAM status as minors and the protections afforded by the state, which affected recruitment, the team focused its outreach efforts to include organizations and providers that work alongside all newcomer youth, with a focus on the unaccompanied. It was critical to connect and build rapport with the organizations that work alongside UAM, many of whom act as guardians (both legally and in practice). While recruitment initially began with convenience sampling, snowball sampling (the process of locating potential information-rich participants through referrals by personal endorsement), was used as interviewees started to identify and help recruit UAMs they knew. The evolution to snowball sampling, which helps to reach hidden populations, though often fraught with selection bias, is supported by earlier research of Afghan refugees (Sulaiman-Hill and Thompson, 2011). Also, our outreach to organizations in all states of the U.S. was expected to reduce the heterogeneity of the sample, if any.

Data collection and setting

Author 1 trained the RAs in free-listing interviews and focus group discussions. Following informed consent and assent, data collection consisted of two phases: first, free-listing interviews with unaccompanied minors (UAMs) and their caregivers, and second, focus group discussions with three groups: UAMs, caregivers, and service providers, all in separate sessions. By collecting data from the UAMs, their caregivers, and service providers, we aimed to compare and obtain a more comprehensive perspective on ecological influences on UAM resettlement in the U.S. SH and FS, fluent in Dari, Pashto, and English, facilitated data collection and interviews in the participants’ preferred Afghan language. At least two researchers were involved in all data collection efforts. Since many participants lived in different states from the interviewers, all data collection was conducted via Zoom using a secure professional account. The audio data was saved on the university’s secure server, per IRB specifications. To protect participants’ privacy, we requested that each UAM or caregiver be alone before the interview began, with the option of having their cameras off (their choice). If a caregiver happened to be in the same room during the initiation of a UAM session, we politely asked the youth to move to a more private space. Privacy and comfort were prioritized throughout.

Free listing interviews

The research team conducted free listing interviews with UAMs and caregivers. Free listing (free recall listing) is a qualitative approach that seeks the emic or insider perspective from individuals or members of a group and asks them to comprehensively list components of a given issue to elicit common conceptions (Hough and Ferraris, 2010; Keddem et al., 2021; Stausberg, 2021). Free listing explores how groups conceptualize or categorize issues as they understand them. While not focusing on the individual, it examines their perspective and the conceptual sphere of elements that constitute the topic of interest (Stausberg, 2021). As a hybrid model, the qualitatively listed items are ranked, graded, dimensioned, and often weighted (Stausberg, 2021).

The free listing interviews were structured to comprise three grand tour questions to understand UAMs’ problems, protective factors, and help-seeking preferences: (1) What are the problems of Afghan youth who have recently resettled in America without their biological mother or father? (reported here); (2) In the Afghan culture (or back home), what helps Afghan youth separated from their biological mother or father who have these problems listed? (3) What do UAMs and their adult caregivers do to get outside help for these problems (here in America)? In keeping with free listing norms, the questions were phrased to ensure that the youth understood that we were not focused on them, but rather on understanding the broad mix of challenges and needs of UAMs (Stausberg, 2021). Each free listing interview lasted 30–60 min, although some took longer, depending on how participants chose to elaborate in their responses.

In the second step, the researchers recruited UAMs, caregivers, and service providers for follow-up focus group discussions to extend and triangulate discourse from the free listing interviews. The researchers used a semi-structured interview guide that reiterated the three questions from the free listing interviews, probing the participants for problems, challenges, and other issues related to UAM resettlement in the U.S. before proceeding to additional issues. The focus group discussions lasted approximately 60 min. In addition to the audio recording, the researchers took detailed notes and memos during the interviews and discussions with participants, including from a caregiver and two youth who did not consent to be audio recorded during the free listing interview. Each UAM and caregiver in the free listing and focus group efforts received a gift card in appreciation for their time.

Data cleaning, management, and transcript return

Audio recordings were transcribed and translated by a professional language agency and back-reviewed by Afghan-language speaking members of the research team to ensure accuracy and prevent loss of meaning. The de-identified transcripts were checked for typographical and other errors and uploaded to MAXQDA for analysis (VERBI Software, 2024). The researchers stored the transcribed text on the college’s secure server. Summaries from the free-listening interviews were shared with new participants in the second phase (focus groups) before proceeding with additional questions. The research team did not return the transcripts to the participants for member checking, comments, repeat interviews, or corrections, primarily to reduce the risk of harm, as earlier researchers note the harmful nature of repeated interviewing of unaccompanied minors (Andisha and Lueger-Schuster, 2024; Moutsou et al., 2023). During the study, HS and FS served as cultural brokers, maintaining ongoing communication with participants to clarify any vague items.

Domain 3: data analysis

The units of analysis were the entire texts from each interview and focus group discussion (Chenail, 2012; M. R. Roller, 2020), and the coding categories were inductive, emerging from the analysis (rather than being chosen a priori) (Bingham, 2023; Braun and Clarke, 2006). Four coders (NE, AM, FS, HA) were involved in analyzing the data, with each member of the team initially reviewing and open-coding the data separately. Guided by the phases of thematic analysis methodology (Braun and Clarke, 2006), the researchers reviewed and continually moved back and forth through data, individually and collectively reading, immersing, and familiarizing themselves with the data while noting initial ideas and observations. In phase 2 and based on the analytic scheme, the researchers generated parent initial and subsequent codes corresponding to the three research questions (primarily problems of UAM, Afghan-oriented solutions, and U.S. support). Codes continued to emerge as we became immersed in the data and harmonized individual codes to a detailed codebook.

The team met several times to review for inter-coder agreement and to combine root words and synonyms, explore meanings, and subsequently count the transcribed verbatim free lists and new items from the focus groups. Afghan researchers and cultural brokers helped to clarify and deepen the meaning of specific phrases used and their translations to ensure accuracy. In Phase 3, the focus shifted to searching for and agreeing on themes and subthemes. In Phase 4, we reviewed and refined our themes, defining and naming them to generate a thematic map. Items were arranged and ranked based on frequency, a measure of their significance to the respondents, where the frequency and position of occurrence of items on individual lists were used as the main parameter for determining the boundary of a “domain” (Stausberg, 2021). In Phase 5, the team defined and named the themes and engaged in memoing (Bingham, 2023; Clarke and Braun, 2018), aimed to identify and critically reflect on the aspects of the transactional-ecological model that the themes captured, as well as the degree of similarity and salience of the issues raised by the participants. Finally, in Phase 6, we aggregated and summarized consensus findings that reflect unifying realities from the participants’ responses. We also selected quotations to support and clarify major and minor themes identified in the analysis.

Data saturation

Eleven UAMs and six caregivers were reached during the free listing interviews, and an additional nine and three, respectively, for the focus groups. Although the researchers did not reach the targeted sample size, we continuously monitored emerging patterns and observed saturation (Fusch and Ness, 2015; Hancock et al., 2016; Naeem et al., 2024). We determined that the sample and data were sufficient based on the scope of the study, nature/clarity of the topic, quality of data, and the evolving study and qualitative design (Morse, 2000). We also determined that we had achieved saturation as fewer new items are added with the increasing number of participants, and relative frequency of top-listed items (Christou, 2025; Squire et al., 2024; Stausberg, 2021). Notably, by situating the data within existing literature/theory (Bingham, 2023; Fusch and Ness, 2015) and triangulating free listing with the additional focus group samples, we reinforced the trustworthiness of our findings. Furthermore, the salient responses obtained reflected data, thematic, and theoretical saturation for the research question, and limited new insights that revealed a new conceptual category emerged (Christou, 2025; Fusch and Ness, 2015; Squire et al., 2024). Alongside the counts of freely listed items, the researchers observed the salience of the frequently mentioned items, including how early in the interview the items were listed (Stausberg, 2021).

Reflexivity

Throughout the study design, data collection, and analysis, the authors met frequently and engaged reflexively (M. Roller and Research Design Review, 2025; Tong et al., 2007) (discussing and reflecting critically at each stage, creating memos and documenting thoughts, examining cultural and philosophical meanings of interactions with participants, our assumptions, values, personal reactions, and emotional connection to the data) – toward the study objectives. We actively and transparently acknowledged and sought to address sources of bias within ourselves and during interactions with the participants.

Results

This study focused on the challenges of paroled UAMs and involved free-listing interviews and focus group discussions with UAMs and their caregivers. The study sample (n = 29) consisted of 20 UAM (11 in free listing interviews and 9 in focus groups) and nine caregivers (6 in free listing interviews and 3 in a focus group discussion) – 12 unique UAM and 7 caregivers across both methods. A final list of 33 issues was derived from UAM, and 20 issues were identified by their caregivers, with 13 items shared for both groups (See Table 2). The UAMs differed slightly from caregivers in terms of priority (first mentions) and frequency of issues. For instance, UAMs’ primary concern was their longing for their parents and separation from family. Emanating from this separation and current distress were expressions of mental health symptoms and the consequences of the absence of traditional parental roles. Participants listed language barriers (inability to communicate in English) and their (structural mesosystem) consequences, including challenges with school enrolment, academic efficacy, obtaining employment, and lack of social connections, as problematic. Other salient issues related to family reunification, which had roots in immigration documentation and legal status, as well as the impact of different policies and systems (placement type, minor age, and agency relationships/systems) on their autonomy or access to resources. We review these findings across the transactional-ecological levels.

Most UAMs arrived in the U.S. in 2021, with a few others in 2022. The UAM reported a high level of disorientation in the early months of arrival, characterized by blurring of the date/time of arrival, the period at the initial camp, and resettlement with a family. Most of the youth interviewed were under the legal guardianship of the state, and a few had aged out since arriving in the U.S. and were not living with an adult caregiver. Take, for instance, my brother here, who is very young - not everyone can be a parent to a person. Whether it’s like a sister or a mother, when your clothes get dirty, you need money to pay for laundry, since my mother and sister are not here to wash them for us. We are hearing harsh speech. We also have the EBT card.” -UAM, male.2.4Emotional and behavioral health. There was a preponderance of mental health problems alluded to by the UAM and their caregivers. The terms “mental health,” “depression,” and “anxiety” were often used by older UAM. Terms used in the Afghan languages (see Table 2) were translated and back translated by the cultural experts and correspond to Western mental health diagnostic screening questions. Several common signs of depression were elicited as the youth listed the most salient problems of UAM. For instance, sadness was the most frequently mentioned emotional and behavioral problem listed by UAM, including feelings of loneliness (none of the caregivers mentioned this). For instance, the UAMs frequently used phrases such as “longing/missing”, translated from “Del Tangi,” which is an emic term encapsulating an intense and emotionally charged deep and physical ache in the chest. The UAM noted that they “missed”, were ‘worried”, were always “thinking about”, and were “concerned” about their parents. Some had experienced loss (death of a parent) during forced migration and injuries. Beyond the sadness, there were expressions of fear and loneliness (a subjective feeling of disconnectedness). Additionally, hopelessness and hints at wanting to give up, a common sign of depression, trauma, or loss, were evident in the UAMs’ interviews again, often in the context of separation from and longing for their parents. This information was triangulated by stories of UAM despair, intention to self-harm, and suicidal ideation reported by caregivers during the focus groups.“… I lost my father. It was so tough. It was so hard for me that I lost hope for life because I was very dear to my father, and that was very hard for me. I couldn’t do anything or go anywhere. I was like a prisoner. I couldn’t go anywhere or do anything.” – UAM, male, 18 years.Many UAMs and caregivers spoke about the inability to focus (due to family separation). Chronosystem

Beyond the time-related basic processes of coming of age (attaining the milestone of 18 years in the U.S.), most of the UAMs have experienced a non-normative transition that involved forceful migration, separation from family, and a harsh encounter with a new culture and society. In outlining the problems of youth who have recently migrated to the U.S., while UAM were distressed and worried about the uncertainty of their parents’ situation back home, the majority of the lists and expressions of challenges focused on the present, on their time in the U.S. While few UAMs referenced past stressors, forced migration, or adversity resulting from the conflict in Afghanistan, a chronosystem perspective suggests that the rupture and effect of the family separation while not easily verbalized and externalized in the present, can be an internalized and enduring experience (Walg et al., 2020) shaping the experiences of the UAMs in forced migration in the U.S.

Another chronosystem-influenced observation from the data revolves around the changing perceptions and circumstances of the UAM as they resettled in the U.S. The changes of the UAM were evident in their physiological aging out, evidence of their resilience, old and new strengths [reported elsewhere], acquisition of new skills (individual level), microsystems changes, such as moving to new and higher levels of schools/education, and moving households (out of the caregivers home to independent living; and many had moved at least four times since arrival). At the macro level of transactions, many caregivers reported significant adjustments to the U.S. culture over the chronosystem.

“When I graduated from the school, I wanted to go to technical college, since I selected the field of mechanics to study. I faced problems for seven months because I could not find anyone to help me find a college where I could continue my studies. Finally, after much searching, my uncle directed me to a place to find help. I am currently studying at college. A free college, and it has been two months since I started studying there.”

Discussion

This study considered the challenges shaping UAM experiences in the U.S. and the apparent transactional nature within the ecological levels of influence (see Table 4). Based on the transactional-ecological model, the UAM experience will be influenced by the relationship between the multiple transactional possibilities across these layers. The model also emphasizes the importance of children (UAM) describing their understanding and experience of the environment’s layers and the changes that occur as they develop. These experiences will be reciprocally determined or regulated over time (Sameroff, 2009).

The study uncovered some of the UAM family experiences, their non-normative forced transition to the U.S., evacuation/resettlement in the U.S. macrosystem, the establishment of new families, schools, and peers, and the UAMs’ experiences in negotiating, developing, and adapting (with or without support) – in line with the integrative model for understanding the adaptation of immigrant-origin youth (Suárez-Orozco et al., 2018). As highlighted in the quotes, UAMs expressed evidence of everyday developmental tasks of the adolescent brain, such as developing self-regulation, learning new cultural expressions, negotiating their identity, self-control, and responsibility (Suárez-Orozco et al., 2018). Youth discussed having to learn to prepare their meals, wash their clothes, and participate in the school system. In addition, this study highlights the UAM navigation of new family norms and values, living with caregivers or older siblings who aged out, including the intersection of their social position (ethnic identity, religion, gender, and resources available).

Also, most UAM participants were male-identifying in line with earlier reports that most unaccompanied minors are male. Earlier reports note that while children constitute about 41 % of the displaced person and refugee population globally, in the U.S., boys, 13–17 years, constituted 68 % of unaccompanied children (Frounfelker et al., 2020; UNICEF, 2021). The few female participants our study reached had aged out. Earlier studies of Afghans (18–60+ years; 50 % female) in forced migration identified being a female gender (in addition to inability to speak English, lower education, being previously married or unemployed) as risk factors for moderate depression and anxiety disorder (Alemi et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2024; Sulaiman-Hill and Thompson, 2011; Welsh and Brodsky, 2010).

While the core theme and the most dominant problem listed by the UAM was the pain of separation from their parents and family, who they longed for to join them in the U.S. (individual and distanced microsystem levels), the most frequent and saliently expressed challenges were emotional and behavioral health themes, and most resulted from family separation. Symptoms of depression, including sadness, loneliness, worry, listlessness, hopelessness, and lack of focus, were expressed. There were also reports of trauma and experiences of childhood adversity. UAMs expressed these problems in tandem with acculturation stressors aligned with symptoms of physical, emotional, behavioral, and cognitive distress experienced by unaccompanied children (UNICEF, 2021).

Individual-level issues (mental health, language, and developmental skills) intersected with microsystem issues (the absence of parental care/guidance, livelihood opportunities, and challenges at school). Furthermore, at the individual level, there was also a preponderance of relationship needs (familial care, social isolation, and friendships). Other studies have emphasized the newcomer youth’s salient need for peer networks and social skills (Z. Hosseini et al., 2025). For instance, findings from educators working with newly paroled Afghan youth in the U.S. (Lee et al., 2024) affirm that strategies such as fostering relationship building, rewarding, and fostering communication and language ability are highly impactful in addressing observed challenges.

At the microsystem level, UAMs expressed a need for parents’ roles, concern for parents’ safety, and a growing sense of responsibility to support their parents, such as earning money to send to them, noting “they were suffering and in a bad situation.” This corroborates earlier reports, including the burden on UMs to assume adult roles before they are ready (UNICEF, 2021).

Furthermore, children form deep emotional bonds with their caregivers, particularly parents in the microsystem (Dolan, 2024). Though absent, UAMs’ biological parents still wielded significant influence and response from UAMs. Most UAMs had not lived without their parents before this time and linked the separation to other problems. The UAMs’ expressions, including intense longing for reunification, indicated a close relationship with their biological parents. However, the UAM’s deep pain emanating from the separation from their parents could not be ascertained as either evidence of strong, secure attachment or a lack thereof. It was evident from the study that the UAMs’ separation was unanticipated, unlike other minors whose parents may have sent them ahead (Moutsou et al., 2023). It was unclear how UAM’s emotional bond with current caregivers, if any, was developing or moderating the challenges faced.

Earlier research notes that forced family separation was the second most common war trauma of U.S. Afghan refugee youth (next to being close to death) and was a significant predictor of PTSD and depression (Mghir et al., 1995). While we did not measure trauma empirically, expressions from UAMs and caregivers appear to align with these. Past studies indicate that the most prevalent coping mechanism of Afghan families in forced displacement or war-related trauma involved the exchange of social support and searching for information about loved ones (Welsh and Brodsky, 2010), including faith connections and rest. Accordingly, most UAMs in our study were adamant about family reunification, appreciated the social support (Sierau et al., 2019), and reported feeling better after being connected to their families over the phone.

Studies highlight the significant role families play in fostering positive psychosocial outcomes for their families in resettlement (Weine, 2011). The UAM acknowledged the role of their caregivers as mentors, a source of social networks, and opportunities. Most UAMs were in foster placements that mirrored a nuclear family environment, which helps restore loss of attachment figures and a buffer for stressors (O’Higgins et al., 2018). We could not ascertain the socioeconomic status of the caregivers; therefore, we could not determine how much, if any, UAMs’ experiences at the new family/microsystem level could be tied to resources available. Extending from the proximal relationships, UAMs spoke favorably of support from the U.S. Afghan community, which would have cultural connotations and elements of comfort. Some UAMs were living with Afghan families, corroborating past research on the benefits of ethnically matched supports (Mitra and Hodes, 2019). The individual-level ethnic affinity is a positive interaction with macro and policy-level influences, such as culture, placement type, special housing, or support, where culturally sensitive placements are preferred (Mitra and Hodes, 2019; O’Higgins et al., 2018).

Another interplay between microsystems in the mesosystem was the downplaying of emotional and behavioral health issues or a higher likelihood of caregiver dismissal of mental health or biological parent needs, particularly in culturally aligned UAM placement respondents. This tone-down corroborates earlier research that notes poor recognition of distress and underutilization of services among unaccompanied minors and their guardians, where only 30 % of minors’ mental health distress was observed or reported by caregivers (Mitra and Hodes, 2019). At the meso- and macrosystem level, UAM needed to understand the health and education systems of the U.S., a common challenge among immigrant and refugee communities (Kumar et al., 2020; Reihani et al., 2021; Stitt et al., 2021; UNICEF, 2019). UAMs shared stories of being unable to access resources and linked these to their case managers, type of placement, language barriers, aging out, or school policy-related issues. There were mentions of caseworker delays and a lack of clarity within the system regarding what supports UAMs qualify for in terms of policy provisions and funding to meet their needs.

Past studies note that a stable and multiculturally affirming environment in schools has a protective effect on internalizing mental health outcomes and integration of UAM and other immigrants (O’Higgins et al., 2018). From our study, there were mixed reactions from UAMs on the school environment. Mostly in school, they received support, found goals to work toward, and had recreational opportunities. Others had challenges such as language barriers, bullying, and perceived discrimination that impeded their engagement with schoolwork or relationships. Cultural shock, which the caregivers mostly cited, was also discussed at the school and health service use levels.

Caregivers (microsystem) were emphatic on problems such as the lack of guidance and resources from the governing systems (macrolevel), including UAM adjustment to the U.S. culture and context (particularly the gender perspective, where primarily UAM boys encounter U.S. women’s empowered roles and positions and must decide how to respond to women as authority figures or service providers.) Recent studies note that the UAMs are adjusting well (Lee et al., 2024).

Both groups of respondents acknowledged that documentation was a significant challenge and barrier to UAMs, which was tied to family reunification and access to resources. The legal processes, paperwork, and legwork needed to resettle UAMs were daunting, including language barriers and travel logistics. Most UAMs felt they did not have guidance on the methods, which was expressed if they were living independently or with an Afghan family, compared to those in more formal foster system settings or with caregivers who could advocate for them. Furthermore, the study highlighted concerns that UAMs risked aging out before establishing solid foundations for naturalization or reunification.

Limitations and trustworthiness of findings

There were many limitations to the accomplishment of this study. The most predominant constraint was that the participants were hard to reach, resulting in a less-than-adequate sample size. The lack of access to the UAMs/caregivers to participate in the study was policy- and institutionally related. There were concerns about the minor status of the UAMs, their vulnerability/adjustment challenges, and the lack of clarity on the policy position regarding engaging with them. Most of the gatekeepers reached could not obtain clearance on releasing the UAMs in their care to participate in the research before the project ended, again speaking to the recursive transactional nature of the macro, meso-, and micro-systems shaping outcomes for the UAMs.

Methodologically, the study targeted 100 UAMs and caregivers each; however, only 20 UAMs and nine caregivers could be reached. Free listing scholars have reported sample sizes ranging from 20 to 378 (Hough and Ferraris, 2010; Keddem et al., 2021). Scholars noted that a sample of 20–30 informants provides sufficient data and can be checked by the degree of saturation achieved (Morse, 2000; Stausberg, 2021). Furthermore, “in general, where greater samples can give more accurate results, it is largely irrelevant for the determination of a domain or for understanding its key features; the accuracy of findings will mostly lie in creating a more exhaustive list of idiosyncratic item which is largely irrelevant for the determination of a domain or for understanding its key features” (Stausberg, 2021) [pp 557], which salience, we sought to achieve. Additionally, the free listing interview provides data that is free of investigator bias; thus, strengthening the reliability of the findings, justifying the method (Stausberg, 2021). Of note, several studies with Afghan refugees in the U.S. and in the literature had small to average samples ranging from 8 to 34, for example - (Mghir and Raskin, 1999; Morioka-Douglas et al., 2004; Welsh and Brodsky, 2010). It is unclear whether the challenges our study experienced in reaching the Afghan sample cut across.

While the sessions with the UAMs and caregivers showed a high level of individual task-specific competence (where listing is defined as a task, with participants well-suited or competent to respond to the research questions adequately) (Stausberg, 2021), the study findings cannot be generalized to all UAM populations. For instance, there were variations by location, in placement types, U.S. state, parole specifications, caregiver socioeconomic status, ethnicity/culture, and community support. In addition, as noted by qualitative scholars (Morse, 1999), the purposeful selection of research participants, including how and to whom the knowledge gained and findings are extended, in albeit small qualitative samples, is significant in contributing to theory, completeness, and saturation. Finally, being a qualitative study, we cannot ascertain that the interactions between the ecological levels of the child’s development are causal and reciprocal (Sameroff and Mackenzie, 2003) in effect but have only used the model as an illustration.

Conclusion and implications

This study focused on the challenges of paroled UAMS, as identified through free-listing interviews and focus group discussions with UAMs and their caregivers, interpreted through the ecological-transactional model. This model incorporates a social-environmental perspective that can support a theory of change for more effective interventions (Miller and Rasmussen, 2024). The findings advance previous studies on unaccompanied children and add new layers to the perspectives of this unique group in the U.S. immigrant community.

The everyday experiences of unaccompanied children (UNICEF, 2021), such as lack of consistent caregivers, disruption in attachment, loss, grief, uncertainty about the future, feelings of responsibility, and challenges with language, communication, and cultural differences, were expressed by the UAMs. Earlier studies note that the adverse impact of daily stressors from social conditions encountered after war and relocation surpasses trauma experienced before migration (Andisha and Lueger-Schuster, 2024; Miller et al., 2008; Miller and Rasmussen, 2024), thus increasing UAM’s susceptibility to risk. This underscores the need to prioritize ongoing culturally appropriate mental health awareness, preventive and therapeutic services for UAM beyond the typical policy-mandated resettlement and hand-off. Furthermore, the sample reached was predominantly male. It is critical to ensure that UAM who identify as women are also prioritized for support, given earlier constraints that impeded their equal access to opportunities (Z. Hosseini et al., 2025; Stitt et al., 2021).

More robust programs can strengthen UAM’s language skills, thereby bolstering their interactions with educators/schools, healthcare, and other formal and informal support. This includes diverse educational choices and access to healthcare services that meet cultural and spiritual needs. Additionally, to maximize the benefits of ethnically and culturally matched care, there is a need to empower the American Afghan community on trauma-informed mental health, given the potential for internalized trauma among the minors. For instance, interventions can use the UAM mental health-related phrases (see Table 3) to facilitate meaning-making, prevention, and interventions. As corroborated by earlier research (Hosseini et al., 2025), the understanding of these local terms can assist in developing culturally equivalent assessments and enhance service provision for UAM.

The UAM expressed intense longing for their parents – is this evidence of strong attachment and a healthy bond with their parents, even in the context of adversity? Is this a resilience factor? How does this contrast with the high level of suicidal, self-harm, and other internalizing and externalizing behaviors reported in the UAM? Are the UAM experiencing attachment disruption, and to what extent is this adding to their stress and connection to their new environment, and how are multiple layers in the ecology interacting? How are past (including adverse childhood experiences, ACEs) and ongoing stressors associated with the current mental health of UAMs? These are questions for future research. Furthermore, based on the ecological-transactional model, it is assumed that the individual UAMs are likewise shaping communities by their interactions and in their evolution as immigrants in the U.S. ecology. Experimental research designs are considered the strongest test of ecological transcultural influences (Sameroff and Mackenzie, 2003), alongside longitudinal research, which is required to track the UAM experiences and their corresponding interventions and responses.

Caregivers ventured that UAM adjustment experiences in resettlement may lead to conflicts with their families upon reunification, as parents may encounter children “different” from the ones they left. Agencies must develop strategies for smoother reunions through promising family-strengthening programs and opportunities for narrative healing that could be adapted to support UAMs (Betancourt et al., 2020; Neville et al., 2022). Community-level promotion of welcome, integration, and optimal mental health, including notable examples from faith-based efforts, has been applicable (Lee et al., 2024; Trinidad et al., 2018). Timely interventions would be most significant in UAM’s critical stage of adolescence, where opportunities for growth and brain plasticity can be harnessed (Monk et al., 2013; von Werthern et al., 2019).

As noted by another study, geopolitical considerations such as prior involvement with the U.S. government facilitated the resettlement of selected parolees, resulting in disparities in service, exacerbating their feelings of distress (Z. Hosseini et al., 2025), in addition to existing differences among Afghan ethnic groups. There is a need for culturally balanced equity considerations as practitioners facilitate the services and resettlement of parolees. Furthermore, the U.S. macrosystem is experiencing significant policy changes and uncertainties with the new government. These fears could imply additional mental health stressors for the UAMs. Immigration policymakers and planners need more advocacy for UAMs caught in the crosshairs of these developments.
