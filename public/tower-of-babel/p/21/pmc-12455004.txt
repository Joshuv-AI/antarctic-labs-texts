The multifaceted impact of a high-salt environment on the immune system and its contribution to salt-sensitive hypertension
Authors: Li Wang, Jihong Hu, Kailun Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Salt-sensitive hypertension (SSBP) is a common form of hypertension which responds strongly to dietary sodium intake. It is also associated with a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular events and target organ damage. Traditional research has focused on how the vascular, renal and neuroendocrine systems regulate SSBP. However, this study explores the profound effects of a high-salt environment on the immune system and its central role in SSBP pathogenesis, revealing key innovative findings in this field. High salt intake activates multiple key signalling pathways (NF-κB, JAK/STAT, MAPK and the NLRP3 inflammasome) in immune cells, such as antigen-presenting cells, macrophages and Th17 cells, triggering significant oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades. Specific mechanisms include high salt inducing immune cells to perceive sodium ions through the ENaC channel and NCX1, activating the SGK1/FOXO1 axis and NFAT5 to drive Th17/Treg imbalance and the release of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-17A, TNF-α and IL-1β.), excessive ROS production and the resulting protein modifications create new antigens (e.g. IsoLG), and gut microbiota dysbiosis (e.g. reduced Lactobacillus and elevated TMAO) amplifies systemic inflammation by reducing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and increasing endotoxin release, thereby activating TLR4/NF-κB and other pathways. This study emphasises the novel mechanisms by which these signalling pathways NF-κB as the core hub of inflammation; JAK2 in CD11c+ APC cells; and p38 MAPK in endothelial dysfunction and their interactions drive SSBP. These inflammatory processes impair vascular endothelial function, affect renal sodium excretion and promote renal fibrosis. They also form a vicious cycle with sympathetic nervous system activation, which collectively drives the onset and progression of SSBP. Understanding these immune-mediated inflammatory mechanisms provides an important theoretical basis for developing novel anti-inflammatory therapeutic strategies for SSBP, such as targeting specific signalling pathways or regulating intestinal microbiota.

Introduction

Hypertension is a significant global risk factor for cardiovascular disease, impacting hundreds of millions of individuals. Recent data from the American Heart Association indicates that approximately 47 % of American adults are affected by hypertension [1]. Salt-sensitive hypertension (SSBP) is a type of hypertension where blood pressure significantly rises in response to high dietary sodium [2]. It is estimated that around 50 % of individuals with hypertension and 25 % of those with normotensive blood pressure exhibit salt sensitivity [3]. This is particularly pronounced among elderly, diabetic, and African American populations [[4], [5], [6]]. In addition to known high-risk groups, low-birth-weight and preterm infants have a significantly increased risk of salt sensitivity in adulthood due to underdevelopment of the renal unit [7]. The detection rate of salt sensitivity is higher in Asian populations than in Caucasians and may be associated with genetic variants (e.g. Gly460Trp polymorphism in the ADD1 gene) [8]. This condition not only elevates the risk of cardiovascular events but is also significantly associated with target organ damage, including renal injury and cardiac dysfunction [2,[9], [10], [11]]. Consequently, it is imperative to develop a more comprehensive understanding of the pathogenesis of SSBP.

Previous investigations into the mechanisms of SSBP regulation have predominantly concentrated on the vascular, renal, and sympathetic neuroendocrine systems. However, recent findings indicate that immune system cells are also capable of sensing sodium [12]. The epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) is critical for sodium homeostasis in the kidney, thereby exerting an indirect influence on blood pressure regulation. Research has demonstrated that ENaC-mediated sodium transport in antigen-presenting cells (APCs) can induce oxidative stress and activate the immune response [13,14]. Furthermore, the inflammatory response has been identified as a significant component of the pathophysiological processes underlying salt-sensitive hypertension. Scholars have posited that inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), not only contribute to the inflammatory responses within local tissues but may also play a role in blood pressure regulation via effects on vascular endothelial function and the nervous system [10]. Research has demonstrated that the activation of pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasomes is not confined to monocytes and macrophages, but also extends to dendritic cells (DCs) and other APCs. These cells absorb excess sodium ions in an ENaC dependent manner under high-salt conditions, thereby initiating an inflammatory response [15]. Furthermore, the nuclear transcription factor κB (NF-κB), a well-established transcription factor, regulates the expression of various pro-inflammatory genes. Concurrently, the Janus kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway facilitates myeloid cell-specific JAK2 function, which is regarded as a potential therapeutic target [16]. Additionally, inhibitors of p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) have been found to mitigate hypertension-associated damage to target organs [17].

There is a strong two-way relationship between inflammation and salt-sensitive hypertension. High salt intake can trigger or worsen inflammation by activating immune cells and increasing pro-inflammatory factors. Conversely, chronic inflammation alters the body's ability to respond to salt loading, creating a self-perpetuating cycle [4]. For instance, persistent low-grade systemic inflammation impairs vascular endothelial function, leading to increased production of angiotensin II (Ang II) and subsequently elevating blood pressure levels [2]. Inflammatory mediators can directly impact the kidneys, leading to reduced filtration, fibrosis, and decreased sodium excretion [18]. In summary, inflammation is both a key feature and a central driver of salt-sensitive hypertension. This review aims to summarize research on the mechanisms of inflammation in this condition and explore the complex underlying processes.

Salt intake impacts the immune system and is linked to oxidative stress and inflammation

In 1995, researchers initially identified that hypertonic NaCl could stimulate IL-8 production in monocytes and THP-1 macrophages, thereby initiating an inflammatory response. This effect was found to be comparable to the IL-8 production induced by IL-1α [19]. Further studies have revealed that high-salt conditions significantly affect the immune system, especially by activating proinflammatory macrophages and T helper 17 cells (Th17), a subset of CD4+ T cells that secrete IL-17A [20,21]. Studies suggest that high salt intake may raise cardiovascular disease risk and worsen autoimmune diseases in Western populations [20]. The modulation of the immune system by elevated salt intake is characterized by a multifaceted mechanism operating at various levels. At the molecular level, a high-salt environment significantly affects immune cell polarization by activating the MAPK and serum glucocorticoid kinase 1 (SGK1) signaling pathways [22]. SGK1,a serine/threonine protein kinase that is regulated by mineralocorticoids and cellular stress, is pivotal in modulating Th17 differentiation through the phosphorylation of the SGK1/forkhead box protein O1 (FOXO1) axis [22].

For instance, monocytes and macrophages detect alterations in sodium concentration under high-salt conditions via the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger 1 (NCX1), which initiates nuclear factor of activated T cells 5 (NFAT5)-dependent expression of pro-inflammatory genes such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, while concurrently inhibiting the production of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-1023,24. This shift in polarization extends beyond innate immune cells and has a profound effect on the adaptive immune system. Elevated salt intake promotes the secretion of pathogenic cytokines, such as IL-17A, Granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and TNF-α. This is achieved through the upregulation of IL-23R and Retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor gamma t (RORγt) expression in Th17 cells, while concurrently reducing the immunosuppressive capacity of regulatory T cells (Treg) [[24], [25], [26]]. This Th17/Treg imbalance has been associated with the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases, such as multiple sclerosis and rheumatoid arthritis [25,27].

The immune dysfunction induced by elevated salt intake exhibits a synergistic interaction with oxidative stress. The excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) compromises the function of vascular endothelial cells and diminishes the bioavailability of nitric oxide (NO), thereby aggravating hypertension [28,29]. Furthermore, the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids by ROS leads to the formation of isoleucine (IsoLG), which can modify proteins to create neoantigens, activate DCs, and enhance inflammatory responses in Th17 cells [15,30]. Clinical studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between high salt intake and elevated serum markers of inflammation, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), IL-6, and TNF-α, in hypertensive patients. Additionally, antioxidants such as Tempol have been shown to mitigate oxidative stress-induced inflammatory damage by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway [[27], [28], [29]].

The gut microbiota serves a crucial intermediary function in this process. Elevated salt intake diminishes the prevalence of lactic acid bacteria, such as Lactobacillus murinus, resulting in a reduction of tryptophan metabolites like indole-3-lactic acid [23]. This reduction subsequently nullifies their inhibitory impact on Th17 cells [23]. Such dysbiosis establishes a positive feedback loop characterized by increased local IL-17A levels in the gut and activation of ENaC, thereby exacerbating sodium retention and inflammatory responses [23,25]. Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), produced by gut microbiota, facilitates vascular fibrosis through the activation of the MAPK/NF-κB pathway and exhibits similar activating effects in endothelial and smooth muscle cells, further underscoring its role in vascular inflammation [[31], [32], [33]]. Additionally, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including butyric and capric acid, inhibit the cyclic AMP (cAMP) signaling pathway via activation of the GPR41 receptor, consequently reducing heart rate and sympathetic nerve activity [34]. The high-salt environment disrupts the homeostasis of the gut flora, contributing to the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria and the release of endotoxin, which activates the systemic inflammatory response through the TLR4/NF-κB pathway. [35]. Animal studies indicate that probiotic supplementation or adherence to a low-sodium diet can reverse the pathogenic differentiation of Th17 cells and mitigate the pathological progression of salt-sensitive hypertension [23].

Key signal pathways

NF-κB pathway

NF-κB is a critical transcription factor extensively involved in regulating various biological processes, with a particularly significant role in mediating inflammatory responses [36,37]. Activation of NF-κB can occur through multiple signaling pathways, including cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which activate NF-κB via classical pathways, thereby enhancing the expression of genes associated with inflammation [38]. High salt activates the macrophage NF-κB pathway by inducing ROS, which promotes the secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α [39], while inhibiting Treg function [40], ultimately exacerbating vascular inflammation and fibrosis.

Research indicates that excessive protein in proximal tubular cells leads to the formation of ROS, which are implicated in the activation of NF-κB and its reliance on inflammatory signaling [41]. Epidemiological studies show that NF-κB expression is linked to more interstitial lymphocytes and macrophages, indicating that NF-κB activation promotes inflammatory cell infiltration and enhances the local inflammatory response [42].

Increased salt intake may strengthen the link between hypertension, impaired glucose metabolism, and tissue ROS production, with NF-κB acting as a secondary messenger [36]. The activation of NF-κB is significantly correlated with oxidative stress, apoptosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration, highlighting its potential role in modulating the impact of hypertension on renal function [37,42,43]. Collectively, these factors contribute to the progression of renal injury induced by uncontrolled hypertension. Consequently, NF-κB serves as a critical mediator linking high salt intake, oxidative stress, and hypertension-induced renal damage, suggesting that inhibiting NF-κB activation could be a viable strategy for the prevention or treatment of hypertension-associated renal injury. JAK/STAT pathway

The JAK/STAT pathway is essential for cellular signaling, crucial for immune responses, cell growth, and differentiation. This pathway is composed of JAKs and STATs, which are capable of responding to cytokine stimulation, thereby modulating gene expression and cellular functions. Research has demonstrated that Optineurin (OPTN) inhibits IL-10 transcription by binding to the JH1 domain of JAK2. This interaction inhibits JAK2 dimerization and phosphorylation, subsequently preventing JAK2-STAT3 interactions and STAT3 phosphorylation, which are crucial for regulating DC maturation and autoimmune responses [46]. Furthermore, animal studies have indicated that cytokines such as IL-6, IL-7, IL-17, IL-12/IL-23, as well as growth factors like GM-CSF, IL-3, and erythropoietin, among others, activate the JAK/STAT pathway [47]. While the JAK2/STAT3/sons of mothers against decapentaplegic (SMAD3) and related genes constitute a principal signaling pathway regulating inflammation and oxidative stress, their specific role in hypertension and SSBP warrants further investigation. Research indicates that transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) enhances JAK/STAT pathway activity, particularly STAT3, while IL-6 augments the TGF-β-SMAD pathway [[48], [49], [50]], suggesting a potential interaction between the JAK-SMAD and TGF-β-SMAD pathways [51]. JAK2 is strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of hypertension [[52], [53], [54]], especially within CD11c cells, where its activation facilitates the development of salt-sensitive hypertension through downstream phosphorylation and activation of STAT3 and SMAD3. This activation stimulates the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-17a, and TNF-α, which subsequently promote the formation of isolevuglandins (IsoLGs) and amplify the inflammatory response [16,55]. The JAK/STAT pathway has both pro-inflammatory (e.g., STAT3 activation) and anti-inflammatory (e.g., M2 macrophage polarization) roles [44,56]. Research indicates that removing JAK2 from CD11c + antigen-presenting cells can reduce salt-induced hypertension, but inhibiting STAT3 systemically might worsen kidney damage [16,57]. Consequently, dysregulated triggering of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway significantly influences the inflammatory response and the progression of hypertensive conditions. MAPK pathway

The MAPK pathway is essential for intracellular signaling, crucially regulating cell growth, differentiation, apoptosis, stress response, and inflammation. This pathway comprises a sequence of protein kinases that convey extracellular signals to the nucleus via a cascade of phosphorylation events, ultimately influencing gene expression and various cellular functions. The MAPK pathway is intricately linked to cardiovascular disease and is activated by numerous factors, such as neurohormones (e.g., catecholamines, Ang II, and endothelin), hypoxia, and ventricular wall stress. These activations can result in cardiac hypertrophy, altered inotropic states, and endothelial dysfunction, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of chronic cardiovascular disease [[58], [59], [60]]. Research has established that MAPK is essential for the production and signaling of inflammatory cytokines in response to environmental stressors, thereby modulating inflammatory responses. Notably, p38 MAPK, which is activated through dual phosphorylation by upstream kinases, can be stimulated by a diverse array of stimuli [61,62]. Preclinical investigations have demonstrated that the inhibition of p38 MAPK effectively suppresses the production of poly-IL-1, TNF, and other pro-inflammatory cytokines [61,62]. p38-MAPK and ERK1/2 have contrasting roles: p38-MAPK triggers vascular inflammation during salt loading [63], whereas ERK1/2 influences blood pressure by controlling kidney sodium reabsorption [64]. Some MAPK inhibitors can enhance SSBP in animals, but their clinical use is restricted by non-specific toxicity [65,66]. In animal models of salt-induced or hyperlipidemic hypertension, resulting from high-salt or high-fat diets, the activation of p38 MAPK is correlated with inflammation and cytokine production, playing a pivotal role in the regulation of inflammation. Furthermore, in salt-sensitive hypertension models caused by high-salt/high-fat diets, p38 MAPK activation is strongly associated with inflammatory cytokine production and endothelial dysfunction. Administering the p38 MAPK inhibitor SB-239063 improved regional glomerular filtration rate, renal perfusion, and reduced albuminuria and histopathological changes in these animals. This suggests that p38 MAPK may play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of hypertension-associated renal injury [67]. Furthermore, in a rat model of salt-sensitive hypertension, it was demonstrated that proinflammatory cytokines can induce the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK in endothelial cells. Conversely, p38 MAPK inhibitors were shown to suppress the expression of ICAM adhesion molecules, which are integral to the inflammatory response [17]. Activated p38 MAPK enhances ROS production via the generation of proinflammatory cytokines, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop that intensifies inflammation and oxidative stress [17]. Evidence indicates that chronic administration of p38 MAPK inhibitors can prevent the rise in blood pressure associated with a high salt/high-fat diet and improve endothelial and renal dysfunction caused by such dietary conditions, ultimately reducing morbidity and mortality [17]. The researchers concluded that treatment with p38 MAPK inhibitors not only ameliorates salt-sensitive hypertension but also provides initial evidence for the involvement of p38 MAPK in hypertension [6,17]. Consequently, the MAPK pathway is integral to cardiovascular disease and inflammatory responses. Other signal pathways

Endothelial dysfunction and NLRP3 in SSBP

High salt intake exacerbates endothelial dysfunction in SSBP by activating NLRP3 inflammasomes via ROS, leading to IL-1β and IL-17 release, vascular inflammation, and impaired vasodilation [68,69]. ENaC-mediated sodium influx in dendritic cells triggers NLRP3 activation, promoting endothelial cell apoptosis and glycocalyx damage, which amplifies SSBP [[70], [71], [72]]. The Notch1-NLRP3 interaction further drives hypertension-related vascular dysfunction [73]. Gender differences influence SSBP, with male Dahl rats showing worse vascular outcomes due to heightened NLRP3 activity, while female sex hormones mitigate inflammation [68,74]. Aging exacerbates endothelial dysfunction via GPR183 pathways, increasing SSBP susceptibility [75]. NLRP3 inhibitors like MCC950 reduce blood pressure and renal fibrosis in SSBP models, highlighting therapeutic potential [69,74]. The κ-opioid receptor agonist U50,488H enhances endothelial function via the PI3K/Akt/eNOS pathway [76]. Gut microbiota interventions, such as Dubosiella newyorkensis, show promise for SSBP treatment [77].

Gut microbiota and immune interactions in SSBP

High-salt diet-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis amplifies SSBP by activating NLRP3 inflammasomes via reduced SCFAs (e.g., butyrate) and increased LPS, promoting IL-1β release and systemic inflammation [[78], [79], [80]]. Dysbiosis, such as elevated Helicobacter, disrupts the intestinal barrier, enhancing TLR4/NLRP3 signaling and Th17 cell activation, which exacerbates hypertension [[81], [82], [83]]. Butyrate inhibits NLRP3, reducing ENaC-mediated sodium retention and RAS activity, thus mitigating SSBP [84,85]. Probiotics (e.g., Lactobacillus) and fecal microbiota transplantation restore microbiota balance, suppress NLRP3/IL-1β signaling, and lower blood pressure in SSBP models [82,86]. Astragaloside IV repairs the intestinal barrier by modulating NLRP3 in macrophages, offering a novel therapeutic approach [84]. NLRP3-deficient mice show altered microbiota diversity, forming a microbiota-inflammasome-immune feedback loop that regulates SSBP [86,87]. Animal models, such as Dahl salt-sensitive rats, replicate some SSBP features like NLRP3 inflammasome activation but fail to fully mimic human-specific factors, particularly gender differences in hormonal regulation (e.g., aldosterone's role in women) [88,89]. These models often oversimplify SSBP's multifactorial nature, limiting their relevance for chronic salt exposure and aging-related salt sensitivity [89,90]. Most studies focus on acute high-salt effects, lacking insights into long-term human SSBP, especially in diverse populations.

Cellular and organisational level

Kidney

Researchers analyzing renal sections from hypertensive patients found APCs, such as macrophages, DCs, and monocytes, accumulating from the cortico-medullary junction into the deeper medulla [14]. This observation suggests that sustained hypertension may induce mechanical damage or ischemia-reperfusion injury to renal tissues, thereby initiating a localized inflammatory response. A diet high in salt results in the retention of water and sodium within the kidneys, which subsequently triggers microinflammation and endothelial damage. These alterations are closely linked to microvascular damage and inflammatory pathways within the renal system [93]. In individuals with chronic kidney disease, there is a notable correlation between salt intake and the progression of renal dysfunction. Evidence indicates that the inflammatory response within the renal tubular interstitium is markedly intensified in chronic kidney disease patients consuming a high-salt diet, as demonstrated by tubular cell injury and interstitial fibrosis [94].

Furthermore, inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6 are markedly increased in the kidneys under high-salt conditions, contributing not only to the inflammatory response but also potentially accelerating renal tubular injury and fibrosis [93]. Animal studies show that activating the transcription factor ETS-1 increases the expression of MCP-1 and MMP-2, which are involved in leukocyte movement and extracellular matrix remodeling [18]. In this context, ETS-1 has been identified as a critical factor in the progression of renal injury in Dahl salt-sensitive rats [95]. ETS-1 may facilitate renal damage in these rats by upregulating the expression of the chemokine MCP-1 and enzymes involved in extracellular remodeling, such as MMP [95]. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome results in the activation of the proteolytic enzyme caspase-1 (CASP-1), which subsequently secretes IL-1β and IL-18. Both human and animal studies have indicated that IL-1β is a significant contributor to renal inflammation [[96], [97], [98]]. Both human and animal studies have indicated that IL-1β is a significant contributor to renal inflammation [15,[99], [100], [101]].

A study found that treating mice with vascular endothelial growth factor C (VEGFC) reduced renal fibrosis and increased CD3+ T cells and F4/80+ macrophages in the kidneys, alleviating hypertensive renal injury [102]. Additionally, research conducted on Dahl rats demonstrated that renal NO deficiency diminishes renal blood flow, resulting in SSBP. However, supplementation with l-arginine or stimulation of soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) enhances NO-cGMP signaling, improves renal perfusion, and prevents SSBP [[103], [104], [105]]. Hypertension and a high-salt diet cause kidney inflammation and damage by accumulating APCs, increasing inflammatory factors, and activating certain transcription factors and vesicles.

Cardiovascular

The study observed a significant reduction in heart rate by approximately 25 beats per minute after an 8-week period in rats subjected to a high-salt diet [9]. In Dahl salt-sensitive rats, a high-salt diet was associated with a marked increase in blood pressure and an exacerbation of cardiac metabolic and inflammatory features compared to those on a low-salt diet [9]. Furthermore, the serum cytokine/chemokine profiles in the high-salt group exhibited significant elevations in IL-1β, IL-4, IL-5, IL-7, GM-CSF, VEGF, and MCP-1 levels. These findings suggest that high salt intake not only impacts the physiological parameters of the cardiovascular system but also induces endothelial dysfunction through various mechanisms, thereby promoting the development of hypertension [9].

In individuals with salt-sensitive hypertension, endothelial cell dysfunction is potentially linked to multiple factors. Primarily, elevated salt consumption results in an increased sodium burden within the body, precipitating a hypervolemic condition. This condition intensifies vascular endothelial pressure, consequently causing endothelial cell damage and dysfunction [106]. Studies have indicated that diminished NO bioavailability is a characteristic feature of SSBP [103]. Endothelial cell dysfunction is often shown by reduced endothelium-dependent vasodilation due to lower NO availability. Thus, in salt-sensitive hypertension patients, this dysfunction might lead to decreased vasodilation.

Research indicates that eNOS activity is inhibited in the endothelium of salt-sensitive hypertensive patients, resulting in decreased NO production [107]. This reduction exacerbates vasoconstriction and contributes to the development of hypertension [107]. Furthermore, vascular dysfunction exhibits gender differences, with the NO-mediated component of endothelium-dependent vasodilation being more pronounced in men than in women [108,109]. During acute salt loads, the absence of such compensatory vasodilation is predictive of a significant increase in arterial pressure in salt-sensitive humans and Dahl salt-sensitive rats [110,111]. Consequently, endothelial dysfunction is not merely a consequence of hypertension progression but also a significant contributing factor to its onset.Research investigating endothelial dysfunction has demonstrated that rats subjected to a high-salt diet exhibit significant endothelial impairment within four weeks [17]. In a separate study, spontaneous hypertensive stroke-prone rats (SHR-SP) experienced a progressive increase in blood pressure over weeks to months, accompanied by a reduction in endothelium-dependent vasodilation, which subsequently led to renal dysfunction and neurological deficits [112]. Endothelial dysfunction may contribute to hypertension progression. In SHR-SP, it is associated with nitrotyrosine buildup in the vascular wall, suggesting that quick endothelial NO inactivation and excess ROS lead to peroxynitrite formation [107].

Excessive salt consumption results in sodium and water retention within the body, subsequently inducing a hypervolemic state. This condition increases vascular pressure and stress, causing damage and dysfunction to endothelial cells. Studies show that people with salt-sensitive hypertension have reduced endothelial responsiveness, evidenced by decreased sensitivity to vasodilatory factors on a high-salt diet [113]. The hypervolemic state induced by high salt intake may further compromise endothelial cell function by promoting oxidative stress [114]. High-salt diets can cause endothelial cells to release pro-inflammatory cytokines, leading to dysfunction and potentially promoting cardiovascular diseases such as atherosclerosis [115]. Consequently, regulating salt intake and enhancing endothelial cell function could represent crucial strategies for managing salt-sensitive hypertension.

Immune cell

Myeloid cells contribute to local inflammation and worsen hypertension on a high-salt diet, possibly through tissue sodium aiding monocyte endocytosis [116,117], as elevated tissue sodium levels associated with high-salt diets appear to stimulate macrophage chemotaxis [118]. There is a growing body of evidence indicating that neutrophil activation and endocytosis precede those of monocytes [119,120]. The increase in blood neutrophil, eosinophil, and monocyte counts induced by a high-salt diet further substantiates this hypothesis [121]. The increase in blood neutrophil, eosinophil, and monocyte counts induced by a high-salt diet further substantiates this hypothesis. CCR2 is a chemokine receptor predominantly expressed on monocytes, playing a crucial role in mediating monocyte infiltration in inflammatory diseases [116,122,123]. Studies show that high salt conditions in vitro lead to more monocytes crossing the endothelium, resulting in increased cutaneous macrophages [122].

Besides, Myeloid cells also enhance the local inflammatory response by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, which not only impact renal function but may also result in cardiovascular system damage [124,125]. The ablation of JAK2 in CD11c + cells has been shown to reduce the phosphorylation of STAT3 and SMAD3, as well as renal immune cell infiltration. CD11c is expressed in bone marrow-derived cells, including monocytes, granulocytes, and B-cell subsets [16]. In salt-tolerant individuals, naïve B cells and DCs exhibited the most significant alterations in gene expression from baseline following salt loading [126]. Conversely, in salt-sensitive populations, the most substantial number of gene expression changes was observed in natural killer (NK) cells and NK/CD8 (Cluster of Differentiation 8) T cells [126]. Notably, immune cells in salt-sensitive groups, particularly monocytes, undergo significant changes [126]. High salt exposure, both in vivo and in vitro, prompts the differentiation of monocytes into DCs [127]. Furthermore, the interaction between 14,15-epoxyeicosatrienoic acid (14,15 EET) and monocyte isoleucine formation in the kidney implies that 14,15 EET may play a regulatory role in oxidative stress and salt-sensitive inflammation [126]. These findings underscore the critical role of medullary cells in the hypertension and inflammatory responses induced by a high-salt diet and suggest potential therapeutic targets.

Th17 cells and IL-17A are crucial in salt-induced vascular dysfunction, but the effects of a high-salt diet (HSD) are debated: some studies suggest HSD inhibits Th17 [92], while others claim it raises IL-17A levels [128]. This inconsistency may stem from different experimental models or salt intervention durations [90,129]. Although NF-κB and JAK/STAT often work together in SSBP [130,131], they can oppose each other under certain conditions like oxidative stress [56,132]. For example, Nrf2 activation can suppress NF-κB and boost STAT3's antioxidant effects, a topic that needs more exploration [56,133].

Conclusion

SSBP represents a multifaceted pathological condition, characterized not merely by aberrant blood pressure but also by the pivotal role of inflammatory responses as central drivers. A comprehensive understanding of the molecular underpinnings of inflammatory mechanisms in SSBP, particularly focusing on the roles of ENaC, NLRP3 inflammasomes, NF-κB, JJAK/STAT pathway, and MAPK pathway, enhances our ability to elucidate the processes by which high salt intake initiates or intensifies inflammatory states. This understanding also sheds light on how chronic inflammation compromises the body's capacity to manage salt loading, thereby perpetuating a self-reinforcing cycle. Furthermore, evidence indicates that myeloid cells contribute not only to local inflammatory responses but also to the exacerbation of hypertension under high-salt dietary conditions, thereby promoting vascular remodeling and dysfunction. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting these critical signaling pathways and cellular components may offer promising avenues for the future management of SSBP.

Limitation and future directions

Despite advancements in understanding SSBP's inflammatory mechanisms, current research has limitations. Future studies could uncover new therapeutic targets and prevention strategies. Much research relies on rodent models, which may not accurately represent human disease due to differences in salt metabolism. Most studies focus on short-term high-salt exposure, lacking insights into long-term human health effects. Factors like genetics, age, gender, ethnicity, and environment are often ignored, affecting individual responses to high-salt diets. Research typically examines isolated pathways or cell types, overlooking complex interactions between systems like the immune and cardiovascular systems. Lastly, while potential biomarkers have been identified, there is no consensus on the most specific and sensitive ones for diagnosing and monitoring SSBP.

Future research should prioritize: Long-term human studies to assess chronic salt intake's impact on SSBP via NLRP3, ENaC, and microbiota interactions. Gender-specific models to explore sex hormone effects on SSBP inflammation, particularly in postmenopausal women [4,89]. Clinical trials validating novel targets (e.g., NLRP3 inhibitors like MCC950, probiotics) in SSBP management [69,82]. Standardized translational guidelines integrating multi-omics for biomarker discovery in SSBP [134]. Interdisciplinary studies on SSBP's interaction with aging and immune dysregulation for therapeutic precision [135]. Addressing these gaps through improved models and targeted clinical studies will enhance SSBP therapeutic development.
