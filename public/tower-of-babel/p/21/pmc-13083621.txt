Zamtocabtagene autoleucel: dual CD20-CD19 targeting for NHL
Authors: Hrishikesh K Srinagesh, Lori S Muffly
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Balke-Want et al1 report the results of a phase 1 study that evaluated zamtocabtagene autoleucel (zamto-cel) for relapsed/refractory (R/R) non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHLs). Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has transformed the treatment landscape for B-cell NHL, yet relapse after CD19-directed CAR-T therapy remains problematic. The efficacy of CD19 CAR-T therapy is limited by both tumor-intrinsic factors, such as target antigen modulation that leads to CD19-negative relapse,1 and by T-cell–intrinsic factors, such as diminished expansion, exhaustion, and lack of central memory and naïve T-cell phenotypes.2 Zamto-cel, an autologous anti–CD20-CD19 tandem CAR-T product that is rapidly produced using fresh T cells, challenges both of these shortcomings.

Zamto-cel is an autologous anti–CD20-CD19 tandem CAR-T product with a 4-1BB costimulatory domain that activates via an OR logic-gate. In this study, zamto-cel was manufactured using the fully automated, closed-loop CliniMACS Prodigy system, obviating the need for cryopreservation and leading to a median vein-to-vein time of 14 days (range, 13-14 days). Twelve patients with R/R NHLs were enrolled with 6 patients receiving dose level (DL) 1 (1 × 106 cells per kg) and 6 receiving DL2 (2.5 × 106 cells per kg). For DL1, patients with a variety of NHL histologies were enrolled, whereas for DL2, enrollment was restricted to patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) who relapsed after 1 line of therapy and who were ineligible for autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT). CAR-T therapy–mediated toxicities were infrequent and low grade; there were no cases of grade ≥3 cytokine release syndrome, and only 1 patient developed reversible immune effector cell associated neurotoxicity syndrome. In terms of efficacy, the best overall response was achieved in 50% of patients who received DL1 and in 100% of patients who received DL2. Durable responses were observed with 3-year progression free survival rates of 66.7% (95% confidence interval [CI], 19.5-90.4) and 33.3% (95% CI, 4.6-67.6) at DL1 and DL2, respectively.

To investigate the mechanism of zamto-cel activity, the authors performed detailed correlative studies. Zamto-cel maximal expansion in vivo was not different between the DLs but was significantly higher in complete responders (CRs) than in non-CRs (mean Cmax, 1092.5 vs 111.3 cells per μL). Immunophenotypic analysis of the zamto-cel drug products (DPs) revealed low expression of exhaustion markers, such as programmed cell death protein-1 and lymphocyte activation gene 3, and relative but nonsignificant increases in CD27 and CD127/interleukin-7R on CD8+ CAR-Ts. To evaluate the phenotypic consequence of the increases in these cell markers, the authors performed single-cell RNA sequencing on CAR+CD27+CD127+ cells in the zamto-cel DPs. They found increased expression of genes related to protein biosynthesis and of KLF2, which regulates the migration of naïve T cells. In contrast, these cells exhibited lower transcription levels of genes related to cell division, suggesting that this CD27+CD127+ immunophenotype represented central memory T cells (Tcm). Indeed, the authors found that the Tcm CD8+ and CD4+ T cells were relatively enriched in the DP of CRs when compared with that of non-CRs and that these Tcm exhibited greater persistence in patients who achieved CR than in those who achieved non-CR.

This phase 1 clinical trial is the second to show a favorable safety and efficacy signal with zamto-cel at the recommended phase 2 dose (2.5 × 106 cells per kg) using fresh, noncryopreserved autologous T cells.3 Although this study and the previously published phase 1 trial by Shah et al used a similar manufacturing system, Shah et al used a point-of-care CAR-T manufacturing strategy, whereas manufacturing for the current trial was performed centrally.3 Reassuringly, zamto-cel performed similarly across the 2 trials. Taken together, these data provide the rationale for 2 ongoing phase 2 pivotal trials, namely DALY2-US (zamto-cel DL2 as third line in R/R DLBCL, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04792489) and DALY2-EU (zamto-cel DL2 as second line in R/R DLBCL and ASCT-ineligible, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04844866).

Although these data strengthen the evidence for zamto-cel as a novel CAR-T product for the treatment of NHL, several questions remain. First, the authors propose that dual targeting of CD19 and CD20 may limit CD19 modulation as a mechanism of post–CAR-T therapy immune escape. However, previous experience with bispecific CAR-T constructs suggests that achieving robust, simultaneous engagement of both antigens is not guaranteed.4 Although all patients had CD20 expression at baseline and 10 of the 12 patients expressed CD19, the extent to which zamto-cel effectively targets both antigens or prevents antigen-loss relapse was not reported and must be evaluated in future studies. A clue may be gleaned from a recently presented preplanned interim analysis of DALY2-US that showed that a few of the patients who relapsed following zamto-cel had target antigen loss.5 Second, although the authors postulated that their manufacturing approach yielded products with improved phenotypic characteristics, their small sample size precluded definitive conclusions. Nonetheless, the vein-to-vein time of 14 days compares favorably with commercially available CAR-T products, which typically range from 30 to 48 days.6 With the results of DALY2-US and DALY2-EU forthcoming, we will likely soon know the answers to these questions and, importantly, whether the early promise of zamto-cel will be realized.
