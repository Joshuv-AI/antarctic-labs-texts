High BMI and smoking jointly cause COPD: a Mendelian randomisation study
Authors: Heidi Mikkelsen, Eskild Morten Landt, Sarah Caroline Weisenfeldt Marott, Marianne Benn, Børge Grønne Nordestgaard, Morten Dahl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe tested the hypothesis that genetically high body mass index (BMI) and genetically high tobacco smoking jointly are causally associated with a higher risk of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) than each risk factor alone.MethodsWe used two large Danish population-based cohort studies, The Copenhagen City Heart Study and the Copenhagen General Population Study linked to national Danish patient discharge and death registries. For the genetic analysis, we included 112 943 adults genotyped for five BMI and one tobacco smoking increasing gene variant. Risk ratios with 95% CIs for COPD were estimated for the risk factors in combination and individually by use of allele scores for BMI and smoking.ResultsSeparately, both high BMI and tobacco smoking increased the risk for COPD (Ps<0.001), yet the highest risk was observed in the group of individuals with a combination of high allele scores for both high BMI and tobacco smoking (Ps≤0.04). Observationally, the highest risk for COPD was observed in the groups of people having high cumulative use of tobacco smoking, irrespective of BMI.ConclusionsThe combination of causal, genetically high BMI and tobacco smoking resulted in a higher risk for COPD than the two risk factors alone.Keywords: Pulmonary Disease, Chronic Obstructive; Tobacco and the lung; Clinical Epidemiology; COPD epidemiology

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is characterised by persistent airflow obstruction and encompasses both chronic bronchitis and emphysema. It has no cure and leads to poor quality of life for millions. In many cases, the primary causes of COPD are tobacco smoke and household air pollution; however, inhaled particles cannot fully explain all cases of COPD, and increasing evidence suggests other important risk factors, including obesity.1,5

Obesity is complex and multifactorial and can be measured and comprehended in numerous ways. One of the most used proxy markers for obesity is body mass index (BMI).6 High BMI has causally been linked to several respiratory diseases and reduced lung function,37,11 but without a clear manifestation of an effect of high BMI on COPD risk.23 12,14 Observationally, the ‘obesity paradox’ phenomenon is observed for the association of BMI and COPD risk, indicating that being underweight poses the highest risk, while normal to overweight individuals tend to have the best prognosis.12 15 16 Being extremely obese may, on the other hand, increase the COPD risk.16 The theories for this phenomenon are many and are likely influenced by the presence of other lifestyle factors such as smoking. Smoking and BMI are known to have a complex interplay, and the combination of smoking and obesity has been shown to aggravate the risk of some diseases.17,19 Maybe the combination of smoking and having a high BMI could also reinforce the COPD risk? To our knowledge, the causal interplay and possible synergistic effect of smoking and obesity on COPD risk has not been fully explored.

A Mendelian randomisation (MR) study is a well-established design to investigate causal effects when randomised trials are not feasible.20,22 MR studies use exposure-associated genetic variants as instrumental variables to test for causal associations with endpoints. These studies are based on the principles of Mendelian genetics, specifically segregation and independent assortment. Further, MR studies have three core instrumental variable assumptions that must be fulfilled. There must be (1) a robust association between instrument and exposure, (2) independency of confounders for the genetic variants and (3) influence of the genetic variants on the outcome through the exposure and no other way. Using genetic variants that remain constant throughout life and fulfil these principles and assumptions, MR studies can minimise the possible confounding and reverse causality that often affect observational studies at a population level. A 2-by-2 factorial MR study can be used to investigate causal additive effects between two sets of exposure-associated genetic variants like for BMI and smoking jointly on an endpoint such as COPD.23 One previous MR study has suggested BMI is a causal risk factor for COPD,14 and three explorative MR studies suggest causal relationships between BMI and COPD mortality.3 10 24 The impact of smoking on COPD has been confirmed by previous MR studies.4 5

The objectives for this study were to examine the causal effect of BMI and tobacco smoking, both independently and in combination, on COPD risk using genetic variants. Our hypothesis is that genetically high BMI and genetically high tobacco smoking jointly are causally associated with a higher risk of COPD than each risk factor alone. Second, for comparison, we also investigated the observational association of BMI and smoking on COPD risk, both independently and combined.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

We included 121 681 individuals from two Danish population-based cohort studies: The Copenhagen City Heart study (CCHS) and the Copenhagen General Population study initiated in 1976 and 2013, respectively (online supplemental Figure 1).25 26 Individuals aged 20 to 100+ years were randomly invited through the National Danish Civil Registration system, and 99% of the participants were of Danish descent. Data were obtained through a physical health examination including blood samples for DNA extraction, pulmonary function tests (eg, spirometry), and questionnaires which were reviewed among the investigators.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease

COPD diagnoses were collected from the National Danish Patient Register following the WHO International Classification of Disease, 8th and 10th revisions (ICD-8: 491–492, ICD-10: J41–J44). Information on death and emigration was obtained using the Danish Civil Registration System and the Danish Causes of Death Register. Follow-up ended at COPD diagnosis, death, emigration or study end date at 31 December 2018, whichever came first.

BMI, smoking and potential confounders

BMI was calculated as kg/m2 based on weight (kg) and height (m) measured at the physical examination. BMI was classified in six groups from underweight (<18.5 kg/m2) to class III obesity (≥40 kg/m2) according to the WHO.27 Smoking status and daily tobacco consumption were self-reported through questionnaires. Cumulative tobacco smoking was calculated in form of pack-years summing up the overall tobacco exposure for each individual up until study entry. One pack-year is defined as 20 cigarettes (or equivalent tobacco amount) smoked every day for 1 year.

Genotyping

Genotyping was performed using the ABI PRISM 7900HT Sequence Detection System with TaqMan assays and was verified by DNA sequencing. For BMI, we used five well-defined genetic variants that are associated with a high effect size on BMI: FTO (rs9939609), MC4R (rs17782313), TMEM18 (rs6548238), BDNF (rs10767664) and GNPDA2 (rs10938397) (online supplemental Table 1).28 A weighted allele score was created with internally derived weights to summarise the effect of the five variants on BMI.28 The weighted allele score was used as an instrumental variable in the genetic studies. For smoking, we used the CHRNA3 (rs1051730) polymorphism, located in the nicotine receptor gene cluster CHRNA3-CHRNB4-CHRNA5. This single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) shows the strongest association with smoking behaviour including cumulative tobacco consumption29; no other SNP has effect size on smoking behaviour comparable to this SNP. A simple allele score of 0–2 was created by counting the number of tobacco-smoking-increasing alleles. Genotype data with missing values were excluded from the analysis. Data and code are available to other researchers on reasonable request or through a collaboration agreement with the authors. Summarised data and scripts for analyses are available according to Danish law.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Stata/MP V.17.0.

Genotype and MR analyses

For the genotypes, deviation from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium was tested using Pearson χ2 test. The strength of the instrument was tested by F statistic (F>10 is considered acceptable). A schematic overview of the study is illustrated in online supplemental Figure 2A. The causal effect of BMI or tobacco consumption on COPD risk was evaluated through instrumental variable analysis using the commands ivreg2 and ivpois in STATA, inclusive of additional diagnostic tests of the instrumental variables including Sargan test, Cragg-Donald Wald F statistic, Anderson Canonical correlation LM statistic and weak identification tests. In addition, MR analyses were also conducted as adjusted analyses. The genetic and observational analyses were conducted in the overall population and in subgroups of ever smokers and never smokers. For the 2-by-2 factorial analysis, genetic scores for BMI and daily tobacco consumption were dichotomised based on the median values (online supplemental Figure 2B). Logistic regression was used to estimate causal risk and calculate ORs with 95% CIs for COPD risk in the four BMI/smoking groups. ORs were calculated for all participants, ever smokers and never smokers. The OR estimates for COPD were compared as proposed by Altman and Bland.30 Two non-parametric tests for trend (nptrend) were used across the groups: the Jonckheere-Terpstra test for the continuous values of BMI and cumulative smoking and the Cochrane-Armitage test for ORs for COPD.

Observational studies

First, the participants were divided into groups based on measured BMI and pack-years. The risk of COPD, represented by HRs with 95% CIs, was calculated using a Cox proportional hazards model, adjusted for age and sex (and for age, sex, alcohol, education and physical activity in a sensitivity analysis). Second, an observational 2-by-2 factorial analysis was constructed, as illustrated in online supplemental Figure 2B. The participants were dichotomised into four BMI/smoking groups based on measured median values. The cumulative incidence for COPD in the four BMI/smoking groups was calculated using logistic regression adjusted for age and sex (and for age, sex, alcohol, education and physical activity in sensitivity analysis). For missing data, fill missing and imputation was used for sex, education, physical activity and alcohol consumption.

Results

121 681 individuals were included from the CCHS and CPGS (online supplemental Table 2). The participants were from 20 to 104 years of age with a median age of 59.3 years. The median BMI was 25.5 kg/m2 and 33.7% were never smokers. The never smoker group was significantly different from the ever smoker group on characteristics such as age, sex, smoke exposure and lung function (online supplemental Table 2). None of the genetic variants for BMI and tobacco smoking deviated from the Hardy-Weinberg expectations (p>0.05), and all instruments had an F statistic above 10 (online supplemental Table 3).

Genetic analysis

Genetically high BMI and COPD risk

108 421 individuals were genotyped for five genetic variants associated with BMI (FTO, MC4R, TMEM18, BDNF and GNPDA2). A higher BMI allele score was associated with higher measured BMI and increasing risk of COPD after adjustment for age, sex and pack-years (figure 1A,C). However, a higher BMI allele score was also, to some extent, associated with higher cumulative smoking in pack-years (figure 1B). Using MR, we found that a genetically higher BMI was a causal risk factor for COPD with a risk ratio at 1.11 (95% CI: 1.02 to 1.22, p=0.02) per 1 kg/m2 increase in BMI, in our overall population (table 1). However, this causal effect was not observed in our subgroup of ever smokers (p=0.07).

Genetically high tobacco smoking and COPD risk

Higher number of smoking-increasing alleles was associated with higher reported pack-years and higher risk of COPD after adjustment of age, sex and BMI (figure 1D,F). The number of smoking-increasing alleles was also associated negatively with BMI, as observed previously in our cohort (figure 1E).31 A causal association between genetically higher tobacco smoking and risk of COPD was seen in ever smokers (current and former smokers) with a risk ratio per 1 pack-year higher smoking at 1.05 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.08, p<0.001), but not in the overall population where approximately 34% were never smokers (1.07, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.17) (table 1).

Combination of genetically high BMI and genetically high tobacco smoking and COPD risk

For the combined effect of genetically higher BMI and smoking, four genetic factorial groups were created by dichotomising at the median. The baseline characteristics of the included individuals in the four groups are shown in table 2. The groups differed in measures of BMI and smoking, but also in age, secondhand smoke exposure and level of education. Genetically, the adjusted ORs for COPD in the four factorial groups are shown in figure 2A,B. The highest risk of COPD was observed in the group with both a BMI allele score above median and risk alleles of the CHRNA3 gene, evident in both the overall population and in ever smokers. Additionally, the groups with only genetically high BMI or genetically high tobacco smoking also exhibited a higher risk for COPD compared with controls. When analysing only never smokers, all four BMI/smoking groups had relatively few COPD events, ranging from 118 to 207 and no significant effect on COPD risk was observed (online supplemental Figure 3).

Observational analysis

High BMI and COPD risk

For the BMI categories, higher BMI was associated with a U-shaped risk of COPD. Specifically, having a BMI categorised as underweight or obese class II or III was associated with increased risk of COPD compared with individuals with BMI categorised as normal weight (figure 3A,B). When adjusting the analysis for age, sex, alcohol, education and physical activity, similar results to those presented were seen (online supplemental Figure 4).

High tobacco smoking and COPD risk

Observationally, pack-years and risk of COPD showed a linear positive association with a maximum HR at 12.1 (95% CI 11.0 to 13.3) for people having smoked 40 or above pack-years (figure 3C,D). Adjusting the analysis for age, sex, alcohol, education and physical activity in a sensitivity analysis did not change the results presented (online supplemental Figure 4).

Combination of high BMI and high tobacco smoking on COPD risk

When investigating the combined association of higher BMI and higher smoking extent on COPD risk, four groups were made as in the genetic analysis, this time dichotomised according to median of reported cumulative smoking (pack-years) and measured BMI (figure 2C,D). Corresponding associations could not be investigated in never smokers, as they have zero pack-years and thus could not be dichotomised. Observationally, the highest risk of COPD was observed in the two BMI/smoking groups with cumulative smoking above median. In the overall population, an increased risk of COPD was observed in the group with a BMI above median and pack years at or below the median with an OR of 1.30 (95% CI 1.15 to 1.48, p<0.001), compared with the group with low BMI and low cumulative smoking. No association of BMI with COPD was observed when it was investigated in combination with cumulative smoking above the median (Ps≥0.10). This was evident in both the overall population and in the ever-smoking group. Similar results were seen in sensitivity analysis adjusting for sex, age, alcohol, education and physical activity (online supplemental Figure 5).

Discussion

The genetic results of this study indicate that both high BMI and smoking are individual risk factors for COPD while having a high BMI in combination with high cumulative smoking jointly causes an even higher risk for COPD than each risk factor alone. This novel observed genetically higher risk in the combination groups, which can simply be due to an additive effect of high smoking extent and high BMI on COPD risk.

When looking closer at the individual effect of smoking on COPD, our genetic and observational findings support the already established knowledge about how damaging smoking is for the risk of developing COPD. The MR studies support the crucial effect smoking has on COPD risk in ever smokers, while in never smokers who comprised 36% of our study population, the effect was not observed—as would be expected. The CHRNA3 genetic variation is associated with smoking heaviness, and not smoking initiation, which can explain the more pronounced effect seen in the ever smoker group.32,34 Observationally, the effect of smoking on COPD is much larger than that of BMI. This fits well with the understanding that smoking is the most important risk factor, and BMI may only give a slight additional risk of COPD or be a risk factor for a less common COPD phenotype.

The possible effect of BMI on COPD risk is somewhat controversial, and differences in study populations, and in measures and definitions of obesity and COPD outcomes, could be part of the explanation for discrepancies between previous study results. Genetically, we observed an effect of BMI on COPD risk in both our MR study of the overall population and the factorial groups investigation. This is supported by the MR study from Sun et al, finding an OR for COPD of 1.81 (95% CI 1.41 to 2.33).14 In ever smokers, our MR results were non-significant, likely due to a smaller sample size compared with the overall population. A speculation could also be whether high BMI could increase the risk of other types of COPD phenotypes, than those dominating in ever smokers.35 36 In our factorial analysis, a weighted genetic allele score for BMI above the median, without having any smoking-increasing alleles in the CHRNA3 gene, increased the COPD risk. In never smokers, no effect of BMI on COPD was observed (online supplemental Figure 3). However, the never smoker group had a lower number of people and COPD outcomes in the group, and thus less statistical power, which could influence this analysis.

Observationally, our U-shaped relationship between BMI and COPD risk mirrors some previous findings on BMI and COPD risk and mortality.2 16 However, often a J-shaped association is reported in literature for COPD risk. The discrepancy between our U-shaped association and the J-shape may be because previous studies pooled the obesity groups (obese ≥30 kg/m2) as opposed to our three separate obesity groups based on WHO classification.12 Pooling our obesity groups also resulted in an attenuated HR for COPD risk in the obese group at 1.06 (95% CI 0.99 to 1.13) (online supplemental Figure 6 vs figure 3). In our current study, few people are represented in the obesity class II and III and even fewer COPD cases. However, the same division was used in the study of lifelong non-smokers by Fuller-Thomson et al, finding a higher risk for COPD in obese compared with people being normal weight and overweight.2 The fact that Fuller-Thomson et al observed the association in lifelong non-smokers minimises the bias from smoking and strengthens the results. The aetiology of the association between high BMI and COPD risk is not clearly explained in the literature. However, obesity is known to have a decreasing effect on lung function either via possible direct mechanical effect, increased inflammation or adipose hormones.36 37

Looking into our weighted genetic allele score for BMI, this score was positively associated with both higher BMI and higher COPD risk, as expected. Furthermore, a trend towards a positive association with cumulative smoking was observed (figure 1B). The genetic variants used for the weighted allele score for BMI in the present study are well-known and have been used for numerous MR studies to investigate the effect of BMI for various diseases,2838,42 and the same applies to the CHRNA3 polymorphism.43,45 A part of the underlying reasons for the complex entangled relationship observed between obesity and high cumulative smoking could come from the same behavioural patterns inherited genetically. The two genetic variants in TMEM18 and BDNF associated with higher BMI have shown pleiotropic effects on cumulative smoking in the form of pack-years smoked and smoking initiation.46 Both genetic variants are expressed in neural tissue and could potentially influence both high BMI and cumulative smoking through similar mechanisms in our MR studies. Smoking and high BMI appear to be difficult to set entirely apart as two individual risk factors for COPD. However, other previous MR studies using more SNPs in their genetic analysis found stronger associations between BMI and COPD,3 10 indicating that more SNPs can help minimise the potential pleotropic effect of TMEM18 and BDNF and that these two SNPs do not carry the observed effect of BMI on COPD risk. Another part of the explanation may be that individuals struggling with obesity throughout their lives due to a genetic predisposition may smoke more to control their weight. Nicotine regulates both eating habits and energy expenditure through the central nervous system and these effects are often mentioned as explanations for why people start and/or continue to smoke.18 Conversely, people who have smoked for a large part of their lives due to being genetically predisposed tend to have a lower BMI maybe because of the appetite-suppressing effect of nicotine.47 To establish the individual effect of genetic BMI and smoking, we tried adjusting the risk of COPD for smoking and BMI, respectively, and the relationship remained for smoking and COPD. Adjusting our MR studies for cumulative smoking attenuated the effects of BMI on COPD risk, pointing towards an interconnected relationship between BMI and smoking, and further studies on this are needed.

Limitations

A limitation is that our cohorts have a relatively small number of genotyped SNPs, which restricts our ability to conduct a comprehensive polygenic risk score to validate our findings. Further, replications in either a larger population or a more obese population would be preferable to investigate the individual effects of BMI on COPD risk in more detail. Using only BMI as a proxy for obesity can observationally introduce confounders and including other measurements such as waist-hip ratio and fat distribution could potentially improve the analysis. When we adjust for BMI/smoking to investigate the individual effect, a possibility exists that this could lead to collider stratification bias. Importantly, the results were similar if these adjustments were not included. Overall, observational studies can easily be confounded, which may also apply to our study. However, combining observational and genetic studies strengthens the results, as each type of study has unique strengths that contribute to more robust findings.

Implications of study

This study suggests that interventions are needed both towards smoke cessation and weight control individually, but also in combination to reduce the prevalence of COPD. The complicated interplay between BMI and smoking observed can also indicate that interventions towards weight control may indirectly influence smoking initiation and consumption. The findings of this study indicate that both BMI and smoking are individual risk factors for COPD, and their combined effect further increases the risk for COPD.

Conclusions

Using 112 943 adults from the CPGS and CCHS, we find that genetically high BMI and tobacco smoking jointly can cause the highest risk of COPD. BMI and smoking also had an individual causal effect on COPD risk, however, only in the overall population and ever smokers, respectively. Observationally, tobacco smoking alone was associated with the highest risk of COPD, but being obese in class II and III or underweight did also show associations to COPD risk.
