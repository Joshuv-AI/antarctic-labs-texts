Competition Binding of CmbHLH1L and CmNLP6/7L to CmbHLH63 Modulates Leaf Senescence in Chrysanthemum
Authors: Yuna Yang, Ziyan Qian, Yinglin Li, Rui Liu, Hong Qiao, Zhengyao Shao, Debao Huang, Jiafu Jiang, Fadi Chen, Sumei Chen, Likai Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLeaf senescence impacts the quality, productivity and post‐harvest performance of crops and ornamental plants. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms governing this process remain poorly understood. In this study, we identified CmbHLH63 as a ‘brake signal’ of leaf senescence in chrysanthemum and elucidated its critical regulatory role within the CmbHLH1L‐CmbHLH63‐CmNLP6/7L complex. CmbHLH63 promotes leaf senescence, while it is downregulated during this process. Notably, CmbHLH63 lacks intrinsic transcriptional regulatory activity; however, the expression of a majority of genes was repressed in plants overexpressing CmbHLH63. Further investigation revealed that this repression is achieved through recruiting the transcriptional repressor CmbHLH1L. We further found that CmNLP6/7L, a transcriptional activator, competes with CmbHLH1L for the interaction with CmbHLH63, leading to the release of their inhibition on downstream gene CmNLP6/7L. We found that the accumulation of CmbHLH1L is increased in the cytoplasm and decreased in the nucleus during leaf senescence. Mass spectrometry assays and subcellular localization studies revealed that CmbHLH1L undergoes lysine acetylation at position 140, which prevents its nuclear accumulation, thereby elevating CmNLP6/7L expression during leaf senescence.

Introduction

Leaf senescence, characterised by the yellowing and degradation of chlorophyll in leaves, has a profound impact on the quality, productivity and post‐harvest performance of crops and ornamental plants. It is a complex biological process that is tightly regulated and influenced by various internal and external factors, including nutrient deficiency, reduced light availability and stress conditions (Guo et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2020). Understanding and manipulating leaf senescence have become crucial strategies for enhancing crop productivity and sustainability (Zhou and Yang 2023). The ability to delay the onset or prolong the senescence process can improve nutrient remobilisation, photosynthetic efficiency and stress tolerance (Guo and Gan 2014). In the case of ornamental plants, modulating leaf senescence can be utilised to create variegated leaves with enhanced aesthetic value (Chang et al. 2013; Shao et al. 2022; Chao et al. 2024). Notably, delaying leaf senescence often has a significant positive impact on both the ornamental and economic value of these plants. Consequently, there is an urgent need to unravel the underlying regulatory mechanisms governing leaf senescence signalling.

To date, numerous signal transduction pathways associated with leaf senescence have been established in model plants, such as Arabidopsis thaliana and rice, as well as in various other plant species (Guo et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). Several transcription factors (TFs) have been identified as key regulators of leaf senescence, including NAC (NAM, ATAF1/2 and CUC2), WRKY, bHLH (Basic Helix–Loop–Helix), ERF (Ethylene Responsive Factor) and MYB (Cao et al. 2023).

The bHLH TF family represents one of the largest TF families in Arabidopsis, with some members being well‐characterised as crucial regulators of leaf senescence (Cao et al. 2023; Toledo‐Ortiz et al. 2003). Among these, the subgroup IIIe bHLH factors MYC2, MYC3 and MYC4 redundantly mediate jasmonic acid (JA)‐induced leaf senescence by directly activating the expression of SAG29. Conversely, subgroup IIId bHLH factors, including bHLH03, bHLH13, bHLH14 and bHLH17, antagonise the subgroup IIIe factors by directly repressing SAG29 (Qi et al. 2015). Furthermore, ATBS1‐INTERACTING FACTOR 2 (AIF2), a non‐DNA‐binding bHLH transcription factor, interacts with INDUCER OF CBF EXPRESSION 1 (ICE1) through their C‐terminal. The AIF2‐ICE1 complex up‐regulates C‐REPEAT BINDING FACTORs (CBFs), which negatively regulate dark‐ or brassinosteroid (BR)‐induced leaf senescence (Kim et al. 2020). Conversely, BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT 1 (BZR1), a positive regulator of BR signalling, suppresses AIF2 transcript levels and accelerates leaf senescence (Kim et al. 2020). In apples, MdbHLH93 directly activates the transcription of MdSAG18 and promotes leaf senescence (An et al. 2019). Interestingly, MdBT2 directly interacts with MdbHLH93, inducing its degradation through ubiquitination and thereby delaying leaf senescence (An et al. 2019). Additionally, MdbHLH3 regulates ethylene biosynthesis and leaf senescence by promoting the expression of dehydratase‐enolase‐phosphatase complex 1 (MdDEP1) in apples (Hu et al. 2020). A recent study has reported that the histone variant HTB4 delays leaf senescence through the epigenetic control of bHLH038, bHLH039, bHLH100 and bHLH101 transcription factors, which mediate iron homeostasis (Yang, Wang, et al. 2023). Despite the growing body of evidence indicating the importance of bHLH members in leaf senescence, their significance and the underlying regulatory mechanisms are not yet fully elucidated.

A recent study by Ariga et al. (2022) has revealed the involvement of a nitrate‐activated transcriptional cascade in Arabidopsis, which plays a critical role in chloroplast development and the maintenance of chloroplast function. The mutation of NIN‐LIKE PROTEIN 7 (NLP7), a key component of this cascade, results in a reduction in chlorophyll content in leaves. Specifically, the NLP7‐HB52/54‐VAR2 transcriptional cascade is implicated in the utilisation of photosynthetic light energy, particularly under high light conditions, thereby influencing chloroplast development and function (Ariga et al. 2022). NLP7 is well documented as a nitrate sensor and has been shown to regulate the expression of a large number of genes in response to changes in nitrate levels (Liu et al. 2017; Liu, Liu, et al. 2022). It predominantly functions as a transcriptional activator (Alvarez et al. 2020). However, the regulatory mechanisms governing NLP7, especially in the context of the leaf senescence process, are still not fully understood.

To this end, we have selected chrysanthemum ( Chrysanthemum morifolium Ramat.) as a model organism for investigating leaf senescence. Chrysanthemum is widely recognised as one of the most extensively traded and cultivated cut flowers globally. Notably, leaf senescence is a common occurrence in cut chrysanthemum, often occurring prior to flower senescence and causing substantial losses in quality and economic value. In this study, we conducted transcriptome analysis and transgenic validation to investigate these mechanisms. Our results revealed that CmbHLH63 acts as an accelerator of leaf senescence by genetically repressing the expression of downstream gene CmNLP6/7L. We further demonstrated that CmbHLH63 can recruit CmbHLH1L, a transcriptional repressor, to suppress CmNLP6/7L expression. Conversely, the transcriptional activator CmNLP6/7L competes with CmbHLH1L to interact with CmbHLH63, thereby relieving the repression on CmNLP6/7L. Interestingly, we observed that CmbHLH1L undergoes acetylation and translocation to the cytoplasm during leaf senescence, which releases the competition with CmNLP6/7L, further enhances CmNLP6/7L expression and contributes to the precise control of the leaf senescence process. These findings provide valuable insights into the regulatory mechanisms underlying leaf senescence in plants and illustrate the functional interaction between CmbHLHs and CmNLP6/7L in the regulation of leaf senescence.

Results

CmbHLH63 Promotes Leaf Senescence

To gain insights into the molecular dynamics of leaf senescence, we conducted RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐seq) experiments on mature and early senescence leaves of chrysanthemum cultivars ‘Fendante’ (Table S1). Our analysis revealed a significant number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) during senescence, with 7630 genes upregulated and 4748 genes downregulated (Figure 1A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed that photosynthesis, stress, senescence and hormone‐related GO terms were enriched in these DEGs (Figure S1A). Given that transcription factors (TFs) play a vital role in regulating leaf senescence (Guo et al. 2021), we examined all the TFs among the DEGs. Results showed that 488 TFs were upregulated, while 272 TFs were downregulated (Figure 1A). These TFs predominantly belonged to families such as C3H, AP2‐EREBP, bHLH, C2H2, WRKY and NAC (Figure S1B). Among the differentially expressed TFs, we found a prominent TF called CmbHLH63 (evm.TU.scaffold_1427.10), which exhibited the most significant downregulation with a Log2(early senescence/mature) value of −7.43 (Figure S1C, Table S2). Moreover, we re‐analysed transcriptome data generated from leaves of chrysanthemum cultivars ‘Fendante’ treated with either ethylene or its inhibitor 1‐Methylcyclopropene (1‐MCP) (Liu, Zuo, et al. 2022) and observed that the expression of CmbHLH63 was repressed by ethylene and elevated by 1‐MCP treatment (Figure S1D). These findings imply a potential involvement of CmbHLH63 in ethylene‐mediated leaf senescence. To validate its association with leaf senescence, we performed a time‐course analysis of CmbHLH63 mRNA levels using qRT‐PCR. The results demonstrated a sharp decline in CmbHLH63 transcript levels during the progression of leaf senescence (Figure 1B,C). Subcellular localization studies demonstrated that CmbHLH63 localises to the nucleus, consistent with its function as a transcription factor (Figure S1E).

To evaluate the function of CmbHLH63 in leaf senescence, we generated overexpression lines by introducing the plasmid 35S:CmbHLH63 (Figure S1F), and conducted a vase test comparing transgenic plants with wild type (WT) plants. The CmbHLH63 overexpression (CmbHLH63ox) lines exhibited an early senescence phenotype, as evidenced by accelerated leaf yellowing (Figure S1G). Furthermore, measurement of chlorophyll contents revealed significantly lower levels in the CmbHLH63ox plants compared to the WT plants (Figure S1H). The senescence‐associated genes (SAGs), commonly used as marker genes for senescence, were selected, and their expression was analysed by RT‐qPCR. The results showed that the expression of these SAGs was consistent with the senescence phenotype of the transgenic plants (Figure S1I). To further investigate the senescence‐inducing effect of CmbHLH63, we conducted leaf senescence assays using detached leaf discs, a widely employed method in previous studies (Li et al. 2013; Sakuraba et al. 2020). After 7 days of treatment, the leaf discs from CmbHLH63ox lines displayed a yellowish colour, while those from the WT plants maintained their green colour (Figure 1D). Consistently, the total chlorophyll levels remained lower in the CmbHLH63ox lines compared to the WT plants (Figure 1E). Next, we generated CmbHLH63 silenced plants using virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) assays (Figure S1J). Remarkably, these silenced plants exhibited a delayed senescence phenotype compared to plants transformed with empty vectors (Figure 1F,G, Figure S1K,M). Given that CmbHLH63 promotes leaf senescence while being significantly downregulated during this process, we conclude that it may act as a regulatory ‘brake signal’.

CmbHLH63 Genetically Represses the Expression of CmNLP6/7L to Promote Leaf Senescence

To explore the transcriptional activity of CmbHLH63, we performed a yeast two‐hybrid galactosidase assay using the yeast expression vector pGBKT7, which contained the open reading frame (ORF) of CmbHLH63. The positive control was represented by pCL1, while pGBKT7 served as the negative control. The results indicated that yeast cells transformed with pGBKT7‐CmbHLH63 failed to grow on the SD/‐His‐Ade medium with or without X‐α‐gal (Figure S2A). These findings suggest that CmbHLH63 does not possess transcriptional activation activity in yeast.

To further assess its transcriptional activity, we employed a luciferase (LUC) reporter transient expression assay in chrysanthemum mesophyll protoplasts. We expressed 35S:GAL4DB‐CmbHLH63 in protoplasts, with 35S:GAL4DB as the background expression control and 35S:GAL4DB‐AtARF5 as the control with transcriptional activation activity. Co‐transformation was performed with the reporter vector 5 × GAL4UAS‐LUC (Figure S2B, upper panel). The results demonstrated that the LUC intensity of 35S:GAL4DB‐CmbHLH63 was similar to that of the background control (Figure S2B,C), indicating that CmbHLH63 does not exhibit transcriptional regulatory activity.

Transcriptome analysis comparing leaves from CmbHLH63ox and wild type (WT) plants revealed that the majority of gene expression was repressed in CmbHLH63ox (Figure 2A), indicating that although CmbHLH63 may not possess inherent transcriptional regulatory activity, it likely collaborates with other partners to exert transcriptional repression functions. To further investigate this, we generated chimeric activator lines by introducing the 35S:CmbHLH63‐VP64 construct into chrysanthemum (Figure S2D), where VP64 serves as a transcriptional activator domain. As expected, the CmbHLH63‐VP64 lines displayed a delayed senescence phenotype (Figure 2B,C, Figure S2E). Subsequent transcriptome analysis demonstrated that a large number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were activated in the CmbHLH63‐VP64 plants (Figure 2D), providing evidence that CmbHLH63 promotes leaf senescence by genetically repressing downstream genes.

To gain deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying CmbHLH63‐mediated leaf senescence, we compared the genes regulated by CmbHLH63 in both the CmbHLH63ox and CmbHLH63‐VP64 lines. We identified 690 genes that were down‐regulated in CmbHLH63ox lines but not in CmbHLH63‐VP64 lines. Additionally, 5 212 genes that were not regulated in CmbHLH63ox lines were further up‐regulated in CmbHLH63‐VP64 lines (Figure 2E). Notably, among these genes, we found 27 that were repressed in CmbHLH63ox lines but were further activated in CmbHLH63‐VP64 lines compared to WT plants (Figure 2E).

Among these 27 genes, we identified one that encodes a protein closely related to AtNLP6 and AtNLP7, which we named CmNLP6/7‐Like (Figure S2F). The CmNLP6/7‐Like (CmNLP6/7L) gene exhibited the most significant down‐regulation in the CmbHLH63ox plants (Table S3, Figure S2G). Considering that its Arabidopsis homologue AtNLP7 has been reported to be essential for maintaining chlorophyll levels (Ariga et al. 2022), our analysis specifically focused on the CmNLP6/7L gene. The CmNLP6/7L expression was further validated using qRT‐PCR analysis (Figure S2H). We cloned the promoter sequences of CmNLP6/7L and identified an E‐box (CANNTG), which is a predicted binding motif for bHLH proteins, located at −218 bp from the promoter (Figure 2F). ChIP‐qPCR assays performed on leaves of CmbHLH63ox plants demonstrated the binding of CmbHLH63 to the region of the CmNLP6/7L promoter containing the E‐box motif (Figure 2F). The binding of CmbHLH63 to the promoter sequences was further confirmed through a yeast one‐hybrid assay (Figure S2I).

To investigate the direct regulation of CmNLP6/7L by CmbHLH63, we conducted a LUC reporter assay in N. benthamiana. Agrobacterium carrying 35S:CmbHLH63, with the empty vector as a negative control, was co‐transformed with the reporter vector CmNLP6/7Lp:LUC (Figure 2G, upper panel). The results showed that the LUC intensity of 35S:CmbHLH63 was comparable to the negative control (Figure 2G,H), indicating that CmbHLH63 does not directly regulate CmNLP6/7L.

To further investigate the role of CmNLP6/7L in leaf senescence, we examined the senescence phenotypes of overexpression lines (CmNLP6/7Lox) and silencing lines (amiR‐CmNLP6/7L) (Wang et al. 2024). Compared to wild‐type plants, detached leaf discs from CmNLP6/7Lox plants exhibited a delayed senescence phenotype, while amiR‐CmNLP6/7L plants showed an accelerated senescence phenotype (Figure 2I,J, Figure S2J). Previous studies have indicated that NLP7 directly stimulates the downstream genes HB52 and HB54, subsequently regulating the expression of VAR2 to govern chloroplast development in Arabidopsis (Ariga et al. 2022). We then identified homologues of the aforementioned genes in chrysanthemum and found only HB52 and VAR2 homologues in this species. Consequently, we analysed the expression of CmHB52 and CmVAR2, together with three other genes related to chloroplast maintenance, namely CmVAR1, CmVAR3 and CmTHF1 (Ariga et al. 2022). Our findings revealed that, except for CmVAR3, the expression levels of these genes were generally reduced in amiR‐CmNLP6/7L plants and elevated in CmNLP6/7Lox plants compared to their expression in wild‐type plants (Figure S2K). These results suggest that CmNLP6/7L modulates leaf senescence by influencing chloroplast development.

To verify whether CmbHLH63‐mediated leaf senescence is dependent on CmNLP6/7L, we silenced CmNLP6/7L in CmbHLH63‐VP64 lines using the VIGS assay. Compared to CmbHLH63‐VP64/EV‐pCVA control plants, CmbHLH63‐VP64/CmNLP6/7L‐pCVA plants exhibited an early senescence phenotype (Figure 2K,L, Figure S2L,M). Furthermore, overexpression of CmNLP6/7L (CmNLP6/7L‐TRV) in CmbHLH63ox plants resulted in a delayed senescence phenotype (Figure 2M,N, Figure S2N,O).

In summary, these results collectively indicate that despite lacking intrinsic transcriptional regulatory activity, CmbHLH63 exerts genetic repression on the expression of CmNLP6/7L, thereby positively regulating leaf senescence.

CmbHLH1L Is Required for CmbHLH63‐Mediated Repression of CmNLP6/7L

Considering that the expression of CmNLP6/7L is negatively regulated in CmbHLH63ox transgenic plants and no direct transcriptional activity of CmbHLH63 on CmNLP6/7L was observed, we proposed that other repressor(s) might cooperate with CmbHLH63 to repress transcription. To explore this hypothesis, we conducted a yeast two‐hybrid screening using CmbHLH63 and identified a potential interacting partner, which belongs to the CmbHLH family. The protein has four amino acid substitutions and an additional five amino acids at the C‐terminus, compared to the previously reported CmbHLH1 protein (Zhao et al. 2014). Consequently, we named it CmbHLH1‐like (CmbHLH1L, Figure S3A,B). RT‐qPCR analysis indicated that CmbHLH1L does not change during leaf senescence (Figure S3C).

Firstly, we examined the subcellular localization of CmbHLH1L by expressing the protein fused with a GFP tag. CmbHLH1L‐GFP was observed in both the nucleus and cytoplasm (Figure 3A). To further confirm the interaction between CmbHLH63 and CmbHLH1L, we performed a yeast two‐hybrid assay, which revealed a strong interaction between the two proteins (Figure 3B). Subsequently, we validated this interaction through in vitro and in vivo protein–protein interaction assays, including pulldown and split‐luciferase complementation (Split‐LUC) imaging assays. In the pulldown assay, we constructed HIS‐CmbHLH63 and GST‐CmbHLH1L vectors and expressed recombinant proteins using Escherichia coli BL21. Bands of the expected molecular weight for HIS‐CmbHLH63 were observed in the pulldown assay using anti‐His antibody (Figure 3C). In the Split‐LUC assays, we fused the coding sequences of each protein with either the C‐terminal or N‐terminal half of LUC. These constructs were then transformed into Agrobacterium strain GV3101 and infiltrated into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. The luciferase signal was detected when co‐infiltrating CmbHLH63‐nLUC and CmbHLH1L‐cLUC constructs (Figure 3D), confirming the interaction between the two proteins.

To assess the transcriptional activity of CmbHLH1L, we performed a yeast two‐hybrid galactosidase assay, revealing that CmbHLH1L lacks transcriptional activation activity (Figure S3D). Additionally, chrysanthemum protoplasts expressing 35S:CmbHLH1L exhibited lower LUC intensity compared to those expressing an empty vector (Figure S3E–G), indicating the presence of transcriptional repression activity in CmbHLH1L. To investigate the impact of the interaction between CmbHLH63 and CmbHLH1L on the transcriptional activity of CmNLP6/7L, we performed a LUC reporter transient expression assay in N. benthamiana leaves (Figure 3E, Figure S3H). The results revealed that the LUC intensity of leaves expressing 35S:CmbHLH1L was notably weaker than that of the negative control 35S:CmbHLH63, which lacks discernible transcriptional regulatory activity (Figure 3E,F). Moreover, luciferase expression was further diminished in the presence of both CmbHLH63 and CmbHLH1L (Figure 3E,F). Consistently, chrysanthemum protoplasts co‐expressing CmbHLH63 and CmbHLH1L vectors exhibited the lowest LUC intensity (Figure 3G). These findings suggest that CmbHLH1L acts as a transcriptional repressor, and CmbHLH63 enhances CmbHLH1L‐mediated transcriptional repression in plant cells.

To further investigate the role of CmbHLH1L in leaf senescence, we generated CmbHLH1L gene silencing and overexpression lines (Figure S3I,J). Compared to control plants transformed with empty vectors, leaf discs from CmbHLH1L silencing plants (CmbHLH1L‐pCVA) exhibited a delayed senescence phenotype (Figure 3H,I, Figure S3K), while CmbHLH1L overexpression plants (CmbHLH1L‐TRV) showed an accelerated senescence phenotype (Figure 3J,K, Figure S3L). ChIP‐qPCR assays using leaves from CmbHLH1L‐TRV plants demonstrated that CmbHLH1L binds to the promoter of CmNLP6/7L in vivo (Figure 3L). Additionally, the expression of CmNLP6/7L was elevated in CmbHLH1L‐pCVA plants (Figure S3M), while it was decreased in CmbHLH1L‐TRV plants (Figure S3N). To further verify the requirement of CmbHLH1L for CmbHLH63‐mediated leaf senescence, we silenced CmbHLH1L in CmbHLH63ox lines (Figure S3O). Compared to the CmbHLH63ox/EV‐pCVA control plants, the CmbHLH63ox/CmbHLH1L‐pCVA plants exhibited a delayed senescence phenotype (Figure 3M,N, Figure S3P). Moreover, the repression of CmNLP6/7L in CmbHLH63ox plants was released when CmbHLH1L was simultaneously silenced (Figure S3Q). These results collectively indicate that CmbHLH1L repression is required for CmbHLH63‐mediated leaf senescence.

CmNLP6/7L Interacts With CmbHLH63 to Form a CmNLP6/7L Self‐Activation Loop

In addition to CmbHLH1L, we identified a potential interaction between CmNLP6/7L and CmbHLH63 through yeast two‐hybrid screening. To confirm this interaction, we performed an in vitro pull‐down assay using recombinant proteins purified from Escherichia coli , which demonstrated the physical binding between CmbHLH63 and CmNLP6/7L (Figure 4A). Furthermore, we conducted Split‐LUC assays by fusing CmbHLH63 to the N‐terminal part of the LUC reporter gene and CmNLP6/7L to the C‐terminal part. A strong signal was observed in the leaves when CmbHLH63 was co‐expressed with CmNLP6/7L (Figure 4B). We also investigated the interaction between CmNLP6/7L and CmbHLH1L using the Split‐LUC assay, with CmbHLH63 and CmNLP6/7L serving as positive controls. LUC signals were observed when CmNLP6/7L was co‐infiltrated with CmbHLH63, while no signal was observed with CmbHLH1L (Figure S4A). These findings indicate the absence of an interaction between CmbHLH1L and CmNLP6/7L.

Previous studies have characterised NLP6 and NLP7 as transcription factors (Liu, Liu, et al. 2022). NLP7 has been reported to exhibit varying transcriptional activities, including active activation (Zhang et al. 2021), repression (Cheng, Durand, et al. 2023), or a lack of robust transcriptional activity (Wang et al. 2024), depending on the downstream genes or potentially on its regulatory partners (Marchive et al. 2013). Notably, no canonical NLP7 binding motifs were identified in the CmNLP6/7L promoter sequence, and a yeast one‐hybrid assay failed to retrieve binding enrichment of CmNLP6/7L to the promoter sequence (Figure S4B). To assess the molecular functions of the interactions between CmNLP6/7L and CmbHLH63, we conducted a transient co‐expression assay in N. benthamiana using Agrobacterium that expressed a strain with LUC driven by the CmNLP6/7L promoter, and a second strain for the expression of CmbHLH63 or CmNLP6/7L (Figure 4C). Compared to the control with the presence of CmbHLH63 alone, the LUC signal was similar with the presence of CmNLP6/7L alone, but significantly elevated with the presence of both CmNLP6/7L and CmbHLH63 (Figure 4C,D). These results were further confirmed by a LUC reporter transient expression assay in chrysanthemum mesophyll protoplasts (Figure 4E). Collectively, these data suggest that CmbHLH63 activates CmNLP6/7L expression through its interaction with CmNLP6/7L.

CmbHLH1L Competes With CmNLP6/7L for Interaction With CmbHLH63

Given that both CmbHLH1L and CmNLP6/7L were found to interact with CmbHLH63, we proposed the hypothesis that CmbHLH1L might compete with CmNLP6/7L for binding to CmbHLH63. To investigate this, we co‐infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves with CmbHLH63‐nLUC and CmNLP6/7L‐cLUC constructs, along with varying concentrations of 35S:CmbHLH1L or an empty vector (EV) control. As the concentration of CmbHLH1L increased, the luciferase activity, which serves as an indicator of the interaction between CmbHLH63 and CmNLP6/7L, significantly decreased (Figure 5A). To further validate these findings, we performed a pull‐down assay. The amount of GST‐NLP6/7L pulled down by HIS‐CmbHLH63 decreased when 1× CmbHLH1L was added (Figure 5B). Similarly, as the amount of GST‐CmbHLH1L increased, the amount of GST‐CmbHLH1L pulled down by HIS‐CmbHLH63 increased, while GST‐CmNLP6/7L pulled down decreased (Figure 5B). These results collectively indicate that CmbHLH1L and CmNLP6/7L compete for binding to CmbHLH63.

Moreover, as the concentration of CmbHLH1L increased, the LUC signal decreased (Figure 5C). Consistent results were obtained using the LUC reporter transient expression assay in chrysanthemum mesophyll protoplasts (Figure 5D).

Nuclear Retention of CmbHLH1L Contributes to Leaf Senescence

Considering that CmbHLH1L localises to both the nucleus and cytoplasm (Figure 3A) and competes with CmNLP6/7L to interact with CmbHLH63, we investigated whether the accumulation of CmbHLH1L in the nucleus is affected during leaf senescence. We transiently expressed CmbHLH1L‐GFP in mature and early senescence leaves of N. benthamiana. Compared to mature leaves, the CmbHLH1L‐GFP signal was significantly increased in the cytoplasm and decreased in the nucleus in early senescence leaves (Figure 6A). However, no obvious nucleocytoplasmic translocations were observed for CmbHLH63‐GFP and CmNLP6/7L‐GFP (Figure S5A,B). We then extracted total cellular and nuclear proteins from these leaves and examined CmbHLH1L‐GFP levels by immunoblots using an anti‐GFP antibody. The analysis revealed that the total CmbHLH1L‐GFP protein was similar between mature and early senescence leaves, while the nuclear fraction of the fusion protein was much lower in early senescence leaves than in mature leaves (Figure 6B). Similarly, we extracted total cellular and nuclear proteins from chrysanthemum leaves overexpressing CmbHLH1L‐HA. Immunoblots using anti‐HA antibody showed that compared to mature leaves, much lower levels of CmbHLH1L‐HA were detected in the nucleus of early senescence leaves, while the total protein levels were unaffected (Figure 6C). These data suggest that CmbHLH1L decreases in the nucleus during leaf senescence.

We analysed the protein sequence of CmbHLH1L and identified two putative nuclear localization signal (NLS) sequences (Figure 6D). To investigate the functional significance of nuclear enrichment of CmbHLH1L, we generated a protein with the deletion of both NLS sequences (CmbHLH1LΔNLS, Figure 6D). We then introduced CmbHLH1LΔNLS‐GFP into N. benthamiana and found that most of the expressed proteins were retained in the cytoplasm, with only a small amount translocated into the nucleus (Figure 6E). Additionally, we generated CmbHLH1LΔNLS overexpressing chrysanthemum plants (Figure S6A). Compared to the control plants with empty vectors, the transgenic plants carrying CmbHLH1LΔNLS exhibited a slight promotion of leaf senescence, and this phenotype was much weaker than that observed in CmbHLH1L‐TRV plants (Figure 6F,G, Figure S6B). Consistent with the phenotypes, we observed that the transcriptional repression of the downstream gene CmNLP6/7L was much weaker in CmbHLH1LΔNLS overexpressing plants compared to CmbHLH1L overexpressing plants (Figure 6H). These results indicate that nuclear enrichment of CmbHLH1L is necessary for CmbHLH63‐mediated transcriptional repression and subsequent leaf senescence.

Acetylation‐Mediated Nucleocytoplasmic Shuttling of CmbHLH1L Is Critical for Its Function in Controlling Leaf Senescence

Numerous reports have demonstrated the significant role of post‐translational modifications (PTMs) in regulating protein localization (Zhang et al. 2017; Yang, Sun, et al. 2023). In this study, we investigated the PTMs, including phosphorylation, acetylation and ubiquitination, which are known to influence protein molecular functions, on CmbHLH1L using mass spectrometry. Immunoprecipitated CmbHLH1L‐HA protein from extracts of mature and early senescence leaves of CmbHLH1L‐HA transgenic plants was subjected to PTM analysis. Our results revealed the presence of acetylation, but not phosphorylation or ubiquitination, on the CmbHLH1L‐HA proteins (Figure 7A). Specifically, we identified six lysine residues of CmbHLH1L that were acetylated, three of which exhibited altered acetylation levels during leaf senescence (Figure 7A,B, Figure S7A,B, Table S4). Among them, K78 was specifically detected in senescent leaves, while K81 exhibited decreased acetylation and K140 showed increased acetylation during leaf senescence (Figure 7A, Table S4).

To investigate the importance of lysine acetylation in the nuclear localization of CmbHLH1L, we substituted the acetylated lysine residues (K78, K81 and K140) individually with arginine to mimic deacetylation and transiently expressed these mutant proteins in N. benthamiana leaves. Our results demonstrated that substituting arginine at K140 resulted in reduced cytoplasmic accumulation of the protein, particularly in the early senescence (ES) leaves (Figure 7C). However, substitutions at K78 and K81 did not show any significant effect (Figure 7C). This suggests that the acetylation status at K140 acts as a critical switch for the nucleocytoplasmic shuttling of CmbHLH1L.

Furthermore, we generated transgenic chrysanthemum lines overexpressing CmbHLH1L mutants with lysine substitutions (Figure S7C). In comparison to the control plants transformed with CmbHLH1L‐TRV, the CmbHLH1LK140R‐TRV plants exhibited a more pronounced early senescence phenotype (Figure 7D,E, Figure S7D). Consistent with these phenotypes, we observed a stronger transcriptional repression of the downstream CmNLP6/7L in the CmbHLH1LK140R‐TRV plants compared to the CmbHLH1L‐TRV plants (Figure 7F). These findings strongly suggest that the acetylation status at position K140 is crucial for regulating nuclear accumulation of CmbHLH1L, which is essential for CmbHLH63‐mediated transcriptional repression and subsequent leaf senescence.

Taken together, these data suggest that acetylation modification may function to prevent nuclear accumulation of CmbHLH1L in chrysanthemum. This leads to a physical segregation from nuclear‐localised CmNLP6/7L and CmbHLH63, consequently alleviating its competition with CmNLP6/7L for binding to CmbHLH63. As a result, this acetylation‐mediated mechanism contributes to CmbHLH63‐induced leaf senescence by modulating the expression of the downstream gene CmNLP6/7L (Figure S8).

Discussion

In our study, we have elucidated the critical regulatory role of the CmbHLH1L‐CmbHLH63‐CmNLP6/7L module for leaf senescence in chrysanthemum. We observed that acetylation modification at lysine residues 140 hinders the nuclear localization of CmbHLH1L in chrysanthemum, releasing its competition with CmNLP6/7L for binding to CmbHLH63, leading to an enhanced downstream CmNLP6/7L expression, further influencing the progression of leaf senescence in chrysanthemum. In Arabidopsis, it has been observed that the cellular location of certain bHLH proteins can change in response to different conditions. For instance, the availability of iron can influence the cellular localization of bHLH121 within specific cell types of the plant tissue. Under conditions where iron is abundant, bHLH121 is primarily found in the nuclei of cells in the stele. However, when iron is limited, the signal of bHLH121 is mainly detected in the cortex and rhizodermis cells (Gao et al. 2020). Similarly, in the presence of sufficient iron, bHLH105 is found in all cell types of the root, with lower levels in the epidermis and cortex compared to the stele. On the other hand, only small amounts of bHLH47 are detected in the stele. However, under conditions of iron deficiency, both bHLH105 and bHLH47 increase in abundance across all cell types of the root (Long et al. 2010; Samira et al. 2018; Gao et al. 2019). At the molecular level, it has been reported that protein translocation can be regulated by PTMs. For instance, in rice, the deacetylase OsSRT1 reduces the acetylation of GAPDH and prevents its nuclear accumulation (Zhang et al. 2017). In another example, ethylene triggers the dephosphorylation and cleavage of EIN2, a protein located in the endoplasmic reticulum, leading to the nuclear translocation of a carboxyl‐terminal EIN2 fragment (Qiao et al. 2012). In our study, we discovered that the cellular location of CmbHLH1L changes during leaf senescence, and this change is associated with the acetylation status of CmbHLH1L. However, the regulation of acetylation, the specific acetyltransferases or deacetylases involved in this process, and how these enzymes are precisely controlled in response to leaf senescence are topics that will be investigated in future research.

NLP7 has emerged as a pivotal node in primary nitrate responses (Liu, Liu, et al. 2022). Previous studies have illustrated that nitrate accumulation triggers the nuclear localization of NLP7 (Marchive et al. 2013). CALCIUM‐DEPENDENT PROTEIN KINASES (CPKs) have been observed to translocate to the nucleus upon nitrate exposure, where they phosphorylate NLP7, thereby enhancing its nuclear retention (Liu et al. 2017). Furthermore, under cold conditions, NLP7 undergoes nuclear translocation subsequent to cytoplasmic phosphorylation by PM‐localised CPK28, contrasting the mechanism involving CPKs mentioned earlier, where NLP7 translocates to the nucleus prior to nitrate‐induced phosphorylation, facilitating its nuclear retention (Liu et al. 2017; Ding et al. 2022). In chrysanthemum, CmNLP6/7L expression increases upon the initiation of senescence, yet its subcellular localization remains largely unchanged at this stage, contributing to leaf senescence. Exploring the regulatory mechanisms and elucidating its coordination with CmbHLHs in governing the later stage of senescence is an intriguing prospect for future research.

The classification of bHLH family proteins is based on a common structural feature in their DNA‐binding domain. This domain consists of an alpha‐helix with a basic region at the beginning that interacts with DNA, followed by a loop and a second alpha‐helix. The two alpha‐helices together form a platform for the formation of bHLH dimers. The presence or absence of specific amino acids in the first 17 positions of the bHLH domain determines the binding ability of these proteins to DNA. Based on this criterion, it was predicted that 120 out of 147 Arabidopsis bHLH proteins can bind to specific DNA sequences, such as the G‐box (CACGTG) or E‐box (CANNTG), either by forming dimers with other bHLH proteins or by forming homodimers (Toledo‐Ortiz et al. 2003). We applied this principle to analyse CmbHLH63 and CmbHLH1L, and discovered that both of them have the potential to bind to the E‐box motif. This binding was further confirmed by experiments that involved testing their interactions with the promoter of CmNLP6/7L using the yeast one‐hybrid and LUC assays (Figures 2 and 4). In Arabidopsis, a similar group of bHLH proteins, including bHLH105, bHLH35 and bHLH104, can form either homo‐ or heterodimers. These proteins play a positive role in maintaining iron balance by directly activating the expression of downstream bHLH proteins (Gao et al. 2020; Heim et al. 2003; Zhang et al. 2015; Li et al. 2016; Liang et al. 2017). Interestingly, bHLH105 also acts as a transcriptional repressor when it interacts with bHLH47, another transcription factor that was initially characterised as having a negative role in response to iron deficiency (Long et al. 2010; Tissot et al. 2019). These findings demonstrate that bHLH proteins can form different types of dimers, which can influence their ability to bind DNA and regulate gene expression. Consistently, our investigation revealed that the transcription factor CmbHLH63 exhibits distinct regulatory effects on downstream genes depending on its interaction partners. Specifically, when CmbHLH63 interacts with CmbHLH1L, a transcriptional repressor, it acts to suppress the expression of target genes. Conversely, when CmbHLH63 interacts with CmNLP6/7L, it functions as an activator, promoting the expression of downstream genes. This antagonistic regulation mediated by both activators and repressors ensures precise control of CmbHLH63‐mediated leaf senescence, enabling plants to effectively adapt to dynamic environmental conditions.

Conclusions

Our study identified that the acetylation of CmbHLH1L at position K140 reduces its nuclear accumulation during leaf senescence, thereby releasing its competition with CmNLP6/7L protein and subsequently enhancing CmNLP6/7L expression. The increased levels of CmNLP6/7L contribute to delaying leaf senescence. Our findings provide novel insights into the intricate transcriptional and post‐translational regulatory events involved in leaf senescence.

Materials and Methods

Plant Growth Conditions

The chrysanthemum cultivars ‘Fendant’ were obtained from the Chrysanthemum Germplasm Resource Preserving Centre of Nanjing Agricultural University (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Plants were cultivated from seedlings of similar size at the six‐to eight‐leaf stages in a controlled culture chamber maintained at a temperature of 22°C with a photoperiod of 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness for subsequent sampling and processing. For the induced leaf senescence assay, detached leaf discs were placed on filter paper moistened with ddH2O and incubated in 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness conditions.

Cloning of CmbHLH63 , CmbHLH1L and CmNLP6/ 7L

All primers are listed in Table S5.

To construct the BD or AD vectors for yeast assays, we performed PCR amplification of the coding sequence of CmbHLH1L or other genes using Novozan High‐Fidelity DNA Polymerase. The primers CmbHLH1‐BD‐Eco‐F and CmbHLH1‐BD‐bam‐R, or CmbHLH1‐AD‐Eco‐F and CmbHLH1‐AD‐bam‐R were utilised for amplification. The cDNA obtained from chrysanthemum ‘Fendant’ was used as the template. The resulting PCR products were purified, and the pGBKT7 or pGADT7 vector was digested using EcoRI and BamHI endonucleases. Subsequently, the purified PCR products and digested vector fragments were ligated together. The ligated products were then transformed, and from the transformed cells, a single clone was selected, confirmed by sequencing, and stored at −80°C for subsequent experiments.

To construct the vectors for transient reporter assays, the coding sequence of interest was PCR amplified using the primers, such as CmbHLH1‐cLuc‐Kpn‐F and CmbHLH1‐cLuc‐Kpn‐R, with the cDNA from chrysanthemum ‘Fendant’ serving as the template. The amplified PCR products were purified using standard protocols. The cLUC, nLUC, or other vectors were digested using the appropriate restriction enzymes. The purified coding sequences were then ligated into the digested vectors to generate the desired constructs.

To construct the vectors for the protein expression assays in E. coli , the coding sequence of interest was PCR amplified using the primers, such as CmbHLH63‐pgex‐Eco‐F and CmbHLH63‐pgex‐xho‐R. The pGEX, pCold or other vectors were digested using the appropriate restriction enzymes. The purified coding sequences were then ligated into the digested vectors.

To construct vectors for stable or transient transformation of chrysanthemums, the coding sequence of interest was PCR amplified using specific primers such as pORE‐R4‐GFP‐CmbHLH63‐F and pORE‐R4‐GFP‐CmbHLH63‐R. Subsequently, the amplified PCR products were ligated into the digested vectors, such as pORE‐R4‐35AA, TRV2 or other applicable vectors. For gene silencing vectors, three fragments were individually PCR amplified using specific primers: miR319A and CmbHLH1‐PCVA‐IV, CmbHLH1‐PCVA‐II and CmbHLH1‐PCVA‐III, CmbHLH1‐PCVA‐I and miR319B. The miR319 plasmid was used as the template. Additionally, new fragments were generated by PCR amplification using the primers PCVA‐miR319A and PCVA‐miR319B, with the mixture of the three previously amplified fragments serving as the template. The pCVA vector was digested using KpnI and XbaI endonucleases, and the purified PCR product was ligated into the digested vector.

RNA‐Seq and Quantitative RT‐PCR Analysis

A rapid RNA isolation kit (Hua Yueyang, Beijing, China) was utilised for extracting total RNA. The resulting RNA samples were subjected to multiplexed library construction and subsequently sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The RNA‐seq clean reads were aligned to the chrysanthemum genome release (Song et al. 2023) using HISAT2 (Kim et al. 2015) with default parameters. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using Cufflinks version 2.2.1, following the previously described workflow with default parameters (Wang et al. 2020). Genes exhibiting a q‐value < 0.05, |log2(FC)| ≥ 1 and a reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (RPKM) value > 1 were considered as significantly differentially expressed genes. Gene ontology term enrichment was performed using the web‐based tools Chrysanthemum Genome Database (http://210.22.121.250:8880/asteraceae/homePage). The annotation of candidate genes was conducted by cross‐species analysis between chrysanthemum and Arabidopsis (Cheng, Zhang, et al. 2023).

For qRT‐PCR analysis, cDNA was synthesised through reverse transcription of 1 μg of the isolated RNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Beijing, China). The qRT‐PCR experiments were conducted using the same experimental systems and instruments as described previously (Wang et al. 2020). The primers used in this study are listed in Table S5. The internal reference gene Chrysanthemum CmEF1α (GenBank: KF305681) was utilised as an endogenous control. The relative transcript abundance of the target gene was calculated using the 2−ΔCT and 2−ΔΔCT methods (Livak and Schmittgen 2001).

Transcriptional Activity Analysis

The ORF of CmbHLH63 or other genes was cloned into a pGBKT7 vector using EcoR I and BamH I restriction digestion sites, employing the Seamless Cloning Kit (Sangon, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, the constructed plasmids pGBKT7‐CmbHLH63, pCL1 (positive control) and pGBKT7 (negative control) were transformed into Y2HGold cells (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA). pCL1 was cultured in SD/−Leu medium, while the others were cultured in SD/−Trp medium. After 3 days of incubation at 28°C, single colonies were picked and incubated on SD/‐His‐Ade medium with and without X‐α‐gal. If the protein is transcriptionally active, it should grow on SD/‐His‐Ade medium and exhibit a blue colour in the presence of X‐α‐gal.

To further confirm the transcriptional activity of CmbHLH63 or other proteins in plants, the full‐length coding sequence of CmbHLH63 or other associated genes was inserted into the pENTER1A vector. The pENTER1A‐CmNAC055 vector, generated using BamH I and Xho I digestion sites, was then cloned into the 35S::GAL4‐DB vector through the LR Clonase II reaction (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Additionally, 5× GAL4‐LUC (luciferase luminescent plasmid) was co‐transformed with 35S::GAL4DB‐CmbHLH63, AtARF5 (positive control) and 35S::GAL4DB (negative control) into chrysanthemum protoplasts. The LUC fluorescence value was measured using the GLOMAX chemiluminescence meter (GLOMAX, Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Subcellular Localization

The complete ORF sequence of CmbHLH63 or other genes, excluding the stop codon, was cloned into the pORE‐R4 vector (35S::GFP), incorporating BamH I and EcoR I cloning sites. The resulting construct, pORE‐R4‐CmbHLH63, was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. The Agrobacterium suspension in a buffer was then injected into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves using a syringe. Subsequently, the leaves were subjected to a dark incubation for 2 days followed by a light incubation for 1 or more day(s). Finally, the fluorescence signal was observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM800, Zeiss, Germany).

Chrysanthemum Transformation

For stable chrysanthemum transformation, we introduced the overexpression or repression plasmids into Agrobacterium strain EHA105; we then generated transgenic chrysanthemum as described by Li et al. (2015) and Chen et al. (2024).

Silencing of CmbHLH63 or other genes in chrysanthemums was achieved using Web MicroRNA to design overlapping primers (I/II/III/IV/A/B/) for amplifying amiRCmbHLH63. The precursor fragments required for constructing amiRCmbHLH63 were amplified using miR319 as a template. These fragments were then ligated into CaLCuV vectors using Kpn I and Xba I digestion sites, resulting in the generation of CaLCuV‐CmbHLH63. Subsequently, the vectors CaLCuV, PCVB and CaLCuV‐CmbHLH63 were separately transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Chrysanthemum seedlings at the six‐ to eight‐leaf stage were immersed in Agrobacterium solution under vacuum conditions, followed by incubation in darkness at 10°C for 3 days. The seedlings were then planted in a mixture of vermiculite and loam (3:1 ratio). After 20 days, new leaves were sampled for DNA identification using a rapid DNA extraction kit (Proteinssci, Shanghai, China).

To elevate CmbHLH63 or other genes' expression in chrysanthemums, the complete ORF sequence of CmbHLH63 or other genes was inserted into a pTRV2 vector engineered in the laboratory, utilising EcoR I and BamH I digestion sites. The process of transforming pTRV2‐CmbHLH63, pTRV2 and pTRV1 vectors into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 followed the same methodology as described above.

Yeast One‐Hybrid Assay

The ORF of CmbHLH63 or other genes was cloned into a pGADT7 vector using EcoR I and BamH I restriction digestion sites, employing the Seamless Cloning Kit (Sangon, Shanghai, China). Additionally, the promoter sequence of CmNLP6/7L was cloned into pHIS2 vectors, utilising EcoR I and Sac I digestion sites in the vector. All the constructed vectors were then transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain Y187 using lithium acetate. The transformed yeast cells were subsequently cultured on SD/−Trp‐His‐Leu medium. After 3 days of incubation at 28°C, single colonies were selected and incubated on SD/−Trp‐His‐Leu medium containing different concentrations of 3‐AT. The binding ability of the constructs was determined by comparing the growth of yeast colonies with the negative control pGADT7‐GUS (β‐glucuronidase).

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

The promoter sequence of CmNLP6/7L was cloned into pGreenII‐0800‐LUC vectors. The vector was prepared with Kpn I and BamH I digestion sites, and the fragments were ligated using Solution I (Takara). The resulting pGreen vector was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. For transient expression assays, a mixture of pORE‐R4‐CmbHLH63, other genes and pGreenII‐0800‐LUC, as well as pORE‐R4 and pGreenII‐0800‐LUC, was prepared and cultured in an activation solution. The optical density (OD600) was adjusted to 0.8, and the solution was protected from light for 3 h. The mixture was then introduced into the leaves of N. benthamiana using a syringe. The tobacco plants were cultured in the dark for 48 h and under light conditions for 24 h. Subsequently, 100 mM sodium fluorescein salt was sprayed onto the leaves, and LUC activity was detected and captured using a CCD camera (Tanon 5200, Shanghai, China).

For chrysanthemum protoplast transfection, the constructed plasmids pGreenII‐0800‐LUC, pORE‐R4‐CmbHLH63 or other genes, and pORE‐R4 (empty vector) were extracted and their concentrations were increased to 1000–2000 ng/μL. The pGreenII‐0800‐LUC plasmid was mixed with pORE‐R4‐CmbHLH63 and pORE‐R4 (empty carrier) plasmids separately, and the mixtures were co‐transformed into chrysanthemum protoplasts. The activities of Renilla and firefly luciferase were measured using a dual‐luciferase reporter assay system (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (ChIP‐qPCR) Assay

The transgenic chrysanthemum line expressing HA‐tagged TRV2‐CmbHLH63 or other genes was evaluated using a ChIP‐qPCR assay. Leaf material weighing 2 g from the same position of the plant was used for the ChIP assay. The plant tissue was cut into small squares and crosslinked by vacuum treatment with a final concentration of 1% (v/v) formaldehyde. The chromatin was then isolated from the crosslinked tissue. Next, anti‐HA protein antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added to the sonicated chromatin and incubated overnight to allow the antibodies to bind to the DNA fragments of interest. The bound DNA fragments were then pelleted, and the DNA was eluted from the antibody‐protein complex. Amplification of the eluted DNA was performed using primers specific to the target gene. The subsequent enrichment of the DNA fragments was detected using qRT‐PCR, with each pair of primers being used in three independent replicates.

Yeast Two‐Hybrid Assay

The associated genes were cloned into pGBKT7 or pGAD‐T plasmids. These constructs, together with the pGAD‐T (positive control) and pGAD‐T (negative control), or other combinations, are co‐transformed into Y2H yeast cells (Clontech), respectively. The transformed yeast cells were cultured on SD medium for 3 days at 28°C. Monoclonal colonies were then picked and transferred to SD/−Trp‐Leu and SD/−Leu‐Trp‐His‐Ade media. All experimental procedures were conducted following the manufacturer's instructions, using the Y2H yeast strain (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA).

Nuclear Localization Signals (NLS) Prediction

The NLS sequence of CmbHLH1L was predicted using the online tools of NovoPro (https://www.novopro.cn/tools/nls‐signal‐prediction.html), by inputting the full length of CmbHLH1L amino acid sequence with a threshold set at 0.5.

Total Chlorophyll Content Measurement

Leaves or leaf discs were harvested and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. For each frozen sample, 150 mg was extracted in a 10 mL 95% ethanol (v/v) solution (Li et al. 2024) and incubated at room temperature for 48 h. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min. The total chlorophyll content (mg/g) was determined by measuring the absorbance at 663 nm (A 663) and 645 nm (A 645). The calculation formula for the total chlorophyll content is as follows: Total chlorophyll content (mg/g) = (8.04 × A 663 + 20.29 × A 645) × V × N/(1000 × m), where V represents the total volume of the extraction solution in millilitres, N is the dilution factor, and m is the weight of the sample in grams.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel. Differences between samples were assessed for significance at the p < 0.05 level using t‐tests.
