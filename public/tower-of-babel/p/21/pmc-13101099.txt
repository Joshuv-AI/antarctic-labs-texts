Longitudinal serological response to SARS-CoV-2 among Rohingya refugees in cox’s bazar, Bangladesh: implications for pandemic preparedness in humanitarian settings
Authors: Nabid Anjum Tanvir, Abu Bakar Siddik, Sharmin Aktar Mukta, Mohammad Saiful Islam, Moon Moon Monisha Dey, Shariful Islam Khan, Zahirul Islam, Abu Toha M R H Bhuiyan, Md Rofiqur Rahman, Firdausi Qadri, Valentina Sanchez Picot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRohingya Myanmar nationals are at high risk of contracting severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) due to densely populated camp areas with inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure, along with limited awareness of hygiene practices. This study aimed to characterize the longitudinal SARS-CoV-2 antibody dynamics among Rohingya refugees in Cox’s Bazar to inform vaccination strategies and public health preparedness in humanitarian settings.MethodReverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 positive cases and their household members were enrolled and followed at multiple time points to monitor the IgG antibody response against the SARS-CoV-2 receptor binding domain (RBD) of the spike (S) protein.ResultA total of 194 primary SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR–positive cases were enrolled at baseline, of whom 47% were symptomatic, with the highest burden observed among individuals aged 18–55 years. At enrollment, 56% of primary cases had detectable IgG antibodies (≥ 500 ng/mL). Seropositivity increased to 80%, 88%, and 93% at days 30, 180, and 360, respectively. Among unvaccinated participants, RBD-specific IgG titers peaked at one month and remained detectable up to 12 months following natural infection. Among household members, seropositivity increased from 57% at enrollment to 71% at the one-month follow-up. Among vaccinated primary cases, antibody titers peaked at 1–2 months after vaccination and declined markedly within 4–5 months.ConclusionThis study demonstrates robust antibody responses following natural SARS-CoV-2 infection and a marked rise-but rapid decline-of vaccine-induced immunity among Rohingya refugees. The findings highlight the need for timely booster doses, strengthened vaccination coverage, and continued serological monitoring to maintain protective immunity in high-density humanitarian settings. These insights can support targeted public health interventions and pandemic preparedness strategies for displaced and vulnerable populations.Keywords: Rohingya Myanmar nationals, Cox’s Bazar refugee camps, SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence, Antibody response, COVID-19 in displaced populations, Longitudinal surveillance, Pandemic preparedness

Introduction

Infection with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) can cause coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) that can lead to severe respiratory viral infection [1, 2]. At the early stage of the pandemic, the disease is associated with a high case fatality rate of around 2% to 4% [3, 4]. COVID-19 has been a serious public health threat worldwide [5, 6]. This infection was detected for the first time in December 2019 in the province of Wuhan in China [7, 8]. More than 704 million confirmed COVID-19 cases were reported worldwide including nearly 7 million deaths, as of April, 2024 [9]. For decades, Rohingya people were forced to migrate to the neighboring country, Bangladesh, due to systematic persecution, statelessness, and violence in Myanmar’s Rakhine State. A major influx happened in 1978 and 1991–1992 [10, 11]. Finally, in August 2017, the largest number of people crossed the border to save their lives following mass genocide occurring from 2016 [12–14]. About 700,000 Rohingya fled into the coastal areas of Bangladesh [10, 15, 16]. According to the report of April 2025, 1,096,953 homeless refugees are staying in Cox’s Bazar for shelter [17]. These refugees are vulnerable to numerous communicable diseases because of overcrowded living conditions, inadequate hygiene, and limited access to healthcare facilities. Acute respiratory infection (ARI), acute watery diarrhea (AWD), fever and wound injury were the highest contributor to total morbidity [18]. The newly arrived refugee population were living in a condition where public health facilities, including adequate testing capacity, were lacking. As the refugee camps are densely populated, which makes it impossible to maintain social distancing to reduce the spread of communicable diseases and they suffer from poor health infrastructure, limited access to water and inadequate sanitation and hygiene facilities. Furthermore, limited health literacy among the camp residents, combined with poor access to protective supplies such as face masks and inadequate communication channels for health messaging, has made these settlements more vulnerable to the spread of SARS-CoV-2. After the influx, ARI was responsible for the greatest rates of mortality and morbidity among Rohingya Myanmar nationals. In under five years old age group, morbidity rate was as high as 32% and overall, 25% people suffered from ARIs while the death rates due to ARIs are 37% (children under 5 years of age) and 27% (for all age group) [19]. According to the health survey conducted by MSF in their two PHCCs after the influx, the most common reported illnesses were fever (66%), respiratory diseases (35%) and diarrhoea (15%) [20]. A recent study published that younger children are more vulnerable to ARI particularly predominantly by viruses like respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza viruses (IFVs) and bacteria like S. pnuemoniae [21]. With the pandemic of COVID-19, the scenario has become more complicated. The first COVID-19 confirmed case among Rohingya Myanmar nationals was detected on 14th May, 2020 from Ukhia Upazila [22, 23]. Data as of December 2024, 6,998 Rohingyas have tested positive for COVID-19 including 46 deaths [24].

People infected with SARS-CoV-2 can spread the virus within the communities through close contact with the households [25]. Serological research in this situation may be able to shed light on the actual number of SARS-CoV-2 infections, enabling reliable estimations of infection transmission rates and assisting in the formulation of public health policies such as community isolation and vaccination plans and public health initiatives. The immune response to respiratory viruses like SARS-CoV-2 involves immediate innate immune mechanisms followed by later adaptive immune mechanisms [26]. Pathogen-specific cellular immunity is difficult to measure in routine diagnostic settings, whereas B cell-mediated antibody responses can be more easily monitored through quantitative measurement of antibody levels using Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) [27, 28].

This study aims to enhance our understanding of the epidemiological patterns, immune response dynamics and disease burden of SARS-CoV-2 infection in refugee camps. It assessed the serological status of both primary cases and their households to determine the dynamics of antibody responses over a one-year follow-up period.

Methodology

This was a prospective longitudinal study that was carried out among the Rohingya Myanmar nationals, in the Ukhia Upazila, Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh from March 2021 to September 2022. The study followed consecutive enrollment of all laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR positive cases (as primary cases) and their household members, ensuring near-complete inclusion of eligible participants and improving representativeness of the camp population. Additionally, this study was also supplemented by an exploratory retrospective laboratory analysis of pre-pandemic nasopharyngeal swab (NPS) samples collected between July 2018 and March 2020 only to assess whether SARS-CoV-2 circulation may have occurred prior to the initial recognition of COVID-19 in Bangladesh.

Ethical clearance

We took the ethical clearance from the Bangladesh Medical Research Council (BMRC) (Registration number: 344 05 10 2020). We also obtained approval from the Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS) of Bangladesh and Refugee Relief and Repatriation Commissioner (RRRC) office, Cox’s Bazar. Informed written consent was obtained from all the study participants prior to enrollment according to the ‘Declaration of Helsinki’ regulation and guidelines. For adult participants, consent was obtained directly, while for minors, assent was taken and consent was provided by their parents or legal guardians.

Study sites and population

We enrolled participants as primary cases who were confirmed by SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR positivity and their household members from two Non-Governmental Organization (NGO)-assisted severe acute respiratory illness isolation and treatment centers (SARI ITC) in Cox’s Bazar district, Hope Field Hospital SARI ITC Camp 4 and FH/UNHCR SARI ITC Camp 5. Both the facilities were located in Rohingya refugee camps. Patients who received RT-PCR–positive results at different camp-based testing sites were transferred to these isolation centers for management, and study enrollment was conducted upon their admission.

To capture the full spectrum of SARS-CoV-2 infections, participants of Rohingya ethnicity, with or without COVID-19 symptoms, who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 by RT-PCR were eligible for study enrollment. Household members were defined as individuals living under the same roof and sharing meals with the confirmed primary case. They were enrolled only if they consented and had no prior RT-PCR confirmed SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis, irrespective of symptoms at the time of initial contact. Following enrollment, blood samples (5 mL) were collected from each participant for serological analysis, and their clinical and demographic information was recorded using a structured case report form. All collected samples, including nasopharyngeal swab (NPS) and blood specimens, were transported to the Institute for Developing Science and Health Initiatives (ideSHi) laboratory, maintaining temperature using ice cool box.

For the confirmed primary case group who were enrolled in the study, three follow-ups were conducted after one month (Day 30), six months (Day 180), and twelve months (Day 360) from the first enrollment day; however, only one follow-up was conducted after one month (on day 30) of enrollment for the household members. In every follow-up, blood and NPS specimens were obtained [Fig. 1]. To confirm the SARS-CoV-2 positivity, a real-time PCR-based diagnosis was used, and an in-house ELISA was used to measure the SARS-CoV-2 RBD specific IgG antibody titers upon natural infection as well as vaccination [Fig. 1].

Study overview (enrollment to follow-ups)

Moreover, as part of an exploratory retrospective laboratory analysis, we tested 250 previously stored NPS samples collected before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, from a previous study on the etiology of lower acute respiratory infections conducted among Rohingya participants of all age groups between July 2018 and March 2020 in Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh [21], which were stored in a −80 freezer.

Collection and processing of specimens

The blood (5 mL) was drawn into a red top tube after subjects tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 by quantitative reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR); this was designated as study day 0 (day of enrollment). Blood specimen tubes were centrifuged at 700x g for 15 min, serum was separated from whole blood and stored frozen (−80 °C) until the time of laboratory ELISA experiment conducted to determine the presence of SARS-CoV-2 specific immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies against RBD of SARS-CoV-2. To assess SARS-CoV-2 RBD- specific IgG antibody responses among the study participants with RT-PCR confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, serology was performed at various intervals, such as one, six, and twelve months for the primary cases and once (after one month) for the household contacts.

Receptor binding domain (RBD)-specific IgG antibody titer measurement using ELISA

This study used an in-house ELISA assay for detecting IgG antibody against the receptor binding domain (RBD) of the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2, to measure SARS-CoV-2 specific antibodies. IgG specific anti-RBD monoclonal antibodies were used to measure RBD-specific antibody concentrations (ng/mL).

In the Eon (Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA) ELISA Reader, optical density (OD) was measured at 450 and 570 nm. The OD values were then corrected by deducting the OD at 570 nm from the OD at 450 nm. Moreover, in order to establish the cut-offs for the antibodies and validate the assay, ELISA was carried out using serum specimens obtained from RT-PCR-positive COVID-19 cases and pre-pandemic healthy controls (frozen sera specimens obtained between 2018 and 2019 and kept in the freezer archive) obtained from the Rohingya Myanmar national population in a prior study prior to the pandemic. On the basis of this, the cut-off value for both antibodies were established at 500 ng/mL (0.5 µg/mL) and this assay was described previously [29].

Nucleic acid extraction and SARS-CoV-2 detection

All specimen preparations, pre-treatments, and PCR setup were performed in a Class II Biosafety cabinet in a Biosafety level 2 (BSL2) facility. NPS specimens were collected in viral transport medium (VTM). Viral RNA from the NPS samples was extracted using the QIAamp Viral RNA extraction kit following the manufacturer instructions. Following extraction, RT-PCR was performed to detect SARS-CoV-2 using the commercially available kit (Sansure Biotech Inc. Novel Coronavirus (2019-nCoV) Nucleic Acid Diagnostic Kit).

Statistical Analysis

No formal a priori sample-size calculation was performed; all available confirmed cases within the study period were included. All the categorical variables are reported as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), and continuous variables are reported as medians. Demographics and clinical characteristics of the study participants were compared between groups using the Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. These tests were selected based on data type; non-parametric tests were used when normality or homogeneity assumptions could not be assured. Seropositivity among the enrolled households was assessed in relation to symptoms, age group, sex, and RT-PCR results in Table 3 using multiple logistic regression, with household size included as a comparison factor. Results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the dynamics of SARS-CoV-2 RBD-specific IgG antibody responses following natural infection from Day 0 through 12 months, between participants with and without symptoms. Throughout the study, all analyses were limited to two-group comparisons; no tests involving more than two groups were performed. For other figures showing repeated measurements (e.g., at 1, 6, and 12 months), comparisons were restricted to paired two-group analyses using the same approach. Data processing, statistical analysis, and creation of figures were done with R statistical software (version 4.3.1). The missing data were handled using a complete-case approach—participants missing antibody measurements at a given follow-up were excluded from analyses involving that time point. No imputation was performed. For all tests, statistical significance was defined using a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants

A total of 194 confirmed SARS-CoV-2 positive primary cases and their 152 household members were enrolled in the study. Among the primary cases, 53% were male and 54% of the primary cases lived in households with at least five members.

Among the households enrolled, 59% were female. After one month of enrollment, we were able to do first follow-up for 113 primary cases and 98 household members. Additionally, the second follow-up was completed after six months for 106 primary cases and third follow-up after 12 months for 97 primary cases. We were not able to complete all follow-ups for all participants due to lockdown and relocation to Bhashanchar from Ukhia upazilla in Cox’s Bazar (Table 1).

Among the enrolled primary cases, the 18–55 age group contributed 69% of the total primary cases who were SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR positive. We have found low numbers of primary cases (5%) among children under the age of five during enrollment (Table 1).

During enrollment, 47% (93/194) of the primary cases were symptomatic. Cough (22%) and runny nose (21%) were the most common among them, followed by fever (15%), headache (15%), sore throat (12%), muscle ache (8%), and loss of smell (8%). During enrollment, only six participants (3%) of primary cases had an oxygen saturation level of ≤ 95% (Table 1). Among the household members, runny nose (7%) and cough (5%) were the most prevalent symptoms. During the study period, one death was reported among the enrolled primary cases.

SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR Positivity During Follow-ups

During follow-up, 14 (12%) primary cases tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 by RT-PCR after one month, and 3 (3%) after six months. No SARS-CoV-2 positive case by RT-PCR was reported during 12 months of follow-up. However, for the household members during enrollment, 17 (11%) were tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 by RT-PCR, and after 30 days of follow-up, we found 5 (5%) members positive where only one household member was positive for both timepoints. Retrospective laboratory testing of previously stored NPS samples did not detect any evidence of prior SARS-CoV-2 circulation in this community through RT-PCR.

SARS-CoV-2 RBD specific IgG Antibody Response upon natural infection and vaccination

For the primary cases, we conducted follow-up at multiple time points, including after one month, six months, and 12 months to observe IgG antibody response and re-infection by doing ELISA and qRT-pCR.

At enrollment (on Day 0), 56% (109/194) of the primary cases had detectable levels of SARS-CoV-2 RBD-specific IgG antibodies (≥ 500 ng/mL). Seropositivity increased progressively over the follow-up period, reaching 80% (90/113) after one month, 87% (92/106) after six months, and 93% (90/97) after twelve months (Table 2).

SARS-CoV-2 RBD-specific IgG seropositivity at enrollment and follow-up timepoints

Among the study population, 85 primary cases received one or two doses of Sinopharm vaccines at different timepoints over the study period. None of the household members received vaccinations during the enrolment or one-month follow-up. For serological comparison, IgG antibody titers were analyzed separately for two groups: (i) primary cases who experienced only natural SARS-CoV-2 infection but did not receive vaccination, and (ii) primary cases who were naturally infected and later received vaccination during follow-up.

Following a natural infection (without vaccination), RBD-specific IgG antibody titers among the primary cases peaked after one month (median 2 496 ng/mL), declined after six months (median 2 233 ng/mL), and remained detectable after twelve months (median 2 230 ng/mL). Overall, throughout the follow-up period, antibody titers among participants with natural SARS-CoV-2 infection only showed a gradual numerical decline from the first month to the twelfth month; however, this change was not statistically significant over time. Moreover, when participants were further categorized as symptomatic or asymptomatic, no statistically significant differences in antibody titers were observed between the two groups (Fig. 2A and B).

However, vaccination was initiated midway through the study period, and 85 primary cases received one or two doses of the Sinopharm vaccine at varying time points. Blood samples were collected during pre-planned follow-up visits. Post-vaccination samples were categorized based on the interval between vaccination and blood sample collection: within 1 week, 1–2 months, and 4–5 months. These groups were analyzed for post-vaccination antibody titers, where IgG antibody levels were comparably low (1,749 ng/mL) within 1 week. The median antibody titer among vaccine recipients peaked significantly at 1–2 months (28,900 ng/mL). However, after 4–5 months, the antibody titer (median: 2,349 ng/mL) sharply decreased (p < 0.001). Post-vaccination antibody titers did not differ between symptomatic and asymptomatic participants (Mann–Whitney U test: p = 0.73 at 6 months; p = 0.10 at 12 months). In both symptomatic and asymptomatic groups, antibody titers were significantly higher after one month and remained within the detectable range at twelve months (Fig. 3).

IgG Antibody response among the household members

Among the household members, 57% exhibited a measurable IgG response (≥ 500 ng/mL) on the day of enrollment (median 1856 ng/mL), and after a 30-day follow-up, this number increased to 71% where the median was 3408 ng/mL. Though we did not find any significant differences between the symptomatic group and asymptomatic group, after one month antibody titer increased significantly among asymptomatic groups (Fig. 4A and B).

Table 3 summarizes the serostatus of household members in relation to age, symptoms, sex, and RT-PCR results; primary cases were not included in this analysis. Among the 154 households included in the analysis, seropositive individuals were found in 53% (n = 24) of single-member households. The highest seropositivity rates were observed in households of family size 2 and 4, at 68% and 69% respectively, in contrast we observed no seropositivity in the households of size 5. Adults between 18 and 55 showed the highest seropositivity levels compared to both children under 5 years old and adults older than 55, who had lower rates. Seropositivity rates remained significantly elevated among symptomatic individuals throughout every household size compared to those without symptoms. RT-PCR positivity was generally low but still associated with higher seropositivity. Female individuals exhibited consistently greater seropositivity rates compared to males (Table 3). Children under 5 had higher odds of being seropositive (OR: 11.3), possibly indicating exposure clustering. People who previously tested positive with RT-PCR were more likely to have antibodies (OR: 2.59) (Table 3).

While some symptoms such as headache and muscle ache showed relatively higher median titers, no clear pattern of variation across symptom groups was observed. Discussion

The study describes the burden of SARS-CoV-2 transmission by measuring the serostatus of RT-PCR confirmed primary SARS-CoV-2 positive cases as well as their associated household members (described as residing under the same roof), who were living in refugee camps in Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh, prior to and during the time of COVID-19 pandemic. Our findings provide longitudinal evidence on serological status against SARS-CoV-2 among displaced populations living in resource-limited settings, offering crucial insights into epidemic preparedness in humanitarian crises. There was a risk of COVID-19 spreading in the overcrowded Rohingya refugee camps, where public health care facilities, including WASH, became critical because of the humanitarian crisis [30, 31]. There were challenges of monitoring SARS-CoV-2 transmission in refugee camps due to limited diagnostic access, mobility restrictions, and social stigma [32]. The camp environment-characterized by overcrowding, shared sanitation, and low literacy created unique vulnerabilities compared to the general Bangladeshi population, where physical distancing and timely access to healthcare were more feasible.

Of the primary cases that were enrolled, half (47%) were symptomatic at the time of enrollment, and the remaining cases were found to be SARS-CoV-2 positive but asymptomatic. The results of this investigation align with past studies that demonstrated a significant proportion of SARS-CoV-2 cases were asymptomatic, an important factor in the disease’s dissemination [33]. Along with this, a cross-sectional survey among the Rohingya population conducted prior to this study also reported a similar proportion of asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2–infected individuals in this community [34]. Among the enrolled primary cases, the most commonly reported symptoms included fever, headache, sore throat, cough, runny nose, and muscle ache. Similar symptoms were also documented in another study conducted among general COVID-19 patients [35]. However, the serology data showed that 56% of primary cases (at the time of enrollment) had a positive IgG antibody response, indicating prior infection. A similar serological survey in refugee settlements in Kenya revealed high seroprevalence rates despite limited case reporting, underlining the silent spread of the virus in humanitarian contexts [36]. The high proportion of asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infections in this study is particularly concerning in the context of overcrowded living conditions, where family members often share a single room, facilitating silent transmission by individuals unaware of their infection. In addition, 57% of the households enrolled had positive IgG tests at the time of enrollment, and 17% of the households tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 through RT-PCR, indicating that the virus was primarily spreading asymptomatically throughout the community. These findings highlight the potential for silent transmission of SARS-CoV-2 without exhibiting any symptoms within densely populated refugee settings.

Beyond these transmission patterns, most of the enrolled SARS-CoV-2–infected primary cases were between 18 and 55 years of age, reflecting that this age group possibly carried the highest burden of SARS-CoV-2 infection. A slightly higher proportion of males (53%) than females (47%) were observed among the enrolled participants, which may suggest that males were slightly more affected by SARS-CoV-2 infection in this population. However, a previous study among the Bangladeshi population reported that males were proportionately more likely to test positive for SARS-COV-2 compared to females, which is consistent with the pattern observed in our study within this humanitarian setting [37]. Additionally, during the second and third follow-ups of primary cases, we observed reinfection in a few cases, although some may have reflected persistent RNA detection from prior infection.

Analysis of the serostatus among household members showed that medium-sized households had the highest seropositivity rates. Symptomatic individuals and females had higher seropositivity, and prior RT-PCR positivity correlated with antibody presence. Odds of seropositivity were highest in children under 5 and lower in males, older adults, and those without symptoms. However, the higher odds observed among children under 5 should be interpreted with caution due to the small number of participants in this age group.

Relative to baseline, the proportion of seropositive participants increased at the one-month and six-month follow-up points, suggesting a progressive development of detectable antibody responses among participants. Antibody titers were higher after one month, reflecting the extent and dynamics of the antibody response following natural infection. Significantly, titers remained comparable to the peak levels observed after one month and were still detectable at six and twelve months of follow-up, indicating sustained antibody persistence following natural infection.

A total of 85 primary cases were immunized with one or two doses (Sinopharm vaccine) at various times throughout the study period provided by the Bangladeshi government. After 1–2 months of vaccination, the IgG antibody titers were significantly higher, whereas titers declined markedly after 4–5 months of vaccination. Several previous studies have reported a decline in antibody titers within 4–6 months after the vaccination, which is consistent with our study result [38, 39]. After around 4–5 months, antibody titers became similar between naturally infected and vaccinated participants, reflecting waning immunity among vaccine recipients. This decline highlights the importance of additional (booster) doses to sustain higher antibody levels over time.

This study has several limitations. Enrollment used consecutive sampling from two SARI isolation and treatment centers, which may not represent the entire Rohingya refugee population. Moreover, no priori sample-size calculation was performed; the cohort size depended on case availability during the study period. Some RT-PCR–positive results observed during follow-up may have reflected persistent RNA detection rather than true reinfection. In addition, an exploratory retrospective laboratory analysis of pre-pandemic respiratory samples did not identify molecular evidence of SARS-CoV-2 circulation prior to the first reported case in the camps; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective design and the possibility of undetected infections. We are also concerned about the potential confounders, such as disease severity and time since infection could not be fully adjusted due to data constraints. Considering participants follow-ups, we were not able to cover all the participants because of movement restrictions [40] and relocation (e.g., to Bhasan Char), and a complete-case analysis approach was applied without data imputation. Another limitation point is serologic testing, where it was limited to RBD-specific IgG measured by ELISA, not covered other antibody types or IgG subtypes, antibody neutralization assays, cellular immune response study, which limited our ability to assess the detailed level of protection conferred by natural infection or vaccination.

Despite the challenges, our study was able to examine the serological response over a one-year period for the majority of the enrolled participants. Additionally, the integration of sero-surveillance with molecular diagnostics in this setting can help identify emerging SARS-CoV-2 variants, detect ongoing transmission within the camps, and inform timely public health interventions such as vaccination or outbreak-control measures. Given the logistical challenges in refugee contexts, it is essential to implement flexible and community-adapted strategies to ensure equitable access to vaccines and preparedness for outbreaks. The longitudinal methodology used can serve as a template for future pandemic preparedness in crisis-affected settings. Future research should explore neutralizing antibody dynamics and T-cell immunity. Overall, the findings highlight the need for context-specific immunization and surveillance approaches tailored to displaced populations, and for integrating serological data into national pandemic preparedness frameworks.

Conclusion

This study helps to understand the antibody response and seropositivity against SARS-CoV-2, factors influencing seropositivity, and the antibody dynamics following natural infection and vaccination, which may help policymakers design context-specific interventions, vaccination strategies, and public health measures to effectively control and prevent the spread of SARS-CoV-2 virus within vulnerable populations. Natural infection was associated with sustained IgG responses detectable up to 12 months, whereas vaccine-induced antibody responses showed a rapid rise followed by a marked decline within several months. High seropositivity among household members highlights the potential role of silent transmission in overcrowded camp settings. These findings underscore the likelihood of under-detection of SARS-CoV-2 infections in high-density humanitarian settings, particularly in the presence of asymptomatic cases and limited diagnostic access. Overall, our study highlights the importance of integrating serological testing alongside molecular diagnostic methods to inform context-specific interventions, vaccination strategies, and pandemic preparedness efforts aimed at reducing SARS-CoV-2 related morbidity and mortality in such humanitarian crisis settings.
