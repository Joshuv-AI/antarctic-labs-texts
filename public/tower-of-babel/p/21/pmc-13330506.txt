Introduction

Global regulatory frameworks (Nielsen et al., 2022) are becoming increasingly stringent in response to societal demands for transparency in reporting on the quality of life of farmed animals reared for meat production (Alonso et al., 2020; Galli et al., 2021). Such frameworks require producers to meet specific welfare standards to ensure humane treatment of animals. Failure to comply with these regulations can result in legal penalties, restricted market access, and reputational damage, ultimately affecting the profitability and sustainability of pork production. Moreover, raising pigs to high welfare standards is associated with a reduced stress response (Lagoda et al., 2022) and improved immunity in animals, leading to greater disease resistance (Mart\’\inez-Miró et al., 2016; Steinerová et al., 2024) and fewer injuries (Harley et al., 2012). Hence, higher welfare standards contribute to an improved quality of life, and can positively impact other production goals such as improved meat quality (Boyle & O’Driscoll, 2011; Dawkins, 2019). In contrast, poor welfare standards are associated with increased veterinary costs, reduced production efficiency, and higher mortality, all of which translate into reduced net income and financial losses for producers (Delsart et al., 2020; Maes et al., 2020; Turner et al., 2024).

The ability to monitor animal welfare is therefore of crucial importance to the pig industry. Currently, most of the animal welfare assessments are performed on-farm by trained human evaluators, typically local veterinarians. However, this process is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and costly for the producer. Human-based scoring is prone to significant intra and inter-observer variability; for instance, visual scoring systems for pig locomotion and lameness have shown limited reliability and consistency among different evaluators (Forseth et al., 2023; Tuyttens et al., 2014). Such subjectivity leads to potential bias and inaccuracies in welfare scoring, which can be further exacerbated by the professional relationship between the evaluator and the producer (De Luca et al., 2021). The associated threat to biosecurity resulting from human assessors entering numerous farms is also a concern (De Luca et al., 2021).

On-farm animal welfare assessments are generally infrequent across many livestock-producing regions. In extensive production systems globally, detection and monitoring of welfare problems are particularly constrained by logistical and resource barriers, including limited internet connectivity in remote rural areas and the substantial financial cost of monitoring technologies (Temple & Manteca, 2020). In Europe, time constraints are a primary concern for welfare assessment systems that rely on animal-based measures, as current protocols require several hours of on-farm recordings, and consistency of results over time is particularly challenging when farms are certified based on infrequent recordings (Knierim & Winckler, 2009). In the United States, although public and consumer pressure has led to a range of private and public animal welfare standards, there remain significant challenges in extending welfare science to develop on-site standards that are both rigorous and practically applicable at scale (Rushen et al., 2011). Within Canada, due to the large geographical area and availability of resources, on-farm animal care assessments are infrequent, typically occurring once a year or once every two-three years (Canadian Pork Council, 2018). Consequently, the nature of on-farm welfare assessments does not permit regular monitoring of animal welfare for the certification of animal care standards, highlighting the need for alternative solutions.

Welfare indicators are measurable factors used to assess the well-being of animals, typically categorized into resource-based, management-based, and animal-based indicators (Butterworth, 2009). While resource-based and management-based indicators provide insights into farm infrastructure and operational strategies, animal-based indicators offer a direct evaluation of an animal’s welfare by reflecting its physiological and behavioral responses to its environment (Bøtner et al., 2012), and evidence of what treatment it has received. These indicators, which include factors that reflect animal behavior, physiological changes and animal management such as skin lesions, tail injuries, hernias, and body condition, are considered a more accurate measure of welfare outcomes (Teixeira et al., 2023). In this study, these four specific indicators were selected due to their high prevalence in commercial production and their significance as critical markers of cumulative on-farm management and transport conditions. Furthermore, they are particularly suitable for automated assessment because they manifest as distinct morphological and textural changes on the carcass surface, allowing for objective quantification through computer vision.

Consequently, these observable characteristics are particularly suitable for assessing pig welfare at the slaughterhouse, where carcass inspections can provide a retrospective evaluation into the treatment and health of animals in life (De Luca et al., 2021; Maisano et al., 2020; Van Staaveren et al., 2017). The evaluation of welfare indicators on pig carcasses can form the basis for routine data collection from large numbers of animals from all farms shipping to the abattoir. The advancements in computer vision offer powerful tools enabling the data collection to be feasible for large scale, commercial adoption, creating a reliable, accurate and objective method to collect high-quality welfare data. Such data can be used by the pig industry to better evaluate the effect of on-farm management, transport, and pre-slaughter handling practices on pig welfare and productivity (De Luca et al., 2021; Hernandez et al., 2023). Providing carcass assessment feedback to each part of the production chain can help to identify management practices which compromise welfare, supporting a culture of continuous improvements in pig welfare, and tracking the success of interventions by comparing progress to historical data. Such routine welfare data can also be used for benchmarking farms and abattoirs on a regional and national level, and in the future, may be required in product trade negotiations.

Computer vision systems can process large volumes of visual data rapidly and objectively, making them suitable for real-time monitoring tasks in dynamic environments such as abattoirs. Current computer vision models are capable of performing multiple key tasks such as animal classification, detection, tracking, pose estimation, and segmentation (Fernandes et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021). When different deep learning models are combined, these systems can identify individuals and extract subtle visual patterns, such as bruises, lesions, or deformities, with high precision and consistency (Bedin et al., 2024; Fernandes et al., 2020).

Despite the growing body of research, most computer vision applications in pig welfare have focused on live animal environments, such as barns or onboard transport trucks (Chen et al., 2020; Nasirahmadi et al., 2016). Quantitative analysis of the computer vision literature confirms a strong research emphasis on behaviour monitoring and individual identification in live animals, with fewer than 5% of studies reporting quantitative evidence of productivity or management impact, and welfare validation beyond visual inference remaining limited (Ufitikirezi et al., 2026). In contrast, the application of computer vision for carcass welfare evaluation remains underexplored. A systematic review of computer vision systems for meat safety assurance in abattoirs found that, while 62 articles were identified, CVSs developed specifically for pig and bovine carcass inspection were reported in only 5 and 4 articles respectively, compared to 53 articles for broiler chickens, and only three articles reported results from real-time evaluation in an abattoir setting (Sandberg et al., 2023). The few existing studies have targeted specific conditions: (Blömke et al., 2020a) developed a camera-based system to automatically detect ear and tail lesions as welfare indicators on pig carcasses at slaughter, achieving sensitivity and specificity values above 77% and 96%, respectively; (Bonicelli et al., 2021) applied deep learning to identify and score enzootic pneumonia-like lesions on pig carcasses under routine abattoir conditions; and (McKenna et al., 2020) used convolutional neural networks to classify liver and heart pathologies in porcine offal, reaching agreement with expert pathologists comparable to inter-expert agreement. Despite these promising results, post-mortem analysis remains an underutilised avenue in automated welfare research, even though it provides a unique opportunity to detect cumulative welfare issues (such as healed injuries or chronic conditions) that are often not identifiable through live animal inspection alone.

However, several methodological limitations persist across these applications. A primary constraint is the lack of generalization, as models trained on localized datasets often fail under different lighting or carcass appearance across facilities. Additionally, many algorithms depend on clean visual inputs, making them vulnerable to industrial artifacts like occlusions, debris, or motion blur. Finally, the high computational overhead of advanced deep learning models frequently restricts their use to offline analysis, leaving a gap for solutions capable of robust, real-time processing under demanding operational conditions.

In this paper, we propose a framework for assessing pig welfare through carcass analysis using computer vision techniques. The primary objective of this study is to advance continuous welfare monitoring by developing an integrated, real-time computational framework capable of systematically identifying and quantifying visually observable lesions, hernias, and body condition scores. We hypothesize that localized animal-based indicators on the carcass surface can be objectively extracted through optimized computer vision architectures, transitioning traditional qualitative audits into scalable, high-throughput metrics. To validate this approach, the study focuses on defining the biological and technical rationale for these selected indicators, designing a robust image processing pipeline, and demonstrating the scalability and operational feasibility of the system within commercial abattoir environments.

Materials and methods

This section describes the methodology behind the proposed computer vision system for automated pig welfare assessment in slaughterhouse environments. The system processes real-time video of pig carcasses to perform detection, segmentation, and classification tasks that extract animal-based welfare indicators. This global evaluation quantifies the system's ability to maintain real-time processing speeds while measuring the cumulative accuracy of welfare indicator extraction against a consensus of expert veterinary audits.

To detect hernias, a smaller dataset of 120 annotated images was created, evenly split between mild and severe cases. The severity threshold was based on (Hovmand-Hansen et al., 2021), who identified a significantly higher risk of wound development, sac trauma, and intra-abdominal complications in umbilical outpouchings exceeding 29 cm in circumference (approximately 9–10 cm in diameter) in finishing pigs. Cases below this threshold were classified as mild, and those exceeding it as severe. Additionally, 736 carcasses were annotated for skin lesions with three severity levels, and 350 tail images with lesions were labeled by severity for segmentation. A separate dataset of 155 images, each with a tail segmentation mask and length annotation, was also compiled for tail length estimation.

Animal-based welfare indicators

To assess animal welfare, four key indicators were targeted in this study: skin lesions, hernias, tail lesions, and tail length. These indicators were selected based on their relevance to animal health, handling practices, and housing and transport conditions prior to slaughter.

Skin lesions

Skin lesions are widely recognized as a key animal-based indicator of welfare, particularly in pigs, as they are often linked to aggression, overcrowding, or rough handling during transport and lairage (Lemos Teixeira et al., 2023).

Traditionally, lesion-based welfare assessments rely on counting the number of individual wounds observed on the carcass and assigning them a label based on size or depth (Carroll et al., 2016; Harley et al., 2012). However, in our preliminary evaluations, we found this approach to be prone to inaccuracies due to occlusions, overlapping lesions, and variability in lesion size and shape. Moreover, lesion count alone does not reliably capture the overall extent of skin damage, which represents a more meaningful metric in terms of animal welfare and clinical relevance.

To overcome these limitations, we used semantic segmentation to define severity of skin lesions as the ratio of lesioned skin area to total visible skin. A U-Net with a ResNet-18 backbone, pre-trained on ImageNet, segmented images into normal skin, lesioned skin, and background. The lesion ratio was calculated by excluding the background, and severity was classified as mild, moderate, or severe using two thresholds, with mild including no lesions. The statistical derivation and operational selection of these thresholds are detailed in Section 3.3 and formalized in Table 4. This method offers a more objective and consistent welfare assessment for automated inspection.

Hernias

Hernias are important welfare indicators in pig production, as they may be associated with pain, impaired mobility, and increased susceptibility to infections (Atkinson et al., 2017). At the abattoir level, visual inspection of the groin and umbilical regions can reveal the presence of hernias, providing retrospective insight into the animal’s welfare during the growing phase.

To automate hernia detection and severity classification, we trained an object detector using YOLOv4 with a ResNet-18 backbone pretrained on ImageNet. The model processes lateral view images to identify and categorize hernia severity into two classes—mild and severe—based on a deviation or bump size relative to the diameter of an orange, with mild cases being smaller or equal to ∼10 cm and severe cases larger than 10 cm. This enables fast and objective evaluation at processing facilities.

Tail lesions

Tail lesions are important indicators of welfare issues such as tail biting, inflammation, and necrosis (Smulders et al., 2008). Using the same U-Net with ResNet-18 as for skin lesions, tail images were segmented into background, healthy skin, and lesions.

An initial approach based solely on the lesion-to-tail area ratio proved insufficient to reliably classify lesion severity. To improve discrimination, we applied a colorimetric analysis to the segmented lesion regions using the value (V) channel of the HSV color space. Lesions were categorized by intensity into reddish (low-grade) and blackish (necrotic) areas.

Severity was determined through a two-step thresholding process (Fig. 3). Tails with a necrotic lesion ratio above a set threshold were classified as severe. Tail length estimation

Tail length is a valuable indicator in pig welfare studies, as shortened tails may be the result of tail docking or severe tail biting, both with implications for animal well-being. Tail biting is a common damaging behavior among pigs, often associated with environmental stressors, lack of enrichment, or high stocking density, and lead to pain, inflammation, infection, and is associated with increased disease and abscesses in carcasses (Henry et al., 2021). To mitigate these outcomes, tail docking is widely used in the pig industry as a preventive measure. However, this practice remains controversial due to animal welfare concerns and regulatory restrictions in certain regions (Wallgren et al., 2019).

We developed a robust method that begins with semantic segmentation of the tail region to isolate it from the background. Once the tail was segmented, we applied a skeletonization algorithm to extract its medial axis, preserving the central structure. To refine this representation, we used Principal Component Analysis (PCA), which served a dual purpose: capturing the primary orientation of the tail and identifying and removing outlier points introduced during segmentation. If key terminal points—such as the tail base or tip—are lost in this process, we reintroduce them to ensure continuity. This cleaned and completed skeleton is then used to fit a polynomial curve, enabling accurate tail length estimation.

Since pixel counts do not directly correspond to real-world measurements, we estimated a pixel-to-centimeter conversion factor using the carcass bounding box length, referencing average pig sizes (Condotta et al., 2018; Hetzer et al., 1950). Specifically, we utilized a reference weight of 110 kg, which represents a standard commercial industry market weight, to establish the expected physical dimensions of a carcass with extended limbs (Correa et al., 2006; POVOD et al., 2025). This method enables consistent tail length estimation regardless of carcass distance from the camera, ensuring practical applicability in abattoir conditions. System architecture

Our system processes slaughterhouse video streams in real-time, combining DeepStream and custom Python modules for control, and deep learning models for identifying anatomical structures and welfare conditions. Configuration files define parameters and thresholds, enabling modularity and scalability. The pipeline has two main stages: (1) dorsal and lateral view detection for tracking carcasses, and (2) welfare feature extraction to segment body parts and detect lesions or hernias.

Dorsal and lateral view detection

The pipeline starts with pig detection using a YOLOv4-based model, followed by tracking to maintain identity across frames. Previously, view detection used a cascade of three models (Ferri et al., 2024). We now replace the head orientation classifier by extending the head-tail detector to also label orientations (lateral, dorsal, ventral), reducing model count by one and inference time with minimal accuracy loss (from 0.988 to 0.975). A custom module selects one stable lateral and dorsal frame per pig (see Fig. 1').

Welfare visual feature extraction

Once dorsal and lateral views are selected for each pig, the system extracts visual features related to welfare indicators, including skin lesions, hernias, tail lesions, and tail length. Based on the carcass orientation, the appropriate deep learning models are loaded to analyze specific body regions as defined in Fig. 2.

Each indicator is processed using segmentation or detection models, and the resulting features are translated into quantitative metrics for classification. This modular approach allows for efficient, real-time extraction of multiple welfare indicators, supporting objective and scalable welfare assessment.

Model training and validation

All models were trained using the NVIDIA TAO Toolkit. YOLOv4 with a ResNet-18 backbone was used for object detection due to its compatibility with TensorRT and DeepStream. Semantic segmentation employed a U-Net with the same backbone. Dataset splits were: skin lesions and tail segmentation (70/15/15), hernia detection (80/10/10).

YOLOv4 models were trained for 80 epochs (batch size 8) using Adam (ε = 1e-7), L1 regularization (3e-5), and cosine annealing (lr: 10–7 to 10–4). Loss terms were equally weighted; augmentations included mosaic, jitter, and horizontal flip. Models were pruned with intersection-based equalization (0.1 threshold) and retrained without regularization.

U-Net models were trained on 320 × 320 images for 50 epochs (batch size 3) using Adam (ε = 1e-8), L2 regularization (2e-5), and cross-entropy loss. Augmentations included flip, crop-resize, and brightness changes. Pruning used union-based equalization (0.6 threshold) followed by retraining.

Pruning optimized operational efficiency with negligible trade-offs in accuracy. For YOLOv4, the 0.1 threshold caused negligible parameter reduction for the Head/Tail, Body part, and Hernia models. Only the Pig detection model sustained significant compression, halving its parameters from 53.43 M to 26.72 M (ratio: 0.499) with a minimal validation error variation of 0.013%. Conversely, the stricter 0.6 threshold for U-Net models achieved substantial compression while retraining preserved baseline performance: Skin lesion parameters were reduced by 56.1% (from 15.56 M to 6.84 M; ratio: 0.439) with a marginal Mean IoU shift from 0.7735 to 0.7712, and Tail segmentation parameters dropped to 11.60 M (ratio: 0.745), maintaining a stable Mean IoU (0.7083 to 0.7075).

Model selection was determined by the highest performance metrics achieved on the validation set during training. Crucially, to ensure unbiased evaluation and robust generalization, all quantitative results reported in this study were computed strictly using the independent testing set. The evaluation methodology followed a two-phase pipeline: first, models were evaluated individually using task-specific metrics, mean Average Precision (mAP) for object detection, and Intersection over Union (IoU) alongside the Dice coefficient for semantic segmentation, to select the optimal weights. Subsequently, these finalized models were verified within the integrated end-to-end framework to ensure operational stability and functional consistency under concurrent execution.

Implementation details

The system captures pig carcass frames post-scalding. Detailed descriptions of the environment are provided in Ferri et al. (2024). Models were trained with NVIDIA TAO on a Quadro P6000 GPU and deployed on a Jetson AGX Orin 32 GB using DeepStream SDK.

YOLOv4 was selected for detection (DeepStream compatibility). Tracking uses DeepStream’s NvDCF plugin. Tail segmentation runs independently via PyCUDA, as DeepStream lacks secondary detector support.

The pipeline decodes h.264 video via GSTreamer. A body detector (0.5 confidence, 0.2 IoU NMS) locates carcasses, expanding boxes by 10%. A second detector finds heads and tails (0.9 threshold), enlarging tail boxes by 10%. A head position classifier handles missing tails.

Evaluation of individual models

We evaluated the core models in our pipeline for object detection and segmentation tasks relevant to welfare assessment. A major improvement was replacing the previous head–tail classification setup with a single-stage object detector. By expanding class labels to include head–back, head–side, tail–back, and tail–side, we removed the need for a separate classifier, reducing computational load while maintaining a high mAP of 0.975.

The body part detection model provided reliable spatial references with an mAP of 0.954. The hernia detection model, which classifies mild and severe cases, achieved an mAP of 0.601. Additionally, segmentation tasks showed a mean Intersection-over-Union (IoU) of up to 0.77, confirming the system’s effectiveness for automated welfare assessment.

Skin lesion evaluation

Our initial objective was to count the number of individual skin lesions. However, due to the variability in lesion shapes and the difficulty in separating closely positioned or overlapping lesions, this task proved unreliable. Instead, we shifted to measuring the proportion of lesion-covered skin using a semantic segmentation model trained to distinguish lesions from healthy pig skin.

We compared UNet with a ResNet-18 backbone and Segformer, a transformer-based model. While Segformer achieved strong global metrics, it often failed to isolate the target carcass in multi-carcass images, leading to inaccurate lesion masks and reduced reliability. This issue may stem from Segformer's patch-based tokenization and the limited size of our annotated dataset. Transformer-based models like Segformer rely on patch embeddings, which can struggle to capture fine-grained spatial details, particularly in small datasets (Xie et al., 2021). In contrast, UNet showed more consistent and accurate results, effectively segmenting the target pig and its lesions.

UNet consistently outperformed Segformer in Intersection over Union (IoU) across all classes, with scores of 98.23% for background, 95.58% for pig, and 38.26% for lesions, compared to Segformer’s 92.04%, 89.05%, and 33.74%, respectively. UNet also produced more accurate segmentations, avoiding background confusion and false positives commonly seen in Segformer. Tail lesion evaluation

To evaluate tail skin lesions, we began with a semantic segmentation model to distinguish normal from wounded tissue. We tested classifiers including CNNs, Decision Trees, and Random Forests, but found their performance and interpretability lacking. Given the better visibility it offers, we focused exclusively on the dorsal view, as lateral images often obscure lesions.

We then adopted a colorimetric approach using the HSV (Hue, Saturation, Value) color space. This representation separates color from brightness, making lesion features more discernible. Among the HSV channels, the Value (V) channel showed the clearest distinction between severity levels: severe (necrotic) lesions had consistently lower V values, while mild (fresh) lesions had higher ones.

To quantify this separation, we analyzed the distribution of V values in dorsal images. A grid search was conducted by testing threshold values across the entire range of 0 to 255. The value of 114 was selected as the optimal cutoff because it minimized the classification error against the reference labels provided by human experts. Tail length evaluation

To estimate tail length, we extracted the tail region from segmented images and applied skeletonization to trace its medial axis. We calculated a reference pig length of 170.5 cm. This value was derived by adding the snout-to-tail body length and the extended limb length for 110 kg pigs, based on industry data (Condotta et al., 2018). We utilized the maximum dimensions provided in the reference, as the pigs in our dataset exhibited a slightly larger physical profile than the general average.

To improve endpoint localization, we applied two heuristics based on horizontal mask coverage: the tail base was identified as the first row with at least 40% coverage, and the tip as the last row with at least 10%. These thresholds helped minimize errors from partial segmentations or noise.

To smooth the skeleton path and mitigate outliers, we tested three filtering strategies—Linear Regression, Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC), and Principal Component Analysis (PCA). PCA was selected for its robust performance, computational efficiency, and ease of implementation. For curve fitting along the tail, we compared cubic splines and fourth-degree polynomial regression. Although splines provided smoother curves, the polynomial model was faster and yielded slightly more accurate length estimates, making it the preferred choice for the final evaluation.

Table 7 summarizes the mean squared error (MSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) for different tail length estimation methods. The combination of PCA-filtered skeleton points with polynomial regression significantly outperformed simpler approaches such as bounding box diagonal or raw skeleton length. System performance

To assess the runtime efficiency of the proposed system, we conducted a series of six independent tests using a two-and-a-half-minute evaluation video encoded in H.264 format. The video was processed via a DeepStream-based pipeline to emulate a real-time video stream, representative of an industrial deployment scenario.

For each run, we recorded the average frames per second (FPS) and computed both the mean and standard deviation of the FPS values. Subsequently, we averaged the results across all tests to obtain consolidated performance indicators.

The system achieved an average processing speed of 30.31 FPS, with a corresponding variability (standard deviation) of 7.01 FPS. While this represents a reduction from the baseline speed of 41.65 FPS, the system remains well within real-time operational thresholds, confirming its suitability for continuous video monitoring in slaughterhouse environments.

Discussion

Prior work in automated pig assessment has largely focused on single indicators in isolation, such as ear lesion detection via convolutional neural networks (D’Angelo et al., 2025) or tail-biting detection in farm settings (Hakansson & Jensen, 2023). While these studies demonstrate the viability of individual indicators, none integrates multiple welfare indicators into a single pipeline operating in real time under the high-speed conditions of a commercial slaughterhouse. In terms of detection performance, deep learning systems applied to pig carcasses have reported high accuracies in production-oriented traits, for instance, a YOLOv8-based system achieved 99% average accuracy for carcass length detection and a Mean IoU of 89.10 for backfat segmentation (Wei et al., 2024). However, such systems target productive phenotypes rather than welfare indicators, which present greater morphological variability and pose a more complex detection challenge, contextualizing the accuracies of 86.3–93.0% achieved in the present study.

We propose a lightweight, efficient framework that assesses key animal welfare indicators in pig carcasses by integrating multiple specialized models. Designed for real-time operation with low computational demands, it suits commercial slaughterhouse deployment. The selected indicators (skin lesion severity, tail lesion severity, tail length, and hernia severity) are highly relevant and prevalent in industrial settings, enabling focused, actionable welfare feedback (De Luca et al., 2021; Van Staaveren et al., 2017).

From an animal health and management perspective, aggregating these target indicators provides an objective, automated profile of herd welfare at commercial line speeds. Specifically, monitoring tail lesions and tail length allows producers to track the prevalence of tail damage, which is a key indicator of environmental stress, poor enrichment, or inadequate pen space during the rearing phase. Concurrently, skin lesion severity and hernia profiles offer insights into mixing aggression during transport and pre-existing clinical conditions. Replacing subjective, intermittent visual audits with this continuous data stream enables processing facilities to provide precise, retrospective feedback to suppliers. This allows for targeted adjustments in farm housing and transport logistics, ultimately improving animal health management and supply-chain welfare compliance.

Interpretability is central: lesion severity is quantified using ratio metrics for straightforward understanding. While a fully automatic hernia severity classifier was tested, it lacked interpretability and required more data, limiting its practical use. Manual thresholding based on expert input and data distributions remains crucial, though such thresholds can introduce errors and do not capture all lesion characteristics, and ratios alone are insufficient to explain all variability (Atkinson et al., 2017).

The system infers carcass orientation directly from detection outputs, simplifying the pipeline by removing the need for separate pose classification. It requires carcasses to pass and rotate individually for complete views. Adaptation to new environments demands fine-tuning models and possibly adjusting thresholds, as the system is sensitive to environmental changes such as lighting (Carroll et al., 2016).

Data scarcity, especially for rare cases like hernias, and variable carcass positioning can lead to missed detections (Atkinson et al., 2017). Despite these challenges, the framework is extensible to additional welfare indicators like frostbite, bruising, and ear lesions (Smulders et al., 2008). Recent image-based models successfully classify morphological characteristics such as scratches and hematomas in pigs, supporting this extension (Bedin et al., 2024).

A potential limitation of this framework is the constrained size of the hernia dataset (120 images), which may raise concerns regarding overly optimistic performance metrics. However, this small sample size directly reflects the naturally low prevalence of severe hernias in standard commercial production batches, making real-world carcass examples inherently difficult to compile in large volumes. To mitigate this data scarcity and prevent overfitting, the framework employs transfer learning with robust feature extractors pre-trained on ImageNet and applies extensive data augmentation during training to artificially expand sample diversity. While these models currently demonstrate a promising baseline performance, active data collection is underway across multiple processing facilities to expand this pathological subset, allowing for continuous retraining and ensuring long-term operational robustness in large-scale commercial abattoirs.

Furthermore, the restricted sample size of the hernia dataset dictated the decision to implement a binary classification schema (mild versus severe). Attempting to introduce a 'moderate' category would over-fragment the limited training data, increasing the risk of model overfitting and degraded generalization. Additionally, defining objective visual boundaries for intermediate hernia severities under high-speed commercial line conditions introduces substantial operational ambiguity. Consequently, this binary approach maximizes the practical relevance of the framework by ensuring high system sensitivity in robustly flagging severe pathologies that require immediate industrial or welfare interventions.

An operational consideration regarding the HSV-based segmentation approach is its inherent sensitivity to variations in ambient lighting conditions across different abattoir environments. Achieving consistent results is dependent on maintaining stable illumination within the camera’s field of view. Despite this dependency, empirical testing demonstrated that the HSV space consistently outperformed alternative color models (such as RGB), which suffered heavily from specular reflections on the wet carcass surfaces. Furthermore, we intentionally avoided using an end-to-end deep neural network for this specific task; while deep learning models are powerful, they operate as 'black boxes' that lack transparency. Utilizing a deterministic HSV-based approach ensures full method interpretability, providing traceable and auditable metrics that are essential for veterinary inspection compliance. To ensure cross-facility robustness, these HSV thresholds are implemented as configurable parameters that can be calibrated to match the specific lighting profile of the production line.

Furthermore, the threshold-based segmentation and length estimation modules introduce specific operational dependencies regarding carcass positioning and biological variability. The framework relies on the highly structured and automated nature of modern commercial production lines, where carcasses follow a consistent orientation. While variations in carcass rotation or swinging could present challenges for standardized visual capture, thus representing a constraint on immediate scalability in less standardized facilities, this dependency is effectively minimized in modern abattoirs through mechanical line guides. Additionally, fixed threshold cutoffs remain susceptible to biological factors such as varying skin pigmentation, residual dirt, or surface moisture gradients. While transitioning toward dynamic thresholding algorithms represents an avenue for future work, the current deterministic design successfully balances computational efficiency with the strict interpretability required for industrial deployment.

Additionally, while collecting manual centimeter-level ground truth for tail length was unfeasible due to the high-speed logistics and biosecurity protocols of the commercial line, this constraint is mitigated by industrial standardization. Since commercial pigs are processed at a consistent target weight of approximately 110 kg, their uniform anatomical scale allows the bounding box dimensions to serve as a reliable spatial proxy. The results demonstrate that these approximations are robust enough to capture distinct distributional patterns and physical variations in tail length, fulfilling the operational requirements for automated screening.

Furthermore, biological variations in tail posture represent a known source of geometric bias. Under normal conditions, pigs maintain a relatively extended tail posture. To minimize errors, length estimation is strictly restricted to lateral views where the full profile is visible. To mitigate constraints where the tail overlaps or folds, the framework extracts the skeletonized centerline from a pixel-level semantic segmentation mask. Crucially, the pixel-to-centimeter scaling factor is calibrated using the total length of the pig carcass rather than the tail itself, as the larger body dimensions provide a more stable, error-resistant spatial reference. This calculates the actual anatomical path along the curvature, decoupling the measurement from spatial orientation and minimizing posture-induced errors.

Regarding semantic segmentation, the framework achieved a Mean IoU of 0.77 for skin lesions and 0.71 for tail lesions. While these values reflect the difficulty of precise pixel-level segmentation in biological tissues, they represent a viable baseline for automated monitoring. Qualitative inspection indicates that the models consistently identify the core clinical regions of concern, including active lesions, recent wounds, and necrotic areas. The variance in IoU is primarily localized at the transition zones where healthy skin gradually shifts into damaged tissue. These boundary gradients exhibit high visual variance, introducing natural ambiguity during manual annotation. Consequently, while boundary delineation remains an ongoing challenge, the current segmentation performance effectively captures the primary pathological zones necessary for practical industrial evaluation.

Future work will focus on expanding detection capabilities for a more comprehensive welfare assessment. While alternative model architectures might improve accuracy (Fernandes et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021), developing a single end-to-end model is highly impractical due to the extreme diversity of tasks, which range from bounding-box detection to semantic segmentation and skeletonization. Instead, this work focused on building a modular, multi-task framework where optimization efforts were directed at deployment efficiency. By implementing structural pruning, FP16 quantization, and NVIDIA TensorRT acceleration, the framework ensures the high throughput required for running these diverse tasks concurrently on high-speed industrial lines. This approach provides a practical, scalable foundation that balances reliable performance with operational feasibility.

A key challenge remains balancing model complexity and interpretability with practical deployment constraints. More complex architectures or end-to-end learning approaches could yield higher accuracy but often sacrifice explainability, making it harder for stakeholders to trust or understand decisions (Blömke et al., 2020b; McKenna et al., 2020). Our framework prioritizes interpretability and modularity, which facilitates incremental improvements and easier validation by domain experts. Moreover, the reliance on human-defined thresholds, while imperfect, offers control and adaptability across different slaughterhouse environments (Hernandez et al., 2023; Lemos Teixeira et al., 2023). Future research may explore hybrid approaches that combine interpretable metrics with data-driven methods to optimize both performance and usability.

Conclusions

This study presents a real-time computer vision system for assessing key pig welfare indicators—skin lesions, hernias, tail lesions, and tail length—in slaughterhouse settings. The system combines deep learning, classical image processing, and tailored algorithms to extract relevant welfare features. Hernia detection achieved high reliability, with high precision within the evaluated dataset and strong recall (F1-score 0.93), effectively distinguishing mild and severe cases. Skin lesion classification showed robust performance across lateral and dorsal views, with weighted F1-scores of 0.85 and 0.78 respectively, reflecting balanced accuracy and precision despite lesion variability. Tail lesion severity classification achieved a weighted F1-score of 0.77, demonstrating good discrimination using colorimetric features. Tail length estimation, based on PCA-filtered skeleton points and polynomial fitting, provided accurate and efficient measurements despite pose variations. Tail length was evaluated in pixel units, yielding a mean squared error of 19.82 px2. Overall, the integrated system offers promising, scalable welfare monitoring in commercial slaughterhouses. Crucially, because the system's performance is intrinsically linked to specific environmental factors such as lighting and camera positioning, further calibration and validation under diverse operational conditions and across different commercial facilities are required to ensure robustness and model generalizability. Future work will focus on expanding datasets, improving segmentation in complex scenes, and incorporating additional welfare indicators to enhance robustness and support practical deployment across diverse abattoirs, where new data will be acquired to further improve model generalizability.
