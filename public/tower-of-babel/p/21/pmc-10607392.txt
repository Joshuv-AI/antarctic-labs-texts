Interventional radiology training in the UK: a view from within—a national survey
Authors: Usman Mahay, Paul Jenkins, Linda Watkins, Indrajeet Mandal, Omotolani Lewis, Katrina Elizabeth Harborne, Shian Patel, John Reicher, Wing Yan Liu, Jim Zhong, Mohamad Hamady
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Interventional radiology (IR) training in the UK has evolved since recognition as a subspecialty in 2010 and introduction of a new curriculum in 2021. The changing landscape, increasing workload and COVID-19 have affected training. The purpose of this study was to review trainees’ perspectives on training and develop strategies to further improve training.Methods:Online survey approved by the British Society of Interventional Radiology Council distributed to British Society of Interventional Radiology Trainee members between 9 March 22 and 25 March 2022. The survey was open to all UK based ST4-6 IR trainees and fellows. Descriptive and thematic analysis was undertaken.Results:43 responses were received from 17/19 UK training regions. Females represented 10% (4/41) and 5% (2/43) less than full time (LTFT) trainees. 82% (31/38) felt their curriculum was suitable for their training and 28/38 (74%) were satisfied with IR training. Vascular IR, Interventional Oncology, paediatrics and stroke thrombectomy were identified as areas of training desiring improvement. 45% (18/40) stated exposure to IR led clinics and 17.5% (7/40) to IR led ward rounds. Only 6/38 (15.7%) received structured IR teaching at least once a month. Approximately, a third of respondents (13/38) stated training opportunities were significantly compromised secondary to COVID-19.Conclusion:This survey shows overall good satisfaction with IR training. However, improved training opportunities in vascular IR, interventional oncology, paediatric IR and stroke thrombectomy are required. In addition, access to clinics, ward rounds and protected time for research is needed to improve training quality.Advances in knowledge:New national UK IR training survey.

Introduction

Interventional radiology (IR) has developed and expanded since its inception in the mid-20th century. 1 With an increasing demand for minimally invasive therapies across all organ systems provided by IR, a well-trained IR workforce is needed to deliver a comprehensive IR service and ensure excellent patient care.

The most recent UK workforce census run by the Royal College of Radiologists (RCR) in September 2021, 2 showed significant staffing gaps in IR, with a 28% shortfall at consultant level, and recommended increasing the number of IR trainees. Training of IRs in the UK is under the auspices of the RCR with IR trainees expected to meet the requirements set out in a General Medical Council (GMC) approved diagnostic and subspecialty IR curriculum. 3 Several iterations of the IR curriculum have been developed since 2010 and lead to an award of a subspecialty certificate of completion of training (CCT) in IR and entry on to the GMC’s specialist register. The latest version of the IR curriculum, initially planned for roll out in 2020, was post-poned given the COVID-19 pandemic and therefore, implemented in 2021. 4

One major change from the previous iteration of the curriculum from 2016 to 2021, was the emphasis of capabilities in practice (CIPs) related to more patient facing roles and increased involvement in the patient care pathway. These clinical practice changes align with the Provision of IR Services document set out by the RCR. 5 Further changes included an increased proportion of training dedicated to IR subspecialty training but also the addition of further core diagnostic skills within the clinical radiology curriculum meaning a broader focus on modern generic radiology skills that are required to be met by all diagnostic and IR trainees.

Implementing and delivering a new curriculum brings about many challenges for both educational providers and trainees alike. Although the GMC run an annual national training survey, 6 this focuses on fundamental aspects of post-graduate medical training with relatively little ability to discern the experiences of the IR subspeciality trainee from their diagnostic colleagues. There is an unmet need to establish the views of IR trainees, especially due to the newly introduced overlapping competencies with other speciality curricula. Similarly, the COVID-19 response has altered the experience of trainees over the past 2 years with periods of decreased or absent elective work. 7

We aim to establish an overview of UK-based IR trainees’ experiences of training, perspectives towards the new curriculum and develop strategies to further improve training.

Methods and materials

An electronic survey was compiled using Survey Monkey® platform, approved by the British Society of Interventional Radiology (BSIR) Council and distributed to directly to BSIR trainee members, IR training leads, Training Programme Directors (TPDs) and Heads of Post-graduate Schools of Radiology. This study was open between 9 March 2022 and 25 March 2022. All UK-based IR specialty trainees (ST4-6) and fellows were invited to take part. The survey was designed by BSIRT committee members based on the IR curriculum and procedural logbooks. The survey comprised of 59 questions of which 4 allowed open free-text comments. The quantitative data were analysed in Microsoft Excel 365 and a thematic analysis undertaken of the four free-text questions. Ethical approval was not required for this survey due to the absence of identifiable data and patient information.

Results

A total of 43 responses were received from 17 out of 19 UK training regions. This represents a 30.2% response rate based on the RCR records of 142 registered trainees In September 2021. There was an ethnically diverse group of participants but only 10% (4/41) were females and 5% (2/43) were less than full time (LTFT) trainees. An even distribution of responses was received across ST4 (n = 12), ST5 (n = 14) and ST6 (n = 15), of which 18/43, (42%) had undergone a competitive application process for IR higher training. The majority of trainees were based in tertiary university teaching hospitals with an average of 2.5 (range 1–8) IR trainees in each trust. The majority 28/43 (65%), stated they were on the new 2021 RCR curriculum with median procedural IR sessions of 6 (range 1–9) on a weekly basis with half a day counting as one session. Out of the 15 ST6 trainees, 13 (87%) were on a dedicated IR on-call rota, one was on a mixed rota and the other on a diagnostic rota (Table 1).

Overall, 31/38 (82%) respondents agreed the curriculum they follow is suitable for their training and 28/38 (74%) respondents were satisfied with their IR training.

The thematic analysis results are presented as themes with example quotations from the free text responses. The first free-text question followed on from the question “the curriculum I follow is suitable for my overall training requirements” and asked for further information from those who disagreed. Two themes were identified in this, smaller cohort (7/38) (18.4% of respondents), namely those who felt their curriculum was unrealistic and those who thought the curriculum has a misplaced focus.

Unrealistic:

Question: How can the curriculum be improved?

Of the 38 respondents, 20 either did not offer suggestions for improvement or stated that they were satisfied with their curriculum and a further two expressed appreciations at the new curriculum recognising the breadth of IR and the inability to become competent in all areas. Notably, two specified issues with the transition period between curricula.

Three comments related to including stroke thrombectomy/basic interventional neuroradiology (INR) skills in the IR curriculum and two indicated that the curriculum should be more explicit regarding participation in IR on-call. The overwhelming theme in these responses was a need for guidance regarding the split of diagnostic and interventional training, especially relating this to the now broader diagnostic core competencies.

Need for guidance on DR:IR split:

“Higher IR trainees should have less requirement to keep up non-relevant diagnostic skills (e.g., gynae/MSK MRI will never be part of the workload of a modern 3 + 3 trained IR).”

“Clear distinction as to what an IR is expected to be able to report”.

“more IR centered training with relevant diagnostic imaging to intervention” (sic).

“Stratify and categorise expectation of trainees to achieve competencies not in their subspecialty”.

“Flexibility in diagnostic competency component. I dont see the need to maintain competency in knee MRI” (sic)

Interventional oncology was the area of IR that clearly posed a high concern to trainees in terms of lack of exposure. Seven trainees mentioned this as a specific area of concern for their training. Two were concerned about non-vascular intervention training and a further two about EVAR opportunities. In both cases of the latter, this was due to this being performed by vascular surgeons at their sites. One mentioned a lack of paediatric IR training. There was no overlap between these groups indicating that schemes on the whole are delivering training in a wide breadth of IR procedures.

Discussion

Overall, there is good satisfaction with IR training in the UK with views represented from almost 90% of radiology training schemes. A major factor in this finding is the high-quality trainers which was a core theme revealed by the open text questions. Less than full-time trainees and females were poorly represented in the survey, however, this likely reflects the reality of the IR trainee cohort and workforce. 2 The challenge of reduced female representation within IR is well recognised. It is likely multifactorial and requires dedicated attention to improve.

Vascular IR

One of the main areas of concern was vascular training which reflects a national picture where many vascular procedures are regularly being performed by non-IR consultants. 8 This represents a challenge nationally at a consultant and service provision level. However, from a training perspective, this highlights a particular difficulty with the implementation of the 2021 IR curriculum with the variability of what comprises IR in various geographic areas being widely diverse. The ability to become competent in each defined skill is variable according to geography and it is essential for training that IR departments retain ownership of vascular work.

Paediatric IR

Paediatric IR is another area, in which experience is widely variable with only a small number of trainees undertaking any higher specialist training. However, there is an appetite for paediatric IR skill development which reflects a national desire to develop a stronger network of spokes to the central hubs. Such a network may allow many simple procedures to be performed without requiring paediatric patients and their families to travel excessively. More formal training pathways, along with flexibility to gain out of programme training is required. 9

Interventional oncology

Interventional oncology (IO) is an expanding field, and is increasingly being recognised as a key pillar of oncological therapy. 10 There is a strong interest amongst trainees to acquire the necessary skills as highlight in this survey. However, the opportunities for training development appear to be limited, often with procedures limited to specialist centres or with long waiting lists and only a few cases in a typical week. The further expansion of IO relies on expansion of consultant numbers and buy in from other specialties, in particular anaesthetic support, which in turn will improve training in this field. Increased investment in imaging resources such as CT and MRI scanners is also required to facilitate more procedures such as tumour ablation which are commonly done using cross-sectional imaging therefore holding up scanner room time when the NHS is already experiencing stretched scanner capacity. Flexibility between regions, to allow trainees to access out of area opportunities would help increase training in IO.

Stroke

Increasing the availability of mechanical thrombectomy for stroke is a major national directive, which is fundamentally limited by the availability of well-trained operators. This survey shows that the current general IR trainees are not offered satisfactory exposure in the domain of thrombectomy.

Drains and biopsies

With regards to ultrasound-guided drains and biopsies, a significant number of respondents, 9/40 (22.5%), stated these procedures were excessive for training needs for IR trainees at ST4–ST6 levels. Clearer guidance in the future curriculum regarding skills development for ST IR in the first 3 years would likely be more appropriate. This should free more time for trainees at higher levels to focus on development of advanced techniques.

Core clinical radiology

The new core clinical radiology curriculum requires that all trainees are to be competent to day one consultant level in a wide variety of procedures at CCT. Unfortunately, a handful of free-text comments indicated that some training schemes have interpreted this to suggest that IR trainees should re-do aspects of core diagnostic training to achieve these competencies. This has a wider impact on the training that can be fitted into a busy IR training programme. The requirements for CCT in IR must be reflective of the consultant job in IR. The push towards imaged-guided surgery, 11 and a more clinical patient facing role highlight the need for more clinical time invested in ward rounds and clinics. The authors would encourage programmes to allow maintaining specific specialist diagnostic interests but to renew the focus on being a clinical IR.

More is required of IR trainees than ever before, and the health and well-being of the trainee should be factored into this. Similarly, the transfer to the new curriculum with increased diagnostic demands disproportionately affects those with longer training pathways due to leave, out of programme activity and working less than full time. This highlights a key area where curriculum implementation can be improved in the future.

IR on-call

The provision of IR trainee on-call rota is fundamental to developing a well-trained IR consultant workforce. Only 18/41 (43.9%) of eligible respondents across all grades were taking part in an IR on-call rota with two respondents at ST6 level not even on a dedicated IR on-call rota. This raises two concerns, firstly the lack of training in management of the acutely unwell patient and the associated human factors related to out of hours patient management. Secondly, the imbalance of diagnostic work with IR trainees contributes to a frequent diagnostic rota including weekends and nights and the compensatory time off. The importance of IR trainee on call for the future on call consultant workforce cannot be underestimated. If there is a need for further diagnostic service provision, then this should be supplied by increasing diagnostic trainee numbers, which would be supported by the RCR. IR trainees are expected to be competent in a wide variety of interventional and diagnostic imaging and therefore trainee well-being should be considered in light of these increasing expectations.

Research

Research has recently highlighted a problem with training in the UK and this survey reaffirms that lack of time is a major consideration. This has been highlighted in a recent paper by the UNITE collaborative. 12

COVID-19

COVID-19 has impacted all radiology training across the globe. During the initial waves of the global pandemic, elective training opportunities were placed on hold. Nevertheless, IR services adapted and continued to provide urgent and on-call services with departments utilising day case units to continue providing a comprehensive IR service. This opened up alternative opportunities to learn transferable skills and therefore reducing the adverse impact on training. Furthermore, the increased delivery of online-based learning and simulation paved the way for alternative training methods to further reduce the adverse impact from the global pandemic on IR training. A third of participants (34.2% (13/38) felt training had been compromised by COVID-19, which is significantly below many other specialities. 13–15 The formation of a Short Life Working Group set up by the RCR, 16 to evaluate modern methods of training along with traditional face-to-face learning is welcomed. 17

Limitations

The main limitation of this survey is that it only represents around a third of the IR trainees. The huge variability in IR training and the IR consultant job descriptions between hospitals and regions limits the applicability of this information and many nuances are unfortunately lost. Although not covered in the questionnaire of this survey, a speciality advisory committee (similar to several other specialities) can likely lessen regional discrepancies and inequality in training opportunities by providing detailed guidance on curriculum implementation, facilitating regional cooperation and highlighting areas of future curriculum refinement. The other limitation is the snap shot nature of the survey which could probably reflect a time related issue. The BSIRT intends to undertake annual surveys which will identify trends developing problems and allow more robust training recommendations.

Recommendations

As highlighted above, IR training in the UK is facing several challenges and shortcomings. Most of the obstacles are peculiar to IR trainees, e.g. clinical training, on-call commitments expected for a day one IR consultant, high level competency in a range of emergency procedures as well as competing on training opportunities with other specialities in contrast to the core requirements expected from trainees who are more inclined to diagnostic work on a clinical radiology curriculum.

Governing bodies such as the RCR and GMC along with a learned special interest group, BSIR, should work together to oversee the implementation of the IR curriculum to identify and resolve conflicting elements with newly developed curricula by other specialities. The ST1 IR is a great initiative and should be enriched with a dedicated curriculum path; engagement with TPDs from all training schemes is essential. We will strive to repeat this survey and make it an annual exercise.

Conclusion

In general, this trainees survey shows good level of satisfaction with UK-based IR training schemes. However, LTFT and female trainees remain poorly represented. Increased opportunities in vascular IR, IO, paediatric IR, and mechanical thrombectomy for stroke are required. In addition, access to clinics and ward rounds and time for research are essential to improve training quality.
