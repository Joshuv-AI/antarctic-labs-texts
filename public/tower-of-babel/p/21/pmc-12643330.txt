Aluminum Exposure Impairs Electrochemically Measured Vesicular Storage and Exocytosis via Iron Homeostasis Dysregulation
Authors: Shi‐Hua Chen, Chaoyi Gu, Xing‐Jiu Huang, Andrew G Ewing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWidespread human exposure to aluminum (Al) has raised increasing concerns about its effects on neuronal functions since Al3+ can cross the blood–brain barrier and accumulate in neuronal cells, especially considering its ionic similarity to ferric iron (Fe3+), a metal strongly associated with neurodegenerative diseases. In this study, we demonstrate that Al3+ affects vesicles in an iron‐status‐dependent manner in pheochromocytoma (PC12) cells, causing distinct changes under iron‐normal, deficient, and overloaded conditions. Al3+ competes with Fe3+ for transferrin binding and enters cells via transferrin receptor (TfR)‐mediated endocytosis, ultimately leading to cellular iron deficiency. This disruption alters dopamine‐related proteins and molecular pathways, impairing exocytotic dynamics and reducing the average vesicular transmitter storage. When iron level is overloaded, TfR expression is downregulated to avoid Al3+ affecting vesicles. However, supplementation with ferric ammonium citrate (FAC) fails to reverse Al3+‐induced iron deficiency due to Al3+ simultaneously upregulating TfR, allowing excess Fe3+ to further decrease neurotransmitter release. Furthermore, deferoxamine (DFOM), an Fe chelator, destabilizes exocytotic fusion pores, suggesting a potential drawback of chelation therapy. These findings highlight iron dysregulation as a key pathway through which Al3+ impairs vesicular function, providing a new insight into how metal ion interactions modulate neurotransmission.Keywords: Aluminum, Cellular iron homeostasis, Exocytosis, Release fraction, Transferrin

Aluminum (Al), the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust, is widely used in food packaging, pharmaceuticals, and construction. Humans are at high risk of Al exposure due to the fact that Al3+ can cross the blood–brain barrier and accumulate up to micromolar concentrations in cells.[ 1 , 2 ] Although some research indicates that Al has neurotoxic effects, such as inducing oxidative stress and β‐amyloid deposition, the link between Al exposure and neurodegenerative diseases remains controversial.[ 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] Furthermore, due to the similar ionic radius of Al3+ and ferric iron (Fe3+), Al3+ has been reported to disrupt iron homeostasis by occupying the iron transport sites of transferrin (Tf) and subsequently binding to the transferrin receptor (TfR), thereby altering cellular Fe uptake.[ 7 , 8 ] The disruption of iron homeostasis, including iron overload and iron deficiency, can induce neurodegenerative problems.[ 9 , 10 ] However, there is still no research focusing on the effect of Al3+ on neurotransmission, particularly regarding intracellular iron homeostasis.

It has been proposed that exocytosis, the release of neurotransmitters from vesicles to the extracellular environment, is predominantly partial, followed by direct vesicle recapture.[ 11 , 12 ] The partial release mechanism has been suggested to be crucial for understanding the regulation of synaptic plasticity and diseases.[ 13 ] The fraction of release and the exocytotic dynamics can be studied by combining intracellular vesicle impact electrochemical cytometry (IVIEC) and singlecell amperometry (SCA),[ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ] which provides a pathway to investigate the roles of Al3+ in neuronal communication at the level of single vesicles.

In this study, pheochromocytoma (PC12) cells, a major model for neurosecretion and neuronal differentiation studies,[ 18 , 19 ] were induced to possess different iron levels to investigate the impact of exogenous Al3+ on vesicular storage and release via altering iron homeostasis. Aluminum chloride (AlCl3) was used in this work due to its high solubility in water and the ability to efficiently release Al3+ in the culture medium. Cells were pretreated with 100 µM of either deferoxamine (DFOM) or ferric ammonium citrate (FAC) for 24 h to induce iron‐deficient or iron‐overloaded conditions, respectively. We found that the effects of AlCl3 on vesicles, both the transmitter storage and the release dynamics, are different at different iron levels. We also investigated potential strategies for recovering from the effects caused by Al3+ and observed that supplementation with FAC failed to reverse the Fe‐deficiency condition induced by Al3+. Moreover, metal chelation therapy via DFOM after exposure to Al3+ may introduce other effects on exocytosis, especially the release dynamics.

Carbon fiber nanotip electrodes that showed good response and stable steady‐state currents to dopamine (Figure S1A) have been employed to penetrate the cell membrane and detect vesicles within the cytoplasm.[ 17 , 20 ] Vesicles adsorb and rupture on the electrode surface at a potential of +700 mV versus the Ag/AgCl reference electrode. All catecholamines inside single vesicles, primarily dopamine for PC12 vesicles, are oxidized to generate individual oxidation spikes (Figure S2). Since there is no significant difference between the number of released molecules measured by nanotips and micro‐disk electrodes,[ 21 ] we used carbon fiber micro‐disk electrodes here to measure SCA for better detection instead of using the same electrodes as for IVIEC. The disk electrode with good electrochemical performance (Figure S1B) was placed on top of a PC12 cell when the cell was stimulated by a high K+ stimulation solution, and the same potential as IVIEC was applied. The current transients in the recorded amperometric traces were presented in (Figure S3) and then analyzed to assess single vesicle release and vesicle storage. As shown in Figure S4A, the charge from a single amperometric spike (Q) can be used to calculate the number of molecules (N) with Faraday's equation (N = Q/nF), where n is the number of electrons transferred (2 for catecholamines), and F is the Faraday constant. The peaks from SCA measurements were further analyzed to obtain kinetic information about exocytosis. The peak half‐width (t half) reflects the duration of the exocytotic fusion pore. The rise time (t rise) and fall time (t fall) indicate how quickly the vesicle fusion pore opens and closes, respectively.[ 22 ] Representative amperometric peaks in traces from a control cell obtained by SCA and IVIEC are presented in Figure S4B.

As shown in Figure 1a, after treating cells with AlCl3, the average amount of exocytotic release (N SCA) increases significantly. t half, t rise, and t fall in Figure 1b–d increase slightly without significant differences compared to the control cells. In addition, Figure 2a shows that the average amount of vesicular content (N IVIEC) measured from IVIEC also increases significantly relative to the control. Dopamine is the predominant catecholamine in PC12 vesicles, so mechanistic analyses focus on dopamine‐related pathways.[ 23 ] Reports indicate that Al3+ reduces the activity of dopamine D2 receptors, leading to an upregulation of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) expression.[ 24 , 25 ] Concurrently, protein kinase C (PKC) level declines.[ 26 ] This decreased PKC level and elevated cAMP level enhance the expression of dopamine transporter (DAT),[ 27 ] promoting dopamine reuptake and vesicular loading. The reduction in D2 receptor (D2R) protein level is also expected to elevate tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) activity and increase cytosolic dopamine concentrations, further facilitating dopamine accumulation within vesicles.[ 9 , 28 ] Although diminished PKC activity may cause instability of the exocytotic fusion pore, the heightened cAMP level helps to stabilize and prolong its opening.[ 29 , 30 ] As a result, the increase in vesicular dopamine content leads to an augmented release of dopamine molecules during exocytosis. It has been reported that Al3+ disrupts iron homeostasis by competitively binding to Tf with Fe3+, causing iron deficiency in cells.[ 31 ] The effect of Al3+ on signaling molecules and cellular proteins is similar to that reported in iron‐deficient cells. This corresponds to the competition between Al3+ and Fe3+ for cell entry, resulting in iron deficiency, and the alteration in exocytosis from the Al3+‐treated cells is similar to what was observed from the iron‐deficient cells.

To investigate whether Al3+ affects the intracellular homeostasis of Fe3+ and if this effect alters exocytosis, we further examined the impact of Al3+ at varying iron levels. The effects of AlCl3 on vesicles in various iron statuses, including iron‐overloaded, iron‐deficient, and normal iron levels, differ not only in vesicular transmitter storage but also in exocytotic dynamics. In both FAC and FAC followed by AlCl3 (FAC + AlCl3) treated cells, N SCA, t half, t rise, and t fall in Figure 1 and N IVIEC in Figure 2a were significantly reduced compared to the control or the AlCl3‐only treated cells, respectively. It has been reported that iron overload suppresses TH activity, activates D2R, decreases cAMP level, and destabilizes the fusion pore,[ 32 , 33 ] thus reducing both exocytotic release and vesicular content. Interestingly, cells treated with FAC + AlCl3 exhibited a phenotype similar to those treated with FAC alone, suggesting that AlCl3 does not further affect exocytosis when iron level is over normal. This may result from the suppression of TfR synthesis in iron overload status, which limits Al3+ uptake via TfR‐mediated pathways.[ 34 ] Consequently, the average vesicular content is still decreased compared to the control group, resembling the FAC‐treated cells. Following the decline in the average number of molecules released during exocytosis, t half, t rise, and t fall also decrease.

In cells treated with DFOM followed by AlCl3 (DFOM + AlCl3), the AlCl3 treatment reduces the DFOM‐induced increase in N SCA back to the control level (Figure 1a). In Figure 1b and d, t half and t fall decrease significantly compared to the AlCl3‐only group. Although there is no difference in N IVIEC for DFOM + AlCl3 compared to the AlCl3 or DFOM‐treated cells in Figure 2a, the decrease in the duration of the fusion pore and the rapid closing of the fusion pore result in a reduction of N SCA, and thus decrease the percentage of release to 51% in Figure 2b. When cells are deficient in iron due to pre‐treatment with DFOM, more TfR are induced to bind Al3+.[ 34 ] Although partial Al3+ is chelated by residual DFOM on the cell membrane to form [Al(DFO)], Al3+ still enters the cell to increase vesicular content compared to the control cells, showing that the cellular response to Al3+ and iron‐deficiency state is similar. However, it is confusing that the DFOM + AlCl3 treatment induces a less stable fusion pore, as AlCl3 or DFOM alone increases the N SCA and stabilizes the pore. The chelation of Al3+ by DFOM complicates the situation, necessitating the consideration of the effects of the [Al(DFO)].[ 35 ] We hypothesize that changes occur in the cell membrane due to the chelation complex. This will be discussed in subsequent sections. Generally, the effects of AlCl3 on vesicles at different iron levels vary in vesicular transmitter content and release dynamics. Additionally, the molecular distribution differs as well (Figure S5), further indicating the overall changes in intracellular vesicles, not only the average number of molecules.

Considering that Al3+ can induce cellular iron deficiency, we investigated whether iron supplementation can reverse the effects caused by Al3+ by treating Al3+‐overloaded cells with FAC, a commonly used Fe supplement. As illustrated in Figure 3, FAC treatment leads to a decrease in the number of molecules released by exocytosis and shortens the duration of the fusion pore. Moreover, it accelerates the opening and closing phases of the fusion pore, differing significantly from the control and the Al3+‐treated cells. In Figure 4a, the elevated vesicular content induced by Al3+ is brought back to the control level following FAC treatment. Fe supplementation can restore vesicle loading but cannot normalize exocytotic dynamics. In this situation, Al3+ exposure increases TfR expression and subsequently enhances Fe3+ uptake. As a result, the number of molecules released is reduced, which suppresses the percentage of release to 41% (Figure 4b). Altogether, these reinforce the link between Al3+ exposure and the disruption of iron homeostasis. We conclude that Al3+ influences exocytosis, at least in part, via altering intracellular iron levels.

DFOM, clinically used for Fe/Al overload, was selected for its strong affinity to Al3+ and ability to mitigate Al3+ toxicity.[ 8 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] As shown in Figure 3a, DFOM reverses the AlCl3‐induced increase in N SCA back to the control level. In addition, several parameters related to the release dynamics (t half and t fall) decrease significantly compared to the control (Figure 3b and d), suggesting that the fusion pore becomes even less stable, consistent with the trend observed for the DFOM + AlCl3‐treated cells, which was discussed earlier. In Figure 4a, DFOM also reverses the Al3+‐induced increase in N IVIEC, likely via chelation of free Al3+ after DFOM is endocytosed.[ 39 , 40 , 41 ] The percentage of release in Figure 4b also decreases to 51%.

Notably, in cells treated with [Al(DFO)] complexes, both N SCA and the fraction of release decline. Morphological changes of the plasma membrane were also observed in optical images of the cells treated with [Al(DFO)], including DFOM + AlCl3 (Figure S6F) and AlCl3 + DFOM (Figure S6H), compared to the cells without [Al(DFO)] (Figure S6A–E,G). We hypothesize that [Al(DFO)] enters cells primarily via endocytosis, a nonspecific internalization mechanism also for DFOM alone,[ 41 ] and this induces membrane morphological change. Moreover, cells treated with both AlCl3 and DFOM simultaneously (AlCl3‐DFOM) (Figure S6I), where more [Al(DFO)] complexes are formed, show the most pronounced membrane deformation, further connecting [Al(DFO)] to membrane disruption. This may be due to nonspecific endocytosis of large [Al(DFO)] complexes, which may trigger the formation of numerous endocytic vesicles. This endocytic activity can activate membrane remodeling pathways, increase energy consumption, elevate membrane tension, and ultimately destabilize the plasma membrane.[ 42 ] As a result, fusion pores may become unstable and close more rapidly. These findings suggest that although DFOM is a well‐known metal chelator used for detoxification, its metal‐bound forms, such as [Al(DFO)], can still exert cytotoxic effects.

We propose a model, as shown in Figure 5, to explain the effects of Al3+ on exocytosis and vesicular catecholamine content via disruption of iron homeostasis. In general, Al3+ mimics Fe3+ to bind Tf and enters cells via TfR‐mediated endocytosis. This leads to reduced D2R activity, resulting in an increase in cAMP level and a decrease in PKC level. The downregulation of PKC enhances DAT expression, which in turn increases dopamine reuptake and vesicular loading. Meanwhile, reduced D2R activity upregulates TH activity, thereby increasing cytosolic dopamine levels. Despite the lower PKC level, elevated cAMP stabilizes the exocytotic fusion pore by prolonging the opening phase and facilitating increased dopamine release via exocytosis. Compared to the control cells, it can be observed that the expression of TfR is more dependent on the iron levels. Cells treated with FAC + AlCl3 exhibit exocytotic responses similar to those treated with FAC alone. This is due to the downregulation of TfR expression caused by Fe overload, which limits Al3+ uptake. The excess Fe3+ inhibits TH activity and activates D2R. As a result, cAMP level decreases and the fusion pore is destabilized, and thus both vesicular content and exocytotic release are reduced. When cells are iron‐deficient, induced by DFOM, more TfRs are expressed to facilitate Al3+ uptake. Although some Al3+ binds to residual DFOM on the cell membrane to form [Al(DFO)] complexes, the rest of the free Al3+ still enters the cell and subsequently increases vesicular content. However, endocytosis of [Al(DFO)] may destabilize the fusion pore and thus diminish exocytotic release. Overall, our findings clearly indicate that the impact of Al3+ on vesicular function is highly dependent on the cellular iron status. Iron supplementation via FAC reverses Al3+‐induced vesicular transmitter overloading but does not restore exocytotic function to the control level. This is due to excess Fe3+ uptake as Al3+ exposure increases TfR expression. In contrast, DFOM treatment partially normalizes vesicular content and release but induces membrane deformation, which causes an unstable fusion pore and is likely due to endocytic uptake of [Al(DFO)] complexes. These results highlight the dual and context‐dependent role of Fe3+ in modulating Al3+‐induced alterations in transmission and point to a complex interplay among Al toxicity, iron homeostasis, and vesicle‐mediated secretion. Moreover, our study suggests that metal chelation therapy may introduce other effects on exocytosis at the single vesicle level. Finally, the complexity of the interplay between Al3+ and Fe3+ might explain the difficulty of establishing a direct role of Al3+ in neurodegeneration. While our findings point to a robust Al3+–Fe3+ connection, the precise molecular mechanisms linking Al3+, Fe3+, and catecholamine release remain to be identified and will be an important direction for future studies.

In conclusion, the effects of Al3+ on vesicular transmitter content and exocytosis are closely related to cellular iron statuses. The impact of Al3+ on vesicles aligns with the response of cells during iron deficiency. This is due to the fact that Al3+ competes with Fe3+ to enter cells through TfR‐mediated endocytosis. This disruption affects key proteins and molecules involved in the synthesis, transport, and reuptake of dopamine, and ultimately influences vesicular storage and neurotransmitter release. Additionally, Al3+‐induced iron deficiency cannot be entirely recovered by iron supplementation, as Al3+ facilitates increased entry of Fe3+ into the cells, which further decreases the release fraction. On the other hand, iron chelation by DFOM partially restores the exocytotic release but introduces membrane structural instability because of the formation and endocytosis of the [Al(DFO)] complex. Our findings indicate that the impact of Al3+ on neurotransmission is closely linked to cellular iron status, highlighting its potential role in neurological dysfunction under iron dysregulation. Given the parallels observed between Al3+ and Fe3+, other medically relevant trivalent ions, such as gallium, with similar chemical properties, should also be considered for their potential effects on neuronal function. Moreover, our single‐cell analysis reveals that metal chelation therapy may impair the exocytotic process, an important consideration when developing chelation‐based treatments for neurodegenerative diseases. Future work will expand to other chelators and trivalent ions to inform safer therapeutic strategies.
