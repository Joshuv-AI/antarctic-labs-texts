Effect of Induced Hypertension Therapy According to the Mechanism of Single Subcortical Infarction
Authors: Seung Taek Oh, Jun Young Chang, Dong-Wha Kang, Sun U Kwon, Sang Hee Ha, Bum Joon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeInduced hypertension therapy (IHT) is effective for treating early neurological deterioration (END) in patients with single subcortical infarction (SSI). However, the underlying pathophysiology of SSI is diverse and may affect the efficacy of IHT.MethodsWe reviewed patients with SSI who experienced END and received IHT were enrolled. END was defined as ≥2-point increase in the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, ≥1 motor point increase. IHT was performed by using phenylephrine infusion to raise systolic blood pressure by 10%–20% over baseline. SSI was classified into three subtypes: distal (dSSI), proximal (pSSI), and SSI with parental artery disease (SSIPAD; with stenosis at parental artery disease <50%). Responders were defined as patients who showed neurological improvement (decrease of ≥2 points in NIHSS, ≥1 motor point decrease) within one day after IHT initiation. Multivariable analysis identified factors associated with responders.ResultsCharacteristics were compared between responders and non-responders, and factors associated with response to IHT were investigated. Among 96 patients (mean age, 64.7±12.9 years; 61.5% male), 49 (51.0%) patients were categorized as responders. Multivariable analysis showed that pSSI (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]=18.10, 95% confidence interval [CI] 5.04–80.60, p<0.001) and SSIPAD (aOR=4.56, 95% CI 1.31–19.00, p=0.024) were associated with a positive response to IHT compared to dSSI. Additionally, less white matter changes (Fazekas scale 0–1) were associated with a better response to IHT (aOR=0.15, 95% CI 0.03–0.67, p=0.019).ConclusionsResponse to IHT varied according to SSI subtypes and the severity of small vessel changes.Keywords: induced hypertension therapy, single subcortical infarction, early neurological deterioration, disease progression, lacunar infarct

INTRODUCTION

Single subcortical infarction (SSI) is a common form of stroke that can be classified into three distinct categories: distal SSI (dSSI), proximal SSI (pSSI), and SSI with parental artery disease (SSIPAD).1 This classification is based on size, location, and the presence of atherosclerosis, reflecting diverse underlying mechanisms.1 A considerable portion of patients with SSI experience early neurological deterioration (END), a phenomenon that can significantly worsen their overall prognosis.2 END presents a particular challenge in SSI patients because, unlike strokes caused by large artery atherosclerosis or cardioembolic events, SSI does not lend itself to rescue endovascular treatments such as thrombectomy after END.3 As a result, finding effective strategies to manage END in SSI patients is crucial but remains difficult.

One promising approach that has garnered attention is induced hypertension therapy (IHT), which aims to enhance perfusion by increasing blood flow through collateral vessels.3 This therapy is both effective and well-tolerated in ischemic stroke patients, particularly those who are experiencing END.3 Furthermore, recent studies suggest that even in cases of small vessel disease, the concept of the ischemic penumbra—a region of brain tissue that is at risk but potentially salvageable—may still apply.4 Traditionally, the penumbra has been associated with large vessel occlusions, but emerging evidence indicates that it could also be present in cases of SSI.2,4

However, due to the heterogeneous nature of SSI, the response to IHT may vary depending on the specific type of SSI.1 This variability underscores the importance of tailored treatment approaches that consider the unique characteristics of each patient’s condition.

METHODS

Patients and clinical data

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of SSI patients admitted to Asan Medical Center between January 2017 and May 2022. The inclusion criteria for this study required that patients 1) had undergone diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) to confirm the diagnosis of SSI, and 2) had experienced END and subsequently received IHT as part of their treatment. Patients were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: 1) insufficient clinical or imaging data that would preclude accurate analysis, 2) more than 50% stenosis in the parent artery, which could indicate large artery atherosclerosis as a potential cause of stroke, and 3) other identifiable stroke etiologies, such as atrial fibrillation or arterial dissection.

Clinical data were meticulously collected from electronic medical records and the institution’s stroke database. This data included patient demographics (such as age and sex), vascular risk factors (including hypertension, diabetes, and smoking status), and vital signs at the time of admission. Additionally, neurological deficits were assessed using the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score at the time of admission and were regularly monitored for four hours following the initiation of IHT to track any changes in the patients’ neurological status. This study received approval from the Asan Medical Center (IRB no. 2022-0830), and patient informed consent was not required due to the retrospective nature of the study. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

END and IHT

END was defined as ≥2-point increase in NIHSS score, ≥1-point increase in motor score.5 IHT was not considered for patients with cardiac arrhythmia, heart diseases, and initial systolic blood pressure (SBP) above 180 mm Hg.6 The time interval between the onset of END and the initiation of IHT was recorded. IHT was administered via continuous intravenous phenylephrine infusion to increase blood pressure (BP) by 10% to 20% above baseline.3 The initial dose of phenylephrine was 0.5 mcg/kg/min, with a maximum dose of 3.0 mcg/kg/min. To reach the target BP, the dose was increased by 0.2 mcg/kg/min every 20 minutes while closely monitoring the BP response at the same intervals. Once the target BP was achieved, BP was monitored for 2 hours. Blood pressure variability was primarily assessed using the standard deviation of the SBP.

Major safety outcomes included aggravation of symptoms by intracranial hemorrhage or cerebral edema, and myocardial infarction during IHT. Minor safety outcomes included headache, palpitation, chest pain, dysuria, and urticaria.3 The response to IHT was characterized by a reduction of ≥2 points in NIHSS score, ≥1-point decrease in motor score within one day after IHT initiation.6

Neuroimaging and types of SSI

MRI examinations were performed on the day of admission from the emergency department, following the center’s imaging protocol. The scans were conducted using either a 1.5-T Siemens Avanto (Siemens Medical Solutions) or a 3.0-T Philips scanner (Philips Healthcare). The MRI sequences included DWI, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), gradient echo (GRE) or susceptibility-weighted imaging, and magnetic resonance angiography.

SSI was defined as an infarction occurring within the territory supplied by perforating arteries, as identified on DWI. These infarcts affected deep subcortical regions of the brain, which include the basal ganglia, internal capsule, corona radiata, and pons.7 In cases where parent artery stenosis was observed to be less than 50%, the infarctions were classified as SSIPAD. For cerebral infarctions that occurred without any evidence of parent artery disease, further classification was carried out to differentiate between pSSI and dSSI. This distinction was based on whether the infarction extended to the base of the parent artery or not, a key factor in understanding the potential mechanisms and risks associated with each type of SSI (Fig. 1).7

White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) were assessed using FLAIR imaging. To systematically categorize the severity of WMHs, we employed a modified Fazekas scale, which classifies these hyperintensities into two categories: grades 0–1 (representing mild changes) and grades 2–3 (representing more severe changes). We also investigated the presence of cerebral microbleeds and lacunes, which are common markers of small vessel disease. Cerebral microbleeds were identified as lesions with low signal intensity, typically measuring less than 10 mm in diameter, on GRE images. Lacunes were defined as small lesions with a signal intensity equivalent to that of cerebrospinal fluid and a diameter of 3 mm or more, with high signal intensity along their borders on FLAIR images.8 The MRI images were determined by consensus between two stroke experts (SH Ha and ST Oh) who were blinded to clinical information.

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of responders and non-responders to IHT were compared. For categorical variables, we employed the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the expected frequency counts in the contingency tables. Fisher’s exact test was used in cases where cell counts were low, ensuring accurate statistical inference. For continuous variables, we utilized either Student’s t-test, when the data followed a normal distribution, or the Mann–Whitney U-test for non-normally distributed data.

To further explore the factors associated with a favorable response to IHT, we conducted both univariate and multivariate analyses. In the univariate analysis, each variable was examined independently to identify potential factors linked to the therapeutic response. Variables associated with response to IHT (p<0.1) in the univariate analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression model, along with key demographic factors (age and sex) to adjust for confounding.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of response to IHT, allowing us to account for the interplay between various factors and isolate those with a direct impact on treatment outcomes. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing; https://www.R-project.org/), a versatile and widely accepted software environment for statistical computing and graphics.

RESULTS

Among the 6,959 patients whose medical records were reviewed, 1,156 (16.6%) cases were identified and classified as SSI. Of these SSI patients, 154 (13.3%) experienced END, a condition that warranted the consideration of IHT. Out of the 154 patients, IHT was performed in 117 (76.0%) cases. However, 21 (17.9%) of these cases were excluded from the final analysis according to the exclusion criteria, which may have included insufficient data or the presence of other complicating factors. As a result, a total of 96 cases were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2). The mean age of the patients included in the analysis was 64.7±12.9 years, with a majority being male (59 patients, or 61.5%). The neurological severity of the patients at the time of admission, as measured by the NIHSS scores, had a median score of 5.0 points (interquartile range [IQR], 3.0–7.0). After experiencing END, the median NIHSS score increased to 7.0 points (IQR, 3.0–7.0), indicating a worsening of the neurological condition. Among the patients who underwent IHT, 49 (51.0%) were categorized as responders, meaning they showed significant improvement following the therapy.

Responder vs. non-responder

The baseline characteristics of both responders and non-responders were compared and are summarized in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of traditional vascular risk factors (such as hypertension, diabetes, or smoking status) or stroke severity (NIHSS score) at admission. This suggests that these common risk factors did not play a major role in determining the response to IHT. However, other factors emerged as significant. Responders also had less severe WMHs on imaging, with only 6.1% of responders having WMHs graded as Fazekas 2–3, compared to 21.3% of non-responders (p=0.038). Furthermore, there was a significantly higher prevalence of pSSI (61.2% vs. 19.1%) and SSIPAD (40.8% vs. 31.9%) among responders compared to non-responders (p<0.001). Also, none of the patients who received IHT experienced severe adverse events such as symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage or acute myocardial infarction. There was no difference in adverse events including minor adverse events between the two groups (Table 2).

Factors associated with response to IHT

According to the univariable analysis, SSI subtypes—particularly pSSI—and less severe WMHs (Fazekas scale 0–1) were significantly associated with responders.

Furthermore, multivariable analysis showed that patients with pSSI were much more likely to respond to IHT compared to those with dSSI, with an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 18.10 (95% confidence interval [CI] 5.04–80.60; p<0.001). Similarly, SSIPAD was associated with a higher likelihood of responding to IHT compared to dSSI (aOR=4.56; 95% CI 1.31–19.00; p=0.024). Additionally, less white matter changes (Fazekas scale 0–1) were associated with a better response to IHT (aOR=0.15; 95% CI 0.03–0.67; p=0.019) (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we identified that approximately half of SSI patients who experienced END demonstrated a favorable response to IHT, particularly those with less severe WMH and specific subtypes of SSI, such as pSSI and SSIPAD, in comparison to dSSI. These findings highlight the importance of pathomechanism and burden of SSI affecting the efficacy of IHT among SSI patients.

The primary mechanism responsible for END in SSI patients is believed to be the expansion of the infarcted region within the diffusion-perfusion mismatch zone, a concept supported by previous research.9 The diffusion-perfusion mismatch reflects an area where tissue may still be salvageable if adequately reperfused.10 Studies have shown that increased cerebral blood flow via elevated BP can be beneficial in these cases, as it enhances cerebral perfusion by augmenting blood volume and improving microcirculation.3 Notably, our study observed that patients with pSSI and SSIPAD responded more positively to IHT compared to those with dSSI. Perfusion abnormalities are notably more prevalent in patients with pSSI and SSIPAD where the atherothrombotic involvement of the proximal perforating arteries, through microatheroma or branch atheromatous disease, leads to impaired downstream perfusion.11,12 In such cases, IHT may help by improving blood flow in areas with limited perfusion, thereby reducing infarct growth.12,13 Nevertheless, further research directly comparing perfusion dynamics between pSSI and SSIPAD is warranted to determine whether perfusion alterations truly account for their superior response to IHT.

Conversely, perfusion alterations are thought to play a lesser role in dSSI caused by lipohyalinotic degeneration, where vessel wall thickening and stiffening from chronic hypertension result in different patterns of injury that are less responsive to increased blood flow.7,12 However, due to the retrospective design of our study, only a small number of patients underwent perfusion weighted image, and the data was not sufficient for quantitative analysis. Therefore, we were not able to demonstrate changes in perfusion in patients who underwent IHT. In our additional subgroup analysis, the peak SBP during IHT showed a significant difference among the three groups (196±24 mm Hg in dSSI vs. 191±15 mm Hg in pSSI vs. 200±17 mm Hg in SSIPAD, p=0.048) (Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). The optimal target BP required for sufficient hemodynamic response may differ depending on the underlying mechanism, which warrants further large-scale studies.

Furthermore, our findings suggest that the severity of WMH is a key factor influencing patient outcomes following IHT. WMH, a well-established neuroimaging marker of small vessel disease, reflects chronic ischemic damage and cumulative injury to the brain’s white matter.14 Previous studies have indicated that a higher burden of WMH correlates with poorer clinical outcomes due to disrupted global network connectivity and reduced cerebrovascular reserve capacity.15 Although phenylephrine acts via α1-receptor–mediated vasoconstriction in larger peripheral vessels to raise systemic BP, its beneficial effect depends on the cerebral microvasculature’s ability to translate the increased perfusion pressure through autoregulatory vasodilation at the arteriolar and perforator level.16 In patients with severe WMH, chronic microangiopathy impairs this compensatory response, exhausting cerebrovascular reserve.15 As a result, even with elevated systemic pressure, the impaired small vessels cannot sufficiently dilate, thereby attenuating the perfusion gain from IHT.

Previous studies have reported early motor restoration following IHT in patients with small vessel occlusion stroke, with vascular stiffness potentially serving as a predictor of motor improvement.17 Another study indicated that the time interval between END and the initiation of IHT is an important factor for IHT response, suggesting that initiating IHT within 3 hours of END onset may lead to better outcomes.18 In the current study with SSI patients, responders also tended to have a shorter duration between END and IHT compared to non-responders, although the difference was not statistically significant (3.75 hours vs. 2.00 hours, p=0.480). These findings suggest a similar trend in our cohort, but further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm this association.

There are several limitations to this study. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. Second, the number of perfusion-weighted imaging studies in our cohort was limited, which precluded a detailed quantitative analysis of the diffusion–perfusion mismatch.

Additionally, the lack of long-term follow-up data restricts our ability to assess the sustained impact of IHT on functional outcomes and overall recovery in these patients. Finally, this study included patients who underwent MRI with both 1.5T and 3T scanners, which may have introduced variability in the detection and quantification of WMH, lacunes, and cerebral microbleeds due to differences in image resolution and sensitivity across field strengths. Future studies with larger, multi-center cohorts and longer follow-up periods are essential to validate our results and explore the long-term efficacy of IHT.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that the response to IHT in SSI patients varies significantly depending on the subtype of the infarct and the extent of small vessel disease, as indicated by the severity of WMH. Patients with pSSI or SSIPAD, as well as those with less severe white matter changes, appear to benefit more from IHT, highlighting the potential for more targeted therapeutic interventions in these populations. Further research is warranted to better understand the mechanisms at play and to develop more personalized treatment strategies that optimize outcomes for SSI patients at risk of END.
