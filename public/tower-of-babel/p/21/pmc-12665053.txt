Transcription Factor GmERFA Interacts With GmNFYA and Acts as a Negative Regulator of Seed Fatty Acid Accumulation in Soybean
Authors: Yue Liu, Yang Hu, Jun‐Jie Wei, Zhi‐Hao Jiang, Jia‐Qi Han, Meng Jin, Lu‐Yao Zhang, Ling‐Yi Zheng, Wei Li, Ying‐Dong Bi, Yong‐Cai Lai, Bin Zhou, De‐Yue Yu, Feng‐Xia Zhang, Guo‐Dong Wang, Cui‐Cui Yin, Jian‐Jun Tao, Shou‐Yi Chen, Wan‐Ke Zhang, Wei Wei, Jin‐Song Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSoybean is one of the most important oilseed crops, and its seed oil content directly determines the economic value and industrial applicability worldwide. However, how soybean seed oil accumulation is regulated remains less understood. Here, through RNA‐seq analysis and screening for the interacting proteins of a positive oil regulator GmNFYA, we identified an AP2/ERF‐type transcription factor GmERFA, which acts as a negative regulator of oil accumulation. Knocking out GmERFA and its homologue by genome editing increased seed total fatty acid content, while overexpression of GmERFA leads to a reduced fatty acid level in transgenic soybean. GmERFA interacts with GmNFYA to inhibit its transcriptional activation of GmbZIP123 and GmZF392, both of which promotes seed oil accumulation. The GmERFA also directly binds to the promoter regions of GmbZIP123 and GmZF392 and represses their gene expression. Through further analysis of more than 300 soybean accessions, an elite allele of ERFA with Hap3 promoter is identified to correlate with lower promoter activity, lower gene expression but higher seed oil content. The Hap3 ERFA may be selected and fixed during soybean domestication. Together, our study discovers a brake gene for oil accumulation and may function in a novel molecular network GmERFA‐GmNFYA‐GmbZIP123/GmZF392 at the later stage of soybean seed development. Manipulation of the gene, its elite allele, and the whole pathway should benefit breeding for high oil cultivars in soybean.

Introduction

Soybean ( Glycine max ) is the valuable vegetable crop that is rich in oil and protein worldwide, and makes a significant contribution to the overall health and nutrition of the human diet and animal feeding (Wang et al. 2020). Soybean oil accounts for over half of the global oilseed production, and serves as a vital source of both edible oil and industrial raw materials, making it of significant economic value (Duan et al. 2023). Statistical data indicated that soybean oil has consistently increased its proportion in global oilseed production over the past decade, reaching a dominant share of 60.72% in 2023/2024 (www.statista.com). These data indicate that soybean oil plays a pivotal role in addressing global lipid demands across food and non‐food sectors.

Soybean fatty acids (FAs) are generally stored as triacylglycerols (TAGs) that contain saturated FAs (palmitic acid [16:0] and stearic acid [18:0]) and unsaturated FAs (oleic acid [18:1], linoleic acid [18:2] and linolenic acid [18:3]) in seeds (Lee et al. 2007). In plants, acetyl‐CoA is the initial substrate for synthesis of all fatty acids. Within plastids, acetyl‐CoA is produced from pyruvate generated during glycolysis. As the central precursor for de novo synthesis of FAs, acetyl‐CoA is carboxylated by acetyl‐CoA carboxylase to form malonyl‐CoA. Subsequently, the fatty acid synthase system sequentially catalyses a series of reactions including condensation, reduction, dehydration and reduction, ultimately elongating the carbon chain and producing palmitic acid (C16:0) and stearic acid (C18:0). The synthesised FAs are subsequently transported to the endoplasmic reticulum to participate in TAG assembly (Li, Jin, et al. 2017). Finally, the TAGs are stored in cytosolic oil bodies surrounded by a phospholipid monolayer embedded with the oleosins, caleosins and steroleosins (Manan et al. 2017).

As is widely recognised, the developmental process of seeds was invariably accompanied by the accumulation of storage reserves (Ruuska et al. 2002). The storage compounds found in most seeds, which account for over 90% of the seed dry weight, primarily consist of starch, storage protein and oil. In Arabidopsis, oil content accumulates gradually from early embryogenesis to the maturation phase, with a decreasing trend at later maturation stages (Baud et al. 2002). During the domestication of soybeans, seed oil content exhibited a significant upward trend. The oil content increased from 8% to 10% in wild soybeans to 18%–22% in cultivated accessions (Patil et al. 2018). During the early maturation stage of soybean seed development, the initiation of oil accumulation is observed, followed by peak accumulation rates at the middle maturation stage, with a gradual decline manifesting at the late maturation stage (Collakova et al. 2013).

In Arabidopsis, the major transcription factors regulating lipid biosynthesis include LEC2, LEC1, L1L, ABI3, FUS3 and WRI1, etc. Overexpression of LEC2 upregulates the expression levels of LEC1, ABI3, FUS3 and WRI1, which in turn enhances the accumulation of TAGs (Kim et al. 2013). WRI1 is a critical regulator of seed lipid biosynthesis and acts as a direct target gene of LEC1 to regulate the accumulation of FAs in Arabidopsis seeds (Baud et al. 2009). The MYB family transcription factors TT2 inhibit the process of FA biosynthesis in seeds via suppressing the expression of FUS3 (Wang et al. 2014).

Extensive studies have demonstrated that transcription factors play an indispensable role in lipid biosynthesis of soybean (Hu, Liu, Wei, et al. 2023; Wei et al. 2025). GmDof4 and GmDof11 could improve FA content through activating the accD and long‐chain acyl‐CoA synthetase genes (Wang et al. 2007). GmMYB73 interacts with GL3 and EGL3 to suppress the expression of GL2, thus promoting the conversion of phosphatidylcholines (PCs) to TAGs (Liu et al. 2014). GmDREBL activates the expression of WRI1 by binding directly to its promoter, thereby promoting FAs biosynthesis (Zhang et al. 2016). Overexpression of GmbZIP123 enhances the oil content via activating the expression of SUC and cwINV genes (Song et al. 2013). GmNFYA increases FA content by activating the expression of WRI1 and the lipid synthesis genes FatA, SAD and KASI (Lu et al. 2016). GmZF351, an activator of soybean oil accumulation, promotes WRI1 and WRI‐regulated gene expression directly (Li, Lu, et al. 2017). GmZF392 promotes FA accumulation by activating downstream gene expression involving lipid biosynthesis (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021). This study also reveals a novel cultivar‐specific molecular network involving three transcription factors GmNFYA‐GmZF392‐GmZF351, which plays a significant role in the regulation of soybean lipid biosynthesis pathway. GmZF392 interacts physically with GmZF351, and both of the gene expressions are positively regulated by GmNFYA.

The carbon resources are fundamental substrates for the biosynthesis of both FAs and proteins (Nguyen et al. 2016). Meanwhile, various studies indicate that soybean seed oil content and protein content are negatively correlated (Huynh et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2024). It is interesting to note that many genes that affect oil or protein content also influence seed size/weight (Hu, Liu, Wei, et al. 2023). GmJAZ3, a transcriptional repressor, decreases FA content, while it increases the protein content and seed size/weight, likely by influencing sugar transportation (Hu, Liu, Tao, et al. 2023). Overexpression of GmOLEO1 improves the lipid content, while it decreases the protein content and seed size/weight (Zhang et al. 2019). The GmSWEET10a and GmSWEET10b are identified to enhance the seed oil content and seed size/weight, but reduce the seed protein content in soybean (Wang et al. 2020). These findings demonstrate that there are many pleiotropic genes that coordinately modulate key seed traits, including FA content, protein content and seed size/weight in soybean.

Many studies have revealed that AP2/ERF transcription factors critically regulate multiple developmental processes in plants, including floral organogenesis and senescence, spikelet meristem maintenance, root elongation and morphogenesis, leaf size determination and seed development and maturation (Cheng et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2022; Neogy et al. 2019; Teng et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2025). In addition, other studies have demonstrated that transgenic plants overexpressing AP2/ERF transcription factors exhibit enhanced tolerance to both abiotic and biotic stresses (Ding et al. 2022; Huang et al. 2016; Kai et al. 2022; Li et al. 2022). However, whether the AP2/ERF‐type transcription factor participates in the regulation of lipid accumulation is still less understood, especially in soybean.

Here, we identified a novel AP2/ERF family transcription factor GmERFA through transcriptome analysis and yeast‐two hybrid library screening of the interacting proteins for GmNFYA, a positive oil regulator. Overexpression of GmERFA led to reduced FA levels in transgenic soybean seeds. In contrast, genome editing of GmERFA and its homologous gene GmERFB using the CRISPR/Cas9 system significantly increased the soybean seed oil content. The GmERFA may interact with and inhibit GmNFYA for the reduction of the positive oil regulator GmbZIP123 and GmZF392 gene expression, acting as a brake for oil accumulation at the later stage of seed development. Meanwhile, GmERFA also repressed the expression of GmbZIP123 and GmZF392 through direct binding to their promoters. Our study further deepens the understanding of the connections among different oil regulators and should provide crucial genetic resources and strategies for high‐oil soybean breeding.

Results

Identification of an Oil Regulatory Gene GmERFA

To identify novel genes regulating soybean seed oil content, we performed the oil‐related network analysis using our previous RNA sequencing data from 33 soybean accessions (Hu, Liu, Lu, et al. 2023). Generally, accessions with an oil content greater than 22% are considered high‐oil accessions, while those with an oil content below 20% are classified as low‐oil accessions. The 33 soybean accessions include 15 (7 low‐oil accessions and 8 medium high to high‐oil accessions) from the north‐eastern region of China (NEC) and 18 (9 low‐oil accessions and 9 high‐oil accessions) from the Huang‐Huai‐Hai Region (HHHR) (Figure S1a and Table S1). Genes with fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) values > 0.5 were defined as expressed. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on a fold‐change > 2 between the maximum and minimum expression levels in low‐oil and high‐oil accessions, with an average FPKM > 0.5 across all accessions. A pairwise comparison was performed between each low‐oil accession and each high‐oil accession in NEC and HHHR, respectively. Genes ranked within the top 4% frequency threshold were selected, resulting in the identification of 168 DEGs in NEC accessions and 296 DEGs in HHHR accessions, with 76 overlapping DEGs from accessions of both regions (Figure S1b and Table S2). We also identified more than 200 candidate proteins that interacted with GmNFYA, an oil regulator found in our previous study (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021; Lu et al. 2016), through the yeast library screening (Table S3). From the two analyses above, we identified a shared AP2/ERF‐type transcription factor and named it GmERFA (Glyma.02G016100) for further investigation to elucidate its function in regulating soybean oil accumulation.

GmERFA encodes a protein of 300 amino acids with an AP2 domain (Figure 1a). Gene expression analysis revealed that low‐oil soybean accessions exhibited higher GmERFA expression, whereas high‐oil accessions showed significantly lower expression levels in accessions from both NEC and HHHR (Figure 1b). GmERFA expression was detected in all soybean organs and was highest in soybean seeds (Figure 1c). Furthermore, it is steadily expressed during soybean seed development and increased sharply at the later maturation stage (S6–S7) (Figure 1d). The GmERFA is located in the nucleus and functions as a transcriptional repressor (Figure 1e,f).

To investigate the function of GmERFA, the coding sequence (CDS) of GmERFA was fused into the pBI121 expression vector, driven by the 35S promoter and transformed into Arabidopsis Columbia‐0 (Col‐0). Compared to Col‐0, the three GmERFA‐overexpressing (OX) Arabidopsis lines exhibited reduced rosette size and smaller seeds (Figure S2a–c). The 1000‐seed weight, seed length and seed width from the GmERFA‐OX Arabidopsis were all significantly less than those of Col‐0 (Figure S2d–f). Moreover, the total FA contents and each FA composition in the seeds of GmERFA‐OX Arabidopsis plants were significantly decreased (Figure S2g,h). In contrast, the protein contents increased by 17.6%, 11.2% and 3.3%, respectively (Figure S2i). These results suggest that GmERFA may negatively control seed oil content and seed size/weight, but positively regulate seed protein levels.

GmERFA Negatively Regulates Seed Oil Content in Soybean

To evaluate the function of GmERFA in soybean, we generated stable transgenic soybean plants harbouring 35Spro:GmERFA, and three homozygous transgenic lines with higher transgene expression were identified (Figure 1g). Both Jack and Null plants, which is segregated without T‐DNA insertion from the transgenic plants, were used as control plants. Moreover, PCR amplification was further performed to validate the GmERFA‐OX lines. Forward primers were designed within the CDS of GmERFA, and reverse primers were designed on the expression vector (Figure S3a). The results demonstrated that all three homozygous transgenic lines produced corresponding bands of the target size, whereas no detectable bands were observed in the control plants (Figure S3b). Phenotype analysis revealed that the seeds of GmERFA‐OX soybean plants were smaller than those of Jack and Null (Figure 1h). In these GmERFA‐OX soybean plants, significant decreases were observed in the 100‐seed weight, seed length and seed width (Figure 1i–k). Meanwhile, the total FAs and FA compositions in GmERFA‐OX soybean seeds were significantly decreased (Figure 1l,m). However, the seed protein content was significantly increased by 4.5%, 4.4% and 4.4%, respectively (Figure 1n). These results indicate that the GmERFA overexpression inhibits seed size/weight and fatty acid accumulation but promotes protein levels.

We further utilised the CRISPR/Cas9 system to perform gene editing on GmERFA and its homologous gene GmERFB. Three double knockout mutants in the Jack background, named gmerfa/b #1, gmerfa/b #2 and gmerfa/b #3, with different deletions in the genomic sequences of GmERFA and GmERFB were identified through PCR amplification followed by sequencing (Figure 2a). Sequencing chromatograms and specific nucleotide alterations further demonstrated distinct editing patterns in the three mutant lines (Figure S3d,e). The GmERFA expression in gmerfa/b mutants was much lower than that of Jack and Null (Figure S3c). The gmerfa/b mutants produced larger seeds (Figure 2b). Consistently, compared with Jack and Null plants, the three gmerfa/b mutants showed a significant increase in 100‐seed weight by 8.2%, 5.3% and 8.8%, respectively (Figure 2c). The seed length and seed width of gmerfa/b mutants were also bigger than those of control plants (Figure 2d,e). All mutants exhibited significantly elevated total FA levels compared with control plants, with increases of total FAs ranging from 4.4% to 5.4% (Figure 2f). Meanwhile, the levels of major FA compositions in the seeds of the three gmerfa/b mutants were also significantly increased (Figure 2g). Moreover, the total oil content was significantly increased by 13.3%, 8.7% and 10.4%, respectively (Figure 2h). It is noted that a decline in protein content was observed in the gmerfa/b mutants (Figure 2i). Taken together, these results demonstrate that the GmERFA mutant produces larger seeds with more oil and more weight, but less protein in soybean.

GmERFA Interacts With GmNFYA to Modulate the Accumulation of Seed Oil in Soybean

In our previous study, the transcription activator GmNFYA has been found to promote seed oil accumulation in both Arabidopsis and soybean (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021; Lu et al. 2016). The present GmERFA was also identified from a screen for the GmNFYA‐interacting proteins (Table S3). We demonstrated the interaction between GmERFA and GmNFYA by yeast two‐hybrid system and split‐luciferase complementation (Split‐LUC) assays (Figure 3a,b). Meanwhile, we confirmed the interaction of GmERFA and GmNFYA by bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays and co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) using a wheat germ protein expression system to express the two proteins in the same reaction (Figure 3c,d). Collectively, these results demonstrate that GmERFA physically interacts with GmNFYA for possible regulation of seed oil in soybean.

Considering that GmERFA is a negative oil regulator (Figures 1 and 2) while GmNFYA is a positive oil regulator (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021), and GmERFA interacts with GmNFYA, it is likely that GmERFA may inhibit GmNFYA functions to reduce FA levels. We then further examined the roles of GmNFYA in seed trait control. While GmNFYA promotes fatty acid and TAG accumulation in GmNFYA‐OE soybean (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021), the protein content in these transgenic plants was significantly lower than that in the control plants (Figure 3e). We further generated double knockout mutants of GmNFYA and its homologue GmNFYA' in the Jack background. Three homozygous knockout lines, named gmnfya/a' #1, gmnfya/a' #2 and gmnfya/a' #3, were identified through PCR amplification and sequencing (Figure 3f). Furthermore, we demonstrated distinct editing patterns across the three mutant lines through sequencing chromatograms and specific nucleotide alterations (Figure S4a,b). In the three gmnfya/a' mutants, the expression of GmNFYA was apparently decreased compared with that in Jack and Null (Figure S4c). The seed size/weight of these mutant seeds was also measured, and no significant difference was observed (Figure 3g and Figure S4d–f).

To further examine the role of GmNFYA in oil accumulation, we measured and compared the FA levels between the gmnfya/a' mutants and control plants. Compared to Jack and Null, gmnfya/a' mutant seeds contained lower total FA levels (Figure 3h). Each FA composition also exhibited a significant decrease in these mutant seeds (Figure 3i). Not surprisingly, the decrease in oil content in the gmnfya/a' mutant lines resulted in a significant increase in protein content. The seed protein content in the three gmnfya/a' mutants increased significantly by 8.2%, 9.3% and 14.5%, respectively (Figure 3j).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the GmNFYA is required for FA accumulation in soybean seeds, and the FA level is negatively correlated with the protein level.

GmERFA Regulates the Expression of GmbZIP123 via Both Direct and Indirect Manners

To further elucidate the specific molecular mechanisms by which GmERFA regulates lipid biosynthesis, we investigated its potential target genes involved in this regulatory process. GmbZIP123 worked as a transcriptional activator to promote the seed oil content in our previous study (Song et al. 2013), and RT‐qPCR analysis revealed significant downregulation of the expression of GmbZIP123 in GmERFA‐OX soybean plants compared to Jack and Null (Figure 4a). In contrast, the gmerfa/b mutants exhibited elevated GmbZIP123 transcript levels (Figure 4b). Furthermore, the GmbZIP123 expression in the gmnfya/a’ mutants was lower than that in the control plants (Figure 4c). The coordinated suppression of GmbZIP123 in both GmERFA‐OX soybean plants and gmnfya/a’ mutants suggests the antagonistic regulation between GmERFA and GmNFYA on the downstream target GmbZIP123.

To further determine whether GmERFA directly binds to the promoter of GmbZIP123, an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was performed. In previous studies, the GCC‐box and TTG motif have been identified as the binding sites of the AP2/ERF transcription factors (Hao et al. 2002; Wang et al. 2015). Within the promoter region of GmbZIP123, we identified two probes P1 and P2 (59 bp each), containing the TTG motif and GCC‐box, respectively (Table S4). EMSA analysis revealed that GmERFA directly and specifically bound to the P1 and P2 fragments in the promoter of GmbZIP123 in vitro (Figure 4d). Meanwhile, chromatin immunoprecipitation‐quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP‐qPCR) assay was employed to detect the binding ability of GmERFA to the promoter of GmbZIP123 in vivo. Transgenic hairy roots overexpressing GFP, GmERFA‐GFP and GmNFYA‐GFP were generated in soybean cultivar Kefeng1 using the Agrobacterium rhizogenes ‐mediated transformation system. The P1 and P2 probes of GmbZIP123 were enriched at higher levels in GmERFA‐GFP‐OX soybean hairy roots (Figure 4e). In GmNFYA‐GFP‐OX soybean hairy roots, both probes were also highly enriched (Figure 4e). These results indicate that GmERFA directly binds to the GmbZIP123 promoter and that both GmERFA and GmNFYA associate with the gene promoter likely through physical interaction.

To further investigate the function of GmbZIP123 in regulating FA content, two constructs pBIN438‐GmbZIP123 and pZH01‐GmbZIP123‐RNAi were made and used to generate the GmbZIP123‐OX and the GmbZIP123‐RNAi soybean transgenic hairy roots, respectively (Figure S5a,c). The soybean hairy roots generated from A. rhizogenis strain K599 were used as control roots. Compared to the control roots, the FA content in GmbZIP123‐OX soybean hairy roots showed a significant increase, whereas GmbZIP123‐RNAi hairy roots exhibited relatively lower FAs (Figure S5b,d). Together, these results indicate that GmbZIP123 promotes FA accumulation in soybean and is regulated directly by GmERFA.

Considering that GmERFA directly binds to the promoter of GmbZIP123 and interacts with GmNFYA, we examined the effects of GmERFA and GmNFYA on the promoter activity of GmbZIP123. For the reporter plasmid, ~2Kb promoter sequences of GmbZIP123 were fused to the LUC gene in the vector pCAMBIA2300. For the effector plasmid, the CDS of GUS, GmERFA and GmNFYA, driven by the 35S promoter, were also cloned into pCAMBIA2300 (Figure 4f). The 35S:GUS was used as a negative control. The results showed that GmERFA significantly inhibited the promoter activity of GmbZIP123, whereas GmNFYA markedly activated its promoter activity. Furthermore, GmERFA suppressed the transcriptional activation of the GmbZIP123 promoter induced by GmNFYA (Figure 4g).

The binding ability of GmNFYA to the GmbZIP123 promoter was further analysed since GmNFYA upregulated the activity of the pGmbZIP123:LUC reporter. The EMSA analysis demonstrated that GmNFYA directly bound to the promoter of GmbZIP123 in vitro (Figure 4h). Furthermore, the ChIP signals for P3 (59 bp) fragments of the GmbZIP123 promoter in 35S‐GmNFYA‐GFP transgenic soybean hairy roots were significantly enriched (Figure 4i and Table S4). Meanwhile, in GmERFA‐GFP‐OX soybean hairy roots, the P3 fragments of GmbZIP123 were also highly enriched (Figure 4i). Together, all these results indicate that GmERFA represses FA accumulation both by directly binding to the GmbZIP123 promoter to inhibit its transcriptional activity and by interacting with GmNFYA to suppress GmNFYA‐promoted transcriptional activation of GmbZIP123.

GmERFA Inhibits GmNFYA‐Mediated Transcriptional Activation of GmZF392

GmZF392 was identified as a seed‐preferred transcription factor that positively regulates oil accumulation in soybean plants, and GmNFYA directly binds to the promoter region of GmZF392 to activate its transcription in our previous study (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021). Because the GmERFA interacts with GmNFYA, we then investigated whether GmERFA could control the expression of GmZF392. GmZF392 was significantly reduced in GmERFA‐OX soybean plants but elevated in gmerfa/b mutants in comparison to control plants (Figure 5a,b). Furthermore, the expression of GmZF392 was apparently downregulated in gmnfya/a' mutants (Figure 5c). Because GmZF392 activated the expression of the downstream lipid biosynthesis‐related genes including GmBCCP2, GmKASIIIs, GmTAGs, GmOLEO1s and GmWRI1s in GmZF392‐OX soybeans (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021), we examined the expression of all these genes in GmERFA‐OX soybean plants, gmerfa/b mutants and gmnfya/a' mutants. A consistent trend was observed between the GmZF392 expression and these downstream genes in GmERFA‐OX soybeans, gmerfa/b mutants and gmnfya/a' mutants, respectively (Figure S6a–c).

We performed EMSA and ChIP‐qPCR to further investigate whether the GmERFA can bind to the promoter of GmZF392. The P1 and P2 probes (59 bp each) harbouring the GCC‐box of the GmZF392 promoter region were examined for GmERFA binding (Table S4). Compared to the control GST protein, the GST‐GmERFA protein was found to specifically bind to the two probes (Figure 5d). Moreover, the two fragments of GmZF392 were highly enriched in both GmERFA‐GFP‐OX and GmNFYA‐GFP‐OX soybean hairy roots (Figure 5e). These results demonstrate that GmERFA directly binds to the promoter of GmZF392 in vitro and in vivo and is associated with GmNFYA.

We further evaluated whether GmERFA could affect the promoter activity of GmZF392. The 35S:GUS, 35S:GmERFA and 35S:GmNFYA plasmids were constructed as effectors, and the pGmZF392:LUC was cloned into pCAMBIA2300 as a reporter (Figure 5f). The LUC activity driven by ~2.5Kb pGmZF392, which contains the binding site of GmERFA and GmNFYA, was significantly reduced when GmERFA was over‐expressed in tobacco leaves. Meanwhile, the LUC activity was upregulated by GmNFYA, and co‐expression of GmERFA disrupted this transcriptional activation (Figure 5g). To further investigate whether this interference effect was caused by the interaction between GmERFA and GmNFYA or resulted from the additive effects of their own binding ability, we constructed the reporter plasmid ~1.5 Kb pGmZF392:LUC. The ~1.5 Kb promoter of GmZF392 only contains the binding site of GmNFYA. Interestingly, the LUC activity driven by the ~1.5 Kb pGmZF392 was significantly increased by GmNFYA, while GmERFA exhibited no significant effect (Figure 5h). Co‐expression of GmERFA with GmNFYA completely blocked the activation ability of GmNFYA, indicating that GmERFA can exert its negative effect on GmZF392 through interaction with GmNFYA (Figure 5h). These results demonstrate that GmERFA suppresses the expression of GmZF392 by direct binding to its promoter and by inhibiting GmNFYA activation on the GmZF392 promoter through physical interaction.

Combined results from EMSA and ChIP‐qPCR demonstrated that GmNFYA directly bound to the P3 fragment (59 bp) of the GmZF392 promoter in vitro and was specifically recruited to this genomic region in vivo in GmNFYA‐GFP transgenic hairy roots (Figure 5i,j and Table S4). Meanwhile, the P3 probe of GmZF392 was also significantly enriched in GmERFA‐GFP soybean transgenic hairy roots (Figure 5j). Furthermore, EMSA analysis showed that GmERFA repressed the binding of GmNFYA to the P3 fragment of the GmZF392 promoter (Figure 5k). Therefore, we propose that GmERFA suppresses the transcriptional activation function of GmNFYA by impairing its DNA‐binding affinity to target promoter elements.

We further generated GmZF392‐RNAi soybean hairy roots to examine if the FA content is altered. The results showed a significant reduction in FA content in GmZF392‐RNAi hairy roots (Figure S5e,f), consistent with the positive role of GmZF392 in soybean oil accumulation (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021). Together, these results provide evidence that GmNFYA also directly binds to the promoter of GmZF392 for FA accumulation in collaboration with GmERFA.

We analysed the expression levels of GmERFA, GmNFYA, GmZF392 and GmbZIP123 at different stages (S1–S7) of soybean seed development. Compared to GmNFYA, GmZF392 and GmbZIP123, the expression of GmERFA sharply increased at the later maturation stages (S6–S7) of seed development (Figure S7). Therefore, we hypothesise that GmERFA may act as a brake gene at the later maturation stages of seed development to inhibit GmZF392 and GmbZIP123 expression for termination of oil biosynthesis, in collaboration with GmNFYA.

GmERFA Negatively Regulates Seed Soluble Sugar Content in Soybean

Since sucrose serves as the primary carbon source for FA synthesis in plants (Haslam et al. 2016), and the downstream gene GmbZIP123 of GmERFA is involved in regulating sugar transport (Song et al. 2013), we further examined the seed soluble sugar content in GmERFA‐OX soybean plants and gmerfa/b mutants. Compared to Jack and Null, a significant decrease in soluble sugar content in GmERFA‐OX soybean seeds was observed (Figure 6a). In contrast, the three gmerfa/b mutant lines exhibited marked increases of 40.1%, 10.4% and 16.7% in soluble sugar levels, respectively (Figure 6b). Moreover, we also measured the content of individual monosaccharides in soybean seeds. The results showed that the contents of fructose and glucose were substantially decreased in GmERFA‐OX soybean plants but largely increased in gmerfa/b mutants compared with the Jack control (Figure S8). Furthermore, the expression of sugar transport‐associated genes (GmSWEET10a, GmSWEET10b, GmSUC1 and GmSUC2) and glycolysis‐related genes (GmPK1 and GmENO2) was detected. Interestingly, these genes showed significant downregulation in the GmERFA‐OX plants but marked upregulation in the gmerfa/b mutants (Figure 6c,d). These results indicate that GmERFA may negatively regulate the sugar transport and glycolysis pathway, thereby reducing the supply of carbon sources and subsequently inhibiting FA accumulation in soybean seeds.

We further investigated whether GmERFA could control sugar transport genes and glycolysis genes directly. Within the promoter regions of GmSWEET10a, GmSWEET10b and GmENO2, we identified two probes (59 bp each), containing the GCC‐box (Table S4). EMSA analysis showed that GmERFA directly bound to the promoters of GmSWEET10a, GmSWEET10b and GmENO2 in vitro (Figure 6e–g). Moreover, the promoter regions of GmSWEET10a, GmSWEET10b and GmENO2 were also significantly enriched by GmERFA‐GFP in GmERFA‐GFP‐OX soybean transgenic hairy roots compared with control hairy roots (Figure 6h–j). Meanwhile, GmERFA directly suppressed the promoter activity of GmSWEET10a, GmSWEET10b and GmENO2 in a transient assay (Figure 6k–p). These results demonstrate that GmERFA suppresses the expression of GmSWEET10a, GmSWEET10b and GmENO2 by direct binding to their promoter fragments, thereby inhibiting sugar transport and glycolysis.

GmERFA Is Subjected to Selection During Domestication

The natural variation of the ERFA promoter was analysed through PCR amplification and sequencing of its ~2.5 Kb promoter region in 97 wild soybeans and 244 cultivated soybean accessions (Figure 7a and Table S5). Totally, 134 nucleotide differences were detected in 97 wild soybeans, and 81 nucleotide differences were detected in 244 cultivated soybeans (Figure 7a). Nucleotide diversity (π) calculated using MEGA7 revealed much higher genetic variation in wild soybeans (π = 0.004936) compared to cultivated soybeans (π = 0.001631), with the latter retaining approximately 33.04% of the variations observed in wild soybeans (Figure 7a). Tajima's D analysis for the promoter regions revealed that ERFA was likely subject to natural selection in wild soybeans and underwent artificial selection in cultivated soybeans (Figure 7a). These results suggest that the ERFA gene may be selected during soybean domestication.

The natural variation of the ERFA promoter was analysed, with its promoters classified into three haplotypes based on sequence variations (Figure 7b). In wild soybeans, 64.9% of the accessions were identified as Hap2. In cultivated soybeans, the major type is Hap3 (Figure 7c and Table S5). Phylogenetic analysis of all ERFA promoter sequences revealed that the three haplotypes clustered into distinct groups, with each haplotype forming a monophyletic cluster, strongly supporting the genetic divergence among these haplotypes (Figure 7d). These results indicate that the Hap3 type of the ERFA gene may undergo selection during the domestication of soybean.

We further quantified the expression of ERFA during the mid‐maturation stage of soybean seeds and performed association analysis between different haplotypes and expression levels. The correlation of the seed oil content and protein content with different haplotypes was also compared (Figure 7e–j). In wild soybeans, Hap1 correlates with high gene expression, low oil content but high protein content. In contrast, Hap3 correlates with low gene expression, high oil content but low protein content in seeds (Figure 7e–g). In cultivated soybeans, only Hap2 and Hap3 were identified. Hap2 correlates with slightly higher average ERFA expression, lower oil level (average 18.37%) but higher protein level. On the contrary, Hap3 is correlated with lower average ERFA expression level, higher oil level (average 20.27%) but lower protein level (Figure 7h–j). The Hap3 cultivars show an average increase of 10.34% in oil level compared to the Hap2 cultivars (Figure 7i). We also analysed the correlation between the three haplotypes and 100‐seed weight and found that Hap3 correlates with higher average seed weight in both cultivated and wild soybeans (Figure S9). These findings reveal that Hap3 of the ERFA gene is correlated with higher oil content, higher seed weight but lower protein content in soybean seeds.

Gene expression of ERFA was further compared between 82 wild soybean accessions and 183 cultivated accessions. A significantly lower ERFA expression was observed in cultivated soybeans compared to wild soybeans, largely correlating with the high oil in cultivated soybeans but low oil in wild soybeans (Figure 7k). The promoter activity of the three ERFA haplotypes was further investigated. The results showed that promoter activity decreased from Hap1 to Hap2 and to Hap3 (Figure 7l), which was largely consistent with the expression level of ERFA (Figure 7e,h). Collectively, these results indicated that the Hap3 type ERFA correlates with low promoter activity, low gene expression, but high seed oil content.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a new seed oil regulator GmERFA which acts as a transcriptional repressor for seed oil accumulation in soybean. The GmERFA interacts with GmNFYA and suppresses the activation effect of GmNFYA on GmbZIP123 and GmZF392. Meanwhile, GmERFA also directly binds to the promoters of GmbZIP123 and GmZF392 to inhibit their expression. Furthermore, GmERFA reduces the supply of carbon sources required for FA biosynthesis by directly targeting sugar transport and glycolysis pathways, thereby inhibiting oil accumulation (Figure 7m). The GmNFYA, GmbZIP123 and GmZF392 are all positive oil regulators (Figure 3 and Figure S5) (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021; Lu et al. 2016; Song et al. 2013). Our study reveals a novel module GmERFA‐GmNFYA‐GmbZIP123/GmZF392 for oil accumulation, providing promising targets of molecular design for high oil breeding in soybean.

During the soybean domestication, an increase in seed oil content is often accompanied by a decrease in protein, and in general, seed oil content is positively correlated with seed size/weight (Hu, Liu, Wei, et al. 2023). The lipid biosynthesis of plants is regulated by multiple factors (Wei et al. 2025), and only a limited number of studies have reported that AP2/ERF transcription factors are involved in this process. The WRI1, belonging to this type of transcription factor, has been extensively documented to enhance seed FA content in diverse plant species, including Arabidopsis, wheat, rice, maize and soybean (Baud et al. 2009; Duan et al. 2022; Shen et al. 2010; Snell et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2022). In the present study, GmERFA was identified as a negative regulator of oil accumulation in soybean. By editing GmERFA and its homologous gene GmERFB using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, the seed oil content can be enhanced by 4.4%–5.4% in soybeans, accompanied by a 5.3%–8.8% increase in 100‐seed weight (Figure 2). In GmERFA‐OX soybean plants, the seed protein displayed a compensatory increase by 4.4%–4.5% (Figure 1n). These findings demonstrate that GmERFA exhibits pleiotropic regulatory effects on lipid biosynthesis, protein accumulation and seed size/weight control. Genome editing of GmERFA using genetic methods, combined with traditional breeding, is expected to produce soybean accessions with high oil and high yield.

The NF‐Y network and sugar transport regulation have been shown to critically influence oil biosynthesis (Wei et al. 2025). The regulatory module GmNFYA‐GmZF392‐GmZF351 has been demonstrated to be of great significance in soybean oil biosynthesis in our previous study (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021). Our previous work also revealed that GmbZIP123 promoted the FA content by regulating sugar translocation via direct targeting to SUC and cwINV genes (Song et al. 2013). In the present study, we further proved that the GmNFYA, GmbZIP123 and GmZF392 are required for the increase of FA levels using a gene editing approach and/or transgenic hairy root assay (Figure 3 and Figure S5). Our study connects the GmERFA with the GmNFYA‐mediated pathway, establishing a novel molecular network GmERFA‐GmNFYA‐GmZF392/GmbZIP123 involving four transcription factors. It should be noted that while the GmERFA can achieve its functions through physical interaction with GmNFYA, it also directly inhibits the expression of hub genes GmbZIP123 and GmZF392, further affecting other downstream genes such as GmSWEET10a/b, GmSUC1/2, GmPK1, GmENO2, GmKASIII‐1/2/3, GmBCCP2, GmTAG1‐1/2, GmOLEO2‐1/2 and GmWRI1‐1/2, which are involved in processes from sugar transport, glycolysis, to fatty acid biosynthesis, TAG assembly and oil body formation.

During soybean seed development, oil accumulation initiates at the early maturation stage, reaches peak at the mid‐maturation stage, and gradually declines at the late maturation stage (Collakova et al. 2013). This decline suggests the existence of negative regulators acting as a brake for oil accumulation. Our findings reveal that to effectively implement this ‘braking’ of oil biosynthesis, it is essential not only to suppress oil synthesis‐related genes but also to reduce precursor availability by limiting sugar transport and inhibiting sugar metabolism (Figure 6). The present GmERFA expression showed an overall gradual upward trend and a drastic increase at the later stage of seed development (Figure 1d), consistent with its inhibitory roles in seed oil accumulation, possibly representing a brake gene for oil control. It should be noted that while the GmERFA showed an apparent increase at the later stage of seed development, the downstream genes GmbZIP123 and GmZF392 exhibited reduced and/or low expression (Figure S7), probably reflecting the suppressive role on the two gene expressions. It should be mentioned that although the positive oil regulator gene GmNFYA expression also showed a moderate increase, the highly expressed GmERFA proteins can interact with and inhibit GmNFYA activity, hence leading to a final downregulation of GmbZIP123 and a low expression of GmZF392 for oil regulation. The GmERFA gene is likely active primarily at the late maturation stages of soybean seed development, inhibiting oil accumulation and thereby facilitating the completion of the entire life cycle of soybean seeds. Considering that we have identified several transcription factors including positive regulators GmDOF4/11 (Wang et al. 2007), GmbZIP123 (Song et al. 2013), GmMYB73 (Liu et al. 2014), GmDREBL (Zhang et al. 2016), GmNFYA (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021), GmZF351 (Li, Lu, et al. 2017) and GmZF392 (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021), as well as the present negative regulator GmERFA, it is not clear why soybean needs multi‐factors for oil control. It is possible that these factors may work at different stages and/or processes singly or combinatorially for a proper source/sink allocation and a suitable oil/protein level, producing a seed for better propagation and survival to the environments. Actually, a few of these genes are also involved in abiotic stress tolerance (Lu, Wei, Tao, et al. 2021; Wei et al. 2023).

Modern cultivated soybeans are domesticated from the wild progenitor Glycine soja in China approximately 6000–9000 years ago, undergoing significant morphological and physiological changes (Zhou et al. 2015). Although soybean seed weight and seed oil content vary widely, cultivated soybeans generally produce larger seeds with higher oil content compared to wild soybeans (Kim et al. 2012). Several domesticated soybean genes that contribute to increased seed oil content have been identified, such as GmDREBL, GmZF351 and GmZF392 (Li, Lu, et al. 2017; Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2016). In this study, we found that GmERFA underwent strong selection during soybean domestication. Interestingly, the elite allele Hap3 of ERFA exhibits reduced promoter activity and gene expression but substantially elevated seed oil content and seed weight (Figure 7 and Figure S9). This allele may be subjected to positive selection during soybean domestication and became fixed in cultivated soybeans.

Although the present GmERFA interacts with and inhibits GmNFYA function to affect oil accumulation in the same pathway, their roles in seed size/weight control are different. The GmERFA‐OX transgenic soybean plants produce smaller seeds while its mutants produce larger seeds compared to the WT plants (Figures 1 and 2). In contrast, the GmNFYA‐OE transgenic soybean seeds (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021) and its mutant seeds are very similar to the WT seeds (Figure 3g and Figure S4d–f). The downstream gene GmZF392‐OX soybean plants showed no significant difference in seed size/weight compared to controls (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021). These facts imply that GmERFA may regulate seed size/weight via alternative pathways but not through the GmNFYA pathway. Further study may disclose the components and mechanisms involved.

In conclusion, we identified an AP2/ERF family transcription factor GmERFA, which negatively regulates oil accumulation in soybean seeds through the GmERFA‐GmNFYA‐GmZF392/GmbZIP123 regulatory pathway. The GmERFA interacts with GmNFYA to inhibit GmNFYA‐mediated transcriptional activation of positive oil regulator genes GmZF392 and GmbZIP123, leading to a reduction in oil level. The GmERFA can also directly suppress the expression of GmZF392 and GmbZIP123 for the inhibition of oil biosynthesis. Our study finds a brake gene for oil control. Manipulation of the gene, its elite allele and the whole regulatory pathway may facilitate high oil and/or high yield breeding for soybean and/or other legumes and crops.

Materials and Methods

Soybean Accessions Used in This Study

Fifteen soybean accessions were from the north‐eastern region of China, including 11‐1615, 15‐2293, KD48, KD57, C14‐546‐1, 17‐2220, 11‐1669, HN75, KN23, KN18, HF52, HN68, HN66, HF50 and BD5. Eighteen soybean accessions were from the Huang‐Huai‐Hai Region, including YD12, KX3, XJ2, MC6, JD38, Z92116, GY3, Z8904, SD288, JH13, QH33, ZH35, KD4, ZH45, 92–88, HB‐3, WX2 and M9449.

Plant Materials

Soybean plants were grown in pots/field plots in Beijing, China, from May to October, or grown in a greenhouse from December to May. The Arabidopsis thaliana and Nicotiana benthamiana plants were grown in a greenhouse at 22°C with the photoperiod of 16 h light/8 h dark.

Arabidopsis and Soybean Plant Transformation

The generation of GmERFA‐overexpression Arabidopsis plants was performed in Col‐0 through the floral dip method as described previously (Hu, Liu, Lu, et al. 2023). The CDS of GmERFA was inserted into the downstream of the CaMV 35S promoter in the pBI121 vector. The soybean cultivar Jack was used as a transformation plant for generating all of the mutants and transgenic soybeans. The GmERFA‐overexpressing soybean plants were generated from Bio Run Group (http://admin.biorun.com) by the Agrobacterium tumefaciens ‐mediated cotyledon node transformation method. The gmerfa/b mutants and gmnfya/a' mutants were generated by CRISPR/Cas9 in Wei Mi Group (http://www.wimibio.com). Soybean transgenic hairy roots were generated in soybean cultivar Kefeng1 via Agrobacterium rhizogene K599‐mediated transformation as previously described (Wei et al. 2023).

Library Screening in Yeast

The yeast library screening was performed using the Y2HGold‐GAL4 system with the yeast two‐hybrid screening library kit (Coolaber YH2010‐1T). The genes from various soybean organs, such as roots, leaves and seeds, were included in the cDNA library and used for the yeast library screening.

Yeast Two‐Hybrid Assay

A yeast two‐hybrid assay was performed using Yeast‐maker TM Yeast Transformation System 2 (Takara) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The fusion plasmids were co‐transformed into yeast strain AH109 cells.

Split‐Luciferase Complementation (Split‐LUC) Assay

The GmERFA‐nLUC and GmNFYA‐cLUC constructs were transfected into A. tumefaciens strain EHA105, and were further co‐infiltrated into tobacco leaves. LUC activity was analysed 48 h post‐infiltration using the Night‐SHADE LB 985 imaging system (Berthold, Black Forest, Germany).

Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BiFC) Assay

The CDS of GmERFA and GmNFYA was cloned into the pCAMBIA2300‐nYFP vector and pCAMBIA2300‐cYFP vector, respectively. All vectors were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 and further transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. Fluorescent signals were observed with a confocal laser‐scanning microscope (Zeiss; LSM710).

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) Assay

The Co‐IP assay was performed as described previously (Hu, Liu, Tao, et al. 2023) using the TNT® SP6 High‐Yield Wheat Germ Protein Expression System (Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The coding sequence of GmERFA was fused with the GFP gene in pF3 WG (BYDV) Flexi® Vector, and the CDS of GmNFYA was fused with the 3 × myc tag sequence in pF3 WG (BYDV) Flexi® Vector. The anti‐GFP (Chromotek) beads were used for the Co‐IP assay, and the anti‐myc antibody (Abmart) and anti‐GFP antibody (Abmart) were used for further detection.

RT‐qPCR Assay

The total RNA was extracted using the methods of TRIzol reagent (Ambion) for Arabidopsis tissues or CTAB buffer for soybean tissues. The SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara, Maebashi, Japan) and a Roche LightCycler 480 II were used for RT‐qPCR analysis. The gene expressions of GmTubulin (Glyma.17G258300) and AtActin (AT3G18780) were used as internal controls for soybean and Arabidopsis, respectively. The primers used in this study were listed in Table S6.

Subcellular Localisation

The GmERFA‐GFP and GmHY5‐RFP constructs were transfected into A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 and were co‐infiltrated into tobacco leaves. GmHY5‐RFP was used as a nucleus marker. Fluorescent signals were observed with a confocal laser‐scanning microscope (Zeiss; LSM710).

Measurement of Transcriptional Regulatory Activity

A transcriptional regulatory activity assay was performed in Arabidopsis protoplasts and Nicotiana benthamiana leaves as described previously (Bian et al. 2019). The Agrobacteria EHA105 harbouring the pCAMBIA2300‐GUS vector was used as the negative control for Nicotiana benthamiana. The Renilla LUC gene was used as the internal control for the Arabidopsis protoplast assay.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation‐Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (ChIP‐qPCR) Assay

The ChIP‐qPCR assay was performed as described previously (Hu, Liu, Tao, et al. 2023). Anti‐GFP (MBL, Tokyo, Japan) was used to immunoprecipitate the protein‐DNA complex. The qPCR was performed using the immunoprecipitated DNA fragments. The primers used in the qPCR assay were listed in Table S6.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA) Assay

The EMSA assay was performed as described previously (Hu, Liu, Lu, et al. 2023) using a lightshift chemiluminescent EMSA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Fatty Acid and Protein Content Measurement

The fatty acid content was measured as previously described (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021). The gas chromatography (GC) system (GC‐2014; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was used for FA analysis. The heptadecanoic acid (Sigma) was used as an internal control. The protein content was analysed with the Dumas combustion method. Total nitrogen was measured and then converted to total protein content (Miller et al. 2007).

Total Oil Content Measurement

The total oil content was measured as previously described (Yuan et al. 2024) with brief modification. Add 100 mg of dry seed powder to 650 μL of hexane and mix thoroughly. Rotate the mixture overnight in a shaking incubator at 37°C, followed by centrifugation. The total lipid content was determined by calculating the weight change of the centrifuge tube and normalising it to the initial weight of the seed powder.

Soluble Sugar Content Measurement

The soluble sugar content was measured as previously described (Lu, Wei, Li, et al. 2021). The thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method was used to measure soluble sugar content.

Individual Soluble Sugar Components Measurement

The individual soluble sugar content was measured as previously described with brief modification (Fiehn et al. 2000). Approximately 6 mg of freeze‐dried soybean seeds were used for extraction. The concentrations of metabolites were calculated using the internal standard method and normalised to the unit dry weight of the samples.
