Investigating industrial by-product for soil conditioning addressing environmental risk and waste reduction alternatives
Authors: Eli Syafiqah Aziman, Aznan Fazli Ismail, Siti Fatimah Jubri, Amalina Abd Rahman, Khairiah Rosli, Wan Mohd Razi Idris, Sahibin Abd Rahim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Condisoil is an innovative soil conditioner formulated from industrial by-products specifically Water Leach Purification and Neutralisation Underflow residues derived from rare-earth processing. This study investigates its potential to improve sandy soils for paddy cultivation, a vital sector for Malaysia’s food security. Unlike conventional soil amendments, Condisoil promotes sustainability by repurposing industrial by-products and addressing disposal challenges. However, due to its origin, assessing elemental distribution and soil-to-rice grain transfer factors is essential to ensure its agricultural safety. Activity concentrations of naturally occurring radionuclides (NORM) 226Ra, 228Ra, and 40K in Condisoil-treated soils ranged from 66.5 to 143.1, 49.7 to 70.6, and 391.6 to 432.2 Bq/kg, respectively, over two paddy planting seasons. In rice grains, NORM levels were significantly lower, ranging from 0.93 to 9.20 (226Ra), 0.03 to 0.53 (228Ra), and 89.14 to 147.95 (40K) Bq/kg. Heavy metal As, Sb, Cr, Cd, and Pb concentrations in soils remained below Malaysian safety standards, while rice grains contained in the range of 0.2–1.4, 0.7–3.0, 0.1–0.1, and 0.5–1.8 mg/kg, respectively. TF values for radionuclides and heavy metals ranged from 0.00 to 0.00–0.30 and 0.01–1.0, respectively. The estimated annual effective dose from rice consumption remained below the 1.0 mSv/year safety threshold. While the uptake of most elements was minimal, lead (Pb) and antimony (Sb) exhibited HI values > 1, and the TF value of cadmium (Cd) was > 1, indicating potential health risks. Therefore, environmental monitoring is essential before large-scale application. This study highlights Condisoil’s potential in sustainable agriculture and waste valorization, though strict heavy metal monitoring protocols are necessary to ensure long-term food safety when using industrial by-products as soil conditioners.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-14864-6.

Introduction

Global demand for rare earth elements (REEs) has led to a significant expansion of rare earth (RE) mining areas and an influx of settlers into these regions1. However, the increased development of RE mineral extraction has resulted in the generation of large volumes of radioactive waste with high thorium content, raising local social and environmental concerns2,3. One of the world’s largest rare-earth refineries is located in Malaysia, producing high-quality REE materials for the global market. However, its operations also generate substantial amounts of radioactive waste, particularly Water Leach Purification (WLP) residue, with approximately 450,000 tonnes accumulated between 2012 and 20184. Additionally, the plant produces 1145 tonnes per day of Neutralisation Underflow (NUF) residue, a non-radioactive byproduct, with over 1.11 million tonnes already deposited in landfills5. These figures underscore the growing challenges of waste storage and management for both WLP and NUF residues, as their continuous accumulation presents significant logistical and environmental concerns.

The WLP residue is classified as very low-level radioactive waste (VLLW)2, while NUF is categorized as conventional industrial by-products6. One advantage of NUF and WLP is their high gypsum (CaSO4·2H2O) content7–10, which enhances soil aeration and water percolation while serving as a partially soluble source of essential nutrients, particularly calcium and sulfur, both of which promote plant growth11,12. Condisoil is a nutrient-rich soil conditioner formulated by mixing WLP, NUF, and organic fillers. It consists of 10% WLP, 20% NUF, and 70% organic matter and is believed to contain natural radionuclide concentrations below the regulatory limit of 1000 Bq/kg. This threshold is approved by the relevant authorities for use, and this product is exempted from licensing. According to Hanafi et al.9, Condisoil has been shown to improve soil conditions by creating a favorable ecosystem for nutrient absorption in plant roots. This led to increased cumulative fresh yield in crops such as sweet corn (Zea mays L.), kenaf (Hibiscus cannabinus L.), guinea grass (Panicum maximum Jacq.), green dwarf coconut (Cocos nucifera L.), and teak wood (Tectona grandis)9,13. Therefore, repurposing WLP and NUF as soil conditioners offers a viable solution for reducing rare earth processing residues while enhancing plant growth. This approach presents a mutual benefit for both the agricultural and REE industries.

The core principle of radioactive waste treatment focuses on removing, reducing, or modifying contaminants14. In rare earth (RE) industries, radioactive residues are typically stored in monitored, restricted-access facilities or regulated tailing ponds, often located near production sites. However, limitations in storage space, overcrowded facilities, and the large volume of accumulated waste pose significant challenges. These issues, coupled with public perception and political considerations, are key drivers for countries to explore alternative methods for the disposal or reduction of thorium waste in the RE sector. Consequently, the development of sustainable and efficient waste treatment methods is essential to effectively manage and minimize the growing accumulation of radioactive waste. In pursuit of this, our previous study demonstrated that using carbon electrode techniques for thorium removal reduced WLP residue by up to 40%14–16. Beyond thorium extraction, repurposing WLP and NUF waste as soil conditioners presents a potential alternative for reducing waste volume. However, previous studies indicate that WLP contains a high thorium concentration of 6727.64 Bq/kg, while NUF contains trace amounts of heavy metals, which could pose potential environmental risks10.

The uptake of a radionuclide or heavy elements in the human food chain will depend on the element’s contents in the environment (soil. bedrock, etc.). Thus, the environmental risk posed by the WLP and NUF residue must first be critically investigated, specifically when used to treat crop-growing-agricultural soil. Though promising in the aspect of waste management, the application of industrial by-product as an agricultural booster material must first be carefully studied and discussed to attain the optimum residue repurposing method while maximizing crop yield, industrial and consumer safety.

This paper aims to conduct an experimental study on the uptake of natural radionuclides and heavy metals by paddy plants (Oryza sativa) in relation to their exposure to Condisoil-treated soil to assess the potential implications for food safety. In earlier studies, Condisoil was applied to crops unrelated to Malaysia’s primary food source9,13. However, paddy is essential for rice production in Malaysia, the country’s primary staple food. Given the potential of Condisoil to enhance crop yields by improving soil properties, its use as a soil conditioner could support sustainable rice production, thereby preserving food security and ensuring the continued availability of rice as a key staple food. A special emphasis was placed on the soil-to-plant transfer of the naturally occurring radionuclide found in Condisoil. The findings have been reviewed in light of applicable legal legislation regarding the possible health consequences of human rice consumption. Apart from providing information on the possible effects of agricultural use of Condisoil, this study also provided a conceptual design for reducing the amount of ready-to-dispose WLP residue. Material and method

Description of the study area and sample collection

Three locations were chosen as study sites for the experiment, as shown in Fig. 1. Alor Pudak (AP), Kedah; Merbok (MB), Kedah; and Penaga (PN), Pulau Pinang, Malaysia. Previous studies reported that Condisoil is hypothesized to improve crop soil structure and nutrition9,13. Thus, these three locations were chosen for this study due to soil subsidence and high soil acidity levels, as the application of Condisoil may improve soil structure. The chemical properties of Condisoil were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Bruker XRD D8 Advance system, while its elemental composition was assessed through gamma spectrometry and Instrumental Neutron Activation Analysis (INAA). During land preparation, a mix of fertilizer NPK and Condisoil is incorporated into the soil through broadcasting, where it is evenly spread across the field, often before flooding. Around 900 kg of Condisoil was added to every hectare of the paddy cultivation area. The field is then flooded with water to create optimal conditions for paddy planting. The paddy plantation for this study was conducted over two seasons, with sample collection divided accordingly. In the first season, the soil remained untreated with Condisoil, serving as the control condition. In contrast, the second season involved soil that had been treated with Condisoil to assess its effects.

During the span of the two seasons, a total of 350 soil samples were taken from the three study locations. The sampling points and sample description for each location are shown in Fig. 1 and Table 1. The samples were collected at a depth of 5–15 cm below surface level placed in a polystyrene container. In addition, an approximate weight of 3–5 kg of paddy plant were also collected from every point. Characterization of soil properties

Soil samples were analyzed for particle-size distribution (sand, silt, and clay) and soil chemical properties were assessed, including pH (H2O) and plant nutrient contents (Ca, Mg, K, and P). A detailed explanation of the characterization of soil analysis was provided in our previous study9,13.

Analytical methods

Natural radioactivity analysis

The soil and rice grain samples were dried until a constant mass was achieved, then pulverized using a motorized grinder and sieved through a 500 μm mesh prior to analysis. All solid samples were packed, sealed, and stored in an airtight acrylic counting container for 30 days prior to counting to achieve secular equilibrium with the parent radionuclides (238U and 232Th) and their progenies; the parent and progeny activities are identical, allowing them to decay with half the parent nuclide’s activity. Each sample was triplicated, prepared, packed, and repeated three times in accordance with IAEA guidelines17.

Samples were counted for 12 h using a high-purity germanium (HPGe) gamma spectrometer. To determine the activity concentrations of radionuclides, specific high-energy gamma lines were selected based on their emission intensity, spectral clarity, and association with the respective decay series. The 1764.5 keV gamma line from 214Bi (a daughter of 226Ra in the 238U series) was used as a marker for 226Ra due to its moderate intensity (~ 15.3%) and minimal spectral interference18,19. For 228Ra (from the 232Th series), the 2614.5 keV gamma line from 208Tl was selected for its high emission probability (~ 99%) and spectral distinctiveness18,19. The 1460.8 keV gamma-ray line is a prominent peak in gamma spectrometry arising from the decay of naturally occurring potassium-40 (40K), a radionuclide that constitutes approximately 0.0117% of natural potassium18,19. These peaks were chosen to ensure accurate quantification, especially under conditions where lower-energy peaks may be obscured. As the samples were in secular equilibrium, this approach is consistent with previous studies that validate the use of the 1764 keV, 2614 keV, and 1460 keV lines for determining the activity concentrations of 226Ra, 228Ra, and 40K, respectively20,21.

To ensure detector accuracy, the detector was calibrated using a certified multi-nuclide source consisting of 22Na, 57Co, 60Co, and 137Cs. The HPGe detector (GC3018) used had a 30% relative efficiency and a resolution of 1.8 keV at 1.33 MeV, where the photo peaks were analyzed using the Genie-2000 computer program (Canberra Inc). The detector’s detection limit (DL) and minimum observable activity (MDA) for energy peaks of 1764 keV, 2614 keV, and 1460 keV are (67 ± 0.5, 86 ± 4 and 112 ± 2 Bq/kg) and (6.2 ± 0.1, 5.3 ± 0.1 and 22.1 ± 0.2 Bq/kg) respectively. In this analysis, certified reference materials IAEA Soil-375 were used.226Ra (238U series), 228Ra (232Th series), and 40K activity concentrations were calculated using the equation previously stated in our report20,21. The activity concentrations obtained were later used to assess and discuss the radiological impact to the environment in Section ‘Radiological impact assessment of consumption rice grain samples due natural radionuclide after applying Condisoil”.

Rad toolbox

The Radiation Toolbox or Rad toolbox v 3.0.0 provides ready to access to physical, biological and radiological data of interest in radiation protection of worker and members of the public22. This software was for U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission by Oak Ridge National Laboratory. The data include dose coefficients for intakes of radionuclides, exposure to radionuclides distributed in the environment, and for exposures to photon and neutron radiation fields. The Radiation Toolbox were used to estimate the annual organ equivalent dose due to the ingestion of the food crops for better understanding of the translocation of radionuclides in body and estimating the stochastic effects to specific organs. The calculations are based on committed equivalent dose coefficient to organs of ICRP.

Heavy metal concentration

Heavy metal concentration was determined concurrently using the comparative method by irradiating the standard reference material (IAEA Soil-7) and soil samples in the same neutron flux and enumerating them geometrically. Approximately 0.15–0.20 g homogenized sediment samples and IAEA Soil-7 were weighed into polyethylene vials and sealed with heating solder to prepare for the irradiation process23. Prior to the irradiation, a mixed standard solution was prepared subjected to reference material for rice sample in neutron activation analysis (NAA). About 0.2 g of the mix standard solution was dropped onto filter papers and dried in an oven at 50 °C for 24 h. Later the filter paper was placed in polyethylene vial and seal by heating solder.

Along with the standard reference material, the samples were irradiated for 6 h in the Malaysian TRIGA Mark II reactor’s rotary rack facility at the Malaysian Nuclear Agency. The reactor’s default operating power and the thermal flux of neutrons was 750 kW and roughly 1012 cm−2 s−1, respectively. All NAA measurements were performed using the HPGe detector (GC3018). The specimens were measured over a range of cooling periods, from two days to to four weeks with a maximum distance of six centimetres from the detector. A summary of the irradiation and counting procedure is presented in Table S1 (please refer supplementary material).

The use of NAA in this study was limited by its reliance on delayed detection, which, while reducing immediate dead-time effects, significantly increased the time required for sample analysis. This extended counting time hindered analytical throughput and efficiency. To overcome these limitations and enable faster, more sensitive quantification of heavy metals, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) was employed as a complementary technique. Soil samples were digested using a wet acid digestion method with a nitric acid to perchloric acid ratio of 3:19,13. The resulting solutions were analyzed using a Perkin Elmer ELAN 9000 ICP-MS, providing rapid, multi-element detection with high sensitivity and low detection limits.

Result and discussion

Characterization of Condisoil and soil properties

Figure S1 (please refer supplementary) illuminates the XRD spectra of the Condisoil, in which the presence of other peaks corresponding to the gypsum compound according to the crystallography Open Data (COD) card No. 96-101-1075 was clearly visible aside from the soil conditioner. Thus, the presence of gypsum indicated by XRD analysis shows that Condisoil is suitable for use in agriculture which is consistent with earlier findings by Hanafi et al.9.

The application of Condisoil a had notable effect on the chemical and physical properties of soils across the three study locations: Alor Pudak (AP), Merbok (MB), and Penaga (PN). One of the most significant outcomes was the marked increase in available phosphorus (P) concentration in all soils post-treatment. In Alor Pudak, available P more than doubled (from 1.29 ± 0.3 to 2.99 ± 0.29 mg/kg), while in Merbok, it increased more than fourfold (from 0.77 ± 0.19 to 3.20 ± 1.09 mg/kg). Penaga showed a similar trend, with available P tripling (from 0.95 to 2.92 mg/kg). These results suggest that Condisoil effectively enhanced P availability, likely through improved nutrient solubility, reduced fixation by Fe/Al oxides, or increased microbial activity that promotes P mineralization24,25.

Interestingly, this increase in phosphorus occurred despite relatively minor changes in soil pH. The pH remained in the acidic range (between 3.69 and 4.12) across all locations, with only slight fluctuations observed after treatment. This implies that the improved P availability may not be solely due to pH modification, but rather the Condisoil may have provided buffering effects or included organic matter that facilitated P release or mobility. In terms of exchangeable cations, the effects were more variable. Exchangeable potassium (K) generally increased or remained stable post-treatment. The most notable improvement was seen in Merbok, where K nearly doubled (0.56–1.09 cmol⁺/kg), potentially alleviating K deficiencies and enhancing nutrient balance. Conversely, exchangeable calcium (Ca) and magnesium (Mg) showed slight decreases in Alor Pudak and Merbok but increased in Penaga. These fluctuations may indicate cation displacement or redistribution as part of the nutrient release mechanism of the Condisoil. The increases observed in Penaga suggest that the Condisoil may contribute to cation enrichment under certain soil conditions24,25.

Changes in soil texture components (sand, silt, clay) were also observed, with slight increases in finer particles (silt and clay) in most cases. This could be attributed to improved soil aggregation and the binding of finer particles facilitated by the organic or humic components of the Condisoil26,27. Enhanced soil structure would promote better water retention and nutrient-holding capacity, indirectly contributing to greater nutrient availability. Overall, the soil conditioner demonstrated a positive effect on soil fertility by significantly increasing phosphorus availability and improving cation balance, particularly potassium. These changes, combined with improved physical characteristics, suggest that the conditioner has strong potential as a soil amendment in acidic tropical soils with limited nutrient availability.

Based on Table 2, Condisoil shows the same potential to improve soil properties as other soil conditioners derived from industrial by-product. The use of different soil conditioners depends on the specific soil properties of the studied location. Some soil types require amendments to increase pH levels, while others benefit more from enhanced phosphorus (P) availability, as demonstrated in the work by da Costa Leite et al.28, where a mixture of 25% bauxite residue (BR) and 75% palm oil compost (POC) significantly increased P availability. This improvement led to a growth response in Brachiaria grass, highlighting the importance of tailored soil amendments based on specific nutrient limitations and crop requirements. Such findings reinforce the role of organic-industrial by-product blends in sustainable soil management practices. The activity concentration ranges before applying Condisoil for 226Ra, 228Ra and 40K in the first season were 55.6–150.9, 44.8–74.3 and 374.8–481.9 Bqkg−1 while for the second season when applied as a soil conditioner, they were 60.6–155.5, 41.9–76.1 and 357.9–517.0 Bqkg−1, respectively. The results indicated that all soil samples treatment with Condisoil possesses an activity concentration slightly higher than the average soil for Malaysia but still within the paddy cultivation soil range previously reported (refer Table 3).

Statistical analysis via the ANOVA test shows that the use of Condisoil for the first seasons (control condition without Condisoil) and second seasons (applying Condisoil) of paddy cultivation indicated no increase in NOR concentration in the environment, as the p-values were > 0.05, leading the study to conclude that the application of Condisoil did not result in an increase in natural radionuclides in the soil over the study period.

A vital component in assessing the impact towards both the environmental and end consumer is to study the transfer factors (TFs) as early preparations and treatments of any agricultural product ultimately affect the end products. TFs is mathematically defined as the ratio of the radionuclide of interest concentration in plant parts (Bq/kg, dry weight) to the elements of interest concentration in the soil41.

Table 8 summarizes the calculated TFs of the radionuclides and heavy metals. In this investigation, we observed that the radionuclide, As, Cr and Pb heavy metal transfer factor value reported in prior studies was higher than the transfer factor discovered in this study. However, the TF value for cadmium (Cd) and antimony (Sb) in this study offer critical insights into the uptake potential of these toxic elements in rice cultivated in Condisoil-treated soils. For cadmium (Cd), TF values reached 1.0 at both Alor Pudak and Merbok, while a lower value of 0.5 was recorded at Penaga. The results of this study indicate that rice plants grown in paddy fields treated with Condisoil have a low capacity for uptake and translocation of radionuclides. Significant impact production “Condisoil” towards handling industrial by-product

As more and more rare-earth metals are produced daily, the quantity of radioactive residue would follow suit, growing as the years go by as concerns and negative perceptions amongst locals began to aggrandize over time2,66. While the national economy benefits from the labour of the industry, it is in the local community’s best interest to ensure the public continues to accept and embrace the rare-earth processing technology: any considerable reduction in public acceptability of the technology may have a detrimental impact on the government’s policies2,3. At current stage Malaysia government urgency for Malaysia to find a solution for the massive volume of VLLW radioactive waste management4. In light of increasing public concern regarding the environmental and health impacts of radioactive waste from rare-earth (RE) processing, the Malaysian government has introduced stricter regulatory conditions for industry operations. A key requirement for future licence applications is the mandatory separation or extraction of thorium to prevent the generation of radioactive waste at processing facilities67–69. Pervious works conducted on the electrosorption-based remediation of thorium from WLP residue has indicated a reduction of approximately 40% in the waste residue volume. The concept of reusing WLP residue is to separate thorium from WLP for thorium potential application as a nuclear fuel in Generation-IV nuclear reactors. Besides, the lack of global deployment of a thorium reactor, coupled with the restricted separation and purification of thorium to laboratory-scale operations, has necessitated the alternative ways like Condisoil product to solve the massive radioactive volume. Therefore, it is imperative to identify the best option to either treat or repurpose the residues produced while concurrently expanding the Malaysian RE business.

Each country planning to reuse this industrial by-product must establish standardized operating procedures (SOPs) specific to rare earth (RE) activities, as this Condisoil was generated from byproduct RE industries and is regulated to ensure the safety of both environmental and human health. For example, the United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) establishes detailed regulations under the Part 503 Rule, which defines acceptable limits for heavy metals in biosolids utilized in land application70. In a similar manner, the European Union has set forth standards under Directive 86/278/EEC regarding environmental protection in the agricultural use of sewage sludge, which encompasses maximum permissible concentrations for heavy metals in both the soil and the sludge71. The findings of the study suggest that the application of Condisoil does not contribute to elevated levels of natural radionuclides. However, there is a minor potential for the introduction of heavy metals into the environment. Although the radiological impact on human health appears negligible, ongoing toxicological monitoring is recommended over extended periods to comprehensively assess long-term exposure risks. As mentioned before, NUF and WLP’s gypsum properties in Condisoil can be used as an alternative to conventional fertilizers to supply Ca, Mg and/or S, soil conditioner and/or liming agent in ameliorating deficiencies resulting from soil acidity conditions. Condisoil is currently applied to paddy crops with acidic soil issues. If Condisoil were developed and widely used as an alternative to conventional fertilizers as it is anticipated to deliver nutrients to plants, more rare earth process residue would be utilized to manufacture Condisoil, which could mutually benefit both the agricultural and the REE industries. Thus, the plausible conclusion that could be drawn from this work paves the way for a possible alternative for lowering the volume of processed rare-earth residue especially radioactive residue WLP if Condisoil is utilized as an alternative fertilizer.

Given the potential risks associated with heavy metal accumulation in agricultural systems, this study emphasizes the importance of implementing stringent monitoring protocols and standardized operating procedures (SOPs) for the large-scale application of soil amendments such as Condisoil. Although such amendments may offer benefits for soil remediation or fertility, their use must be accompanied by government-enforced guidelines to ensure that toxic metal levels remain within safe limits. Without adequate oversight, there is a risk that heavy metals may enter the food chain, posing significant health concerns to consumers. Therefore, the establishment of comprehensive regulatory frameworks is essential to mitigate these risks and protect public health.

Conclusion

The study’s findings indicate that the concentration activity of 226Ra, 228Ra, and 40K Condisoil-treated soil is slightly above average soil concentration in Malaysia, however, the concentration of heavy metal (As, Sb, Cr, Cd, Pb) and the natural radionuclide activity are equivalent to prior research concentrations. The results of the analysis also indicated an absence of any raise in natural radionuclides in Condisoil-treated soil during the whole study period. The determination of the activity concentrations of heavy metal and natural radionuclides in the rice grains revealed that their values were equivalent to those reported in earlier research for various types of plants.

Impact assessment revealed that the average annual effective dose (AEDE) from rice grains consumption for all age groups did not exceed the recommended limit 1.0 mSv/y. The uptake of most elements was relatively low. Nevertheless, the hazard index (HI) values for lead (Pb) and antimony (Sb) exceeded the threshold of 1, indicating a potential health risk associated with the consumption of the rice grain. Conversely, cadmium (Cd) demonstrated a transfer factor (TF) value greater than 1, suggesting a higher propensity for translocation from soil to grain. Despite the lower TF values observed for Pb and Sb, their elevated target hazard quotient (THQ) and HI values underscore the need for continued monitoring, as their presence in rice may pose significant health concerns for consumers.

Thus, this study suggests the impact heavy metal intake of rice from studied location should be closely monitored. To mitigate long-term radiological and toxicological exposure to human populations in large-scale applications, the use of Condisoil is recommended in low-exposure zones such as forested areas, grasslands, or highway green belts, where the likelihood of direct human consumption pathways is minimal.

The use of Condisoil for several more years could simultaneously minimize the volume of waste generated by the use of WLP in agriculture. The use of WLP for agricultural purposes may reduce the volume of WLP residue by up to 74% per year which is estimated in this study. In conclusion, the anticipated reduction in rare-earth residue volume should boost favorable attitudes and perceptions about Malaysia’s rare-earth business, which could mutually benefit both the agricultural and the REE industries in the near future. This study underscores the critical need for government-mandated heavy metal monitoring protocols and standardized operating procedures (SOPs) in the large-scale application of Condisoil to ensure consumer safety and minimize potential health risks.
