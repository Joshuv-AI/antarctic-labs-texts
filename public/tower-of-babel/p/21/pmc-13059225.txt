Ultrasound-activated piezoelectric hydrogel promotes functional muscle repair by orchestrating myogenesis and reinnervation
Authors: Yangbao Lyu, Fang Wang, Haihan Gao, Erpeng Yang, Liren Wang, Zaijin Tao, Yang Xiao, Qifu Yang, Yuhan Jiang, Yuming Zhou, Liuqing Yang, Wen Gong, Ke Wang, Fangzhou Yao, Xuanyong Liu, Jiajun Qiu, Xin Ma, Jia Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVolumetric muscle loss (VML) is a debilitating condition characterized by traumatic tissue loss and a subsequent failure of regeneration, resulting in permanent structural and functional deficits. The microenvironment of VML is characterized by a loss of regenerative cues and supportive infrastructure, which disrupts the coordinated cellular processes of muscle differentiation and nerve integration. Electrical stimulation is a potential therapeutic intervention for VML. Here, we developed a wireless electrotherapy strategy using an injectable, conductive, and piezoelectric KOCC hydrogel for the functional recovery of VML. The hydrogel was engineered by incorporating lead-free piezoelectric (K, Na) NbO₃ (KNN) nanoparticles into a dynamically crosslinked network of oxidized sodium alginate and chitosan hydrochloride and calcium ion. Upon exposure to ultrasound (US), the KOCC hydrogel generated controllable, localized electrical fields without the need for implanted electrodes or external wire connections. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that US-stimulated electrical signaling promoted myogenic differentiation, enhanced nerve regeneration and neuromuscular junction formation, and reduced excessive inflammation and fibrosis, which ultimately led to functional improvement in a murine VML model. Introduction

Volumetric muscle loss (VML) constitutes a major clinical problem involving the extensive loss of skeletal muscle tissue due to serious trauma or surgical operations [1]. Following mild injury, skeletal muscle effectively harnesses its resident satellite cell population to facilitate regeneration. In contrast, VML establishes a sustained inflammatory and fibrotic milieu that disrupts myogenic differentiation, resulting in fibrotic deposition rather than functional muscle regeneration [2]. Current standard management of VML primarily relies on autologous muscle transplantation. However, this approach not only fails to induce functional recovery, but also frequently leads to complications such as fibrotic scarring and secondary damage to the donor site. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop novel biological therapies to promote effective muscle regeneration.

Electrical stimulation is a potential therapeutic intervention for VML. Electrical stimulation has demonstrated efficacy across various injury models [3–7]. Much evidence confirms its capacity to enhance cell migration, stimulate growth factor secretion, facilitate nerve ingrowth, promote angiogenesis, and modulate immune microenvironments [8]. Mechanistically, electrical stimulation could activate membrane receptors and modulate ion transport, thus triggering downstream key signaling pathways that orchestrate the tissue repair process [8]. Within skeletal muscle, it has been reported that electrical stimulation could actively direct satellite cells towards myogenic lineages in animal models [9, 10]. Also, electrical stimulation has proven efficacy in promoting neuromuscular junction formation in vitro and in vivo [9, 11]. However, conventional external electrical stimulation requires the wire connection and electrode installation, which increases the risk of tissue injury and inflammation. Furthermore, the external application of electrical stimulation cannot provide uniform activation of the deep target area due to the signal attenuation by surrounding tissues. As an alternative strategy, conductive biomaterials have been used to harness native bioelectric fields, which has shown efficacy in animal models of VML [12–14]. Yet, conductive biomaterials cannot provide active, controllable electrical stimulation, thereby limiting their therapeutic efficacy.

The use of piezoelectric nanoparticles represents a biocompatible strategy of precise electrical stimulation for VML. Non-centrosymmetric crystals exhibit piezoelectricity via stress-induced polarization and charge separation, converting mechanical stress directly into electrical output. For example, (K, Na)NbO3 (KNN) is typical lead-free piezoelectric inorganic particle that has demonstrated exceptional biocompatibility and piezoelectric performance [15]. Some studies utilize inherent physiological mechanics to trigger piezoelectric nanoparticles for the repair of load-bearing tissue such as bone and cartilage [5, 16–18]. By contrast, ultrasound (US)-mediated stimulation offers superior flexibility and broader applicability. Therefore, US-triggered piezoelectric particles represent a highly promising strategy for VML regeneration therapy.

Beyond electrical stimulation, biomimetic scaffolds that reconstruct the muscle microenvironment are important for overcoming VML-induced or other tissue destruction [19–21]. Despite the prevalence of decellularized extracellular matrix scaffolds in the VML preclinical treatment, synthetic hydrogel provides critical advantages, including tunable mechanical/structural properties, batch-to-batch consistency, and reduced immunogenicity risk [22, 23]. Additionally, engineered hydrogels with injectability and self-healing capabilities offer optimal solutions for repairing complex volumetric muscle defects. Critically, synthetic hydrogels can be further engineered to possess conductivity, thereby synergizing endogenous bioelectric cues with active piezoelectric stimulation [24]. Therefore, an integrated strategy that combines the on-demand electrical stimulation from piezoelectric materials with the tunable, injectable, and conductive hydrogel scaffold holds great promise for effectively rebuilding the VML microenvironment and facilitating functional muscle regeneration.

In this study, we developed a wireless electrotherapy strategy based on an injectable and self-healing piezoelectric KNN-oxidized sodium alginate (OSA)-chitosan hydrochloride (CHC)-Ca2+ (KOCC) hydrogel for functional muscle regeneration. This hydrogel was constructed by incorporating biocompatible KNN nanoparticles into a dynamically crosslinked network of oxidized sodium alginate, chitosan hydrochloride and calcium ion (Fig. 1A). The ultrasound-stimulated in situ electrical stimulation demonstrated the ability to influence cellular behavior by creating a pro-regenerative bioelectrical microenvironment. It activated calcium-NFAT signaling pathway in myoblasts and promotes myogenic differentiation, which is impaired in the microenvironment of VML. In the murine VML model, the treatment demonstrated a capacity to promote myofiber formation, nerve ingrowth, neuromuscular junctions regeneration, and resolve detrimental inflammation, leading to both structural and functional recovery of the injured muscle (Fig. 1B). Our findings validate this ultrasound-activated piezoelectric hydrogel as a promising strategy for VML treatment by orchestrating myogenesis and reinnervation.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of KOCC composite hydrogels

OSA was synthesized as previously reported. Briefly, sodium alginate (5 g; Bidepharm, China) was dispersed in 25 mL of ethanol, and potassium metaperiodate (2.5 g) was dissolved in 25 mL of deionized water. This periodate solution was then added slowly to the sodium alginate solution, and the mixture was stirred continuously for 6 h at room temperature. To quench the oxidation reaction, 5 mL of ethylene glycol was added to the solution, and stirring was continued for another 30 min. The resulting solution was purified by dialysis against deionized water for 72 h using a membrane with a molecular weight cut-off of 14,000 Da. Finally, the oxidized alginate solution was frozen and lyophilized. The success of the periodate oxidation and the formation of OSA were confirmed by ¹H NMR analysis, which indicated an oxidation degree between 40% and 50%.For hydrogel preparation, OSA was dissolved in deionized water at a final concentration of 10% (w/v) and magnetically stirred for 1 h until complete dissolution. Separately, chitosan hydrochloride (CHC; Bidepharm, China) and sodium chloride (NaCl) were co-dissolved in deionized water to achieve final concentrations of 6% (w/v) and 3% (w/v), respectively. OC hydrogels were prepared by mixing the OSA and CHC solutions at a 1:1 ratio. To this OC hydrogel, 30 mM CaCl₂ was added to form the OCC hydrogel. KNN nanoparticles were prepared as previously reported [25]. 2% and 4% KNN was added to the OCC hydrogel, denoted as KOCC2 and KOCC4. The KOCC2 hydrogel was used for cell and animal experiments.

Sample characterization

Characterization of the synthesized KNN nanoparticles was performed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU8010), transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-F200). The FTIR spectrometer (Tensor 27, Bruker, Germany) was utilized to identify the absorption peaks of the hydrogels in the range of 400–4000 cm− 1. The surface morphology of the different hydrogels was obtained using SEM (Nova NanoSEM 230, FEI, USA). The X-ray diffraction patterns of the KNN nanoparticle and KOCC hydrogel were collected using a diffractometer (D2PHASE, Bruker, USA).

Rheological measurements

The rheological evaluation of the KOCC hydrogel was conducted on an rheometer (). Samples were tested at 25°C by a 20-mm parallel plate geometry. A frequency sweep from 0.1 to 10 Hz was applied to measure the storage (G’) and loss (G”) moduli. The gel-sol transition behavior was examined through a strain amplitude sweep from 1% to 2500%. To assess the self-healing property, the moduli were monitored over five cycles of alternating low (1%) and high (1600%) strain.

Electrical conductivity performance

To assess the electrical conduction properties of the KOCC hydrogel, measurements were performed at 25 °C with a conductivity meter (914 pH/DO/Conductometer, Metrohm, Switzerland).

Piezoelectric performance

The piezoelectric performance of KNN nanoparticle was assessed using a piezoelectric force microscope (PFM, Jupiter XR, OXFORD INSTRUMENTS, UK). The conductive probe tip was scanned under a bias voltage range of -100 V to + 100 V. The piezoelectric performance of the lyophilized KOCC hydrogel was assessed using PFM (MFP-3D, OXFORD INSTRUMENTS, UK), and the conductive probe tip was scanned under a bias voltage range of -25 V to + 25 V.

Ultrasound-responsive electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical behaviors of different hydrogels were characterized by an electrochemical workstation (PGSTAT302N, Metrohm Autolab, Switzerland). The electrochemical measurements were conducted in a standard three-electrode cell, where a platinum foil and an Ag/AgCl electrode functioned as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. The Dulbecco’s phosphate buffer solution (DPBS) was used as the electrolyte. Electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were acquired from 0.1 Hz to 1000 kHz with a 10 mV sinusoidal signal, with and without ultrasound irradiation. The hydrogel’s current-time (I-t) responses were measured at 30 s intervals on a 1 cm² sample with ultrasound device (UT-1021, Nu-Tek, China; parameter: 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle).

Culture of C2C12 myoblasts

C2C12 myoblasts are maintained in growth medium consisting of high-glucose DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. For routine maintenance, passage the cells at a 1:6 ratio every two days when they reach 30–50% confluence to prevent spontaneous differentiation. For co-culture conditions, the respective hydrogel was positioned inside a Transwell insert placed in the culture well, with the C2C12 cells growing on the well surface below the insert.

Biocompatibility assay

The live/dead staining (C2015S, Beyotime) assays were performed to examine the biocompatibility of the OCC and KOCC hydrogel combined with US or not 24 h and 48 h after treatment.

Scratch assay

Culture C2C12 cells in 12-well plate until cells reach around 90% confluence. Remove growth medium, gently wash wells twice with pre-warmed PBS, then create uniform linear scratches using a sterile 200-µL pipette held perpendicular to the plate surface. After scratching, wash wells twice with PBS to remove dislodged cells and immediately replace with DMEM containing 1% FBS and 1% P/S. Capture initial (0 h) reference images and the final (24 h) images. The plate was subjected to ultrasound treatment or not at 0 h, 8 h, 16 h after scratch.

Myogenic differentiation of C2C12 cells

To induce differentiation, replace the growth medium with myogenic differentiation medium containing DMEM and 2% horse serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) when cells reach 90–100% confluence. Change the differentiation medium every 24 h. The experimental groups are listed as follows: (1) the control group cocultured with no hydrogel ; (2) the OCC group cocultured with the OCC hydrogel; (3) the KOCC group cocultured with the KOCC hydrogel; (4) the OCC + US group cocultured with the OCC hydrogel and subjected to ultrasound; (5) the KOCC + US group cocultured with the KOCC hydrogel and subjected to ultrasound. Ultrasound (1 MHz, 0.1 W cm− 2, 20% duty cycle) was performed for 5 min once every single day started from the first day of differentiation. For NFAT inhibition, 2 µM of Cyclosporin A (MedChemExpress) was added to the differentiation medium.

Immunofluorescence staining of cell

Fix the samples with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min. Wash the specimens three times with PBS. Permeabilize the cells with 0.3% triton for 10 min, followed by three times of PBS washes. A rapid blocking buffer (Beyotime) is used to block non-specific binding sites for 10 min. Incubate with the anti-MyHC (1:100, DSHB), anti-MYOG (1:50, DSHB), anti-NFATC1 (1:50, Santa Cruz) primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The next day, wash the plate three times with PBS. Apply the fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibody and incubate for 45 min at room temperature, protected from light. Wash the plate three times with PBS. Stain the nuclei with DAPI. Store the plate in the dark at 4 °C until imaging.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from samples using Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) via a standard chloroform-isopropanol precipitation method. The quantified RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA with the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan). Subsequent gene expression analysis was carried out by quantitative PCR (qPCR) on a StepOnePlusTM system (Applied Biosystems, USA) using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Japan). The relative expression levels of target genes were normalized to Gapdh mRNA for analysis. The primers used for RT-qPCR were listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Real-time intracellular calcium imaging

Fluo-4 AM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to characterize the intracellular calcium concentration change under the influence of US-stimulated current. After washing with HBSS, C2C12 cells were incubated with 1 μm Fluo-4 AM solution for 20 min. Ultrasound was then applied through a signal generator powered ultrasonic transducer. A fluorescence microscope was used to record the change of the fluorescence intensity before and after ultrasound treatment. At least 10s were recorded for the baseline intensity calculation.

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital (Animal Experiment Registration number: DWSY2025-0079). C57BL/6 mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). The volumetric muscle loss model was modified from a previously reported method. Briefly, mice aged 9 to 10 weeks were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium. After shaving and disinfecting, a longitudinal incision was made in the skin from the knee joint to the ankle joint using a size 11 blade scalpel. The underlying fascia was carefully elevated with forceps and incised longitudinally to expose the full width of the TA. The TA muscle was surgically separated with the scalpel. A full-thickness defect was created by two overlapping 2-mm biopsy punch (Weiling, China) ablations around the middle of the TA. The hydrogel (approximately 15 µl) was injected from the needle to fill the muscle defect area. Finally, the skin was sutured with a 6 − 0 suture using a continuous stitch pattern. The study comprised the following experimental groups: (1) the control group transplanted with no hydrogel; (2) the OCC group transplanted with the OCC hydrogel; (3) the KOCC group transplanted with the KOCC hydrogel; (4) the OCC + US group transplanted with the OCC hydrogel and treated with ultrasound; (5) the KOCC + US group transplanted with the KOCC hydrogel and treated with ultrasound. The mouse received different treatments beginning 3 days post injury. Ultrasound (1 MHz, 0.2 W cm− 2, 20% duty cycle) was performed for 5 min once every other day. The ultrasound parameters were chosen based on established protocols in the literature [4] and were optimized to minimize potential non-specific bioeffects from the ultrasound itself.

RNA sequencing

The experimental cohort for transcriptomic analysis comprised mice receiving OCC hydrogel with ultrasound stimulation (OCC + US) as the control group, and mice receiving KOCC hydrogel with ultrasound stimulation (KOCC + US) as the experimental group. TA muscle samples were harvested from these two groups at 14 days post-injury. Total RNA was extracted from the muscle tissue using TRIzol reagent, followed by mRNA enrichment with oligo(dT) magnetic beads. The purified mRNA was fragmented into approximately 300 nucleotide segments using fragmentation buffer. These fragments were reverse transcribed into cDNA, which was subsequently ligated with adapters and amplified through PCR to construct sequencing libraries. The libraries were subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina platform. Bioinformatic processing included principal component analysis, differential expression analysis with volcano plots, and functional characterization through Gene Ontology enrichment analysis, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway mapping, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA).

Histological and Immunofluorescence staining of tissue

Mice were euthanized with 1% pentobarbital sodium, and TA muscle were harvested at 7- and 28-day post-injury. For H&E and Masson’s staining, tissue was fixed with muscle-specific fixative (Servicebio) for 12 h. The tissue specimens underwent sequential dehydration followed by paraffin embedding. Serial sections were cut at a thickness of 4 μm using a microtome. Histological evaluation was carried out through H&E staining and Masson’s staining according to established laboratory protocols. For immunofluorescence staining, TA muscle was embedded in OCT and frozen in pre-cooled isopentane. The specimens were then processed using a cryostat to obtain 10-µm cryosections. The sections were preserved in -80 degree until use. Retrieve the muscle frozen sections and let them thaw to room temperature. Fix the sections with 4% PFA for 10 min. Wash the slides three times using PBS. Permeabilize the tissues with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min, followed by three times of PBS washes. Block the sections using a rapid blocking buffer for 10 min. Incubate with the anti-laminin (1:200, Sigma-Aldrich), anti-PGP9.5 (1:800, Huabio), anti-iNOS (1:200, Huabio), anti-CD206 (1:600, Huabio) primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The next day, wash the slides three times with PBS. Apply the secondary antibody and incubate for one hour at room temperature, protected from light. Wash the slides three times with PBS. The NMJ structure was staining with CF®594 -conjugated α-Bungarotoxin (Biotium). DAPI is used to stain the nuclei. The sections were sealed with an anti-fade mounting medium under a coverslip. Store the slides in the dark at 4 °C until imaging.

Gait analysis

Gait analysis were performed using VisuGait system (Xinruan, Shanghai). The gait was recorded when the mouse steadily walk across the narrow beam. For each mouse, at least three segments of uninterrupted walking were recorded and analyzed using the software provided with the VisuGait imaging system.

Hindlimb grip strength assessment

Grip strength meter was used for grip strength assessment. Mouse was put over the grid with its hindlimb grasping the grid. While keeping the mouse’s torso parallel to the grid, gently pull the animal backward by its tail, away from the grid. For each mouse, seven times of peak force were recorded. The weight of mouse was also recorded for normalization.

Image analysis

All images were analyzed using Fiji software and Cellpose with standardized quantification pipelines. For the scratch assay, percentage of migration area was quantified as [(T0 area – T24 area)/T0 area] × 100 from phase-contrast images; MYOG⁺ nuclei percentage calculated as (MYOG⁺DAPI⁺ nuclei/Total DAPI⁺ nuclei) × 100; The fusion index was defined as the percentage of nuclei residing within MyHC-positive myotubes that contained more than two nuclei; The newly form myofibers were identified by centrally nucleated fiber in the laminin-staining section. The ROIs of centrally nucleated fiber were automatically segmented by Cellpose-SAM and further processed in ImageJ to calculate the average area; Fibrotic area percentage was calculated from Masson’s trichrome sections via color deconvolution thresholding. PGP9.5+ positive area were delineated by adjusting the threshold range and calculated for percentage. The number of iNOS+ or CD206+ cells and the NMJ were manually measured by experienced researchers.

Statistics

All data collected in the study are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way and Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Multiple group comparisons following ANOVA will be performed using Turkey’s test or Dunnett’s test dependent on the experimental design. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001, ns, not significantly different indicate statistical significance.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of KOCC hydrogels

KNN nanoparticles were prepared following a reported method [25]. Imaging analysis by both scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that the KNN nanoparticles had a cubic morphology and an average grain size of around 300 nm (Fig. 2A and B, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Further elemental mapping via Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) verified that the constituent elements (including K, Na, Nb, O, and Bi) were homogeneously distributed throughout the KNN nanoparticles (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 1B). The fabrication of the KNN-oxidized sodium alginate (OSA)-chitosan hydrochloride (CHC)-Ca2+ (KOCC) hydrogel is illustrated in Fig. 1A. First, an injectable and adhesive hydrogel was developed through crosslinking of OSA, CHC and Ca2+. OSA is a chemically modified polysaccharide derivative generated by periodate oxidation of sodium alginate, which introduces aldehyde groups (-CHO) that form dynamic Schiff bases with amino-containing polymers for self-healing hydrogel networks [26]. CHC is a water-soluble cationic polysaccharide obtained by protonation of chitosan, providing abundant protonated amino groups (-NH₃⁺) that enable electrostatic complexation with anionic polymers and enhanced bio-adhesion through mucosal charge interactions [27]. To prevent excessive ionic crosslinking and enhance hydrogel homogeneity, sodium chloride (NaCl) was pre-dissolved in the chitosan hydrochloride solution prior to its addition to the OSA solution [28].

The OCC hydrogel network is expected to be stabilized by a combination of dynamic covalent bonds and robust physical interactions, which collectively contribute to its injectability, self-healing, and mechanical properties. The first crosslinking mechanism is the formation of dynamic Schiff bases between the -CHO on the OSA chains and the -NH₂ on CHC. Concurrently, strong electrostatic interactions occur between the protonated ammonium groups (-NH₃⁺) of the cationic CHC and the carboxylate groups (-COO⁻) remaining on the OSA backbone. Furthermore, the abundance of hydroxyl groups (-OH) from both OSA and CHC, along with amino and aldehyde groups, facilitates the formation of an extensive hydrogen-bonding network throughout the matrix. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to verify the proposed crosslinking mechanisms within the OCC hydrogel (Fig. 2D). The formation of dynamic Schiff base bonds was confirmed by key spectral changes including the disappearance of the characteristic aldehyde peak of OSA at 1739 cm-¹ upon reaction with CHC, along with significant attenuation of the peak at 1518 cm-¹ corresponding to the symmetric bending vibration of -NH₃⁺ in CHC [29]. Concurrently, a new peak emerged at 1652 cm-¹ in the OC spectrum, which is attributed to the C = N stretch in Schiff bases [29]. The asymmetric stretching vibration of carboxylate groups shifted from 1601 cm-¹ in pure OSA to 1598 cm-¹ in the OC hydrogel, suggesting enhanced electrostatic interaction with ammonium groups. The broad hydroxyl band in the 3200–3500 cm-¹ region also exhibited noticeable broadening, indicating the formation of a more extensive hydrogen-bonding network. The incorporation of Ca²⁺ ions was found to further modify the network structure. In the final OCC hydrogel, the carboxylate asymmetric stretching peak (at 1596 cm-¹) showed an additional shift from its position (at 1598 cm-¹) in OC, confirming the chelation of Ca²⁺ ions. A slight shift of the broad hydroxyl band to higher wavenumber was also observed, likely resulting from the interplay between ionic crosslinks and hydrogen bonds. Taken together, these FTIR spectral changes confirm the synergistic crosslinking via Schiff base reactions, electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and ion-coordination interactions.

With successful establishment of crosslinked OCC hydrogel, we then incorporated KNN nanoparticles to render the hydrogel piezoelectric. We performed x-ray diffraction (XRD) to validate the successful incorporation of KNN nanoparticles (Fig. 2E). The XRD patterns of KOCC demonstrated distinct peaks at 2θ = 22.2°, 31.6°, 45.3°and 56.3°, which correspond to the characteristic peaks of KNN nanoparticles. The macroscopic appearance of hydrogels and injectable property are shown in Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. 2.

The microscopic morphology of the different hydrogels was observed by SEM (Fig. 2G). The SEM image of the OC hydrogel revealed a three-dimensional network with irregular and loss pores. Upon the introduction of Ca2+ ions, the OCC hydrogel exhibited a noticeably more uniform and denser porous architecture compared to OC. This significant structural change can be attributed to the additional ‘egg-box’ crosslinks formed by Ca²⁺ with the guluronate blocks of alginate. The KOCC hydrogel with 2% KNN maintained the interconnected porous structure, and the KNN particles were predominantly well-dispersed and embedded within the pore walls. Further increasing the KNN content to 4% resulted in a markedly altered morphology. Large KNN particle aggregation and less homogeneous pore structure were observed, suggesting that high concentration of KNN nanoparticles may compromise the structure of the hydrogel. Therefore, hydrogel with 2% KNN was used for subsequent study.

Rheological characterization confirmed the mechanical properties and dynamic nature of the KOCC hydrogel. As shown in Fig. 2G, in the frequency sweep test (0.1–10 Hz), the storage modulus (G’) consistently exceeded the loss modulus (G’’), demonstrating a solid-like gel state. The strain amplitude sweep (Fig. 2H) revealed a critical strain of approximately 1499%, beyond which G’ and G’’ crossover, indicating a gel-to-sol transition due to network breakdown. Reversible sol–gel transition and structural restoration under cyclic loading (1%-1600% strain) confirm the self-healing property of the KOCC hydrogel. (Fig. 2I). The KOCC hydrogel exhibited a conductivity of 2.05 S m-¹, which is on the same order of magnitude as human muscles [30].

Ultrasound-stimulated electrical stimulation of KOCC hydrogels

The piezoelectric property of KOCC hydrogel was evaluated using piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM). As shown in Figs. 2A, B, distinct variations in the PFM phase and amplitude were observed across the KOCC hydrogel surface, indicating the presence of piezoelectric domains. Figure 2C, D, present the characteristic amplitude-to-voltage curves and phase hysteresis loop of the KOCC hydrogel. A significant displacement approaching 160 pm is observed under an applied electric field, confirming a pronounced piezoelectric effect. The presence of a characteristic phase hysteresis loop with a contrast of approximately 175° provides direct evidence for polarization switching.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) reveals a significantly enhanced charge transfer capability in the KOCC + US sample. The Nyquist plot (Fig. 2E) exhibits a mildly smaller impedance arc, and the Bode plot (Fig. 2F) shows a phase peak shifted to a higher frequency compared to KOCC alone. Consistent with these observations, equivalent circuit fitting indicates that the charge transfer resistance (Rct) drops from 4.317 × 103 Ω in KOCC to 3.519 × 10³ Ω in KOCC + US. These results collectively demonstrate that ultrasonic treatment endows KOCC with lower internal impedance and faster, ultrasound-responsive charge transfer. The ultrasound-stimulated current density change is demonstrated in Fig. 3G, H. The current density of KOCC exhibited synchronous oscillation with the five on/off cycles of US stimulation. Moreover, the overall current density showed a consistent increase with either higher US intensity or a greater concentration of KNN nanoparticles. The schematic illustration of electrochemical assessment is demonstrated in Fig. 3I.

Ultrasound-stimulated KOCC hydrogels promote myoblast migration and differentiation in vitro

After activation and proliferation, the myoblasts need to effectively migrate into the wound bed and subsequently undergo myogenic differentiation to participate in the muscle defect healing. Defects in muscle progenitor migration and fusion are a critical characteristic of degenerative VML [31, 32], which leads to impaired myogenesis and reinnervation. Therefore, we first investigated whether US-activated KOCC could promote C2C12 cells migration in vitro by scratch assay. The cells were assigned to the following five treatment conditions: (1) control (untreated); (2) OCC coculture; (3) KOCC coculture; (4) OCC coculture with US stimulation; (5) KOCC coculture with US stimulation. All treatment conditions exhibited good biocompatibility (Supplementary Fig. 3). After 24 h of migration and intermittent intervention, the KOCC + US group demonstrated significantly greater cell migration compared to the control group, while other interventions including non-piezoelectric OCC hydrogel and US treatment showed no significant improvement (Fig. 4A, B). Although the ultrasound-activated alternating current from KOCC may not fully recapitulate the endogenous electric fields present in wounds, our results demonstrate that it enhances C2C12 migration. This is consistent with previous reports that alternating current can promote cell migration in other cells [33].

We further explored whether our piezoelectric hydrogel could enhance myogenic differentiation in vitro. C2C12 myoblasts co-cultured with hydrogel were induced to differentiate and treated with US daily or not. Immunofluorescent staining of MYOG and MyHC were performed to characterize early and late myogenic differentiation, respectively. On day 3 of differentiation, significantly higher percentage of MYOG+ positive nucleus was found in the KOCC + US group relative to other groups, indicating an enhanced commitment of myoblasts to the myogenic lineage (Fig. 4C, D). On day 5 of differentiation, MyHC staining and subsequent morphometric analysis revealed that the KOCC + US treatment significantly promoted myotube maturation. The fusion index and average myotube length were markedly increased, which was not observed in any other treatment conditions (Fig. 4E-G). In addition, the KOCC + US treatment led to a significant increase in the average nuclei per myotube over the control group, and a marginal increase over the OCC + US group (p = 0.0517) (Fig. 4H). In line with this, mRNA quantification by RT-qPCR on day 3 demonstrated a significant upregulation of key myogenic regulatory genes, including Myog, Mef2c and Myf5) (Fig. 4I, Supplementary Fig. 4). Our findings are consistent with prior study that alternating electric fields can enhance the myogenic differentiation of human mesenchymal stem cells [34]. A recent study by Duan et al. reported that US stimulation alone can promote the differentiation of C2C12 myoblasts [35]. This finding highlights the potential bioeffects of US itself. However, much lower treatment duration and frequency were used in our current study while reaching comparable results. Taken together, these findings indicate that the US-activated piezoelectric hydrogel promotes myoblast differentiation in vitro.

Mechanisms underlying piezoelectric signaling-driven differentiation of C2C12 myoblasts

We subsequently investigated the mechanism by which the piezoelectric hydrogel promotes myogenesis. Given that calcium signaling is a vital coordinator of skeletal muscle differentiation and could be modulated by electrical stimulation [36], we examined the intracellular calcium levels of C2C12 cells upon hydrogel and US treatment using real-time fluorescence imaging. The observed significant increase in calcium fluorescence intensity indicates that the piezoelectric stimulation from the KOCC hydrogel under US triggered the opening of Ca2+-associated membrane receptors and subsequent calcium influx (Fig. 5A, B, D). A key downstream effector of calcium signaling in myogenesis is the transcription factor NFATC1. On day 2 of differentiation, C2C12 cells co-cultured with hydrogel were subjected to US stimulation. Forty minutes post-stimulation, the cells were fixed and harvested for immunostaining to assess the subcellular localization of NFATC1. A marked increase in NFATC1 nuclear/cytoplasmic (N/C) ratio was observed in the KOCC + US group relative to the control conditions (Fig. 5C, E). This result is consistent with reported findings that electrical stimulation promotes NFATC1 nuclear translocation in C2C12 cells. Since the calcium-calcineurin axis serves as the primary upstream regulator of NFATC1, the nuclear translocation of NFATC1 is most likely induced by calcium influx under piezoelectric stimulation. The nuclear translocation of NFATC1 drives the expression of genes critical for muscle development or regeneration, including MYOG, MYOD, and specific myosin heavy chains both in human and mouse [37–41]. Thus, the significant increases in fusion index and myotube length we reported can be directly attributed to this NFATC1-driven genetic program. To functionally validate the necessity of this pathway, we inhibited NFAT signaling using cyclosporin A. The treatment significantly reversed the upregulation of key myogenic genes (Myog and Myf5) induced by KOCC + US (Fig. 5F), confirming the central role of the calcium-NFAT axis in mediating the pro-differentiation effect. The US stimulation alone could not significantly trigger calcium influx or NFAT nuclear translocation in our study. This contrasts with a recent report demonstrating ultrasound-induced calcium signaling [42], possibly due to their use of higher-frequency and higher-intensity focused US. Taken together, these findings implicate the calcium-NFAT pathway as the mechanism for piezoelectricity-driven myogenesis, providing a molecular basis for further investigation into wireless bioelectronic modulation.

Ultrasound-stimulated KOCC hydrogels promote early myofiber formation and accelerate inflammation resolution in VML

The above in vitro experiments have shown that ultrasound-stimulated KOCC hydrogel promotes calcium influx and accelerates the migration and differentiation of myoblasts. To evaluate the therapeutic effect of our ultrasound-responsive hydrogel in promoting muscle regeneration in vivo, we performed muscle injury by creating two consecutive, full-thickness, 2-mm biopsy punch holes through the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle in mice as previously reported (Supplementary Fig. 5) [43]. This method successfully resulted in approximately 22% muscle loss in our preliminary studies (Supplementary Fig. 6). The animals were divided into five groups: (1) the control group (VML only); (2) the OCC group; (3) KOCC group; (4) OCC + US group; (5) KOCC + US group. Following VML model establishment, either OCC or KOCC hydrogel was injected into the defect site according to group assignment. The OCC + US group and the KOCC + US group were subjected to 5-min US treatment every other day, beginning on the postoperative day 3. TA muscles harvested at 7 days post-injury underwent immunostaining to assess early regeneration. The KOCC + US group exhibited regenerating myofibers with the largest average cross-sectional area (CSA), characterized by the presence of central nuclei (Fig. 6A, C). This result demonstrates that the combined treatment with piezoelectric hydrogel and US accelerates early muscle regeneration. Since piezoelectric stimulation has been shown to mitigate excessive inflammation in other tissue [44], we therefore hypothesized that our KOCC + US treatment would modulate the early inflammatory phase. A marked reduction in the density of iNOS-positive cells was observed in the KOCC + US group compared to the control and OCC + US group, as quantified by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 6B, D). Notably, despite the marked reduction in pro-inflammatory signals, the KOCC + US group showed no concomitant increase in CD206+ M2-like macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 7). The observed reduction in iNOS+ cells is critical, as persistent inflammation is known to impair muscle progenitor differentiation [45], thereby facilitating the subsequent recovery process. Future research could utilize ultrasound or specific nanoprobes to enable real-time, non-invasive monitoring of muscle regeneration processes in vivo [46, 47]. In summary, our data indicate that the KOCC + US combined therapy synergistically promotes myofiber regeneration while mitigating detrimental inflammation during the early stages of repair.

To further confirm and explore the in vivo effect of the KNN-loaded conductive hydrogel, we performed RNA-seq at 14 days post-injury between the OCC + US group and KOCC + US group. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation between the KOCC + US and OCC + US groups along the first principal component (PC1), which accounted for 61.61% of the total transcriptional variance (Fig. 6E). Differential expression analysis yielded 956 significantly dysregulated genes (p < 0.05, |log₂FC| > 1), with 468 upregulated and 488 downregulated, as visualized in the volcano plot (Fig. 6F). We further performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway mapping, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (Fig. 6G-K, Supplementary Fig. 8). Functional analysis collectively demonstrated a significant suppression of pro-inflammatory signaling. This was evidenced by the enrichment of relevant GO terms (e.g., inflammatory response, cellular response to chemokine) and KEGG pathways (e.g., NF-κB, TNF, IL-17 signaling), and further confirmed by the marked downregulation of these pathways in GSEA. This molecular profile supports the anti-inflammatory effect observed in histological analysis. Furthermore, GO enrichment of “response to calcium ion” and GSEA upregulation of the “calcium signaling pathway” collectively demonstrate enhanced calcium-mediated signaling. This aligns with our in vitro evidence that ultrasound-activated hydrogel induces calcium influx. Significantly, GO enrichment terms (e.g., “muscle system process”, “muscle contraction”, “striated muscle tissue development”) combined with GSEA enrichment of the “Cardiac muscle contraction” pathway demonstrate profound transcriptional alterations in muscle biology-related genes. Therefore, this transcriptomic data demonstrates that piezoelectric hydrogel treatment induces pro-regenerative transcriptional program involving suppressed inflammation, activated calcium signaling, and enhanced muscle development.

Ultrasound-stimulated KOCC hydrogels promote late structure and functional recovery in VML

Given the enhanced early regeneration and inflammation resolution mediated by the ultrasound-activated KOCC hydrogel, we further evaluated its long-term therapeutic potential to facilitate architectural restoration and reinnervation [48]. Four weeks post-hydrogel implantation, the harvested muscle specimens were weighed and processed for histological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome (Fig. 7A). The percentage of fibrotic area was quantified from Masson’s trichrome-stained sections. In terms of muscle mass restoration, only the KOCC + US group exhibited significantly greater relative TA muscle weight than the control group (Supplementary Fig. 9). Regarding fibrosis, although the OCC, KOCC, and OCC + US groups all resulted in a modest reduction in the fibrotic area compared to the control, the KOCC + US treatment produced the most substantial effect, achieving a level of fibrosis significantly lower than that of all other groups (Fig. 7C).

Functional muscle recovery depends critically on the reinnervation by motor neurons [49, 50]. Axon ingrowth and Acetylcholine receptors (AChR) were visualized with PGP9.5 immunostaining and fluorophore-conjugated α-bungarotoxin (α-BTX) respectively in muscle cryosections (Fig. 7B). The KOCC + US group exhibited a higher count and greater density of AChR clusters within the regenerated muscle than all other groups (control, OCC, KOCC, and OCC + US) (Fig. 7D). In addition, the KOCC + US group displayed the largest PGP9.5-positive axon area (Fig. 7E). We also quantified the fraction of innervated AChR clusters (defined as α-BTX⁺ cluster within 2 μm of nerve terminal [51]), in which the KOCC + US group achieved significantly higher values than all other groups (Supplementary Fig. 10). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the US-activated piezoelectric hydrogel attenuated the fibrotic remodeling after VML wounds, and facilitated histological NMJ formation, thereby providing a critical foundation for muscle function restoration.

To evaluate restoration of neuromuscular function following VML injury and treatment, we performed gait analysis and hindlimb grip strength testing four weeks after hydrogel implantation and US treatment. The schematic diagram of gait recording platform and footprint acquisition are presented in Fig. 8A, B. Representative footprints from each group are shown in Fig. 8C. In untreated controls, VML-induced TA dysfunction resulted in significant gait abnormalities. The KOCC + US group significantly improved the stride length relative to the control group, indicating the recovery of locomotor function; this improvement was not observed with KOCC hydrogel or US treatment alone (Fig. 8D). The TA muscles regulate ankle dorsiflexion and are critical for maintaining the braking phase of gait. Treatment with KOCC hydrogel combined with US restored the proportion of the braking phase relative to the total stance phase after VML injuries (Fig. 8E). No significant changes in brake time or stand duration were observed between the KOCC + US and control groups (Supplementary Fig. 11). A non-significant trend toward decreased stride width was observed in the KOCC + US group relative to the control group (Fig. 8F). Notably, as increased stride width is a known compensatory mechanism for gait instability, this trend may reflect an improvement in locomotor control [52]. Grip strength was measured at 2 weeks and 4 weeks post-injury and were normalized to body mass (Fig. 8G). Untreated control group only retained 56% and 67% of uninjured muscle at 2 weeks and 4 weeks post-injury, respectively. The KOCC + US group exhibited the highest relative grip strength at 2 weeks and 4 weeks post-injury (72% and 92% of uninjured muscle, respectively), which was significantly greater than that of all other groups. Although grip strength in the OCC, KOCC, and OCC + US groups were marginally higher than controls at 4 weeks post-injury, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Taken together, these results establish that only the synergistic combination of KOCC hydrogel with US stimulation produces significant functional recovery, achieving near-complete restoration of grip strength and measurable gait improvement.

Conclusion

Collectively, this study establishes an ultrasound-responsive electrotherapy strategy for volumetric muscle loss using an injectable, self-healing, conductive, and piezoelectric KOCC hydrogel. US-responsive KOCC hydrogel effectively promotes myogenic differentiation of C2C12 myoblasts in vitro. Mechanistically, the US-responsive KOCC hydrogel may promote myogenic differentiation through calcium-NFAT signaling pathway. In a murine VML model, ultrasound-activated electrical signals from the hydrogel successfully promote myofiber and nerve regeneration, resolving excessive inflammation and fibrosis, and restoring neuromuscular junctions. In addition to structural recovery, mice treated with the KOCC hydrogel and US combination therapy showed significant functional improvement, as assessed by gait analysis and grip strength tests. The strategy holds significant potential for clinical translation by providing a minimally invasive and effective solution for functional muscle regeneration.
