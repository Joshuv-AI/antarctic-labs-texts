Perceived knowledge, attitudes, and practices of healthcare professionals toward the use of noninvasive neuromodulation technology in the treatment of cognitive disorders
Authors: Min Cai, Shuai Zhao, Baojun Wu, Ge Dang, Junyao Li, Wenyuan Liu, Wenhao Zhang, Yi Guo, Jianhua Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to assess healthcare professionals’ perceived knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) toward the use of noninvasive neuromodulation technology as an adjunctive treatment for cognitive disorders.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted at The First Affiliated Hospital of Henan Medical University, enrolling healthcare professionals from multiple centers from April 2025 to August 2025. KAP scores were measured using a structured questionnaire. Factors associated with practice were identified using multivariable logistic regression. A path analysis within a structural equation modeling (SEM) framework, incorporating mediation effects, was conducted to explore the interrelationships among the KAP dimensions.ResultsA total of 479 valid responses were included. The mean perceived knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were 5.36 ± 5.10 (possible maximum: 16; 33.50% of maximum score), 21.32 ± 3.25 (possible maximum: 25; 85.28% of maximum score), and 20.05 ± 4.07 (possible maximum: 25; 80.20% of maximum score), respectively. Both perceived knowledge (OR = 1.284, 95% CI: 1.156–1.427, P < 0.001) and attitude (OR = 2.635, 95% CI: 2.180–3.186, P < 0.001) were independently associated with practice. SEM analysis revealed that perceived knowledge exerted a direct positive effect on attitude (β = 0.489, P = 0.012) and practice (β = 0.261, P = 0.009), while attitude directly influenced practice (β = 0.642, P = 0.005). Additionally, perceived knowledge indirectly affected practice through its influence on attitude (β = 0.314, P = 0.008).ConclusionAlthough healthcare professionals demonstrated positive attitudes and practices toward noninvasive neuromodulation technology, their level of perceived knowledge remained insufficient. Targeted educational and training initiatives are warranted to enhance knowledge, which may further strengthen attitudes and practices related to this emerging therapeutic approach.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08224-5.Keywords: Knowledge, attitude, and practice; Healthcare professionals; Noninvasive neuromodulation; Adjunctive therapy; Cognitive disorders

Background

The global aging population has led to a growing prevalence of cognitive disorders, ranging from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (which frequently represents an early, at-risk stage) to advanced Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and other dementias, which significantly compromise quality of life in older adults and pose substantial challenges for healthcare systems worldwide [1–3]. Pharmacological treatments, including FDA-approved acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine) and NMDA receptor antagonists (memantine), offer only modest symptomatic benefits and do not meaningfully alter disease progression [4–6]. The widespread use of psychotropic medications, such as antipsychotics and antidepressants, for behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia is hampered by limited efficacy and a risk of adverse effects in older patients [7]. These limitations highlight the urgent need for adjunctive therapies that are both safe and clinically effective [6].

Noninvasive neuromodulation technologies—including transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) [8, 9], transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) [10–12], and vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) [8, 13]—have emerged as promising adjunctive interventions for cognitive disorders. These techniques are characterized by favorable safety profiles, repeatability, and the potential to enhance neuroplasticity. A meta-analysis of 21 randomized controlled trials involving 806 patients with MCI or AD demonstrated that TMS significantly improved cognitive function compared with sham stimulation, with both short-term and sustained benefits (> 4 weeks follow-up). Notably, patients with mild to moderate cognitive impairment experienced greater improvements than those with severe deficits, and combining cognitive training with TMS did not provide additional benefit [9].

Knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) studies are widely used in public health to systematically assess what individuals know (perceived knowledge), how they feel (attitude), and what they do (practice) regarding a specific health topic. KAP assessments can guide the design of interventions to improve professional behavior and ultimately enhance patient outcomes [14–16]. Despite accumulating evidence supporting the use of noninvasive neuromodulation for cognitive disorders, few studies have examined healthcare professionals’ KAP regarding these interventions. Most existing research focuses on efficacy or technological advances, leaving a gap in understanding real-world awareness, acceptance, and usage in clinical practice [17, 18]. Identifying gaps, misconceptions, and barriers in healthcare professionals’ perceived knowledge and attitudes is critical to developing effective educational and implementation strategies [14, 15]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate healthcare professionals’ perceived knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding the use of noninvasive neuromodulation technologies as adjunctive treatments for cognitive disorders, providing evidence to inform future training, dissemination, and intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted at The First Affiliated Hospital of Henan Medical University and enrolled healthcare professionals from multiple centers from April 2025 to August 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan Medical University (formerly the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinxiang Medical University, Henan, China; approval number: EC-025-645). As this study did not involve any human intervention or identifiable private information, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the committee.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) valid professional qualification as a licensed physician or healthcare professional; (2) working at a position related to cognitive disorders or having experience with noninvasive neuromodulation technologies; and (3) voluntary participation.

Procedures

An investigator-designed questionnaire was used to assess KAP regarding noninvasive neuromodulation technologies. Questionnaire design was guided by the 2023 Chinese Expert Consensus on Noninvasive Neuromodulation Technology for the Treatment of Alzheimer’s Disease and relevant literature [19, 20].

The initial draft was reviewed and refined based on feedback from three senior experts specializing in cognitive dysfunction and noninvasive neuromodulation. In the perceived knowledge domain, the experts recommended reducing the original 12 items, simplifying content, and adopting a three-level response scale (“Very Familiar / Familiar / Not Familiar”) to better match the professional background of participants. In the attitude and practice domains, items were streamlined and clarified to highlight the study objectives. A pilot survey with 49 participants was conducted to assess reliability, yielding a Cronbach’s α of 0.963, indicating excellent internal consistency. No items were reported as difficult to understand.

The final Chinese-language questionnaire comprised 29 items across four dimensions: 11 items on demographics, eight on perceived knowledge, five on attitudes, and five on practices. Scoring for the perceived knowledge domain assigned 2 points for “Very Familiar,” 1 point for “Familiar,” and 0 points for “Not Familiar” (total range 0–16), with scores above 80% considered indicative of good perceived knowledge. Attitude and practice domains employed a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very negative to 5 = very positive), with total scores ranging from 5 to 25. Higher scores indicated more positive attitudes or better practices.

Questionnaire distribution and quality control

The questionnaire was distributed online via the Wenjuanxing mini-program on WeChat. To maximize sample diversity, recruitment channels included (1) internal recruitment within The First Affiliated Hospital of Henan Medical University, (2) obtaining participants through professional occasions such as the annual academic meeting of the Neurostimulation Branch of the Chinese Stroke Society (or other national/regional relevant academic conferences) and encouraging them to disseminate the questionnaire via QR code using snowball sampling, and (3) online promotion within relevant professional communities. A total of 479 valid responses were collected, most of which were obtained through online dissemination. The participants completed the survey by scanning a QR code. To ensure data quality, each IP address was restricted to a single submission, and all items were mandatory. Questionnaires were reviewed for completeness, internal consistency, and logical validity. Responses were deemed invalid if completion time was < 90 s, answers failed logical or trap questions (e.g., “The capital of China is Shanghai”), or if all items in any KAP dimension were answered identically.

Sample size calculations

Sample size was estimated using standard formulas for cross-sectional surveys [21, 22], with α = 0.05 (Z₁–α/2 = 1.96), allowable error δ = 0.05, and p = 0.5 to maximize sample size. The minimum required sample was 384 participants. Considering an expected response rate of 80%, at least 461 valid questionnaires were needed.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), AMOS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations and compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to examine relationships among KAP dimensions, with subgroup analyses based on prior training in noninvasive neuromodulation and participation in cognitive disorder-related clinical research. Logistic regression was employed to identify factors associated with practice; variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analyses were entered into multivariable models. The practice scores were dichotomized based on an 80% cutoff [23]. Structural equation modeling (SEM) with path and mediation analyses was used to explore the relationships among KAP dimensions. Model fit was evaluated using chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (CMIN/DF; 1–3 = excellent, 3–5 = good), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; <0.08 = good), incremental fit index (IFI; >0.8 = acceptable), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI; >0.8 = acceptable), and comparative fit index (CFI; >0.8 = acceptable). All tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 501 questionnaires were collected. 22 questionnaires were excluded for the following reasons: 6 participants declined to participate; 2 were completed by individuals aged < 18 years; 1 was completed by an individual aged > 100 years; and 13 provided incorrect answers to the attention-check (trap) question. Consequently, 479 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis. Among the 479 participants (mean age 38.19 ± 8.84 years), 41.5% were male and 58.5% female. Physicians represented the majority of respondents (65.3%). Over half held intermediate or senior professional titles, and 81.4% specialized in internal medicine, neurology, or rehabilitation medicine. Most participants possessed a bachelor’s degree or higher (89.9%), and 51.2% had at least 10 years of clinical experience. Regarding noninvasive neuromodulation technology, 40.9% had received relevant training or education, and a similar proportion had participated in clinical studies related to cognitive disorders. Concerning departmental adoption, 30.9% reported that neuromodulation technology was currently implemented, 29.0% had implementation plans, 15.7% had no plans, and 24.4% were unsure (Table 1).

Characteristics of the participants

Reliability of the questionnaire

The overall Cronbach’s α for the 479 questionnaires was 0.959, indicating excellent internal consistency. The coefficients for the perceived knowledge, attitude, and practice dimensions were 0.971, 0.940, and 0.956, respectively, demonstrating very good reliability.

Perceived knowledge, attitude, and practice

The mean perceived knowledge score among healthcare professionals was 5.36 ± 5.10 (possible maximum: 16; 33.5% of possible maximum), indicating limited familiarity with noninvasive neuromodulation technologies. In contrast, the mean attitude score was 21.32 ± 3.25 (possible maximum: 25; 85.3% of possible maximum), indicating a generally positive perception of these interventions. The mean practice score was 20.05 ± 4.07 (possible maximum: 25; 80.2% of possible maximum), suggesting relatively good engagement in related clinical practices (Table 1). Survey results indicated that fewer than 15% of respondents were very familiar with key aspects of noninvasive neuromodulation technologies, including their core mechanisms, typical characteristics, and relevant clinical factors. The proportion reporting familiarity ranged from 33.4% to 49.9% across various items, while a substantial segment (often exceeding 45%) was not familiar with critical elements, including the differentiation between TMS and tDCS, efficacy evaluation, and international research advances. Familiarity with mechanisms such as neuroplasticity regulation, synaptic modulation, and recent international research frontiers was particularly limited, with more than half of the respondents not familiar with cutting-edge topics. These findings highlight notable perceived knowledge gaps among healthcare professionals regarding both foundational and advanced concepts of noninvasive neuromodulation in clinical practice (Supplementary Table S1). Survey findings demonstrated predominantly positive attitudes toward noninvasive neuromodulation technologies for cognitive disorders among healthcare professionals. Most respondents either strongly agreed (41.1%-41.9%) or agreed (45.7%-49.9%) that these technologies are valuable auxiliary treatments warranting wider application, are safe and noninvasive, and that understanding their principles is necessary for clinical staff. Only a minority expressed neutral views (8.4%-13.2%) or disagreement (< 1% for each item). Notably, over 90% held a favorable attitude toward introducing these technologies in their departments and felt confident in their safety profile. Additionally, nearly all participants acknowledged the need for more high-quality research to further validate efficacy in cognitive disorder management. These results highlight strong professional endorsement coupled with continued demand for robust clinical evidence (Supplementary Table S2). Survey findings revealed that a majority of healthcare professionals reported actively engaging in key practices related to noninvasive neuromodulation technology for cognitive disorders. Between 29.9% and 32.8% were strongly consistent, and 44.1% to 47.2% were consistent with practices such as keeping up to date with the latest technology developments, assessing patient suitability for adjunctive neuromodulation, documenting patient feedback, explaining side effects to patients, and participating in relevant professional training. Neutral responses ranged from 16.7% to 18.8%, while inconsistent or strongly inconsistent practices were infrequent, accounting for less than 6% of any item. These results indicate widespread proactive engagement and a commitment to best practices in the clinical application of noninvasive neuromodulation, but also suggest opportunities for further training to address those with more neutral or inconsistent engagement (Supplementary Table S3SM2).

Correlations between KAP dimensions

Across all participants, perceived knowledge scores were positively correlated with attitude scores (r = 0.465, P < 0.001) and practice scores (r = 0.564, P < 0.001). Attitude scores also demonstrated a strong positive correlation with practice scores (r = 0.760, P < 0.001) (Table 2). Subgroup analyses indicated that participants who had received training or education in noninvasive neuromodulation exhibited stronger correlations among KAP dimensions than those without training. A similar pattern was observed among participants who had participated in clinical studies or projects related to the treatment of cognitive disorders (Table 2).

SEM analysis

The SEM analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1) demonstrated a good model fit, with all indices meeting recommended criteria (Supplementary Table S4). Path analysis revealed that perceived knowledge had direct positive effects on attitude (β = 0.489, P = 0.012) and practice (β = 0.261, P = 0.009). Attitude also exerted a strong direct positive effect on practice (β = 0.642, P = 0.005). Additionally, perceived knowledge indirectly influenced practice through attitude (β = 0.314, P = 0.008), highlighting a significant mediating role of attitude in the relationship between perceived knowledge and practice (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined healthcare professionals’ KAP regarding the use of noninvasive neuromodulation technologies as adjunctive treatments for cognitive disorders. Our findings indicate that while participants generally held positive attitudes and engaged in favorable practices, their perceived knowledge of these technologies was limited. The observed gap between perceived knowledge and attitudes/practices underscores the need for targeted educational interventions. Such a gap could also be related to institutional protocols, experiential exposure, or a positive perception of innovation rather than a comprehensive theoretical understanding, which will have to be investigated in the future. Multivariable and structural equation modeling analyses further suggest that enhancing perceived knowledge may not only improve understanding but also positively influence attitudes and clinical practice behaviors, highlighting the pivotal role of perceived knowledge in shaping effective implementation of noninvasive neuromodulation in clinical settings.

The survey indicated that healthcare professionals’ familiarity with key concepts of noninvasive neuromodulation technologies was generally modest. Only a small proportion of participants reported being very familiar with the main types and mechanisms of TMS, tDCS, and tACS, while a substantial proportion were only partially familiar or not familiar at all. Knowledge gaps were particularly pronounced regarding clinical limiting factors and international research frontiers. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which report that clinicians, including rehabilitation specialists, often have moderate or low knowledge of TMS and tDCS. Limited access to neuromodulation equipment and a lack of hands-on experience contribute to these deficits, and even self-rated knowledgeable specialists often exhibit gaps in understanding safety, contraindications, and side-effect profiles [17].

The findings indicated widespread positive attitudes and high acceptance among healthcare professionals towards noninvasive neuromodulation technologies as auxiliary treatments for cognitive disorders. Most participants strongly endorsed the clinical value and safety of these modalities, reflecting broad professional confidence in their use. The recognition of the necessity for healthcare personnel to understand these technologies underscores their perceived relevance for modern clinical practice. At the same time, the call for more high-quality research evidence highlights that, despite optimism and readiness for adoption, professionals remain mindful of the current limitations in long-term efficacy data and standardized protocols. This pattern suggests that, while knowledge gaps persist, a solid foundation exists for expanding training efforts and evidence generation, thereby supporting their integration into clinical practice as research continues to mature [24, 25]. Positive attitudes toward TMS and tDCS are generally reported among clinicians, particularly those who have been exposed to these technologies in their workplace or through training [26]. The presence of equipment, prior experience, and perceived knowledge sufficiency correlate with more favorable attitudes, suggesting that practical barriers rather than attitudinal hesitancy may limit adoption [17]. Educational initiatives and exposure are consistently associated with greater acceptance and a greater willingness to implement neuromodulation in practice [18, 27].

The findings demonstrated a high level of engagement among healthcare professionals in integrating noninvasive neuromodulation into clinical practice for the treatment of cognitive disorders. Most respondents consistently reported behaviors such as staying up to date with technological advances, evaluating patient suitability, documenting post-treatment changes, communicating risks, and pursuing further training. This widespread proactive approach reflects the increasing normalization and institutional acceptance of these therapies within relevant medical specialties. Nevertheless, a proportion of respondents remained neutral or less consistent in their practices, indicating variability likely related to experience, access to training, or departmental resources. These results underscore clinicians’ robust readiness to adopt evidence-based neuromodulation practices, while also highlighting areas for targeted skill development and institutional support to ensure consistent, high-quality implementation across diverse clinical settings [13, 28]. It has been reported that a lack of standardized training, insufficient clinical guidelines, and limited equipment availability represent core obstacles to routine clinical application [17, 18]. Inconsistent understanding of safety protocols (e.g., management of epilepsy risk and side effects) further hinders confident use, and variability in attitudes and practices was observed across institution types, regions, and educational backgrounds, underscoring the need for unified, comprehensive professional education [17].

This study highlights a knowledge-practice paradox in the context of noninvasive neuromodulation (NINM) techniques, including TMS and tDCS. Participants demonstrated low objective perceived knowledge, in contrast to high attitudes and practice levels. A critical analysis reveals that this paradox reflects complex technological, methodological, and safety dynamics during the early adoption phase of NINM [29]. NINM techniques such as TMS and tDCS have gained rapid clinical acceptance due to their noninvasive profiles, safety, and minimal side effects. These attributes promote clinical optimism, leading to high levels of practice, even when a deep understanding of device mechanisms, indications, and contraindication is lacking. Researchers have observed that enthusiasm for novel interventions often outpaces foundational perceived knowledge as new technologies are introduced into practice [30–32]. The study sample was recruited through online channels and professional conferences focused on neuromodulation, yielding a participant pool with significant prior interest in NINM. This cohort’s attitudes and practice rates are likely higher than those of general clinicians, accentuating the observed perceived knowledge-practice disparity. Such bias is a hallmark of early adopter studies and should be viewed as a valuable finding in a motivated subgroup, not as a mere methodological error [29, 33]. The use of subjective self-report measures to evaluate perceived knowledge introduces social desirability bias and inflates perceived familiarity. In this study, 33.50% of low scores were due to subjective familiarity rather than objective correct responses, indicating that actual perceived knowledge gaps may be more severe than reflected in survey results. The literature shows that early exposure often generates confidence disproportionate to true competence [33]. The disconnect between practice and perceived knowledge signals urgent concerns for clinical safety. While NINM’s safety profile is encouraging, inappropriate device settings, insufficient understanding of contraindications, and improper patient selection remain risks when high practice rates are driven by limited perceived knowledge. It is essential to prioritize objective training and continual evaluation to prevent adverse outcomes [34, 35]. The observed pattern is characteristic of early technology adoption: rapid rise in clinical application on a foundation of incomplete perceived knowledge. This phenomenon calls for targeted education, standardized certification, and longitudinal monitoring to safeguard practice and patient safety [29, 36].

Therefore, the limited but emerging evidence indicates perceived knowledge gaps, moderate to positive attitudes, and variable practical application of noninvasive neuromodulation technologies among healthcare professionals. These gaps limit the broader promotion and standardization of clinical practice, underscoring the need for targeted education, training, and resource allocation to optimize adoption and patient outcomes [17, 18]. In the present study, only the perceived knowledge and practice scores were independently associated with the practice scores. The correlation analyses showed that the KAP dimensions were positively correlated with each other. In addition, the SEM analysis showed that perceived knowledge positively influenced attitude and practice, and that attitude positively influenced practice. The subgroup correlation analyses also showed stronger correlations among participants who had received training or education in noninvasive neuromodulation technology or had participated in any clinical studies or projects related to the treatment of cognitive disorders, compared with those who did not. Those results are supported by the KAP theoretical framework, which holds that knowledge is the basis for practice, while attitude is the driving force [14, 15]. Hence, strengthening knowledge and fostering positive attitudes toward noninvasive neuromodulation technology should lead to better clinical application of the technology. It could take the form of continuing education activities, such as lectures, meetings, reading materials, an interactive website, and podcasts. Future studies should examine such interventions and their impact on patient outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, although the participants were from multiple centers, they covered a relatively limited geographical area, which may limit the generalizability of the findings due to regional sociodemographic characteristics, healthcare access, and local policies. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Although SEM was used to explore relationships among KAP dimensions, the results were based on statistical modeling and predetermined hypotheses, limiting causal interpretation [37–39]. Third, convenience sampling may have favored participants with higher digital literacy, potentially introducing selection bias. Finally, as with all KAP studies, social desirability bias cannot be excluded [40, 41]; high attitude and practice scores despite low perceived knowledge scores may partly reflect this bias.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that healthcare professionals exhibit limited perceived knowledge but generally positive attitudes and practices regarding the use of noninvasive neuromodulation technologies as adjunctive treatments for cognitive disorders. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to enhance perceived knowledge and build trust and confidence among clinical professionals in NINM technology (i.e., shaping attitudes) by providing high-level clinical evidence and safety data, which may, in turn, further improve clinical practices. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of such interventions and investigate the relationships between healthcare professionals’ KAP and patient outcomes to inform strategies for the wider adoption of noninvasive neuromodulation in clinical care.
