Global burden and trends of stomach cancer, Colon and rectum cancer, peptic ulcer disease, and gastritis and duodenitis from 1990 to 2021: a systematic analysis for the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Quan-yao Li, Hui Liu, Wen-chao Qiu, Ying Lu, Xi Li, Min-zhi Zhuang, Ying Zhu, Jun Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeographical disparities are evident in the burden of digestive system diseases. To date, however, a comparative analysis that simultaneously examines the burden of common digestive cancers alongside non-malignant conditions using the most recent GBD 2021 data is lacking. A comprehensive global assessment of the burden of digestive system diseases is therefore warranted.MethodsThis study analyzed incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of four digestive system diseases from the GBD 2021 database. The incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs were further examined across age groups, sexes, and regions. The ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, ASDR, and EAPC were used to evaluate the disease burden.ResultsIn 2021, gastritis and duodenitis exhibited the highest ASIR and ASPR, whereas colon and rectum cancer presented the highest ASMR and ASDR. From 1990 to 2021, the ASIR and ASPR of colon and rectum cancer demonstrated an upward trend, while its ASMR and ASDR declined. By contrast, all metrics for stomach cancer, peptic ulcer disease, and gastritis and duodenitis showed declining trends.ConclusionThe global burden of digestive diseases continues to pose a substantial public health challenge. Greater attention should be directed toward high-risk factors, early screening, and effective prevention and treatment strategies.

Background

Stomach cancer, colon and rectum cancer, peptic ulcer disease, and gastritis and duodenitis are four of the most common gastrointestinal diseases. These conditions not only impair patients’ health and quality of life but also impose a substantial economic burden [1]. Among digestive system cancers, stomach cancer and colon and rectum cancer account for a large proportion of both incidence and mortality. According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2022, the incidence and mortality of stomach cancer and colon and rectum cancer rank among the leading malignancies worldwide. Although the overall incidence of stomach cancer and colon and rectum cancer is decreasing, both have shown a rising incidence in younger age groups [2]. Peptic ulcer disease, a chronic ulcerative condition, has shown a gradual increase in incidence in association with changes in lifestyle and dietary patterns [3]. Moreover, complications such as gastrointestinal bleeding, pyloric obstruction, and perforation can pose a serious threat to patients’ lives. Gastritis and duodenitis, the most prevalent forms of chronic inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract, continue to affect more than half of the world’s population, although their prevalence has declined in developed countries [4]. Their occurrence is closely associated with mucosal injury caused by lifestyle factors, Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection, or the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [5–7] and is often accompanied by symptoms such as epigastric distension, dull epigastric pain, acid reflux, and belching. In severe cases, gastrointestinal bleeding, perforation, and peritonitis may occur [8]. With disease progression, gastritis also carries a risk of malignant transformation. Relevant studies have demonstrated that the relative risk of stomach cancer in patients with gastric atrophy is 15.195 [6,9]. Substantial regional differences exist in the burden of digestive system diseases, owing to distinct etiologies, risk factors, and prevention strategies.

In recent years, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework has been increasingly applied to characterize the epidemiological landscape of various diseases. Notably, several studies have extended traditional burden measurements to incorporate dimensions such as healthcare quality and inequality. For instance, Xia et al. systematically analyzed the global burden of motor neuron disease from 1990 to 2021 and introduced a Quality of Care Index (QCI) to evaluate disparities in healthcare delivery across regions with different SDI levels [10]. However, no study to date has comprehensively assessed the burden of digestive system diseases together with the most recent epidemiological changes. A clearer understanding of the global burden of digestive system diseases is therefore essential to inform their prevention and treatment. Using the GBD 2021 data, the present study analyzed the disease burden in order to comparatively examine how diseases at different stages of the same organ system have evolved epidemiologically under the influence of socioeconomic development and lifestyle changes across countries and regions. We performed the first comparative analysis of the epidemiological trends of neoplastic and non-neoplastic digestive tract diseases from 1990 to 2021. By integrating frontier analysis and the Health Inequality Index, the study evaluated the development-dependent burden and provided short-term projections through 2031. The aim was to comprehensively evaluate the burden of digestive system diseases and to provide evidence to inform the optimization of medical resources and the reduction of economic burden.

Methods

Database source

This study used the GBD 2021 database, a health database covering 371 diseases and injuries and 88 risk factors across 204 countries and regions, with a primary focus on epidemiological burden [11]. The study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines. Data on the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of four digestive system diseases were obtained from the GBD 2021 (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results) and analyzed in this study.

Statistical analysis

All data were extracted from the GBD 2021 Results Tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results). Detailed information on the data sources, methods, and statistical modeling has been provided in previous reports [12]. According to the tenth edition of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), the four diseases were assigned the following codes: gastric cancer (C16), colon and rectum cancer (C18–C20), peptic ulcer disease (K25–K28), and gastritis and duodenitis (K29). The age-standardized rate (ASR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were used to represent the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of the four digestive system diseases across countries and regions. The trends in age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), age-standardised mortality rate (ASMR), and age-standardized DALYs rate (ASDR) for the four digestive system diseases from 1990 to 2021 were assessed using the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) and its 95% CI.

The SDI is a composite indicator that comprehensively reflects the developmental status of a country or region based on per-capita income, mean education level, and fertility rate [11,13]. Frontier analysis was employed to characterize the relationship between SDI and the corresponding health outcomes (ASIR/ASPR), and the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and Concentration Index (CI) were used to further quantify health disparities and inequalities across countries and regions. Future trends in the global burden of the four digestive system diseases were estimated using an Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model, which was used to project global ASIR and mortality from 2021 to 2031 based on the ASIR and ASPR from 1990 to 2021. Model adequacy was confirmed by the Ljung–Box test on residuals (P > 0.05), and forecast uncertainty was presented as 95% CI. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2 and the web-based version of the software. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of the four digestive system diseases globally and by region

In 2021, the global incidence of stomach cancer was 1,230,233 (1,052,350–14,099,700), that of colon and rectum cancer was 2,194,143 (2,001,272–2,359,390), peptic ulcer disease was 2,854,370 (2,438,231–3,264,252), and gastritis and duodenitis was 27,202,524 (21,851,453–33,647,647). Gastritis and duodenitis exhibited the highest ASIR, at 323.2 cases per 100,000 population. From 1990 to 2021, the ASIR of colon and rectum cancer showed an upward trend, whereas the ASIR of stomach cancer, peptic ulcer disease, and gastritis and duodenitis declined; the ASIR of stomach cancer demonstrated the largest decrease (Table 1 and Figure 1).

Incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs for the four digestive system diseases globally and by region in 2021.

In 2021, the global prevalence of stomach cancer was 2,393,213 (2,059,698–2,771,476), that of colon and rectum cancer was 11,679,120 (10,774,527–12,538,400), peptic ulcer disease was 6,567,782 (5,798,379–7,597,596), and gastritis and duodenitis was 38,293,409 (31,167,468–47,226,499). Gastritis and duodenitis presented the highest ASPR, at 454.3 cases per 100,000 population. From 1990 to 2021, the ASPR of colon and rectum cancer showed an upward trend, whereas the ASPR of stomach cancer, peptic ulcer disease, and gastritis and duodenitis declined; the ASPR of peptic ulcer disease demonstrated the largest decrease (Table 1 and Figure 1).

In 2021, the global mortality of stomach cancer was 954,374 (821,751–1,089,577), that of colon and rectum cancer was 1,044,072 (950,188–1,120,169), peptic ulcer disease was 230,217 (193,005–270,857), and gastritis and duodenitis was 42,795 (37,095–49,202). Colon and rectum cancer had the highest ASMR, at 12.4 cases per 100,000 population. From 1990 to 2021, the ASMR of stomach cancer, colon and rectum cancer, peptic ulcer disease, and gastritis and duodenitis all declined, with the ASMR of peptic ulcer disease showing the largest decrease (Table 1 and Figure 1).

In 2021, the global DALYs of stomach cancer were 22,786,633 (19,576,344–26,118,869), those of colon and rectum cancer were 24,401,100 (22,689,369–26,161,518), peptic ulcer disease 6,057,594 (5,162,099–7,041,854), and gastritis and duodenitis 2,812,628 (2,174,996–3,611,598). Colon and rectum cancer exhibited the highest ASDR, at 283.2 cases per 100,000 population. From 1990 to 2021, the ASDR of stomach cancer, colon and rectum cancer, peptic ulcer disease, and gastritis and duodenitis all declined, with the ASDR of peptic ulcer disease showing the largest decrease (Table 1 and Figure 1).

Factors influencing the EAPC in the burden of the four digestive system diseases

To better explain the burden of the four digestive system diseases, factors potentially influencing the EAPC were analyzed. For stomach cancer, the EAPC was not significantly associated with ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, or ASDR in either 1990 or 2021. For colon and rectum cancer, the EAPC was significantly negatively associated with ASMR and ASDR in 1990 (P < 0.01), but this association attenuated by 2021. For peptic ulcer disease, the EAPC showed a similar pattern with respect to ASIR and ASPR as that observed for colon and rectum cancer. For gastritis and duodenitis, the EAPC was negatively correlated with ASIR and ASPR in both 1990 and 2021 (P < 0.01); the EAPC was also negatively correlated with ASMR in 1990 (P < 0.01), although this correlation gradually weakened by 2021 (P < 0.05). In addition, the EAPC was associated with ASDR in 1990, but this association had disappeared by 2021 (Figure 2).

Burden of SDI on the four digestive system diseases

Across countries and regions, the ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR were associated with SDI and with the levels expected on the basis of SDI for each location. In 2021, high–middle SDI regions were characterized by higher ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR for stomach cancer and for colon and rectum cancer. By contrast, low SDI regions exhibited higher ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR for peptic ulcer disease and for gastritis and duodenitis. In 2021, a positive association between SDI and both ASIR and ASPR was observed for colon and rectum cancer, whereas a negative association between SDI and both ASIR and ASPR was observed for peptic ulcer disease and for gastritis and duodenitis across the four digestive system diseases in 204 countries and regions worldwide. In addition, a negative correlation between SDI and both ASMR and ASDR was identified for gastritis and duodenitis (Figure 3 and Table 1).

Burden of the four digestive system diseases by age, sex, and region

Examination of the burden of the four digestive system diseases by age, sex, and region in 1990 and 2021 revealed that the incidence and mortality of stomach cancer were concentrated in the 80+ age group, while the prevalence was concentrated in the 25–29 and 30–34 age groups. DALYs were concentrated in the 70–74 and 75–79 age groups. The incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of stomach cancer were higher in males and were concentrated in high-middle SDI regions (Figure 4A).

Incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of the four digestive system diseases by age, sex, and region (A: stomach cancer; B: colon and rectum cancer; C: peptic ulcer disease; D: gastritis and duodenitis; a: incidence; b: prevalence; c: mortality; d: DALYs).

The incidence, mortality, and DALYs of colon and rectum cancer were concentrated in the 80+ age group, whereas the prevalence was concentrated in the 75–79 age group. The incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of colon and rectum cancer were higher in males and were concentrated in high SDI regions (Figure 4B).

The incidence, mortality, and DALYs of peptic ulcer disease were concentrated in the 80+ age group, whereas the prevalence was concentrated in the 75–79 age group. The incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of peptic ulcer disease were higher in males and were concentrated in low-SDI and low-middle SDI regions (Figure 4C).

The incidence and prevalence of gastritis and duodenitis were concentrated in the 55–79 age groups, whereas the mortality and DALYs were concentrated in the 80+ age group. The incidence, morbidity, mortality, and DALYs of gastritis and duodenitis were higher in females than in males, and were concentrated in high-middle SDI regions (Figure 4D).

Frontier analysis of ASIR and ASPR for the four digestive system diseases in 2021

Frontier analysis demonstrated the health benefits with respect to ASIR and ASPR for the four digestive system diseases in countries and regions at different developmental levels from 1990 to 2021. The results indicated that, with increasing SDI, the disease burden and effective differences across countries gradually widened, suggesting that countries or regions with higher SDI have greater potential for reducing the burden of digestive system diseases (Figure 5).

Health inequality analysis

The linear relationship and distribution of the ASIR and ASPR across the four digestive system diseases with respect to inequality were assessed using the SII and the CI. The SII for ASIR and ASPR of stomach cancer and colon and rectum cancer gradually decreased from 1990 to 2021, indicating that the ASIR and ASPR of these two cancers approached greater equality by 2021. The CI further suggested that the ASIR and ASPR of stomach cancer and colon and rectum cancer gradually shifted from relatively low to relatively high socioeconomic strata. By contrast, the SII for ASIR and ASPR of peptic ulcer disease gradually increased from 1990 to 2021, indicating that the ASIR and ASPR of peptic ulcer disease were closer to equality in 1990. The CI suggested that the ASIR and prevalence of peptic ulcer disease gradually shifted toward relatively low socioeconomic strata as economic development progressed. Findings for gastritis and duodenitis were consistent with those observed for peptic ulcer disease (Supplementary Figure 6).

Projections of ASIR and ASPR for the four digestive system diseases between 2021 and 2031

Using the ARIMA model, the ASIR and ASPR of the four digestive system diseases were projected from 2021 to 2031. The ASIR and ASPR of stomach cancer and of gastritis and duodenitis were projected to decline continuously from 2021 to 2031. For colon and rectum cancer, the ASIR was projected to continue increasing while the ASPR remained stable. For peptic ulcer disease, the ASIR and ASPR were projected to decline steadily from 2021 to 2031, in contrast to the rapid decline observed from 1990 to 2021 (Supplementary Figure 7).

Discussion

The international community is increasingly recognizing the substantial threat that digestive system diseases pose to global health. The prevalence of these diseases has been rising, posing growing challenges to public health policy and healthcare systems worldwide. The present study provides a comprehensive estimate of the burden of digestive system diseases at the global, regional, and national levels from 1990 to 2021. Between 1990 and 2021, gastritis and duodenitis showed a downward trend in more than half of regions, and the EAPC showed a significant negative association with its incidence and prevalence, indicating that there is still room for improvement in its prevention and treatment. In both 1990 and 2021, the relationship between EAPC and mortality was consistent with positive global progress in reducing mortality from gastritis and duodenitis. For colon and rectum cancer, the ASIR and ASPR increased in most regions, while the ASMR and ASDR decreased; the EAPC was negatively associated with mortality and DALYs in 1990, an association that gradually disappeared by 2021, possibly reflecting substantial progress in treatment and the prolongation of patient survival. Globally, the incidence, prevalence, and mortality of peptic ulcer disease and of gastritis and duodenitis were higher in middle, low–middle, and low SDI regions, and the incidence and prevalence of gastritis and duodenitis in China were considerably higher than those in middle SDI regions. Conversely, in 2021, the incidence of stomach cancer and of colon and rectum cancer was higher in high SDI and middle–high SDI regions, while the incidence, prevalence, and mortality of stomach cancer in China were considerably higher than those in middle–high SDI regions.

The present study showed that SDI was associated with the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of the four digestive system diseases. In 2021, a negative association between SDI and both ASIR and ASPR was observed for peptic ulcer disease and for gastritis and duodenitis. Previous studies have shown that low SDI countries have a higher incidence of non-cancer digestive system diseases [14–16]. It is plausible that limited medical conditions, lower living standards, and a lack of basic health facilities in low SDI regions contribute to the high incidence and low cure rates of these diseases. A randomized trial demonstrated the substantial effectiveness of a hand hygiene program in reducing acute gastroenteritis in child care centers, which mainly affects children through H. pylori infection [17]. A negative association between SDI and both mortality and DALYs was observed for gastritis and duodenitis; high SDI regions have more advanced healthcare systems, whereas low SDI regions have insufficient medical resources and limited access to treatment, meaning that patients in these regions often present with more severe disease at initial diagnosis, which may be associated with a higher risk of complications and death. In addition, Xia et al. recently introduced a Quality of Care Index (QCI) for motor neuron disease and found that low SDI regions showed particularly limited QCI improvement from 1990 to 2021 [10]. This is consistent with our findings on digestive diseases, suggesting that disparities in healthcare quality—not merely in service access—may contribute to the persistently higher burden in less developed regions. Some studies have shown that, with increasing disease severity, patients are more likely to forgo treatment because of family responsibilities [18]. Our findings showed that mortality and DALYs of colon and rectum cancer increased with increasing SDI in middle and middle–high SDI regions, suggesting that major therapeutic breakthroughs may still be lacking in high SDI regions; that is, more patients are detected but fail to receive optimal treatment, making it difficult to extend the survival of patients with colon and rectum cancer. At the same time, the failure to widely implement early cancer screening may delay treatmentof colon and rectum cancer, leading to a higher risk of death. Disease burden analyses have indicated that the burden of colon and rectum cancer is greater in middle and high SDI regions [19]. Some studies have suggested that the decline in the burden of peptic ulcer disease and of gastritis and duodenitis is likely associated with the widespread implementation of H. pylori eradication therapy, improved sanitation, and increased use of proton pump inhibitors in many countries [3,15]. By contrast, the rising incidence of colorectal cancer in high-SDI regions may reflect dietary transitions, sedentary lifestyles, and an increased prevalence of obesity [20]. However, the simultaneous decline in colorectal cancer mortality suggests progress in early detection and treatment [21]. The persistently high burden of stomach cancer in East Asia, including China, may be related to the high prevalence of H. pylori infection, dietary factors (e.g., salted foods), and incomplete coverage of population-based screening. Frontier analysis of the ASIR and ASPR for the four digestive system diseases indicated that countries or regions with high SDI had greater potential for reducing the disease burden. Therefore, high-SDI regions should focus on high-risk factors for colon and rectum cancer, promote early screening, and ensure timely detection and treatment.

Compared with previous GBD studies, our findings are broadly consistent with prior analyses of individual diseases. For instance, Xie et al. reported a global decline in the burden of peptic ulcer disease from 1990 to 2019, which we confirm through 2021 [3]. Liu et al. documented similar trends for gastritis and duodenitis [15]. For colorectal cancer, Pan et al. highlighted a rise in early-onset cases, which is consistent with our observation of an increasing ASIR in high–middle SDI regions [19]. The novel contribution of our study lies in the simultaneous comparison of neoplastic and non-neoplastic conditions, which reveals that, while nonmalignant diseases are declining globally, colorectal cancer incidence continues to rise in developed regions—a divergence with important policy implications.

The burden of the four digestive system diseases displayed clear age and sex distribution patterns. The incidence and mortality of stomach cancer, colon and rectum cancer, and peptic ulcer disease increased with age, particularly after the age of 80. The ASDR for colon and rectum cancer, peptic ulcer disease, and gastritis and duodenitis also increased with age and was concentrated in those aged 80 years and over, highlighting the growing disease burden associated with population aging. Previous studies have shown that the burden of stomach cancer is attributable to the increasing burden of population aging [22], global population growth and aging trends are likely to further increase the burden of these diseases. This trend has substantial social and economic implications worldwide and calls for policies and strategies to ensure healthy lives for the elderly population. Among the four digestive system diseases, with the exception of gastritis and duodenitis, the burden was predominantly observed in males. Previous studies have found that stomach cancer and colon and rectum cancer are significantly associated with H. pylori infection, smoking, alcohol consumption, body mass index (BMI), insufficient physical activity, excessive intake of red and processed meat, and inadequate intake of calcium and dietary fiber [23–26]. A potential explanation for the higher burden of peptic ulcer and gastric cancer in men compared with women is that men may have higher exposure to smoking and H. pylori infection. According to SEER data, estrogen may exert a protective effect against cardia and intestinal-type stomach cancer [27]. The greater burden of colon and rectum cancer in men than in women has been associated with higher red meat intake, lower physical activity, and inadequate calcium intake [28]. Karaye et al. [29] found that the increase in alcohol-related deaths among women has been greater than that among men in the United States in recent years; this shift in women’s drinking patterns may have contributed to a higher occurrence of gastritis and duodenitis. Therefore, implementing sex-specific preventive measures may help promote gender health equality.

The health inequality analysis revealed that the SII for the incidence and prevalence of peptic ulcer disease and of gastritis and duodenitis gradually increased, indicating substantial inequality in the global burden, while the CI showed that their incidence and prevalence tended to shift toward relatively low socioeconomic strata. Population growth and global aging in most countries, increased access to upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, improved medical sanitation, the widespread use of proton pump inhibitors, and increased awareness of the importance of H. pylori eradication may have contributed to the current decline in the incidence and prevalence of peptic ulcer disease and of gastritis and duodenitis. For less developed countries, although medical technology may not yet be sufficiently advanced, ulcer occurrence can still be prevented by addressing H. pylori infection through measures such as improving infrastructure and the dietary environment [30]. Therefore, improving the standard of care for digestive system diseases in developing countries, raising public health awareness among residents, and optimizing the allocation of relevant infrastructure may help alleviate the inequitable global burden of digestive system diseases driven by socioeconomic disparities.

We projected the incidence and prevalence of the four digestive system diseases from 2021 to 2031. The results indicated that the incidence of colon and rectum cancer is expected to continue to rise, while the prevalence is expected to remain stable. The incidence and prevalence of the other three digestive system diseases are projected to decline. To address the anticipated increase in the global burden of colon and rectum cancer, cost-effective strategies are required. First, public awareness should be enhanced by disseminating knowledge regarding the prevention and treatment of colon and rectum cancer. Finally, early detection and treatment are effective measures that can contribute to improving survival rates and quality of life.

This study presents the global burden of four common digestive system diseases based on the GBD 2021; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the estimation of the global disease burden relies on mathematical models and on the quality and availability of primary data from epidemiological surveys, which vary in quality and completeness across countries, leading to greater estimation uncertainty. Second, the COVID-19 pandemic introduced substantial uncertainty into mortality estimates for all diseases, particularly in the most severely affected regions. Third, other potentially influential factors could not be captured by our analysis, such as the coverage of national screening programs, detailed dietary patterns, and the prevalence of specific H. pylori strains. The absence of such country-level granular data may have introduced estimation bias. Finally, ARIMA forecasts are based on historical trends and assume continuity; they do not account for potential future medical breakthroughs or policy changes, and projection uncertainty widens over time. Future studies should therefore integrate multi-source individual-level data with external validation sets and incorporate risk factor attribution and Bayesian ensemble forecasting models to reduce estimation bias and projection uncertainty.

Conclusion

The burden of digestive system diseases remains a major public health challenge worldwide. This study identified substantial differences in the burden of the four digestive system diseases across SDI levels, age groups, and sexes. These findings underscore the importance of focusing on high-risk populations, implementing early screening programs where appropriate, and strengthening prevention and treatment strategies.
