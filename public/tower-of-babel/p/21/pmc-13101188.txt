Medical software for precision diagnostics of infection with immunoprofiling and artificial intelligence
Authors: Enkelejda Miho, Susanna Marquez, Ulrik Stervbo, Kirsten D Mertz, Jan Kruta, Erik Schkommodau, Meysman Pieter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhat does it take to realize one-click precision diagnostics across infections? Traditional biomedical methods often are limited to detect a single pathogen at a time, thus neglecting the complex dynamics of the immune response and its degrees of individuality, the host-microbe interactions, and the connection to other disease-states that can significantly affect diagnoses.Main bodyHere we review methods employed in the clinical microbiology laboratory, sequencing and machine learning technologies, that enable precision diagnostics of infectious diseases. We provide a roadmap of the regulatory pathways and touch upon engineering requirements necessary for developing and bringing to market a medical software for physicians and citizens. We anticipate that high-throughput sequencing technologies, together with artificial intelligence, unlock the use of complex information of the immune response at the single-molecule level thus enable the precise diagnosis of multiple infections, the predictions of clinical outcomes based on individual immune baselines, microbial colonization, and therapeutic history.ConclusionsThese methods, combined in a medical software, hold the potential to concomitantly diagnose and predict the clinical course of additional immune-related diseases such as cancer and autoimmunity. Thus, uncovering the immunity-interface to several diseases can have a direct impact on population immunity, One Health, and pandemic preparedness.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Machine learning, Software, High-throughput nucleotide, Sequencing, Host microbial interactions, Immunity, Herd, Communicable diseases, Pandemic, Preparedness, Autoimmunity, Neoplasms, Disease progression, One Health

PART I

Pathogens cause infectious diseases, cancer, and autoimmunity

Infections emerge and re-emerge as a predominant contributor to global mortality across all age groups [1]. Numerous infectious agents can induce autoimmune diseases [2, 3] and have been recognized as oncogenic [4]. In addition, the antimicrobial resistance of pathogens evolving in response to treatment poses a major threat to health. Outcomes from infectious diseases are directly associated with time to pathogen identification. Therefore, a prompt and precise diagnosis is crucial to initiating appropriate treatment and preventing further spread.

Diagnosis of infection is generally based on the identification of the pathogen causing the disease (Fig. 1). Pathogens causing infections can be viruses, bacteria, fungi, algae, protozoa and parasites. Diagnosis is performed through pathogen-specific tests, for example by isolating the pathogen in culture, or by microscopic visualization of the pathogen in tissue lesions, or by detecting an immune response of the host [6–9]. Today, molecular diagnostics is widely used in clinical practice [10]. More recently, sequencing technologies and microfluidics have advanced the detection of pathogens [6].

Diagnostics of infectious diseases. (A) traditional methods of diagnostics of infectious diseases are performed in a clinical microbiology laboratory mainly by direct identification of the infection agent or the specific immune response to it. Testing methods generally involve isolation of the pathogen in culture, its identification through electron microscopy and histopathology, by molecular methods, and the detection of a specific immune response to it. (B) Metagenomics, unlike traditional genomics which focuses on the genetic material of a single organism, uses high-throughput sequencing and computational analyses to study the microbiome to identify and discover several microbes in the microbial community, their relative frequency in the community, and gene expressions associated with antimicrobial resistance. Immunomics uses next-generation sequencing and computational analyses to profile immune repertoires, collections of B- and T-cell receptors, by analyzing gene expression in response to infections, to understand how the immune system reacts and adapts to pathogens and differentiates between various strains of pathogens based on how they interact with the immune system. Information on V(D)J germline usage is also used to characterize immunity and infection and indicate relative frequencies of immune clones that are indicative of infection. Artificial intelligence advances these types of insights to detect and predict complex patterns and interactions in the host-microbe ecosystem, and ultimately classify infections based on a pathogen-imprinted personal immunity. (C) Software engineering to develop scientific insights into commercially available medical software should be conducted in organizations within the framework of quality management systems (QMS). QMS are essential for ensuring that processes, documentation, and overall quality standards are consistently maintained in an organization. Documentation provides a structured approach to record standard operation procedures, policies, and results, ensuring alignment with requirements, clarity and traceability. Management involves processes and human resources to ensure they align with regulatory requirements based on organizational goals. Audits are systematic reviews to verify compliance with established standards and identify areas for improvement. Audits drive continuous improvements and feedback mechanisms, fostering ongoing refinement of processes to achieve higher quality and efficiency. Alignment to regulatory requirements is achieved through adherence to international standards and local guidelines through certification for QMS, software lifecycle development, risk management, and other standards. Upon development, qualification and classification of the medical software by the manufacturer, the organization applies to regulatory authorities (e.g., FDA in USA, notified body in EU, and swissmedic in Switzerland) for authorization to commercialize the product by demonstrating the safety and efficacy of the medical device software. The software medical device can then enter clinical routine for diagnostics and the citizen market for prevention. However, clinical routine and pathogen-specific detection technologies—such as PCR—have several limitations. Overall, they are specific to a certain type of pathogen; consequently, multiple experimental procedures are needed to test for various (known or suspected) pathogens. Furthermore, specific procedures need to be established for emerging pathogens. The diagnostic precision of testing (repeatability and reproducibility), as well as sensitivity, specificity and accuracy vary across antigens, methods and among laboratories. In addition, our evolving understanding of pathogens as causative agents of autoimmune diseases and cancer, and the crucial role of the host milieu and host-microbe interactions in disease outcomes of the same pathogens (e.g., Escherichia coli could be normal flora or cause septic shock [6, 11]), call for innovative assays that impart a qualitative shift from our current understanding of pathogens to be identified as isolated agents of “one” disease, towards a vision of “One Health.” Considering the burden of disease and the threat of emerging pathogens to public health, technologies that enable precision diagnostics are urgently needed.

Traditional methods of clinical diagnostics of infection

Traditional diagnostics relies on a physical examination, radiological studies, and testing performed in clinical microbiology and immunology laboratories, or with point-of-care (POC) devices [12]. Laboratory tests using microbial cultivation, immunologic methods, experimental animals, electron microscopy, and novel molecular methods have advanced the identification and characterization of viruses [13], bacteria [14, 15], fungi [8, 16], algae [17, p]rotozoa [18], and helminths as etiologic pathogens of infectious diseases. Generally, these methods aim to detect directly the infectious agent, recently employing artificial intelligence methods for its exact classification [19]. Diagnosis can also be performed by examining the host immune response to a specific pathogen. This can be performed by serological tests, such as enzyme immunoassays (e.g., ELISA), western immunoblotting, immunofluorescence, or lateral flow assay, which detect antibodies produced by the host in response to the pathogen. These antibody tests are commonly used for the identification of current and past infections or vaccinations [20]. However, conventional diagnostic methods can be costly and time-consuming. For instance, viral diagnostic methods range from 30 minutes to 21 days depending on the assay and virus [21]. In addition, the assays are pathogen-specific, so that one assay can generally only assess one pathogen or few variants.

Molecular diagnostics is a specific branch of tradition methods used in clinical practice [10]. This includes qualitative and quantitative PCR as well as sequencing technologies. Genomic DNA or RNA are sequenced to identify the pathogen. If RNA is the starting material, a first step of reverse transcription is needed [22]. Qualitative PCR, which assesses the presence or absence of a pathogen, is the most widely used molecular diagnostic method to identify infectious agents such as parasites, viruses, bacteria, and fungi [10]. Real-time PCR is a type of quantitative PCR where a fluorescent signal is used to detect the number of cycles needed to cross a background threshold, so that a lower number of cycles corresponds to a higher number of pathogen molecules in the patient sample [22]. This method is mostly used to detect viruses and bacteria.

PCR-based methods are collectively specific and highly sensitive. The assays with RNA as a starting material may require strict pre-analytics to prevent inaccuracies due to degradation in poorly handled samples. Depending on the variation in the pathogenic nucleic acid sequence, these methods can also detect different strains and sub-variants of a pathogen, if followed by (Sanger) sequencing. In some cases, e.g., Human Herpesvirus 6, only nucleic acid sequencing can truly separate the strains [23]. It’s important to note that this method only works for pathogens and strains where the sequence is known, primers exist, and samples need to be free from contaminants. In all cases, the precise detection is crucial, as the correct treatment of infections depends on the precise identification of the causative agent.

At last, while microorganisms develop structural properties differently within tissue than on an agar plate [8], cultural methods generally output a yes/no response on the presence of one pathogen at a time and don’t consider the ecosystem, either of the co-existing communities of pathogens or the host.

Sequencing to diagnose infection

Sequencing technologies produce large-scale data that enables the characterization of pathogens, the immune response of the host, and host–pathogen interactions (Fig. 1). In parallel, unprecedented advances in artificial intelligence have transformed the questions that can be asked and the speed at which they can be answered. The integration of complex data with pattern prediction algorithms can provide the tools for a paradigm shift in precision diagnostics.

The main advantage of sequencing is the concomitant exploration of pathogen and host at the single-cell and single-molecule level [24–27]. High-throughput, long-read, and single-cell sequencing technologies detect a broad spectrum of pathogens, and enable the surveillance of host–pathogen interactions in diverse microbial ecosystems including the gut microbiome [24, 28–36]. Clinical metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has emerged as a comprehensive analysis of microbial genetics in samples from patients [37, 38]. Genome sequencing has the advantage that it can also identify antibiotic resistance genes, thus can become an alternative to in vitro antibiotic susceptibility testing methods for determining antibiotic resistance and guiding personalized treatment in infections [39, 40]. The utility of quality-controlled reference genomes from next-generation sequencing of infectious diseases for diagnostic purposes has been clinically demonstrated [41–45]. On the host side, the applicability of immune sequencing has been demonstrated in several diseases for repertoires of both B-cell receptors (BCR) and T cell receptors (TCR) sequences [18, 46].

Sequencing the immune repertoire

On the host side, the immune system responds adaptively to pathogens, vaccination, autoimmune disease and cancer, by generating distinct immune repertoires [47, 48]. The outcome of infection depends not only on the dose and route of pathogens and virulence properties, but also on immune factors of the host. Unlike pathogen-specific or serological diagnostics that detect predefined antigens or antibodies, AI-driven immunoprofiling decodes the entire immune receptor repertoire, capturing individualized, system-level immune responses independent of known pathogens. Immunoprofiling coupled with AI has the ability to derive multiple clinically relevant insights from a single sequencing assay, including clonal identity, disease classification, amyloidogenic potential, and treatment-response prediction, as a “one-shot” diagnosis. High-throughput sequencing of entire immune repertoires has been able to identify protective and susceptible immune signatures from peripheral blood [49]. Sequencing of the variable domain of the receptor encoded in DNA (rearranged genomic gDNA or complementary cDNA from transcribed mRNA) of the different cells can capture the great diversity of the immune repertoires [50]. The unique variable amino acid domain of the cell-surface receptors of B- and T-cells determines their cell identity and the binding specificity to a variety of pathogens, cancer neoantigens and autoantigens.

Several technologies provided as research protocols or services are available for the high-throughput sequencing of immune repertoires [51–55]. Experimentally, libraries are prepared from collections of immune receptors by attaching designed primers to target the conserved gDNA or RNA regions flanking the TCR/BCR variable domains. Briefly, short random nucleotide sequences, known as unique molecular identifiers (UMI), are designed to tag each template primer-receptor molecule with a unique identifier. UMIs enable the accurate quantification of individual molecules and reduce amplification bias and errors during sequencing. Adaptors are typically ligated to the ends of DNA fragments and serve as the binding sites for sequencing primers, which are necessary for sequencing the DNA fragments on a sequencing platform. This library of variable domains is amplified through multiplex PCR or 5′ RACE methods and subsequently sequenced [18, 52, 55]. Single-cell sequencing captures heterogeneity and rare subpopulations of individual cells compared to bulk sequencing [55, 56]. It is important to note that also UMIs can be affected by sequencing erros and PCR bias can impact downstream analysis [57].

High-throughput sequencing of immune repertoires combined with data science [58–61], machine learning [62, 63] and software development [64] has the potential to revolutionize precision diagnostics across infectious diseases. The deconvolution of the complex and diverse immune repertoire enables diagnostics by identifying pathogen-specific receptors or repertoire patterns in the personalized collection of billions immune B- and T-cell variable sequences of an individual [49, 65–67].

So far, investigations of immune repertoire sequences have been reported across infectious agents and vaccinations [51, 68]. Applications of immune repertoires have been reported in malaria [69, 70], Mycobacterium tuberculosis [71], meningococcal bacteria [72], Staphylococcus aureus [73, 74], human herpes virus [75], dengue virus [62, 63, 76], HIV-1 [77, 78], hepatitis B virus (HBV) [72, 79–81], influenza [72, 82–85], hepatitis C virus (HCV) [72, 86, 87], cytomegalovirus (CMV) [88], human polyomavirus [89], ebola virus [90], and are particularly extensive in the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [91–94]. Furthermore, certain immune repertoire sequences have been suggested to constitute a pathogen-specific library for diagnosis and immune status profiling [93, 95–98].

An introduction to the computational analysis of high-throughput sequenced immune repertoires has been reported elsewhere [59, 99]. Briefly, in infection diseases, analyses of immune repertoires mainly focus on some measure of repertoire diversity–e.g., in terms of clonal frequencies and similarities, V (D)J germline gene usage, somatic hypermutations (only B-cells), complementarity-determining 3 region distributions and overlap. However, these methods can only characterize the immune status broadly and do not capture signatures of specific infections (Fig. 1). Sequence-similarity methods such as network and clonal overlap analyses detect architectural patterns of entire repertoires in sequence space [5, 100] and can detect antigen-specific immune receptors [101]. Analyses similar to these ones will, however, fail to detect the immune status if used as sequence-based pairwise clonal similarity methods [102]. A further limitation of traditional computational analyses is that most of them do not account for the complex dynamic individual baseline of immune profiles. Although the technology has shown remarkable potential for clinical application insights, it is under development. Fine tuning of next-generation sequencing (NGS) sampling depth, experimental protocols, and immunoinformatic pipelines are in progress. Typical organizational challenges towards regulatory compliance for commercialization increase exponentially with the evolving legislation, while validation and evaluation of machine learning algorithms can be another obstacle to the adoption of this technology in the clinical setting.

Considering the vast potential sequence space of immune repertoires, the extraordinary personal profile of their composition, and individual histories of microbial exposure, analyses that consider a personal baseline could provide personalized insights into infection-specific patterns of the immune status. In the context of personal immunity, individual histories of microbial exposure, as well as pre-existing and cross-reactive immunity can provide valuable insights into baseline dynamics [103–106]. From the perspective of evidence-based population immunity, the baseline to consider is the (evolutionary) convergent immune repertoire signatures observed in healthy individuals [107–110].

We anticipate that specific immune lineages, indicative of infection and predictive of clinical outcomes [111–113], retain global antigen-specific and cross-reactive sequence- and repertoire-patterns within the diversity of the individual immune repertoire, and within the convergent patterns of population immunity [66, 114, 115]. However, detecting these patterns remains challenging due to the complex and dynamic nature of the immune repertoire—a challenge that emerging methods of artificial intelligence are increasingly enabled to address.

PART II

Artificial intelligence to realize the potential of immunoprofiling

Immune profiling technologies have generated large-scale datasets [116]. The AIRR Data Commons of the global AIRR Community attempts to facilitate sharing of the data across diseases and institutions (http://www.airr-community.org). Artificial intelligence (AI) includes several machine learning methods that can discover complex patterns in large data. The interrogation of the entire immune repertoire in one analysis captures both known and emergent molecular signatures. This integrated readout reduces diagnostic fragmentation, accelerates clinical decision-making, and provides a systems-level view of immune dysfunction unattainable with conventional serological or single-biomarker assays.

Machine learning (ML) models that study microbial communities and immune repertoires have been reported in literature [33, 62, 117–123, 123–125]. Supervised ML models trained with known examples make predictions from pathogen genomic data, immunomics and biomarker data [126]. Supervised machine learning steps are training (or learning) a model in order to map relations of variables to classes of diagnosis e.g., various infections or stati, from a labelled dataset, validating this model to optimize its parameters, and evaluating it on unseen data. Unsupervised ML models identify patterns in data without requiring labeled examples; in principle, unsupervised learning works on grouping classes by some similarity measure. ML has been used to detect antibody repertoire patterns of several infections [72, 127, 128]. For example, ML methods predict accurately COVID-19 infection based on TCR sequence features [129] and the severity of the clinical course of infection [129], and sequences specific to distinct pathogenic epitopes [130]. Sequenced TCR repertoires have been used for diagnostics in infection, discriminating between acute and historical exposure to SARS-CoV-2 [131]. Various AI models based on gene segment frequencies and somatic hypermutation rates, highly similar CDR3 sequence clusters, and inferred potential structural or binding similarity based on embeddings of CDR3 sequences generated with the Evolutionary Scale Modeling-2 (ESM-2) protein language model [132] have shown to predict patterns of disease status from immune repertoires [128]. AI models may be particularly helpful in cases where a spectrum of infection exists [133], or where traditional methods have limitations, for example in screening for latent tuberculosis and predicting active tuberculosis or reactivation based on immune status [134]. AI applied to immune repertoires is still only used for research; however, the established use of AI for other clinical applications [135] justifies a future feasibility of its introduction in clinical practice also for immunology-based applications.

In addition to immunoprofiling, integrating electronic health records to laboratory values and multi-omics data—such as genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics—could offer a more comprehensive view of disease mechanisms and can improve the accuracy of machine learning models [126] in identifying immunity patterns. This holistic approach enhances diagnostic precision and facilitates personalized treatment strategies by leveraging complementary insights from diverse data types [136]. The integration of health and laboratory records with digital pathology data [137] and multi-omics profiling creates opportunities to refine precision diagnostics in clinical research, progressing toward a digital twin of immunity. However, the fragmentation of the health information systems still represents a current significant challenge to further data integration.

Although not yet implemented in routine clinical labs, these methods are available in the clinical microbiology diagnostic environment. A growing body of research supports their potential in infection profiling [138]. Today, large language models assist remarkably with generating code, interpreting findings, and retrieving information. However, bioinformatic benchmarking is important and necessary. Machine learning depends on input data, and various (but commonly used) tools for the initial annotation and preprocessing of raw sequences [139, 140]. The type of pre-analysis of immune statuses indicative of infection can result in artifact signatures [102, 141]. In addition, the methods proper to machine learning encoding and models can affect the extent to which infection patterns are detected [62, 142]. Generally, models often lack rigorous benchmarking for key performance metrics such as specificity (true negative rate), sensitivity (true positive rate), and overall reproducibility, thus immune patterns might reflect inherent bias in the laboratory protocols for data generation or bioinformatic analyses. Therefore, despite patterns have been shown to be distinctive of the immune status, there are bioinformatic challenges associated with the clinical adoption of insights from sequencing data [143]. Bioinformatic pipelines should be qualified and classified as separate modules in the context of medical device software, because their results provide information and insights on a first characterization of the immune repertoire. For example, clonal expansion has been associated with pathological states [94, 144]. To improve clinical acceptance, explainability and interpretability tools which assign importance values to features in a model’s prediction, can play a crucial role. These methods allow clinicians to understand and trust the decisions made by machine learning models, especially in high-stakes environments like infection diagnostics.

As hospitals increasingly shift toward a prevention-focused strategy [145], immunoprofiling has a high potential for personalized prevention and epidemiological control of natural infection. Improvements in sequencing technologies and progress in artificial intelligence methods are closing decade-long promises of immunoprofiling, but significant challenges remain. Operational challenges, including high costs, lack of standardized protocols, and the need for specialized training, hinder the incorporation of sequencing into routine clinical practice [146–148]. Furthermore, regulatory hurdles in translating research in the clinical laboratory [149] are well known, while new challenges related to the integration of AI are emerging [150, 151]. These include data complexities and imbalance, model reproducibility and explainability [152] and concerns regarding increased security risks given the multiplication of processing activities, and less effective anonymization techniques [153].

Integration of immunoprofiling into routine clinical practice

Medical software can support clinicians in making patient-specific diagnoses and therapeutic decisions. However, before innovative research insights are translated as medical software, and become commercially available to physicians and citizens, the manufacturer needs to obtain marketing authorization (clearance as Premarket Notification 510 (k), Premarket Approval (PMA) in USA, or CE mark from notified bodies in EU) of the software for medical use. The approval certifies that the product is compliant with FDA and EMA regulations, ensuring data safety and performance of the software. The manufacturing organization needs to demonstrate its ability to provide medical devices that consistently meet regulatory and customer requirements through a Quality Management System (QMS) for medical devices (Fig. 1).

Regulatory compliance is one of the major bottlenecks to bringing scientific innovation as a product to the clinic. This is not only relevant for laboratory tests, but also for software products that fall under the category of medical devices (e.g., software that is used to influence a clinical decision) or in-vitro diagnostics (e.g., software that is used to analyze samples obtained from the human body). The regulatory challenges are related to ensuring the quality, safety and scientific validity of the product, through verification and validation activities, identification of the limits of detection, and evaluating the possible risks of the product, among others. The quality, safety and effectiveness of a research project itself are also key for regulatory compliance, and these properties are demonstrated by possessing, using, and maintaining a quality management system. The lack of best practices in the development of research software, including quality management systems, is a longstanding issue hindering knowledge transfer in academia [67, 154]. An additional difficulty is the fact that regulatory requirements are evolving, and staying up to date requires significant investment and resources. Therefore, hospital and research labs often opt to develop in-house diagnostic tests. These tests are often developed and used within a single laboratory or institution, lacking the same level of external validation as commercial tests. For example, in Europe, in-house devices are exempted from CE-marking if they are manufactured and used only within a health institution established in the European Union, and they comply with the provisions of Article 5(5) of the IVDR. Compliant with the subset of regulatory requirements mandatory for in-house use, this software cannot be distributed beyond the originating laboratory or institution where it was developed. Recent changes in regulations for Laboratory Developed Test (LDT) or in-house diagnostic testing, both in Europe and in the US, impose stricter oversight due to the need for heightened scrutiny. The FDA has introduced the Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR), a mending Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) part 820 [155] to align more closely with the international consensus standard for Quality Management Systems for medical devices used by other regulatory authorities. The European Council has also adopted new rules updating Regulation (EU) 2017/745 on medical devices (MDR) and Regulation (EU) 2017/746 on in vitro diagnostic medical devices (IVDR) [156]. It is evident that regulations differ by country, and although there are international harmonization efforts, compliance in one country does not guarantee compliance in another. These regulations have required multiple amendments and adjustments to the calendar of enforcement to help prevent shortages and ease the transition to greater transparency, while hospital labs are invariably under-staffed and under-trained in regulatory matters to do the extra work required under article 5(5) IVDR.

The Hauschild guideline to essential quality management systems (QMS) components for academic organizations is a feasible starting point in universities to reflect development of the software as a product [154]. Important standards for medical software are ISO 9001 Quality Management Systems, ISO 13485 Medical devices – quality management systems, ISO 14971 Application of risk management to medical devices, IEC 62304 Medical device software – system life cycle processes, IEC 62366 Medical devices: application of usability engineering to medical devices (Part 1 and 2), and IEC 82304 Health software—Part 1: General requirements for product safety. Principles of software engineering [157] and project management [158] can greatly support the endeavor.

Given the substantial knowledge transfer from academic institutions through spin-off companies, these considerations can be implemented through early collaborations of universities with companies. At last, to obtain marketing authorization, safety and performance must be demonstrated; this can be planned and performed in collaboration with university hospitals. However, these regulatory and engineering standards often do not consider the design principles to develop software fit for clinicians. Therefore, medical software should take into account human factors [159] that facilitate the completion of work and avoid “death by a thousand clicks” [160].

PART III

In vitro medical device software (EU) and software as a medical device (US) to support infection diagnostics through immunity and AI

Medical software is qualified and classified uniquely on its intended purpose, as described by the manufacturer. Once qualified as a medical device, the manufacturer categorizes it into one of the risk classes. This classification into risk classes regulates market access. Software engineering from design, to development, testing and deployment is embedded in an organization quality management system (Fig. 1).

Qualification of medical software

In our practical case, a software preprocessing and analyzing next-generation sequencing data of immune repertoires from human biospecimens, with the intended use to support clinical decision making regarding immune status for infectious disease diagnosis (e.g., caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, in Europe and in Switzerland), may be qualified both as

Medical Device Software⎯intended by the manufacturer to be used for one or more of the following specific medical purposes: (i) diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis, treatment or alleviation of disease, (ii) providing information by means of in vitro examination of specimens derived from the human body, including organ, blood and tissue donations, and which does not achieve its principal intended action by pharmacological, immunological or metabolic means, in or on the human body, but which may be assisted in its function by such means) or

(ii)In vitro Diagnostic Medical Device Software⎯whether used alone or in combination, to be used in vitro for the examination of specimens, including blood and tissue donations, derived from the human body, solely or principally for the purpose of providing information on one or more of the following: (i) concerning a physiological or pathological process or state; (ii) concerning the predisposition to a medical condition or a disease; (iii) to predict treatment response or reactions.

In vitro Diagnostic Medical Device Software⎯whether used alone or in combination, to be used in vitro for the examination of specimens, including blood and tissue donations, derived from the human body, solely or principally for the purpose of providing information on one or more of the following: (i) concerning a physiological or pathological process or state; (ii) concerning the predisposition to a medical condition or a disease; (iii) to predict treatment response or reactions.

The qualification depends on the software definition as a medical device software according to Article 2(1) of Regulation (EU)2017/745 – Medical Device Regulation (MDR) or the definition of in vitro diagnostic medical device according to Article 2(2) of Regulation (EU)2017/746 – IVDR. Because the intended use depends on the manufacturer, it is important to consider that: (i) IVDs generally do not require invasive testing on humans, which can reduce costs compared to MDR evaluations; (ii) focusing more on a narrower scope of analytical performance (e.g., accuracy, sensitivity, specificity) and clinical performance, which can often be derived from existing datasets or less invasive studies compared to clinical evaluations (e.g., potential new clinical investigations, multi-center trials, and extensive follow-up) required under the MDR, can be advantageous. Taken altogether, these considerations suggest that our example software qualifies as IVD because of its intended use in vitro for examination of the immune response for the purpose of providing information to the clinician and patient regarding the individual physiological and pathological state, and predisposition to infection, or predicting a potential reaction. However, the full intended purpose should be clearly expressed in the intended purpose statement, reflected in the instructions for use (IFU), and should include the specific elements outlined in Regulation EU 2017/746 Annex I, 20.4.1 (c) as detailed in the MDCG 2022–2 Guidance on general principles of clinical evidence for In Vitro Diagnostic medical devices (IVDs).

Classification of medical software

Classification follows qualification. The Medical Device Software according to 2017/745 – MDR is classified as class IIa if it is intended to provide information which is used to make decisions with diagnosis or therapeutic purposes (Rule 11). According the classification rules reported in (EU) 2017/746 – IVDR Annex VIII, and specifically to Rule 6 (e), the In vitro Diagnostic Medical Device Software is classified as Class B (as it does not classify according to Rule 3 for which software that is classified in Class C is used for determining infective disease status or immune status (Rule 3, e) or disease staging (Rule 3, g), where there is a risk that an erroneous result would lead to a patient management decision resulting in a life-threatening situation for the patient or for the patient’s offspring). Either as an MD or IVD software, claims related to the intended medical purpose of the software must be supported by clinical evidence. Information for a scientist to make a first assessment of an idea can be the Guidance on Qualification and Classification of Software in Regulation (EU) 2017/745 – MDR and Regulation (EU) 2017/746 – IVDR, and the Manual on borderline and classification for medical devices under Regulation (EU) 2017/745 on medical devices and Regulation (EU) 2017/746 on in vitro diagnostic medical devices.

In the USA, FDA [161] defines Software as Medical Device (SaMD) “software intended to be used for one or more medical purposes that perform these purposes without being part of a hardware medical device.” Based on significance of information provided by SaMD to healthcare decision (i.e., drive clinical management), and state of healthcare situation or condition (i.e., serious, not critical or not serious), SaMD is of class II according to the document “Software as a Medical Device: Possible Framework for Risk Categorization and Corresponding Considerations” IMDRF/SaMD WG/N12FINAL:2014.

In the last decade, the number of approved artificial intelligence based medical devices has increased in the USA and Europe [150]. Although the classification and regulation of software, particularly when integrating AI models, can be controversial and subject to various regulations (e.g., the EU Artificial Intelligence Act, FDA developed guiding principles for Good Machine Learning Practice (GMLP)) and interpretations across legislations, medical device regulations generally serve as the primary reference for AI-related topics in medical devices. The Artificial Intelligence/Machine Learning-enabled Working Group of the International Medical Device Regulators Forum also publishes useful guidance documents.

Clinical evidence for an NGS-AI in vitro medical device software

Considering the regulatory pathways discussed here, we will proceed with our example qualified as an in vitro diagnostic (IVD) software. Laboratory devices and stand-alone medical software for the diagnosis of infectious diseases fall under the in vitro diagnostic products (IVDs) and are devices regulated under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act) in the USA, the In Vitro Diagnostic Devices Regulation (EU) 2017/746 IVDR) in Europe, and the Swissmedic Ordinance on In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices (IvDO; SR 812.219) in Switzerland.

Although there are differences across the regulatory paths for approval (i.e., in USA: FDA Premarket Approval (PMA) process, De Novo classification), or clearance (with FDA 510(k) when the device is equivalent to a device already in market), and certification (i.e., in EU: CE marking certificate issued by a Notified Body (NB), allowing the device to be marketed in the European Economic Area (EEA)) of medical devices in different legislations, the regulations consistently apply to ensure standards are met regarding the safety and performance of the medical device, in this case software. Generally, market approval requires certifications to verify that: (i) the manufacturer has a certified Quality Management System (QMS), (ii) the safety and performance of the device comply with regulatory requirements (US), and the production quality is ensured, confirming that the manufacturer’s processes consistently produce devices that meet regulatory standards (EU). In fact, when the conformity assessment is performed by a notified body (NB), it audits first the quality management system (2 phases: 1st remote or in situ, 2nd in situ) before assessing the technical documentation. For our practical example of IVD software, we delineate the specific development of the medical device software product in Europe; however, the principles described here are broadly applicable.

Generally, a performance evaluation is required for IVDR to provide sufficient clinical evidence that demonstrates conformity with relevant General Safety and Performance Requirements (GSPR) under the normal conditions of the device’s intended use (EU)2017/745 MDR and (EU)2017/746 IVDR. The performance evaluation of a medical software in support of clinical decision for infection diagnostics demonstrates: (i) scientific validity or valid clinical association, demonstrating valid clinical association between input data and clinical condition considering the state-of-the-art, e.g., association of immune repertoire patterns with infection (ii) validation of the technical performance, that is, the ability to accurately, reliably and precisely generate the intended output, from the input data (e.g., from FASTQ files to annotated VDJ repertoire or single-sequences specific to infection), while demonstrating generalizability with real-life datasets (e.g., data generated through a different CE marked or FDA cleared/approved NGS technology device), and (iii) validation of the clinical performance to demonstrate the ability of the software to yield clinically relevant output in accordance with the intended purpose, e.g., identification of infection is equivalent (retrospective study) or of benefit (prospective clinical investigation) when compared to a method employed in routine clinical testing of infectious diseases. The contents of IVDR documents are explicitly described in EU 2017/746 Annex XIII: the performance evaluation plan (PEP) and the performance evaluation report (PER).

Since the multitude of potential indications for infectious agent/s detection, validation can be modular in nature and is also permissible on a module level when the functionality of the modules is independent of the other modules. In the case of infectious diseases, an example of an IVD stand-alone software that identifies tuberculosis infection and predicts clinical outcomes based on the immune repertoire tested through NGS, sequence data annotation and preprocessing, and by applying artificial intelligence methods, should be validated as a separate module from dengue infection (or even gammopathies). However, if scientific evidence provides a plausible method by which the predictions of clinical associations are extendable across conditions, supporting data can be evaluated by regulatory agencies.

Various ways to commercial distribution

To extend applications of NGS of immune repertoires to the clinics, a few contract research organizations (CROs) provide laboratory kits that are indicated as compliant with EU IVDR 2017/746 regulations as a precision Diagnostics (Dx) product (e.g., Illumina’s MiSeqTMDx, NextSeq 550Dx and NovaSeq 6000Dx; Qiagen’s NeuMoDx is a virus PCR testing platform Class A in Europe; Thermo Fisher’s Oncomine Dx Target Test for oncology diagnostics targeting 46 cancer driver gene variants with NGS). However, kits specific for immunoprofiling often come with disclaimers such as “end users will be required to perform additional validation when integrating our product for diagnostic applications,” “for research use only” or “clinical research applications.” Other providers indicate NGS Dx tests as CE-IVD without clarification on their clinical significance or usability. It remains to be established to which extent could IVDR certified solution be used for immunoprofiling. While experimental and analytical pitfalls remain [162], there are service providers that have certified NGS kits specific to immunoprofiling (i.e., immunoSEQ platform is FDA-authorized for specific clinical applications like clonoSEQ, used to monitor minimal residual disease (MRD) in certain cancers [163]). While ISO 15189:2022 provides accreditation standards for medical laboratories, defining the requirements for quality and competence in laboratories performing next-generation sequencing tests (such as CLIA/CAP accredited laboratories in the USA), the Quality Management System (QMS) for medical device manufacturers is outlined in the harmonized standard ISO 13485:2016, titled “Medical devices - Quality management systems - Requirements for regulatory purposes.” There are clinical trial laboratories that perform clinical research with NGS technology for biomarker testing, using a fully automated FDA and CE-IVD-approved platform. The most prevalent certified Dx physical device platforms are qualitative in vitro diagnostic tests that use targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology for clinical tumor profiling. Interestingly, only one [164] medical software is listed for “immunology” applications out of 950 FDA approvals [165]. This medical decision support software is the software supporting a multi-analyte (specific autoimmune antibodies) detection and screening system submitted in 2012 as a modification to the cleared device. This suggests that the general modus operandi for medical software is to be cleared/certified as an extension to an approved or marketed IVD instrument. In Europe, one out of two IVD applications filed will be granted certification, while most of the applications are filed to request a QMS-and-product certificate rather than the QMS certificate alone.

Writing the history of medicine forwards: from infection to health

We detect what we can measure. We hypothesize (or imagine) the rest. “Prior to the work of Koch and Pasteur demonstrating in 1876 that anthrax is caused by the microscopic bacterium Bacillus anthracis, the concept of infectious diseases did not exist.” [6]. Today infectious diseases exist. Today we can measure immunity to infection and its peculiar interactions in the individual. Could the concept of immunity redefine our understanding of infection, encapsulate the One Health vision, and contribute to a novel definition of health?

In infection, the identification of the genomic sequence of pathogens from infected individuals already enables precision diagnostics and optimized therapeutic interventions. Twenty years after the promise, microbiological high-throughput sequencing technology may be close to becoming a routine method in the diagnostic microbiology laboratory [166, 167]. The epidemiological advantage is based on the ability to specifically identify the microbial source, track transmission and timing, and therefore spread, of the disease in the population [168]. However, a pathogen does not necessarily cause disease. As “no microbe can be a pathogen without a host” [169] and the understanding of ‘Oh, it’s a pathogen in this patient, but it’s not a pathogen in that patient’ opens venues for an in-depth analysis of immunity and paves the way for precision diagnostics for infectious diseases. High-throughput sequencing of the adaptive immune BCR and TCR can guide diagnostics of infectious diseases based on the immune response signatures to the pathogen, and therefore independent from the specific causative pathogen. Combined with artificial intelligence methods, immunoprofiling uncovers plastic and elastic patterns of immunity at the personal, single-cell sequence, and repertoire levels. Therefore, entire immune repertoires which detect the host’s individual response to infection or vaccination, rather than antibody tests, could be used as surveillance tools to inform public policy. Furthermore, population strategies for mapping the population immune repertoire enables not only rapid diagnostics but also the prompt discovery of antibodies as passive immunotherapy of viral infections in new epidemics. Because major human pathogens are multi-host pathogens [170], it is important to highlight the advantage that this technology brings by enabling the investigation of the interdependency of human and animal health in unprecedented detail. This supports the concept of “One Health” as in the “One Health approach, in which human, animal and environmental health are considered together” [171]. At last, we can learn important lessons from immunity of other vertebrates in the light of evolution [172]. Although recent strategies have focused exclusively on metagenomics efforts [171] for one health, the host is essential a true One Health approach.

The benefits of validating immune BCR and TCR repertoire sequencing, artificial intelligence methods, and medical software in clinical settings extend beyond infectious diseases, vaccination status, and the “One Health” framework. For example, although diseases like certain cancers and autoimmune disorders have been linked to viral infections, a unified approach to indentifying diseases with an infectious origin, or caused by an underlying pathogen, remains elusive. Immune repertoire sequencing and AI, used across diseases, have the potential to elucidate the cross links between infectious agents and other diseases. We foresee immunoprofiling as an integration interface in clinical diagnostics across diseases, and possibly redefining a number of these diseases in the future. Ultimately, the immune response can be seen as an elastic representation of a personal history of infectious agents and pathological processes that becomes plastic with age. In conclusion, today we can measure a specific immune repertoire signature of disease; tomorrow these signatures might serve as a surrogate measure of personal health[173].
