A hybrid vision transformer with ensemble CNN framework for cervical cancer diagnosis
Authors: Aymen M Al-Hejri, Riyadh M Al-Tam, Archana Harsing Sable, Basheer Almuhaya, Sultan S Alshamrani, Kaled M Alshmrany
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide, necessitating early and accurate detection methods. This study introduces a hybrid framework utilizing Vision Transformers (ViT) and ensemble learning-based convolutional neural networks (CNN) models for cervical cancer classification based on Pap smear images. Two prominent datasets, Mendeley LBC and SIPaKMeD, are employed, encompassing nine distinct categories of cervical cell abnormalities. The proposed approach integrates pre-trained CNN models of DenseNet201, Xception, and InceptionResNetV2 to extract high-level features, further fused through ensemble learning. These features are then processed by the ViT-based encoder model designed for improved interpretability and accuracy. Experimental results demonstrate that the hybrid model achieves superior accuracy rates of 97.26%, a recall of 97.27%, a precision of 97.27%, and 96.70% for the F1-score on the Mendeley LBC dataset. For the SIPaKMeD dataset, there was an accuracy of 99.18%, a recall of 99.18%, a precision of 99.15%, and a 99.21% F1-score. On the combined dataset, the model outperformed individual pre-trained models with 95.10% accuracy and a 95.01% F1-score. Moreover, the framework incorporates augmentation with Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, specifically Grad-CAM, to provide transparent and interpretable diagnostic outcomes, enhancing its utility in clinical settings. This research underscores the potential of hybrid AI frameworks in revolutionizing cervical cancer diagnostics by offering accurate, efficient, and interpretable solutions.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Vision transformer, Ensemble learning, Pap smear images, Explainable AI

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a significant global health challenge and is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths among women, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1]. In 2020 alone, over 604,127 new cervical cancer cases were reported, resulting in nearly 341,831 deaths worldwide [2]. The high mortality rate is largely due to late-stage diagnosis, as cervical cancer progresses from precancerous lesions over several years.

In the World Health Organization report 2024, approximately 660,000 new cases of cervical cancer and 350,000 deaths were reported globally, with the worst incidence and mortality rates discovered in low- and middle-income nations due to limited access to screening and treatment services [3].

However, early detection through routine screening, such as Pap smear analysis, can significantly improve patient survival rates [4]. Despite its effectiveness, Pap smear analysis faces several limitations, including high labor costs, time-consuming procedures, and reliance on skilled pathologists. Additionally, low sample homogeneity and inter-observer variability further complicate diagnosis, particularly in rural or resource-limited settings [5, 6]. To address these challenges, there is an increasing need for computer-aided diagnostic (CAD) systems that can enhance cervical cancer detection by automating analysis and ensuring standardized, objective results. With the growing volume of medical image data, efficient processing techniques, such as medical image compression, are also crucial to optimizing data storage and transmission [7]. While traditional machine learning (ML) approaches have been applied to cervical cancer detection, deep learning (DL) techniques have demonstrated superior performance in recent years [8, 9]. However, many DL-based methods suffer from key limitations, including dependence on large annotated datasets, lack of interpretability, and suboptimal feature extraction [10]. Furthermore, DL models often function as “black boxes,” making it difficult to understand their decision-making process and increasing concerns regarding reliability in clinical settings. Encouraged by the success of attention mechanisms in medical image processing, recent studies have explored the application of Transformer-based models to medical image analysis [11–16].

DL methods, including CNNs, have progressively emerged as a prominent subfield in medical image recognition and diagnosis [17, 18]. These methods utilize ML algorithms that develop models from data to make predictions, and they are applied for detecting diseases and conditions by using both the visual and non-visual information, which improves the performance of disease diagnosis and screening [19].

This study builds upon these advancements by proposing a novel cervical cancer classification framework that emphasizes both accuracy and interpretability. The proposed model integrates an ensemble of pre-trained CNNs, including DenseNet101 and Xception, with a hybrid Transformer encoder method. By combining multiple feature extraction techniques, our framework enhances classification performance while addressing the interpretive issues associated with DL models. Related work

The classification of cervical cells has been accomplished by the implementation of several ML and DL methodologies. This section presents a collection of important critical evaluations of deep-learning approaches used to evaluate cervical cells. These approaches include transfer learning, feature fusion ensemble, and transformer models in the domain of pap smear imaging. A group of methods utilizes deep feature fusion approaches by fusing the deep features produced from different layers in a network. The dense connection is commonly used since this type of deep connection enables information flow between layers to directly pass through short connections, which helps to further strengthen the discrimination ability of the network [20, 21]. For example, P. Sukumar et al. [22] utilized specific features of images, such as histograms, gray levels, and pattern features, to distinguish between normal and aberrant cervical cells. These features were combined to determine the probability of abnormal cell growth. However, the ensemble techniques outperformed individual machine-learning algorithms, as in the study [23] that focused on identifying cervical cells using a combination of machine-learning models. The results showed that the ensemble technique performed better than an individual machine learning model. In addition, another study reveals that the authors have developed innovative models using CNN, ViT methods, and advanced ensemble learning strategies, specifically leveraging the SIPaKMeD pap-smear dataset [24]. The compelling results demonstrate that models based on ViT significantly surpass traditional CNNs in performance, and the ensemble learning techniques applied to these ViT models exceed the benchmarks set by previous research.

A comparative analysis uses five deep learning models: ResNet-101, DenseNet-161, AlexNet, VGG-19, and SqueezeNet [25]. The study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of their classification on a cervical dataset. Notably, DenseNet161 achieves the highest accuracy among the models assessed.

M. Sholik et al. [26] involved deep feature extraction techniques derived from the layers of transfer learning and vision transformers. Subsequently, these features were subjected to dimensionality reduction using principal component analysis and linear discriminant analysis. Various classifiers, including SVM, K-NN, and Logistic Regression (LR), were evaluated on the Mendeley LBC and SIPaKMeD datasets, achieving accuracy rates of 99.08% and 98.52%, respectively. This study introduced useful CNN and ViT models, but their lack of interpretability limits clinical trust. Moreover, high accuracy on certain datasets does not ensure reliability on new data due to potential variations, requiring thorough validation. Other models presented introduced the SIPaKMeD Pap smear images dataset and utilized support vector machines and deep neural networks to classify cervical cells [27]. Their results achieved a 95.35% accuracy in classifying five different classes using feature and image-based methods. This categorizes cells into five specific classes based on their cytomorphological features. While this classification is beneficial, it may limit the dataset’s applicability to other types of cervical cells. Furthermore, the authors [28] developed a DL model using a graph convolutional network (GCN) to investigate potential relationships within cervical cell images. A graph structure is established to represent cluster correlations and achieve an accuracy of 98.37%. The classification performance for abnormal cells, such as Atypical, Koilocytotic, and High-N/C-Ratio, is not satisfactory due to the limited size of the training data for these categories. This restriction may hinder the model’s ability to learn effectively from these less-represented classes, leading to lower accuracy than normal cells like superficial and granulocyte cells. Talo et al. [29] employed a transfer DenseNet-161 learning mode that is a pre-trained model to cut down on the amount of time required for model training. They achieved a 98.96% accuracy rate using their proposed model. While this study achieved commendable accuracy, it relied solely on a single DenseNet-161 model and was limited to one dataset. Regarding the ensemble bagging, the authors in [30] developed a random forest approach that was employed for the feature selection of the SIPaKMeD images dataset al.ong with an ensemble bagging classifier that aggregated the outcomes of five classifiers, resulting in an accuracy of 94.09% and 90.84% for the seven classes. The multi-class classification accuracy remains relatively low, indicating the need for further improvement, particularly across the nine classes within the combined datasets in our study.

Rahaman et al. [31] developed an HDFF model that uses deep feature fusion to detect cervical cancer at an early stage. The researchers used various techniques such as transformation and contrast adaptation augmentations, which resulted in a sixfold increase in training data to address the issue of imbalanced data. The HDFF model combines the features of four transfer models, namely VGG16, VGG19, Xception, and ResNet50, resulting in an impressive accuracy of 99.14%. The study achieved the highest accuracy on the SIPaKMeD dataset among all surveyed studies; however, the ensemble and fusion of the four DL models introduce additional complexity that warrants further consideration.

Yaman et al. [32] utilize transfer learning to extract features using either the DarkNet19 or DarkNet53 networks. The features were extracted from the SIPaKMeD and Mendeley LBC datasets using neighborhood component analysis and classified using the SVM model. The SIPaKMeD dataset achieved an accuracy of 98.26%, whereas the Mendeley LBC dataset had an accuracy of 99.47%. The proposed model utilized Neighborhood Component Analysis (NCA) to select only the 1,000 most informative features from an initial set of 21,000. Moreover, for leverage from ensemble fusion features, R. Pramanik et al. [33] developed an ensemble of three CNN transfer models employing fuzzy distance-based methods to identify cervical cancer from three standardized datasets on the disease. Regarding the Mendeley LBC dataset, the model achieved a remarkable accuracy rate of 96.96%. This study employed three transfer learning methods with ensemble learning across three separate datasets and achieved notable results. However, to ensure greater dataset diversity and robust multi-class performance, the proposed model should be applied to a combined dataset. In addition, O. Attallah [34] proposed a CerCan·Net model that utilized three efficient CNNs, namely MobileNet, DarkNet-19, and ResNet-18, which have fewer layers. They merged the features obtained from these multiple CNN layers. The CerCan·Net demonstrated an accuracy rate of 97.7% for the SIPaKMeD dataset and 100% for the Mendeley datasets using the surficial 200 engineering features. Encouraged by the great success of the self-attention mechanism as a part of the transformer model in machine translation tasks, several papers are attempting to apply the self-attention module to medical image analysis.

N.K. Chauhan et al. [35] evaluated the performance of nine DL models for cervical cancer screening. These models were fine-tuned using transfer learning for both 5-class and 2-class Pap smear image classification. Two-step data augmentation was applied to improve classifier performance and prevent overfitting. Among all models, VGG-16 achieved the highest accuracy: 94.89% for 5-class and 97.16% for 2-class classification.

Subsequent studies focus on transformer encoders, with particular emphasis on their application to medical image analysis. For instance, in the study [12] the MaxViT model involved replacing MBConv blocks with ConvNeXtv2 blocks and MLP blocks with GRN-based MLPs. This was achieved by utilising the SIPaKMeD and Mendeley LBC datasets, as well as the pap smear datasets. The proposed model successfully decreased the number of parameters and improved the final accuracy to 99.02% for the SIPaKMeD dataset and 99.48% for the LBC dataset. While the MaxCerVixT model shows great promise for cervical cancer detection, its limitations related to data quality, diversity, real-world applicability, interpretability, and resource requirements must be addressed to enhance its effectiveness and adoption in clinical practice.

Moreover, the researchers developed the CervixFormer Swin transformer along with a fusion concatenating ensemble aimed at the classification of cervical malignancies [36]. The training process utilized four distinct datasets, including the Mendeley LBC and SIPaKMeD Pap Smear datasets, employing the explainable GradCAM technique to facilitate visualization. Upon evaluation using a private dataset, the model achieved a noteworthy accuracy of 94%.

The researchers employed SeNet, which blends the architectures of ResNet101 and DenseNet169 [37]. This approach ensured that the ViT module would incorporate the extensive spatial context that was often overlooked by traditional CNNs. The findings from each module were then integrated using a weighted voting method. Their proposed framework achieved remarkable success, recording an accuracy of 97.44% on the Mendeley dataset and 95.88% on the SIPaKMeD dataset. In addition, B.Z. Wubineh et al. [38] introduce a model named the RES_DCGAN. This model is designed for data augmentation and aims to enhance the classification of cervical cells using the ResNet50V2 architecture integrated with a self-attention mechanism. This combination is intended to improve the model’s generalizability and performance in classifying cervical cells. DenseNet121 with self-attention achieved accuracies of 92% and 95% in multiclass and binary classification, respectively, using the SIPaKMeD dataset.

The study introduced an XAI approach for classifying cervical cancer from liquid cytology images [39]. The study utilized the Mendeley dataset with four types of lesions. The Grad-CAM visually suggests the areas of the lesion that prompted the classifier’s choice. Three CNN architectures with varying convolutional layers were evaluated. The proposed model achieved 100% accuracy for the binary classification and 97% accuracy for the four-class classification.

Despite significant progress in cervical cell classification using DL techniques, several critical gaps remain unaddressed, as presented in Table 1. First, many studies rely heavily on single datasets, such as SIPaKMeD or Mendeley LBC, which limits the generalizability of their models to diverse clinical environments. The lack of model validation on combined or heterogeneous datasets restricts robustness, particularly for multi-class classification involving rare cell types. Second, while ensemble-based methods have demonstrated improved accuracy, they often introduce increased model complexity, which poses challenges for clinical deployment, interpretability, and computational efficiency, especially with more than two individual models. Additionally, although advanced feature selection and fusion strategies have been explored, few studies have examined their real-world interpretability in a clinical setting, an essential aspect for gaining trust among healthcare practitioners. Lastly, imbalanced class distribution, particularly for abnormal cell types, remains a recurring limitation that affects model performance. These gaps indicate a pressing need for research focused on unified models trained on diverse datasets, with an emphasis on explainability, scalability, and clinically relevant performance across all cell categories.

Materials and methods

In this section, the description of the developed models for detecting cervical cancer by utilizing Pap-smear images is provided by applying transfer learning with ensemble learning and ViT transformer techniques. Initially, cervical cell cancer data from the Mendeley LBC and SIPaKMeD datasets, which contain Pap smear images, were prepared. Figure 1 illustrates the sequential procedure of constructing the model, beginning with the splitting of the data and the augmentation of the training dataset. Each dataset is individually used to develop models, and then the two datasets are combined to train models again, including a variety of equipment and tools to capture cervical cells. Subsequently, the pre-trained transfer learning models are introduced individually. Afterward, the high-level features are combined to form the ensemble learning model, which acts as the fundamental framework for the convolutional network. In the end, the model is fed into the transformer encoder model to classify cervical cell cancer, while also offering an explainable analysis.

Dataset description

Mendeley LBC pap smear slides dataset

Mendeley LBC Pap Smear was obtained from Babina Diagnostic Pvt., India [40]. This data consisted of information from 460 patients who had undergone cervical screening examinations. It has four categories of cervical cancer: Negative, LSIL (Low-grade intraepithelial lesions), HSIL (High-grade intraepithelial lesions), and SCC (Squamous Cell Carcinoma). There was a total of 963 images that were taken from Pap smear slides with a magnification of 400 times. Of these, 613 images were classified as belonging to the normal or NILM group, while 350 images were classified as belonging to the abnormal category.

SIPaKMeD dataset

During the year 2018, academics affiliated with the University of Ioannina in Greece were responsible for the production of the dataset [27]. In the SIPaKMeD database, there are 4049 images of women’s cells that have been meticulously isolated from 966 cluster cell images of Pap smear slides. These images may also be added to the database. An OLYMPUS BX53F optical microscope was used to acquire the images, which were obtained through the utilization of a CCD digicam modification. Sup-intermediate, Parabasal, Koilocytotic, Dyskeratotic, and Metaplastic cancer cells are included in the dataset, among the majority of cancer cells that have been classified into these five groups. AI models

DenseNet201

The DenseNet201 represents an advanced iteration derived from the dense-network framework. This architectural design employs a unique approach by creating direct connections between every layer in a feed-forward manner, facilitating interactions among all constituent layers [42]. Moreover, the DenseNet201 architecture incorporates multiple pooling layers while maintaining a compact structural form. Consequently, these design choices contribute to a reduction in both the number of parameters and the overall complexity of the model, thereby improving its operational efficiency.

InceptionResNetV2

The stem of the network is based on InceptionV4; however, the architecture of the network is comparable to that of InceptionResNetV1 [43]. There is a shortcut link located on the far-left side of each module. A combination of inception architecture and residual connections is used to achieve superior classification results. Both the input and the output of the convolutional operation in the inception module must be identical for the inception convolutional operation to become successful.

Xception

The Xception model, a type of deep convolutional neural network, has demonstrated significant advancements in its use for medical classification tasks. The pre-trained version of the network is 71 layers deep and has been trained on over a million images from the ImageNet database [44]. The development of this technology was undertaken by researchers employed by Google. Google introduced the concept of Inception modules in convolutional neural networks as a transitional stage between normal convolution and the depth-wise separable convolution operation. The layers below 106 have been fine-tuned and made untrainable.

The proposed AI ensemble and transformer encoder learning models

Applying ensemble learning principles has led to the development of various DL models instead of relying solely on an individual DL model [45]. Consequently, integrating these multiple models represents the final phase in the prediction process [46]. Enhanced classification outcomes can be achieved by leveraging a broader range of pertinent data from differing classifiers. The ensemble method for feature extraction introduced in this study integrates two pre-trained DenseNet101 and InceptionResNetV2 deep learning models, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

The architecture of the proposed concatenated ensemble-based features model

In the proposed ensemble learning framework, deep features are extracted from two CNN architectures: DenseNet101 and InceptionResNetV2. To obtain these features, the classification layers of each model are removed, and the final convolutional blocks are utilized for deep feature extraction. The hybrid model is constructed by fusing the high-level representations obtained from both CNN backbones. Specifically, global average pooling (GAP) is applied to the output of each model to transform the extracted feature maps into vector representations. These high-level feature vectors are then concatenated to form a comprehensive feature space. The resulting fused feature vector is subsequently embedded and passed to the prediction stage, which is based on a ViT architecture.

A transformer is a type of DL model that uses a self-attention mechanism to give different parts of the input data different levels of importance. It does this by using an encoder-decoder architecture. This study employs the ViT framework, integrating an encoder methodology to identify early-stage cervical cancer from pap-smear images. ViT encoder yields two outputs from the processed input image: a comprehensive token embedding vector that spans all layers, as well as the attention weights from each head. The self-attention mechanism facilitates the production of a representation for a singular input sequence by creating relational connections among various positions within that sequence [47, 48]. Explainable AI

Explainable AI (XAI) is a growing area of study in machine learning, particularly in the field of medicine. A philosophy of explainable artificial intelligence for static images is that a high-performing DL model accurately recognizes the relevant part of the image without placing excessive emphasis on insignificant details [51]. There are multiple approaches for creating visual representations of predictions from image classification models, many of which utilize post hoc techniques. Experimental setup

The proposed AI hybrid model is characterized by its end-to-end training approach. For both our training phase and the early stopping callback, a learning rate of 0.001 was implemented, utilizing an Adam optimizer with a clipping value of 0.2 and a patience parameter set to 30. Our training methodology for all AI models comprises a comprehensive iteration over 100 epochs, aimed at fine-tuning the hyperparameters effectively. The image is 256, a patch size of 2, and an input dimension of 20, complemented by a drop rate of 0.01 applied across all layers, along with 8 attention heads. In this context, to illustrate the transformation of high-dimensional vectors into lower-dimensional counterparts while ensuring no loss occurs, the parameters for both embed_dim and num_mlp have been meticulously calibrated through empirical techniques, achieving optimized values of 64 and 256, respectively.

Results and discussion

This study introduces various methodologies within the realms of ML and DL, including transfer learning and ensemble learning, which focus on integrating multiple models. Additionally, it explores the transformer-based ViT model for the classification of cervical cell carcinoma. The study employs two distinct datasets: the Mendeley LBC Pap smear dataset and the SIPaKMeD dataset, conducting both individual and combined experimental analyses.

Experiment results for Mendeley LBC dataset

Initially, the pre-trained transfer learning models, namely DenseNet101, Xception, and InceptionResNetV2, are employed. The process involves freezing and partially fine-tuning the layers. The models received fine-tuning starting at layers 481, 642, and 106 for the DenseNet201, InceptionResNetV2, and Xception, respectively. The deep features obtained from the DenseNet101 and InceptionResNetV2 models are combined without including any classification layers and then fed into the hybrid transformer model.

The DenseNet101 model and InceptionResNetV2 have achieved an accuracy of 95.89% and 95.21%, respectively, which are better than the Xception model. However, the ensemble model combines the high features of DenseNet101 and InceptionResNetV2 models without classification layers, it exceeds the accuracy of pre-trained models and achieving a 96.58%. Limitations and future work

The proposed hybrid transformer model incurs a high computational cost due to its large number of fine-tuned layers, trainable parameters, and extended training time. While more streamlined models offer greater computational efficiency, they tend to underperform in terms of accuracy. In addition, there is a lack of availability of datasets with imbalanced labels, or integrating the data alongside the medical clinic data, and the interpretability of XAI for clinicians. For future work, we aim to combine many datasets of cervical cancer from different modalities and carry out federated learning models [56], and share these models as platforms in available web sources and Android [57], for multiple diseases that will be accessed by patients, and suggest feedback for doctors and patients. Conduct a full computer-aided system with segmentation lesions, such as Detectron2, and YOLOvX [58, 59].

Conclusions

This paper proposed the deep features-fusion ensemble and the ViT approach to classify cervical cell carcinoma using the two Mendeley LBC Pap smear and the SIPaKMeD datasets. Initially, the pre-trained transfer learning models, namely DenseNet101, InceptionResNetV2, and Xception, are employed individually. Then, freezing and partially fine-tuning the layers. The deep features are combined without including any classification layers and then fed into the hybrid transformer encoder model. All models are employed per dataset alone, then employed again on both datasets with nine labels. The ensemble model exceeded the accuracy of pre-trained models, achieving 96.58%, 98.69%, and 94.44% for the Mendeley LBC Pap smear dataset, and SIPaKMeD dataset, and both combined, respectively. On the other hand, the hybrid transformer encoder model has outperformed previous models, recording accuracies of 97.26%, 99.18%, and 95.10% for the Mendeley LBC Pap smear dataset and the SIPaKMeD dataset, respectively, when combined. XAI interprets the classification findings to accurately determine and differentiate the location of lesions. These models provide promised outcomes and have the potential to offer significant feedback in various medical domains.
