Unsteady CFD simulation of a rotor blade under various wind conditions
Authors: Sa. Kasmaiee, Si. Kasmaiee, A. Farshad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Wind and gusts can significantly impact the performance of rotors and turbines. The transient behavior of the rotor should be carefully examined to account for these effects. This paper investigates the unsteady aerodynamic characteristics of a rotor blade under different wind conditions, such as direction, speed, and angle. A 3D transient Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulation using the dynamic mesh technique is performed to analyze the rotor dynamics. The unsteady Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (URANS) equations with the k-ω SST turbulence model are solved. The rotor blade used for this study is the U15XXL Combo KV29 industrial blade, which has not been numerically analyzed before. The results show that wind in the same direction as the rotation reduces the thrust more than lateral or opposite wind. Lateral wind with a speed lower than 5 m/s decreases the blade performance, but higher speeds increase it. Higher lateral wind speeds also cause two peaks in the torque curve, forming a butterfly wing shape in the polar torque plot and multiple extrema in the torque curve with increasing speed. The maximum thrust shifts slightly to the left with increasing lateral wind speed. The lateral angle does not affect the average thrust produced in one blade revolution but only causes a spatial shift. The thrust production decreases as the angle approaches the opposite direction of rotation. The motion amplitude decreases, and the curve becomes smoother as this angle increases. A nearly straight line similar to no side wind is observed at 60 and 90 degrees, which is attributed to the constant effective angle of attack during the rotation cycle.

Introduction

Rotor and propeller performance in edgewise flight has attracted considerable attention recently due to the widespread use of fixed or variable-pitch propellers in modern air taxi and unmanned aerial vehicles1–3. Compared to fixed-wing aircraft, which are more suitable for long flights in open environments4–6, multicopters have advantages for short duration missions in open or confined spaces due to their simplicity, maneuverability and vertical take-off capability7. The steady and unsteady flow phenomena around the rotor blade can be investigated through experimental8 or various numerical methods. The numerical methods can be further classified into 1-D Blade Element Momentum (BEM) methods and 3-D Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) techniques. Recently, CFD analysis has been shown to predict the efficiency of hovering rotors9 accurately. The hover condition is a critical design point because of its high power consumption and the challenge of predicting the strength and position of the vortex core10. The CFD technique can accurately handle a rotating blade's complex unsteady flow phenomena. Although it is computationally expensive, the CFD technique has been widely used as an effective analysis tool in academia due to the rapid advancement of High-Performance Computing (HPC) clusters11. According to the literature related to CFD, Lanzafame et al.12 performed CFD analysis for performance prediction of the NREL Phase IV rotor. Bai et al.13 developed a BEM model for small rotor blades, successfully validated through CFD analysis. Tong et al.14 studied the effects of blade airfoil chord length and rotor diameter on the aerodynamic performance of straight-bladed vertical-axis turbines. Yoon and Lee15 investigated the aerodynamic parameter analysis and design of a quadrotor air vehicle in hover. Their results indicated that optimized twist angle and twist-starting position enable maximum hovering thrust in the proposed quad-copter. Lei and Wang16 studied the aerodynamic characteristics of a micro quadrotor aircraft considering the horizontal wind disturbance. They showed that the rotors' thrust increases gradually with wind speed, resulting from increased pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces. Cai et al.17 simulated the unsteady aerodynamic configuration of a full-scale horizontal axis wind turbine with consideration of wind shear, tower shadow and yaw motion. Their results indicated that each blade simultaneously reaches its maximum and minimum aerodynamic loads during the rotation cycle. Al-Ghriybah et al.18 studied the effect of the inner blade position on the rotor's performance to determine the inner blade's best position. The behavior of the blade tip vortices of a turbine equipped with a brimmed diffuser shroud was investigated by Takahashi et al.19. Their simulation results indicated that a pair of vortices consisting of a blade tip vortex and a counter-rotating vortex was generated between the blade tip and the inner surface of the diffuser. The rotor was evaluated for steady and unsteady aerodynamics analysis under surge motion20, yawed gusts21, ground effect22,23, and variable pitch24. Zhang et al.20 demonstrated that the mean power of the twin-rotor is increased by up to 13% under surge motion compared to the single rotor and that the small spacing ratio enhances the power gain. They revealed that surge motion significantly impacts the wake interaction between the twin rotors and facilitates wake recovery. Cai and Gunasekaran21 examined the propeller performance under steady and sinusoidally time-varying freestream (stream-wise or longitudinal gust). They reported that the propeller thrust, power, pitching, and rolling moment increase with the incidence angle at the same advance ratio. Tanabe et al.23 simulated a variable-pitch-controlled quadrotor drone using a high-fidelity CFD solver in the ground effect. They detected a flow recirculation for the quadrotor drone at an intermediate height of approximately 0.5 times the rotor diameter. Also, they observed that the required power of the quadrotor drone varies significantly with the rotor height from the ground and that a local power increase occurs around a rotor height of 0.5D. Nowadays, the use of artificial intelligence is being considered in all branches of engineering25–27. In the literature, studies have been conducted to improve the efficiency of rotors by optimizing blade shape28,29 or using flow control methods30–32. The effect of the functional parameters of the blowing and suction jets on pitching airfoils in dynamic stall conditions was discussed by the authors in32–34. The authors30 recently optimized the blowing jet performance for a dynamic stall airfoil. They found that the optimal jet location was near the leading edge vortex formation, about 2 to 5 percent of the chord. The jet angle of 55 to 70 degrees improved the aerodynamic performance better. Salah et al.35 studied the performance of dual-rotor wind turbines (DRWT). They show that the DRWT significantly affects the efficiency of wind energy.

Considerable research has been carried out on fixed and variable-pitch rotor blades, focusing on commercial blades featuring symmetrical blades like Naca0012 or Naca0015. Several studies have also been conducted on the NREL rotor. However, the authors did not find a study that works on the Combo KV29 rotor. On the other hand, most of the works investigated the steady state of the flow, and limited studies investigated the unsteady flow behavior. In the unsteady flow studies, limited cases were investigated due to the high computational time; for example, Ali and Kim11 only investigated 3 cases of wind condition (without tilt, with tilt -20, and with yaw -20). On the other hand, numerical modeling of rotor aerodynamics and performance under wind poses several challenges. Therefore, the resulting rotor requires more in-depth studies for performance evaluation under different operational conditions. This study examined this rotor numerically under different winds for the first time. In addition, we tried to consider all the parameters and conduct a comprehensive study on wind's effect on the rotor's performance. In total, 16 different scenarios were considered for wind speed and direction, and a comprehensive study was conducted on the effect of wind on the performance of this rotor. Three-dimensional computational fluid dynamics was employed for this investigation. The motion is modeled as a dynamic mesh and the flow is solved unsteadily to investigate the physics of the flow more accurately. In the following sections, the governing equations of the problem are presented first. Then, the modeling approach is described, and after validating the solution and ensuring its accuracy, the performance of the rotor is examined in the presence of wind. Several wind scenarios are considered, and the effect of wind direction, angle, and speed on thrust and rotor torque is investigated. Flow physics is studied by examining contours and isosurfaces.

Simulation set-up

U15XXL Combo KV29 industrial rotor blade was used. This is an industrial blade produced by T Motor Company. This engine is used in unmanned vehicles such as quadrotors. The reason for using it in this study was that the main and final goal is designing a quadrotor that is capable of transporting hospital equipment and supplies so that transportation in the hospital can be done more easily and quickly. For this reason, this industrial rotor was used, and aerodynamic analyses were performed on it in different conditions to design its control system. This rotor has features such as considerable power, a matched propulsion solution, stability, and reliability that are necessary for our quadrotor. The experimental model of this rotor was prepared, and then a computer-aided design (CAD) model of the rotor was created using Solidwork software. The CAD model was generated from a mesh file obtained by 3D laser scanning of the rotor. A view of this model can be seen in Fig. 1. This blade's length, width, and depth are 62, 6.51, and 2.16 inches, respectively.Figure 1CAD model of U15XXL Combo KV29 blade.

CAD model of U15XXL Combo KV29 blade.

In this study, a second-order two-dimensional finite volume method was applied, and a SIMPLE algorithm was utilized to couple pressure–velocity. Air density was considered as an ideal gas. The turbulent property was modeled using URNAS models. The k-ω SST was used because of its reported accuracy in previous studies11. The discretization method adopted the second order upwind scheme. The internal iteration of 20 iterations each time was selected. The simulation convergence was checked by monitoring residuals less than 10−6. We considered the time step to be small, and this caused each step to have little change compared to the previous step, and 20 repetitions are enough to converge to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}^{-6}$$\end{document}10-6. Because we set the condition as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}^{-6}$$\end{document}10-6, many times, it did not even reach 20 repetitions, and it was left out of the loop after 10 to 15 repetitions.

Computational domain & boundary conditions

Considering that the dynamic mesh model is supposed to be used, we must define the rotating computational zone near the model and the stationary zone in the rest. Therefore, the calculation area should be divided into two parts according to Fig. 2. In order to be able to use this computational area in the dynamic mesh method, the rotating zone is designed as a cylinder. The rotating zone is twice the length of the blade. The length of the stationary zone from the back of the blade is about 20 times the length of the blade, and from the front, it is 6 times the length of the blade. In Section "Domain independency", the domain independence is examined to ensure the sufficiency of the domain size. The boundary condition of the stationary zone's side walls was considered a pressure outlet, the cross section near the blade was assumed as inlet velocity, and the end section of the cylinder was considered a pressure outlet. The wall with the no-slip condition was also considered on the blade. The boundary surface of the inner cylinder was also considered as the interface of two zones. In Fig. 2, computational domain and boundary conditions are specified.Figure 2Computational domain and boundary conditions of the zones.

Computational domain and boundary conditions of the zones.

The mesh was created using the commercial software Pointwise. Structured and unstructured hybrid grids were used for meshing. On the surface of the blade, the main part of the blade was a meshed structure, but the tip of the blade and its hub were meshed in a triangular shape. The boundary layer was meshed as 10 cubic structured layers, and according to the selected turbulence model, the size of the first layer was considered such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${y}^{+}$$\end{document}y+ of this layer is about one. Except for the first ten layers related to the boundary layer, the mesh inside the computational zone was also considered unstructured. The T-rex feature of pointwise software has been used to build this mesh. In Fig. 3, a sample of the mesh inside the moving and fixed cylinder and the structured mesh of the boundary layer can be seen. This is a cross-sectional view of the grid in the z = 0 plane. In Fig. 4, the final mesh used on the blade's surface can be observed. Time independency

In order to solve the unsteady flow, it must first be determined how many cycles (rotation of one complete revolution of the blade) are needed for the simulation to converge. In fact, we continue the solution until the unsteady solution in a blade motion cycle has little difference from the previous cycle. For this purpose, mesh 3 (one million and seven hundred thousand cells) with a time step of 0.0001 s was considered. Considering that a maximum of 90% throttle is used in real flight, we consider the same engine speed for checking. This engine speed is equal to 2111 rpm. According to the diagram of thrust and torque versus the number of cycles for this engine revolution in Fig. 6, from the 5th cycle onwards, the results have not changed much with respect to the subsequent cycles. Therefore, cycles 6 to 7 were considered as the solution of the transient state in this simulation, and all simulations continued until cycle 7.Figure 6The effect of the number of rotation cycles on the angular speed of 2111 rpm for (a) production thrust (b) production torque.

The effect of the number of rotation cycles on the angular speed of 2111 rpm for (a) production thrust (b) production torque.

Numerical validation

For the conditions of 30, 60, and 90% throttle, the simulation was performed and compared with the experimental results. The experimental results were obtained from data from the rotor blade manufacturing company, which is available on the manufacturer's website39. The percentage error of the simulation with the experimental results can be seen in Fig. 8. As can be seen, the accuracy of the predicted torque is acceptable. Its error is below 10% and has captured the experimental curve's trend well. In the thrust force chart, the error of the simulation is limited to 17%. The percentage error increases with the increase in rotational speed, which is expected because as the rotational speed increases, the interaction of the rotating area with the still air increases, making the physics of the flow more complicated. When the physics of the flow becomes complicated and the interaction of eddies increases, the turbulence model cannot predict the flow's behavior correctly. The damping effect of the model increases, causing the simulation results to be lower than the real ones, and the error increases. But pay attention, this turbulence model is still more accurate than other models11 and has fewer errors than other Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) models. In other studies, this amount of error has been common for simulations. For example, consider the recent study of Wang et al.40, who investigated a vertical axis wind turbine with a low rotational speed of 68.78 rpm. They investigated the effect of the wind velocity. According to their results, when the wind speed was low and the interaction between the rotating area and the still air was less, the error was about 10% with the experimental results, and when the speed was high, their error increased to 20%, so their maximum error was 20%. This high error occurred when the vortex structure increased and they had more complex flow physics (similar to the present study). Therefore, our result is similar to previously reported numerical data at the quantitative levels and matches the experimental result. In other words, the maximum error for complex samples is about 20% in other studies. Hence, the accuracy of the simulation is appropriate for low and high rotational speed and the simulation is done correctly.Figure 8The effect of rotational speed and comparison with experimental results in (a) production thrust (b) production torque.

Results and discussion

Wind inflow effects on the performance of the turbine blade

The angle and direction of the wind's interaction with the objects cause changes in the systems' efficiency41–43. In different vehicles, the wind can hit the blade horizontally or vertically. For example, vertical wind with an angle is often observed in wind turbines or propeller motors. The horizontal wind is seen in devices such as quadrotors. On the other hand, in air vehicles with tilt-rotor change mechanisms, we see changes in the wind direction from horizontal to vertical and vice versa. Therefore, all these situations are considered, and the wind direction and angle effect are investigated in this study. In Section "The effect of wind direction ", the effect of wind direction on blade performance was investigated. Then, in Section "Effect of speed in lateral wind", the effect of speed in lateral wind was investigated and finally, the wind angle was examined on the blade's performance. A total of 16 different cases were simulated, and the effect of wind on different conditions was investigated.

The effect of wind direction

In this section, we examined how wind direction affects blade performance. Wind turbines and quadrotors are examples of practical applications where side wind influences their blade efficiency and performance. The blade performance can improve or deteriorate depending on wind direction, angle and amplitude. Generally, wind can act on the blade in two ways: laterally (perpendicular to blade rotation direction) and longitudinally (along blade rotation direction), which can be either in the same direction or opposite to rotation. Conclusions

This paper presents a CFD numerical study of the unsteady aerodynamic performance of a rotor blade under different wind conditions. The rotor blade used is the U15XXL Combo KV29 industrial blade, which has no available CAD model. Therefore, a 3D laser scanning technique was applied to generate the CAD model from a mesh file based on an experimental sample. A 3D transient CFD simulation using the dynamic mesh method was performed to analyze the rotor dynamics. The URANS equations with the k-ω SST turbulence model were solved to simulate the rotor with angular velocities of 863, 1592 and 2111 rpm under various wind directions, speeds and angles.
