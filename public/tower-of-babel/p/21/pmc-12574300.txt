The effects of combining anodal transcranial direct current stimulation with robot-assisted gait training on lower limb motor function and the motor cortex regulation of stroke patients
Authors: Yujia Zhang, Yuqian Zhang, Beisi Zheng, Shishi Chen, Hong Yu, Lei Dai, Wanying Zhang, Haiping Huang, Xinyi Su, Manting Cao, Jianer Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe therapeutic effect and underlying mechanism of combining transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) with robot-assisted gait training (RAGT) for stroke patients remain unclear.MethodsThe goal of this study is to examine whether anodal tDCS can augment the beneficial effects of RAGT on motor function of the lower limbs in stroke survivors. Furthermore, the study aims to explore the brain motor function network underlying regulation mechanism of this integrated therapeutic approach.The patients were randomly assigned to two groups: the anode tDCS + RAGT group (n = 22) and the RAGT group (n = 21). In the experimental group, patients underwent a 20-minute session of anodal tDCS followed by a 20-minute RAGT session. Conversely, the control group patients only received 20 min of RAGT. These sessions were conducted 5 days a week for a duration of 4 weeks. Hemoglobin alterations in the primary motor cortex (M1) and pre-motor cortex/supplementary motor cortex area (PMC/SMA) of patients at rest were measured using Functional Near Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS). Additionally, the Fugl-Meyer Lower Extremity Assessment (FMA-LE) was utilized to evaluate the functionality of the lower extremities.ResultsA total of 38 stroke patients successfully completed the study. Significant improvements were observed in the FMA-LE scores of both groups following treatment. Notably, the anode tDCS + RAGT group demonstrated a remarkable enhancement in FMA-LE compared to the control group. Furthermore, the combination of anodal tDCS + RAGT strengthened the functional connectivity between LM1-RPMC/SMA.ConclusionsThe combination of anodal tDCS and RAGT can enhance lower limb motor function in stroke patients, while also modulating the connectivity within the motor network. Additionally, the elevation of functional connectivity between the bilateral cerebral hemispheres, quantifiable through fNIRS, could potentially serve as a valuable biomarker in stroke patients.Trial registrationThis study has been registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (no. ChiCTR2400088222). This article has been registered on August 13th, 2024, and it was registered retrospectively.Keywords: Stroke, Anodal tDCS, Robot-assisted gait training, Functional near-infrared spectroscopy

Introduction

Stroke is a prevalent disease that frequently leads to permanent disability, thus emerging as a significant factor affecting human health [1]. Notably, approximately 50–60% of stroke survivors experience varying degrees of lower limb motor dysfunction, despite undergoing rehabilitation [2]. This dysfunction elevates the risk of falls, impairs daily activities, and seriously compromises patients’ quality of life [3]. Consequently, aiding post-stroke patients in enhancing their lower limb motor function stands as a pivotal area of focus in post-stroke research.

The rehabilitation treatment of motor dysfunction is based on the principle of motor relearning to promote the plasticity of motor neural network. Robot-assisted Gait Training (RAGT) includes intensive, repetitive, task-oriented exercise training that is essential in motor relearning, and has been proved to be effective in promoting motor recovery in patients with chronic stroke [4–6]. RAGT can reduce the labor intensity of therapists while providing task specific training to improve the dysfunction after stroke [7, 8]. Studies have shown that [9], compared with traditional gait training, RAGT can optimize the movement and muscle activation mode of the affected lower limb joints of stroke patients during walking.

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) possesses the capability to modulate cortical excitability, thereby facilitating the induction of brain plasticity [10]. tDCS employs a mild direct current applied to the cerebral cortex to trigger plasticity by modifying the neuronal membrane potential and altering the ion distribution across the membrane, thereby promoting the restoration of neurological function in the brain tissue of stroke patients [11]. According to the interhemispheric competitive inhibition model, tDCS effectively restores the balance of competitive inhibition between the bilateral hemispheres by either increasing the excitability of the affected hemisphere via the anode or decreasing the excitability of the contralateral hemisphere through the cathode. This approach is utilized in the treatment of motor dysfunction resulting from stroke [10, 12, 13].

However, it is important to acknowledge that numerous uncertainties still surround the precise effectiveness of tDCS in facilitating rehabilitation. Firstly, despite the existence of a theoretical framework explaining how tDCS influences neurological function, a comprehensive large-scale clinical trial is yet to validate its ability to significantly aid in the restoration of neurological function within the brain tissue of stroke patients. Additionally, the assertion that tDCS effectively restores the equilibrium of competitive inhibition between the bilateral hemispheres remains unsupported by adequate evidence-based medical data. Secondly, while the interhemispheric imbalance model has garnered considerable attention from the academic community in recent years, its actual correlation with stroke recovery remains a subject of debate. Several studies have even challenged the universality of this model. In recent years, despite studies indicating potential benefits of combining anodal tDCS with RAGT in stroke treatment over single interventions [14, 15], conclusive evidence remains elusive regarding its superior efficacy compared to behavior training alone. This implies that while the combination of tDCS and RAGT holds promise as a supplementary stroke rehabilitation method, it’s premature to consider it the definitive post-stroke rehabilitation standard. Furthermore, its precise mechanism of action requires additional comprehensive investigation.

Resting state functional connectivity (RSFC) pertains to the temporal correlation of neurophysiological activities across various brain regions in the absence of any externally imposed tasks. This method serves as an efficacious approach to delineate brain functional networks [16].

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies conducted during resting states have revealed distinct differences in functional connectivity patterns between stroke patients and healthy individuals. Furthermore, these abnormal functional connectivity patterns have been linked to the recovery of motor function in patients following a stroke [17]. Research has indicated that the patterns of functional connectivity within core motor area networks, including the primary motor cortex (M1), premotor cortex (PMC), and supplementary motor area (SMA), are intimately linked to the restoration of motor function following a stroke [18]. During the initial phase of a stroke, the functional connections between these regions frequently experience disruptions, manifesting as reduced connection strength compared to neurotypical individuals. However, with the implementation of rehabilitation training interventions, the functional connectivity of the affected hemisphere gradually improves, accompanied by significant enhancements in interhemispheric connectivity. Notably, the magnitude of connectivity enhancement correlates positively with the effectiveness of recovery, highlighting the critical importance of optimizing the connectivity patterns within these regions to facilitate motor function recovery after a stroke [19].

Studies have demonstrated a direct correlation between the values obtained through functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) and fMRI, suggesting that optical brain connectivity is closely linked to functional brain structure. This evidence supports the reliability and validity of fNIRS-RSFC [20]. While fMRI remains the gold standard for assessing resting cortical activity during stroke recovery, it still faces certain limitations, including a restricted monitoring environment, elevated costs, and unsuitability for claustrophobic patients or those with metal implants. fNIRS offers superior portability compared to fMRI, boasts reduced noise levels, demonstrates relatively low sensitivity to motion artifacts, and is cost-effective, making it an ideal choice for patients in resting mode [21–24].

Hence, the objective of this study was to investigate the possibility of enhancing motor function recovery after stroke through the combination of anodal tDCS and RAGT. We hypothesized that anodal tDCS, when combined with RAGT, could potentially enhance the recovery of lower limb motor function and facilitate improved functional connectivity within the motor cortex network of stroke patients. Therefore, this study primarily aims to investigate two key aspects: (1) the therapeutic efficacy of combining anodal tDCS with RAGT in stroke patients, and (2) the utilization of fNIRS to explore the impact of this combined therapy on the functional connectivity patterns of the cerebral motor cortex in this patient population.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study took place at the Zhejiang Rehabilitation Medical Center between August 2023 and September 2024. Forty-three patients were randomly assigned to two groups: one group received anodal tDCS combined with RAGT, while the other group underwent RAGT treatment alone. As illustrated in Fig. 1, five patients were excluded due to either being transferred or discharged. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) fulfillment of the diagnostic criteria for stroke, which was confirmed by CT/MRI images; (2) age ranging from 18 to 75 years old, experiencing a first-time stroke, and being within 1 to 6 months of the onset of the disease. 3) The modified Ashworth grade of the lower limbs must be ≤ 2, and the Brunnstrom stage should be ≥ III; 4) Standing balance should be at level II; 5) The ability to comprehend and adhere to instructions is essential; 6) Participants should have no skull defects, visible brain edema, or evident brain atrophy. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Individuals with severe cognitive impairment or speech disorders that hinder cooperation during training; (2) Hemianopia or any form of visual impairment; (3) Vestibular dysfunction, including symptoms like dizziness and tinnitus; (4) Presence of lower limb fractures, severe bone and joint disorders, or severe heart, lung, liver, or kidney diseases that impede training; (5) Postural hypotension; (6) A history of epilepsy, metal pacemakers, metal implants in the brain, or skull defects that render transcranial direct current stimulation treatment or evaluation unsuitable; (7) Expected poor compliance or inability to complete the trial. All participants provided written informed consent. This study has been ethically approved by the medical ethics committee of Zhejiang Rehabilitation Medical Center (no. ZKYLL23081003) and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (no. ChiCTR2400088222).

Intervention

Transcranial direct current stimulation

The is 300 portable transcranial direct current stimulator, manufactured by Sichuan Intelligent Electronic Industry Co., Ltd. in China, was utilized. The mode of stimulation employed was anodic. Prior to commencing treatment, rectangular electrode pieces (dimensions: 5 cm × 7 cm) were inserted into sponges soaked in isotonic saline and secured using elastic bandages. In alignment with the international EEG 10/20 system, the anode electrode was positioned on the leg motor cortex of the affected hemisphere, slightly outside the CZ location, while the cathode was placed on the contralateral superior orbital margin [25, 26]. All patients underwent a stimulation intensity of 2.0 mA, with a duration of 20 min, five times per week, for four weeks, totaling 20 sessions [27].

Lower limb rehabilitation utilizing robot-assisted training

The GR-A1 treadmill lower limb rehabilitation robot, developed by Walker Rehabilitation, consists primarily of an exoskeleton orthotic, a weight loss support system, and a sports treadmill. During training sessions, patients wear a safety belt connected to the robot system for weight support. The leg machine device guides patients to walk on the treadmill, simulating a full physiological walking cycle. Rehabilitation therapists can fine-tune various walking parameters within the robot’s control software, including hip flexion and extension angles, knee flexion angles, and lower limb lengths. The walking speed of the robot is adjustable from 0.5 m/s to 3.0 m/s, and the weight reduction can be varied from 50 to 0%. These parameters are customized to suit each patient’s specific needs during their initial training session. As training progresses, the robot’s parameters are adjusted based on the patient’s actual progress. Patients typically walk with robot assistance for 20 min per session. The entire training program spans 4 weeks, with 5 sessions per week, totaling 20 courses.

Conventional therapeutic approaches

Conventional drug treatment involved administering standardized secondary prevention measures for cerebrovascular disease based on etiology. These primarily consisted of managing blood pressure, blood glucose, and blood lipids, enhancing cerebral circulation, nourishing nerves, epilepsy prevention, and other therapeutic interventions. As for conventional rehabilitation, it encompassed a holistic approach that utilized neurodevelopmental therapies such as Bobath, neuromuscular ontology promotion, Brunnstrom, and Rood techniques. This was complemented by joint mobility drills, muscle strengthening exercises, muscle tension improvement training, center of gravity transfer practices, balance function exercises, daily living activity coaching, and physical factor therapy.

Assessment

The function of patients was evaluated both before and after treatment. None of the evaluators participated in the tDCS intervention or RAGT training, and they accessed the data using random numbers. The grouping information remained concealed during the entire process of data cleaning and analysis.

fNIRS data acquisition

On the day preceding treatment and on the first day following the completion of four weeks of treatment, the subjects were subjected to an fNIRS assessment. For this, we employed a portable functional near-infrared spectroscopy brain imaging system (LIGHTNIRS) sourced from Japan. While the subjects were in a resting state, we continuously recorded near-infrared light signals at wavelengths of 780 nm, 805 nm, and 830 nm, with a 13.33 Hz sampling rate. The head cap used for data acquisition features 8 emission light sources and 8 receivers, forming 22 channels. These channels encompass the subjects’ left primary motor cortex (LM1), right primary motor cortex (RM1), left pre-motor cortex/supplementary motor cortex area (LPMC/SMA), as well as the right pre-motor cortex/supplementary motor cortex area (RPMC/SMA).

The subjects were instructed to close their eyes, relax, and refrain from moving. Following a 3-minute period of silence while seated in a comfortable position, they were fitted with a head cap, closed their eyes again, and maintained a motionless and meditative state to suspend regular thought processes. Subsequently, 6 min of resting state data were gathered. During the 6-minute fNIRS resting state monitoring session, participants were required to maintain stillness, keep their eyes closed, and stay awake, without any additional perceptual stimuli or behavioral responses [28].

Clinical scale assessment

The lower limb motor ability of patients was assessed using the FMA-LE scale, which ranges from 0 to 34. Higher scores on this scale signify improved lower limb functionality. Presently, the FMA scale is acknowledged as the prevailing clinical method for evaluating efficacy [29]. The minimum clinically important differences (MCID) for stroke FMA-LE were defined as 6 points [30].

fNIRS pretreatment and analysis

A 3D digital locator from Polhemus (USA) was utilized to capture the exact coordinate details of each channel on the standard head mold. These coordinate data were then imported into the NIRS-SPM software for spatial calibration, resulting in a three-dimensional channel distribution map (refer to Fig. 2) [31]. The recorded 22 channel coordinates were transformed into Montreal Neurological Institute coordinates. Based on the standard Brodmann brain localization, the channels were categorized into four regions of interest (ROI): LM1 (17, 19), RM1 (20, 21), LPMC/SMA (3, 4, 9, 10, 11, 18), and RPMC/SMA (5, 6, 7, 12, 13, 14) [32, 33]. Prior to data analysis, the fNIRS data of patients with right-sided lesions underwent a left-right flip to ensure consistency, with all subjects’ brain lesion locations unified to the left side [34]. In this investigation, the oxy-Hb signal was chosen as the metric for hemodynamic response due to its heightened sensitivity to cerebrovascular blood flow changes compared to deoxy-Hb signals [35].

Channel distribution map

The resting state fNIRS data underwent preprocessing using the homer2 toolbox (developed by the MGH Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging in Boston, MA, USA) within MATLAB 2013a (provided by MathWorks in Natick, MA, USA). The preprocessing involved five key steps:

(1) Raw near-infrared data undergoes conversion from a light intensity signal to an optical density signal.

(2) To automatically inspect motion artifacts channel by channel, the Motion Artifact Reduction Algorithm (Mara) is employed, with parameters configured as follows: tmotion = 1.0, tmask = 1.0, stdevthresh = 50.0, and ampthresh = 5.00.

(3) Additionally, spline interpolation is used to rectify motion artifacts.

(4) To eliminate physiological noise and drift, a 0.01 ~ 0.1 Hz band-pass filter is applied [36].

(5) The modified Beer-Lambert law facilitates the conversion of the filtered fNIRS optical density signal to oxy-Hb [37].

Functional connectivity

The variation in oxy Hb concentration for each channel was derived from the 6-minute resting state fNIRS data collected from subjects. Following this, the oxy Hb values were utilized to compute the phase locking value (PLV) between each channel pair, serving as the RSFC index. This index solely considers the relative phase difference between the two signals, x(t) and y(t) [38].

The PLV method reveals the functional connectivity among various brain regions by examining the phase relationship of near-infrared signals. It computes the stability of the phase difference between two near-infrared signals within a designated time frame. A higher PLV value signifies greater stability in the phase difference, indicating stronger synchronization of neural activities and closer functional connectivity between the two brain regions, through the application of the formula below:

The PLV ranges from 0 to 1. A PLV of 0 indicates that the phase difference between X(t) and Y(t) is uniformly distributed between -π andπ, signifying no phase synchronization. Conversely, a PLV of 1 denotes that the phase difference is constant within the − π to π range, representing complete phase synchronization. This metric is employed to assess the synchronization level of fNIRS signals, yielding a 22 × 22 connectivity matrix. This matrix reveals the stability of phase differences between two time series and sheds light on the connectivity between the ipsilateral and contralateral hemispheres [39]. The results of the resting state functional connectivity analysis are visualized using BrainNet-view [40].

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 software was utilized for statistical data processing. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of data distribution. Measurement data adhering to a normal distribution was represented as the mean ± standard deviation (x ± s). For measurement data not conforming to a normal distribution, it was expressed using the median (with upper and lower quartiles) [M (P25, P75)]. An independent sample nonparametric test was conducted to examine differences in age and disease duration between the two groups prior to treatment. Differences in FMA-LE scores between the groups before treatment were analyzed using an independent samples t-test. Categorical variables, such as gender, stroke type, and location of onset, were described using the Chi-square test. A paired samples t-test was employed to compare resting state functional connectivity in both the experimental and control groups before and after treatment. The statistical results were adjusted using the FDR multiple comparison correction method (P < 0.05).

Result

Baseline characteristics

The study encompassed forty patients, with twenty patients receiving a combination of anodal tDCS and RAGT, while the remaining twenty patients underwent RAGT therapy alone. Both groups were administered the same dosage of standard treatment. However, during the course of the treatment, one patient from the experimental group withdrew due to a temporary transfer, and another from the control group discontinued due to a temporary discharge. Consequently, a total of thirty-eight patients successfully completed the study. Notably, there were no statistically significant differences observed between the two groups in terms of age, gender, onset type (infarction/hemorrhage), onset location (left/right), and disease progression (p > 0.05). Clinical outcome

Prior to treatment, there was no statistically significant difference in FMA-LE scores between the experimental and control groups (p > 0.05). However, after treatment, both groups showed improvement in their FMA-LE scores compared to pre-treatment levels, with statistically significant differences within each group (p < 0.05). The experimental group demonstrated clinically significant improvement in FMA-LE scores (P < 0.05), whereas the improvement in the control group was not clinically significant (P < 0.05) (refer to Table 2). Discussion

In our study, we employed fNIRS and FMA-LE to assess the impact of combining anodal tDCS with RAGT, as opposed to single RAGT, on RSFC and lower limb motor abilities in stroke survivors. Following a four-week intervention, our findings revealed an improvement in FMA-LE scores specifically in the group that underwent anodal tDCS paired with RAGT, as compared to the RAGT -only group. Furthermore, by utilizing fNIRS, we investigated the distinct functional connectivity mechanisms between the combined anodal tDCS and RAGT treatment, and RAGT alone, within the cerebral motor cortex of stroke patients with lower limb motor impairments. Notably, patients who received the combined therapy exhibited a significant enhancement in RSFC functional connectivity within the contralateral M1- PMC/SMA, indicating that this integrated approach effectively boosts the functional connectivity of the cerebral motor cortex in stroke survivors.

In this study, the FMA-LE assessment was utilized to evaluate the motor function of patients’ lower limbs. The results indicated that after four weeks of treatment, both groups showed significant improvement in their FMA-LE scores. However, when compared to the control group, the experimental group demonstrated a more pronounced improvement in their FMA-LE score. This difference was statistically significant and clinically relevant, suggesting that the combination of anodal tDCS and RAGT may offer a more effective treatment approach for enhancing lower limb motor function in stroke patients.Numerous prior investigations have established that the application of anodal tDCS can augment knee extensor strength, postural stability, and overall lower limb strength in stroke survivors [41, 42]. By applying anodal tDCS stimulation to the motor cortex of the affected leg, neuronal excitability is elevated. The combination of anodal tDCS and RAGT facilitates the improvement of lower limb motor function through the reinforcement learning of neural networks [43]. Research indicates that the integration of anodal tDCS with other rehabilitative therapies may emerge as a pivotal treatment modality for enhancing lower limb motor function [44–46]. A study discovered that anodal tDCS combined with conventional physical therapy promotes the recovery of lower limb motor function in stroke patients more effectively than conventional physical therapy alone [47]. Likewise, a recent study revealed that anode high-definition transcranial direct current stimulation (HD tDCS) used as a complementary tool significantly boosts the effectiveness of robot gait training for individuals with chronic stroke [48].

Previous studies have indicated that the functional connectivity of PMC, M1, and SMA serves as a potential biomarker for assessing motor function recovery following a stroke [49, 50]. Typically, most stroke survivors experience varying degrees of motor dysfunction, which is attributed to damage within the core motor area network (comprising of M1, PMC, and SMA) [18, 51–53]. These regions play a crucial role in the planning, initiation, and execution of motor commands. Specifically, PMC and SMA are responsible for encoding complex motor output patterns and selecting appropriate motor plans to attain the intended outcomes. Meanwhile, M1 breaks down motor actions into simpler components and relays them to the spinal cord for execution [54].

The restoration of motor function following a stroke relies heavily on neural plasticity, both structurally and functionally. Post-stroke rehabilitation training has the potential to reinforce existing neural connections and establish novel neural pathways [55]. A recent study employed resting qEEG to monitor the functional network connectivity of stroke survivors undergoing rehabilitation. The findings indicated a notable increase in the correlation coefficient of the affected hemisphere, along with enhanced interhemispheric connectivity. This suggests that rehabilitation training leads to improved functional network connectivity within specific cortical regions [19]. Furthermore, another study demonstrated an enhancement in functional connectivity between the ipsilateral PMC and contralateral M1, indicating a regular restoration of motor function in stroke patients [56]. This restoration is attributed to the combined effects of long-term therapy [50]. Previous research has employed brain connectivity analysis to assess the correlation between cortical damage and motor performance, revealing that damage to the M1 and SMA cortices is linked to reduced motor function in the lower limbs of stroke patients [57]. Consequently, this study utilized fNIRS to investigate the possible neurophysiological mechanisms of anodal tDCS combined with RAGT on the primary motor area network of the cerebral hemisphere in stroke survivors.

Another randomized controlled study demonstrated that tDCS effectively enhances the functional connectivity between cerebral hemispheres in stroke patients. This includes improved connectivity between the ipsilateral and contralateral regions of M1 and PMC/SMA areas of the brain [58]. Additionally, certain researchers employed fNIRS to assess the functional network connectivity of stroke patients following anodal HD-tDCS therapy. They discovered a significant enhancement in connectivity between the ipsilateral M1 and PMC, indicating that HD-tDCS can modulate the functional connectivity within the motor network, thereby boosting motor learning capabilities [59]. Likewise, in an fMRI study, anodal tDCS was found to augment cortical excitability not only in M1 but also in SMA and contralateral PMC, which maintain robust functional connectivity with M1 [60]. The research evidence indicates that an increase in the functional connection between M1 and PMC/SMA in the hemisphere of stroke patients may contribute to improving the motor function of their lower limbs and predicting motor recovery after a stroke. This study discovered that, following the combination of anodal tDCS and RAGT, there was an elevation in the RSFC between m1 and PMC/SMA in the contralateral hemisphere of these patients. This finding suggests that the augmented RSFC in the core motor area supports the efficacy of anodal tDCS combined with RAGT in ameliorating cerebral neurovascular activity disorders. The utilization of fNIRS to monitor brain activity alterations emerges as a potential method for examining the effects of stroke on brain network functional connectivity. Furthermore, the enhanced results observed in the FMA-LEassessment for the group receiving both anodal tDCS and RAGT surpassed those solely in the RAGT group, further underscoring the clinical significance of fNIRS in assessing motor cortex functional connectivity for evaluating lower limb motor function in stroke patients.

This study bears certain limitations. As a single-center study with a small sample size, its findings have a general impact on the overall conclusion. Hence, there is a pressing need to conduct a multi-center study with a larger sample in the future. In our forthcoming research, we intend to broaden the scope of sample recruitment through collaborative efforts across multiple centers, aiming to enhance the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Simultaneously, we will implement stricter sample screening criteria to ensure subject homogeneity and minimize the influence of confounding factors. Although this paper’s patient sample size is limited, and the study did not account for detailed factors like the lesion location of stroke patients (such as cortical and subcortical regions), we hypothesize that patients with cortical injuries may exhibit greater sensitivity to the neural modulation of tDCS. This hypothesis can potentially pave the way for more targeted and precise research in the future.

Furthermore, our evaluation was limited to assessing the treatment effect after just four weeks, without conducting a long-term follow-up. Despite this, our study revealed an immediate improvement in LM1-RPMC/SMA functional connectivity through fNIRS. This finding offers valuable insights into the potential mechanisms underlying long-term efficacy. However, to confirm the long-term impact, further verification is indeed necessary. Future follow-ups should incorporate extended periods (e.g., 3 and 6 months) and advanced imaging techniques like fMRI. This approach will enable us to investigate the sustained effects of tDCS combined with RAGT on nerve remodeling.

In future research, we intend to increase our sample size by recruiting more stroke patients. We plan to conduct more comprehensive studies on various aspects and stages of the disease, as well as perform continuous follow-up evaluations on patients. This approach aims to refine treatment plans, enhance treatment efficacy, and reduce treatment costs. Additionally, it’s important to note that this study did not incorporate a sham tDCS control, which could potentially introduce a placebo effect into the results. To address this, future studies can utilize a double-blind sham stimulation design, such as short-duration low-intensity current stimulation, to further validate the independent effect of tDCS.

This study further explores the clinical transformation of anode tDCS combined with ragt therapy, approaching it from the viewpoint of evidence-based medicine and health technology evaluation. The discussion is enhanced by considering three key aspects: Firstly, regarding clinical feasibility, we not only scrutinize issues related to patient compliance and equipment accessibility, but also focus on the impact of individual variations. We propose developing personalized plans aided by multimodal evaluation and underscore the importance of real-world research to assess equipment operational efficiency.Secondly, in terms of cost assessment, we employ a health economics model to compare crucial indicators and perform sensitivity analysis, quantifying long-term benefits. Lastly, concerning practice transformation, we aim to establish a multicenter collaboration network based on personnel training and process standardization. This network will facilitate hospital-community linkages, conduct patient education, and enhance the discussion from diverse perspectives. Our ultimate goal is to provide valuable references for future research and practical applications.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the combination of anodal tDCS and RAGT can strengthen the functional connectivity between LM1-RPMC/SMA, ultimately ameliorating lower limb motor dysfunction in stroke patients. Additional research is advisable to gain a deeper insight into the mechanism and clinical attributes of this therapy.
