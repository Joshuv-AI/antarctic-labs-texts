Intelligent antigen design combined with yeast display enhances targeted immune responses
Authors: Yun-Fei Ma, Guang-Hui Yin, Yu Pan, Ye Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInducing strong and targeted humoral immune responses, a central goal of next-generation vaccine development, involves a complex series of interconnected steps. However, a comprehensive study that addresses and integrates all steps is still missing. Here, we present a study that addresses these steps to elicit precise neutralizing antibody (nAb) responses targeting focused epitope motifs. We present a new protein design model called EpiTopoFold for constructing a topology that precisely accommodates and stabilizes the epitope motif. After ten thousand in silico simulations, we designed a de novo antigen L9, which structurally incorporated three epitope motifs from the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the coronavirus spike protein. To enhance antigen immunogenicity, we displayed L9 on the surface of yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae). In mice, the yeast-displayed L9 induced stronger and broader neutralizing responses than the alum-adjuvanted L9 or commercial RBD protein. Together, this work establishes a proof-of-concept framework that integrates intelligent antigen design with yeast surface display to enable robust and targeted nAb responses for next-generation vaccine development.

Introduction

Antigens elicit adaptive humoral immunity and protect hosts from pathogen infection by inducing antibody responses. These antibody responses are mediated through the recognition of discrete antigenic epitopes [1–3]. However, only a subset of antigenic epitopes (core B-cell epitopes) is responsible for eliciting neutralizing antibody (nAb) responses that directly mediate protective immunity [4–6]. Consequently, the induction of nAb or broadly neutralizing antibody (bnAb) responses focused on core epitopes has emerged as a fundamental objective for achieving precise and personalized immune responses in vaccinology [7–10].

Targeted induction of nAb responses is a complex process that involves multiple interconnected steps. The first and foundational step is the accurate mapping of core B-cell epitopes. Most B-cell epitopes are conformational and composed of multiple discontinuous segments, which complicates their accurate identification [11, 12]. Conventional epitope mapping approaches are labor-intensive and often fail to distinguish core neutralizing epitopes from non-neutralizing ones, thereby limiting their utility for precision vaccine design [13–16]. Following epitope identification, rational scaffold design is required to structurally link discontinuous epitope segments while preserving their native conformations. These epitopes can be grafted onto natural protein scaffolds or reconstructed using de novo scaffolds [17–19]. After individual epitope antigens are generated, the integration of multiple epitope antigens represents the next critical step. De novo–designed vaccine antigens incorporating multiple core epitopes could enhance protection against pathogens. However, current strategies largely rely on simple mixtures of individual epitope antigens, which increase manufacturing complexity [20]. The final step concerns vaccine delivery. Nanoparticle-based approaches and multimerization strategies have been used to enhance the immune response in previous studies [21–24]. Nevertheless, these methods failed to simultaneously balance safety and immune enhancement. Previous studies typically addressed subsets of these steps and focused on single antigens [20, 23, 25]. A systematic and rational framework that integrates these steps and addresses existing limitations is needed for precise vaccine design.

Yeast surface display is an effective platform for antigen delivery. It enables multivalent presentation of antigens on the yeast surface and enhances antibody responses [26]. Yeast cell wall components, including β-glucans and mannans, further provide intrinsic adjuvant effects that promote antigen uptake and activation of antigen-presenting cells [27, 28]. Yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) is classified as Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) and has been widely used in biotechnology and vaccine development [29]. Leveraging these advantages, we employed yeast surface display to enhance the immunogenicity of designed antigens.

Here, we establish a framework that overcomes these challenges and enables the targeted induction of potent nAb responses. We used this framework to design a de novo antigen L9 that intelligently integrated three core epitopes from the receptor-binding domain (RBD) region of the coronavirus spike protein. When displayed on the surface of yeast, L9 elicited strong RBD-specific antibody responses and broadly neutralizing activity against coronavirus variants in mice. Collectively, this work presents a rational framework for precise and personalized vaccine design against diverse pathogens.

Methods

Identification of conserved epitope motifs

We collected information on three bnAbs (CV38–142, C135, and S309) against SARS-CoV-2 through a literature search [30–32]. Our previously developed tool, IBEIP (V3.0, https://sourceforge.net/projects/ibeip/) [16], was used for identifying epitope motifs corresponding to these bnAbs. The IBEIP accurately maps the epitopes by analyzing various molecular interactions between the antigen–antibody complex, including hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, buried surface area, and electrostatic interactions. These interactions are incorporated in two sequential steps according to their relative strength and spatial range. In the first step, IBEIP employs weaker but more spatially extensive interactions—van der Waals forces and buried surface area—to identify all potential epitope fragments. In the second step, it applies stronger but more localized interactions—hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions—to refine epitope selection. The antigen–antibody complex structures (CV38–142: 7LM9, C135: 7K8Z, S309: 6WPT) were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB: https://www.rcsb.org/). The IBEIP was a Python script. We ran the IBEIP script (parameter settings: contact distance = 5 Å, effective distance of Hbond = 3.2 Å, effective distance of electrostatic interaction = 15 Å) on the PyMOL software (https://pymol.org/).

De novo design of scaffolds for epitope motifs

In this study, we developed an EpiTopoFold model for de novo design topology of discontinuous epitope motifs mapped by the IBEIP software. The EpiTopoFold model defined the epitope motif as a fixed core layer. To bridge discontinuous epitope segments, EpiTopoFold hierarchically expanded this core layer by iteratively adding one or more layers of supporting secondary structures. Each additional layer consisted of one or more secondary structure elements, with every position independently sampled as either an α-helix (H) or a β-strand (E).

We ran TopoBuilder (https://github.com/LPDI-EPFL/topobuilder; parameter settings: default_z = 11, default_x_h = 11, default_x_e = 5, edge = 0) [20] to add loops for linking these secondary structure elements. We used a criterion termed EDI to evaluate the rationality of connectivity between secondary structure elements. E (edges) assesses whether the topology satisfies the predefined edge constraints. D (directions) evaluates whether the topology conforms to the specified directional arrangement. I (intersections) determines whether loop crossings or knots occur within the connected topology. The TopoBuilder assessed all linking directions among these secondary structure elements and only conserved reasonable linking. We design a small number of sequences (50) for each generated topology by running Rosetta FunFolDes (https://rosettacommons.org/software; parameter settings: use_cst = true, max_trials = 10, rmsd_threshold = 5.0, nstruct = 50) [33]. All designed sequences were subsequently subjected to energy evaluation using Rosetta (score_jd2; parameter settings: gdtmm = false, silent_read_through_errors = false, archive = false, assign_ss = false, skip = false), providing an objective measure of intrinsic structural stability. From the complete design pool, the 300 lowest-energy sequences were selected for top-frequency and mean-energy analysis. The optimal topology was defined by both the highest frequency of occurrence within the top 300 designs (top-frequency) and the lowest average Rosetta energy score (mean-energy), indicating a favorable balance between structural compatibility with the discontinuous epitope and global fold stability. The EpiTopoFold code is available at https://sourceforge.net/projects/epitopofold/.

Silico sequence design and structure prediction

We ran the Rosetta FunFolDes tool (parameter settings: use_cst = true, max_trials = 10, rmsd_threshold = 5.0, nstruct = 50) to design sequences for optimal topologies of epitope motifs. First, we ran TopoBuilder (parameter settings: default_z = 11, default_x_h = 11, default_x_e = 5, edge = 0) to link discontinuous topology and generate input files for the Rosetta FunFolDes model. We used AlphaFold2 (https://deepmind.google/science/alphafold/; parameter settings: benchmark = False, num_multimer_predictions_per_model = 5, use_precomputed_msas = False, run_relax = True) [34] to predict the 3D structure of designed antigen sequences. The PyMOL program was used to visualize the 3D structure of the antigen and calculate the root mean square deviation (RMSD) value between the structures of designed and raw antigens.

Protein expression and purification

The L9 gene was chemically synthesized (SYNBIO) and cloned into a mammalian expression vector. Endotoxin-free plasmids were amplified in Escherichia coli DH5α and purified using a MaxiPrep kit (SMART, SI014K10) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Expi293F cells (Thermo) were cultured in suspension at 37°C with 5% CO₂ and transiently transfected using PEI Max (Polysciences, 24 765–1). Transfection was performed at a cell density of 2.5–3.0 × 106 cells/ml, and protein expression was allowed to proceed for 4 days. Culture supernatants were harvested by centrifugation and subjected directly to nickel-affinity chromatography. Bound proteins were eluted with imidazole-containing buffer (Aladdin, I108707-1 kg) and dialyzed overnight against phosphate buffer saline (PBS) (pH 7.4). Protein purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE under reducing and non-reducing conditions. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra of purified L9 protein were recorded using a J-1500 spectropolarimeter (JASCO, Japan). The protein sample was analyzed at a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL over a wavelength range of 190–250 nm at 25°C. Thermal stability was evaluated by monitoring the ellipticity at 205 nm during heating from 25°C to 85°C.

Flow cytometer and confocal microscopy analyses of yeast surface display

The codon-optimized L9 gene was PCR-amplified and cloned into the pCTcon2 vector between NheI and BamHI sites downstream of the Aga2 fusion domain. The recombinant plasmid pCTcon2-L9 was transformed into S. cerevisiae EBY100 competent cells using a standard lithium acetate method. Transformants were selected on tryptophan-deficient synthetic dropout (SD–Trp) medium.

For surface expression, positive clones were cultured in SD–Trp medium and subsequently induced in galactose-containing medium to activate expression under the control of the GAL1 promoter. Yeast cells were induced for 72 h at 20–25°C with shaking.

Following induction, yeast cells displaying L9 were washed with PBS and incubated with a CoraLite® Plus 647–conjugated anti-Myc tag monoclonal antibody (Proteintech, CL647–60003) to label surface-displayed proteins. After washing to remove unbound antibody, fluorescence signals were analyzed by flow cytometry to assess the surface display efficiency of L9 on EBY100 cells. The fluorescence signals of L9 were also analyzed by confocal microscopy (Nikon). Fluorescence intensity of individual yeast cells was measured from raw 16-bit TIFF images using ImageJ (https://imagej.net/ij/index.html) and reported as arbitrary units under identical acquisition settings.

Immunization of mice

BALB/c female mice between 6 and 8 weeks of age were purchased from the Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (CAMS), and were housed in specific pathogen-free conditions at the Central Animal Care Services of the Institute of Medical Biology, CAMS. All mouse experiments were approved by the animal ethics committee of the Institute of Medical Biology, CAMS. Mice (n = 5 per group) received intramuscular immunizations (three times) in the hind legs at 2-week intervals. The L9-protein group was injected with 10 μg L9 protein and 35 μg aluminum adjuvant. The L9-yeast group was injected with 1 × 108 yeast cells. Mice received PBS or 1 × 108 yeast transformed with an empty vector as negative controls. The RBD protein (2019-nCov, Beyotime) was used as a positive control (10 μg protein and 35 μg aluminum adjuvant). Twelve days after the immunization, blood samples were obtained via tail vein blood collection.

Antigen-specific IgG assay

The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to analyze L9 and RBD antibody responses. Ninety-six-well plates were coated with L9 or RBD antigens (0.1 μg per well) at 4°C overnight. Plates were blocked with 2% BSA for 2 h at 37°C. Plates were washed 3 times with PBST (PBS with 5‰ Tween 20). Serum samples were added to the plate and serially diluted in block buffer. Following incubation for 1 h at 37°C, plates were washed three times with PBST. After washing, a 1:10 000 dilution of Goat-anti-Mouse IgG-HRP (KPL, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) was added to plates. After incubation for 1 h at 37°C, plates were washed 3 times with PBST. Each well of the plates was added with 50 μl 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB; Sigma). The colorimetric reaction was stopped after 10 min by adding 50 μl 0.05% H2SO4. The absorbance values at 450 and 630 nm were measured with an ELISA plate reader (Tecan, San Jose, CA).

Pseudo-virus neutralization assay

Three SARS-CoV-2 pseudotyped viruses (BQ.1, BA4/5, and BA.1) were obtained from the Institute of Medical Biology, CAMS. Serum neutralizing activity against SARS-CoV-2 was evaluated using a luciferase-based pseudovirus neutralization assay. Briefly, serum samples were initially diluted 1:50 in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) in the first column of a 96-well plate, followed by serial threefold dilutions in DMEM. SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus was diluted in complete DMEM to a final concentration of 1.3–2.3 × 104 TCID5₀/ml, and 50 μl of the diluted pseudovirus was added to each well. The serum–pseudovirus mixtures were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO₂ for 1 h to allow antibody–virus interaction.

Following incubation, 100 μl of 293 T-ACE2 cells (2 × 105 cells/ml) were added to each well. Plates were then incubated at 37°C with 5% CO₂ for 48 h. After incubation, culture supernatants were removed, and cells were lysed by adding 20 μl of cell lysis buffer (Promega) per well and incubating for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 100 μl of luciferase substrate (Promega) was added to each well, and luminescence was measured (BioTek: CYTATION). The IC50 of sera against the virus was analyzed using Prism 9 (GraphPad). Statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-test for comparisons between two independent groups (GraphPad Prism 9).

Results

Mapping core epitopes responsible for broadly neutralizing antibodys responses

Rapidly and accurately mapping core epitopes is a crucial precondition for eliciting targeted immune responses. We employed the Intelligent Batch Epitope Identification and Partitioning (IBEIP), developed in our previous study, to map core B-cell epitopes in the SARS-CoV-2 model [16]. IBEIP can rapidly and accurately map epitopes by analyzing various molecular interactions between antigen and antibody. Here, we focused on the epitopes of three widely reported bnAbs (CV38–142, C135, and S309) against SARS-CoV-2 variants [30–32]. The bnAb CV38–142 was originally identified from the serum of the SARS-CoV virus. Crystal structures of spike antigen-nAb complexes were downloaded from the PDB database for IBEIP analysis (CV38–142: 7LM9, C135: 7K8Z, S309: 6WPT).

The raw mapped epitopes of the three bnAbs were conformational and discontinuous, comprising five to seven discrete epitope segments (Fig. 1A). The epitopes recognized by bnAb CV38–142 comprised seven discontinuous segments (329FNATK333, 360F, 423WNTRNIDATS432, 435 N, 437 N, 485 T, 495R) from the RBD region of the SARS-CoV spike protein. For bnAb C135, the epitope comprised five segments (345TR346, 437NSNNL441, 448 N, 450 N, 499P) from the RBD region of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. The epitope of the bnAb S309 was also located on the RBD region of SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and comprised seven segments (333TNL335, 337P, 339GEV341, 343NATR346, 354NRKRISNC361, 440NL441, 509R). To reduce the complexity of antigen design, we simplify the epitope structures in two steps. Firstly, we linked epitope segments separated by original short non-epitope segments (length < 7 residues). Then, we removed short epitope segments (length < 3 residues). After simplifying, the number of three epitope segments was reduced from 5–7 to only two (Fig. 1B). The simplified epitope segments of bnAb CV38–142 comprised two segments (Epi-I: 329FNATK333, 423WNTRNIDATSTGNHN437). The Epi-II (345TR346, 437NSNNLDSKVGGNYN450) was the simplified epitope of bnAb C135. For bnAb S309, the final epitope was Epi-III (333TNLCPFGEVFNATR346, 354NRKRISNC361).

Core epitope mapping of three SARS-CoV-2 bnAbs. (A) Mapping and simplification of the three core epitopes. (B) Epitope structure of Epi-I. (C) Epitope structures of Epi-II and Epi-III.

The mutation of the SARS-CoV-2 antigen resulted in immune escape and may reduce the cross-neutralizing activity of bnAbs [35]. We analyzed the conserved frequency of epitope sequences across nine SARS-CoV-2 variants. All epitope sequences of Epi-II and Epi-III were absolutely conserved across SARS-CoV-2 variants, except 440 N (77.80%) from Epi-II and 339G (77.80%) from Epi-III, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B). The epitope Epi-I sequence was extracted from the SARS-CoV spike protein. Nine of 17 Epi-I residues were conserved across SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 variants (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Two of three epitopes present high conservation. The simplified three epitopes from SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 spike were used for epitope-guided antigen design.

Constructing scaffolds for discontinuous epitope segments

Epitopes need scaffolds to link discontinuous epitope segments into complete antigens. Suitable scaffolds make it easy to design corresponding protein sequences. Rosetta FunFolDes can transfer epitope motifs into a supplied scaffold template and design corresponding protein sequences for the scaffold template, but it cannot generate such scaffold template. TopoBuilder is capable of designing scaffold templates for FunFolDes to stabilize discontinuous epitope motifs. However, TopoBuilder is a manual strategy and largely depends on the experience of the researcher. To overcome these limitations, we developed a topology-driven scaffold design model, EpiTopoFold, which guides TopoBuilder in systematically exploring protein secondary-structure arrangements to identify optimal scaffold templates for discontinuous epitope segments (Fig. 2A; see Materials and methods for details). The EpiTopoFold model adds protein secondary-structure to epitope segments layer by layer. Then, we identified the optimal scaffold by assessing the designability of each scaffold using two parameters (top-frequency and mean-energy). The top-frequency is defined as the percentage of sequences from the same scaffold among the top sequences with the lowest Rosetta energy score. The mean-energy is defined as the mean Rosetta energy score of the top sequences from the same scaffold. The optimal scaffold presents high top-frequency and low mean-energy.

(B, D, and F) Mean energies of scaffold topologies among the top 300 sequences for Epi-I, Epi-II, and Epi-III, respectively. (C, E, and G) The top frequencies of scaffold topologies among the top 300 sequences for Epi-I, Epi-II, and Epi-III, respectively.

To identify an optimal scaffold, we used the EpiTopoFold model to add 1–2 layers and 1–3 protein secondary-structure (α-helix or β-strand) in each layer for each epitope. We used TopoBuilder [20] to find out rational orders of connection between epitope segments and added secondary structures. More than 200 EpiTopoFold scaffold topologies were generated for each epitope. We used Rosetta FunFolDes [33] to design 50 sequences for each rational connection topology. In total, more than 10, 000 sequences were generated per epitope through computational design. Among tens of thousands of designed sequences, we only reserved the top 300 sequences with the lowest Rosetta energy score for calculating the top-frequency and mean-energy. The top 300 sequences were derived from 16, 22, and 31 different scaffold topologies for Epi-I, Epi-II, and Epi-III, respectively (Supplementary Table S1–S3). For Epi-I epitopes, the scaffold topology Epi-I-0-1 presented the lowest mean-energy (188.39) and the second-highest top-frequency (0.15) (Fig. 2B and C). The Epi-I-0-1 comprised an additional α-helix and two epitope Epi-I segments (Fig. 2C). Six different scaffold connection orders were generated for the secondary structure elements of Epi-I-0-1 (Supplementary Fig. S2). Among them, only two orders satisfied the EDI criteria, and Epi-I-0-1 corresponded to the B1H_A1H_B2H topology. For Epi-II epitopes, the topology Epi-II-0-1 presented the lowest mean-energy (105.59) and the highest top-frequency (0.17) (Fig. 2D and E). The secondary structure arrangement of Epi-II-0-1 was similar to that of Epi-I-0-1 (Fig. 2E). Two connection orders (B1H_A1H_B2H and B2H_A1H_B1H) satisfied the EDI criteria (Supplementary Fig. S3), and Epi-II-0-1 corresponded to the B1H_A1H_B2H topology. For Epi-III epitopes, the topology Epi-III-2-1 presented the lowest mean-energy (159.22) and the highest top-frequency (0.17) (Fig. 2F and G). The Epi-III-2-1 comprised two additional α-helixes and Epi-III epitopes (Fig. 2G). For Epi-III-2-1, 24 scaffold connection orders were generated (Supplementary Fig. S4). Only two orders (B1H_A1H_A2H_B2E and B2E_A2H_A1H_B1H) satisfied the EDI criteria, and the final selected topology for Epi-III-2-1 was B1H_A1H_A2H_B2E. These optimal scaffold topologies were used for the protein sequence design.

To assess the reliability of EpiTopoFold, we compared it with TopoBuilder. For TopoBuilder, more than 200 scaffold templates were generated for each epitope. For each scaffold template, 50 sequences were designed using Rosetta FunFolDes. The top 300 sequences with the lowest Rosetta energy scores were selected for comparative analysis. These top 300 sequences from TopoBuilder were derived from 38, 27, and 37 distinct scaffold topologies for Epi-I, Epi-II, and Epi-III, respectively (Supplementary Tables S4–S6). Top-frequency and mean energy were used to evaluate the designability of scaffold templates generated by the two approaches. The scaffold topologies generated by EpiTopoFold exhibited significantly lower mean energies than those generated by TopoBuilder across all three epitopes (P < .001; Supplementary Fig. S5A). We further compared the topology frequency distributions between the two methods. EpiTopoFold produced a more concentrated distribution of scaffold topologies with higher top-frequency (Supplementary Fig. S5B–D), whereas TopoBuilder generated a broader distribution with lower top-frequency. In addition, the top five scaffold topologies generated by EpiTopoFold showed significantly higher top-frequency than those generated by TopoBuilder for Epi-I (P < .001) and Epi-II (P < .05) (Supplementary Fig. S5E).

Scaffold sequence design and integration of three epitopes

We ran Rosetta FunFolDes protocol [33] to design protein sequences for these epitope scaffolds (20 000 sequences for each scaffold). The optimal sequence presented both good stability and high structural consistency. Thus, we identified the optimal sequences in two steps. First, we only retained the top 500 sequences with the lowest energy values (Supplementary Fig. S6A–C). Then, we ran AlphaFold2 [34] to predict the structures of these sequences and analyzed epitope conformation consistency between predicted and natural structures.

For the top 500 sequences, the range of Rosetta energy scores was −30.96 ~ 28.31, −52.15 ~ 15.51, and − 21.75 ~ 65.01 for Epi-I-0-1, Epi-II-0-1 and Epi-III-2-1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S7A). The mean Rosetta energy score of Epi-II-0-1 (−2.58) is the lowest compared with Epi-I-0-1 (0.14) and Epi-III-2-1 (31.70). We analyzed conformation consistency by calculating the RMSD between epitope conformations from predicted and original structures. The mean epitope RMSDs of Epi-II-0-1 (7.46 Å) and Epi-III-2-1 (7.40 Å) were lower than that of Epi-I-0-1 (19.34 Å) (Supplementary Fig. S7B).

To identify the optimal sequences for each epitope scaffold, we only retained the sequences with an epitope RMSD smaller than 3 Å in AlphaFold2 results. The RMSD cutoff of 3 Å was selected based on commonly used criteria for assessing structural similarity and consistency in protein structure comparison studies [2, 20, 36, 37]. Three to five sequences were left for three epitope scaffolds. We also analyzed the epitope conformation from structures generated from Rosetta. Among three sequences of scaffold Epi-I-0-1, the sequence Epi-I-0-1-163 presented the lowest epitope RMSD for both Rosetta (0.75 Å) and AlphaFold2 structures (1.32 Å) (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S8). For Epi-II-0-1and Epi-III-2-1, the optimal sequences were Epi-II-0-1-146 (Rosetta: 0.79 Å, AlphaFold2: 2.25 Å) and Epi-III-2-1-008 (Rosetta: 0.79 Å, AlphaFold2: 1.56 Å) (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S8).

To elicit multiple nAb responses simultaneously, we integrated three epitopes into one antigen (Fig. 3A). We used three different protein linker sequences (linker1: GGGG, linker2: PPPP, linker3: PPPPPP) to link sequences Epi-I-0-1-163, Epi-II-0-1-146 and Epi-III-2-1-008 in all possible orders. Finally, we got 18 different antigen sequences (L1 to L18) and used AlphaFold2 to predict their protein structures. Among 18 integrated sequences, the L9 (Epi-II-0-1-146/linker2/Epi-III-2-1-008/linker2/Epi-I-0-1-163) presented the highest conformation consistency for all three epitopes (Epi-I RMSD: 0.74 Å, Epi-II RMSD: 0.75 Å, Epi-III RMSD: 1.95 Å) (Fig. 3C–E).

We evaluated whether linker type and epitope integration order influence the structural properties of the integrated antigens, including antigen stability, epitope exposure, and epitope conformational consistency. No significant differences were observed among the three linker types in antigen stability, epitope exposure, or epitope conformational consistency (Supplementary Fig. S9A–C). In contrast, epitope integration order substantially affected antigen stability and epitope conformational preservation, while having minimal influence on epitope exposure (Supplementary Fig. S10A–C). Across all six integration orders, the three epitopes remained highly exposed, with epitope solvent-accessible surface area percentages close to 100% (Supplementary Fig. S10B), indicating that epitope accessibility was largely preserved regardless of arrangement. However, structural stability varied among different integration orders. Antigens arranged in Order-II-I-III exhibited the lowest Rosetta energy score, indicating the most favorable overall structural stability, and showed significantly lower energy than those arranged in Order-III-II-I (Supplementary Fig. S10A). Meanwhile, epitope conformational consistency also depended on integration order. For Epi-I, higher structural similarity to the native epitope was observed in Order-III-I-II and Order-II-I-III. For Epi-II, Order-III-II-I, and Order-I-II-III showed better conformational preservation. For Epi-III, the favorable conformational consistency was observed in Order-II-III-I and Order-I-III-II (Supplementary Fig. S10C). Together, these results indicate that linker composition has relatively limited influence on integrated antigen architecture in our design system, whereas epitope integration order plays a more important role in determining overall structural stability and epitope conformational preservation.

We next analyzed the sequence and structural similarity between the non-epitope regions of L9 and RBD of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein to assess their potential resemblance to native RBD epitopes. Six non-epitope fragments (Fragment 1–6, each >6 amino acids in length) were identified between six epitope fragments (Supplementary Fig. S11A). The sequence similarity between L9 non-epitope fragments and corresponding RBD regions ranged from 4.4% to 37.5% (Supplementary Fig. S11B). Structural comparison further showed low conformational similarity, with RMSD values ranging from 4.48 Å to 16.83 Å (Supplementary Fig. S11C). These results indicate limited sequence and structural resemblance between the non-epitope regions of L9 and the native RBD.

Displaying L9 antigen on the surface of yeast and immunization

SDS–PAGE analysis indicated that the molecular weight of purified L9 protein was ~25 to 35 kDa (Fig. 4B). To experimentally evaluate its structural stability, purified L9 was analyzed by CD spectroscopy. The far-UV CD spectrum displayed characteristic α-helical signatures, with a positive band near 192 nm and negative ellipticity at 208 and 222 nm, consistent with the AlphaFold2-predicted structure (Supplementary Fig. S12A). Thermal denaturation analysis monitored at 205 nm from 25°C to 85°C revealed only minimal changes in ellipticity without a detectable unfolding transition (Supplementary Fig. S12B), indicating that L9 maintains a stable secondary structure across a broad temperature range. To improve the antigen immunogenicity, we next displayed the L9 antigen on the surface of yeast. The L9 sequence was cloned into the pCTcon2 vector in frame with Aga2. The resulting Aga2-L9 fusion was displayed on the yeast surface via the Aga1–Aga2 disulfide bond (Fig. 4A). Surface expression was stained with anti-Myc antibody. The flow cytometry analysis showed that the expression ratio of L9-yeasts was 69.0% while the control yeast was only 1.98% (Fig. 4C). Confocal microscopy images also validated the expression of L9 on the surface of yeast (Fig. 4D and E).

We analyzed the immunogenicity of the L9 antigen in a mouse model. Mice (n = 5) were immunized with either 10 μg L9 protein (35 μg aluminum adjuvant) or 1 × 108 L9-yeast cells through intramuscular injection (Fig. 5A). PBS, yeast carrying the empty vector and RBD protein were used as control groups. Serum samples were collected 12 days after the first and second boosts. RBD and L9 specific antibody responses were quantified via ELISA. The RBD-protein group presented the strongest IgG endpoint titer against the RBD antigen (Fig. 5B). The IgG endpoint titer was undetectable in the PBS-blank and Yeast-control groups (Fig. 5B). For the first boost, RBD-specific IgG of the L9-protein group was undetectable, while the IgG endpoint titer of the L9-yeast group was 3.38 (log10) against the RBD antigen. After the second boost, both the IgG endpoint titer of L9-protein and the L9-yeast group were enhanced. The RBD-specific antibody response in the L9-yeast group was 3.20-fold (log10, P < .001) higher than that in the L9-protein group after final immunization (Fig. 5B). After the final immunization, the L9-yeast group showed an IgG endpoint titer of 3.68 (log10) against the L9 antigen, which was significantly higher than that of the L9-protein group (P < .05) and the RBD group (P < .001) (Supplementary Fig. S13).

Discussion

The strong and targeted induction of nAb responses is one of the research hotspots in next-generation vaccine development. However, the structural complexity of B-cell epitopes and the multiple steps involved in the overall process make this goal challenging to achieve. To solve these issues, we developed a systematic and intelligent framework that integrates epitope identification, scaffold topology design, and antigen engineering to enable epitope-guided antigen design and the targeted induction of multiple nAb responses. We validated the reliability of this framework in the SARS-CoV-2 virus model.

Accurate and rapid mapping of core B-cell epitopes ensured a successful initiation of targeted immune responses. The IBEIP tool, developed in our previous study [16], identifies B-cell epitopes recognized by an antibody through analysis of corresponding antigen–antibody complexes from databases. Using IBEIP, we mapped three core B-cell epitopes in the RBD regions of SARS-CoV (Epi-I) and SARS-CoV-2 (Epi-II and Epi-III) spike proteins for targeted vaccine design. These mapped epitopes consist of 5 to 7 spatially proximal but discontinuous segments, demonstrating the intrinsic structural complexity. We added the proximal short non-epitope segments and removed short epitope segments to reduce the epitope complexity. Our strategy could help researchers quickly and accurately get core epitopes from different pathogen antigens.

Rational scaffold topology design is essential for maintaining the structural stability and conformational consistency of core B-cell epitopes. Our EpiTopoFold model could systematically evaluate all secondary structure arrangements and identify optimal scaffold topology by analyzing two quantitative parameters—top-frequency and mean-energy. Unlike conventional topology design strategies such as TopoBuilder, which rely on manually defined topology sketches, EpiTopoFold reformulates scaffold design as a systematic topology exploration problem. This conceptual shift from manual trial-and-error design to quantitative topology optimization enables more efficient identification of highly designable scaffold architectures. Using this approach, we identified optimal scaffold topologies for epitopes Epi-I, Epi-II, and Epi-III, respectively. Our EpiTopoFold model enabled rapid and rational scaffold design for the complex B-cell epitopes.

Integrating epitopes from different pathogen antigens into a single vaccine antigen has the potential to enhance protective efficacy and broaden pathogen coverage. We integrated three epitope antigens under different linkers and orders and found that only the L9 (Epi-II-0-1-146/linker2/Epi-III-2-1-008/linker2/ Epi-I-0-1-163) antigen maintained high conformational consistency for all three core epitopes. Notably, while linker type had limited effects on antigen structural stability, epitope exposure, and epitope conformation, the integration order showed a more pronounced influence on structural stability and epitope conformation, with relatively minor effects on epitope exposure. These results suggest that epitope arrangement is a critical determinant of antigen structural integrity, and systematic optimization is required when integrating multiple epitopes.

Small-sized protein antigens usually present weak immunogenicity [20, 23]. L9 is a small-sized protein consisting of 191 amino acids, with a molecular weight of 25 to 35 kDa. As the yeast (S. cerevisiae) presents the GRAS and adjuvant characteristics [27–29], we expressed L9 antigen on the surface of yeast (L9-yeast group) to enhance its immunogenicity. In the mouse model, immune serums from the L9-yeast group presented enhanced Ab response to the RBD region [3.20-fold (log10), P < .001] and elicited broad cross-neutralization against SARS-CoV-2 variants (BQ.1, BA4/5, and BA.1) than the L9-protein and commercial RBD-protein groups. As L9 is structurally and sequentially identical to the RBD only at the three designed epitopes, the strong RBD-binding and neutralizing activities of sera from L9-immunized mice suggest that L9 elicits functional antibodies targeting these epitopes. These results support the reliability of our framework in directing nAb responses toward epitopes from different pathogen antigens.

In summary, we present a framework for achieving targeted immune responses through intelligent epitope antigen design and yeast display. We provided suitable and reliable methods for each module of precise vaccine design. Although this study demonstrates an initial validation of the framework using a SARS-CoV-2 model, it remains to be further evaluated across additional pathogens. In principle, this method may be applicable when the target pathogens require immune responses directed toward similar three-dimensional epitope architectures as those characterized in this study. However, its practical performance will require further experimental validation. In addition, structural evaluation in the current study relied primarily on AlphaFold2 predictions, which may introduce model-dependent bias. Incorporating complementary structural prediction tools or experimental structural validation in future studies will further strengthen the robustness and reliability of the framework. Collectively, our work establishes a rational and versatile strategy for precise vaccine development with generalizable potential.
