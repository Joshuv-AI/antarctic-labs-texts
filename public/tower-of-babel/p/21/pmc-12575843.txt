A novel spatial framework to validate arsenic exposure gene expression profiling in bladder cancer using multiplex FISH and AI-powered digital pathology
Authors: Sonalika Singhal, Samarth Singhal, Kevin L. Gardner, Anushka Dikshit, Emerald Doolittle, Donald Sens, Mary A. Sens, Michael L. Miller, Sai Tun Hein Aung, Sandeep K. Singhal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bladder cancer exhibits marked spatial heterogeneity in gene expression and immune infiltration. In this exploratory pilot study, we integrate multiplex fluorescence in situ hybridization (mFISH) with AI-assisted digital pathology to characterize the spatial distribution of a previously validated three-gene arsenic-responsive risk model (NKIRAS2, AKTIP, HLA-DQA1). Initially identified in arsenic-exposed individuals and associated with bladder cancer risk, this gene panel achieved 94% training and 75% validation AUC in prior genomic models (PMC8760535). We analyzed five bladder tumor specimens using whole-slide mFISH imaging and HoverNet-based nuclear segmentation to quantify gene expression at single-cell resolution. Spatial profiling revealed elevated expression scores in tumor-adjacent regions, with a strong positive correlation to tumor grade (Pearson’s r = 0.83). These gene-enriched regions exhibited spatial clustering of tumor cells. Additionally, tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) density was inversely correlated with tumor grade, suggesting immune exclusion in high-grade tumors. Our findings demonstrate the feasibility of combining spatial transcriptomics with AI-driven histopathological analysis for biomarker validation. This integrative framework provides a foundation for future population-scale studies leveraging spatial omics to evaluate arsenic-associated gene signatures and assess their relevance in bladder cancer risk stratification and disease progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23396-y.

Introduction

Arsenic, one of the most toxic environmental elements, has been detected at harmful levels in groundwater across more than seventy countries, posing health risks to millions worldwide1. Chronic arsenic exposure, primarily through contaminated drinking water and irrigation practices-contributes to a spectrum of arsenicosis-related disorders affecting multiple organ systems, including kidneys, bladder, lungs, and skin2. Among the malignancies linked to arsenic, bladder cancer (urothelial carcinoma) is particularly concerning3. With an annual global burden of ~ 500,000 new cases and 200,000 deaths4, and 80,000 new diagnoses and 17,000 deaths in the United States alone, bladder cancer remains one of the most common malignancies worldwide5.

In prior work (Fig. 1 A), our group identified a 147-gene signature associated with arsenic exposure and bladder cancer susceptibility6. Further computational modeling refined this signature to three principal genes: NKIRAS2, AKTIP, and HLA-DQA1 which collectively demonstrated high predictive accuracy for bladder cancer risk (AUC = 0.94 in the training cohort and AUC = 0.75 in validation). NKIRAS2 functions as a cytoplasmic tumor suppressor7, AKTIP modulates apoptosis predominantly at the plasma membrane8, and HLA-DQA1 is part of the major histocompatibility complex, playing a vital role in immune regulation9,10. Dysregulation of HLA genes has been linked to various cancers, including lung cancer11.

Elucidating the spatial distribution of these biomarkers can enhance our understanding of bladder cancer pathogenesis, particularly in the context of arsenic-induced carcinogenesis. It will allow mapping these alterations at a cellular level, providing detailed insights into how specific genes and signatures (NKIRAS2, AKTIP, HLA-DQA1) distribute within different cellular compartments of the tumor microenvironment. This level of spatial resolution helps identify localized gene expression patterns that could influence tumor behavior and disease progression12,13.

Technological innovations such as multiplex fluorescent in situ hybridization (mFISH) now enable high-resolution spatial analysis of gene expression within tumor specimens14. RNAscope is an advanced multiplex fluorescence RNA in situ hybridization (RNA mFISH) method that enables the simultaneous detection of multiple RNA targets within intact tissue sections. This capability provides precise spatial information about gene expression patterns at single-cell resolution, which is essential for understanding tissue heterogeneity, cellular interactions, and the microenvironmental context within diseases such as cancer15,16. Compared with conventional transcriptomic approaches, mFISH provides cell- and subcellular-level information, capturing intra-tumoral heterogeneity and tumor-stroma-immune cell interactions17 (Fig. 1B-C). Leveraging this single-cell resolution imaging technique, researchers can map gene expression relative to morphological landmarks such as the urothelial lining and invasive fronts and quantify rare cellular subpopulations or colocalized gene combinations in the tumor microenvironment18 (Fig. 1D). In clinical diagnostics, fluorescence-based imaging further facilitates exploratory morphological biomarker evaluation, influencing therapeutic decisions, e.g., predicting immunotherapy response19.

Against this backdrop, the present study applies mFISH and digital pathology-based image analysis to validate our previously developed three-gene model (NKIRAS2, AKTIP, and HLA-DQA1) in an independent set of bladder cancer samples. This study is designed explicitly as an exploratory pilot investigation involving five bladder cancer cases. Small-scale pilot studies are routinely employed in biomedical research to assess feasibility, refine methodologies, and gather preliminary data to inform larger studies. For example, Ji et al. (2020)12 and Thrane et al. (2018)13 effectively utilized similarly small cohorts to demonstrate the applicability of spatial transcriptomic approaches in cancer research. Additionally, as the previously identified arsenic-exposed gene signature originated from an Asian population, our study strategically used samples from the same ethnic background to control for ethnic variability across samples20–22.

By overlaying mFISH signals with H&E-stained whole-slide images, we aim to (1) quantify the tumor-vs.-non-tumor distribution of the signature genes, (2) assess spatial co-expression at varying nearest-neighbor distances, and (3) correlate these patterns with clinical parameters such as tumor grade and patient age, as well as arsenic exposure history. This spatially resolved approach is intended to refine our gene-based risk prediction model, offering enhanced diagnostic and prognostic potential in arsenic-linked bladder cancer.

Results

Computational cell type annotation in tissue samples

To characterize tumor microenvironmental composition, Fig. 2 displays the distribution of neoplastic, inflammatory, stromal, necrotic, and nolabe (unlabeled) cells across five bladder tumors specimens (07 A, 08 A, 10 A, 11 A, 24B, (n = 5 samples)). The total annotated cell counts varied significantly across samples, with counts ranging from approximately 250,000 in 07 A to 140,000 in 08 A, highlighting substantial inter-sample heterogeneity (Fig. 3A). Among these, 07 A exhibited the highest number of neoplastic cells (~ 180,000), while 11 A had a relatively higher proportion of inflammatory cells (~ 80,000) compared to its tumor burden. We assessed the concordance between computation tumor cell classification in and expert pathologist annotations by calculating tumor cell sensitivity. Across the five annotated bladder cancer samples, sensitivity values ranged from 79.97% to 91.40%, indicating high agreement between the two approaches (Supplementary Figure-1).

When examining the model equation values (three-gene signature scores) for top 25% expression in necrotic regions versus overall tissue (Fig. 3B), necrotic regions generally showed higher equation values, with an average mean difference of ~ 31.78 (two-sided paired t-test, p = 0.0462), suggesting an association between necrosis and increased gene expression or microenvironmental stress responses. However, there was variability across samples, for instance, sample 08 A shows a necrotic region score of ~ 230 versus ~ 150 in the overall tissue, while 10 A has scores of ~ 100 for both necrosis and overall. The consistent upregulation in necrotic areas suggests that cellular stress or localized hypoxia may drive increased expression of the three-gene risk signature.

Figure 3C (Supplementary Table 1) presents the correlation matrix between tumor grade, three gene model equation (“Overall Model”) expression, expression in major cell-type frequencies (neoplasm, necrosis, connective, inflammatory), and patient age. Notably, tumor grade is strongly and positively correlated with the overall equation score (Pearson correlation coefficient, r ≈ + 0.83), indicating that more aggressive tumors tend to express higher levels of the genes represented in the model. The overall equation also shows a high positive exploratory correlation with neoplasm expression (Pearson correlation coefficient, r ≈ + 0.72), suggesting that regions with more extensive tumor or tissue abnormalities are associated with increased gene expression. Age exhibited an exploratory inverse correlation with several cell-type expression risk model scores, particularly those associated with connective tissue, inflammatory response, and necrotic features (e.g., Pearson correlation coefficient, r ≈ -0.87; see Supplementary Table-1). This suggests that the transcriptional landscape in these samples is more likely driven by tumor-intrinsic biology rather than chronological age. A modest positive correlation (Pearson correlation coefficient, r ≈ + 0.36) was noted between inflammatory cell abundance and the overall gene expression equation, suggesting a possible (but limited) role of immune activity in shaping expression patterns. Collectively, these observations support the hypothesis that tumor grade and cell-type composition exert stronger influence on expression signatures than patient age, though further investigation in a larger cohort is warranted to confirm these trends given the limited sample size (n = 5).

Spatial profiling of gene expression in bladder tumors

Multiplex fluorescence in situ hybridization (mFISH) was utilized to spatially map individual and combinatorial gene expression in five bladder tumor specimens (10 A, 08 A, 11 A, 24B, and 07 A). Expression of NKIRAS2, AKTIP, and HLA-DQA1 was observed across both tumor and non-tumor regions within the analyzed bladder cancer tissue sections. “Non-tumor” regions were defined as areas outside the pathologist-annotated tumor boundaries and may include a mix of stromal, immune, and epithelial cells from the tumor-adjacent microenvironment. While prior studies have linked these genes to oncogenic signaling, immune regulation, and cellular stress responses, our analysis did not include histologically verified normal bladder tissue. Therefore, any comparisons between tumor and non-tumor regions in this study should be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive, rather than conclusive assessments of tumor vs. normal biology. These findings serve to illustrate spatial heterogeneity in gene expression within tumor-bearing tissue and provide a foundation for future studies incorporating matched normal controls.

Figure 4A presents the proportion of tumor and non-tumor cells within the analyzed FFPE tissue sections for each case, illustrating notable inter-sample variability in cellular composition. Sample10A exhibited the highest proportion of tumor cells (88.6%) within the assessed section, while 08 A, 11 A, and 24B displayed moderate proportions (27.3%, 22.1%, and 25.9%, respectively). Sample07A demonstrated an approximately balanced distribution of tumor (52.8%) and non-tumor cells (47.2%). These findings reflect the composition within the specific tissue sections analyzed and do not imply broader conclusions about overall tumor burden or tumor size. Interestingly, samples from older patients (e.g., 07 A, age 76; 10 A, age 69) displayed higher tumor proportions within the sections analyzed, suggesting a hypothesis-generating observation of potential age-associated changes in the tumor microenvironment that warrants further investigation. Figure 4B illustrates the expression levels of the three selected genes (AKTIP, NKIRAS2, and HLA-DQA1), revealing distinct inter-sample heterogeneity. AKTIP exhibited the highest relative expression across all samples, with peak levels observed in 10 A (~ 41%) and 07 A (~ 34%), followed by NKIRAS2 (~ 40% in 10 A). HLA-DQA1 showed the lowest expression, averaging ~ 10% across cases. While sample 07 A appeared to have a more balanced expression distribution among the three genes, sample 08 A exhibited a descriptive pattern characterized by a clear hierarchy (AKTIP > NKIRAS2 > HLA-DQA1), without implying statistical significance.

Figure 4C provides a spatial representation of combinatorial gene expression patterns within the analyzed FFPE tissue sections. Sample10A exhibited a dense and heterogeneous pattern predominantly characterized by co-expression of AKTIP and NKIRAS2. In contrast, samples 08 A and 11 A displayed more scattered distributions, with localized clusters co-expressing all three genes (AKTIP + NKIRAS2 + HLA-DQA1). Samples 07 A and 24B showed more dispersed spatial patterns, indicating weaker clustering of gene expression combinations within the analyzed sections. Among the observed gene pairs, AKTIP + NKIRAS2 was the most frequently represented combination, accounting for 58% to 78% of detected co-expression events. The triple combination of AKTIP + HLA-DQA1 + NKIRAS2 varied from 12% in sample 08 A to 29% in sample 11 A, while co-expression of HLA-DQA1 + NKIRAS2 remained consistently low (< 5%) across all samples. These descriptive patterns suggest that certain gene combinations may preferentially localize within tumor versus non-tumor compartments within the analyzed sections. While the prominence of AKTIP + HLA-DQA1 + NKIRAS2 within tumor regions may imply potential roles in tumor biology, and AKTIP + NKIRAS2 enrichment in adjacent non-tumor areas may reflect possible protective or regulatory functions, these observations are hypothesis-generating and require further validation in larger, well-characterized cohorts. Given the exploratory nature of this pilot study and the limited sample size, these spatial patterns should be interpreted cautiously.

Close proximity cell distribution

In this section, we evaluated spatial co-expression patterns by quantifying the frequency and distribution of cells simultaneously expressing combinations of the three target genes AKTIP, NKIRAS2, and HLA-DQA1 at single-cell resolution. Unlike Sect. Spatial profiling of gene expression in bladder tumors, which assessed gene expression at the individual transcript level, this analysis focuses on the spatial organization of multi-gene co-expression within pathologist-annotated tumor and non-tumor compartments. These co-expression profiles serve as spatial molecular signatures, capturing localized clustering or dispersion of transcriptionally active cell populations across the tissue microenvironment.

Figure 6 and Supplementary Figure-2 illustrate these spatial dynamics through nearest-neighbor (NN) distance analysis and density contour mapping (0–100 μm). Hypothesis-generating spatial segregation (two-sided t-test, p < 0.0001) was observed between tumor and non-tumor cells across all samples. In four of the five bladder cancer cases (07 A, 08 A, 11 A, 24B), tumor cells demonstrated more compact spatial organization, with higher expression intensities occurring within NN distances of 20–40 μm. Conversely, non-tumor cells exhibited broader spatial distributions, typically extending from 25 to 100 μm. Sample 10 A showed the most distinct tumor-stroma separation (two-sided t-test, p < 0.0001), while sample 08 A displayed greater heterogeneity and diffuseness in gene expression. When the analysis was restricted to the top 25% of high-expression cells, spatial segregation patterns became even more pronounced at shorter distances (0–25 μm). High-expression regions exhibited distinct clustering in samples 07 A and 11 A, while more diffuse high-intensity regions were seen in 08 A and 24B. Expression density values ranged from 2.5 × 10⁻⁸ to 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ probability density /µm² in sample 24B, and from 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ to 3.0 × 10⁻⁸ probability density /µm² in sample 07 A. Notably, sample 07 A showed clustering of non-tumor cells beyond 50 μm, suggesting underlying differences in tissue architecture.

Overall, these findings indicate that the highest levels of model gene expression are predominantly localized within close-proximity tumor regions, with a marked decline in expression beyond 25–50 μm from the tumor centroid. This pattern suggests a spatial enrichment of gene expression within tumor-associated regions on gene activity. While these results are statistically exploratory within the scope of this study, we acknowledge that the small sample size limits generalizability. Therefore, the spatial patterns identified here should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, offering preliminary insights into the spatial organization of gene expression within the bladder tumor microenvironment.

Association between gene expression profiles and clinical factors

Analysis of both individual and combined gene expression revealed substantial inter-sample variability related to tumor grade, with only minimal association to patient age. In the bar chart (Figure-7 A) represents the cumulative expression of selected gene combinations (AKTIP + HLA-DQA1, AKTIP + NKIRAS2 + HLA-DQA1, AKTIP + NKIRAS2, and NKIRAS2 + HLA-DQA1) across different tumor samples categorized by tumor grade and patient age. The gene expression values range from approximately 5 to 20, with notable differences among the gene combinations. The highest gene expression for AKTIP + HLA-DQA1 is observed in sample 07 A (Grade 4, Age 76), whereas the lowest is seen in sample 08 A (Grade 2, Age 45), indicating potential tumor heterogeneity and age-related transcriptional changes. However, older patients (e.g., 07 A, age 76; 10 A, age 69) had higher tumor proportions in their analyzed sections, this observation is descriptive and should not be extrapolated to infer age-related biological differences without further evidence.

AKTIP + NKIRAS2 + HLA-DQA1 showed the highest expression values in 07 A (~ 20 Mean integrated fluorescence intensity (A.U.)) and 11 A (~ 18.75 A.U.), whereas AKTIP + NKIRAS2 maintained a moderate range (~ 16.5–19.25 A.U.). AKTIP + HLA-DQA1 consistently registered lower expression levels (~ 6.5–9.5 A.U.). Notably, there seems to be an association between Tumor Grade and NKIRAS2 + HLA-DQA1 with Grade 1 showing the lowest mean value (~ 9.4 A.U.), Grade 2–3 in moderate range (~ 10.35–11 A.U.) and Grade 4 having the highest value (~ 12.9 A.U.), implying a positive correlation (r = 0.738 A.U.).

Figure 7B shows the distribution of model expression values across different tumor grades. While pairwise comparisons yielded low p-values (e.g., Pearson’s r equals p < 2.22e-16). This suggests a highly significant difference between groups, implying that the model expression value is strongly associated with tumor grade. The median expression values appear to increase with tumor grade, supporting an exploratory correlation between the gene model and tumor progression.

Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection23,24 (UMAP) further validated these findings. While three-gene expression alone (Fig. 7C) showed a continuous distribution, integrating the model equation (Fig. 7D) led to distinct, non-overlapping clusters corresponding to tumor grades. Grade 1 tumors (10 A) were well-separated (silhouette score 0.72), indicating a more uniform gene expression pattern, whereas Grade 3 tumors (24B) displayed higher heterogeneity (silhouette score 0.59). Overall, higher-grade tumors exhibited both elevated model expression and greater variability, supporting the prognostic relevance of the three-gene model for stratifying bladder cancer progression. These results should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory due to the small sample size (n = 5).

TILs and tumor grade association

A computational analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) across five bladder cancer samples reveals a significant association between TIL density and tumor grade (Fig. 8A). Using an automated image analysis pipeline, TIL-rich regions were identified based on morphological features and spatial clustering of lymphocyte-dense areas within whole-slide images (WSIs). Notably, age appears to be more closely associated with TIL area. Older patients, such as 11 A (Age 71, 93.9 mm²), 10 A (Age 69, 58.5 mm²), and 07 A (Age 76, 38.1 mm²), exhibit larger TIL areas compared to younger patients, including 08 A (Age 45, 27.6 mm²) and 24B (Age 49, 11.4 mm²).

Statistical analysis revealed a negative exploratory correlation between tumor grade and both tumor area (Pearson’s r = -0.29) and TIL area (Pearson’s r = -0.43) (Supp. Table-2). This may reflect that higher-grade tumors may not necessarily be larger but could exhibit structural fragmentation or localized increases in immune infiltration per unit area. While a moderate positive correlation was observed between age and tumor area (Pearson’s r = 0.64), this association is exploratory in nature and should not be overinterpreted, as the dataset does not provide sufficient statistical power or adjustment for confounding variables to support a definitive conclusion. The inverse exploratory correlation between tumor grade and immune infiltration (Pearson’s r = -0.43 to -0.44) aligns with the hypothesis that high-grade bladder tumors may adopt immune evasion mechanisms, potentially reducing their susceptibility to immunotherapy. These findings offer preliminary insights into the spatial relationships among tumor grade, immune cell distribution, and patient characteristics, laying the groundwork for future investigations into the tumor microenvironment in bladder cancer. While the spatial distribution of TILs may reflect localized immune compartmentalization, it is important to note that no functional immune profiling was conducted. As such, these observations are hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as evidence of immune evasion or used to infer immunotherapy relevance without validation in larger, clinically annotated cohorts.

Discussion

This pilot study demonstrates the feasibility of integrating multiplex fluorescence in situ hybridization (mFISH) with AI-enabled digital pathology to investigate spatial gene expression heterogeneity in bladder cancer. By analyzing five spatially resolved tumor samples, we explored potential associations between tumor cellular composition, gene expression, immune infiltration, and patient-specific features such as age. Although our findings are preliminary, they offer important hypotheses for future validation in larger cohorts.

Tumor annotation sensitivity exceeding 80% across all samples supports the initial robustness of our computational framework. However, the morphological complexity of bladder cancer, particularly in necrotic or borderline regions, suggests that iterative refinement may be required for generalization across diverse clinical specimens.

Our exploratory analysis highlights a three-gene model comprising AKTIP, NKIRAS2, and HLA-DQA1 that demonstrates elevated expression in high-grade tumor regions. This gene combination was recurrently enriched in tumor-centric compartments across several samples (e.g., 07 A, 08 A, 11 A). The correlation between model expression values and tumor grade (r = 0.83) suggests this signature may be linked to tumor aggressiveness. These findings parallel previous efforts linking gene expression programs to bladder cancer progression25,26, although our single-cell spatial resolution provides added context.

UMAP projections showed a distinct separation of low-grade tumors (e.g., Grade 1, silhouette score = 0.72), suggesting the possibility of spatially distinct molecular subtypes. However, due to the limited sample size, these observations remain exploratory and require rigorous validation.

Spatial modeling revealed a tumor-centered expression gradient, with high gene expression concentrated within 25–50 μm of tumor centroids. This gradient declined sharply with distance, particularly for the top 25% of expressing cells, possibly reflecting localized signaling or metabolic regulation. Significant inter-sample variability (p < 0.0001) suggests spatial topology may offer insight into tumor-specific regulatory landscapes. These results are consistent with prior spatial transcriptomics studies identifying steep expression drop-offs at tumor boundaries12.

Quantitative TIL analyses suggested a negative correlation between tumor grade and immune infiltration (r ≈ -0.43), potentially reflecting immune exclusion or stromal remodeling in aggressive tumors. Although HLA-DQA1 expression was elevated in select tumor compartments, its correlation with immune cell percentages (r = 0.7) suggests potential involvement in modulating tumor-immune interactions. These findings echo studies implicating HLA dysregulation in immune escape mechanisms in bladder cancer27,28.

We observed a moderate positive correlation between patient age and tumor area (r = 0.64), as well as inverse correlations with the frequencies of certain cell types (r ≥ -0.87). These exploratory trends may suggest age-related changes in the tumor microenvironment, such as altered immune surveillance or extracellular matrix remodeling. However, given the small cohort and lack of comprehensive clinical metadata, these findings are not conclusive and should be interpreted with caution.

The primary limitation of this study is the small sample size (n = 5), which constrains statistical power and generalizability. All results are hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger, clinically annotated cohorts. Furthermore, the cross-sectional nature of our spatial analyses limits longitudinal inference regarding treatment response or disease progression.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the feasibility of integrating multiplex FISH-based single-cell gene expression with digital pathology to explore spatial patterns in bladder cancer. The preliminary observations suggest potential associations between tumor grade, necrosis, immune cell distribution, and age-related features with localized gene expression, providing a hypothesis-generating foundation for future validation studies. These findings collectively highlight the importance of spatially resolved gene expression profiling in understanding bladder cancer heterogeneity.

The three-gene model (AKTIP, HLA-DQA1, NKIRAS2) may offer preliminary insight into spatial gene expression patterns within tumor and non-tumor compartments, suggesting potential utility for future risk stratification studies and personalized therapeutic research, pending further validation. TIL-centric analysis suggests interplay between immune distribution and tumor aggressiveness, indicating that AKTIP + NKIRAS2 expression might inform immunotherapy responsiveness. Our spatial framework may help guide future combination therapies targeting high-expression niches (e.g., necrotic or hypoxic zones) to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Despite the limited scope, this study supports continued development of biomarker-driven spatial diagnostics in bladder cancer.

Methods

This study was deliberately structured to integrate data from independent sources to address three complementary research aims: (1) to characterize gene expression alterations associated with chronic arsenic exposure resulting from contaminated drinking water (147 genes); (2) to investigate the potential involvement of these previously identified genes in bladder cancer pathogenesis (only 3 out of 147); and (3) to determine whether these gene alterations could be spatially mapped and studied within within bladder tumor tissues using multiplex spatial transcriptomic technologies. The first two objectives were comprehensively addressed in our prior study6, while the present work focuses on spatial validation within tumor tissues. Although the datasets were generated independently and under unlinked conditions, the findings consistently converged to support the same biological hypothesis, thus reinforcing the validity of our observations as hypothesis-generating and providing a foundation for subsequent large-scale validation studies. Furthermore, given that the initial gene signature was identified in arsenic-exposed Asian populations, we intentionally selected bladder cancer samples from the same ethnic background to minimize ethnicity-related confounders, a methodological approach consistent with established practices in environmental exposure research20,22. This design enhances the biological relevance of our findings within this pilot cohort while recognizing the need for broader validation in future investigations.

Data details

The bladder cancer tissue specimens were obtained from Bio-Techne, collected through collaborations with RCG Health Care and Biosciences, India. As per the provided certification from Dr. Govardhan H B, Consultant Oncologist, all archival human specimens were collected under ethical and legal protocols in the country of acquisition, with informed consent permitting the release of biospecimens and relevant clinical information to third parties for biomedical research purposes. The use of these de-identified samples is in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines and received Research Ethics Board (REB) exemption due to their anonymized nature. Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples were collected from patients who underwent surgery for Stage I to Stage IV bladder cancer between 2016 and 2017. Clinical data, including surgical date, ethnicity, gender, age, diagnosis type, and disease grade, were extracted from patient medical records (Table 1) and correlated with the total number of tumor and non-tumor cells identified in each whole-slide image (WSI).

Bladder tumor tissues were subjected to multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) using the RNAscope™ Multiplex Fluorescent V2 assay (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA) to detect spatial RNA expression of NKIRAS2, AKTIP, and HLA-DQA1. Target-specific probes were conjugated with Opal fluorophores: Opal 520 for NKIRAS2, Opal 570 for AKTIP, and Opal 690 for HLA-DQA1 (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA). Nuclear DNA was counterstained with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole). Assay specificity and RNA integrity were verified using manufacturer-recommended positive and negative control probes.

High-resolution imaging was performed on a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.4 NA oil immersion objective. Fluorophores were excited with lasers at 488 nm (Opal 520), 561 nm (Opal 570), 633 nm (Opal 690), and 405 nm (DAPI), with emission signals captured in spectrally distinct channels to prevent bleed-through. Images were acquired at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels with 0.1 μm pixel size, using standardized acquisition parameters across all samples. Channel separation, individual signal analysis, and multichannel image reconstruction were conducted using ZEN software (Zeiss, v3.5) to ensure reproducibility and quantitative fidelity.

Overlaying multiplex on H&E stains

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was applied to the FISH-stained tissue slides to facilitate precise annotation and identification of cell types expressing specific FISH signals. The slides were processed and annotated at the Digital and Computational Pathology Laboratory at Columbia University. The initial delineation of tumor regions was conducted manually by experienced board-certified pathologists at the Digital and Computational Pathology Laboratory at Columbia University, using H&E-stained whole slide images and established morphological criteria. By taking a negative of these annotation the non-tumor regions were defined. These annotations were used as the gold standard for subsequent computational analysis in QuPath version 0.5.129. The multiplex fluorescent images were overlaid onto the H&E-stained slides using QuPath, an open-source software for digital pathology and WSI processing. The registration was achieved with the Warpy30 extension in ImageJ, integrated within QuPath. Using the image combiner Warpy, manually align both the multiplex images by rotating and adjusting the scale and size, keeping the pathologist’s annotations turned on is recommended for accurate alignment. Once satisfactory alignment has been achieved, Warpy converts both images into a single combined image for further analysis.

Cell detection on overlayed WSI tissue

Following the overlay of multiplex and H&E stains, the whole tissue section was annotated by detecting cells. Cell detection was performed in QuPath using the DAPI channel to identify nuclei, with an intensity threshold set at 25 to define detection criteria for all cells. To ensure the accuracy of these computational annotations, all automated assignments were cross-validated and adjusted, if necessary, by the same team of pathologists. This hybrid approach ensured high-fidelity classification by integrating both manual pathology expertise and computational assistance.

Cell classification

Cell classification was performed using an integrated computational pipeline that combined QuPath (version 0.4.2) with HoverNet for nuclear segmentation and phenotype assignment. After automatic detection of nuclei across the tissue sections, QuPath’s inbuilt Watershed Cell Detection Algorithm was used to segment individual nuclei and extract a range of morphological and intensity-based features. These included nuclear area, shape descriptors such as eccentricity and circularity, Haralick texture features, and mean fluorescence intensity for each gene probe (AKTIP, NKIRAS2, and HLA-DQA1). These features were used to generate classifiers in QuPath for each gene, with initial classification thresholds set at the mean intensity value per probe and refined through iterative inspection. Cells were assigned to one of eight categories: AKTIP, HLA-DQA1, NKIRAS2, AKTIP + NKIRAS2, AKTIP + HLA-DQA1, NKIRAS2 + HLA-DQA1, AKTIP + HLA-DQA1 + NKIRAS2, or unlabeled if they were below threshold for all probes. Classifier performance was evaluated by comparing automated predictions against ground truth annotations provided by a board-certified pathologist on representative regions. Standard performance metrics including precision, recall, and F1-score were calculated to assess classification accuracy. The HoverNet model used in this study was pretrained on multiple publicly available datasets, including CoNSeP (colorectal adenocarcinoma), PanNuke (a pan-cancer multi-tissue dataset), MoNuSAC, and Kumar. With PanNuke dataset and Kumar Dataset consisting annotated data for bladder cancer tissue. Additional manual review and refinement were performed in regions with complex morphology, necrosis, or staining artifacts. Final cell annotations derived from this process were used to compute model equation scores and evaluate associations with spatial patterns of gene expression, tumor structure, immune infiltration, and patient characteristics.

Image analysis and export

WSI processing was carried out in QuPath, which facilitates the analysis of mIF images derived from tissue sections. The software supported the upload of both mIF data (targeting NKIRAS2, AKTIP, and HLA-DQA1) and annotated H&E images, using Bio-Formats and OpenSlide31 for compatibility. After registration, the multiplex images were aligned with the H&E slides.

For the annotation of non-tumor regions, negative annotations of tumor regions were used. A DAPI intensity threshold of 25 was employed for cell detection within QuPath. Cells were classified based on their expression of NKIRAS2, AKTIP, and HLA-DQA1 using mean thresholds for all possible gene combinations, allowing for detailed visualization of gene expression.

Upon completion of image processing, cellular data was exported in CSV format, containing columns for ‘Parent’ (tumor or healthy), ‘Centroid X’ and ‘Centroid Y’ (coordinates), and intensity values for each gene in the nucleus and cytoplasm. Combined gene expression values were calculated using a logistic regression equation derived from a previous study:

Model = Logit(p) = 2.265 + 13.24 * (NKIRAS2) − 4.20 * (AKTIP) − 1.55 * (HLA-DQA1).

The model equation score is a normalized composite metric bounded between 0 and 1, derived from the co-expression levels of AKTIP, NKIRAS2, and HLA-DQA1 within each cell. A higher score reflects a greater combined expression of these genes, it serves as a quantitative readout of local transcriptional activity involving the selected genes. This score was used to spatially map gene expression patterns and explore potential associations with tumor grade, immune infiltration, necrosis, and patient age.

The resulting data was processed using Python version 3.13.1 and Pandas version 2.2.332 to optimize performance with SIMD techniques. For data analysis, libraries such as Scikit-Learn version 1.6.133, SciPy version 1.15.234, and Matplotlib version 3.10.135 were used for processing and visualization.

Computational approach to identify cell types

Cell typing

A pre-trained convolutional neural network model with HoverNet36 architecture trained on MoNuSeg37 dataset was used for cell typing from the H&E image. Its configurations were adjusted to accurately segment and label nucleus for our H&E images. The model labelled each nucleus into the 5 categories, neoplasm, inflammatory, necrosis, connective and nolabel along with its confidence value. A model confidence threshold of 0.9 was chosen on the basis of maximum overlap using regions marked by Pathologist and the model’s predictions. The coordinates and cell type information was converted from a json format to a QuPath readable geojson format. Further this geojson format was converted into csv format to be mapped with our original quantification files for downstream analysis.

Tumor-Infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) patch detection

TILs were identified using a patch-based classification approach leveraging a pre-trained Visual Geometry Group (VGG)38 convolutional neural network. The algorithm39 used was trained on 23 histopathological cancer types from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)40, including bladder urothelial carcinoma, to ensure generalizability across tissue types. Patches from the multiplex-stained tissue sections were extracted and classified for TIL presence, using a confidence threshold of 0.5 to optimize classification accuracy. The output TIL predictions were converted into QuPath-compatible GeoJSON format and overlaid on the histological images for visual inspection and spatial mapping. While this method enables efficient identification of TIL-rich regions, it is important to note that TIL detection in this study was based on morphological features and machine learning-based patch classification, not on functional immune profiling or immunophenotyping. The final TIL annotations were reviewed by a pathologist to confirm consistency with expected tissue structures and exclude potential false positives.

Downstream analysis

Basic statistics

R (version 4.4.2), Python and matplotlib were used for calculating and visualizing basic statistics and tests for each of the five patients. For each patient composition based on cell type annotations and gene classification were calculated and visualized. Central tendencies for all three genes and their combinations were calculated after normalization. Average equation values were calculated for tumor and non-tumor cells, which were plotted against tumor grade to assess the role of equation in tumor progression.

Dimensionality reductions

To evaluate the importance of combined gene expression scores in predicting the tumor stage, a combined dataset was made by randomly sampling 10,000 cells from each of the tissues with information on each of the genes, their equation values and their grade. The dataset was then split into training and test set in a 70:30 ratio, following this two Multilayered Perceptron (MLP) model with 100 neurons in first hidden layer and 50 neurons in second hidden layer were trained on the train set using Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function for 1000 iterations, first MLP model was trained on the normalized values of three genes and the other was trained using all three genes and equation value. A significant drop in the MSE loss (from 0.529 to 0.054 on training set and from 0.533 to 0.057) was observed in both training and test set when equation was included in the features for the second MLP. To further emphasize this, we employed Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), to plot the learned embeddings from the MLPs into a two-dimensional vector space.

t-SNE41 plots were created using the t-SNE module in the scikit-learn library for python. It was used to visualize the distribution of gene combinations in different tissues, by creating two-dimensional plots using the normalized values for the pairwise combination of genes. A perplexity value of 10 was found to give the most easily interpretable plots.

Spatial proximity analysis

To assess the spatial distribution of tumor and non-tumor cells, Spatial density maps of gene co-expression were generated using a two-dimensional Gaussian kernel density estimator with a fixed bandwidth. The parameters were selected to ensure consistent smoothing across all samples. The resulting density values, expressed in density per µm², reflect local concentrations of cells co-expressing specific gene combinations and were used to visualize and compare spatial clustering patterns within tumor and non-tumor regions. we performed a nearest neighbor (NN) proximity analysis using cell coordinates in our quantification file. We defined two conditions: (1) all cells and (2) cells that have high combined gene expression score, selected based the threshold determined at the 75th percentile. For each condition, we calculated NN distances using the k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) algorithm (k = 2), implemented via the FNN package version 1.1.4.1[10.32614/CRAN.package.FNN] in R (version 4.4.2). To mitigate sampling bias, we conducted multiple iterations, each involving random sampling of up to 10,000–50,000 cells per group based upon the tumor and non-tumor cell count in each tissue. The NN distance distributions for tumor and non-tumor cells were visualized using kernel density plots, and a two-sample t-test was performed to compare their distributions under different threshold conditions. Statistical measurement (p-value) was annotated on the density plots, providing insights into the spatial clustering of tumor cells in relation to gene expression levels. To further elucidate our findings, we also constructed contour plots using ggplot2 version 3.5.1[10.32614/CRAN.package.ggplot2] package in R to visualize the effect of equation on cellular distribution. The same conditions as above were taken but only for tumor cells.

Calculating TIL and cell type scores

To quantify tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and assess cell type-specific scores, we calculated the TIL score by determining the total area of algorithmically identified39 TIL patches and normalizing it against the total tissue size and total tumor area, which was computed by summing the areas of all tumor annotations made by pathologists. Further, each algorithmically identified cell type annotation was mapped back to the quantification file using its centroid coordinates, allowing for the calculation of mean expression values for individual genes, their combinations, and equation values for each of the detected five cell types. Non-parametric correlation coefficient was then used to assess the association of these computed scores with clinical factors, providing insights into the relationship between TIL presence, gene expression, equation and clinical factors.
