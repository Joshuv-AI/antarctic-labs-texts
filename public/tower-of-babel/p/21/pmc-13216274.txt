Heterostructure-engineered diamond-graphene composites for high-performance and stable electromagnetic wave absorption
Authors: Yingjie Zhang, Xigui Yang, Shoulong Lai, Jinxu Qin, Chaofan Lv, Hang Liu, Jinhao Zang, Chongxin Shan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing electromagnetic wave absorbing (EWA) materials that simultaneously deliver strong attenuation and withstand harsh environments remains a major challenge due to long-standing trade-offs between dielectric performance and structural robustness. Here, we report a heterostructured diamond–graphene composite synthesized under moderate high-pressure and high-temperature conditions, in which multilayer graphene is embedded within a nanodiamond framework through covalently bonded interfaces. This architecture enables modulation of the sp3/sp2 hybridization ratios, yielding tunable dielectric responses, improved impedance matching, and multiple, synergistic energy-dissipation pathways. The optimized composite achieves a minimum reflection loss of −60 dB and an effective absorption bandwidth of 4.0 GHz. Simultaneously, the material exhibits exceptional environmental tolerance, including high thermal stability, reliable absorption at high temperatures, large fracture toughness, and enhanced corrosion resistance, which originates from the mechanically interlocked diamond/graphene networks and chemically stable carbon interfaces. Our results establish a scalable heterointerface-engineering strategy for constructing multifunctional carbon architectures that unify strong microwave attenuation with outstanding thermal, mechanical, and chemical durability, offering a promising platform for next-generation EWA materials suitable for harsh or demanding environments.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of wireless communication technology has led to a substantial increase in electromagnetic radiation in the environment1–3. This growing pollution poses potential human health risks from prolonged exposure and causing serious electromagnetic interference due to strong induction effects4,5. To mitigate these issues, ideal electromagnetic wave absorbing (EWA) materials should be lightweight, highly efficient, thin, and capable of operating across a broad frequency range6. Conventional EWA materials, such as ferrites, metals, conductive polymers, and dielectrics materials, have been used to reduce reflection and radiation7,8. However, their practical applications are limited by issues like impedance mismatch, narrow bandwidth, single loss mechanism, and poor resistance to corrosion or oxidation9. These limitations reduce their effectiveness and durability, particularly under demanding environmental conditions6. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop advanced EWA materials that offer both high absorption efficiency and strong environmental stability.

Carbon materials—including graphene, carbon nanotubes, and carbon dots—have garnered considerable attention for electromagnetic wave absorption because of their low density, tunable electrical properties, and cost-effective synthesis10,11. Unfortunately, single-component carbon nanomaterials often exhibit poor impedance matching and insufficient energy attenuation, primarily attributed to their excessively high electrical conductivity and the absence of diverse loss mechanisms12–14. To overcome these shortcomings, various strategies such as multicomponent design or element doping have been explored to optimize electromagnetic parameters and enhance energy dissipation by regulating composition, interface architecture, and defect density14–17. Despite notable progress, existing carbon-based composites still face persistent challenges, including weak filler-matrix interactions, non-uniform dispersion, and limited resistance to oxidation and corrosion under extreme environmental conditions6,18. To address these limitations, the integration of diamond and graphene via covalent bonding presents a promising avenue19–24. Diamond contributes exceptional chemical and thermal stability25, while graphene imparts high electrical conductivity and facilitates efficient charge transport23,24. The formation of robust covalent interfaces between these two components maybe improve impedance matching, introduce multiple energy dissipation pathways26, and enhance mechanical robustness. This synergistic design strategy offers a pathway toward high-performance EWA materials capable of withstanding harsh operating environments.

Here we report an efficient strategy for the fabrication of heterostructured diamond composites engineered within a diamond matrix for designing high-performance EWA materials under moderate pressure and temperature conditions27. The material consists of multilayer graphene domains embedded in nanodiamond (ND) matrices, with covalently bonded interfaces. The optimized material shows a maximum effective absorption bandwidth (EABmax) of 4.0 GHz at 2.7 mm and a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −60 dB at 3.0 mm, while maintaining stable performance across a broad temperature range (300–973 K). The composite also exhibits a fracture toughness of 16.8 MPa · m1/2, a Vickers hardness of 41.9 GPa, an oxidation onset temperature of 1145 K, a corrosion potential of −0.210 V, and a low corrosion current density of 10−6 A/cm2. These findings provide a promising strategy for designing multifunctional EWA materials, offering new possibilities for advanced applications in harsh environments such as aerospace, military systems, and advanced electronics.

Results and discussion

Design and preparation of diamond composites

Figure 1a illustrates the synthesis route for the diamond composites (DC). ND particles with an average diameter of ~5 nm were first annealed at 1200 °C, inducing partial graphitization and forming fullerene-wrapped diamond nanostructures. These pre-graphitized NDs were then used as precursors for high-pressure and high-temperature (HPHT) treatment. Under conditions of 7.0 GPa and 1100–1300 °C, three composite samples were obtained, each exhibiting a distinctive hybrid microstructure in which multilayer graphene fragments are coherently embedded with a diamond matrix (Fig. 1b). For clarity, the products synthesized at 1100 °C, 1200 °C, and 1300 °C are denoted DC-1, DC-2, DC-3, respectively. A key distinction of the present diamond–graphene composites is the substantially reduced synthesis pressure enabled by the pre-graphitization strategy. Unlike Diaphene, which requires ~12 GPa, the composites form at 7 GPa while retaining comparable mechanical properties. This reduction arises from an interface-mediated phase transformation pathway introduced by the pre-formed sp² carbon shell. During HPHT treatment, the graphitic layers accommodate compressive strain through bond rehybridization and interlayer sliding, redistributing local stress and lowering the effective barrier for sp²→sp³ reconstruction at the diamond surface28–30. In addition, the intimate contact between graphene and nanodiamond provides coherent interfacial templates that facilitate localized nucleation of sp³ bonds, bypassing bulk diffusion-controlled transformation31. These cooperative effects, such as stress accommodation, reduced nucleation barriers, and constrained carbon migration, shift the transformation from a pressure-dominated bulk process to an interface-controlled reconstruction mechanism, enabling hybrid diamond–graphene architectures to form under milder conditions. Structurally, both materials feature interconnected ND grains bridged by few- to multi-layer graphene domains, forming continuous sp²-sp³ covalent interfaces that promote efficient load transfer and improve toughness. Consistently, the DC composites exhibit Vickers hardness values ranging from 41.9 to 51.7 GPa and fracture toughness spanning 10.5 to 16.8 MPa·m1/2, closely matching those of Diaphene. Beyond mechanical robustness, the covalently bonded diamond/graphene interface imparts a suite of advantageous properties, summarized in Fig. 1c. Notably, DC-3 exhibits an oxidation onset temperature of 1145 K, exceeding those of natural diamond, nanopolycrystalline diamond (NPD), and Diaphene. The thermal conductivity of 3.5 W·m−1·K−1 highlights the strong phonon scattering inherent to the nanostructured architecture, while the corrosion potential of −0.210 V. reflects enhanced electrochemical stability. Microstructure and dielectric properties

To examine the microstructure of the diamond composites, we performed scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). High-resolution high-angle annular dark field-STEM (HAADF-STEM) images (Fig. 2a, b) reveal a ND matrix embedded with multilayer graphene domains. Magnified images reveal an atomic-scale continuity between graphene and diamond (Fig. 2c, d), providing direct evidence of covalently bonded sp2-sp3 interfaces21 rather than simple van der Waals contact. Carbon K-edge electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) mapping acquired across the interfacial region (Fig. 2e) further confirms mixed bonding character: graphene domains exhibit a prominent π* at 284 eV, whereas diamond grains show the σ* feature at 292 eV19. These observations demonstrate the formation of covalently bridged diamond/graphene heterointerfaces throughout the composite. X-ray diffraction patterns (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 1a) display the coexistence of broad graphitic (002) peak and characteristic diamond reflections at (111), (220), and (311). Rietveld refinement yields diamond phase fractions of 78.3%, 74.1%, and 63.9% for DC-1, DC-2, and DC-3, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2), consistent with progressive graphitization at higher synthesis temperatures. Despite the HAADF-STEM observation of multilayer graphene, no discernible 2D band is detected in Raman spectra (Supplementary Fig. 1b), likely due to interfacial bonding, local lattice distortions, and defect formation that disrupt the double-resonance process32,33. Electromagnetic parameters (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 3) indicate that dielectric loss in the DC composites originates from the combined contributions of conduction loss and multiscale polarization mechanisms. Abundance sp³/sp² heterojunctions introduces strong interfacial polarization due to the conductivity contrast, while defect-rich graphene layers support dipolar relaxation34. Graphitic networks further facilitate electron hopping and conduction loss17, increasing the magnitude of ε′′ while maintaining the frequency-dependent trends observed in ND. The prominent loss peaks near 6–8 GHz and 11–15 GHz are attributed primarily to interfacial and defect-mediated relaxation associated with the diamond-rich matrix, explaining why graphene incorporation enhances the overall dielectric loss but does not shift the relaxation frequencies35. Cole–Cole plots demonstrate multiple relaxation semicircles for all DC samples (Supplementary Fig. 4), in contrast to the simpler profile of graphene powder, reflecting a more heterogeneous distribution of dipolar species and interfacial polarization sites. DC-2 and DC-3 show broader and more numerous semicircles than either ND or CVD diamond, consistent with their richer population of sp2-sp3 interfaces. Quantitative analysis of conductive (εc″) and polarization (εp″) components (Fig. 2h) further reveals that conduction loss dominates below 4 GHz, whereas polarization loss becomes the principal attenuation mechanism at 4–18 GHz. For DC-3, εc" accounts for ~77% of total ε″ at 3 GHz, but decreases to 24% at 11 GHz and 15% at 14 GHz, accompanied by a corresponding increase in polarization contributions (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 5). Magnetic loss is negligible for all sample, as evidenced by the nearly uniform complex permeability (Supplementary Fig. 3), confirming the nonmagnetic nature of carbon-based materials. Instead, impedance matching is strongly modulated by the sp³/sp² ratio. As the diamond fraction decreases with increasing synthesis temperature, impedance matching improves. For DC-3 (63.9% sp3), the majority of impedance values fall within the optimal 0.8–1.2 window (Fig. 2i), enabling efficient microwave coupling and broadband attenuation.

Incorporation of the calculated dielectric parameters into the impedance model shows that the defective interface yields a matching coefficient closer to unity at a thickness of 1.0 mm, indicating that vacancy defects at the heterointerfaces promote improved impedance matching and thereby enhance EWA performance. Defective sites at the diamond–graphene interface serve as pivotal centers for energy dissipation, as the local chemical heterogeneity induces both permanent dipoles and charge-trapping states that amplify dielectric loss. The presence of dangling bonds and sp³–sp² transitions creates a distribution of dipole moments and relaxation times, which manifest as broadened and overlapping semicircles in the Cole–Cole representation (Supplementary Fig. 4), thereby elevating ε″. To further assess the role of structural length scale, we constructed additional interface models with systematically varied diamond grain sizes, labeled size-1, size-2, and size-3 (Fig. 3d–f). The resulting complex dielectric constants are presented in Fig. 3g. Below 12 GHz, the largest-grain model (size-3) exhibits the highest real permittivity, reflecting enhanced charge-storage capability. However, above 12 GHz, its permittivity decreases sharply and becomes lower than that of the smaller-grain models. The imaginary permittivity (Fig. 3h) increases monotonically with grain size, indicating progressively stronger dielectric loss. Despite this trend, the impedance matching coefficients (Fig. 3i) reveal that the smallest-grain model achieves the most favorable matching at 1.0 mm thickness. This suggests that reduced grain size facilitates better wave penetration and balanced energy dissipation. The first-principles results demonstrate that both interfacial defect chemistry and grain-size modulation play decisive roles in optimizing EWA behavior. These insights highlight the critical importance of microstructural design in enabling next-generation diamond/graphene absorbers with tunable and superior electromagnetic attenuation performance.

EWA performance

In the ND precursor, the high content of sp3-hybridized carbon limits electron transport and suppresses polarization-relaxation processes, resulting in poor microwave absorption (Fig. 4a–c). As the synthesis temperature increases, the sp2 carbon content rises, thereby modifying the dielectric properties and electrical conductivity of the composites (Supplementary Fig. 6). These structural and compositional transitions directly influence EWA performance, as reflected by the evolution of RLmin, EABmax, and optimal thickness (Fig. 4d–f). Specifically, DC-1 exhibits an RLmin of −12.5 dB with an EABmax of 2.6 GHz at 6.0 mm (Supplementary Figs. 7g–i). DC-2 shows a marked improvement with an RLmin of −30.0 dB and an EABmax of 3.6 GHz at 2.8 mm (Supplementary Figs. 7m–o). Notably, DC-3 delivers the strongest performance, enabled by its optimized graphene content. Achieving Z values close to 1.0 over a broad frequency range is critical to ensure maximum electromagnetic wave entry into the absorber. Although graphene is highly conductive and ND is insulating—conditions that usually lead to poor impedance matching—the formation of covalently bonded sp²-sp³ interfaces introduces heterogeneous microstructures containing defects and interfaces of varying dimensions19. These features suppress the overall conductivity and significantly improve impedance matching. As shown in Fig. 3g–i, DC-1 and DC-2 maintain Z values mostly above 1.2, whereas DC-3 exhibits a broad optimal matching region (Z = 0.8–1.2), consistent with its superior EWA performance. To elucidate the role of interfacial structures, ND and nanographene (NG) were mechanically mixed at ratios of 8:2, 7:3, and 6:4 and treated under the same HPHT conditions as DC-3 (Supplementary Fig. 8). The resulting composites (DG-1, DG-2, DG-3) showed progressively improved EWA performance with increasing NG content; however, all remained inferior to DC-3. For example, DG-3 achieves an RLmin of −55.7 dB at 4.5 mm and an EAB of 3.3 GHz at 2.0 mm, whereas DC-3 reaches −60 dB at a smaller thickness of 3.0 mm and a broader EAB of 4.0 GHz at 2.7 mm (~3.8 GHz at 2.0 mm). This disparity highlights that physical mixing produces heterogeneous, weakly bonded interfaces and discontinuous sp² domains, which hinder charge transport and degrade impedance matching26. In contrast, the pre-graphitization strategy of the DC composites yields continuous covalent sp²-sp³ networks, enabling efficient conduction and polarization losses that result in superior EWA capability. Importantly, impedance matching alone is insufficient. The absorber must also possess strong energy-attenuation capability to dissipate incident microwaves; otherwise, reflected energy from the backing layer may reemit into free space. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9, DC-3 exhibits the highest attenuation coefficient, consistent with its elevated imaginary permittivity and enhanced dielectric loss behavior.

Compared with state-of-the-art carbon-based and magnetic absorbers36–42 (Fig. 4j and Supplementary Table 1), DC-3 achieves both an ultrahigh RLmin (−60 dB) and a broad EAB of 4.0 GHz, placing it among the leading carbon-based EWA materials. Importantly, this degree of attenuation at such a small thickness surpasses many high-performance absorbers, which typically require 4–5 mm to achieve comparable suppression. The optimized sp²/sp³ hybrid architecture in DC-3 therefore provides an advantageous balance between minimal thickness, strong attenuation, and broadband absorption, underscoring its potential for lightweight and miniaturized electromagnetic shielding and stealth applications.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we analyzed both conduction and polarization contributions to dielectric loss (Fig. 4k). The highly conductive sp2-rich networks enhance conduction loss via efficient charge transport. Meanwhile, the numerous heterointerfaces create local electric-field gradients where electrons preferentially accumulate on the graphene side, intensifying interfacial polarization. Dielectric loss arises from two primary contributions—conduction loss and polarization loss. The internal architecture of the composites contains numerous heterogeneous diamond–graphene interfaces, at which electrons preferentially accumulate on the graphene side, thereby strengthening interfacial polarization and contributing to enhanced energy dissipation2. Defective and edge carbon atoms introduce permanent dipoles, supporting dipolar relaxation and further boosting dielectric loss. Overall, the integrated diamond–graphene structure enables strong conduction and interfacial polarization losses while maintaining favorable impedance matching, collectively yielding efficient and broadband microwave attenuation.

Serviceability in extreme environments

To assess the suitability of the DC composites for use under extreme environments, we examined their mechanical, thermal and chemical stability. Thermal transport measurements reveal that DC-3 exhibits a remarkably low thermal conductivity of 3.5 W·m−1·K−1 at room temperature (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 10). This strong insulating behavior arises from its nanostructured architecture, with diamond nanograins surrounded by abundant interfaces and defect-rich boundaries that strongly scatter phonons and charge carriers. The composite also displays excellent oxidation resistance, with an onset temperature of 1145 K, surpassing natural diamond, NPD, and Diaphene (Supplementary Fig. 11). This enhancement may originate from the sp³-sp² covalent interfaces19,20,24, where graphene layers act as an oxygen-blocking barrier and the diamond nanograins become energetically stable25. Moreover, interfacial stress and charge redistribution further reduce surface energy and suppress oxidative reactions21. The cooperative effect of these factors leads to the delayed onset of oxidation, demonstrating that interface engineering is an efficient strategy to improve the environmental tolerance of diamond-based composites. Fracture toughness and Vickers hardness were measured under a 9.8 N load (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 12). The DC samples show fracture toughness values of 10.5–16.8 MPa·m1/2, with Vickers hardness values of 51.7 ± 2.4 GPa (DC-1), 44.5 ± 3.1 GPa (DC-2), and 41.9 ± 1.5 GPa (DC-3). Flexural strength is critical for maintaining structural stability under mechanical loading, ensuring that EWA components preserve their geometry and performance during service. Although the flexural strength of DC-3 (20 ~ 60 MPa, as shown Supplementary Figs. 12–13) is lower than that of CVD diamond (350.7 MPa, as shown Supplementary Fig. 12e), it remains high for carbon-based composites43. However, the stress–strain curves exhibit pronounced non-brittle behavior (Supplementary Fig. 13), attributable to the diamond–multilayer graphene architecture. The graphene layers hinder crack propagation through crack bridging, interfacial sliding, and deflection, thereby suppressing catastrophic brittle fracture typical of monolithic diamond and ensuring adequate mechanical integrity for load-bearing or vibration-intensive electromagnetic applications. Electrochemical corrosion resistance was evaluated in NaCl solution (Fig. 5c). DC-3 show a corrosion potential (Ecorr = −0.21 V) and a corrosion current density (Icorr <10−6 A/cm2), indicating strong electrochemical passivation. The positive shift in Ecorr suggests that the composite acts as an anodic barrier, suppressing electron release and minimizing corrosion18. Bode impedance spectra (Fig. 5d) further demonstrate high |Z| values at low frequencies, confirming robust protection. This behavior correlates directly with the sp³/sp² ratio: the diamond matrix supplies chemical inertness, while sp2 domains promote charge delocalization and minimize local potential gradients. Excessive sp², however, increases surface reactivity, underscoring the need for an optimized hybrid ratio (Fig. 5e). Thus, an optimal balance between the two ensures both structural stability and electronic equilibrium at the interface22, effectively improving corrosion resistance through synergistic chemical passivation and charge delocalization. Finite element simulations of power-loss density (PLD) distribution (Fig. 5f) consistently show that DC-3 exhibits the most intense and spatially extensive PLD at 7 GHz (C-band), 11 GHz (X-band), and 15 GHz (Ku-band)11, directly reflecting its superior microwave attenuation capacity. High-temperature absorption measurements (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 14) demonstrate that DC-3 maintain stable microwave attenuation between 773–973 K. Although HPHT synthesis reliably produces covalently bonded sp³-sp² interfaces and high-performance composites, scale-up remains limited by the requirements of ultra-high pressure, high temperature, and high energy consumption. Strategies to reduce cost and improve throughput include tailoring precursor chemistry to lower transformation thresholds, adopting spark plasma sintering (SPS)44 or hot-pressing, and exploring hybrid HPHT-SPS approaches. In addition, emerging metal-catalyzed pathways, such as liquid Ga-, Ni-, or Cu-mediated formation of sp³-sp² interfaces45–50 offer promising alternative routes to large-scale production of covalently integrated carbon heterostructures. Overall, DC-3 combines high mechanical strength, low thermal conductivity, excellent oxidation and corrosion resistance, and stable high-temperature microwave absorption, making it a promising candidate for demanding aerospace, aviation, and high-performance engineering applications.

In summary, we have developed a diamond composite that combines efficient electromagnetic wave absorption with exceptional environmental tolerance by integrating multilayer graphene within a nanodiamond matrix through sp2-sp3 heterointerfaces. This engineered architecture enhances mechanical robustness, enables favorable impedance matching, and activates multiple energy-dissipation pathways, allowing the material to retain strong absorption performance even under extreme operating conditions. The optimized composite achieves a minimum reflection loss of −60 dB together with an effective absorption bandwidth of 4.0 GHz, outperforming many previously reported carbon-based absorbers. This heterointerface design strategy, combined with scalable fabrication route, provides a versatile framework for creating multifunctional and highly stable carbon-based EWA materials through controlled tuning of diamond and graphene domains. The demonstrated thermal, mechanical, and chemical durability further positions the composite as a compelling candidate for aerospace, marine, and advanced electronics applications, where reliable operation in harsh environments is essential.

Methods

Sample preparation

Nanodiamond powers (99%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical technology Co., Ltd. The initial nanodiamonds were first annealed in air at 450 °C for 60 min to remove amorphous carbon and sp2-bonded carbons. Subsequently, the samples were dried under vacuum in a tube furnace at 1200 °C for 10 min to induce structural transformation. High-pressure high-temperature synthesis was conducted using a China-type cubic large-volume press system equipped with a custom-designed sample assembly. Temperature was monitored with type-C thermocouples, and pressure was estimated based on established calibration curves. The recovered samples were 13.5–14.0 mm in diameter and 1.5–2.0 mm in height.

Structural characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed using a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å). Phase composition and volume fractions were determined through Rietveld refinement analysis. Raman spectra were recorded at room temperature using a Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution system with a 532 nm excitation laser. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were conducted on an ESCALAB 250 Xi system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to analyze elemental chemical states. Thin lamellae for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were prepared via focused ion beam using a Helios G4 system. High-resolution TEM and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images were acquired using a FEI Themis Z spherical aberration-corrected microscope operating at 300 kV. Electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) was acquired to determine the bonding sates of the composites.

Electromagnet parameter measurement

The electromagnetic parameters of the composites were measured using a vector network analyzer (Agilent E5071C) in the frequency range of 1–18 GHz. For testing, the samples were fabricated into toroidal shapes with outer and inner diameters of 7.00 mm and 3.04 mm, respectively. The reflection loss (RL), effective absorption bandwidth (EAB), attenuation coefficient, and impedance matching coefficient were calculated using custom Python scripts and CST Microwave Studio software. Electrical resistivity (ρ) as a function of temperature was measured in the range of 2–300 K using the standard four-probe method with a Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS, Quantum Design Inc.). Platinum wires were affixed to the polished sample surface using silver paste to ensure good electrical contact. The electrical conductivity (σ) was calculated from the measured resistivity values according to the relation σ = 1/ρ.

Corrosion resistance measurement

The electrochemical measurements were conducted to further evaluate the stability of DC samples. Bulk specimens were tested using Princeton VersaSTAT 4 electrochemical workstation, employing a of 3.5 % NaCl solution as the electrolyte and a saturated calomel electrode as the reference electrode. Electrochemical impedance (EIS) measurements were performed within the frequency range of 0.1–105 Hz with an amplitude of 10 mV. Tafel polarization curves were recorded over a potential range of −1 V to 1 V at a scan rate of 1 mV/s to determine the corrosion potential and the corrosion resistance of the samples.

High temperature microwave absorption testing

Microwave absorption performance at elevated temperatures was assessed using the waveguide method with a vector network analyzer (Ceyear 3672B) in the X-band frequency range. Samples were machined into rectangular parallelepipeds with dimensions of 22.86 mm × 10.16 mm × 1 mm to ensure compatibility with the waveguide system. The multiple synthesized samples were ground and consolidated using high-purity silica gel (SiO2·nH2O) to form specimens suitable. A controlled heating rate of 5 °C/min was applied, followed by a 10-min isothermal holding period at each target temperature (773 K, 873 K, and 973 K) to ensure achieve thermal equilibrium prior to measurement. In addition, to exclude any potential influence of the high-purity silicone (a high transparency adhesive used for high-temperature microwave absorption measurement51) on the measurements, a control experiment was performed at 873 K, and its effect was found to be negligible (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Mechanical and thermal measurements

The standard Vickers hardness of samples was measured using a microhardness tester (KB5-BVZ) on polished surfaces. An applied load was maintained for a dwell time of 10 s. The Vickers hardness (Hv) was calculated using the equation: Hv = 1854.4 F/L2, where F (N) is the applied load and L (μm) is the average diagonal length of the Vickers indentation. Five indentations were obtained at each to calculate an average hardness value. Fracture toughness (KIC was determined from KIC = 0.016(E/Hv)0.5F/C1.5, where F (N) is the applied load, C (μm) is the average length of the radial cracks measured from the indent center, and E (1140 GPa) is Young’s modulus of diamond. The flexural strength of the synthesized samples was evaluated using a three-point bending test. The thermal stability of the samples was tested using a NETZSCH STA 449F3 system, with a heating rate of 5 K/min across a temperature range from 300 to 1473 K. The thermal diffusivity was determined using an LFA467HT HyperFlash system, with measurements conducted at intervals of 50 K from 300 to 800 K, and at least three data points collected at each temperature to ensure accuracy.

Numerical simulation

Power loss density (PLD) distributions were simulated using COMSOL Multiphysics®. To optimize computational efficiency, a simplified cubic model with dimensions of 6 mm × 6 mm × 4 mm was employed. Microwave excitation at frequencies of 7, 11, and 15 GHz was introduced along the z-axis from the top surface. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in both the x and y directions to emulate infinite lateral extension and reduce edge effects.

First-principles calculations

All calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) within the GGA-PBE framework and projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotentials. The 500 eV cutoff energy was used. Structural relaxation was conducted with a 10 × 3 × 1 k-point mesh until forces were below 0.02 eV/Å and energy convergence reached 10−6 eV. We constructed diamond/graphite heterostructures, then created a carbon vacancy model and 3, 6, 9-layer variants. Dielectric constants were computed after full optimization of all five systems.
