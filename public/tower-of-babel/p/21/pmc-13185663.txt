Distinct effects of fibromyalgia pain, depression and anxiety on perception of affective touch and pain
Authors: Benedetta Albinni, Marisa Zimmerman, Vincent Alasha, Jacob Ross, Lindsey Crankshaw, Sandy R. Hu, Nathaniel Schuster, Laura Case
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gentle stroking that activates C-tactile afferents is typically pleasant, calming and pain relieving. Deep pressure touch frequently elicits similar affective responses. However touch is often less pleasant for individuals with chronic pain. It is important to understand the reasons for this clinical difference because of the close relationship of pain with reduced social connection, and the widespread use of touch-based therapies for pain relief. It is not known whether differences in touch perception in chronic pain relate to pain-specific processes or to comorbid affective symptoms. The current study compared affective touch perception in adults with the chronic pain condition fibromyalgia (FM) and no-pain (NP) controls, and the contributions of pain, depression, anxiety and trauma history to differences in touch perception. We hypothesized that deep pressure – like gentle stroking – would be less pleasant in FM and that these differences would be associated with depression and trauma history. Touch was rated as significantly less pleasant and more intense in FM. Chronic pain intensity best predicted reductions in touch pleasantness, whereas preferences for brushing versus tapping (affective touch preference) were positively predicted by depression and anxiety scores. In contrast heat pain unpleasantness was significantly predicted only by depression and anxiety. These results suggest that blunted touch pleasantness in FM is best explained by pain-specific processes, whereas pain unpleasantness is more closely associated with affective processes.

Introduction

Social touch is important for maintaining close relationships and buffering stress (Field, 2010; Gallace & Spence, 2010; Morrison, 2016). Slow, gentle stroking on skin with hair follicles is an optimal stimulus for C-tactile (CT) afferents (Bohic & Abraira, 2022), unmyelinated sensory afferents whose firing is closely associated with ratings of touch pleasantness (Loken et al., 2009). CT afferents have thus been closely linked to social touch behaviour and touch pleasantness (Case et al., 2016; Gordon et al., 2013). However other forms of touch, including deep pressure, also elicit affective responses and pain relief. Deep pressure has been found to reduce anxiety (Case et al., 2020) and experimental pain (Honigman et al., 2016), and it improves outcomes of massage therapy (Field et al., 2010). However the experience of social touch may differ in individuals with chronic pain. Understanding this clinical difference is important because of the role of social touch in social connection (Baumgartner et al., 2023) and the buffering effects of social connection on chronic pain (Morrison, 2023; Suvilehto et al., 2015). In addition chronic pain patients often seek touch-based therapies like massage for their pain, and these therapies may be particularly well suited to address the affective components of chronic pain (McGlone et al., 2024).

Touch is often less pleasant for individuals with chronic pain. We have previously shown that in the primary chronic pain condition fibromyalgia (FM), patients experience altered perception of CT-targeted gentle stroking (Case et al., 2016). In contrast to the preference for slow brushing observed in healthy volunteers, FM patients provide similar pleasantness ratings for slow and fast brushing (as well as intensity). Blunted pleasantness in FM was also observed by a separate research group for both slow (CT-optimal) and fast stroking (Boehme et al., 2020). A third study reported no differences in CT touch pleasantness in FM, but the mean ratings reported indicate reduced pleasantness in FM that the small study was underpowered to statistically detect (Tagini et al., 2024). Blunted perception of affective touch has now also been demonstrated in patients with lower back pain (Nees et al., 2018) and migraine (Lapp et al., 2020), whereas general reductions in touch pleasantness have been observed in postherpetic neuralgia and complex regional pain syndrome, conditions with altered C-fibre innervation density and sensory loss (Fusaro et al., 2022; Gossrau et al., 2021).

Although reductions in touch pleasantness in chronic pain and especially FM are evident, their cause is unclear. FM is a highly heterogeneous condition (Loevinger et al., 2012) that is not without controversy (Arnold et al., 2016). Typically FM is characterized by chronic pain caused by central sensitization, an amplification of pain in the central nervous system due to increased excitability and/or reduced inhibition of specific neural networks (Woolf, 2018). However some FM patients also present with peripheral pain generators (Arnold et al., 2016). Many experts agree that FM can be considered the far end of a continuum of chronic pain conditions ranging from predominantly nociceptive (peripheral) to predominantly neuropathic to predominantly centralized (Arnold et al., 2016). Alterations in the peripheral somatosensory system are one potential cause of differences in touch perception, such as the small-fibre pathology observed in some FM patients (Marshall et al., 2024; Sommer & Üçeyler, 2025), or the changes in spinal cord processing (Zain, 2025). However due to the significant role of affect in both touch and pain perception (Baliki et al., 2006; Hashmi et al., 2013; Rolls et al., 2003; Villemure & Bushnell, 2009), touch alterations might also be driven by negative mood.

Depressive symptoms are elevated in chronic pain. Major depressive disorder (MDD) co-occurs in about a third of chronic pain patients (Miller & Cano, 2009) and 62%–86% of FM patients (Gracely et al., 2012). MDD is associated with greater aversion to social touch, reduced comfort ratings of touch and reduced neural responses to interpersonal touch (Mielacher et al., 2024). Depression is also associated with reduced pleasantness of observed social touch and fewer current intimate touch experiences (Tinker et al., 2023). However two studies have found no correlation between affective touch perception and depression scores (Croy et al., 2016; Davidovic et al., 2018), and one reported a positive relationship between depression scores and higher brushing pleasantness in FM (Case et al., 2016).

Trauma history might also shape differences in affective touch perception. Trauma history is associated with greater unpleasantness of pain (Gómez-Pérez & López-Martínez, 2013) and reduced pleasantness of skin-to-skin stroking (but not stroking via a brush) (Strauss et al., 2019). Young adults with elevated rates of trauma exhibit blunted preferences for CT-targeted touch (Devine et al., 2020). Similarly individuals with a history of traumatic parental bonds and disorganized attachment perceive gentle stroking as unpleasant and respond with brain activation related to the fight-or-flight response (Spitoni et al., 2020). Reductions in blood plasma oxytocin levels have also been observed in individuals with an emotional trauma history (Donadon et al., 2018; Heim et al., 2009). This is significant because several current theories of FM and chronic pain closely link trauma, amplified threat perception and heightened nociceptive processing (Burke et al., 2017; Elman & Borsook, 2018; Korgan et al., 2025; Pinto et al., 2023; Strigo & Simmons, 2024). FM is associated with higher childhood trauma (Combas et al., 2022; Goldberg et al., 1999), higher rates of physical and emotional trauma (Yavne et al., 2018) and higher rates of dissociative experiences (Combas et al., 2022). In addition most FM patients meet diagnostic criteria for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Gardoki-Souto et al., 2022). In pain patients at large a history of abuse heightens pain severity and interference, and both affective distress and ‘fibromyalgianess’ of pain mediate associations between abuse and pain (Nicol et al., 2016).

In the current study we sought to determine whether chronic pain intensity, trauma history or affective symptoms underlie the differences in affective touch perception observed in adults with FM. We chose to study FM because of its high affective comorbidities and relevance to centralized/nociplastic pain components of other chronic pain conditions. In addition we sought to determine whether alterations in pleasant touch are specific to CT-optimal affective touch or also extend to other affective stimuli like deep pressure. We hypothesized that pleasantness of both affective touch and deep pressure would be reduced in individuals with FM, and that differences in touch pleasantness would be predicted most strongly by depression and trauma history. For comparison with pleasant touch we tested the influence of affective factors on heat pain.

Finally we tested whether respiration rate was predictive of touch pleasantness or altered by pleasant touch. Elevated respiration rate has been associated with affective conditions, including PTSD (Bryant et al., 2008). Because respiration rate is altered by gentle stroking in infants (Van Puyvelde et al., 2019a; Van Puyvelde et al., 2019b) and is associated with pain perception (Arsenault et al., 2013; Busch et al., 2012; Chalaye et al., 2009; Martin et al., 2012; Wells et al., 2020; Zautra et al., 2010), we additionally explored whether baseline respiration rate or respiration rate during touch stimulation would be associated with touch pleasantness ratings. This would provide initial evidence for respiration rate as a potential mediator of affective symptoms on touch perception.

Methods

Ethical approval

The current study was approved by the UC San Diego Human Subjects Protection Program IRB (191669) and pre-registered on clinicaltrials.gov NCT04206397. The study conformed to the standards set by the latest revision of the Declaration of Helsinki. Eligible participants received a detailed explanation of the study protocol, risks and benefits before providing written informed consent. Consent was signed electronically via the REDCap platform, with a research team member available by phone to ensure understanding and address any questions.

Participants

A total of 105 participants were enrolled in the current study at UC San Diego between September 2021 and October 2024 (age M = 40.4 (13.0), 94% female). Of these 81 completed session 1 (data from session 2 are reported elsewhere). This sample consisted of 40 patients with FM (age M = 40.7 (14.1)) and 41 no-pain (NP) controls with no indication of current or chronic pain (age M = 39.5 (12.0)). See Table 1 for full demographics. Research assistants were aware of pain diagnoses but blinded to study hypotheses. Touch was administered using mechanical devices, and ratings were recorded via a computer to avoid experimenter bias.

Eligibility screening and inclusion criteria.

Participants were first evaluated for general inclusion criteria, including age (18–65 years), fluency in English and FM diagnosis (FM group) or no indication of current pain or chronic pain (NP group). FM diagnosis by a physician was self-reported, and verification via medical records was obtained for the majority of cases (see Table 2 for verification rate and FM duration). FM participants were required to meet the 2016 revised American College of Rheumatology diagnostic criteria (Wolfe et al., 2016) for FM for >1 year (based on phone screen) and report no other chronic pain conditions of greater pain severity.

Exclusion criteria included sensory, motor or anatomical differences or injuries relevant to study procedures, anomalies of the central nervous system, pregnancy, inability to rate pain or sensations, major medical conditions, history of blood clots, current use of opiate medication, inability to identify a heat stimulus ≤50°C, history of fainting or seizures, history of frostbite, open cuts or sores, fracture of limb and history of Reynaud’s phenomenon.

Intake and questionnaires.

After consent was obtained participants completed an authorization for access to electronic health records and provided a detailed history of their use of opiate medications, to verify current lack of use for all participants and to document past opiate exposure for participants with FM. Approximately 1 week before session 1, participants completed a series of REDCap questionnaires. The questionnaires pre-registered as primary outcome measures in the current study were (1) the Chronic Pain Grade Questionnaire (CPGQ) (Von Korff et al., 1992) for evaluating pain severity and interference in daily activities, (2) the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) (Bernstein et al., 2003) to assess past trauma and (3) the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) (Zigmond & Snaith, 1983) to measure symptoms of anxiety and depression. Additional questionnaires for exploratory analyses not analysed here included the Longing for Interpersonal Touch Picture Questionnaire (LITPQ) (Beßler et al., 2020); the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) (Buysse et al., 1989); the Freiburg Mindfulness Inventory (FMI) (Walach et al., 2006); the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) (Osman et al., 1997); the Chronic Pain Acceptance Questionnaire (CPAQ-R) (Fish et al., 2013); the Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire (PSEQ) (Nicholas, 2007); the Social Connectedness Scale (SCS) (Lee & Robbins, 1995); the Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness, Version 2 (MAIA-2) (Mehling et al., 2018); the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (Bennett et al., 2009) (FM group only); and the Self-Compassion Scale (Neff, 2003).

Session 1. Session 1 took place at the Altman Clinical and Translational Research Institute (ACTRI) at UC San Diego and took ~1 h to complete. Upon arrival participants of childbearing potential completed a pregnancy test, and all participants underwent an opiate-focused drug screening. Pregnant individuals or current opiate users were dismissed. Next participants were trained on study rating scales (described later). Participants were fitted with a BioNomadix RSP respiratory transducer belt, and respiratory cycle was recorded using a Biopac MP160 over a 5-min resting period and throughout the session. The recording was synchronized to onset of data collection in E-Prime.

After resting respiration was collected participants rated their baseline mood and anxiety. They were then introduced to the affective touch tasks, which included 1 min each of gentle brushing, deep pressure, neutral touch and heat pain. Gentle brushing was administered at the CT-optimal velocity of 3 cm/s at ~0.3–0.4 N force using a soft goat hair watercolour brush attached to a custom robotic brushing device. Deep pressure was applied using a lymphoedema compression sleeve controlled by a custom-built device, oscillating at a rate of ~6×/min between 15 and 56 mmHg. Light tapping (intended as a neutral stimulus) was administered at ~1 Hz using two finger-like extensions of a motorized robot hand (Kidzrobotix) that tapped at a rate of ~1.5×/s. Finally heat pain was administered by a handheld QST.lab T09 thermode with two adjacent 7.4×12.2 mm plates activated at 45°C. Each innocuous touch type was repeated twice in a pseudorandom order followed by two repetitions of heat pain (see Fig. 1 for stimuli and timeline). Participants rated each touch block on visual analogue scales (VAS) for pleasantness (extremely unpleasant = −100 to extremely pleasant = 100) and intensity (no sensation = −100 to pain threshold = 0 to intolerable pain = 100). Heat pain was applied to the ventral upper arm (dermatome C5), and affective stimuli were applied to the ventral forearm (dermatome C5).

Finally a classic conditioned pain modulation (CPM) paradigm to test descending pain modulation (Yarnitsky, 2010) was conducted administering heat pain at the individually calibrated temperature via the thermode and hand immersion in a cold water-bath (PolyScience MX20R-30-A11B) (results and full methods published separately). Study session 2 occurred at a later time; results are reported separately.

Data analysis

Nearly all touch and heat pain ratings in the study exhibited significant deviation from normality (Shapiro–Wilk test P ≤ 0.05 for all ratings except for baseline heat intensity). Therefore Mann–Whitney U tests were conducted to assess differences between FM patients and NP participants in ratings of touch pleasantness and intensity. Missing items on questionnaires were imputed as the subscale mean for that participant.

To examine the predictors of affective and sensory responses, we conducted a series of generalized linear model (GLM) regression analyses. The primary predictors included depression (assessed using the HADS), chronic pain level (assessed using the CPGQ) and childhood trauma (assessed using the CTQ). Age was included as a covariate of non-interest due to its significant correlation with touch pleasantness in the current study (brushing: rs (79) = 0.254, P = 0.022; compression: rs (79) = 0.198, P = 0.077; tapping: rs (79) = 0.221, P = 0.047) as well as past literature (Sehlstedt et al., 2016). The dependent variables were pleasantness ratings for each touch type. We also explored a measure of relative affective touch, defined as the difference between brushing pleasantness and tapping pleasantness, to examine individual differences in processing CT-optimal touch. Whereas tapping does activate CTs brushing usually elicits a more robust and sustained CT response (Watkins et al., 2017; Wessberg et al., 2003; Xu et al., 2024); similar approaches have been taken in the CT touch literature to examine individual variability in preferences for stimuli that elicit stronger CT activation (e.g. Perini et al., 2015).

In addition we conducted exploratory analyses of the intensity ratings of each touch type, as well as of the unpleasantness and intensity of heat pain. To control for potential pharmacological effects, we repeated these models including binary factors for current pain medication (which included gabapentinoids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and tricyclic anti-depressants (TCAs)) or SSRI/SNRI use as covariates. For exploratory purposes correlations between the primary touch ratings and clinical predictors were computed. Finally to check for a role of anxiety – which was highly correlated with depression (rs (79) = 0.741, P < 0.001) – we conducted parallel analyses substituting anxiety (assessed using the HADS Anxiety Subscale) for depression.

Respiration data were analysed using BIOPAC AcqKnowledge software (BIOPAC Systems, 2019). Respiration data were downsampled to 62.5 samples/s and bandpass filtered with a low-frequency cut-off of 0.05 Hz and a high-frequency cut-off of 1 Hz. Respiration cycles were identified from positive and negative peaks, and respiration rate was calculated as breaths per minute. For each stimulus of interest (e.g. brushing for 30 s), the average respiration rate over the stimulus duration was calculated. Additional regression models were conducted to examine whether baseline respiration rates predicted the pleasantness ratings of touch stimuli (brushing, compression, tapping) and heat pain. For a further exploratory step we also tested whether baseline respiration ratings predicted intensity ratings. To account for possible confounding factors, we included pain medication use and SSRI/SNRI use in the models.

Results

Chronic pain associated with lower touch pleasantness and higher touch intensity

On average brushing and compression were rated as non-painful and either neutral or pleasant by both FM and NP participants, whereas tapping was rated as unpleasant by FM and pleasant by NP participants (see Fig. 2). Affective touch stimuli were rated as significantly less pleasant by FM patients for all touch types (brushing: U = 574.5, Z = −2.320, P = 0.020, confidence interval (CI) [ 44.0, 4.0], r = 0.26; compression: U = 568.0, Z = −2.381, P = 0.017, CI [−32.0, −2.0], r = 0.26; tapping: U = 295.0, Z = −4.960, P < 0.001, CI [−50.0, −19.0], r = 0.55). In addition all touch types were rated as significantly more intense by FM than NP participants (brushing: U = 526.5, Z = −2.773, P = 0.006, CI [6.0, 38.0], r = −0.31; compression: U = 423.5, Z = −3.746, P < 0.001, CI [14.0, 43.0], r = −0.42; tapping: U = 365.5, Z = −4.294, P < 0.001, CI [19.0, 47.0], r = −0.48). Similarly heat pain was rated as more unpleasant (U = 555.5, Z = −2.500, P = 0.012, CI [−39.0, −2.0] r = −0.02) and more intense (U = 478.0, Z = −3.231, P = 0.001, CI [12.0, 44.0] r = −0.35) by the FM group.

Descriptive results of questionnaires

All questionnaires demonstrated excellent or near-excellent internal consistency (see Table 3). FM patients reported significantly higher pain, childhood trauma, anxiety and depression than participants in the NP group (see Table 3 for descriptive statistics).

Chronic pain associated with lower touch pleasantness and age associated with higher touch pleasantness

For brushing and tapping age positively predicted touch pleasantness, whereas chronic pain level negatively predicted touch pleasantness (brushing: F (4,76) = 3.061, P = 0.021; age: β = 0.257, t = 2.366, P = 0.021, CI [0.041, 0.474]; CPGQ: β = −0.332, t = −2.386, P = 0.020, CI [−0.609, −0.055]; compression: F (4,76) = 1.984, P = 0.105; tapping: F (4,76) = 5.367, P < 0.001; age: β = 0.218, t = 2.104, P = 0.039, CI [0.012, 0.424]; CPGQ: β = −0.316, t = −20.390, P = 0.019, CI [−0.580, −0.053]; see Fig. 3). Results were unchanged when pain medication and SSRI/SNRI use were added to the model as potential influencing factors. Results modelling anxiety instead of depression obtained the same findings, except that anxiety significantly predicted reduced pleasantness of tapping as well (F (4,76) = 6.277, P < 0.001; anxiety: β = −0.292, t = −2.058, P = 0.043, CI [−0.571, −0.005]; unchanged after modelling pain medication and SSRI/SNRI use). In addition controlling for pain medication and SSRI/SNRI use made the model predicting brushing pleasantness from anxiety marginally significant (pain medication: (F(5, 75) = 2.321, P = 0.051; age: β = 0.281, t = 2.487, P = 0.015; CPGQ: β = −0.353, t = −2.215, P = 0.030; SSRI/SNRI: F(5, 75) = 2.322, P = 0.051; age β = 0.280, t = 2.480, P = 0.015; CPGQ: β = −0.352, t = −2.262, P = 0.027).

Chronic pain associated with higher intensity of compression

The regression model for brushing intensity was not significant (F (4,76) = 1.491, P = 0.213). Results were unchanged after controlling for pain medication. However the model was significant after controlling for SSRI/SNRI use, with SSRI/SNRI use predicting higher brushing intensity (F (5,75) = 2.984, P = 0.016; SSRI/SNRI: β = 0.331, t = 2.895, P = 0.005). For compression intensity CPGQ was a significant positive predictor (F (4,76) = 4.028, P = 0.005; CPGQ: β = 0.351, t = 2.580, P = 0.012, CI [0.080, 0.622]; these results were unchanged after controlling for pain medication and SSRI/SNRI use). For tapping the model was significant, but no coefficient reached statistical significance (F (4,76) = 4.377, P = 0.003; results were unchanged after controlling for pain medications and SSRI/SNRI use). Finally each model was tested with anxiety scores instead of depression scores. For brushing intensity the model was not significant (F (4,76) = 1.638, P = 0.174; unchanged after controlling for pain medication). After controlling for SSRI/SNRI use, the model showed significance with SSRI/SNRI use as a significant predictor (F (5,75) = 3.187, P = 0.012; SSRI/SNRI β = 0.337, t = 2.953, P = 0.004). For compression intensity the model was significant (F (4, 76) = 3.965, P = 0.006; CPGQ: β = 0.390, t = 2.623, P = 0.011, CI [0.094, 0.687]). Results were unchanged by the addition of SSRI/SNRI or pain medication use. For tapping intensity the model was significant (F (4,76) = 3.972, P = 0.006); however no coefficient reached significance (unchanged after controlling for pain medication and SSRI/SNRI use).

Depression and anxiety predict higher relative pleasantness of affective touch, and anxiety predicts higher relative intensity of affective touch

Finally we conducted the same regression analyses to determine predictors of relative differences in brushing versus tapping ratings (as a measure of preference for stronger CT input), separately for pleasantness and intensity. For pleasantness depression was a significant positive predictor (overall model: F (4,76) = 2.715, P = 0.036; depression: β = 0.390, t = 2.863, P = 0.005, CI [0.119, 0.661]; see Fig. 4). Controlling for pain medication made the model marginally significant (F (5, 75) = 2.321, P = 0.051; depression: β = 0.383, t = 2.807, P = 0.006). Controlling for SSRI/SNRI use made the model non-significant, likely due to the high association of depression and SSRI/SNRI use (F (5, 75) = 2.153, P = 0.068). For intensity the overall model was not significant (F (4,76) = 2.426, P = 0.055). The finding did not change after controlling for pain medication use but became significant after controlling for SSRI/SNRI use (F (5,75) = 2.681, P = 0.028; depression: β = −0.315, t = −2.333, P = 0.022).

When anxiety was modelled instead of depression, anxiety was a significant predictor for brushing versus tapping pleasantness (overall model: F (4,76) = 4.019, P = 0.005; anxiety: β = 0.542, t = 3.640, P < 0.001, CI [0.245, 0.837], unchanged after controlling for pain medication and SSRI/SNRI use). Anxiety also predicted brushing versus tapping intensity (overall model: F (4,76) = 3.128, P = 0.019; anxiety: β = −0.431, t = −2.838, P = 0.006, CI [−0.732, −0.127]; unchanged after controlling for pain medication and SSRI/SNRI use).

To further explore the role of depression in affective touch preference, we conducted a Mann–Whitney U test to compare pleasantness of brushing and tapping between participants with clinically significant (HADS >7) (N = 26) and those with non-significant depression scores (N = 55). Ratings of brushing pleasantness did not differ (U = 705.5, Z = −0.096, P = 0.923, CI [−22.0, 22.0], r = 0.0107), but participants with clinically significant HADS depression scores rated tapping as significantly less pleasant (U = 442.5, Z = −2.757, P = 0.006, CI [−38.0, −5.0], r = −0.3063).

Exploratory correlations between the primary touch ratings and clinical predictors are presented in Table 4.

Depression and anxiety associated with greater heat pain unpleasantness

The regression model predicting heat unpleasantness was significant (F (4,76) = 3.709, P = 0.008). Higher depression scores were significantly associated with reduced heat pain pleasantness (β = −0.300, t = −2.253, P = 0.027, CI [−0.565, −0.035]; unchanged after controlling for pain medication or SSRI/SNRI use; see Fig. 5). The regression model predicting heat intensity was also significant (F (4,76) = 4.446, P = 0.003); however no coefficient reached significance (unchanged after controlling for pain medication and SSRI/SNRI use).

When anxiety was modelled instead of depression, the regression model predicting heat unpleasantness was significant (overall model: F (4, 76) = 3.752, P = 0.008). Anxiety was the only significant predictor, with higher anxiety predicting reduced heat pain pleasantness (β = −0.342, t = −2.287, P = 0.025, CI [−0.642, −0.046]; unchanged after controlling for pain medication or SSRI/SNRI use). The regression model predicting heat pain intensity was significant (overall model: F (4, 76) = 4.215, P = 0.004; no significant factors; unchanged after controlling for pain medication and SSRI/SNRI use).

Respiration rate was not associated with touch pleasantness

Baseline respiration rate did not differ significantly between FM and NP (NP M: 12.7 (3.30), N = 34; FM M: 12.4 (4.22), N = 35; t (67) = −0.290, P = 0.773). The regression models predicting touch unpleasantness from baseline respiration were not significant (brushing: F (1,67) = 0.130, P = 0.720; compression: F (1,67) = 0.220, P = 0.640; tapping: F (1, 67) = 0.450, P = 0.505). Similarly the regression models predicting touch intensity from baseline respiration were not significant (brushing: F (1,67) = 0.321, P = 0.573; compression: F (1,67) = 3.270, P = 0.075; tapping: F (1,67) = 1.240, P = 0.269). Baseline respiration remained non-significant after controlling for SSRI/SNRI and pain medication use.

Discussion

We observed significant reductions in touch pleasantness in participants with FM compared to NP controls, for all touch types tested (CT-optimal brushing, compression and tapping). No differences in preference for CT-targeted touch (brushing vs. tapping) were observed. In addition all touch types were rated as significantly more intense by FM patients. These findings are consistent with the largest previous study of affective touch perception in FM, which found reductions in the pleasantness of both slow and fast brushing (Boehme et al., 2020), and with generally heightened sensory sensitivities observed in FM to both touch and non-touch stimuli (Wilbarger & Cook, 2011).

The novel aim of our study was to determine whether altered touch processing in FM is driven by chronic pain intensity or by comorbid affective conditions such as anxiety, depression or trauma history. In contrast to our hypothesis depression, anxiety and trauma history did not significantly predict ratings of touch pleasantness. This is surprising given prior findings of altered touch unpleasantness in individuals with trauma history (Combas et al., 2022; Devine et al., 2020; Strauss et al., 2019) and depression (Case et al., 2016; Tinker et al., 2023). However preferences for brushing versus tapping (CT affective touch preference) were predicted by depression and anxiety scores. Interrogating the effect we found that participants with clinically significant depression scores rated tapping as significantly less pleasant and often mildly unpleasant, which contributed to this finding. Tapping has been shown in naturalistic studies to successfully communicate getting a person’s attention (McIntyre et al., 2022). Because depressed individuals exhibit more negative attitudes towards social touch, especially touch from strangers – and because seeking a person’s attention can be for either positive or negative purposes – it may be that the ambiguity of the gesture lent itself to a more negative interpretation in individuals with higher depressive symptoms (Triscoli et al., 2019).

The general lack of influence of affective symptoms on touch pleasantness in the current study may be attributed to the largely impersonal nature of the touch stimuli. One prior study of individuals raised in foster care with associated trauma showed a reduced effect of CT velocity but no overall significant reduction in touch pleasantness (Devine et al., 2020), whereas another found reductions in pleasantness exclusively for interpersonal skin-to-skin stroking but not impersonal touch (Strauss et al., 2019). In the current study all touch was mechanical, although the tapping device was finger like, and the thermode used to deliver heat pain was handheld by the experimenter. It is therefore possible that the affective influences we observed on brushing versus tapping, and on heat pain, are best explained by (unintentionally) more closely approximating human touch. However pleasantness reductions in the current study were related to depression scores, not trauma history.

Touch pleasantness was significantly predicted by chronic pain intensity and age. Age was associated with higher touch pleasantness, whereas chronic pain intensity was associated with reduced touch pleasantness. These results suggest a strong link between chronic pain and alterations in pleasant touch perception, which are not explained by depression or trauma history. These results are consistent with prior demonstrations of higher touch pleasantness in older age (Sehlstedt et al., 2016). In addition these results are consistent with recent evidence in mice demonstrating how pain-related pathology can render touch unpleasant. Specifically chronic pain mediates changes in the appetitive value of affective touch in mice, associated with brain areas that receive direct spinal cord touch input (Zain, 2025). Indeed disinhibition in the spinal cord can unmask interconnections that allow low-threshold afferents to begin activating neurons responsible for transmitting noxious information (Keller et al., 2007; Torsney & MacDermott, 2006). In addition there is an overlapping ensemble of neurons in the basolateral amygdala (BLA) encoding both light touch and pain, which have demonstrated the expansion of neural activity to light touch after induction of a neuropathic pain state in mice (Corder et al., 2019). This highlights a role of altered pain and touch processing leading to allodynia, which may explain the pleasantness reductions to touch types in FM.

Touch pleasantness reductions in FM could also relate to small-fibre pathology observed in some FM patients (Marshall et al., 2024; Sommer & Üçeyler, 2025) or to autoimmune processes. Recent findings have suggested an autoimmune (but not inflammatory) basis for FM, supported by induction of several FM symptoms in mice after transfer of purified serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) from patients with FM (Goebel et al., 2021). FM is inconsistently associated with higher levels of certain IgG autoantibodies, believed to induce tissue-level changes that sensitize or activate nociceptors (Goebel, 2025). Due to the high rates of trauma and PTSD associated with FM, it has been proposed that trauma and distress may elicit the production of harmful non-inflammatory autoantibodies in at-risk individuals in FM (Goebel, 2025). To the best of our knowledge, the influence of IgG on pleasant touch afferents has not been studied.

In contrast to findings for pleasant touch, we found that depression and anxiety – not chronic pain – were the strongest predictors of heat pain unpleasantness ratings, but not heat pain intensity. This is consistent with the modulation of affective (but not discriminative) components of pain by mood (Villemure & Bushnell, 2002; Villemure & Bushnell, 2009). Whereas controlling for pain medication use did not significantly alter most regression models, SSRI/SNRI use was a significant predictor of higher perceived intensity for brushing in several models. This may suggest that serotoninergic modulation influences central mechanisms for tactile input, selectively enhancing the sensory salience of certain stimuli. Importantly the influence of SSRI/SNRI use was limited to intensity and did not affect pleasantness ratings or affective touch preference. Crucially the modulation of tactile intensity by serotonergic medication and the selective influence of mood on pain unpleasantness point to distinct neurochemical systems governing sensory salience versus affective appraisal. This reinforces the idea that hedonic alterations in FM are not uniform but may result from the interplay between central sensitization, neuromodulatory imbalances and possibly peripheral immune contributions.

Finally in contrast to past findings on pain (Arsenault et al., 2013; Busch et al., 2012; Chalaye et al., 2009; Martin et al., 2012; Wells et al., 2020; Zautra et al., 2010), resting respiratory rate did not differ between the FM and NP groups and was not associated with touch or pain perception. These findings suggest that baseline autonomic arousal, at least measured by respiration rate, does not contribute significantly to the observed alterations in touch pleasantness in FM.

The present findings underscore the complexity of sensory-affective processing in FM. Although alterations in touch pleasantness appear relatively independent of mood or trauma history, they remain tightly linked to chronic pain, possibly involving peripheral or spinal mechanisms. This suggests that in FM, affective touch may be altered not through affective dysregulation but via altered sensory evaluation processes entwined by pain chronification. However it is possible that mood might alter more personal forms of affective touch such as skin-to-skin stroking (Strauss et al., 2019). We also acknowledge that pain patients without visible signs of illness often receive less empathy in healthcare settings (Paul-Savoie et al., 2018) and may enter the clinic environment with negative associations from past experiences of invalidation and stigmatization; these factors may alter touch perception in complex ways. Future work should aim to clarify how pain, mood and systemic factors converge to reshape not only sensory processing but also the emotional and social meaning of touch in individuals with FM and other chronic pain conditions.

In conclusion this study advances our understanding of sensory-affective alterations in FM by disentangling the complex interplay between chronic pain, mood and touch perception. The findings highlight the need to consider FM not only as a pain disorder but also as a multisystem condition involving altered sensory valuation and affective processing. Addressing these multidimensional disruptions may open new avenues for therapeutic interventions aimed at restoring the affective dimensions of touch and improving patients’ quality of life. Future research integrating neuroimmune, neurochemical and psychosocial perspectives will be essential to fully unravel the mechanisms underlying affective touch differences and their role in the lived experience of FM.
