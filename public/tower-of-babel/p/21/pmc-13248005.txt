Using Metabarcoding Techniques to Map Multiple Dung Beetle‐Vertebrate iDNA Networks in a Southeast Asian Forest
Authors: Xin Rui Ong, Rosie Drinkwater, Samuel K S Cheong, Rebecca Ker Loh, Hie Lim Kim, Adriana Lopes dos Santos, Nalini Puniamoorthy, Eleanor M Slade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGiven the high levels of endemism, diversity, and increasing anthropogenic impacts in tropical regions, studies on species interactions and ecological networks are urgently needed to understand community responses to environmental change. Increasingly, molecular methods are used to monitor biodiversity and identify species interactions. In this study, we developed an optimised DNA metabarcoding protocol to construct quantitative dung beetle‐vertebrate trophic networks in a heterogenous tropical forest. We show that while the highest probability of detection is within 3 h of feeding, vertebrate DNA can still be detected in most beetles after 24 h, including around 50% of beetles with visually empty guts. Using the vertebrate taxa detected from DNA in the gut contents, we constructed dung beetle‐vertebrate networks across 50 forest sites. Our networks displayed high trophic generalism and nestedness. By using group‐specific primers, we documented interactions between dung beetles and multiple vertebrate species, including amphibians, birds, and rare mammals, such as the Sunda slow loris and Sunda pangolin. Our optimised molecular techniques contribute towards the development of new methods for comprehensive biodiversity assessments and ecological network studies that are urgently needed to monitor biodiversity in the hyper‐diverse tropics.Keywords: conservation, ecological networks, invertebrate‐derived DNA, metabarcoding, Scarabaeinae, Southeast Asia, species interactions, tropical biodiversity

Introduction

Species interact through complex ecological networks, including those that are mutualistic (e.g., plant‐pollinator; Vizentin‐Bugoni et al. 2018), antagonistic (e.g., bat‐prey; Hemprich‐Bennett et al. 2020), and commensalistic (e.g., dung beetle‐mammal dung; Chiew et al. 2022). Anthropogenic pressures can cause changes and extinctions of these interactions, resulting in ecological cascades that may lead to a loss of ecosystem resilience and to multiple species extinctions (Andresen et al. 2018; Tylianakis and Morris 2017; Valiente‐Banuet et al. 2015). Yet, conservation strategies are usually informed by biodiversity metrics (e.g., species richness, diversity) or the presence of rare or threatened species, while overlooking ecological interactions (Tylianakis and Morris 2017; Harvey et al. 2017; Tylianakis et al. 2010). In tropical regions, high levels of endemism and diversity, coupled with increasing anthropogenic impacts have prompted many studies on the effects of anthropogenic drivers on tropical biodiversity (e.g., Brodie et al. 2015; Scriven et al. 2015). However, there are still significant gaps in knowledge of the effects of environmental changes on species interactions and ecological networks (Tylianakis and Morris 2017), which urgently need to be filled for the effective conservation and management of threatened habitats.

Increasingly, ecological network studies have adopted ‘replicated network’ approaches to achieve a more robust assessment of how environmental changes affect species interactions (Ma et al. 2018). However, constructing multiple networks across different environmental conditions is considered ambitious and labour‐intensive when using conventional means (e.g., observations of pollinator visitations) to determine species interactions (Derocles et al. 2018; Evans et al. 2016). To overcome these challenges, DNA metabarcoding offers a scalable alternative for constructing multiple networks across environmental gradients (Ma et al. 2018; Derocles et al. 2018; Evans et al. 2016; Cagnolo 2018). For terrestrial ecosystems, invertebrate‐derived DNA (iDNA) has proven effective in revealing interactions and building trophic networks between invertebrates and other species (e.g., Pedersen et al. 2024; Srivathsan et al. 2023).

Dung beetles have been suggested as potential iDNA samplers for biodiversity monitoring by sequencing vertebrate DNA from their gut contents (e.g., Pedersen et al. 2024; Drinkwater, Williamson, et al. 2021; Gillett et al. 2016). They are also a model taxon for biodiversity research as they respond rapidly to environmental changes, are excellent bioindicator species, and can be sampled cost‐effectively (Nichols and Gardner 2011). Previous studies have inferred dung beetle‐vertebrate interactions through co‐occurrence surveys or dung attraction experiments (Chiew et al. 2022; Nichols et al. 2009; Ong et al. 2020; Raine et al. 2018). The detection of vertebrate DNA from dung beetle gut contents improves on these methods as it confirms actual feeding links rather than just attraction to vertebrate‐derived resources or co‐occurrence in the same area (Pedersen et al. 2024; Drinkwater, Williamson, et al. 2021). Such methods are particularly important for understanding forest canopy communities, where dung beetles may be utilising the excrement of arboreal vertebrates, animals that are often difficult to monitor using conventional methods (e.g., camera trapping). There are few studies on dung beetle‐vertebrate interactions, and the impacts of environmental change on these networks (Raine and Slade 2019). While multiple studies have determined the effects of forest structure on tropical animal diversity and network interactions (e.g., Bogoni et al. 2016; Thiel et al. 2021; Tylianakis et al. 2007), no studies, to our knowledge, have attempted to build multiple dung beetle‐vertebrate networks across a heterogenous forest area or investigate the vertical stratification of these interactions.

In this study, we established a DNA metabarcoding pipeline to construct trophic networks of ground and canopy dung beetle communities within secondary forests in Singapore and reveal direct feeding interactions between dung beetles and vertebrates. To inform sampling timeframes in field surveys, we first performed a controlled feeding experiment (using elephant dung) to establish the appropriate window for successful detection of degraded DNA from dung beetles and how long these detections persist post‐digestion. We focused on beetles from the genus Onthophagus, the most abundant and diverse genus of Southeast Asian dung beetles. We also tested the effect of sample storage conditions on successful DNA detection. Based on these findings, we developed an optimised metabarcoding protocol to detect vertebrate DNA from the gut contents of dung beetles sampled from ground and canopy traps deployed across 50 forest sites. Using metabarcoding techniques, we demonstrated that multiple networks can be constructed across large spatial scales, providing high‐resolution insights into individual dung beetle–vertebrate interactions. Using six network metrics (adapted from Chiew et al. 2022; Tylianakis et al. 2007; see Section 2 for detailed predictions of each metric), we further investigated the effects of forest structure on these networks and if they were vertically stratified.

Materials and Methods

Feeding Experiment to Determine Window for Successful DNA Detection

Sample Collection and Experimental Design

To determine the window of detection for degraded DNA from dung beetle gut contents, we performed a controlled feeding experiment (adapted from Drinkwater, Williamson, et al. 2021) on small‐sized beetles from the genus Onthophagus. This is the most abundant and diverse genus of Southeast Asian dung beetles and is likely to have a different detection window than that of the large‐sized beetles (Catharsius sp.) previously ascertained by Drinkwater, Williamson, et al. (2021). We used 144 individual Onthophagus beetles (see Appendix S1 for specimen collection details) and starved them for 48 h to purge any previously ingested dung. We then fed them elephant dung as it could be clearly differentiated from material that might have been ingested prior to the feeding experiment, since elephants are not present in Singapore's forests. We sampled their gut contents at eight time points across a 24 h window (0 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 6 h, 10 h and 24 h) based on the findings of (Drinkwater, Williamson, et al. 2021; Drinkwater, Jucker, et al. 2021), which found that the amount of DNA in the guts of large‐sized beetles sharply decreased 6 h post‐feeding. As we expected small‐sized dung beetles to have shorter digestive tracts and digestion times than large ones, we increased the number of earlier time points (i.e., 30 min, 1 h, 2 h and 3 h) and selected 24 h as the final time point. At each time point, we randomly selected 18 beetles among the remaining beetles and euthanised them in 90% ethanol. To determine the effect of storage conditions on the successful detection of DNA, we stored nine randomly selected individuals from each time point in 90% ethanol at −80°C and stored the remaining nine in ethanol at room temperature (see Appendix S1 for additional details on the feeding experiment).

DNA Extraction and Quantification

Detailed methods of the gut dissection, qPCR, and PCR techniques are presented in Appendix S1. For each gut sample, we visually determined and scored the gut fullness into four categories: Empty, empty (with specks of dung), partially full and full (Figure S1). We preserved the gut samples in 100% ethanol and stored them at −80°C. We extracted the DNA from each sample and used quantitative PCR (qPCR) to determine the amount of elephant DNA that could be detected from the guts at each time point. To quantify the amount of DNA in each sample, we used a standard curve of seven samples of known concentrations of elephant DNA. To confirm the identity of the gut contents, we performed PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing of each sample. We confirmed the successful detection of elephant DNA by matching the Sanger results against the NCBI GenBank database with BLAST (Camacho et al. 2009).

Constructing Quantitative Dung Beetle‐Vertebrate Trophic Networks

Sample Collection

To compare between canopy‐ and ground‐active dung beetle communities, we deployed 50 canopy and 50 ground human dung‐baited live pitfall traps across the Central Catchment Nature Reserve (CCNR; 1.3533° N, 103.8184° E) in Singapore from January to March 2023 (see Appendix S1 for trapping set up and detailed site information). The CCNR spans over 2000 ha of native‐dominated secondary forests that vary in structural complexity along a successional gradient (Yee et al. 2016, 2011). We selected 50 survey sites to ensure comprehensive coverage of the CCNR (Figure S3). Sites were spaced at least 500 m apart to ensure independence between sites (da Silva and Hernández 2015), and each trap was left in the field for 24 h. Given that tropical dung beetles typically move an average of 100 m per day (Gray et al. 2022), vertebrate DNA detections from sampled dung beetles are likely to be from vertebrates that were present in the vicinity of each site. Upon collection, we directly euthanised all beetles in 90% ethanol and transported them to the lab for immediate storage at −80°C. We sorted and identified each beetle specimen to species based on morphological traits and DNA barcodes and assigned unidentified species to morphospecies. All specimens from this study are deposited in the Tropical Ecology and Entomology Lab (TEE Lab), Asian School of the Environment, Nanyang Technological University.

Vegetation Structure

To characterise the forest structure at each survey site, we recorded four environmental variables: basal tree area, shrub cover, ground cover, and mean canopy height. We estimated basal tree area (m2/ha) of each site using the Bitterlich system (Bitterlich 1947). As a substitute for a basal area factor 10 (BAF 10) angle gauge, we used a 2 cm diameter coin that was held approximately 66 cm from the eye. Using each site's coordinates as the centre point, we counted the number of trees in a 360o direction that appeared wider than the coin's diameter. In a 10 m radius around the centre point, we visually estimated shrub cover (%) based on increments of 20% of the area covered by plants between 0.5 cm and 2 m height. At the centre point, we estimated ground cover using the Canopeo mobile application (Oklahoma State University 2015), which calculates the percentage of greenery through a photo of the ground taken at chest level. For canopy height, we downloaded the high‐resolution 1 m global canopy height map (Tolan et al. 2024) and calculated the mean canopy height of a 100 m radius buffer area around each site.

DNA Extraction and Metabarcoding

To minimise contamination from human DNA, we followed the washing protocol described in Pedersen et al. (2024), where each whole beetle was cleaned sequentially in distilled water, 2% NaClO and 70% ethanol. For each beetle, we dissected out the gut, assessed gut fullness and extracted DNA using the same methods as in our feeding experiment (see above and Appendix S1). For large‐bodied Catharsius cf. renaudpauliani beetles, we modified the protocol by tripling the volumes of proteinase K, buffer AL, and ethanol to ensure that the gut contents were fully submerged in the digestion buffer. To account for the increased volume of digestion buffer, we transferred the buffer into two separate spin columns, resulting in two DNA extracts for each individual C. cf. renaudpauliani gut sample (extracts were kept separately for PCR amplification and sequencing, and their sequenced reads were combined during the bioinformatics step; see below). We performed the DNA extractions in batches of 15 samples, with one negative control per batch.

For targeted amplification of vertebrate DNA, we performed DNA metabarcoding using two primer sets that have been used in previous vertebrate metabarcoding studies (Drinkwater, Williamson, et al. 2021; Drinkwater, Jucker, et al. 2021; Lynggaard et al. 2022; Schnell et al. 2018). Specifically, we used the 16S mammal‐specific primers (16Smam1forward: 5′‐CGGTTGGGGTGACCTCGGA‐3′ and 16Smam2 reverse: 5′‐GCTGTTATCCCTAGGGTAACT‐3′; amplicon size: ca. 95 bp; Taylor 1996) and vertebrate‐specific primers of the 12S rRNA gene (12SV05 forward: 5′‐TTAGATACCCCACTATGC‐3′ and 12SV05 reverse: 5′‐TAGAACAGGCTCCTCTAG‐3′; amplicon size: ca. 97 bp; Riaz et al. 2011). We used the ‘tagged PCR’ approach (Bohmann et al. 2022) where our primers were tagged with unique 9 bp indexes (adopted from Loh et al. 2023; Meier et al. 2016) to enable multiplexing of multiple samples on the Illumina Next‐Generation sequencing platform. To reduce PCR inhibition, we used 10× dilutions of the extracted DNA from Catharsius cf. renaudpauliani (about 8 times larger than Onthophagus sp.) and Paragymnopleurus maurus (about four times larger than Onthophagus sp.) gut samples. For both primer sets, we set up PCR reactions in a total volume of 20 μL, which consisted of 10 μL of 2xTaq MasterMix (Cowin Biotech), 1 μL of each forward and reverse primers (5 μM), 6 μL of ddH20 and 2 μL of sample DNA. The PCR thermocycling conditions were: 5 min initial denaturation at 94°C, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 1 min, annealing at 57°C (mammal‐specific) or 48°C (vertebrate‐specific) for 30 s, extension at 72°C (1 min), followed by a final extension at 72°C (5 min). We had three independent PCR replicates (following Loh et al. 2024) for each sample (1971 replicates in total) and controls. We used separate PCR plates for each of the three replicates, with each plate containing a negative. To account for tag‐jumping (see Schnell et al. 2015), each PCR replicate was amplified with a unique and matched tagged pair of forward and reverse primers (e.g., replicate 1—forward primer tag 1 and reverse primer tag 1; replicate 2—forward primer tag 2 and reverse primer tag 2). We used the same combination of forward and reverse primer tags for the PCR replicates of each sample.

We checked for successful PCR amplification on 2% E‐gels (Invitrogen E‐Gel Double Comb Agarose Gels) for a subsample of reactions from each PCR plate. For sequencing, we pooled the products from each PCR plate separately, including all samples, extraction controls, and negative controls. We outsourced PCR purification, library preparation, and the Next‐Generation Sequencing (Illumina Novaseq platform) to Axil Scientific Pte Ltd. We sequenced with 150 bp paired‐end reads aiming for 2 Gb of data for each pooled product (i.e., approximately 69,000 reads per sample).

Bioinformatics

We assessed the quality of the raw reads using FastQC (Andrews et al. 2012). We demultiplexed raw reads (FASTQ files) of each PCR pool using the demultiplexer Python script (https://github.com/DominikBuchner/demultiplexer). We then processed demultiplexed reads using the APSCALE pipeline, which comprised of primer merging, adapter removal, quality filtering, Operational Taxonomical Units (OTU) clustering (sequence similarity threshold of 97%), denoising and filtering using the LULU algorithm (Buchner et al. 2022). This produced a matrix of replicates (rows) and OTUs (columns) for each primer set (i.e., 16S matrix and 12S matrix), with the number of reads as cell values.

We applied a series of post‐bioinformatic filtering steps to remove contamination and low‐frequency artefacts (following Drake et al. 2022). First, to account for contamination within each PCR replicate, we removed any OTUs within the replicate with read counts that were less than 1% of the total read count for each replicate. Next, we removed any read counts that were less than 10 to remove low‐frequency artefacts. To address contamination detected by negative controls and blanks while avoiding the elimination of true detections, we applied a less conservative method of retaining OTUs that were detected in at least five sample replicates (see Table S2 for a summary of read counts after each step in the bioinformatics analysis). We compared this method against stricter filtering methods (i.e., removing all sample read counts that were lower than the highest read count across negative controls and blanks) to determine if there were differences in the number of vertebrate species detected. As the resulting vertebrate detections were similar among the methods (i.e., 16S: 42 species across all methods; 12S: 60 to 62 species across all methods), we presented the findings from the less conservative method, which retained the highest number of samples and detections (see Appendix S1 for a comparison of the different contamination filtering methods).

After the post‐bioinformatic filtering process, we used blastn to match all remaining OTUs against the NCBI core_nt database. From the BLAST results, we retained vertebrate (Phylum Chordata) hits with sequence lengths of at least 80 bp, and with e‐values less than 1e−10. For each OTU, we first retained matches with the highest percentage identity, longest query cover, and lowest e‐value, and then assigned the taxonomy based on the highest number of BLAST matches (see Table S4 for final BLAST results and taxonomic assignment). We combined OTUs that matched the same species and assigned a genus‐ or family‐level classification to OTUs that had multiple species or genera matches with equal numbers of BLAST matches (e.g., 12S OTU_566: Macaca sp. for Macaca arctoides [1 BLAST match], Macaca fascicularis [1 BLAST match] and Macaca mulatta [1 BLAST match]; 16S OTU_300: Family Cercopithecidae for Mandrillus leucophaeus [1 BLAST match], Piliocolobus tephrosceles [1 BLAST match] and Trachypithecus francoisi [1 BLAST match]). We summed the read counts for each individual beetle across its DNA extracts (i.e., for Catharsius cf. renaudpauliani individuals) and PCR replicates.

For our network analyses, we retained only native vertebrate species. We excluded human detections as they could be contaminations or real signals, but were not relevant to the study objectives. We also excluded exotic species (e.g., Mandrillus leucophaeus , Potamochoerus porcus and exotic fish species) that may originate from the Singapore Zoo (adjacent to the CCNR) or introduced through the pet trade and recreational fishing, as well as domestic species (e.g., dog) and livestock used for human consumption (e.g., Bos taurus and Ovis aries ) (see Table S3 for all vertebrate detections). We found less than 10 detections across most exotic species, with the exception of Macaca mulatta (26 detections; all co‐detected with long‐tailed macaque Macaca fascicularis ) and the Java mouse‐deer ( Tragulus javanicus ; 13 detections). As the Java mousedeer is morphologically indistinct from the lesser mousedeer (Low et al. 2009), the sequences in Genbank are potential misidentifications (Genbank Accession IDs: AY121991.1, LN873134.1) and are likely the lesser mouse‐deer ( Tragulus kanchil ), which is native to Singapore. Hence, we treated Java mouse‐deer detections as the lesser mouse‐deer and included it in our subsequent analyses. Based on the vertebrate identifications from the 12S and 16S markers, we constructed a presence–absence matrix with each unique sample as a row, vertebrate species as columns and cell values as presence (1; either 12S or 16S read counts > 0) or absence (0; both 12S and 16S read count = 0). For each vertebrate species, we noted if it was detected in both 12S and 16S, only in 12S or only in 16S primer sets.

Statistical Analysis

To determine whether gut fullness and dung beetle genera were potential indicators of vertebrate richness, we fitted a generalised linear mixed model (GLMM) using a Poisson error distribution with vertebrate richness as a response variable, site as a random effect, and dung beetle genera, gut fullness and their interaction as main effects. We excluded Parascatonomus sp. from the analysis as its low sample size (20 individuals as compared to 306 Onthophagus, 117 Catharsius and 104 Paragymnopleurus beetles) would result in a skewed and unbalanced analysis if included.

To determine differences in dung beetle‐vertebrate interactions across vertical strata, we first calculated the sampling completeness and plotted species accumulation curves (Figure S4) of dung beetle and vertebrate communities found in ground and canopy traps using the iNext package (Hsieh et al. 2019). We then constructed dung beetle‐vertebrate trophic networks for traps where direct interactions between dung beetle and vertebrate species were recorded (i.e., positive detections of vertebrate species in the dung beetle gut samples). Each network was based on a matrix of dung beetle species × vertebrate species, with the number of beetle individuals containing the DNA of each vertebrate species as cell entries. To determine differences in structure among the networks, we calculated six network‐level metrics used in previous studies (e.g., Chiew et al. 2022; Ong et al. 2020; Tylianakis et al. 2007) using the bipartite package (Dormann et al. 2008):

Nestedness (0 to 100; 0: perfect nestedness): Low nestedness indicates the presence of more exclusive interactions between dung beetle and vertebrate species; high nestedness indicates that specialist interactions between dung beetle and vertebrate species are nested within generalist interactions.

Nestedness (0 to 100; 0: perfect nestedness): Low nestedness indicates the presence of more exclusive interactions between dung beetle and vertebrate species; high nestedness indicates that specialist interactions between dung beetle and vertebrate species are nested within generalist interactions.

Specialisation (H2′; 0 to 1; 0: No specialisation): Low specialisation indicates a lower frequency of specialist interactions; High specialisation indicates a higher frequency of specialist interactions.

Specialisation (H2′; 0 to 1; 0: No specialisation): Low specialisation indicates a lower frequency of specialist interactions; High specialisation indicates a higher frequency of specialist interactions.

Interaction evenness (0 to 1; 0: No interaction evenness): Low interaction evenness indicates a high variation in interaction frequencies among dung beetle and vertebrate species; High interaction evenness indicates similar interaction frequencies among dung beetle and vertebrate species.

Interaction evenness (0 to 1; 0: No interaction evenness): Low interaction evenness indicates a high variation in interaction frequencies among dung beetle and vertebrate species; High interaction evenness indicates similar interaction frequencies among dung beetle and vertebrate species.

Links per species (L/S): Mean number of interactions across the total number of dung beetle and vertebrate species observed in a network. LS < 1 indicates fewer interactions than the total number of observed species (i.e., simpler network structure). LS > 1 indicates more interactions than the total number of observed species (i.e., more complex network structure).

Links per species (L/S): Mean number of interactions across the total number of dung beetle and vertebrate species observed in a network. LS < 1 indicates fewer interactions than the total number of observed species (i.e., simpler network structure). LS > 1 indicates more interactions than the total number of observed species (i.e., more complex network structure).

Generality: Low generality indicates that each dung beetle species interacts with a low number of vertebrate species; high generality indicates that each dung beetle species interacts with multiple vertebrate species.

Generality: Low generality indicates that each dung beetle species interacts with a low number of vertebrate species; high generality indicates that each dung beetle species interacts with multiple vertebrate species.

Functionality (a version of network vulnerability): Low functionality indicates that each vertebrate species supports a low number of dung beetle species; high functionality indicates that each vertebrate species supports multiple dung beetle species.

Functionality (a version of network vulnerability): Low functionality indicates that each vertebrate species supports a low number of dung beetle species; high functionality indicates that each vertebrate species supports multiple dung beetle species.

Due to possible co‐extinctions of large forest vertebrates and specialist dung beetle species in nineteenth‐century Singapore, combined with the high trophic generalism in tropical dung beetles (Chiew et al. 2022; Ong et al. 2020; Frank et al. 2018), we predicted high nestedness in all dung beetle‐vertebrate networks. Furthermore, we expected that dung beetle and vertebrate diversity would decrease in more disturbed sites, resulting in simplified networks with lower interaction evenness and functionality, fewer links between species, and higher nestedness and generality.

We were unable to derive these network metrics for 47 canopy (out of 50) and 23 ground (out of 50) traps because a minimum of two species for each trophic level (i.e., two dung beetle and two vertebrate species) is needed for the calculations. These traps were either empty, captured only a single dung beetle, or only detected a single vertebrate species. To address this, we combined the data from the ground and canopy traps in each site to construct site‐level dung beetle‐vertebrate networks. We were able to calculate the six metrics for each site‐level network for 32 out of 50 sites. To investigate how these network metrics were associated with changes in forest structural variables, we fitted linear models (LM) with each metric as a response variable and all four forest structural variables as main effects.

To compare between canopy and ground traps, we combined 24 canopy traps that had positive vertebrate detections to construct an overall canopy network comprising of five dung beetle and 13 vertebrate species. Likewise, we combined 41 ground traps that had positive vertebrate detections to construct an overall ground network comprising of eight dung beetle and 18 vertebrate species. We calculated the six network metrics and made qualitative comparisons between the two networks. For all site‐level networks, and the single ground and canopy networks, we tested the significance of their network metrics by comparing the metrics of the observed networks against 1000 randomised networks generated through null models using Patefield's algorithm (Blüthgen et al. 2006). This comparison between observed and null networks allowed us to determine if the observed network structures and metrics were influenced by actual interactions between species (i.e., significant differences between observed and null) or could have been produced by stochastic processes (i.e., no significant difference between observed and null). We performed all statistical analyses using R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team 2025).

Results

Window of DNA Detection From Gut Contents of Onthophagus Dung Beetles

We performed a controlled feeding experiment on 144 individual Onthophagus beetles, and used quantitative PCR (qPCR) to determine the amount of elephant DNA that could be detected from the beetles' gut contents across eight time points (0 min to 24 h post feeding). We successfully amplified DNA from beetles across all eight time points. Our qPCR reactions had a 97.4% efficiency and an R 2 value of 0.905 (Figure S2), indicating good amplification efficiency and precision. Based on the BLAST results, we detected elephant DNA in 122 out of 144 samples. Due to possible contamination, two samples that were matched with Bos taurus and Homo sapiens were omitted from our statistical analyses. No BLAST hits were assigned to 20 samples that had high (indicating minimal amounts of the target DNA) or undetected qPCR threshold cycle (CQ) values. Out of these 20 samples, 17 were of individuals with empty or empty (with specks) guts, and three were of individuals with partially full or full guts.

We further examined the effects of time post‐feeding, gut fullness and storage conditions on DNA copy number. Beetles with full guts had a higher DNA copy number than beetles with partially full or empty guts (F 3,116 = 37.704, p < 0.001, Figure 1A). Beetles that were stored in ethanol at −80°C had a higher DNA copy number than beetles that were stored only in ethanol (F 1,116 = 4.2, p = 0.042, Figure 1B). While there was no significant effect of time post‐feeding on DNA copy number (F 7,116 = 1.083, p = 0.379, Figure 1C), we found an increased proportion of beetles with empty guts after the 3 h time point (Figure 1D). We detected elephant DNA in 66 out of 68 beetles with full guts (97.1%) and in 16 out of 29 beetles with empty guts (55.2%). We also detected elephant DNA in the majority of beetles after 24 h, with only an approximately 1.5‐fold reduction of amplified DNA in guts between the first and last time point (elephant DNA detected in 17 out of 18 beetles (94.4%) from the first (0 m) time point, and in 14 out of 18 beetles (77.8%) from the last (24 h) time point).

Vertebrate Detections From Wild‐Caught Dung Beetles

Using 50 ground and 50 canopy human dung‐baited live pitfall traps deployed across the secondary forests of the Central Catchment Nature Reserve in Singapore, we collected and sequenced the gut contents of 547 beetles belonging to 14 dung beetle species across four genera (Table S1). Information on raw sequencing reads, post‐bioinformatic filtering, quality control checks and taxonomic assignments are presented in Tables S2–S4. From the dung beetle gut contents, we detected 18 native vertebrate species, comprising two amphibian species, two bird species, and 14 mammal species (Figure 2). Of the 18 species, 13 species were matched in both 16S and 12S barcodes, indicating a high taxonomic consistency between both primer sets. Four species were only detected in 16S barcodes, and one species only in 12S barcodes. We successfully detected rare native mammals, including the Sunda flying lemur ( Galeopterus variegatus ), Sunda slow loris ( Nycticebus coucang insularis ), and Sunda pangolin ( Manis javanica ). Of the 18 vertebrate species, 12 species were detected from both ground and canopy traps. This included arboreal species, such as the Sunda flying lemur, plantain squirrel ( Callosciurus notatus ) and Asian palm civet ( Paradoxurus hermaphroditus ), and ground species, such as the wild boar ( Sus scrofa ). The Sunda slow loris was only detected from canopy beetles, while the two amphibian species (detected only in Catharsius cf. renaudpauliani beetles), brahminy kite ( Haliastur indus ), lesser short‐nosed fruit bat ( Cynopterus brachyotis ), and sambar deer ( Rusa unicolor ) were only detected from ground beetles (Figure 2).

Field survey: 18 native vertebrate species detected from dung beetle gut contents. Number of detections corresponds to the number of dung beetle individuals where each vertebrate species was detected.

Of the 547 beetles caught, 97 (17.7%) had full guts, of which 58 (59.8% of 97 beetles) had positive detections of vertebrate DNA. Among the 84 beetles (15.4%) with empty guts, vertebrate DNA was detected in 37 beetles (44% of 84 beetles). Among the four dung beetle genera sampled, we detected 13 vertebrate species from Catharsius beetles, 13 species from Onthophagus beetles, 10 species from Paragymnopleurus beetles and five species from Parascatonomus beetles. For vertebrate detections within each individual beetle, we found a significant interaction between dung beetle genera and gut fullness on vertebrate richness (genera gut fullness: χ 2 6,514 = 13.7, p = 0.033; variance explained by site: 4.34%). Individual Onthophagus and Catharsius beetles with full guts had higher vertebrate richness, while individual Paragymnopleurus beetles had low vertebrate richness across all gut fullness categories (Figure 1E).

Quantitative Dung Beetle‐Vertebrate Trophic Networks

Across the 50 sites, we constructed 32 site‐level (i.e., combined canopy and ground) quantitative dung beetle‐vertebrate trophic networks (Figure 3; see Appendix S3 for interactive versions of these networks) and generated network metrics (i.e., nestedness, specialisation, interaction evenness, links per species, generality and functionality for each network) (see Section 2 for details of the metrics). Across all site‐level networks, we did not find a significant difference from the null models for all network metrics, suggesting little differences among the networks across the forest area (Table S5). Upon visual inspection, network complexity was influenced by the number of detected vertebrate species, where there were more dung beetle‐vertebrate interactions in sites with higher detected vertebrate richness (Figure 3). Contrary to expectations, there were no clear effects of forest structure on all network metrics (see statistical results for each network metric in Table S6).

Discussion

Documenting and monitoring invertebrates and their interactions with other living organisms is a challenging task, especially in understudied and hyper‐diverse tropical regions, such as Southeast Asia (Luke et al. 2023; Slade and Ong 2023). The importance of using novel technologies and methods has been highlighted in several recent papers and special issues (Blackman et al. 2024; Lamarre et al. 2020; Sheard et al. 2024; van Klink et al. 2024). In particular, molecular approaches hold promise as they could circumvent the constraints associated with using conventional means to document species interactions over large spatial and temporal scales (Ma et al. 2018; Derocles et al. 2018; Cagnolo 2018). Dung beetles feed on vertebrate dung and can be sampled cost‐effectively, making them ideal sources for vertebrate DNA and studies that examine the impact of environmental changes on dung beetle‐vertebrate trophic networks (Drinkwater, Williamson, et al. 2021; Nichols and Gardner 2011; Frank et al. 2018). Our study presents novel quantitative dung beetle‐vertebrate trophic networks across a large spatial scale and uniquely documents direct interactions between dung beetles and vertebrates in both ground and canopy communities in a Southeast Asian tropical forest. To aid future research, we present refined protocols for the extraction and quantification of vertebrate DNA from dung beetle guts and a recommended window of DNA detection from small‐sized Onthophagus beetles, which is the most abundant and diverse genus in this region.

Window of DNA Detection From Gut Contents of Onthophagus Dung Beetles

Before constructing interaction networks, it is important to determine how long vertebrate DNA remains detectable from dung beetle gut contents after a feeding event (Drinkwater, Williamson, et al. 2021). Our study revealed that gut fullness was strongly associated with time post‐feeding and significantly influenced the detection of DNA in the beetle gut contents. From our feeding trial, we determined that the highest probability of vertebrate detection is within 3 h of feeding, which corresponds to a high proportion of beetles with full guts and positive detections of elephant DNA (full guts: 97.1%; empty guts: 55.2%). However, in our field samples, the difference in positive detections of vertebrate DNA between beetles with full and empty guts was less pronounced (full guts: 59.8%; empty: 45.2%). Instead, the gut fullness of wild‐caught dung beetles was associated with the number of detected vertebrate species, where beetles with fuller guts generally had higher vertebrate richness. Notably, wild‐caught Onthophagus beetles with full guts exhibited more vertebrate species detections than those with partially full or empty guts.

Our results also suggest that a 24‐h sampling period is sufficient for obtaining a quick estimate of both dung beetle and vertebrate diversity. We were successful in amplifying DNA from beetles sampled 24 h post‐feeding in our feeding experiment (77.8% of 18 beetles) and managed to detect vertebrate DNA from beetles with guts that looked visually empty (feeding experiment: 55.2% of 29 beetles; field surveys: 45.2% of 84 beetles). However, as the live traps were retrieved after 24 h, this could have resulted in increased time for digestion and therefore fewer dung beetles with full guts (17.7% vs. empty guts: 42.5%) and lower vertebrate detections. In addition, the beetles that were attracted to our dung‐baited traps were likely searching for food and were thus more likely to have empty guts. Therefore, to increase the success of detecting higher numbers of vertebrate species, we recommend increasing the frequency of collections (e.g., collections depending on logistics and availability of resources). Based on the movement ecology and dispersal distances of tropical dung beetles (Gray et al. 2022), having shorter collection frequencies would also increase the resolution and likelihood of vertebrate presence around the same vicinity where each dung beetle was collected.

To increase the scalability and cost‐effectiveness of this approach, we suggest determining the gut fullness of each beetle before DNA extraction and prioritising the sequencing of beetles with full guts, using homemade digestion buffers (compared to Qiagen DNA extraction kits), and/or pooling gut contents of all beetles in each trap. Additionally, including complementary sampling methods, such as flight interception traps, would help to increase the number of vertebrate species detected as they sample different dung beetle communities with different feeding preferences (e.g., non‐dung feeding). Another way to detect more taxa, especially rare species with lower abundances, is to increase the sequencing depth (Smith and Peay 2014). For example, we were unable to reliably derive network metrics for 47 out of 50 canopy traps due to the lower abundance of canopy‐active dung beetles and the lower number of vertebrate species detected.

Vertebrate Communities Detected From Wild‐Caught Dung Beetles

The use of DNA metabarcoding allowed us to detect rare and elusive species in both ground and canopy communities that are often difficult to survey using conventional methods, such as camera trapping and observational surveys (Derocles et al. 2018; Drinkwater, Williamson, et al. 2021; Drinkwater, Jucker, et al. 2021). Here, we detected rare species, such as the Sunda slow loris and Sunda pangolin and dung beetle interactions with arboreal species (e.g., lesser short‐nosed fruit bat, plantain squirrel and brahminy kite) that are difficult to establish through dung attraction experiments. Interestingly, a recent iDNA study in Singapore that used fly faecal samples detected three native reptile, three avian and two mammal species, including the elusive Raffles' banded langur, which were not detected in our study (Srivathsan et al. 2023). Conversely, our study detected three amphibian, one avian, and five mammal species that were not detected in the fly faecal samples. These findings highlight the potential value of using multiple iDNA samplers with different feeding ecologies (e.g., dung beetles and carrion flies) to obtain a more comprehensive assessment of vertebrate communities (Massey et al. 2022).

We also recommend that future studies use multiple primer sets to minimise biases associated with the use of a single primer and to improve our coverage of detected vertebrate species (Alberdi et al. 2018; Zhu and Iwasaki 2023). By using two primer sets, our approach showed that dung beetles do not just feed on mammal dung but a wider range of vertebrate groups, including the first detection of two amphibian species in the large nocturnal tunneller, Catharsius cf. renaudpauliani. Dung beetles use vertebrate dung for both feeding and breeding, and are known to use resources other than mammal dung (e.g., reptile dung: Ong et al. 2020; Lopes et al. 2023; carrion: Ueda et al. 2015). While this study is one of the first to document the direct feeding preferences of dung beetles, it is unable to elucidate whether the beetles would use the same dung types for breeding. Previous work suggests that the beetles might be more specific when choosing dung for breeding, as the brood ball needs to contain enough high‐quality food to rear the larvae (Raine et al. 2018). Therefore, it is unlikely that large‐bodied Catharsius beetles would be able to use amphibian dung for breeding, suggesting that they are opportunistically feeding on these non‐mammal dung types. We are also unable to rule out the possibility of DNA detected from other sources, for example, the surrounding environment (e.g., soil), via carrion feeding (which could also explain the detections of exotic fish species), or through secondary DNA detections in the dung of carnivorous or omnivorous vertebrates that feed on other vertebrate species (e.g., amphibians, reptiles and birds).

While the diversity of canopy dung beetle communities is lower than that of ground dung beetle communities (also shown in Abdul Rahman et al. 2021), our findings revealed a lack of vertical stratification of dung resources as both communities displayed similar feeding preferences and utilised dung derived from both ground‐dwelling and canopy vertebrates. For instance, the wild boar and Sunda flying lemur were detected in both canopy and ground beetles. This pattern may reflect the limited quantities of dung in the canopy, especially in Singapore's forests, which are largely comprised of low and degraded forests that have regrown in areas cleared for timber and plantations in the 19th century (Yee et al. 2016; Corlett 1992). Furthermore, these forest canopies are largely dominated by small to medium‐sized vertebrates such as rodents and long‐tailed macaques, which also frequently come down to the ground (Corlett 1992; Sha 2013).

Quantitative Dung Beetle‐Vertebrate Networks

Our networks reveal true feeding links between dung beetles and vertebrate species across a large spatial scale. These networks displayed high nestedness and low specialisation, as consistent with previous studies (Chiew et al. 2022; Ong et al. 2020; Frank et al. 2018). We found that interactions between dung beetle and vertebrate communities were vertically stratified. Moreover, the more structurally complex ground networks are likely more resilient and less sensitive to disturbances than canopy networks. As our study was conducted within a single forest area that had similar vegetation structures (Yee et al. 2016; Corlett 1992), our results revealed no clear effects of forest structural variables on most network metrics. This is similar to findings from dung beetle‐mammal networks in the tropical forests of Sabah, Malaysia, where network structure and metrics were largely similar between primary and disturbed forests, and only became simplified in highly fragmented forests and oil palm plantations (Chiew et al. 2022).

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the effectiveness of molecular approaches and the use of dung beetles to document and monitor dung beetle‐vertebrate interactions. Our findings demonstrate that dung beetles are ideal samplers of vertebrate DNA. Importantly, we obtained positive detections within a 24‐h sampling timeframe and even from beetles with visually empty guts. Our approach can also be used in complement with other iDNA samplers with different feeding ecologies (e.g., carrion flies, leeches; see Drinkwater, Williamson, et al. 2021; Massey et al. 2022) and conventional methods (e.g., camera trapping) to obtain a comprehensive assessment of vertebrate diversity, especially for understudied locations. Our optimised molecular techniques contribute towards the development of new methods for comprehensive biodiversity assessments and ecological network studies that are urgently needed for hyper‐diverse yet understudied locations that are undergoing rapid environmental change.
