Multi-modal data fusion and deep reinforcement learning for dynamic resource scheduling in intelligent manufacturing systems under variable market demand
Authors: Xiaobo Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs intelligent manufacturing systems increasingly pursue mass customization and rapid market responsiveness, efficient resource allocation has become a critical determinant of production competitiveness. Dynamic resource scheduling in such systems involves complex challenges stemming from highly variable market demand and complex production conditions. This study extends a multi-modal data fusion approach that incorporates deep learning methods to solve dynamic resource scheduling with variable demand scenarios. The approach involves the fusion of sensor, production, and market data using intermediate fusion methods, the adoption of an LSTM with attention model to predict demand, and Double Deep Q-Network (DDQN) methods to control resource scheduling decisions. Simulation outcomes show that the system achieves 82.6% resource utilization and 89.1% on-time delivery performance in nominal scenarios; however, it performs poorly during peak hours, leading to severe delays accounting for 12.8% of total deliveries. The multi-modal fusion approach outperforms single-source methods by 9.6% in prediction accuracy but exhibits limitations in cross-industry generalization (67.9% efficiency retention) and computational efficiency (18–35 min for large-scale scenarios). These findings provide realistic insights into the practical deployment of AI-driven scheduling systems in manufacturing environments.Keywords: Intelligent manufacturing, Dynamic scheduling, Multi-modal data fusion, Deep reinforcement learning, Demand forecasting, DDQNSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Intelligent manufacturing systems represent the paradigm shift that impacts current manufacturing models directly because resource allocation efficiency translates into competitiveness and market responsiveness1. However, the increased complexity introduced by modern manufacturing environments is due to several simultaneous variables: different equipment setups, varied product requirements, uncertain processing timescales, and changing job arrival rates. This is because a static approach to manufacturing system planning is no longer effective when reacting to events occurring in real time, such as machine failure, insertion of urgent orders, and shortages of material supply2. This trend becomes even more critical with the current trend that requires mass customization and single-piece flow manufacturing.

This situation of demand volatility makes resource scheduling a challenge, especially in industries characterized by seasonality, promotions, and rapidly changing consumer behaviors3. Conventional forecasting methods, such as moving averages and simple exponential smoothing, are inadequate in managing demand non-linearities and market shifts. These forecasting limitations lead to either excess inventory or stock shortages. Semiconductor industries face additional complexity from shorter product life cycles and growing customer demands for faster delivery4. Challenges in global supply chain networks have underlined the need for manufacturing systems to be more adaptive. Thus, there is a need for more sophisticated resource scheduling models that integrate demands with current production floor conditions to enable more adaptive resource allocation5.

Multi-modal data fusion has emerged as a fundamental approach for improving decision-making across diverse domains by integrating heterogeneous information sources. Baltrusaitis et al.6 established a comprehensive taxonomy of multimodal machine learning encompassing representation, alignment, translation, fusion, and co-learning challenges. Lahat et al.7 provided foundational analysis of fusion methods, theoretical prospects, and open challenges across signal processing domains. Gao et al.8 further reviewed deep learning architectures for multimodal data fusion, categorizing approaches by network structure and fusion strategy. In manufacturing contexts, multi-modal data fusion has found its application to improve the quality of decision-making in manufacturing systems by integrating various sources of information9. Modern smart factories produce large amounts of data from multiple sources of information: process sensors providing information about temperature and vibration; Manufacturing Execution Systems (MES) tracking production and inventory; and Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems handling orders and deliveries of products10. However, integrating these multiple sources of data faces various technical challenges with respect to temporal synchronization, dimensional heterogeneity, and semantic differences11. Traditional fusion approaches using simple feature concatenation can fail to model modality-specific patterns, whereas late fusion approaches can disregard meaningful inter-modal relationships12. Advanced fusion strategies based on deep learning models can potentially tackle optimal representation learning for different modalities, but application of these models to dynamic scheduling of manufacturing remains limited13.

Deep learning techniques have brought about a paradigm shift in manufacturing research, with applications extending from visual quality inspection using object detection networks14 to production scheduling through policy learning, as opposed to mathematically modeling complex system behavior15. The foundational Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm demonstrated human-level control in high-dimensional environments through experience replay and target networks16. Van Hasselt et al.17 subsequently proposed the Double DQN algorithm to address overestimation bias inherent in standard Q-learning, decoupling action selection from value estimation. Wang et al.18 introduced the dueling network architecture that decomposes Q-values into state value and action advantage streams, which forms the basis of the network design adopted in this study. Beyond evolutionary methods, hybrid approaches combining metaheuristics with tree search have also shown promise in multi-machine scheduling under complex production constraints19.Building on these foundational advances, deep reinforcement learning algorithms have proved effective in dealing with combinatorial optimization scenarios with large state-action spaces20. These algorithms produce policies by iteratively interacting with simulation environments and accumulating improvements through rewards21. Some possess capabilities to represent graph networks naturally found in job shop scheduling22. Attention mechanisms enable models to focus on prominent temporal patterns in past demand series23. Nevertheless, important gaps remain, including inadequate integration of demand prediction with scheduling optimization, insufficient focus on multi-modal data fusion for DRL models, and insufficient evaluation of system robustness under real-world operational constraints.

To address these gaps, this study develops a closed-loop pipeline that feeds multi-modal fusion outputs and demand predictions directly into the scheduling agent, and evaluates the integrated system under degraded data, out-of-distribution disruptions, and cross-industry transfer conditions.

In this study, an integrated framework for dynamic resource scheduling has been developed to address these gaps. Rather than proposing novel individual algorithms, the principal contribution lies at the system architecture level and the comprehensiveness of empirical evaluation. Specifically, the contributions are threefold: (1) a system-level integration architecture coupling multi-modal data fusion, demand prediction, and scheduling optimization into a closed-loop pipeline, enabling the DDQN scheduling agent to proactively respond to predicted demand changes rather than reacting to observed disruptions; (2) a systematic empirical evaluation assessing practical deployment concerns including data incompleteness robustness, out-of-distribution disruption response, cross-industry generalization, and computational scalability; and (3) transparent reporting of system limitations, including the 78.3% fusion accuracy ceiling, 12.8% severe delay rate under peak demand, and 67.9% cross-industry efficiency retention. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the methodology. Section 3 presents experimental results. Section 4 discusses findings, implications, and limitations. Section 5 concludes with a summary and future directions.

Methods

Problem formulation and system architecture

The problem of dynamic resource scheduling is modeled as a sequential decision-making process, with decisions taken at certain time points. The scheduler allocates available resources (machines, operators, tools) to waiting tasks, considering conflicting objectives like makespan, tardiness, energy, and resource usage. The problem is NP-hard due to the exponential search space generated by the combined action space of tasks, sequences, and resource allocation for hundreds of simultaneously executed tasks. The system architecture consists of four functional layers as shown in Fig. 1.

Four-layer system architecture for multi-modal data-driven dynamic scheduling.

The data acquisition layer aggregates diverse data streams such as sensor inputs from equipment for temperature, vibration, and power usage at 500 Hz; MES databases for work-in-progress updates every minute; ERP systems for real-time order changes; and external market data providing daily demand forecasts. This data is processed for temporal synchronization to correlate points with different sampling rates. Discrepancies between high-speed sensor data and low-speed business system updates are resolved using buffer systems.

Multi-modal data fusion framework

The fusion framework supports intermediate-level integration using modality-specific feature extractors and learned fusion functions24. Sensor data organized as spatial-temporal tensors (height × width × time × channels) passes through three-layer convolutional networks extracting local patterns: 3 × 3 kernels in the first layer capture basic temporal correlations; 5 × 5 kernels in the second layer aggregate spatial relationships; final 1 × 1 convolutions reduce dimensionality while preserving learned features. Production sequences are expressed as variable-length time series and fed into bidirectional LSTM networks with 128 hidden units per way, allowing the network to consider both past and future for current state representation.

Production state classification is formulated as a five-class problem: normal operation, machine degradation, material shortage, quality deviation, and demand surge, defined from operational logs and validated with domain experts. Classification accuracy is measured as the proportion of correctly identified states across all decision cycles. Misclassification consequences vary by error type: confusing normal operation with machine degradation triggers unnecessary preventive actions, while misclassifying material shortage as normal operation directs jobs to unavailable resources, potentially causing cascading delays. Attention-based fusion combines the feature vectors specific to individual modalities based on attention weights, reflecting the learned relative importance. The mechanism computes attention scores by comparing query vectors (derived from concatenated modality features) against key vectors through scaled dot-product operations. Softmax normalization makes the attention weights sum up to a unit over the respective modalities, making it easier to interpret the relative information contribution from each modality. The value vectors, weighted by these attention weights, get aggregated to form joint feature representations, which can be leveraged by the next stages of making predictions and decision-making.

Missing data are imputed using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE), which estimates missing values by modeling conditional distributions from observed data25. For continuous sensor readings, the missing values are predicted using the random forest regressors based on temporally adjacent readings and other sensor channels. Categorical variables related to production status are imputed via logistic regression, based on job characteristics, machine conditions, and completion histories. Five simultaneous imputations aggregate predictions, while variability among them provides the basis for uncertainty estimates. Sensitivity analysis confirmed that imputation quality degrades substantially beyond 20% missing data, motivating the focus on ≤ 10% loss scenarios in performance evaluations.

Demand fluctuation prediction model

The demand forecasting module employs an LSTM architecture with a multi-head attention mechanism to handle complex order arrival patterns26. Three LSTM layers with 128, 128, and 64 hidden units respectively represent the previous 24 months of demand history hierarchically. The configuration was determined through grid search over candidate sizes (Table 1). The smaller configuration (64-64-32) achieved 9.4% validation MAE, only marginally better than standard LSTM without attention (9.7%), indicating insufficient capacity for the attention mechanism. The larger configuration (256-256-128) reduced MAE to 7.9% but increased training time by 68% and exhibited a wider training-validation gap (4.9% vs. 7.9%), suggesting overfitting on the limited dataset. The selected 128-128-64 architecture achieved 8.2% MAE with a moderate training-validation gap (5.8% vs. 8.2%), balancing capacity and generalization. Dropout regularization (rate = 0.2) prevents overfitting on the training dataset, an important aspect when historic data are limited, as indicated in manufacturing.

Multi-head attention operates on LSTM output sequences through four parallel attention heads, each learning distinct temporal dependency patterns27. The outputs from the four attention heads are combined before a linear transformation projecting the outputs back down to 64-dimensional space. The attention mechanism allows the model to focus on relevant periods when predicting future demand. For instance, it emphasizes past demand in corresponding periods of previous years for seasonal products. Visualizing attention weights improves interpretability by indicating the periods that most impact predictions.

Training optimized mean absolute error loss through Adam optimizer with learning rate 0.001, weight decay 1e-5, and gradient clipping at norm 1.0 to prevent exploding gradients in recurrent networks. A mini-batch size of 64 balanced computation and gradient estimate variance. Early stopping prevented overfitting by monitoring validation metrics for 50 epochs. The model with the lowest validation error was chosen as final, not the one at the end of training.

Deep reinforcement learning-based scheduling

The DDQN learning algorithm acquires scheduling policies through interactive trials with a discrete-event simulation environment, which represents the manufacturing system28. State representation encodes comprehensive system status: individual machine availability (idle, busy, maintenance); job queue characteristics (count, urgency, resource requirements); current production progress (completion percentages, slack time); predicted demand from the forecasting module. This 128-dimensional state vector undergoes normalization to zero mean and unit variance, improving training stability. The dimensionality was determined through ablation experiments comparing 64, 128, 192, and 256-dimensional representations (Table 2). The 64-dimensional vector achieved only 79.3% resource utilization due to insufficient encoding of job queue characteristics and machine interdependencies. Increasing to 128 dimensions yielded substantial improvement (82.6% utilization, 89.1% on-time delivery). The 192-dimensional representation produced marginal gains (83.0% utilization) at approximately 50% longer training time. The 256-dimensional vector exhibited slight degradation (82.1% utilization, 88.3% on-time delivery) with 1,800 convergence episodes, attributable to overfitting given limited training scenario diversity. The 128-dimensional configuration was selected as the optimal trade-off between capacity and efficiency.

The DDQN uses a dueling network structure to learn state value estimates and action advantage estimates, as shown in Fig. 2. Two fully connected layers (256 units each with ReLU activation) extract shared features from state vectors. The layer width was selected through experiments comparing 128, 256, and 512 units per layer (Table 3). The 128-unit configuration achieved lower converged reward (82.4 vs. 114.7 for 256 units, on the scale shown in Fig. 2) and 80.1% resource utilization, indicating insufficient capacity for complex scheduling decisions. The 512-unit configuration reached comparable reward (108.3) but required approximately 1,650 episodes for convergence versus 1,200, with 43% longer per-episode computation. The slight reward decrease for 512 units suggests mild overfitting consistent with limited training scenario diversity. The 256-unit architecture was therefore adopted as the best empirical trade-off for the given problem scale. The network then branches into value stream (128 units → scalar state value) and advantage stream (128 units → action advantage for each possible scheduling decision). Final Q-values combine value and advantage through aggregation formula: Q(s, a) = V(s) + A(s, a) - mean(A(s,·)), ensuring identifiable value and advantage components.

Action space encompasses discrete scheduling decisions: job-machine assignments, operation prioritization, and resource reallocation among competing tasks. To guarantee computational tractability, the action space is limited to 64 valid actions per state by incorporating domain knowledge heuristics that avoid suboptimal actions (e.g., assigning jobs to incompatible machines). Reward function combines multiple normalized objectives: r = -0.4×normalizedmakespan − 0.3×normalizedtardiness − 0.2×energyconsumption + 0.1×utilizationimprovement. Since makespan and total tardiness are episode-level quantities determined only upon completion of all jobs, per-step rewards use incremental approximations. At each decision step, the makespan component is computed as the change in the current maximum machine completion time caused by the latest job-machine assignment. The tardiness component accumulates the tardiness of jobs completed since the previous step, normalized by the number of active jobs. These step-level proxies provide directional learning signals during training; final episode-level metrics based on true makespan and total tardiness are used for policy evaluation and model selection. These weights were determined through sensitivity analysis comparing five weighting schemes (Table 4): equal weights (0.25/0.25/0.25/0.25), makespan-dominant (0.6/0.2/0.1/0.1), tardiness-dominant (0.2/0.6/0.1/0.1), energy-dominant (0.1/0.2/0.6/0.1), and the selected balanced scheme (0.4/0.3/0.2/0.1). Each scheme was evaluated over 30 independent training runs using the same simulation environment and hyperparameters.

Experience replay maintains a buffer of 50,000 state-action-reward-next_state tuples, randomly sampling mini-batches of 64 transitions during training to break temporal correlations. Target network updates every 100 training steps stabilize learning by providing consistent Q-value targets during Bellman equation optimization. The epsilon value in epsilon-greedy exploration decreases from 1.0 to 0.05 linearly over 1,600 episodes. The exploration-exploitation trade-off is achieved. Overestimation bias is reduced using the double Q-learning methodology. The online network is used for the selection of the action. The target network is used for the calculation of the value.

Experimental setup and evaluation metrics

Experiments utilized simulation environments implemented in Python 3.8 with TensorFlow 2.10 for deep learning components and SimPy for discrete-event manufacturing simulation. The simulation configured sensor data streams at 500 Hz, MES updates at one-minute intervals, and 24 months of historical demand data incorporating seasonal and promotional variations across four manufacturing scenarios. Computational hardware consisted of NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs (24GB memory) for neural network training and Intel Xeon processors for simulation execution. To evaluate out-of-distribution robustness, three structurally novel disruption scenarios were designed: supply-side disruption (simultaneous multi-supplier failure reducing material availability by 40%), constraint-level disruption (regulatory compliance change requiring production reconfiguration within 48 h), and resource-side disruption (30% workforce unavailability). Each disruption was injected during days 14–21, matching the original scenario. Model performance was monitored for 45 days to allow sufficient observation of recovery dynamics. Partial recovery was defined as return to within 3% points of pre-disruption MAE. Baseline algorithms were selected to represent three categories: Priority Dispatching Rules (PDR) as the traditional rule-based baseline, Genetic Algorithm (GA) and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) as established metaheuristics, and two DRL baselines—standard DQN without the double Q-learning mechanism to isolate overestimation bias reduction, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) as a representative policy gradient method. The DQN and PPO baselines used the same state representation, reward function, and training configuration as DDQN to ensure fair comparison. Similarly, the GA and PSO baselines incorporated the same LSTM-predicted demand values into their fitness evaluation functions, ensuring that all methods operated on identical information and that observed performance differences reflect scheduling decision quality rather than information asymmetry. To evaluate the performances of the proposed model, several indicators including resource utilization (productive time / total available time), on-time delivery rate (orders meeting deadline / total orders), Mean Absolute Error of demand forecasts, makespan, and total normalized energy consumption were used.

Results

Multi-modal data fusion performance analysis

To evaluate the effectiveness of the multi-modal data fusion framework, it was tested on three different fusion approaches to yield optimal ways of integrating disparate manufacturing data streams. As shown in Fig. 3, intermediate fusion achieved the highest five-class production state classification accuracy of 78.3%, correctly identifying operational conditions across normal operation, machine degradation, material shortage, quality deviation, and demand surge categories. This indicates a relative improvement of 9.6% over early fusion techniques (68.7%), with a further increase of 3.2% over the late fusion (75.1%). This can be largely due to intermediate fusion’s capacity for modality-specific feature extraction through individual CNN and LSTM encoders before fusion on a higher-level representation.

Performance comparison of multi-modal fusion strategies under different data completeness scenarios.

The availability of data affects fusion performance for all techniques. In a simulation that randomly deleted 10% of sensor data to mimic communication breakdowns and sensor failures, intermediate fusion accuracy reduced to 71.2%, dropping 7.1% points from the full data case. However, early fusion was more vulnerable to incomplete data, with accuracy decreased to 62.4%. The impact of incomplete data was exacerbated when simulating 20% losses. In this case, while intermediate fusion maintained 66.8%, early fusion accuracy decreased to 58.3%. Despite data incompleteness, the learned feature extractors in intermediate fusion partially compensated for the missing information.

Computational efficiency analysis shows that there is a trade-off between fusion quality and processing latency. Early fusion requires minimal overhead (1.2 s average processing time) as it simply concatenates raw inputs before feeding to downstream models. Late fusion demonstrates moderate computational demands (2.1 s) through parallel processing of individual modalities. Intermediate fusion incurs highest computational cost (2.8 s) due to separate feature extraction networks and subsequent fusion operations. In the application areas that require sub-second responses, a latency value of 2.8 s might be considered high, particularly when followed by subsequent steps of demand forecasting and scheduling optimization.

Temporal synchronization among data sources introduced an average latency of 2.3 s due to the buffering requirements for aligning high-frequency sensor data (500 Hz) with the lower MES updates (1 min intervals). This latency contributes to the total fusion latency in the system for the features being available after 5.1 s from the time data is acquired. System log analysis revealed that synchronization failures occurred in 3.7% of production cycles, predominantly during shift changes and equipment reconfiguration periods. Such failures triggered fallback to incomplete data processing, thereby degrading system reliability. To quantify the downstream impact of fusion misclassification, scheduling decisions made with ground-truth production states were compared against those based on fusion-predicted states. With perfect state information, the DDQN agent achieved 85.7% resource utilization and 93.4% on-time delivery, declining to 82.6% and 89.1% under the 78.3% fusion accuracy condition—losses of 3.1 and 4.3% points attributable to fusion errors. The most consequential misclassifications involved confusing material shortage with normal operation (34% of errors), directing jobs to resource-constrained machines. Misclassifications between machine degradation and quality deviation (28% of errors) had milder impact as both states trigger similar conservative responses. Under the degraded 71.2% accuracy condition (10% data loss), performance declined further to 79.4% utilization and 84.6% delivery, with the marginal impact per accuracy point increasing threefold, confirming non-linear error propagation through the scheduling pipeline.

Demand fluctuation prediction and system response

Demand prediction accuracy exhibited strong temporal dependence, with forecast errors increasing nonlinearly as prediction horizon extended. Figure 4 presents Mean Absolute Error (MAE) across different forecasting windows for LSTM with attention mechanism, standard LSTM, and ARIMA baseline. For one-day-ahead predictions, the attention-augmented LSTM achieved 8.2% MAE, outperforming standard LSTM (9.7%) and ARIMA (11.4%). Such short-term accuracy advantage is owing to the attention mechanism’s ability to identify meaningful historical patterns and filter out unnecessary noise in the latest demand series.

Demand prediction error across forecasting horizons for different models.

As prediction horizon expanded to three days, MAE for the proposed model increased to 12.4%, maintaining approximately 50% error reduction relative to ARIMA (16.8%). However, the performance gap narrowed considerably for medium-term forecasts. At fourteen-day horizon, the attention-LSTM model exhibited 23.6% MAE compared to 29.7% for ARIMA, representing only 6.1% point improvement. This implies diminishing marginal returns on attention models, particularly in longer prediction intervals where prime demand drivers (seasonal patterns, promotional events, macroeconomic factors) overshadow the short-term variability modeled by sequence-based neural networks.

The uncertainty quantification test using Monte Carlo Dropout showed considerable prediction variability in medium-term forecasts. The 95% confidence interval for a fourteen-day forecast spanned ± 18.4% of the mean demand value, indicating substantial uncertainty. This large margin of error limits the applicability of data to production planning, which involves holding costs and stockout risks. The sensitivity analysis revealed that abrupt demand changes beyond the training distribution reduced model accuracy by an additional 11–15% points, highlighting the model’s sensitivity to abrupt market changes.

The simulated supply chain disruption scenario (days 14–21 in Fig. 4) illustrates system vulnerability to exogenous shocks. The original disruption involved sudden rises in material prices and competitor production shutdowns. Although these specific patterns were absent from training data, their underlying structure—market-driven demand shifts—shares similarity with the training distribution. Attention-LSTM prediction error spiked to 25.3% compared to 18.7% pre-disruption baseline, while ARIMA reached 35.8%. Partial recovery (return to within 3% points of pre-disruption MAE) occurred within approximately 7 days after disruption onset, though full accuracy recovery required approximately three weeks.

To evaluate robustness against structurally novel disruptions, three additional out-of-distribution scenarios were tested: (1) simultaneous multi-supplier failure causing 40% raw material shortage; (2) abrupt regulatory compliance change requiring production line reconfiguration; and (3) workforce disruption with 30% labor unavailability. As shown in Table 5, prediction degradation was more severe under these novel types. Attention-LSTM MAE increased to 29.6%, 27.1%, and 32.8% for scenarios (1), (2), and (3) respectively. Partial recovery required 16–24 days from disruption onset, compared to 7 days for the original scenario. Full accuracy recovery was observed only for the regulatory change scenario (approximately 32 days); the remaining two did not achieve full recovery within the 45-day monitoring window (Table 5).

Dynamic resource scheduling effectiveness

The DDQN-based scheduling agent demonstrated measurable improvements across multiple performance dimensions under normal operating conditions. Figure 5 presents heatmap comparison across six evaluation metrics for DDQN, Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), and Priority Dispatching Rules (PDR). Resource utilization reached 82.6% with DDQN compared to 80.4% for standard DQN, 79.8% for PPO, 76.3% for GA, 74.8% for PSO, and 68.5% for PDR. All differences between DDQN and non-DRL baselines were statistically significant at p < 0.05 (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 30 independent runs). The difference between DDQN and standard DQN (2.2% points, p = 0.03) confirms the practical benefit of the double Q-learning mechanism in reducing overestimation bias for scheduling decisions. The difference between DDQN and PPO (2.8% points, p = 0.02) was also significant; PPO exhibited more stable training dynamics with lower inter-run variance but required approximately 1,500 episodes for convergence due to its on-policy nature, compared to 1,200 for DDQN. This 6.3% point advantage over GA translates to approximately 8% additional productive capacity from existing equipment, though substantial room for improvement remains given the 17.4% unutilized capacity.

Heatmap comparison of scheduling algorithms across six performance metrics.

On-time delivery performance achieved 89.1% for DDQN scheduling, compared to 86.7% for DQN, 85.9% for PPO, 83.5% for GA, 81.2% for PSO, and 77.8% for PDR. The narrower gap among DRL methods (2.4–3.2% points) compared to the gap between DRL and non-DRL methods (5.6–11.3% points) suggests that the primary performance advantage stems from the reinforcement learning paradigm itself, with the specific algorithm choice providing incremental improvement. Notably, PPO achieved a higher robustness score (75.2) than DQN (72.6), approaching DDQN (76.4), attributable to its clipped policy update mechanism that constrains policy shifts under novel states. However, the 10.9% late delivery rate under normal conditions indicates persistent scheduling challenges even with advanced optimization. Energy efficiency metrics, normalized to 100-point scale, scored 85.3 for DDQN versus 78.7 for GA, reflecting the multi-objective reward function’s explicit consideration of energy consumption alongside throughput and tardiness objectives.

Convergence speed emerged as a notable weakness of the DDQN approach, scoring only 72.3 on the normalized scale compared to PDR’s 88.6. The deep reinforcement learning algorithm required about 1,200 episodes for training to ensure steady performance, corresponding to 18–35 min of processing time based on scenario complexity. Comparatively, the genetic algorithm used 5–8 min for convergence, and decisions in the priority-based dynamic rescheduling method are instantaneous based on simple priorities. Where unexpected disturbances trigger dynamic scheduling, differences in reaction time significantly impact system flexibility.

High-load performance characteristics illustrate the limitations of practical implementation. Figure 6 highlights the simulation of scheduling performance on four production levels of escalating demand intensity. Under peak demand conditions, on-time delivery declined to 74.2% with severe delays (≥ 2 days) affecting 12.8% of orders. Paradoxically, average resource utilization dropped from 82.6% during normal operations to 79.3% at peak load, reflected by the increased rate of equipment switching and setup time for handling urgent demands through disrupted production schedules.

Order delivery performance and resource utilization under varying production loads.

Robustness analysis under machine breakdown scenarios demonstrated that DDQN scheduling recovered faster than traditional approaches. Following simulated equipment failure, the DDQN agent generated revised schedules achieving 85% of pre-failure throughput within 2.4 h, compared to 3.8 h for GA and 4.5 h for PDR. However, the absolute recovery time of 2.4 h continues to indicate significant lost productivity, particularly with premium products or timeliness orders. The importance of equipment redundancy and the maintenance plan has still not diminished with the inclusion of sophisticated rescheduling techniques. Sensitivity analysis of reward weights revealed pronounced trade-offs among scheduling objectives. The makespan-dominant scheme (0.6/0.2/0.1/0.1) achieved the highest resource utilization (83.4%) and makespan reduction (84.2) but on-time delivery declined to 84.7%. The tardiness-dominant scheme (0.2/0.6/0.1/0.1) achieved the highest on-time delivery (91.3%) but reduced resource utilization to 78.8% due to frequent production line switching. The energy-dominant scheme (0.1/0.2/0.6/0.1) yielded the best energy efficiency (88.9%) but degraded on-time delivery to 83.7%, only marginally above GA performance (83.5%). Equal weighting produced moderate performance without excelling in any metric. The selected scheme (0.4/0.3/0.2/0.1) did not achieve the best score on any single metric but was the only configuration maintaining competitive performance across all four objectives simultaneously.

Ablation analysis on state space dimensionality confirmed sensitivity to representation capacity. Reducing the state vector from 128 to 64 dimensions caused a 3.3% point drop in resource utilization and 4.9% point decline in on-time delivery, narrowing the advantage over GA to 3.0 and 0.7% points respectively. Expanding to 192 dimensions yielded only 0.4% point improvement (p = 0.23, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 30), while 256 dimensions showed slight degradation consistent with overfitting given limited training scenario diversity. The 128-dimensional design is therefore near-optimal for the tested configurations.

Model generalization and limitations

The results of cross-scenario transfer learning experiments showed significant variability in scheduling efficiency retention rates while transferring trained models from one manufacturing domain to another that are quite different. This was illustrated in Fig. 7. The within-scenario performances (diagonal elements) ranged from 91.5% to 94.2% and showed that even after training and testing in similar scenarios, models do not perform equally well due to operational domain variability.

Cross-scenario generalization performance heatmap for trained scheduling models.

Within similar industries, a degree of progress was achieved through transfers. An automotive assembly-trained model maintained 84.6% of its efficiency in machinery manufacturing, and this is probably due to similarities like discrete part processing and assembly constraints. However, inter-domain transfers between non-similar industries showed a lack of good generalization. An efficiency retention of 67.9% was achieved when an automotive assembly-trained model was applied to pharmaceutical manufacturing, where activities like batch processing and quality constraints are predominant.

Electronics production is faced with unique challenges characterized by small-batch, high-variety production dynamics. Even across all models considered, a degradation of efficiency of 15–23% was noted when used for electronics problems, regardless of their training data origin. There is a clear inability of the current architectures to aptly handle the schedule complexity of high-mix, low-volume facilities, shown through the action space of the DDQN agent, which is not fit for the fast product changeover activities seen in electronics production, characterized by high instability of product portfolios.

Data volume also introduced practical constraints. A minimum of 18 months of production data covering different operational conditions, such as seasonality of demand, machine downtime, and staffing levels, was inevitably required for effective model training. New plants, especially those that introduce a different manufacturing process, often do not have enough historical information. This leads to a cold-start challenge. Transfer learning could ease this to a certain extent. However, challenges of generalizability constrain this possibility.

Discussion

Before discussing specific findings, it is important to clarify the nature of the contribution presented in this work. This study does not propose modifications to the DDQN algorithm itself, nor does it introduce new learning mechanisms for multi-modal fusion or demand prediction. Each individual component—intermediate fusion, LSTM with attention, and DDQN—is based on established methods. The contribution lies in the system-level integration of these components into a unified scheduling framework and, critically, in the breadth and realism of the empirical evaluation. Most existing DRL-based scheduling studies evaluate performance under normal operating conditions using standard metrics such as makespan and resource utilization. In contrast, this study systematically examines conditions that are rarely addressed together in a single framework: degraded data quality, demand prediction uncertainty across multiple horizons, high-load and peak-demand scenarios, out-of-distribution disruptions, and cross-industry transfer. This evaluation-oriented contribution is intended to bridge the gap between algorithmic research and practical deployment assessment.The multi-modal data fusion brings about a distinct improvement in the efficacy of scheduling systems by improving situational awareness. Nevertheless, these improvements are highly dependent on the fusion approach used and its continued focus on data quality. The high precision of intermediate fusion is attributed to the hierarchical learning of its features through an approach that extracts features specific to each modality for fusion, taking into account both specific and inter-modal associations of data29. Convolutional layers identify spatial features among the sensor data structures effectively, while the LSTM networks identify the production sequences. Following this, the attention mechanism weighs the features based on relevance, reducing the effect of noise in the information streams.

The 78.3% classification accuracy under optimal conditions leaves considerable room for improvement relative to the reliability standards expected in production environments. The five-class error rate of 21.7% implies misclassification in approximately one out of every five decision cycles. The controlled comparison with ground-truth states reveals that these errors cost 3.1% points in resource utilization and 4.3% points in on-time delivery, establishing that the reported scheduling performance of 82.6% and 89.1% represents a measurable degradation from the 85.7% and 93.4% achievable with perfect state information. Importantly, not all misclassifications carry equal weight; confusion between material shortage and normal operation accounts for 34% of errors but disproportionately impacts scheduling quality by directing jobs to resource-constrained machines. Under 10% data loss conditions, scheduling performance declined to 79.4% utilization and 84.6% delivery, with the per-point sensitivity of utilization to accuracy loss increasing threefold (from 0.14 to 0.45), confirming non-linear error propagation. These findings raise serious concerns about deployability in industrial environments where intermittent sensor failures are common, and suggest that fusion accuracy improvement should be prioritized as the most effective lever for overall system performance gains30. Research aimed at implementing robust architectures with graceful performance degradation in situations of insufficient data is therefore of high priority.

The nonlinear increase in demand prediction errors with longer forecast horizons indicates natural limitations of data-driven techniques in modeling the complex behavior of market forces driven by various factors31. In short-horizon predictions, correlation patterns exist in the current demand sequence, which are learned by attention-equipped LSTM networks. However, as prediction intervals increase, demand starts to depend on various external factors, which cannot be modeled using past trends only. The 23.6% MAE for fourteen-day forecasts, while superior to ARIMA baselines, remains problematic for production planning applications where inventory buffers and capacity reserves must account for forecast uncertainty.

The wide 95% confidence intervals observed for medium-term predictions (± 18.4% of mean demand) underscore the inherent difficulty of uncertainty quantification in manufacturing contexts. The classical way of treating forecast uncertainty with safety stock calculations assumed normal distributions of errors and constant error variance, which was not always the case in real-life settings32. The error distributions showed positive skewness and fat tails, particularly during times of market turmoil. Monte Carlo Dropout allows for theoretically grounded estimates of uncertainty through approximate Bayesian inference, although the computational cost of multiple stochastic forward passes (50 samples per prediction) limits its use in real-time systems. The development of efficient techniques for uncertainty estimation, ready for usage in online systems, is a key area for future work.

Deep reinforcement learning has shown efficacy in optimizing scheduling decisions, thus making a case for learning algorithms on complex combinatorial optimization problems, although various practical limitations remain to be addressed33. The observed increase in resource utilization to 82.6% indicates a significant gain over classical heuristics, despite large amounts of unused capacity. This remaining idle time reflects inherent limits in manufacturing systems, such as priority recovery times for maintenance, recovery after quality inspection, material movement constraints, and operator availability. However, analysis of idle time distributions reveals that approximately 40% of unused capacity is attributable to poor scheduling rather than hard constraints.

A severe delay rate of 12.8% during peak demand conditions reflects a noticeable weakness in robustness. DDQN predominantly used training data from the normal range of operations, thereby generating a biased policy towards frequently visited states34. For demands outside the historical distribution, policy generalization can be weak, leading to non-desirable outcomes during peak demands. Curriculum learning, a progressive introduction of increasingly difficult situations during training, could be an improvement; however, care needs to be exercised against over-specialization on the artificial distribution of training data. The additional out-of-distribution experiments reinforce this conclusion. Structurally novel disruptions produced 27–114% greater prediction degradation relative to the original market-driven disruption and substantially prolonged recovery periods. The variation in degradation severity is informative: regulatory compliance changes, which partially resemble equipment reconfiguration events in training data, showed the mildest degradation (27.1% MAE, 16-day partial recovery), whereas workforce disruptions caused the most severe impact (32.8% MAE, 24-day partial recovery) because labor availability was not explicitly represented in the prediction model’s input features. This pattern suggests that robustness is not a uniform property but depends on the structural distance between novel events and trained conditions. For practical deployment, disruption detection mechanisms should be incorporated to trigger fallback to conservative rule-based scheduling when prediction confidence drops below acceptable thresholds.

Computational efficiency constraints create barriers for the adoption of deep learning-based scheduling systems. The requirement of 2.8 s for multi-modal fusion, along with the 18–35 min of convergence time for DDQN in large-scale situations, does not fit within the requirements of production decision-making35. Despite challenges regarding latency and data privacy, local computation is facilitated by architectures that implement edge computing. Edge devices usually lack sufficient resources to perform deep learning tasks. Model compression methods such as pruning, quantization, and distillation are emerging solutions for speeding up these tasks. Their efficacy depends on the architecture of interest.

The poor cross-industry generalization performance (67.9% efficiency retention) highlights fundamental challenges in developing universal manufacturing scheduling solutions36. Domain shift in different industries entails more than variations in products or equipment; it entails different optimization goals, constraints, and philosophies. For instance, in pharmaceutical production, compliance with regulations and batch traceability are of utmost importance, unlike automotive production, where just-in-time and low-inventory operations are prioritized. Attempts to capture this diversity in a single model form are difficult. An alternative is to pre-train models on general manufacturing data and fine-tune them with industry-specific examples, though this approach requires maintaining multiple models and substantial domain expertise.

To contextualize these findings, comparison with existing DRL-based scheduling research is informative. Prior studies applying DDQN or its variants to job-shop and flexible manufacturing scheduling have primarily focused on demonstrating algorithmic performance gains over traditional heuristics, typically reporting 4–11% point improvements in resource utilization depending on problem complexity37,38. The 6.3% point gain observed in this study falls within this established range, confirming that the integrated framework does not sacrifice scheduling quality despite the additional complexity of multi-modal input processing. The computational overhead observed here is higher than in some prior work, likely reflecting the additional cost of the multi-modal fusion and demand prediction modules in the pipeline. For demand prediction, LSTM+Attention accuracy aligns with literature for manufacturing-based studies39, and multi-modal fusion benefits (9.6% accuracy improvement) closely match those reported for additive manufacturing quality prediction (8–12% improvement)40. However, a key distinction is that most prior work evaluates DRL scheduling using single-source production data under normal operating conditions, without systematically assessing robustness to data incompleteness, demand uncertainty, or cross-domain transfer. The present study addresses this gap by providing a comprehensive evaluation across these dimensions, revealing practical limitations—such as the 21.7% fusion error rate, extended recovery under novel disruptions, and 67.9% cross-industry efficiency retention—that are rarely reported in existing literature. These comparisons suggest that while the individual components do not represent algorithmic advances, the system-level integration and the realistic scope of evaluation provide practical value not available from studies examining these components in isolation. All results were obtained from simulated environments, and real-world deployment challenges are discussed further in the conclusion.

Conclusion

This study presented an integrated framework for dynamic resource scheduling in intelligent manufacturing, combining multi-modal data fusion, LSTM-based demand prediction with attention mechanisms, and DDQN-based scheduling optimization. The main findings are as follows. First, intermediate fusion outperformed early and late fusion strategies by 9.6% and 3.2% in production state accuracy respectively, though at the cost of higher computational latency (2.8 s). Second, the attention-augmented LSTM achieved 8.2% MAE for short-term demand prediction, but accuracy degraded substantially at longer horizons (23.6% MAE at 14 days) and under out-of-distribution disruptions (27.1–32.8% MAE). Third, DDQN scheduling improved resource utilization to 82.6% and on-time delivery to 89.1% under normal conditions, but performance declined under peak demand (74.2% on-time delivery, 12.8% severe delays) and cross-industry transfer (67.9% efficiency retention).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. All experiments were conducted in simulated environments; real-world deployment may face additional challenges including legacy system integration, sensor reliability, and organizational factors. The computational requirements (18–35 min for DDQN convergence, 5.1 s for full fusion pipeline) may not meet the latency constraints of real-time production systems. The framework’s robustness to novel disruption types is limited, as demonstrated by the extended recovery periods under out-of-distribution scenarios.

Future research should address these limitations through several directions: developing lightweight model variants suitable for edge deployment, incorporating explicit disruption detection mechanisms with fallback strategies, exploring transfer learning approaches to reduce cold-start data requirements for new manufacturing environments, investigating continual and online learning strategies that allow the scheduling agent to incrementally update its policy as the production environment evolves after deployment, and conducting pilot validation studies in real production facilities.
