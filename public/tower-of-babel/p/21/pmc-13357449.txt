Gut microbiota and metabolomic alterations underlying the therapeutic effects of SQJZ capsules in a rat model of chronic renal failure
Authors: Rui Tan, Tianwen Que, Feng Pan, Nan Tong, Dan Wu, Haifeng Zhu, Anrong Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of this study is to investigate the therapeutic effects of Shengqing Jiangzhuo (SQJZ) Capsules on chronic renal failure (CRF) by examining their influence on intestinal microbiota and metabolomic profiles. SQJZ’s active ingredients were identified using UPLC-Q-Exactive-Orbitrap-MS. A chronic renal failure model was induced in rats via 16 weeks of intragastric adenine administration, followed by 35 days of daily treatment. Blood samples were analyzed for UA, BUN, Scr, Cys C, TNF-α, and IL-6. Kidney tissue underwent Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining, Masson’s Trichrome staining, and immunohistochemical analysis to assess histopathological changes, as well as alterations in macrophage and neutrophil populations. Immunohistochemistry evaluated renal inflammatory and fibrotic proteins (NLRP3, caspase-1, TGF-β1, fibronectin, and collagen I). RT-qPCR measured mRNA levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, while immunofluorescence staining evaluated intestinal tight junction proteins (Occludin, Claudin-1, ZO-1). Gut microbiota changes were analyzed via 16S rRNA gene sequencing, serum metabolites through untargeted metabolomics, and Spearman correlation assessed links between microbial taxa and metabolites. Rats in the model group showed significantly higher levels of UA, BUN, Cys C, Scr, TNF-α, and IL-6, along with reduced body weight, compared to the control group (P < 0.001). Treatment with SQJZ Capsules at various doses significantly lowered these levels and improved body weight (P < 0.001). HE staining revealed renal fibrosis and inflammation, with increased mesangial cell proliferation and collagen deposition in the model group (P < 0.01). Intestinal tissues showed increased thickness, reduced villi, and fewer goblet cells. SQJZ Capsules at high, medium, and low doses significantly improved renal structural damage compared to the model group. This was shown by reduced mesangial cell and matrix proliferation, less glomerular injury, decreased inflammatory cell infiltration, and reduced fibrosis. Intestinal villus structure was restored, inflammatory cells decreased, goblet cell numbers increased, and mucosal thickness was reduced. Masson’s trichrome staining revealed significant collagen deposition and severe renal fibrosis in the model group versus the control group (P < 0.01). Immunohistochemical analysis showed a significant rise in macrophages and monocytes in the kidneys of model group rats compared to controls. SQJZ Capsule treatment notably reduced these inflammatory cells, with the high-dose group showing the most significant effects (P < 0.01). All treatment groups showed a significant reduction in collagen deposition and renal fibrosis (P < 0.01), with the high-dose group showing the most improvement. Immunohistochemistry revealed that SQJZ Capsules reduced renal expression of NLRP3, caspase-1, TGF-β1, fibronectin, collagen I, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, with the strongest effects in the high-dose group. Immunofluorescence confirmed increased Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1 expression, especially in the high-dose group. SQJZ Capsules also normalized gut microbiota, particularly Erysipelatoclostridium and Lactobacillus levels. Untargeted metabolomics found 530 differential metabolites, mainly affecting phenylalanine, α-linolenic acid, ascorbate and aldarate, tryptophan, and arachidonic acid pathways. These findings suggest that SQJZ Capsules may offer a multi-target therapeutic strategy for CRF by modulating the gut microbiota and associated metabolic pathways. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is currently acknowledged as a major chronic illness associated with considerable disability and mortality rates worldwide, posing a significant threat to public health. Recent data suggest that the global prevalence of CKD has escalated to approximately 850 million individuals, accounting for about 9.1% of the global population (Mandai 2025). Initially asymptomatic with a gradual onset, this condition can develop into chronic renal failure (CRF). CRF involves the buildup of uremic toxins like indolyl sulfate and p-cresol sulfate, causing decreased glomerular filtration rate, electrolyte imbalances, and acid–base issues, ultimately reducing kidney function. Key symptoms of CRF include proteinuria, hematuria, hypertension, and edema (Clase 2011). Contemporary standard Western interventions for CRF primarily emphasize dietary modifications, blood pressure management, and the reduction of proteinuria to decelerate disease progression. In cases of end-stage disease, dialysis or kidney transplantation becomes necessary. Consequently, postponing the progression of CRF, decreasing the incidence of end-stage CRF, and enhancing clinical outcomes continue to be significant public health priorities and clinical challenges.

The “gut-kidney axis” concept suggests that gut microbiota imbalances are strongly linked to the progression of CRF, with significant differences in the gut microbiota of CRF patients compared to healthy people (Evenepoel et al. 2017). In CRF, uremia disrupts gut microbiota and weakens the intestinal barrier, allowing toxins to enter the bloodstream, which worsens inflammation and accelerates kidney damage (Vaziri et al. 2013). Conversely, gut microbiota dysbiosis changes microbial enzyme profiles, leading to metabolites that enter the bloodstream, significantly impacting the serum metabolome and directly influencing host pathophysiology (Wu et al. 2021).

The treatment of kidney diseases within Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) underscores the importance of syndrome differentiation and a holistic approach. This methodology prioritizes the regulation of organ functions and the optimization of renal network circulation. Such an approach is attributable to TCM’s multi-target therapeutic strategy, low toxicity, and favorable cost-effectiveness (Wei et al. 2015; Yue et al. 2023). Recent research indicates that several constituents of TCM possess the capacity to effectively modulate renal function (Yue et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2019; Zhu et al. 2020). SQJZ Capsules, an in-hospital formulation developed by Professor Tong Anrong, comprise multiple herbal components and have demonstrated significant clinical efficacy in the management of CRF (Clase 2011). Furthermore, prior validation studies have shown that SQJZ Capsules exhibit substantial therapeutic effects in an adenine-induced CRF model, significantly enhancing renal function and decelerating disease progression (Luo 2019; Xiong 2017). Nonetheless, the mechanisms through which SQJZ Capsules modulate gut dysbiosis and the associated metabolic disturbances in CRF are not yet fully understood. Consequently, elucidating the interaction between gut microbiota and related metabolites could facilitate the identification of novel therapeutic strategies for CRF patients.

Recent research has investigated the potential of TCM to mitigate CRF through the modulation of gut microbiota. However, the majority of these studies have concentrated solely on either microbial composition or individual metabolites. In contrast, the current study employs an integrated approach, combining microbiome and metabolomics analyses, to systematically elucidate the renoprotective effects of SQJZ Capsules via the gut-kidney axis. This integrative strategy, which merges the holistic and multi-target principles of TCM with systems biology methodologies, offers novel mechanistic insights into the therapeutic effects of SQJZ Capsules in CRF.

Methods

Grouping, modeling, and administration

Following a 3-day acclimation period, the rats were randomly assigned to one of five groups: control, model, and three treatment groups receiving high, medium, or low doses of SQJZ Capsules, with each group comprising eight rats. To induce CRF, all groups except the control group were administered a 2.5% adenine suspension (1 g in 40 mL saline) orally at a dosage of 200 mg/kg per day for 16 weeks (Hu et al. 2010). The SQJZ Capsule suspension solutions were prepared at concentrations of 0.048 g/mL, 0.096 g/mL, and 0.192 g/mL using distilled water for the low-, medium-, and high-dose groups, respectively. The rats received these treatments twice daily for a duration of 35 days, during which their body weight was monitored. Twenty-four hours after the final administration, the rats were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg), and blood samples were collected from the abdominal aorta. Serum was separated, and kidney and intestinal tissues, along with cecal contents, were harvested. The kidney and intestinal tissues were rinsed, sectioned into pieces approximately the size of soybeans, and fixed in formalin. Remaining samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Rat kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated, and embedded to create paraffin sections. These were sliced into 5-µm sections, treated with xylene and ethanol, and underwent antigen retrieval with pH 6 citrate buffer. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide methanol, and sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody. After washing, secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG and HRP conjugate) were applied, followed by DAB solution. Hematoxylin counterstaining, differentiation with 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol, and bluing with ammonia water were performed. The sections were then dehydrated, cleared with xylene, sealed with neutral gum, and examined under a microscope.

HE staining

The renal and intestinal tissues of the rats were initially fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for a duration of 24 h. Subsequently, the tissues underwent dehydration and were embedded in paraffin to prepare sections. Following the baking process, the sections were subjected to dewaxing and hydration treatments. Hematoxylin staining was then performed, followed by rinsing, differentiation using 1% hydrochloric acid-alcohol, and subsequent eosin counterstaining. After the dehydration process and sealing of the sections, the histopathological alterations in the renal and intestinal tissues were examined microscopically, and images were systematically captured for analysis.

Masson staining

Masson staining was performed on kidney sections in accordance with established protocols for baking, dewaxing, and hydration. This protocol involves a series of sequential treatment steps: staining with potassium dichromate for 3 min, hematoxylin for 3 min, differentiation with acidic ethanol for 10 min, fuchsin and red staining for 10 min, phosphomolybdic acid staining for 10 min, and toluidine blue staining for 5 min. Subsequently, the dehydrated sections were rendered transparent using xylene. After sealing, the degree of renal fibrosis in each group of rats was assessed microscopically.

Isolation of RNA and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from mouse kidney tissue using an RNA extraction kit. After confirming the concentration and purity with NanoDrop, 1 µg of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT kit. SYBR Green premix was used for amplification on a real-time PCR machine with the following program: 95 °C for 30 s, 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 30 s, for 40 cycles. Product specificity was analyzed with a melt curve, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method with GAPDH as the internal control. Primer sequences are shown in Table 1.

Immunofluorescence

Rat kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by dehydration and paraffin embedding. Subsequent to deparaffinization using xylene and rehydration through a graded ethanol series, antigen retrieval was conducted in a pH 9.0 EDTA buffer at 95 °C for 20 min. The sections were then blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide and normal goat serum, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin. This was followed by a 30-min incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature. Tyrosine signal amplification was achieved using TYR-570, TYR-520, and TYR-650 dyes, with heat-induced epitope retrieval applied after each staining cycle. Finally, the sections were counterstained with DAPI and mounted with an anti-fade reagent for fluorescence microscopy imaging.

Biochemical and ELISA detection of kidney function and inflammatory factors

Five milliliters of blood was collected from the rat abdominal artery and centrifuged at 3000 rpm, 4 °C for 5 min using a high-speed centrifuge. The supernatant was used to measure UA, BUN, and Scr levels using an automated biochemical analyzer. ELISA was used to detect Cys C, IL-6, and TNF-α levels in the serum of each group of rats. All procedures were performed according to the kit instructions. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and standard curves were plotted to calculate the concentrations of Cys C, IL-6, and TNF-α in rat serum.

LC–MS analysis

Polar metabolites were separated utilizing the Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in conjunction with the Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide chromatographic column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 µm). The mobile phase consisted of Phase A, an aqueous solution containing 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonia water, and Phase B, acetonitrile. The sample tray was maintained at a temperature of 4 ℃, and the injection volume was set at 2 µL. Mass spectrometric detection was performed using the Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo), operated under the control of Xcalibur software (v4.4) for both primary and secondary mass spectrometry acquisition. Key mass spectrometry parameters included a sheath gas flow rate of 50 Arb, an auxiliary gas flow rate of 15 Arb, and a capillary temperature of 320 ℃. The first-level full scan resolution was set at 60,000, while the secondary scan resolution was 15,000. Collision energy was set at SNCE 20/30/40, and the spray voltage was + 3.8 kV for positive ion mode and − 3.4 kV for negative ion mode. Following the conversion of raw data into mzXML format using ProteoWizard software, metabolite identification was performed utilizing an R package co-developed with BiotreeDB (V3.0) as the reference database. Subsequently, visual analysis was executed using an independently developed R package. The initial dataset comprised 4 QC samples and 30 experimental samples, from which a total of 50,618 features were extracted.

16S high-throughput DNA sequencing

Based on previous pharmacodynamic studies, the high-dose SQJZ Capsules group was chosen for 16S rRNA sequencing to evaluate their effects on chronic renal failure in rats. Intestinal samples were stored at − 80 °C, and three samples from each group (control, model, and high-dose) were sent to Shanghai Baiqu Biomedical for DNA extraction. DNA was quantified with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer, and quality was checked using 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform, with sequences grouped into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity. The QIIME2 dada2 software was used to analyze species composition across samples/groups at various classification levels. It assessed alpha diversity and sequencing depth, calculated beta diversity at the ASV/OTU level, and examined intergroup differences using unsupervised clustering and statistical tests. At the species level, it combined unsupervised/supervised methods and statistical tests to explore abundance differences and identify marker species. A species association network was built to calculate topological indices and identify key species. Functional potential prediction based on marker genes (16S) was used to analyze differential metabolic pathways and their species composition. Finally, high-quality charts and statistical analyses were produced.

Spearman correlation analysis

A Spearman correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationship between significantly altered bacterial genera in the intestinal microbiota of rats post-administration and the significantly different biomarkers identified in metabolomic studies. This analysis aimed to determine the potential dependency between the distribution of intestinal microbiota and endogenous metabolites, thereby elucidating the interaction between intestinal microbiota and metabolites.

Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Intergroup differences were assessed through one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of a statistically significant difference.

General conditions and changes in body weight of rats in each group

Throughout the experimental period, the rats in the control group demonstrated a glossy coat, high activity levels, a positive mental state, and no abnormalities in food consumption, defecation, or urination. Conversely, the animals in the model group exhibited characteristic pathological conditions, including a dry coat, localized alopecia, reduced activity, and lethargy, as well as symptoms such as decreased appetite, dark urine, and loose stools. After a 35-day intervention with SQJZ Capsules, the body weight of the rats in the model group was significantly lower than that of the control group (P < 0.001). However, the body weight of the rats in the low-, medium-, and high-dose SQJZ Capsule groups showed a significant increase compared to the model group (P < 0.001), as depicted in Fig. 2.

Histopathological impact of SQJZ Capsules on rat kidneys with CRF

Among different rat groups, histological examination using HE staining revealed significant differences between the experimental groups. In the model group, when compared to the control group, there was pronounced infiltration of inflammatory cells, a reduction in glomerular volume, thickening of the basement membrane, and substantial deposition of blue collagen fibers within the renal tubulointerstitium and its epithelial structures. In contrast, the groups treated with high, medium, and low doses of SQJZ Capsules exhibited a marked reduction in mesangial cell and stromal proliferation within the renal tissue. These groups also demonstrated decreased glomerular damage, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, and alleviated fibrosis, indicating significant histopathological improvement (Fig. 4A). Notably, the high-dose group exhibited the most pronounced therapeutic effect. The histological examination of intestinal tissues using HE staining revealed significant alterations in the model group compared to the control group. Specifically, there was a change in the thickness of the intestinal basal layer, a reduction in the number of villi and goblet cells, an increase in the thickness of the mucosal layer, and substantial neutrophil infiltration. In contrast, administration of SQJZ Capsules at high, medium, and low doses markedly ameliorated intestinal mucosal injury, with the high-dose group demonstrating the most pronounced effects. These effects included the repair of intestinal villi, decreased infiltration of inflammatory cells, an increase in goblet cell numbers, and a reduction in mucosal layer thickness, as illustrated in Fig. 4B. Furthermore, Masson staining results indicated significant collagen fiber deposition and pronounced fibrosis in the renal tissue of the model group compared to the control group (P < 0.01). Treatment with SQJZ Capsules significantly reduced collagen fiber deposition and improved fibrosis in renal tissues across all dosage groups, with the high-dose group exhibiting the most substantial improvement (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4C).

Effect of SQJZ Capsules modulate intestinal flora composition in rats with CRF

Analysis of intestinal flora diversity

Building upon prior pharmacodynamic evaluations, we selected the high-dose group of SQJZ Capsules for 16S rRNA sequencing to assess the impact of these capsules on chronic renal failure in rats. The observation of a plateau phase in both the alpha rarefaction curve and the richness level curve indicates that the sequencing depth is adequate, thereby affirming the reliability of the experimental data (Fig. 7A, B). The results from the alpha diversity analysis revealed that the Chao1 index and Shannon index in the model group were significantly elevated compared to those in the control group (P = 0.023, P = 0.018). Conversely, the indices in the SQJZ Capsule group were lower than those observed in the model group. These findings suggest that the administration of SQJZ Capsules may diminish the richness of intestinal flora in rats with chronic renal failure (Fig. 7C, D). The β diversity analysis revealed a relatively concentrated distribution of samples within each group. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) were performed on the three sample groups, demonstrating significant differences among the blank group, the model group, and the SQJZ Capsule group (Fig. 7E, F).

Analysis of the composition and structure of the intestinal

An examination of the microbial composition within three distinct groups of gut microbiota revealed the abundance of bacteria at the phylum, family, and genus levels, as illustrated in Fig. 9A–C. At the phylum level, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria were identified as the predominant bacterial groups. Metabolomics analysis

Analysis of metabolic pattern determination

Figure 11 presents the distribution trend of metabolomics data as analyzed by principal component analysis (PCA), emphasizing the variance among samples from distinct groups. The analysis reveals a pronounced separation between the control group, the model group, and the SQJZ Capsule group, with a notable degree of parallelism within each group. These findings indicate significant alterations in metabolites within the model group, which tend to return to baseline levels following drug intervention. Subsequently, PCA was applied to identify and exclude outliers. To mitigate overfitting, Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) was employed, supplemented by 200 permutation tests. The R2 value’s proximity to 1 suggests robust model stability, while Q2 serves as the model’s predictive index. The results confirm that the model is not overfitted and demonstrates both strong stability and excellent predictive analytical capability (Fig. 12).

Identification of differential metabolites

In this study, the identification of differential metabolites was conducted using the Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) values from Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) and fold change (FC) values from univariate analysis. Metabolites were considered differential if they met the criteria of VIP > 1.0, FC < 0.8 or > 1.2, and P < 0.05. A volcano plot (Fig. 13) was utilized to illustrate the upregulation and downregulation of these metabolites across different groups. The quantitative distribution of upregulated and downregulated metabolites is detailed in Table 2. In comparison to the normal group, the CRF model group exhibited a total of 495 differential metabolites, with 420 significantly upregulated and 70 significantly downregulated. Following treatment with SQJZ Capsules, the CRF rats displayed 530 differential metabolites relative to the model group, of which 339 were significantly downregulated and 191 significantly upregulated. The top 20 differential metabolites for the comparisons between the blank and model groups, as well as between the model and SQJZ Capsule groups, are presented in Table 3. Among the differential metabolites identified, several key molecules associated with kidney dysfunction and uremia pathology were significantly modulated after treatment with SQJZ Capsules. One notable example is phenylacetic acid, a typical gut-derived uremic toxin, which was significantly elevated in the model group and markedly reduced after intervention, suggesting that the drug may reduce the production and absorption of this toxin by regulating the gut microbiota. The levels of N-acetylglycine and several fatty acid metabolites also tended to normalize after treatment, indicating that SQJZ Capsules may improve energy metabolism disorders and mitochondrial function in CRF states. The trends in these key metabolites are consistent with the pathway enrichment analysis results, including phenylalanine metabolism and fatty acid oxidation, further suggesting that SQJZ Capsules may exert a nephroprotective effect through multi-target coordinated actions.

To further investigate the relationship between characteristic intestinal flora and metabolites, the Spearman correlation analysis was employed to examine the associations between differential flora and differential metabolites across the blank group, model group, and SQJZ Capsule group (Fig. 15). Discussion

In the context of TCM, kidney disease is frequently associated with the concepts of “deficiency, turbidity, stasis, and dampness,” with a prevalent occurrence of spleen-kidney deficiency. Chronic conditions such as renal insufficiency and nephrotic edema are generally characterized by qi deficiency, blood stasis, and internal damp turbidity, which may ultimately result in the accumulation of toxins and subsequent renal impairment (Chen et al. 2016). Professor Tong Anrong posits that this disease originates from a deficiency in the spleen and kidney, which results in an impaired ascent of clear yang and descent of turbid yin. The therapeutic approach should be informed by the physiological characteristics of these organs, focusing on modulating their functions of ascent, descent, ingress, and egress. Beyond conventional syndrome differentiation therapies—such as fortifying the spleen and augmenting qi, warming yang and resolving turbidity, and tonifying the spleen and kidney—attention should also be directed towards the ascending and descending properties of herbs, their four qi and five flavors, and the regulation of organ qi dynamics (Pan and Tong 2016).

In 2011, Meijers and colleagues introduced the concept of the “gut-kidney axis,” emphasizing the pathophysiological relationship between the intestine and the kidney (Björn K I and Pieter 2011). Subsequently, Pahl et al. advanced this concept by developing the “CKD-colon axis,” which underscores the bidirectional relationship between gut dysbiosis and kidney disease (Madeleine and Nosratola 2015). Kidney dysfunction not only disrupts the gut microbiota and compromises the intestinal barrier, but also permits gut-derived microbes and metabolites to enter the circulation through the compromised mucosa. This process triggers persistent systemic inflammation, thereby creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates kidney injury (Tao and Kong 2019).

Lan et al. (2023) demonstrated that the polysaccharides present in Fufang-Zhenzhu-Tiaozhi formula have the potential to mitigate diabetic kidney injury by modulating the “gut-kidney axis.” Targeting this axis to influence the gut microbiota-metabolite-signaling pathway network is emerging as a crucial therapeutic strategy for diabetic nephropathy. TCM formulations, such as Qing-Re-Xiao-Zheng formula and Shenyan Kangfu tablet, exhibit promise in this regard, underscoring the modulation of gut microbiota as a pivotal approach for the treatment of diabetic nephropathy (Wu et al. 2024). The gut microbiota, often referred to as the “second human genome,” plays a significant role in regulating metabolism, immunity, and energy absorption. Dysbiosis can lead to the release of toxins into the circulation through a compromised intestinal barrier, thereby triggering inflammation, metabolic and immune dysfunction, and accelerating the progression of chronic kidney disease (Li et al. 2024; Yu and Peng 2021). Renal dysfunction leads to the accumulation of uremic toxins, some of which permeate into the gastrointestinal tract, thereby disrupting the gut microbiota and establishing a detrimental gut-kidney feedback loop. An increase in Clostridium and Lactobacillales populations has been shown to exacerbate tubular atrophy, fibrosis, and inflammation (Lin et al. 2019; Shuanghong et al. 2016; Shinobu et al. 2021). Clinical investigations have demonstrated that patients with CRF exhibit an elevated Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio in their fecal matter, along with increased gut microbiota α diversity. The disturbance of microbial equilibrium may result in bacterial translocation, systemic inflammation, and subsequent renal fibrosis. Importantly, metabolites derived from the gut are pivotal in the pathogenesis of CRF. The administration of SQJZ capsules orally has been shown to interact with gut bacteria, whose enzymatic activities can modify pharmaceuticals, influencing their activation, inactivation, toxicity, stability, bioavailability, or excretion. Furthermore, pharmaceuticals can alter the composition of the microbiota, establishing a bidirectional interaction that impacts therapeutic outcomes (Pant et al. 2023). Inflammation, typically a protective mechanism, transforms into a chronic pathological response in CKD (Cobo et al. 2018). EHF has been shown to inhibit NF-κB, TNF-α, and TGF-β1 in a dose-dependent manner, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects in rats with CRF (Zhang et al. 2017).

This study demonstrates that SQJZ Capsules effectively downregulate the expression of NLRP3, caspase-1, TGF-β, FN, and Col1aⅠ, while significantly reducing renal function markers such as UA, Scr, BUN, and Cys C. Additionally, the capsules inhibit inflammatory mediators including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. Histological and fibrotic assessments of intestinal and renal tissues indicate a restoration of intestinal villus height, a reduction in inflammatory cell infiltration, and an increase in goblet cell numbers. Notably, there was a significant increase in the number of macrophages and monocytes in the renal tissues of mice, accompanied by elevated expression of related chemokines. However, following treatment with the SQJZ Capsule, these numbers decreased significantly. Furthermore, there was an upregulation of intestinal Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1 expression, which are essential tight junction proteins that maintain epithelial barrier integrity. These findings suggest that SQJZ Capsules may mitigate renal fibrosis and enhance kidney function by restoring gut barrier function and reducing serum metabolic toxins.

The 16S rRNA and metabolomics analyses revealed that CRF in rats was associated with a reduction in Firmicutes and Actinobacteria, alongside an increase in Bacteroidetes. Administration of SQJZ Capsules partially ameliorated these alterations, thereby rectifying the CRF-induced dysbiosis of the gut microbiota. At the genus level, members of the Lachnospiraceae family are pivotal producers of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (Alba et al. 2023; I-Wen et al. 2020). These SCFAs are known to attenuate inflammation and have been implicated in reducing the risk of CKD progression (Giorgia et al. 2022; Meng et al. 2024). Additionally, Ruminococcaceae and Lactobacillaceae are prevalent in healthy individuals and constitute the functional core of the gut microbiota. These beneficial bacteria predominantly produce SCFAs, particularly butyrate, which are crucial for maintaining health by providing nutrition and energy to intestinal epithelial cells, preserving gut barrier integrity, reducing inflammation, and enhancing colonic motility (Yifan et al. 2022; Li et al. 2020).

Metabolomics data suggest that the differential metabolites of phenylalanine, alpha-linolenic acid, ascorbate/aldarate, tryptophan, and arachidonic acid are most closely linked to the pathology of CRF and the therapeutic intervention of SQJZ capsules. The accumulation of phenylalanine catabolites, such as phenyllactic and phenylacetic acids, can impair renal cells, promote inflammation, and accelerate renal decline (Xie et al. 2024). Furthermore, alpha-linolenic acid has been shown to significantly alleviate kidney injury (Kaplan et al. 2023). The ascorbate and aldarate metabolism pathway serves a protective function against oxidative damage in cells and is integral to the pathophysiology of diabetic nephropathy, obesity-related kidney injury, and renal fibrosis (Cong et al. 2023; Hong et al. 2020; Hong & Kang 2024). In the context of CKD, there is an upregulation of several tryptophan catabolic pathways, notably the kynurenine and uremic toxin pathways, which contribute to persistent renal inflammation and the manifestation of uremic symptoms (Jörg et al. 2009). Arachidonic acid plays a role in inhibiting glomerular thromboxane B2, leukotriene B4, and 12-HETE, leading to a reduction in glomerular filtration rate and renal blood flow (Wei et al. 2020). These metabolic disruptions exacerbate fatty acid metabolism and inflammation, which are consistent with our findings in CRF kidneys. This suggests that SQJZ capsules may exert their therapeutic effects by modulating amino acid metabolism.

This study investigates the mechanisms by which SQJZ Capsules influence CRF through analyses of gut microbiota, metabolomics, and pathological assessments. However, the precise molecular targets and underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated. Future research will aim to elucidate how these capsules modulate gut microbiota and target specific metabolic pathways to restore renal function and mitigate the progression of CRF.
