Substrate Stiffness Regulates the Osteogenesis of PDLSCs Via ERK-Mediated YAP Nuclear Translocation
Authors: Yuting Fu, Yijie Wang, Bo Cheng, Rui Zou, Wanting Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesPeriodontitis is an inflammatory disease and may lead to the progressive destruction of alveolar bone. Periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) exhibit significant regenerative potential, and their osteogenic differentiation is influenced by the biophysical microenvironment, particularly substrate stiffness. However, the mechanistic pathways through which stiffness modulates PDLSCs osteogenesis remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to elucidate the role of ERK1/2 and YAP signalling pathways in regulating PDLSCs osteogenic differentiation in response to substrate stiffness.MethodsGelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogels with varying stiffness were fabricated to culture PDLSCs. Cell viability, spreading, and osteogenic differentiation were evaluated through live/dead staining, immunofluorescence, qRT-PCR, Western blot analysis, and alizarin red staining. The involvement of ERK1/2 and YAP signalling pathways was investigated using specific inhibitors. An in vivo rat periodontitis model was developed to validate in vitro findings, with periodontal ligament stiffness and alveolar bone resorption assessed via nanoindentation and micro-computed tomography (micro-CT).ResultsPDLSCs cultured on stiffer substrates exhibited enhanced osteogenic differentiation, as evidenced by upregulated expression of osteogenic markers (RUNX2 and OCN) and increased mineralization. Stiff substrate promoted ERK1/2 phosphorylation and YAP nuclear translocation. Inhibition of ERK1/2 reduced YAP nuclear localization and suppressed osteogenic marker expression. In vivo, periodontitis-associated reductions in periodontal ligament stiffness correlated with decreased YAP nuclear translocation and significant alveolar bone resorption. YAP inhibition mirrored these effects, underscoring its critical role in osteogenesis.ConclusionsSubstrate stiffness regulates PDLSCs osteogenesis via ERK-mediated YAP nuclear translocation. These findings advance our understanding of the mechanotransduction mechanisms underlying PDLSCs differentiation and highlight potential therapeutic targets for periodontal regeneration.

Introduction

Periodontitis is characterized by progressive destruction of the teeth-supporting apparatus, including the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone.1 Current therapeutic approaches for periodontitis primarily consist of mechanical scaling/root planing (SRP) and antibacterial and anti-inflammatory measures,2 which is difficult to reestablish the compromised periodontal tissue attachment.3 Moreover, bone grafts, frequently utilized in clinic, are associated with variable clinical outcomes, postsurgical morbidity, and elevated surgical expenses. In contrast, stem cell-based therapy, as a facet of regenerative medicine, offers the potential to repair damaged or destroyed tissues and address the issue of tissue donor shortages.4,5 Stem cell therapy has been proposed as a viable alternative to induce and promote bone repair processes.6 Preliminary clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of stem cells in promoting periodontal tissue regeneration in animal models with periodontal defect.7, 8, 9 Periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs), a type of mesenchymal stem cells residing within the periodontal ligament, exhibit self-renewal capabilities, multipotency, and immunomodulatory properties.10 Owing to the pivotal role in osteogenic differentiation and their promising therapeutic potential for treating destructed periodontium,10 PDLSCs are considered optimal seed cells for periodontal tissue repair and regeneration.11,12

Stem cell differentiation is pivotal in dictating the reparative capacity of tissues and is modulated by surrounding microenvironment. In recent years, there has been a burgeoning interest in the influence of biophysical microenvironments on stem cell behavior.13, 14, 15 Among the various mechanical cues present in the extracellular matrix (ECM), substrate stiffness is a critical determinant. It has been documented that substrate stiffness can significantly alter the functions of PDLSCs, including their differentiation, migration and proliferation.16 Specifically, substrate stiffness has been implicated in the regulation of PDLSCs' osteogenic differentiation, with studies indicating that increased substrate stiffness is associated with enhanced osteogenic potential.17 A recent study delineated that the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs on polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrates was contingent upon substrate stiffness, with stiffer substrates promoting greater osteogenic differentiation.16 This finding underscores the role of substrate stiffness in augmenting osteogenic differentiation in PDLSCs. Advancing our understanding of mechanisms underlying PDLSCs' osteogenic differentiation in response to matrix stiffness is crucial for harnessing their regenerative potential, which is indispensable for optimizing the efficacy of PDLSCs-based therapies.

Stem cell fate can be modulated by biophysical cues from ECM through mechanotransduction, which converts these biophysical signals into intracellular biochemical signals. The transcriptional regulator Yorkie-homologue YAP (Yes-associated protein) has been identified as a pivotal mechanical rheostat within the cell,18 with its localization and activity being correlated to the ECM stiffness.19 High stiffness triggers YAP translocation to the nucleus, further activating downstream signaling.20 During this process, YAP functions as a transcription factor, controlling the transcription of genes related to differentiation.21, 22, 23 A study has demonstrated that the application of cyclic stress stimuli to human periodontal ligament cells promotes both the nuclear translocation of YAP and osteoblastic differentiation. Furthermore, the knockdown of YAP suppressed the cyclic stretch-induced osteogenesis, while overexpression of YAP in PDLSCs enhanced osteogenic differentiation. These findings reveal that YAP nuclear translocation is a key regulator of osteogenic differentiation in PDLSCs.24 However, the specific mechanisms by which matrix stiffness regulates YAP nuclear translocation in PDLSCs are less studied and remain inconclusive.

The extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), a constituent of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade, plays a pivotal role in transducing extracellular environmental cues to the nucleus.25 ERK is implicated in the regulation of osteogenic differentiation in stem cells induced by ECM stiffness.25 Employing a hydrogel matrix as a surrogate for ECM, a study has demonstrated that ECM stiffness-induced ERK activation is crucial for transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ)-driven osteogenic differentiation in mesenchymal stem cells (MSC).26 In our preceding study, we observed that matrix stiffness enhanced osteogenesis in human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) by activating ERK1/2.27 Additionally, a study explored the relationship of ERK with TAZ/YAP activation, discovering that phospho-ERK expression levels decreased in response to increasing concentrations of verteporfin, a YAP inhibitor, in a dose-dependent manner. This suggests that YAP may modulate the phosphorylation status of the ERK signalling pathway.28 Nevertheless, the precise relationship between ERK and YAP nuclear entry in PDLSCs remains elusive. Current knowledge is insufficient to elucidate the mechanisms underlying osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs in response to matrix stiffness. Based on our previous studies and relevant literature, we hypothesized that substrate stiffness can influence the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs through the nuclear translocation of YAP, a process potentially mediated by ERK.

In this study, we engineered substrates of varying stiffness using gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogels and assessed the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs on these platforms. We further investigated the signalling pathways, specifically ERK1/2 and YAP, potentially implicated in the regulation of osteogenic differentiation. To substantiate our in vitro findings and further explore the mechanistic underpinnings, we employed an animal model. The null hypothesis was that substrate stiffness, ERK1/2 activity, YAP nuclear translocation, and PDLSCs osteogenic differentiation are mechanistically independent, with no correlations among these events. This study provides insights into the molecular mechanisms governing osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, potentially enhancing our understanding and contributing to PDLSCs-based bone regeneration therapies in the field of regenerative medicine.

Materials and methods

Preparation of GelMA hydrogel

GelMA macromers were synthesized based on the polymerization of gelatin Type A (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA) and methacrylic anhydride (MA, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). The entire process was carried out in the dark. 10 g of Gelatin (Type A) was dissolved in 100 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution at approximately 60 °C to achieve a homogeneous gelatin solution. 8 mL of methacrylic anhydride was slowly added to the gelatin solution at a rate of about 0.5 mL/min while maintaining the temperature at 60 °C. After 4 hours, the reaction product was diluted with 400 mL of preheated PBS at 45 °C and then dialyzed with Milli-Q water at 60 °C for 7 days using a dialysis membrane (8-14 kDa, Sigma-Aldrich) to remove any unreacted methacrylic anhydride and salts. The dialyzed GelMA solution was frozen and then lyophilized.

To create hydrogels with varying stiffness, different concentrations of GelMA macromers (8% and 12% w/v) were dissolved in PBS containing photoinitiator 2-hydroxy-1-(4-(hydroxyethoxy) phenyl)-2-methyl-1-propanone (Irgacure 2959; Sigma-Aldrich) (1 wt %) at 50 °C. The solution was then added into a 6-well plate to mould a GelMA hydrogel substrate. Exposed to 365 nm ultraviolet light (CL-1000; Funakoshi, Tokyo, Japan) at a distance of 20 cm for 1 minute, the GelMA substrates were sterilized successfully (Figure 1A). GelMA hydrogel samples for examination were moulded into a cylinder shape (diameter of 10 mm, thickness of 5 mm). Their stiffnesses were measured by a dynamic magnetic biomaterial test system (ELF 3200, Bose, Boston) at room temperature. The stress-strain data were acquired through the compression procedures. Subsequently, the stiffness of each sample was calculated by analysing the mean slope of its stress-strain curve. Cell culture

PDLSCs were obtained from Key Laboratory of Shaanxi Province for Craniofacial Precision Medicine Research, College of Stomatology, Xi'an Jiaotong University and cultured in Dulbecco minimal essential medium (Gibco, Gaithersburg, MD) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37°C with 5% CO2. PDLSCs used for this study were from the third to fifth passages.

Spreading and viability of PDLSCs

To measure the cell spreading area, PDLSCs were separately seeded onto the soft/stiff GelMA hydrogel substrates at a density of 1×105 cells/mL for 12 hours and 24 hours within a 6-well plate. PDLSCs were rinsed 3 times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 minutes and permeabilized for 20 minutes and blocked for 30 minutes with 0.2% Triton X-100 and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA,Sigma-Aldrich) respectively. F-actin was stained with rhodamine-labelled phalloidin (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) to visualize the cytoskeleton. Cell nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Sigma-Aldrich), to visualize the cell boundaries. PDLSCs were incubated in phalloidin and DAPI at 37 °C for 20 minutes and 10 minutes, respectively. PDLSCs on soft/stiff GelMA hydrogel substrates were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the spreading area was quantified using ImageJ software according to the previously published protocols.27

To assess the cell viability, PDLSCs were seeded onto the soft/stiff GelMA hydrogel substrates using Live/Dead staining assay. After incubating with live/dead fluorescent dyes (Invitrogen, USA) for 15 minutes. PDLSCs were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and the cell viability was quantified using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

Following a 14-day culture of PDLSCs on GelMA hydrogels in osteogenic medium containing 100 nM dexamethasone, 0.28 mM ascorbate-2-phosphate, and 10 mM b-glycerol phosphate, total RNA was extracted using the RNAzol reagent from MRC (Cincinnati, OH). Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was then synthesized with the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan) and a reverse transcription instrument, the S1000 (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). The transcriptional levels of the genes Runx2 and OCN were determined using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II kit (Takara) on a 7500 sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA), according to the manufacturer's protocol. The average data for each group were quantified against GAPDH as a reference gene. Western blot

The expression levels of RUNX2, ERK1/2 and phosphorylated ERK1/2 (p-ERK1/2) were evaluated by Western blot. For ERK1/2 and p-ERK1/2, PDLSCs were incubated on GelMA hydrogels for 4 hours. For RUNX2, PDLSCs were incubated on GelMA hydrogels for 2 weeks. After collection, cells were washed twice with PBS. Total proteins were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of these protein samples were separated by standard electrophoresis by 12% sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto poly (vinylidene difluoride) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA). They were then blocked and incubated with diluted primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies used were anti-ERK1/2 antibody, anti-phospho-ERK1/2 antibody and anti-Runx2 antibody (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA). The membranes were rinsed using a Tris-buffered saline/Tween, followed by a 2-hour incubation at room temperature with secondary antibodies (1:5000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Signal detection was performed using a chemiluminescence apparatus (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Alizarin red staining

After a 21-day osteogenic induction culture, PDLSCs were rinsed with PBS and fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 15 minutes. Following fixation, the cells were stained with alizarin red S solution (40 mM; pH 4.1; Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 minutes to visualize calcium deposits in the extracellular matrix. For further quantification of the results, the stained cells were incubated in 500 μL of a 10% (w/v) solution of cetylpyridinium chloride in disodium hydrogen phosphate at 37 °C for 10 minutes. Ultimately, the optical density of the samples was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (BioRad Laboratories).

Animals and anaesthesia

The experiment was carried out according to the guidelines of the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Xi'an Jiaotong University. Twenty healthy, 6-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, weighing approximately 200 ± 50 grams, were selected. They were raised in the Department of Laboratory Animal Science at Xi'an Jiaotong University under stable environmental conditions of 23 °C, 40% relative humidity, and a 12-hour light-dark cycle, with free access to food and water. During the experiment, each rat was intraperitoneally injected with 10% chloral hydrate to induce and maintain anaesthesia, adhering to all applicable institutional guidelines for animal care. All animal experiments were approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of the Medical Department of Xi'an Jiaotong University.

Periodontitis induction and verteporfin intervention

A volume of 10 μL of Pg-LPS (1 mg/mL; Solarbio, Beijing, China) was injected into the palatal gingival tissue between the first and second molars on the left maxillary quadrant of the rats using a precision microliter syringe (Gaoge, Shanghai, China). As a comparative control, an equal volume of saline solution was injected into a same location on the right side. Injection was given at 48 hours intervals and periodontitis was induced after a total of 6 injections of Pg-LPS. For the verteporfin intervention group, 10 μL verteporfin (10 mM; Sigma, USA) solution was injected into the palatal gingival tissue at the relevant site (consistent with the Pg-LPS injection area, between the first and second molars on the left maxillary quadrant). For the comparison, an equal volume of DMSO (verteporfin diluent) was injected on the right side at 48 hours intervals.

Subsequently, the animals were euthanized, and their maxillae were collected and bisected into periodontitis-affected and control segments. Specific sections of the fresh maxillae were designated for periodontal ligament (PDL) stiffness measurements, while other sections were preserved in a 70% ethanol solution for subsequent micro-CT analysis. The remaining maxillary samples were immersed in a fixative solution containing 4% paraformaldehyde in preparation for histological examination.

Histological examination of periodontitis

The fixed maxillary bone specimens were first decalcified in a 0.5 M ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution for 4 weeks, with the EDTA solution changed every 2 d. Subsequently, the specimens were dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol concentrations (80%, 95%, and 100%) and then embedded in OCT (Optimal Cutting Temperature compound, SAKURA, USA). After obtaining sagittal sections (5 mm), the tissue sections were baked at 55 °C for 30 minutes. The tissue sections were stored at −80 °C for further use.

In order to analyse the periodontal structure and ensure the successful induction of periodontitis, hematoxylin-eosin (HE) was used to stain these tissue sections. Briefly, after rinsing with distilled water, tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 minutes and dipped in ammonium hydroxide to enhance the nuclear staining. After rinsing again with water, the sections were counterstained with eosin for 1 minute and then dehydrated by passing them through an ethanol series again (80%, 95%, and 100%). Finally, the sections were cleared in xylene and mounted with a coverslip using resinous glue. Images were taken by microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan)

Periodontal tissue analysis

For PDL stiffness analysis, fresh tissue samples were measured by nanoindenter (OPTICS11, Netherlands) equipped with a microsphere indentation tip (diameter: 2.5 mm, stiffness: 0.49 N/m). The loading and unloading rate was set at 0.1mN/s, with a maximum indentation force of 1 mN. Load-displacement curves were converted to load-indentation curves. The curves were then fitted to the Hertz model as follows:

P=43E1−v2R1/2h3/2, where P is the load on the PDL, v is the Poisson's ratio (the value is set to 0.5), R is the radius of the spherical tip, h is the indentation depth, and E is the Young's modulus of PDL to be measured. The Young's modulus measured here reflects the stiffness of PDL.

Bone levels were assessed with an eXplore Locus SP MicroCT scanner (GE Healthcare, USA) with appropriate X-ray energy (80 kV, 80 mA) and a voxel size of 9 mm. The acquired images were analysed by VGStudio MAX software. The region of interest (ROI) for analysis was defined as all surrounding bone tissue from the starting plane (the furcation area between the distal root of the first molar and mesial root of the second molar) to the ending plane (the apical region). Within this ROI, bone-related parameters including bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and trabecular number (Tb.N) were analysed across all sequential layers.

Immunofluorescence staining

For cell immunofluorescent staining, PDLSCs on soft/stiff GelMA hydrogels were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma Aldrich) for 20 minutes, rinsed 3 times with PBS and then permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X for 10 minutes. Finally, they were blocked for 30 minutes with 5% BSA. The primary antibodies used here were as follows: anti-RUNX2 antibody (1:100; Abcam, USA), anti-phospho-ERK1/2 antibody (1: 100; Abcam, USA), anti-YAP antibody (1: 200; Cell Signaling Technology, USA). AlexaFluor 488 (1: 1000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA) was used as secondary antibodies. F-actin was stained with rhodamine-labelled phalloidin (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) for 30 minutes to visualize the cytoskeleton. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 minutes.

For immunofluorescent staining of rat tissue sections, the samples were first permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes. Subsequently, they were blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 minutes. Then, the sections were incubated with anti-YAP antibody (1:200; Cell Signaling Technology, USA) at 4 °C overnight. After that, they were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature. AlexaFluor 488 (1:500; Abcam, USA) was used as the secondary antibody. DAPI (0.5 mg/mL; Cell Signaling Technology, USA) was used to stain cell nuclei.

The collection and superimposition of images were processed by fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and ImageJ, respectively.

YAP and RUNX2 nuclear/cytoplasm ratio quantification

The cytoplasm and nucleus of PDLSCs were labelled using F-actin and DAPI staining, respectively. The nuclear/cytoplasm ratio of RUNX2 and YAP was calculated following established methods used by other researchers.29 This ratio was defined as the total fluorescence intensity in the nucleus (Inucleus) divided by the total fluorescence intensity in the remaining cellular compartment, weighted by the respective areas of the nucleus and the cytoplasm:

Ratio=(Inucleus/Anucleus)(Icell−Inucleus)/(Acell−Anucleus)where Anucleus is the nuclear area quantified by DAPI staining, Acellis the total cellular area demarcated by F-actin staining, and Icell is the total fluorescence intensity across the entire cell. Fluorescence intensities and cellular areas were quantified using Image J, with subsequent exclusion of images exhibiting fluorescence saturation.

Statistical Analysis

All the experiment data in our study were collected at least 3 replicates per condition. Statistical data were analysed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Values were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). PDLSCs spreading areas were analysed by 2-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and other multiple comparisons among the experimental groups were evaluated by 1-way ANOVA. Significance levels were determined as follows: (*) P < .05, (**) P < .01, (***) P < .001, and (ns) no significance.

Results

Characterization of the GelMA hydrogel

To simulate different stiffness substrates, we prepared soft/stiff GelMA hydrogels by tuning the concentration of GelMA macromers (ie, 8% and 12%) and inoculated PDLSCs onto them (Figure 1A). By adjusting the macromers concentration, we obtained substrates with stiffness values of 14.75 ± 2.77 kPa and 56.37 ± 6.82 kPa, respectively. Subsequently, we utilized SEM to characterize the nanocomposite of the soft and stiff GelMA hydrogels (Figure 1C) and observed that the stiff GelMA hydrogel exhibited smaller pores in comparison to soft materials (4.69 ± 0.62 μm vs. 9.55 ± 1.56 μm) (Figure 1D). The variation in hydrogel porosity indirectly reflects the mechanical properties of the material, aligning with the designed gradient of hydrogel stiffness. This confirms the successful fabrication of hydrogel substrates with varying stiffness, providing the necessary conditions for subsequent cell experiments.

In order to investigate whether PDLSCs can survive on GelMA hydrogel substrates, we employed live/dead staining to evaluate PDLSCs viability on 3 d of cell culture, examining the impact of matrix stiffness on cell activity. The results showed that there was no significant difference in cell viability, indicating that cell viability remained largely unchanged on GelMA hydrogels with different matrix stiffness (Figure 1E and 1F). Additionally, we also found that PDLSCs preferentially located in stiffer microenvironments. Compared with the soft matrix, cells adopted an elongated shape, and cells spreading areas on the stiff matrix were significantly larger at 12 hours, while no significant difference was observed at 24 hours (Figure 1G and 1H). The above findings indicated that the survival status of PDLSCs exhibits minimal differences on soft/stiff substrates, laying a foundation for further studies.

The effect of matrix stiffness on PDLSCs osteogenic differentiation

Substrate stiffness regulates osteogenesis in cells, which is related to nuclear localization of transcription factors.30 To investigate the effect of matrix stiffness on the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, we first evaluated the expression of RUNX2 and OCN in PDLSCs cultured for 14 d in osteoinduction medium. In the immunostaining results of RUNX2, the stiff substrate exhibited intense fluorescence, with RUNX2 predominantly localized in the nuclei and showing a prominent and concentrated pattern. In contrast, the soft matrix showed weak RUNX2 fluorescence mainly in the perinuclear region with a diffused pattern (Figure 2A). The quantification of RUNX2 nucleus/cytoplasm ratio also verified a more active transcriptional state of RUNX2 in the nuclei of cells on the stiff substrate (Figure 2B). In addition, as demonstrated by quantitative real-time PCR analysis and Western blot analysis, both RUNX2 and OCN displayed a higher expression level on stiff substrate (Figure 2C and 2D). These results suggested that OCN and RUNX2 of PDLSCs are upregulated both transcriptionally and translationally with the increase in substrate stiffness. To further verify the effect of matrix stiffness on osteogenic differentiation, we employed alizarin red staining to evaluate the ability of PDLSCs forming mineralized nodules on different stiffness substrates. The results showed that as matrix stiffness increased, the number and size of mineralized nodules in the cells also increased (Figure 2E). Quantitative analysis of the mineralized nodules using a microplate reader revealed a significant rise in nodule content with increasing matrix stiffness (Figure 2F). The immunofluorescence intensity of the phosphorylated ERK1/2 was considerably stronger in PDLSCs cultured on the stiff substrate (Figure 3A and 3B). The outcomes of Western blot analysis also manifested a significant increase in the p-ERK1/2 level in cells on stiff substrate (Figure 3C). To further elucidate the role of ERK1/2 in YAP nuclear translocation during the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, we subsequently blocked MAPK signalling pathway with ERK1/2 inhibitor (U0126) (Figure S1). In the cells treated with U0126, the nuclear fluorescence signal of YAP was remarkably diminished, with a relatively more pronounced fluorescent signal in the cytoplasmic region (Figure 3F). Blocking ERK1/2 substantially reduced the YAP nuclear/cytoplasm ratio (Figure 3G). This implies that ERK1/2 activation can promote the translocation of YAP into the nucleus. Additionally, we detected a decreased expression of OCN and RUNX2 following the blocking of ERK1/2 by U0126 (Figure 3H and 3I), suggesting that the downregulation of p-ERK1/2 may inhibit osteogenic differentiation by downregulating the nuclear translocation of YAP.

To investigate the role of YAP in periodontal osteogenesis, we inhibited YAP using verteporfin, a FDA-approved YAP-selective inhibitor, which has previously been demonstrated to downregulate YAP mRNA expression.33 In our study, immunostaining results showed that in contrast to the control group and the DMSO group (solvent control group), the LPS group (periodontitis induction group) and the verteporfin group had a lower expression of YAP and its nuclear translocation was remarkably inhibited (Figure 4C). The quantification of the extent of YAP nuclear translocation also showed the YAP nuclear translocation rates in the LPS group and the verteporfin group were consistent, substantially different from the control groups (Figure 4E). These results indicate that verteporfin treatment not only affects the total expression level of YAP but also inhibits its nuclear localization. Micro CT results showed that alveolar bone was resorted in both the LPS group and the verteporfin group (Figure 4D). Meanwhile, we evaluated the bone volume fraction (BV/TV) percentage and the trabecular bone number (Tb.N) value. Compared with the other 2 groups, the bone mass in the LPS group and the verteporfin group was significantly reduced (Figure 4F and 4G). Based on these data, it can be demonstrated that during periodontitis, the YAP nuclear translocation is inhibited, which in turn leads to alveolar bone resorption.

In line with the aforementioned in vitro experimental results, we found that a stiffer substrate can promote YAP nuclear translocation through the activation of ERK1/2 phosphorylation, thereby enhancing osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs. In contrast, on a softer substrate, the phosphorylation level of ERK1/2 in PDLSCs is lower, which fails to effectively inhibit YAP nuclear translocation, resulting in a low osteogenic differentiation state (Figure 5). Discussion

Our research demonstrates that ERK-mediated YAP nuclear translocation is pivotal in regulating osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs in response to increased stiffness. The null hypotheses were rejected. We observed a significant enhancement in PDLSCs osteogenesis on substrates with higher stiffness. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we specifically examined the interplay between ERK1/2 signalling and YAP nuclear translocation, establishing the critical role of the ERK/YAP signalling axis in stiffness-induced osteogenic differentiation. Despite advancements in treatment modalities, the restoration of periodontium damaged by periodontitis remains a substantial clinical challenge. Our findings offer novel insights into the biophysical regulation of stem cell behaviour, potentially informing the development of advanced stem cell-based therapeutic strategies for periodontitis.

Periodontitis frequently results in the resorption of alveolar bone. In our study, we observed a significant decrease in periodontal ligament stiffness during periodontitis, aligning with previous findings.34,35 The stiffness gradient at soft-hard tissue interfaces is diminished under inflammatory conditions compared to healthy tissues, primarily due to a reduced mineral content gradient.34 This reduction in stiffness may also be attributed to the degradation of extracellular matrix collagen, driven by the sustained release of biochemical factors such as enzymes, cytokines, and chemokines.36 These factors promote protease-mediated collagen degradation and relaxation, leading to alterations in integrin-dependent mechanotransduction and a subsequent decrease in cellular actomyosin contractility (outside-in signalling). This cascade reduces extracellular matrix-directed cellular traction forces (inside-out signalling), ultimately resulting in abnormal collagen fibre formation and further compromising matrix stiffness.37 Given the strong association between substrate stiffness and cellular osteogenic differentiation,16 the reduced stiffness of the periodontal ligament matrix in periodontitis is a critical factor contributing to alveolar bone defects. Notably, beyond osteogenic differentiation, matrix stiffness may also regulate the commitment of PDLSCs to various lineages, including osteoblasts, fibroblasts, and cementoblasts associated with periodontal tissues. For instance, softer matrix was found to promote fibroblastic differentiation of PDLSCs and enhance collagen fibre alignment.38 These findings suggest that differential matrix stiffness can modulate distinct aspects of PDLSCs differentiation, which may serve as a critical determinant for periodontal tissue regeneration.

ERK has been identified as a positive regulator of osteoblast differentiation and bone formation27,39 Current researches attribute matrix stiffness-induced MSC osteogenesis to elevated phosphorylation level of ERK1/2.40,41 Consistent with these findings, our results demonstrated that increased substrate stiffness promotes the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs via ERK1/2 activation. Inhibition of ERK1/2 by U0126 led to decreased expression of osteogenic markers RUNX2 and OCN, underscoring the pivotal role of ERK1/2 in PDLSC osteogenesis. However, the role of the ERK signalling pathway in osteoblastic differentiation remains controversial and appears to be context-dependent, varying across experimental systems.42 Continuous ERK activation, cross-talk with other signalling pathways, and variations in cell types and mechanical stimuli can yield divergent outcomes. For instance, Kim et al revealed a dichotomy in ERK function, demonstrating that while ERK activation promotes early osteoblast precursor differentiation, it inhibits bone formation in committed osteoblasts through the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway by suppressing the secretion of pro-angiogenic and osteogenic factors necessary for optimal bone microenvironment formation.43 Inhibition of the ERK1/2 signalling pathway has been shown to block osteogenic differentiation of human adipose-derived stem cells44 and hMSCs.45 Conversely, ERK pathway inhibition has been reported to enhance ALP activity and mineralization in BMP4-treated muscle-derived stem cells and BMP2-treated C2C12 and MC3T3-E1 cells.42,46 Under tensile stress, ERK phosphorylation suppresses BMP4-induced adipogenesis in MSCs, suggesting that mechanical stretching may influence MSCs osteogenic differentiation.47,48 In contrast, under compressive stress, ERK pathway activation is more closely associated with chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs and chondrocyte function.48,49 Wu et al further demonstrated that ERK signalling is more robustly activated under compressive stress compared to tensile stress,48 highlighting the complex mechanosensitive nature of ERK-mediated signalling in stem cell fate determination.

Generally, elevated nuclear localization of YAP is favourable for promoting osteogenesis.50 ERK and YAP are often co-activated during cellular signal transduction processes. Recent studies have elucidated the pivotal roles of both ERK and YAP in mechanotransduction, where they contribute to the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, and, in some cases, tumorigenesis.51 Additionally, evidence suggests a functional interplay between the ERK and Hippo signalling pathways, with crosstalk between ERK and YAP influencing various cellular functions. Jia et al demonstrated that activated YAP enhances proliferation, accelerates the cell cycle progression, inhibits apoptosis, and delays senescence in PDLSCs. Moreover, their findings indicated that the Hippo-YAP signalling pathway modulates ERK activity.52 In the context of ultraviolet B-induced skin injury, fibroblast growth factor 10-mediated activation of ERK led to subsequent YAP activation, with both pathways collaboratively regulating epidermal cell functions.53 However, the precise relationship between ERK and YAP during osteogenic differentiation in periodontium remains incompletely understood. In our study, we confirm the hypothesis that increased substrate stiffness promotes PDLSCs osteogenesis by upregulating ERK1/2 activity, which subsequently facilitates YAP nuclear translocation. Inhibition of ERK1/2 significantly reduced the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio of YAP, indicating that ERK activation is essential for YAP nuclear translocation. Furthermore, our in vivo experiments demonstrated that inhibiting YAP nuclear translocation results in pronounced bone resorption. Collectively, these findings confirm that substrate stiffness enhances PDLSCs osteogenesis through ERK-mediated YAP nuclear translocation, providing valuable mechanistic insights into the regulation of periodontal regeneration.

The periodontal inflammatory microenvironment induces matrix degradation, leading to reduced stiffness of the periodontal ligament as demonstrated by our experimental periodontitis model where stiffness decreased from approximately 60 kPa in healthy tissue to approximately 20 kPa in diseased states. This biomechanical deterioration represents more than just a passive consequence of inflammation-it actively drives disease progression by disrupting critical mechanotransduction pathways. Reduced stiffness impeded osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs by disrupting ERK-mediated YAP nuclear translocation, establishing a vicious cycle that accelerated progressive alveolar bone resorption. This mechanobiological impairment explains the clinical manifestation of tooth mobility resulting from diminished mechanical support due to stiffness reduction. These insights open new therapeutic possibilities, including the development of stiffness-matched biomaterials in the 50-70 kPa range that could potentially restore physiological mechanosignaling, as well as pharmacological approaches targeting key mechanotransduction components like ERK and YAP. While these findings significantly advance our understanding of the role of biomechanics in periodontitis, important questions remain regarding the temporal relationship between stiffness changes and inflammatory bone loss, as well as potential cell population-specific responses to biophysical cues, which warrant further investigation through longitudinal studies and single-cell analyses.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, it is important to note that, cells in vivo exist within a complex 3-dimensional (3D) microenvironment, which differs substantially from the 2-dimensional (2D) culture systems traditionally employed in vitro. While this study utilized 2D culture models, certain cellular behaviours and mechanotransduction responses may vary under 3D conditions.54 Future research should focus on developing biocompatible 3D culture models to better mimic the native extracellular matrix (ECM) and further elucidate the influence of substrate stiffness on PDLSCs osteogenesis. Second, the role of ERK/YAP signalling in stiffness-mediated differentiation within a 3D microenvironment warrants comprehensive investigation. Besides, the rat periodontitis model may not capture all facets of human disease. Future studies should employ human-derived samples to validate these findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that substrate stiffness promotes osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs through ERK-mediated YAP nuclear translocation. Our results establish the ERK/YAP signalling axis as a crucial mechanotransduction pathway governing stiffness-induced PDLSCs differentiation. These findings provide important mechanistic insights into how biophysical cues regulate stem cell behaviour at the molecular level. Importantly, our work suggests that precise modulation of ECM stiffness combined with targeting the ERK/YAP pathway could offer new strategies for controlling stem cell fate. These discoveries may pave the way for developing innovative regenerative approaches for treating periodontitis and other periodontal diseases.
