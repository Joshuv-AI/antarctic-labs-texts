Quality of adverse drug reaction reports submitted to the national pharmacovigilance center of Nepal: a pre- and post-training evaluation
Authors: Sushil Nepal, Shyam Shah, Narayan Prasad Dhakal, Umanga Tripathee, Santusta Adhikari, Sunil Shrestha, Birna Trap, Tamara Hafner, Andy Stergachis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPharmacovigilance (PV) plays a critical role in monitoring and evaluating adverse drug reactions (ADRs) to ensure the safety of marketed medical products. This study aimed to assess the quality of ADR reports submitted to the national PV center of Nepal by comparing pre- and post-training periods, and to identify factors associated with ADR reporting quality.MethodsA retrospective analysis of ADR reports submitted to the national PV center was conducted over a three year period. Reports from January 2020 to November 2022 were classified as the pre-training phase while reports from December 2022 to April 2023 consitituted the post-training phase. In December 2022, the research team developed and implemented a capacity building plan that included a two-day training program for regional PV center contact persons, consisting of pharmacists and other health care professionals (HCPs) responsible for submitting ADR reorts through the VigiFlow system. The quality of ADR reports was assessed using the validated Adverse Drug Report Quality Algorithm (AQUA-12) tool. Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel and R.ResultsA total of 86 reports were evaluated, 57 from the pre-training phase and 29 from the post-training phase. Overall, 35% (n = 30) of the reports were classified as high quality according to the AQUA-12 criteria. Prior to the training, only 16% (n = 9) met the high quality level whereas this proportion increased to 72% (n = 21) following the training. This improvement in ADR reporting quality was statistically significant (p = 0.000; p < 0.05).ConclusionThe training significantly improved the quality of ADR reports, underscoring the value of continuous quality improvement within PV systems. Targeted programs for health care professionals, particularly pharmacists and regulatory authority personnel, are essential to strengthen ADR reporting quality and enhancing patient safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09466-3.Keywords: Quality, Adverse drug reactions, Pharmacovigilance, Nepal

Introduction

Pharmacovigilance (PV) is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as “the science and activities related to the detection, evaluation, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related safety problem” [1]. High quality adverse drug reaction (ADR) reports are fundamental for safeguarding public health, as they enable the identification, detection, and assessment of the risks of medical products [2]. Once medical products enter the market, continuous monitoring of their safety throughout their life-course is essential to ensure that risks are promptly recognized and appropriately managed and communicated.

In Nepal, spontaneous reporting (SRs) of ADRs to the national PV center is the primary mechanism for identifying and managing medicines-related risks. Healthcare providers voluntarily submit suspected ADR case reports to regional PV centers, which are subsequently forwarded to the Department of Drug Administration (DDA), the national medicines regulatory authority of Nepal. This approach represented a widely used method of post-marketing surveillance worldwide [3]. Spontaneous reports provide essential information to support regulatory decision-making, guide safety communications, and inform regulatory actions. SRs are relatively easy to implement and enable routine PV at low cost [4, 5]. However, SR systems are commonly affected by underreporting and variable report quality, including incomplete or missing information.

Pharmacovigilance activities in Nepal began in 2004 when the country became a member of the WHO International Drug Monitoring Program (IDMP), initially as an associate member then full member in 2006. The DDA, under the Ministry of Health and Population, serves as Nepal’s National Pharmacovigilance Center (NPC). The NPC operates within the Management, Planning, and Coordination Division, DDA. Although the Drug Act of Nepal 1978, which governs medicines, does not explicitly include provisions for PV, its preamble indirectly addresses issues related to drug safety.

In Nepal, notifiers submit paper-based SRs to regional PV centers, where each report is reviewed and evaluated. If necessary, PV centers may ask notifiers to provide additional information. Verified reports are entered into VigiFlow, which transmits them to the NPC [6]. NPC checks all reports for completeness and causality before forwarding them to the WHO Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC). The UMC ultimately issues safety alerts to the DDA through newsletters, drug bulletins, and regulatory recommendations, as shown in the Fig. 1 [6].

An assessment of Nepal’s PV system using the Indicator-Based Pharmacovigilance Assessment Tool (IPAT) [7] in January 2021 identified critical gaps in Nepal’s PV system [8]. These included underreporting, suboptimal ADR report quality, absence of a national safety and advisory committee, inadequate functioning of national and regional PV centers, limited resources, insufficient awareness of PV among health care professionals (HCPs), inadequate legislation and guidance documents, and need for enhanced coordination and stakeholder engagement [8, 9]. Similarly, the WHO Global Benchmarking Tool Assessment report found Nepal’s PV system maturity to be suboptimal [10]. These findings underscore the need for a robust national PV program to increase ADR reporting and enhance report quality.

In 2014, data from the UMC indicated that only 13% of SRs globally had a satisfactory level of completion of information [11]. A 2022 report on PV in Nepal introduced revised standard operating procedures for reporting ADRs into the VigiFlow system. These updated guidelines were distributed to regional PV centers to enhance the quality and accuracy of the reports [12]. Regulatory agencies have recognized the importance of quality management systems in PV, with completeness of SRs being a critical quality parameter [13]. Also, according to Good Pharmacovigilance Practices, a valid SR of an ADR should include identifiable information about the notifier and the user, suspicious drugs, suspected ADRs, and additional details such as medical conditions, comorbidities, concomitant medications, clinical progression of the patient, therapy administered for the ADR, complementary diagnostic measures, and responses to drug suspension and reintroduction [14, 15]. Incomplete ADR reports are reported due to the challenges in ADR reporting, such as underreporting, reporting bias, and difficulties in causality assessment, weakening PV system’s ability to prevent harm [16]. When ADRs are underreported, the true incidence and severity of adverse effects are not evident, which leads to overestimation of drug safety and inappropriate continued use [17]. To date, no published studies have systematically assessed the quality of ADR reports submitted in Nepal.

This study aimed to assess the quality of ADR reports submitted to the national PV center of Nepal by comparing pre- and post-training periods, and to identify factors associated with ADR reporting quality.

Methodology

The United States Agency for International Development (USAID) Medicines, Technologies, and Pharmaceutical Services (MTaPS) Program collaborated with DDA to conduct a retrospective cross-sectional study using the Adverse Drug Reactions Report Quality Algorithm (AQUA-12) tool to evaluate the quality of ADR reports that the DDA received before and after a training.

Training assessment

To improve the quality and quantity of ADR reports, MTaPS supported the DDA in conducting a comprehensive PV training program from December 3–4, 2022. Previously, the WHO and the DDA had conducted annual one-day orientation sessions for regional PV centers; however, these sessions primarily focused on reporting procedures and did not emphasize report quality. In collaboration with WHO and the DDA, MTaPS revised the annual training plan and developed a targeted capacity-building strategy aimed at strengthening the quality of ADR reporting. A total of 60 participants attended the training, including ADR notifiers from national and regional PV centers as well as planned regional PV centers responsible for submitting reports through the VigiFlow system. All participants were healthcare professionals actively involved in ADR reporting, comprising pharmacists, medical doctors, nursing officers, and a statistics officer.

The training intervention consisted of 16 h delivered over two days through interactive sessions that combined lectures, group discussions, real-time case studies, and hands-on practice with ADR reporting tools. Training materials, developed by team members AS, SN and SS, covered key topics including an introduction to PV, the PV in Nepal, reporting guidelines and SOPs, causality assessment, signal detection, risk management, and safety communication. Additional training sessions for NPC staff focused on report completeness checks, causality assessment, and signal detection.

Data collection

Every ADR report submitted to NPC was included in the assessment, regardless of quality or completeness but duplicate reporting were removed for analysis. All SRs sent to the national PV center included at least one suspected drug as identified by the notifier, so all ADR reports the DDA received during the study time met the inclusion criteria for the study. Researchers categorized ADR reports received from January 1 to November 30, 2022, as pre-training data, and those obtained from December 5, 2022, to April 1, 2023, as post-training data. Researchers excluded the reports submitted during the training period from December 3 to 4, 2022. Consequently, the study relied entirely on secondary data, that is, 86 ADR reports that the DDA received between January 2022 and April 2023.

AQUA-12 quality assessment tool

The ADR reporting form used by regional PV centers contained four basic elements: patient information, suspected adverse reactions, suspected medication, and notifier information. These forms were not modified for the study rather, mandatory fields were marked with an asterisk (*) in the revised ADR reporting form to ensure their consistent completion. However, after receiving an ADR report from the regional centers, the national PV center’s focal person routinely followed up with reporters to obtain additional details, as necessary. The completed reports were then updated in VigiFlow, which served as the data source for this assessment. Permission to use the AQUA-12 tool (version 2, 16.08.21) was obtained from its original authors prior to study implementation. AQUA-12 was developed and validated by members of the Adverse Drug Reaction Review Committee (ADRRC) at Alfred Health, Melbourne, Australia, and is designed to evaluate the quality of spontaneous ADR reports (Supplementary file) [18]. Although VigiGRADE is a WHO-endorsed and validated tool for assessing the completeness of individual case safety reports (ICSRs) [19], it is primarily limited to calculating completeness scores and does not capture dimensions essential for identifying training needs. Our study aimed to assess reporting gaps and areas for improvement to generate higher-quality ADR reports and evaluate the effects of the training. For this purpose, AQUA-12 was considered more suitable, as it includes additional components such as reporter details, clinical history, outcome of ADRs, and severity classification.

The total quality score ranges from 0 to 12. Reports scoring 10–12 were categorized as high quality, while those scoring 1–9 were categorized as low quality.

A total of 86 ADR reports were assessed. Scoring was conducted independently by two authors (SN and ShS). Disagreements were resolved through discussion between the two assessors, with escalation to the national PV contact person planned if consensus could not be reached; however, all discrepancies were resolved between the two authors.

Data variables and outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was the proportion of high-quality reports, defined as those scoring 10 to 12 on the AQUA-12 tool. In addition, the study examined factors potentially associated with report quality, encompassing both high- and low-quality reports. Data variables were extracted from the DDA’s ADR report database. They included the notifier’s profession, the treating clinical unit (hospital, medical college, clinic, nursing home, or palliative care facility), the type of reaction observed, the severity of the reaction (serious, non-serious, or severe), and the suspected medication class or classes.

Data analysis

Summary statistics for categorical variables are presented as counts and proportions. Descriptive analysis were conducted using Micorsoft Excel, while inferential statistics were performed in using R. Categorical variables were compared using using the Chi-squared test when expected cell counts were sufficient. For analyses involving small sample sizes or expected frequencies of fewer than 5, Fisher’s exact test was used to ensure statistical accuracy. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

The study did not involve or use human subjects or identifiable personal data, human tissue, or animals. The study involved the secondary data analysis without any personally identifiable information obtained from the VigiFlow System after being forwarded to the Uppsala Monitoring Center. The study was approved by the Ethical Review Board of Nepal. Approval number 526_2022 P, obtained in 2022, covered the protocol for the training of the PV contact persons. Approval 618_2024, obtained in 2024, cleared the restropective study protocol and approved access to the VigiFlow data.

Significant differences were observed in notifier types, with a higher proportion of reports submitted by physicians during the pre-training period. Minimal differences were noted between the pre- and post-training periods for variables related to severity, outcome, and the gender of the notifier. Approximately two-thirds of the training participants submitted ADR reports following the training. Additionally, two planned regional centers began reporting to the NPC after the training.

Discussion

This study evaluated the quality and completeness of ADR reports received by the NPC, Nepal, between January 2020 and April 2023, comparing data collected before and after HCP training. The training sessions targeted HCPs, primarily pharmacists, as well as staff from regional PV centers and disease control programs, all of whom are responsible for reporting ADRs into the VigiFlow system. Of the 60 participants, 55 were pharmacists, two were nursing officers, and three were medical officers. The training focused on enhancing the identification, collection, recording, and reporting of ADRs.

This study observed a notable improvement in the quality of ADR reports following the training, with a higher proportion of high-quality reports in the post-training period compared to the pre-training period. Specifically, the proportion of high-quality reports increased 4.5-fold, from 16% before the training to 72% after the training, demonstrating the impact of the multifaceted training or intervention, as supported by previous literature on practice variations, quality indicators, and education-focused strategies [20]. This improvement highlights the effectiveness of the training and changes implemented in the reporting process, potentially enhancing the completeness, accuracy, and overall reliability of ADR reports. The observed increase in high-quality reports underscores the importance of continuous quality improvement efforts in PV systems to ensure timely and accurate detection of adverse reactions to medicinal products.

Furthermore, this study’s analysis identified differences in report quality by notifier type. This finding highlights the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration and the active involvement of pharmacists in PV activities. Pharmacists now play a vital role in patient-centered medical facilities, particularly in managing chronic diseases, and are valued members of primary care teams rather than conventional dispensers. This direct involvement in patient care makes them valuable contributors to ADR reporting, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions to enhance reporting practices among HCPs [21–23]. Most of the trainees were hospital pharmacists, with a smaller number being physicians responsible for reporting ADR into the VigiFlow system. Continuous education and training are essential to maintain and improve the quality of ADR reports from all HCPs [23–26]. Even a 15-minute video tutorial can significantly improve the quality of ADR reports [27]. However, the long-term effects of such training remain uncertain, necessitating more frequent training, ideally at no more than 12 months, as per the findings from a recent meta-analysis on health behavior change after the training interventions [28]. For sustainable interventions in low- and middle-income countries like Nepal, multipronged approaches are crucial for improving health worker performance, as they effectively address the complexity of health worker practices better than single interventions like disseminating guidelines and training alone [29].

Regarding ADR outcomes, high-quality reports were more likely to indicate recovery or resolution than low-quality reports. This finding underscores the importance of capturing comprehensive clinical information in ADR reports, including patient outcomes, to facilitate accurate assessment and interpretation of adverse reactions. Only high-quality reports can undergo causality assessment and be used for risk management. Enhanced reporting practices that capture the occurrence of ADRs and their clinical impact and outcomes are crucial for informing regulatory decisions, guiding clinical management, and improving patient care [30].

While the observed improvements in report quality are encouraging, this study also highlights areas that require further enhancement. Despite the overall upward trend, most reports still fell within the the moderate or poor-quality categories. Addressing factors that contribute to lower-quality reports, such as incomplete data entry and inadequate reporting practices, remains a key priority for strengthening PV efforts. Proactive actions by regional PV centers can reduce medication-related risks, improve patients’ quality of life, prevent iatrogenic conditions, and minimize healthcare expenditures [31]. Additional strategies to enhance reporting quality may include targeted training programs for reporters, implementation of standardized reporting tools, and ongoing quality assurance mechanisms to monitor and evaluate reporting performance over time. Providing regular feedback to reporters through newsletters, awareness campaigns, and educational materials can further promote engagement and foster a culture of high-quality reporting [30].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is that it is the first to analyze the quality of spontaneous ADR reports in Nepal. It underscores the imperative for ongoing training, capacity building, and follow-up trainings to improve both the quality and quantity of ADR reports in the country.

The study has several limitations. The sample size was small, there was no control group, and the follow-up period was limited to four months due to project timelines and the number of available ADR reports. Consequently, the ability to observe longer-term trends and effects is limited. Additionally, other factors potentially influencing report quality could not be assessed due to the use of secondary data. Future research with larger samples, longer follow-up periods, and possibly a control group is needed to provide more conclusive evidence. To ensure generalizability, future studies should include disaggregated data and sufficient sample sizes for robust statistical testing.

Implications

Integrating ADR reporting into the daily practices of HCPs requires time and sustained effort. Currently, the collection, assessment, and reporting of ADRs remain suboptimal, which may compromise medication safety. This suboptimal quality is partly due to limited teaching of basic PV principles in university curricula and insufficient opportunities to apply ADR concepts in practice. Additionally, existing curricula in Nepal do not emphasize the importance of reporting quality.

Challenges to effective reporting include unfamiliarity with Good Pharmacovigilance Practice guidelines, cumbersome reporting procedures, limited understanding of report completeness, and the absence of a fully functional national PV system.

Several strategies can help foster good reporting practices. First, it is essential to identify HCPs involved in ADR management. The national medicines regulatory authority should ensure that these HCPs receive training to enhance their knowledge and skills in recognizing ADR syndromes, notifying relevant authorities, suggesting appropriate investigations, reporting ADRs, and conducting causality assessments to identify suspected medications. Furthermore, developing a nonmandatory e-learning platform on ADR reporting can enhance PV literacy among HCPs [32]. Continuous improvements can also be supported by acknowledging notifiers with feedback, disseminating information through newsletters, raising medication safety awareness via posters, pamphlets, and campaigns, and instituting mandatory reporting of serious ADRs [30]. These strategies are crucial for ensuring sustainable improvements in the quality and completeness of ADR reporting.

Conclusion

The training significantly improved the quality of ADR reports, underscoring the value of continuous quality improvement within PV systems. Physicians and pharmacists are the major contributors to ADR reports in Nepal, as most reports originate from regional PV centers. Targeted continuing education and training programs for physicians, pharmacists, nurses, and other HCPs and regulatory authority personnel, are essential to strengthen ADR reporting quality and enhancing patient safety.
