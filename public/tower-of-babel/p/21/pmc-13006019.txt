Reassessing the Leadership Principles of the Duke Healthcare Leadership Model for Use in Undergraduate and Graduate Medical Education
Authors: Nick Tsipis, Steve Cook, Joe Doty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntentional leadership development core curriculum continues to be absent in both undergraduate and graduate medical education – as opposed to elective courses and voluntary experiences. To help address this gap, a leadership model was developed from 2014 to 2017, to help focus educators on specific skills necessary for healthcare professionals to learn and develop their own leadership skills. As this original model was dated, and in the spirit of continuous improvement, we set out to update and improve on the original leadership model. The methods included surveys and focus groups with a focus on improving the original model. The result was an updated model with a new title which should better help educators and learners focus on specific skills needed for leaders in healthcare.Keywords: leadership, education, medicine, healthcare

Background

The original version of the Healthcare Leadership Model (HLM), Figure 1, was developed by a group of medical students, residents and fellows to promote the importance of a competency-based leadership model for healthcare education. Our research at that time concluded that there was a paucity of intentional leadership development curricula in both undergraduate (UME) and graduate medical education (GME).1 For example, while both the National Center for Healthcare Leadership and the American Association of Medical Schools (AAMC) offer information, programs and talk to the importance of leadership competencies in UME and GME, robust and intentional curricula have yet to matriculate into our medical schools. Elective offerings abound, but robust and required curricula are rare. Or, as one faculty member noted, “Yes, this stuff is important in healthcare, but it’s not how we were trained and we don’t have time for it”.

Original Model.

The original model (Figure 1) was created between 2014 and 2017 through an extensive literature review, focus groups of faculty and trainees, and a mixture of qualitative and quantitative analysis. The attained goal was to synthesize the core skills/competencies necessary trainees to be better leaders – primarily in a healthcare setting.

The definition of leadership the group used in order to focus the research was “the ability to influence others for the benefit of patients and patient populations”. Since publication, this definition and the model have been used as a framework to enhance education and patient care throughout educational medical communities. The model recognizes that there is a need for medical trainees at all stages to receive education that equips them with the skills and tools necessary to address professional leadership challenges – all of which can effect patient care. The model is based on the core principle of Patient Centeredness and competencies of Emotional Intelligence, Critical Thinking, Integrity, Selfless Service and Teamwork2 (Figure 1).

As the field of leadership curricular development in medical education has expanded and advanced,3 members of the Feagin Leadership Program4 were tasked with reassessing the original model’s current validity and use in a medical education setting. In order to do this, the team began by reviewing the model’s original development processes for insight into its intended functionality. We then collected survey responses from a representative sample across medical departments, the School of Medicine (SOM), the School of Nursing, and a Physician Assistant Program to elicit the model’s current and future applicability.

The research team solicited input from medical school students, residents, fellows, nursing students, physician assistant (PA) students and faculty. Departments included both surgical and non-surgical specialties. Input was also received from volunteer focus groups (total was more than 60 respondents) which included students, residents and faculty, across all specialties.

The study was deemed exempt from full review by the Duke University Institutional Review Board (IRB) after it was determined that all respondents were anonymous – no identifying information. Anonymous surveys and focus groups with no personal identifying information are historically exempt from full review. Participants were all voluntary and informed consent was not required as all responses were anonymous and there was no risk to participants.

Results

The team received 429 responses to the standardized survey. Only descriptive/categorical data was used due to the small sample size (by category – students, resident, etc) and the nature of the survey items. This data reflected that the model was well established primarily with the medical school.

- 67.8% of all survey respondents were aware of the model in the medical education setting

- 98% of medical school students (to include nursing students and PA students)

- 54.5% of residents

- 12.5% of fellows

When asked to rank the HLM competencies based on individual values, survey respondents ranked Integrity highest and Selfless Service lowest. When asked to rank current HLM competencies based on their department’s practices, participants ranked Critical Thinking highest and Emotional Intelligence lowest. These results were consistent across each respondent category (student, resident, faculty) and we report the aggregated results due to very small samples from nursing and PA students and faculty.

Aggregated results of ranked competencies based on individual values were:

1. Integrity

2. Emotional Intelligence

3. Teamwork

4. Critical Thinking

5. Selfless Service

Aggregated results of ranked competencies based on departmental practices were:

1. Critical Thinking

2. Teamwork

3. Integrity

4. Selfless Service

5. Emotional Intelligence

Aggregated results of ranked competencies based on challenges to day-to-day life:

1. Selfless Service

2. Emotional Intelligence

3. Teamwork

4. Critical Thinking

5. Integrity

When asked what competencies are missing from the current HLM model (Figure 1), the top responses were Communication, Humility,5 Self-care, Diversity, Equity and Inclusion. These are some of the same competencies that surfaced and were discussed as part of the original leadership model development research.

At the aggregate level, of the participants who were aware of the model, 63.3% felt Selfless Service was most difficult to execute on a day-to-day basis. Lastly, 78.9% of participants felt leadership education programs were very important (42.1%) or extremely important (36.7%) in medical education.

Discussion

Leadership models specifically in healthcare settings are rare. The Uniformed Services of the Health Sciences (USUHS) uses a Leader-Follower Framework (LF2) centered around Character, Competence, Context and Communication.6. Also, there is a Medical Leadership Competency Framework (MLCF) from the National Health Service in the United Kingdom.1 We discovered early in our research and teaching efforts that leadership models from business or the military did not resonate with medical students and healthcare professionals.

Based on the interpretation of survey results,7 our team elected to focus on the following objectives as they pertain to the HLM:

1) to recommend adjustments to the model and,

2) to increase the knowledge and implementation of the model within GME.

These objectives allowed us to increase the model’s applicability within the medical education community and focus efforts on developing leadership skills of medical trainees at the early stages of their residency training. To do this, we would specifically target exposure and implementation within the Graduate Medical Education by incorporating educational sessions on the revised model (Figure 2) during intern orientation using a short evidence-based curriculum. This would allow us to evaluate the revised model’s use and applicability in the future to determine if the existing model fits with resident views of important leadership core competencies.

New Model – Educational Framework.

Recommendations for the Healthcare Leadership Model

Based on the survey results and input from respondents,7 our team recommended three modifications to the HLM (see Figure 1).

Modified Title: Healthcare Leadership Model → Healthcare Leadership Model: An Educational Framework –Figure 1

The original title of the Healthcare Leadership Model was refined to include the notion that it exists as an educational framework. UME and GME are robust in their leadership initiatives and there are other leadership development efforts existing in parallel across other health systems and medical schools. In an effort to clarify how the new HLM fits in with this larger context, it was important to add this clarifying scope in text. This improved specificity may also help faculty understand how the competencies in the model can be integrated and translated into other curricular components, eg, using the skills of emotional intelligence when taking a patient history.

Modified Competencies: Selfless Service → Service

As became clear in the survey and talking to respondents and students, selfless service is one of the most challenging competencies to embody day in and day out. Many students and trainees felt this was at odds in some fashion with a growing body of literature on physician burnout. Being completely selfless, if that means not taking care of one’s own needs (physically, emotionally, spiritually), results in the burnout challenges that healthcare professionals are constantly experiencing. Therefore, in the spirit of emphasizing a culture of wellness and balance, we removed the word selfless from the model and just emphasized service.

Modified Definitions: Emotional Intelligence (EI)

Prior Definition

The ability to recognize and understand thinking and emotions in yourself and others, and to use this awareness to effectively manage your behavior and relationships.

Survey respondents reported EI was very important to them while also acknowledging that it is difficult to practice on a day-to-day basis. We recommended expanding the definition of EI to allow for more learning and discussion around this sometimes nebulous and misunderstood construct. After researching EIs role in healthcare,8 we proposed the following definition:

Proposed Definition

1. Emotional Intelligence Developing Self-Awareness: Being aware of one’s own values, principles, and assumptions and how they interplay within the team dynamic.

2. Cultivating Personal Resilience: Ability to cope with and contextualize demanding situations.

3. Maintaining Personal Balance: Prioritizing activities to maintain mental and physical health as well as cultivate a positive outlook.

4. Cultural Humility: The ability to maintain an interpersonal stance that is other-oriented in relation to aspects of cultural identity that are most important to the person.

When viewed holistically, this board definition, which includes the words Emotional Intelligence, incapsulates and provides more practicality than the standard components of emotional intelligence – self awareness, self management, social awareness, and relationship management.9 We also recommended including an explicit statement defining cultural humility (see above) after survey responses to the question, “What is missing from the model?” reflected a desire to reference humility, respect, inclusivity, and diversity. Including cultural humility in the definition seems to address this gap. Note also that the definitions also explicitly discuss the importance of resilience – “Cultivating Personal Resilience” and “Maintaining Personal Balance”. Additionally, resilience and humility can be by-products of self-awareness and self-management.

Unchanged Competency Definitions

1. Critical Thinking: using all available sources of knowledge to create appropriate and effective solutions while being aware of internal and external biases.

2. Integrity: Being honest and acting in accordance with strong moral principles in one’s personal and professional life.

3. Teamwork: collaborating and maximizing one’s role to enhance team performance and cohesion.

Limitations to our study include the subjective understanding and meaning of all the leadership competencies in the study. Each respondent could have a different definition of each of the competencies. Also, respondents only came from one medical school which can challenge the generalizable of the results.

Conclusion and Future Directions

The initial research and development of the HLM and subsequent integration into a medical education community provided a first step towards understanding best practices in leadership curricula in medical education. As interest in including leadership development curriculum into undergraduate and graduate medical education continues to gain momentum, we hope our research and model will contribute to these efforts.10 And finally, our team was sensitive to changes in perceptions, experiences, culture and trends in medical education and healthcare which informed our desire to ensure the model was current and most effective in addressing the education needs of both faculty and learners.
