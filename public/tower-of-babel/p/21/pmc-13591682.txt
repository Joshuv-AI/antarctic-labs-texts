Association of serum uric acid levels with cognitive function in the hypertensive population: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Qingling Peng, Chao Yu, Wei Zhou, Peixu Zhao, Xiaoli Song, Xinyi Wang, Congcong Yan, Tao Wang, Lingjuan Zhu, Xiaoshu Cheng, Huihui Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association between serum uric acid (SUA) and cognitive function is controversial in previous studies. Hypertension group are prone to cognitive decline. We aimed to investigate the correlation between serum uric acid levels and cognitive function among Chinese hypertensive patients using the Chinese adapted version of Mini-Mental State Examination (CAMSE).MethodsWe included 9,471 subjects from the China Hypertension Registry study. Serum uric acid was measured using biochemical analysis instruments. The CAMSE assessment was performed to evaluate the cognitive function. Participants with different educational backgrounds used different CAMSE score cutoffs to define cognitive impairment (CI): < 24 for participants with junior high school education or above (≥ 7 years of schooling), < 20 for those with primary school education (1–6 years), and < 17 for illiterate participants.ResultsWe enrolled 9,471 hypertensive participants (mean age 63.6 ± 9.8 years; 47.9% males, n = 4540), with mean CAMSE score22.1 ± 6.4 and SUA 428.6 ± 121.1 µmol/L. Baseline characteristics differed across SUA quartiles (all P < 0.05). Multivariable and RCS analyses showed SUA was associated with CAMSE score and CI. Fully adjusted, each one-unit lnSUA increase correlated with 0.47 higher CAMSE score (95% CI: 0.02, 0.92) and 28% lower CI (OR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.58, 0.90). Q1 vs. Q2: 0.35 lower CAMSE score (95% CI: -0.65, -0.05) and 24% higher CI (OR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.08, 1.43); Q3/Q4 vs. Q2 had no differences. A SUA threshold of 445.9 µmol/L was identified: below it, each lgSUA increase reduced CI by 57% (OR: 0.43; 95% CI: 0.27, 0.69). Subgroup analyses showed no interactions (all P> corrected α).ConclusionThere is a threshold effect of SUA level on CAMSE score and cognitive impairment in Chinese hypertensive patients. In participants with SUA < 445.9 µmol /L, decreased serum uric acid level was independently associated with cognitive decline.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05044-8.

Introduction

The global incidence and prevalence of dementia are on the rise [1], with a projected 114 million individuals expected to be affected by dementia in 2050 [2]. In China, out of the 249 million elderly individuals aged 60 years and above, an estimated 15.07 million are living with dementia [3]. A recent study conducted in the United States reveals a staggering increase of over 145% in deaths caused by Alzheimer’s disease within the past decade, potentially reaching a total of 13.8 million cases by 2060 [4]. Dementia poses significant cognitive impairments and imposes a substantial economic burden on society [5]. However, existing treatments aimed at enhancing cognition or slowing down disease progression exhibit only slight efficacy [6, 7].

Cognitive impairment can be regarded as an indicator of the preclinical stage of dementia [8]. Individuals with cognitive impairment have a higher risk of accelerated cognitive decline and progression to dementia [9]. Dementia can be diagnosed in a variety of ways, and the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) is the most common and widely used cognitive screening tool worldwide [10, 11]. However, the original MMSE contains items that may be influenced by cultural background and educational level, which can affect its accuracy in diverse populations. Accordingly, a Chinese adapted version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (CAMSE) has been developed and validated for use in the Chinese population [12], and this instrument was employed to assess cognitive function in the present study. Serum uric acid is generated through the metabolism of exogenous purines (purine-rich foods with high protein content) and endogenous purines (damaged or dead cells) [13]. Approximately 90% of the filtered uric acid in the kidney is reabsorbed by renal tubules, while only about 10% is excreted in urine as a means to eliminate unwanted amino acids and nitrogen [13]. Serum uric acid levels are influenced by the interplay between dietary intake of purine, xanthine oxidase activity, and renal uric acid excretion [14]. Serum uric acid serves as a natural water-soluble antioxidant within the human body [15]. It exhibits significant antioxidant properties by directly scavenging oxygen and hydroperoxide radicals, as well as forming stable complexes with iron ions [16, 17]. The brain’s limited antioxidant capacity renders it susceptible to oxidative stress [18]. Previous studies on SUA and cognitive impairment have yielded mixed results. Rulin et al. found a cognitive protective effect of high SUA in older US adults [19]. However, Yousef et al. showed an inverse association between high uric acid levels and cognitive function in middle-aged and elderly Chinese [20]. Huang et al. suggested a U-shaped association between SUA level and global cognitive function [21].

Nevertheless, whether SUA is associated with cognitive impairment in hypertensive individuals remains uncertain. Recent findings from the China Hypertension Survey indicate that hypertension accounts for around 27.9% among adults aged over 18 in 2018—a crucial risk factor associated with cognitive decline [22]. Given the above situation, exploring other modifiable risk factors holds significant clinical value for early prevention of cognitive decline, particularly among hypertensive patients. We aimed to investigate the correlation between serum uric acid levels and cognitive function in hypertensive patients.

Methods

Participants

We performed a cross-sectional analysis using baseline data from the China H-type Hypertension Registry Study (ChiCTR1800017274), which was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on July 20, 2018. Conducted in Wuyuan County, Jiangxi Province, this study recruited participants via community-wide screenings from March to August 2018, with data for the present analysis collected during the same period. The present study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Biomedical Institute of Anhui Medical University (Reference No. CH1059) and the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Reference No. 2018019). All study participants signed written informed consent. The eligibility criteria of participants were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years and (2) hypertension defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, self-reported diagnosis of hypertension previously, or using antihypertensive drugs before. Exclusion criteria for this study included: (1) inability to demonstrate informed consent due to psychological or neurological impairment; (2) due to planned relocation or unable to follow up according to the plan; Patients were deemed by the study physician to be ineligible for enrollment or unable to complete follow-up. Ultimately, 14,234 hypertensive participants were recruited for our study between March 2018 and August 2018. We excluded participants without CAMSE score (n = 3945) and clear educational level (n = 57). Given the possible impact of stroke on cognitive function [23, 24], we excluded stroke patients (n = 761). A total of 9,525 hypertensive participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Data collection for this analysis took place between March 2018 and August 2018. The researcher used standardized questionnaires to collect demographic characteristics and lifestyle of participants (e.g. age, sex, education setting, smoking status, and alcohol consumption), as well as medical and medication history (e.g. self-reported diabetes, medication usage, and cardiovascular diseases). The indexes, height, weight, waist circumference (WC), SBP and DBP were measured by well-trained staff. Smokers who had smoked at least once a day for six months were classified as current cigarette smokers. Drinkers who drank at least once a week for one year were considered current drinkers. Diabetes was defined as self-reported or physician-diagnosed by the use of glucose-lowering drugs or as fasting blood glucose (FBG) greater than ≥ 7.0 mmol/L. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the weight (kg) divided by the squared height (m2). Blood pressure was obtained by electronic monitors (HBP-1300; Omron) on the right arm placed at the heart level in the sitting position after the participants rested for at least 5 min; the blood pressure was calculated as the mean of the last three readings. All indexes and blood samples were measured and collected by professionally trained staff. Overnight fasting venous blood samples were collected from each participant at enrollment. Serum creatinine, serum urea, homocysteine, fasting lipids [including triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C)], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and fasting glucose were measured using automatic clinical analyzers (Beckman Coulter AU680) at the core laboratory of the National Clinical Research Center for Kidney Disease, Guangzhou, China.

Assessment of cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed using the Chinese adapted version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (CAMSE) [25, 26]. The CAMSE is a culturally modified version of the standard MMSE, specifically developed and validated for the Chinese population. It maintains the original MMSE structure and total score range (0–30) while incorporating culturally appropriate replacements for items that are less suitable for Chinese respondents. For example, the reading and writing tasks in the original MMSE were replaced with verbal tasks (sentence construction and repetition) and a culturally adapted attention task, making the instrument more accessible for individuals with limited literacy without altering its overall difficulty or cognitive domain coverage (see Table S6 for complete CAMSE items). Higher scores are associated with better cognitive function [25]. To minimize the influence of educational level on test performance, we defined cognitive impairment (CI) using education-specific cutoff points validated for the Chinese population: CAMSE score < 24 for participants with junior high school education or above (≥ 7 years of schooling), < 20 for those with primary school education (1–6 years), and < 17 for illiterate participants [27]. These cutoffs were established through ROC analysis in a large-scale epidemiological study of the Chinese elderly population, with optimal cut-off points of 16/17 for illiterate individuals (sensitivity 87.6%, specificity 80.8%), 19/20 for those with 1–6 years of education (sensitivity 93.6%, specificity 92.7%), and 23/24 for those with ≥ 7 years of education (sensitivity 94.3%, specificity 94.3%) [28]. This adaptive administration, combined with education-specific cutoffs, aimed to minimize the score disparities attributable to varying educational backgrounds, thereby providing a more accurate reflection of their true cognitive status. All assessments were conducted by trained interviewers using standardized procedures. For all participants, instructions were delivered verbally in simple, clear language, and responses were recorded accordingly. Formal written permission and license for the use and reproduction of the MMSE and its Chinese adapted version have been obtained for this study.

Covariate

Excoriates were selected using a univariate screening approach, a commonly employed method in clinical epidemiological studies. Specifically, univariate regression analyses were first performed between each candidate covariate and the outcome variable. Covariates with a P-value < 0.1 in the univariate regression analyses were included in the final multivariable models. The selected covariates included sex, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), current drinking (yes or no), current smoking (yes or no), antihypertensive drugs (yes or no), statin-lowering drugs (yes or no), antiplatelet drugs (yes or no), total cholesterol, TG, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the total population were described according to SUA quartiles. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Nonparametric tests were used for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables to compare differences across SUA quartiles. To address potential selection bias, we compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants. To minimize information bias in cognitive assessment, education-specific cutoffs were adopted and standardized procedures were applied for illiterate individuals. Given the skewed distribution of SUA, all statistical analyses were performed using log-transformed SUA data. Multivariable linear regression was used to evaluate the association between SUA and continuous CAMSE score, and multivariable logistic regression was applied to examine the association between SUA and binary cognitive impairment (CI). To visually and statistically test for potential nonlinear associations, generalized additive models (GAM) and restricted cubic splines (RCS) were employed. If nonlinearity was detected, inflection points were calculated using a recursive algorithm, and two-piece linear regression models were then constructed on both sides of the inflection point. Three hierarchical models were used in all analyses: a crude model with no adjustment; Model Ⅰ adjusted for age and sex; and Model Ⅱ adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), coronary heart disease (CHD), diabetes mellitus, heart failure, current smoking status, current drinking status, antihypertensive drugs, lipid-lowering drugs, glucose-lowering drugs, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Participants with missing data for any variables included in the multivariable models were excluded from the corresponding analyses (complete-case analysis). To further explore potential effect modification, stratified analyses were performed according to age, sex, BMI, WC, current smoking, current drinking, SBP, DBP, and eGFR. To control for the inflation of type I error due to multiple testing, we applied a Bonferroni correction to the interaction analyses [29]. With 9 predefined subgroup interaction tests, the corrected significance threshold was set at α = 0.0056 (i.e., 0.05/9). Interaction P-values below this threshold were considered statistically significant, while those above were interpreted with caution as hypothesis-generating findings. To verify the robustness of our findings and account for potential variations in CAMSE cutoffs, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a uniform cutoff (CAMSE < 24) for the definition of cognitive impairment. The consistency of results between the primary analysis and sensitivity analysis was assessed to evaluate the stability of the association between SUA and cognitive impairment.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 9471 participants with hypertension were included in the study. The mean age was 63.6 ± 9.8 years (47.9% male, n = 4540). Educational level varied significantly with age (P < 0.001), with illiteracy rates increasing from 22.43% among those aged < 60 years to 45.33% among those aged > 70 years (Table S3). The mean CAMSE score was 22.1 ± 6.4, with literate participants outperforming their illiterate counterparts across all cognitive domains, particularly in calculation/attention and language/visuospatial skills (Table S4). The mean serum uric acid (SUA) level was 428.6 ± 121.1 µmol/L. Participants with higher SUA levels were more likely to be male and have higher BMI, WC, DBP, CAMSE score, and percentages of current smoking, current drinking, diabetes mellitus, and the use of antihypertensive drugs, but lower levels of SBP, eGFR, and percentages of cognitive impairment (CI) (Table 1). Significant differences were found between SUA quartiles regarding age, sex, BMI, WC, SBP, DBP, eGFR, current smoking, current drinking, and use of antihypertensive drugs (all P < 0.05).

Association between SUA and cognitive function

In this study, we constructed a multivariable linear regression model, multivariable logistic regression model, and RCS to reveal the association between SUA and CAMSE score as well as cognitive impairment (CI) (Fig. 2). Three models were fitted to investigate the association between SUA and CAMSE score, and between SUA and CI. Specifically, in the continuous variable analysis, under the fully adjusted model, SUA was associated with CAMSE score and CI prevalence: for each one-unit increase in lnSUA, the CAMSE score increased by 0.47 (95% CI: 0.02, 0.92) (Table 2), and the prevalence of CI decreased by 28% (OR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.58, 0.90) (Table 3). In the SUA quartile analysis, compared with the second quartile (Q2), each one-unit increase in lnSUA in the first quartile (Q1) was associated with a 0.35 decrease in CAMSE score (95% CI: -0.65, -0.05) and a 24% higher prevalence of CI (OR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.08, 1.43). However, no significant differences were observed in CAMSE score or CI prevalence between the third quartile (Q3), fourth quartile (Q4) and the second quartile (Q2), with all P values > 0.05 (Table 2). RCS analysis revealed non-linear relationships between SUA and both CAMSE score and CI. A piecewise regression model identified a significant inflection point at SUA = 445.9 µmol /L (P for log-likelihood ratio test = 0.004, 0.044; Tables 4 and 5). For each one-unit increase in lgSUA, the risk of CI decreased by 47% in participants with SUA < 445.9 µmol /L (OR: 0.53; 95% CI: 0.38, 0.74), whereas a threshold saturation effect was observed when SUA exceeded 445.9 µmol /L.To verify the robustness of our findings and address potential limitations of CAMSE cutoffs, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using a uniform cutoff (CAMSE < 24) to define CI. Consistent with our primary analyses, this sensitivity analysis also revealed a similar threshold effect of SUA on CI, with the lowest SUA level associated with a higher risk of CI (Table S1). This further confirms the stability of the observed association between SUA and CI.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the association between SUA and CI. Analyses were stratified by age (65 vs. ≥ 65 years), sex, BMI (24 vs. ≥ 24 kg/m²), WC (85 vs. ≥ 85 cm), SBP (140 vs. ≥ 140 mmHg), DBP (90 vs. ≥ 90 mmHg), eGFR (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 ml/min/1.73 m²), and lifestyle factors (current smoking, current drinking). In each analysis, models were fully adjusted for all covariates, excluding the stratification variable. The association between SUA and CI remained consistent across all subgroups, with no significant interactions observed after Bonferroni correction for multiple testing (corrected significance threshold α = 0.0056; Fig. 3). Furthermore, the interaction term for literacy status was not statistically significant (P = 0.721), indicating that the association between SUA and cognitive function did not significantly differ between literate and illiterate participants (Table S5).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the association between serum uric acid (SUA) and cognitive function in hypertensive patients. Using multivariable regression and restricted cubic splines, we identified a non-linear, threshold-shaped relationship between SUA and cognitive impairment. An inflection point was observed at 445.9 µmol/L. Below this threshold, lower SUA levels were significantly associated with worse cognitive function: each one-unit increase in lnSUA was associated with a 0.47-point increase in CAMSE score and a 47% lower prevalence of cognitive impairment. Above the inflection point, this association was no longer statistically significant. Subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness of this finding, with no significant interactions detected across strata. Additionally, sensitivity analysis using a uniform cutoff (CAMSE < 24) to define cognitive impairment also showed a similar threshold effect, further reinforcing the stability of our results. These results support the concept that low SUA levels may serve as a potential risk marker for cognitive impairment in this population. Further prospective studies are warranted to verify the temporal relationship and clinical implications of SUA for cognitive function.

While prior research has explored the association between serum uric acid (SUA) and cognitive function, the present study provides several key advances that refine our understanding of this relationship within a high-risk clinical population. Our observation that lower SUA levels are associated with poorer cognitive function aligns with several lines of evidence. Prospective cohort studies have linked higher SUA to a reduced risk of all-cause dementia, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and vascular dementia (VaD) [30]. A meta-analysis further identified low SUA (292 µmol/L) as a potential risk factor for AD and Parkinson’s disease dementia [31]. Mechanistically, this is supported by uric acid’s role as a potent endogenous antioxidant, scavenging reactive oxygen species and mitigating oxidative stress—a key pathway in neurodegeneration [14, 32]. Our study extends this evidence by quantifying the specific SUA threshold (445.9 µmol/L) below which this risk becomes significant in a hypertensive population, offering a more precise biomarker for risk stratification. The relationship between SUA and cognition is complex, and some studies report conflicting results. For instance, a French cohort suggested that higher SUA might exacerbate cognitive decline [33], while a Mendelian randomization analysis found no causal link with AD [34]. These discrepancies likely stem from methodological and population differences, including variations in age, comorbidities, study design (e.g., assuming linearity), and cognitive assessment tools. Notably, our study excluded individuals with stroke—a major mediator of vascular cognitive impairment [35]—which may have reduced confounding by overt cerebrovascular disease. Furthermore, the identified threshold effect helps reconcile these contradictions: it suggests that the risk is non-linear and primarily relevant at the lower end of the SUA distribution, whereas at higher levels, The neuroprotective antioxidant effects may be counterbalanced by SUA’s pro-inflammatory and pro-atherogenic properties, particularly in the presence of cardiometabolic diseases [30, 36]. In this study, higher SUA levels were not linearly associated with better cognitive outcomes, possibly because hypertension itself alters vascular reactivity and oxidative balance, thereby modifying the relationship between SUA and cognition [37]. This aligns with observations in other disease-specific populations. For example, a U-shaped relationship was reported in patients with type 2 diabetes, with an increased risk of mild cognitive impairment at both low and very high SUA levels [21]. Such patterns underscore that the SUA-cognition relationship is not uniform but is shaped by underlying health status, including comorbidities like renal dysfunction [37], heart failure [38], and nutritional status [39].

The mechanism by which uric acid affects cognitive function remains unclear. There are several possible biological mechanisms that could explain our results. Firstly, oxidative stress plays a key role in the pathogenesis of most demented diseases [32, 40]. Oxidative stress can cause the production of a large number of reactive oxygen species, leading to an imbalance of lipid metabolism, secondary cell damage, and cell apoptosis [41]. Uric acid removal of oxygen and hydroperoxyl radicals, singlet oxygen, oxo-heme oxidants and can form stable complex compounds with iron ions to exert its important antioxidant properties in the human body [16]. Secondly, SUA also improves dementia by lowering amyloid-β (Aβ) and tau toxicity. Senile plaques (SP) caused by Aβ deposition and neuronal fibrillary tangles (NFT) caused by hyperphosphorylated tau are the initial events leading to neuronal degeneration and dementia [42]. YE et al. found that higher uric acid levels relieved the adverse effects of Aβ and tau on longitudinal deterioration of MMSE and ADAS-cog score in female subjects [43]. Moreover, serum uric acid may also affect cognitive abilities through nutrition metabolism. Hypouricemia is a well-recognized biomarker of malnutrition status [39], and some data suggest that the presence of malnutrition in the elderly may contribute to cognitive impairment [44].

The present study has the following strengths. First, this is one of the largest studies to specifically examine the SUA-cognitive function relationship in a community-based hypertensive population, a group at high risk for cognitive decline yet insufficiently studied in this context. Second, we employed segmented regression and RCS to objectively identify a nonlinear threshold effect, moving beyond the assumption of a linear association prevalent in prior research. This threshold in the specific population provides a more precise biomarker for risk stratification. Third, the use of education-specific CAMSE cutoffs and culturally adapted administration for illiterate participants minimized a major source of bias, enhancing the accuracy of cognitive assessment in our population. Finally, we adjusted for a broad range of potential confounders, including detailed cerebrocardiovascular risk factors and medication use, increasing the robustness of the observed association.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our results. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; longitudinal studies are needed to confirm whether low SUA precedes cognitive decline. Second, despite extensive adjustment, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Notably, data on specific medications affecting urate levels (e.g., diuretics, allopurinol), dietary purine intake, and geriatric syndromes (e.g., depression, sensory impairment) were not available, which may influence both SUA and CAMSE performance. Third, potential selection bias exists due to loss to missing CAMSE data. Supplementary analyses indicated that excluded participants were older, more likely to be female, had higher systolic blood pressure, higher total cholesterol and LDL-C levels, greater use of antihypertensive medications (Table S2). Since these high-risk individuals likely had higher cognitive impairment incidence, their exclusion suggests that our results may underestimate the true strength of the association rather than creating a spurious one. Fourth, despite using the CAMSE and education-specific cutoffs, measurement limitations remain. The CAMSE may be subject to ceiling effects, limiting its sensitivity to mild cognitive impairment, and residual confounding by cultural factors or educational quality cannot be fully excluded. However, sensitivity analyses using continuous CAMSE scores and a uniform cutoff (CAMSE < 24) yielded consistent results (Figure S1, Table S1), supporting the robustness of our findings. Finally, generalizability is limited as the study was restricted to hypertensive patients in rural southern China. The findings require validation in diverse populations.

Conclusion

In this large cross-sectional study of community-dwelling hypertensive adults in rural China, we observed a non-linear, threshold-shaped association between serum uric acid (SUA) levels and cognitive function. An inflection point was identified at approximately 445.9 µmol/L. Below this threshold, lower SUA levels were significantly associated with poorer cognitive performance and a higher prevalence of cognitive impairment; above this level, the association plateaued and was no longer statistically significant. These findings were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses, including the use of continuous CAMSE scores and alternative cognitive impairment definitions, and were consistent across various subgroups.
