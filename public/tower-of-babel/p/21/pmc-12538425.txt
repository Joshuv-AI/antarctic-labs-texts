Understanding first-line treatment patterns and survival outcomes across sociodemographic groups of women with metastatic triple-negative breast cancer in the United States: a real-world study
Authors: SM Tolaney, L Spring, YG Abdou, MK Rehnquist, C Hogea, A Estrin, N Sjekloca, C Lai, K Kalinsky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) has poor prognosis, and disproportionately affects young black women and BRCA1/2 mutation carriers. We describe sociodemographic differences in real-world treatment patterns and survival outcomes in women with metastatic TNBC (mTNBC) in the United States.Patients and methodsThis retrospective, real-world cohort study used de-identified data from patients in the Flatiron Health database diagnosed with mTNBC who received first-line (1L) treatment between January 2018 and July 2022. Patient characteristics, treatment patterns, real-world overall survival (rwOS), and time to next treatment or death (TTNTD) were determined in the overall population and by race, socioeconomic status (SES) index, United States region, and treatment setting.ResultsOf 1555 women diagnosed with mTNBC, 930 women received 1L treatment and were included. Overall, 55% of women were white, 43% were from the South, and 83% were treated at community centers. 1L treatment patterns were similar across all subgroups. Overall, 69% of patients received chemotherapy only, 28% received programmed death-(ligand) protein 1 [PD-(L)1] inhibitors, and 2% poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi). Documented testing rate for PD-L1 expression was 80% and highest in the Northeast (91%), Midwest (86%), and community centers (82%). Among 559 women with documented PD-L1 expression, 82% were treated with PD-(L)1 inhibitors in any metastatic line. Documented testing rate for BRCA1/2 mutation was 55% and highest in the West (63%) and in academic practices (59%). Among 508 women with documented BRCA1/2 mutations, 56% were treated with PARPis in any metastatic line; no black woman received a 1L PARPi regardless of mutation status. There were no statistically significant differences in survival outcomes across sociodemographic groups, and, overall, patients had poor survival (rwOS: 12.0 months; TTNTD: 4.2 months).ConclusionsThis study demonstrated generally similar treatment patterns and poor survival outcomes across sociodemographic groups, highlighting an unmet need for more efficacious treatments for all women with mTNBC.Key words: metastatic triple-negative breast cancer, treatment pattern, survival, race disparities, socioeconomic status disparities, geographical disparities

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most common cancer in women worldwide.1 In the United States, ∼317 000 new cases of invasive BC are expected in 2025 with one-third diagnosed as metastatic BC (mBC).2 Five-year survival rate for patients with mBC is 32%.3 Triple-negative BC (TNBC) is defined as both hormone receptor-negative (<1% expression of estrogen receptor and progesterone receptor in tumor cells) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative [HER2 0 or 1+ by immunohistochemistry (IHC), or HER2 2+ by IHC and negative by in situ hybridization (ISH)] by the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO)/College of American Pathologists (CAP) guidelines.4,5 TNBC accounts for 15%-20% of all BCs with a high prevalence in young black women (∼20% versus ∼10% for other ethnicities).10, 6, 7, 8, 9 TNBC has an aggressive phenotype and is characterized by a higher incidence before the age of 40 years, a higher risk of developing distant metastasis, and lower survival rates than other BC subtypes.10, 11, 12, 9 In addition, TNBC disproportionately occurs in patients with breast cancer gene 1/2 (BRCA1/2) mutations who also express programmed death-(ligand) protein 1 [PD-(L)1].8,13,14

Since 2018, several novel therapies have been approved for patients with metastatic TNBC (mTNBC) whose tumor harbors BRCA1/2 mutations [poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi)] or has a certain level of PD-L1 expression [PD-(L)1 inhibitors].15, 16, 17, 18, 19 However, populations enrolled in clinical studies often lack the sociodemographic diversity (especially racial diversity) observed in clinical practices.20,21 Several studies have revealed racial, socioeconomic, and geographic inequalities in timely diagnosis, treatment initiation, and access to new therapies.8,22, 23, 24, 25 Furthermore, patient age and frailty at start of treatment have been shown to correlate with treatment tolerability and survival outcomes.26, 27, 28 These disparities lead to poorer survival outcomes, especially in black women with mTNBC.24,29,30 Thus, improving our knowledge of sociodemographic factors in mTNBC is key for the development of effective strategies to improve survival outcomes across all populations.

This retrospective real-world cohort study aimed to assess the impact of sociodemographic factors on real-world treatment patterns, BRCA1/2 and PD-L1 testing, and survival outcomes in women with previously untreated mTNBC in the United States.

Patients and methods

Data sources and study design

Data were obtained from the nationwide, longitudinal Flatiron Health database and patient demographics, diagnoses, biomarkers, and outcomes were derived from electronic health records from ∼280 cancer clinics in the United States (∼800 sites of care). The data were curated via technology-enabled abstraction from structured and unstructured records.31 The de-identified data were subject to obligations to prevent re-identification and protect patient confidentiality. These data sources included information on sociodemographic factors of interest and an adequate sample size for each sociodemographic subgroup despite the relatively rare cancer subtype (mTNBC).

Adult females with an mTNBC diagnosis as defined by ASCO/CAP4,5 who had initiated first-line (1L) systemic treatment for mTNBC between 1 January 2018, and 31 July 2022 (index period) were eligible. The study period extended to 31 January 2023 to ensure a minimum of 6 months of potential follow-up (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105841). Patients who had other primary cancers (excluding non-metastatic, non-melanoma skin cancers) within 5 years before mBC diagnosis were excluded. Patients who received hormonal therapy, HER2-targeted treatment, or a clinical study drug were also excluded. However, patients who received the HER2-targeting treatment trastuzumab deruxtecan for HER2-low (IHC1+ or IHC2+/ISH-negative) disease were included. Treatment with sacituzumab govitecan in the 1L, an off-label indication, also disqualified patients for inclusion. Patients who were missing visit records, vital information, medication administration dates, or laboratory tests/results surrounding the metastatic diagnosis date (30 days before and 90 days after diagnosis) were excluded.

This observational study was carried out in accordance with ethical principles that are consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki, International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice, and the applicable legislation on non-interventional studies and/or observational studies.

Treatment patterns

Treatments of interest included chemotherapy (as single or multiple agents, hereafter referred to as ‘chemotherapy only’), PD-(L)1 inhibitor-based regimens, PARPi-based regimens, trastuzumab deruxtecan for HER2-low disease, and other systemic treatment for mTNBC not mentioned as an exclusion above. The proportion of patients receiving treatments of interest in the 1L setting for mTNBC were described. Time from diagnosis to treatment initiation and duration of 1L treatment were also assessed.

Biomarker testing

Tumor PD-L1 expression testing data were only reported for patients with a first mTNBC diagnosis date on or after 8 March 2019, when the first PD-L1 inhibitor, atezolizumab, received accelerated approval in the United States for mTNBC.17 The proportion of patients tested and their tumor PD-L1 expression status were described based on qualitative status (the nature of the test or thresholds used were not reported in the database). The proportion of patients whose tumor expressed PD-L1 and who were treated with PD-(L)1 inhibitor therapy in any metastatic line was also reported.

Similar data were described for patients with BRCA1/2 mutations who received a PARPi after their first mTNBC diagnosis date on or after 12 January 2018 (first PARPi approval in the United States for mTNBC).

Survival outcomes

Real-world overall survival (rwOS) was defined as the time (months) from 1L treatment start (index date) until death from any cause, loss to follow-up, or end of study period, whichever occurred first. Time to next treatment or death (TTNTD) was defined as the time (months) from index date until start of the next line of therapy or death, loss to follow-up, or end of study period, whichever came first. TTNTD was used as a surrogate for real-world progression-free survival (rwPFS) because data on the next line of treatment and death are often better captured in a real-world database than progression data; furthermore, the consistency between TTNTD and rwPFS has been observed in other studies.32, 33, 34, 35

Subgroup analysis

Demographics, metastatic treatment patterns, and survival outcomes were described in relation to race (self-reported as white, black, or other), socioeconomic status (SES) index [first quintile (Q1; lowest SES) to fifth quintile (Q5; highest SES)], United States region (Northeast, Midwest, South, West, or other), and treatment setting (academic or community practice).

Statistical analysis

Data were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous data were summarized as mean (standard deviation) or median [interquartile range (IQR)], and categorical variables were described as a number (percentage). For any strata with 10 or fewer patients, the results were suppressed in order to minimize the risk of re-identification of patients. Missing data were not imputed but number (percentage) of patients with missing data were reported for categorical and continuous variables.

The Kaplan–Meier method was used to describe median [95% confidence interval (CI)] time to event for rwOS and TTNTD. For rwOS analyses, patients who were alive at the end of study or lost to follow-up were censored at their last confirmed activity date; patients for whom a date of death was not recorded were censored at their last known date alive before the end of study period (data cut-off). For TTNTD analyses, patients who were still on their 1L treatment or who died during the study period were censored at the end of the study or end of activity, whichever occurred first.

Cox proportional hazards regression was used to compare unadjusted and adjusted differences in rwOS and TTNTD by sociodemographic group. The following confounding factors were included: age (categorical), race, United States region, payer type, type of disease occurrence, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, treatment setting, and immunotherapy-containing line. Medians (95% CI) were estimated, and unadjusted and adjusted hazard ratio (HR) estimates (95% CI) were reported. The missing indicator method was used to retain patients with missing covariate data in the analysis; they were captured in the ‘unknown’ category for covariates for which they were missing data.

Results

Patients

Among the 10 967 women with mBC in the Flatiron Health database, 14% (n = 1555) had mTNBC. Of black and white women with mBC, 23% and 13% had a diagnosis of mTNBC, respectively. The proportion of women with mTNBC in the Q1 and Q5 SES index was 18% and 11% of the women with mBC for each quintile, respectively. There was a higher proportion of women with mTNBC among women with mBC from the South (16%) than from other United States regions (12% in the Northeast, Midwest, and West). No differences were observed in the proportion of women with mTNBC who received care at community or academic centers (14%-15% of women with mBC).

Of the 1555 women with mTNBC, 40% (n = 625) had no evidence of receiving 1L treatment (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105841) due to death, lack of data, or delayed treatment.

Thus, 930 women received a 1L treatment of interest within the index period and were included in the current study. Overall, 55% (n = 516) of women were white, 43% (n = 397) were from the South, and 83% (n = 774) were treated at community centers as shown in Table 1. The proportion of women who received 1L treatment was similar between white (61%) and black (62%) women, but slightly lower for other races (53%). For SES index, there was a higher proportion of women in Q5 (65%) who received 1L treatment compared with women in Q2 (56%). This proportion was similar across United States regions and treatment settings. A greater proportion of black women were in the Q1 SES index (33%) compared with white women (18%). Of note, the distribution of patients across sociodemographic factors was similar for women without evidence of receiving 1L treatment and for women who were treated in the 1L (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105841).

Treatment patterns

The median time from mBC diagnosis to 1L treatment was 0.95 months (IQR 0.56-1.61 months) overall and was consistent across races (Table 1), United States regions, SES quintiles, and treatment setting (data not shown). In 1L, most women received chemotherapy only, as monotherapy (42%, n = 394) or multiple chemotherapy agents (27%, n = 250). The most common other therapies used were PD-(L)1 inhibitor-based regimens (28%, n = 261); 2% (n = 18) of patients received PARPi-based regimens. The categories of 1L treatment were similar across sociodemographic groups but specific usage varied between groups. Use of chemotherapy with a single agent was highest in the Northeast (49%) and Midwest (48%) of the United States and for women in the SES index Q3 (46%). Black women (31%) and women in the SES index Q4 (32%) were more likely to have received chemotherapy as a combination of agents. Use of PD-(L)1 inhibitor-based regimens was highest among white women (30%), women in the West (33%), and women in the SES index Q5 (31%). Use of a PARPi-based regimen was similar overall across all subgroups but was not received by any black women in the 1L (Figure 1).

Biomarker testing

All 930 women were diagnosed after 12 January 2018, and were included in the analysis of BRCA1/2 testing. Overall, 55% (n = 508) had evidence of BRCA1/2 testing with the highest testing proportions in the West (63%) and in academic practices (59%). The proportion of women tested was similar to the overall population across race and SES quintiles. Among the 508 women with testing, 8% (n = 43) had documented BRCA1/2 mutations [similar rates were observed in black (≤15%) and white (8%) women], of which 56% (n = 24) were treated with PARPis in any metastatic line during the study period. PARPi-based regimens were used primarily for white women (68%), women in the Northeast (67%) and Midwest (71%) of the United States, and women in the SES indexes Q4 and Q5 (both 67%). PARPis were only received by five or fewer black women with BRCA1/2 mutations in any metastatic line (Table 2).

The subgroup of patients with a diagnosis after 8 March 2019 included 75% (n = 696) of the women from the overall cohort. Of these, 80% (n = 559) had evidence of PD-L1 expression testing with high testing proportions in the Northeast (91%) and Midwest (86%) of the United States. Testing rates were lower in academic centers (71%) than in the overall subgroup. The proportion of women tested was similar to the overall subgroup across race and SES quintiles. Among the 559 women with testing, 17% (n = 96) had documented PD-L1 expression [similar rates were observed in black (15%) and white (17%) women], of which 82% (n = 79) were treated with PD-(L)1 inhibitors in any metastatic line during the study period. PD-(L)1 inhibitor-based regimens were received by a higher proportion of women in the South of the United States (88%) and women in the SES indexes Q3 (95%) and Q4 (87%) than in the overall population. PD-(L)1 inhibitors were used in a lower proportion of women in the Northeast (67%) and Midwest (75%) of the United States, black women (78%), at academic centers (77%), and women in the SES index Q1 (71%) compared with the overall population (Table 2).

Survival outcomes

For women receiving 1L treatment, there was a numerical advantage in survival for white women and for women in the higher SES index quintiles; however, there were no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) in rwOS across sociodemographic groups in analyses unadjusted and adjusted for confounding factors. The overall median rwOS was 12.0 months (95% CI 10.7-13.1 months); among white and black women, it was 12.3 months (95% CI 10.7-14.85 months) and 11.3 months (95% CI 9.1-13.1 months), respectively (Figure 2A). There were no statistical differences in rwOS across United States regions (Figure 2B). Among SES index quintiles, median rwOS ranged from 10.7 months (95% CI 8.7-14.5 months) for women in Q2 to 14.1 months (95% CI 11.0-17.5 months) for women in Q4 (Figure 2C). Women treated in academic centers had a 3-month higher median rwOS compared with those treated in community centers, although this was not statistically significant: median rwOS was 14.5 months (95% CI 11.7-16.95 months) and 11.3 months (95% CI 10.25-12.9 months), respectively (Figure 2D).

Discussion

This retrospective, real-world cohort study did not show statistically significant treatment pattern differences by sociodemographic factors. While showing some numerical differences, rwOS and TTNTD did not statistically differ across any sociodemographic group.

In this study, among all women with mBC, black women were 1.8 times more likely to be diagnosed with mTNBC compared with white women. These data are consistent with other studies showing an increased prevalence of mTNBC in black populations.7,8,36 Furthermore, a higher proportion of women in the South and in the lowest SES index quintile were diagnosed with mTNBC compared with the overall population. This may reflect that populations in the south of the United States include a higher proportion of black people and lower median household incomes than in other United States regions.37, 38, 39

In the current study, 1L treatment patterns were similar across sociodemographic groups and most patients were treated with chemotherapy only. This contrasts with other real-world studies that have reported disparities in treatment patterns for TNBC, especially a lack of guideline-adherent systemic treatments for black patients as well as patients without insurance and patients in low SES groups.40

BRCA1/2 mutations are often shown to be more prevalent in the black population despite lower testing rates.41, 42, 43, 44, 45 This difference was not observed in the current study as black and white women had similar proportions of documented testing for BRCA1/2 mutations and positive test results; however, while 18 total patients received a PARPi in the 1L, none were black, which may indicate disparities in access to newer targeted therapies even after positive biomarker test results.25 This may also reflect the low usage of PARPi overall and the lower proportion of black versus white women in the study population; further, it cannot be determined from the database if these treatments were offered and declined by the patients due to lack of insurance coverage or for other reasons. Similarly, the current study did not identify differences in BRCA testing rates by SES quintiles; however, another real-world study reported low testing rates in patients with TNBC in the lower SES tertile and mentioned patient refusal or lack of insurance to explain the absence of testing despite the offering by their health care providers.46

Testing for PD-L1 expression was similar across race and SES index and higher for patients in the Northeast and Midwest of the United States compared with the overall population. Surprisingly, in the Flatiron database, lower documented testing was observed in academic versus community centers, which may be due to health care providers not clearly reporting the data, as academic centers tend to have better testing rates than community centers.47 Approximately 80% of patients with PD-L1 expression received a PD-(L)1 inhibitor, with similar rates across races. This contrasts with a recent real-world study of the National Cancer Database by Freeman et al. reporting lower use of immunotherapy in black versus white patients with mTNBC even after covariate adjustment.48 Similarly, these authors reported that patients treated at community centers were less likely to receive immunotherapy than those treated elsewhere; in contrast, the current study showed lower use of PD-(L)1 inhibitors in any metastatic line at academic centers versus community centers, in alignment with the lower testing. None the less, in the 1L, ∼30% of patients had received a PD-(L)1-based regimen across all sociodemographic factors.

Overall, median rwOS and TTNTD were 12.0 and 4.2 months, respectively, and there were no statistically significant differences across sociodemographic factors. Survival times were in line with previously reported real-world outcomes in patients with mTNBC primarily treated with chemotherapy.29,49, 50, 51, 52, 53 Furthermore, rwOS was similar to that reported in another real-word study of patients with mTNBC who initiated 1L treatment between 2011 and 2022. Punie et al. reported that, despite the approval of PARPi and PD-(L)1 inhibitors and PARPi for mTNBC after 2018, chemotherapy remained the primary treatment in 1L with a gradual increase of PD-(L)1 inhibitors use in this setting. The study also provides insights on treatment received in subsequent lines, which mainly included PD-(L)1 inhibitors and sacituzumab govitecan (after its approval in 2020); PARPi and trastuzumab deruxtecan use was low across all lines.54 In the current study, while not statistically significant, black women tended to have lower rwOS than white women, consistent with other real-world studies.29,55 In addition, survival was shorter in patients in lower SES index quintiles, also consistent with previous reports that patients with TNBC from less favorable socioeconomic settings had higher risk of mortality than patients in more favorable socioeconomic settings.8,30

Given the nature of real-world data collection, missing data, tied to several factors such as clinical practice standards, disease severity, data redaction, and other means to protect patient privacy and prevent re-identification, may have led to misclassification of sociodemographic groups, unmeasured or residual confounding, or under-capture of outcome data. Some factors like age or frailty have not been included in this study. Furthermore, the SES index was developed using an adapted index of composite area-level measures of SES, including income, poverty, home values, rental costs, education, blue collar employment, and rate of unemployment based on an approach validated by Yost et al. (i.e. ‘Yost index’) and may not reflect individual SES level; for example, SES index by race may not have been accurately captured.56 Additionally, insurance coverage was reported by the patients, rather than by billing resources, which may lead to inaccuracies and information bias. Coupled with de-identification and infrequent updates, the data may not capture changes in insurance coverage status over time. This may have impacted treatment patterns reported in this study. Some patients included in this study were treated during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic; thus, treatment patterns and outcomes data may have been impacted and may not reflect standard decision algorithms. In addition, among the 1555 women with mTNBC in the current study, ∼40% had no evidence of receiving 1L treatment, the majority of whom had evidence of death before receiving treatment. For those with no evidence of treatment and no death recorded, it was not possible to determine the reason for the lack of 1L treatment, which could include receiving treatment outside of the Flatiron network. Women with mTNBC were identified using the ASCO/CAP definition of hormone receptor (<1% tumor-positive nuclei by IHC); thus, women with results of 1%-10%, who generally have similar survival outcomes and are often considered as hormone receptor-negative in clinical practice, were not accounted for in this analysis.4,5,57 Of note, rwOS may have been affected by treatment received in subsequent lines, which are not reported in this study. Finally, these findings should be considered with the caveat that patients in the Flatiron database appeared to be diagnosed with later stages of disease and primarily in the community settings compared with patients in other datasets and may not be generalizable to all patients with mTNBC in or outside the United States.31

In conclusion, no statistically significant sociodemographic disparities were observed in treatment patterns and survival outcomes in the population of patients with mTNBC who have received 1L treatment in this retrospective, real-world cohort study. However, our data have shown that survival was poor for this population across all sociodemographic groups and independent of treatment received. Thus, this study highlights an unmet need for more efficacious treatments for women with mTNBC across all sociodemographic subgroups; further research is warranted that focuses on improvement of treatments and outcomes for all women with mTNBC.
