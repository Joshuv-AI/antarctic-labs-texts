Gatherings in Esophagology: Innovations and Future Directions in the Diagnosis and Management of Reflux Disease
Authors: Jennifer Cable, Robert Giuli, Mark R Fox, Rachel L Rosen, Luis R Valdovinos‐García, Stefano Siboni, John O Clarke, Nikki Johnston, Tina L Samuels, Carmelo Scarpignato, Ellen M Stein, Ronnie Fass, José María Remes‐Troches, Abraham Khan, Afrin Kamal Rahman, Kristle Lynch, Daniela Jodorkovsky, Marcelo F Vela, Alexandra Vandenberg, Braden Kuo, Pelin Ergun, Sigh Pichamol Jirapinyo, Emanuele Asti, Salvatore Tolone, Eric G Sheu, Renato Salvador, Marco Sozzi, Inna A Husain, Justin C Wu, Anthony Hobson, Nirmal S Sharma, Wai‐Kit Lo, Daniel R Duncan, C Prakash Gyawali, Walter Chan, Daniel Sifrim, Edoardo Savarino, Nicola de Bortoli, Benjamin D Rogers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecent advances in the diagnosis and management of reflux disease were the central focus of the inaugural Gatherings in Esophagology (GiE), which convened experts across gastroenterology, surgery, otolaryngology, pulmonology, and basic research. The sessions highlighted innovations in reflux monitoring—including high‐resolution manometry, wireless pH monitoring, and novel salivary biomarkers—while critically evaluating their diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility. Presentations explored the limitations of traditional proton‐pump inhibitor therapy, the emergence of potassium‐competitive acid blockers as a new class of acid suppressants, and the evolving role of adjunctive treatments such as mucosal protectants, reflux reducers, and neuromodulators for refractory symptoms. The discourse extended to advanced interventional procedures, including transoral incisionless fundoplication, magnetic sphincter augmentation, and the RefluxStop device, with discussion of patient selection, efficacy, and complication management. Discussants emphasized the pathophysiology and management of extraesophageal manifestations of reflux, the interplay between reflux and pulmonary disease, and the diagnostic challenges in pediatric populations. The meeting also addressed the integration of behavioral therapies, the role of the microbiome, and the application of artificial intelligence in reflux diagnostics. Collectively, these insights underscore a shift toward precision medicine in reflux disease, emphasizing individualized diagnostic strategies and tailored therapeutic approaches to improve patient outcomes.Keywords: diagnosis, esophagology, future directions, GiE, innovation, management, reflux disease

The six sessions (see below and in the Supplementary Materials) inaugurated a new way of sharing information and knowledge.

They were organized around the evaluation and management of reflux, allowing participants to take a deep dive into pressing clinical questions, novel technologies, and areas of ongoing uncertainty in the field. Key topics included diagnosing gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), the utility of catheter‐based and wireless pH monitoring, and extraesophageal manifestations of reflux.

Speakers expanded on the pros and cons of traditional proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy for acid suppression and the new potassium‐competitive acid blockers (P‐CABs), as well as adjunctive medical/endoscopic therapies and new invasive reflux management procedures.

The environment was uniquely designed to encourage constant interaction—with no formal chairs, no hierarchy, and no distractions. All attendees, including key opinion leaders, clinicians, researchers, and educators, learned together over the two days, immersed in both science and community.

Session 1: Current and Novel Diagnostics (Chairs: Walter Chan and Prakash Gyawali)

The first session focused on current and novel diagnostic strategies for esophageal diseases. Speakers discussed new technologies and strategies for patients with esophageal symptoms who do not quite fit the criteria for a diagnosis of GERD. Talks focused on esophageal function and motility disorders, noninvasive biomarkers for diagnosing reflux, tailoring reflux testing and diagnostics, and new scoring systems to categorize patients with reflux. In addition, both gastroenterologists and surgeons lent their views on endoscopic assessment of the esophageal‐gastric junction (EGJ) and hiatal hernia.

Esophageal Motility Disorders Related to GERD

Mark Fox, from the University of Zurich and Klinik Arlesheim in Switzerland, discussed GERD through the lens of an esophageal motility disorder. Early studies by Peter Kahrilas in the 1980s showed that failure in primary peristalsis—the ability of the esophagus to clear acid—was just as important to reflux disease as how much acid came up during acid reflux events [1].

Defining the structures and functions of the esophagus via high‐resolution manometry (HRM) is the gold standard for detecting ineffective motility and the sufficiency of the reflux barrier at the EGJ. These factors are an integral part of GERD diagnosis in the Lyon Classification v2.0. Moreover, reflux symptoms are often nonspecific, and HRM can help to exclude symptomatic motility disorders and functional diagnoses, including rumination syndrome that can present with similar symptoms. The Chicago Classification v4.0 provides a standardized system to categorize motility disorders based on HRM findings [2].

In patients with indeterminate results, the diagnosis of both motility disorders and reflux disease can be refined by incorporating provocative tests, which improve the sensitivity and specificity of HRM. The rapid drink challenge and solid swallows in particular can trigger esophageal spasm and better visualize EGJ outflow obstruction. Conversely, approximately 30% patients with ineffective motility with water swallows have normal motility when tested with solids [3, 4, 5].

Noninvasive Markers for Reflux Diagnosis

Rachel Rosen from Harvard Medical School and Boston Children's Hospital presented new findings on salivary biomarkers in the diagnosis of GERD. Historically, there has been a focus on salivary pepsin as a diagnostic biomarker, as it is inexpensive and easy to perform; however, several impedance studies have failed to show a correlation between reflux parameters and salivary pepsin positivity using both single and repeated measurements [6, 7]. A recent meta‐analysis did show that higher concentrations of pepsin correlate with GERD, though the relationship is still imperfect [8].

Another potential biomarker is salivary bile acids. Patients with LPR symptoms and GERD are more likely to have positive bile acids in the saliva [9]. However, similar to salivary pepsin, the correlation between the amount of reflux and salivary bile acid positivity is not perfect. Furthermore, measuring bile acids using mass spectroscopy is expensive and currently only available as a research tool.

Other digestive enzymes, such as elastase and trypsin, have been measured in saliva samples, but they also lack the needed sensitivity as a diagnostic test [10]. Some evidence suggests that salivary bicarbonate is higher in GERD patients, though more data are needed [11].

One newly proposed biomarker is the salivary microbiome. While the data are not yet robust enough, preliminary studies have demonstrated the potential of the salivary microbiome to identify different GI diseases [12].

Rosen concluded that while a range of salivary biomarkers are easy to collect for single or repeated measurements, none have yet proven to be reliably sensitive or specific enough for a clinical diagnosis of GERD.

Catheter‐Based Versus Wireless Reflux Monitoring

There are two primary methods to monitor acid exposure in the esophagus to diagnose GERD: catheter‐based and wireless capsule systems. Luis Valdovinos Garcia from Instituto Nacional de Ciencias Medicas y Nutrición Salvador Zubirán discussed the pros and cons of the two methods.

Catheter‐based pH monitoring has long been the standard practice. This approach provides high‐resolution sampling, with two data points per second, and the capability to measure impedance, which is particularly crucial in patients with borderline acid exposure time (AET).

Wireless capsule technology is a less invasive alternative to catheter‐based monitoring. While the capsule samples less frequently than catheters—every 6 s—it can monitor for longer periods, up to 96 h. This enables physicians to capture day‐to‐day variability in reflux, which can increase diagnostic sensitivity [13, 14]. In patients with negative catheter‐based studies, 34% had positive results using wireless capsules on their worst symptom day [14].

Wireless monitoring is also better tolerated. Catheters can potentially underestimate reflux because they can cause nasopharyngeal discomfort, reduced appetite, and limited physical activity. Wireless systems allow patients to maintain normal routines, leading to more physiological measurements. Patient preference strongly favors the wireless option, with 90% willing to repeat the test, compared to just 50% for catheter studies [15, 16].

Despite its advantages, wireless pH monitoring is more expensive and requires endoscopy for placement, making it less accessible in resource‐limited settings, where catheter reuse may be common due to cost constraints.

Garcia concluded that wireless capsule monitoring offers improved tolerability, longer, more physiological observations, and can improve GERD detection in patients with a negative catheter test and a high suspicion of GERD [17, 18, 19]. However, catheter‐based monitoring with impedance remains essential for comprehensive reflux evaluation within a standard 24‐h period.

Provocative Maneuvers and Scoring Systems Predictive of GERD

Stefano Siboni from the University of Milan described two HRM provocative maneuvers and two scoring systems for GERD assessment. The first provocative maneuver, multiple rapid swallows (MRS), consisting of five swallows 2–3 s apart, measures esophageal contraction reserve. A test is considered positive if the distal contracted integral (DCI) after the MRS is greater than the mean baseline DCI. While the MRS is associated with acid exposure time, especially in patients with slightly impaired esophageal motility, Siboni argued that the current definition is likely flawed. He showed unpublished data illustrating that the current definition of contractual reserve did not distinguish between pathologic and normal reflux across all pH impedance variables. Instead, using the DCI cutoff derived from a receiver operating characteristic analysis performed better.

The second maneuver, the straight leg raise (SLR), has been validated to predict pathologic GERD [20, 21]. Patients lie in a supine position and raise both legs for 5 s. In cases of a weak reflux barrier, this increases intra‐abdominal pressure, which bleeds into the chest and stresses the EGJ. The most recent variation of the leg raise, the supine‐upright transition (SUT), takes advantage of the change of position required by the Chicago classification [22]. This maneuver increases intrabdominal pressure more than the SLR and is more sensitive at detecting GERD. Using both the SLR and SUT can improve diagnostic sensitivity and specificity [22, 23].

Siboni also discussed two scoring systems: the Milan score and the Lyon score. The Milan score is an HRM‐based GERD‐predictive model that takes into account four variables: ineffective esophageal motility, SLR, EGJ contractile integrity, and EGJ type [24]. The Milan score has been validated in various clinical scenarios, including LPS [25], EGJ type 2 [26], and severe GERD [27]. The Lyon score integrates two endoscopic and four pH impedance variables [28]. Based on the Lyon score, patients are stratified into six phenotypes. While the Lyon score has been shown to reliably predict symptom response in European cohorts, it is less effective in Asian populations.

Since the Milan and Lyon scores assess different aspects of GERD (pathophysiology of antireflux barrier disruption vs. consequences of GERD), they can be combined to enhance predictive power. In a study of nearly 200 patients, concordant scores predicted symptom improvement in 89% of cases, versus 63% when discordant, and only 19% when both were negative [29].

How to Diagnose EGJ Disruption and Hiatus Hernia (Hill vs. AFS Grade)

John O. Clarke from Stanford University discussed advances in diagnosing EGJ disruption and hiatus hernia by moving from the Hill grade, which was introduced in 1996, to the AFS grade, an expert‐developed criterion developed in 2022.

The Hill grade categorizes patients into four groups via the appearance and function of the gastroesophageal flap valve during endoscopy [30]:

Types 1 and 2 are generally considered normal, while types 3 and 4 indicate GERD. Interobserver variability is typically low, though there can be discrepancies between types 1 and 2 and types 3 and 4. It is, therefore, most useful for classifying patients at the extremes of the criteria. This Hill grade has several limitations. It only evaluates the flap valve and does not account for hernias. In addition, the scoring system is not predictive of response to treatment.

A more recent classification system, the AFS grade, was developed in 2022 [31]. It grades EGJ disruption based on three parameters:

Patients receive a grade from 1 to 4, similar to Hill, but with improved anatomical precision and consideration of both the hernia and flap valve. Further validation is needed to determine if AFS grading can predict outcomes for treatments such as TIF.

Development and Assessment of a Daily Reflux Symptom Diary

The session included one original research talk. Tina Samuels from Nikki Johnston's lab at the Medical College of Wisconsin presented her work on developing a new daily reflux symptom diary to assess LPR symptoms and treatment response.

The most commonly used metric to assess symptoms in LPR is the reflux symptom index (RSI) [32]. This index has been used in both clinical practice and trials to support labeling claims; however, recent guidance by the FDA advised that it falls short on several key standards required to support regulatory approval. Namely, the RSI was not developed with the involvement of patients, several items query about more than one symptom, the response options are numerical and lacking in inherent meaning, and the month‐long recall period is too long for accurate memory.

Another patient‐reported outcome (PRO) tool, the reflux symptoms score‐12 (RSS‐12), overcomes some of these shortcomings—it has higher sensitivity and specificity than the RSI, and patients were involved in its development [33]; however, it still does not meet the standards put forth by the FDA.

Without a fit‐for‐purpose outcomes tool for LPR, treatment will be limited to therapies approved for GERD signs and symptoms, which many LPR patients do not have.

To address this critical gap in LPR management, Samuels has developed a novel electronic PRO designed with the FDA guidance in mind. The Daily Reflux Symptom Diary is an electronic daily diary with a 24‐h recall period that measures LPR symptom severity. The tool will be validated in line with FDA guidance, including a patient cognitive interview and a responder definition based on a patient global impression scale, in an upcoming phase II proof‐of‐concept clinical trial of fosamprenavir for LPR.

Session 1 Discussion

During the panel discussion, speakers discussed the importance of tailoring monitoring and therapeutic approaches based on patient symptoms. For example, there is less need for a pH study before surgery in patients with a high‐risk hernia, though having a baseline measurement could be helpful. For patients with symptoms beyond those of classic reflux, such as regurgitation and belching, impedance measurements are more important, as such symptoms can be a sign of a motility disorder. For more typical reflux symptoms, capsule monitoring may be sufficient.

Speakers also expanded on the differences between catheter‐based and wireless pH monitoring, while several stressed the importance of impedance measurements. One of the key advantages of impedance is the ability to measure mean nocturnal baseline impedance (MNBI), which reflects mucosal impairment and correlates with severe GERD. Beyond that, wireless monitoring is often preferred due to ease of use and the longer timeframe. Speakers also generally prefer wireless monitoring before sending a patient to surgery to ensure they are picking up on the day‐to‐day variability of symptoms.

One major drawback of catheter‐based monitoring is patient discomfort. Catheters can also trigger reactions such as coughing or throat clearing that can be hard to differentiate from symptoms. Therefore, in patients already presenting with these symptoms, wireless monitoring may be more appropriate, though extra‐esophageal symptoms during catheter‐based monitoring can also identify patients with hypersensitivity in the airway or throat.

Speakers also expanded on the potential use of salivary biomarkers to not only diagnose conditions like GERD but also to rule them out and prevent unnecessary treatment and surgery. Another topic was interpreting discrepant results between endoscopy and manometry. There is evidence that HRM is more reliable at identifying hiatal hernia than endoscopy or barium and offers high sensitivity and high specificity. However, no matter the test used, speakers warned against relying on endoscopic classifications alone. Physicians should instead recognize general trends and the overall status of the patient. They recommended looking at the worst‐case scenario if some tests indicate a hernia and others do not.

Finally, speakers expressed skepticism about the role of the diaphragm in reflux disease. Breathing exercises are often recommended to strengthen the reflux barrier and may have benefits by stabilizing vagal function, reducing abdominal pressure, and improving functional bloating, belching, and reflux hypersensitivity. However, they are unlikely to meaningfully improve reflux outside of certain populations, such as those with postprandial reflux.

Session 2: Leveraging New Acid Suppressants in Reflux Management (Chairs: John Clarke and Kristle Lynch)

The second session focused on antireflux medications. For a long time, PPIs and H2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs) dominated the reflux treatment space. Despite the use of antisecretory therapies, unmet needs remain. First, not all patients experience symptom relief. There is a need to better control acid secretion and symptoms, particularly at night, to minimize sleep disruption. In addition, extra‐esophageal manifestations of reflux disease are often poorly managed [34, 35]. Speakers talked about their experience with current GERD therapies and their perspectives on how P‐CABs have changed and will continue to change treatment.

P‐CABs: A New Avenue in Acid Suppression

Carmelo Scarpignato from the United Campus of Malta and the University of Nantes discussed P‐CABs. P‐CABs have a similar mechanism to PPIs—both inhibit proton pump function. However, P‐CABs do so by interfering with potassium availability. They reversibly bind to the potassium binding site of the proton pump, thus inhibiting its function. Unlike PPIs, P‐CABs can block all the available proton pumps, not only those present before a meal, and their full effect is achieved after the first dose, while PPIs require 3−5 days to reach maximum effect [36].

There are currently four approved P‐CABs: vonoprazan, fexuprazan, tegoprazan, and keverprazan. Scarpignato focused on vonoprazan, which was first approved in Japan in 2015 and later in the United States in 2022 for the treatment of H. pylori infection and subsequently for the treatment of erosive and nonerosive reflux disease.

Pharmacokinetic (PK) data show no difference in vonoprazan PK parameters between Japanese and Caucasian individuals [37]. This allows researchers to extrapolate the wealth of PK data from Japanese populations to Caucasian populations, showing no impact of age, race, sex, weight, or renal function on vonoprazan levels [38, 39].

Vonoprazan has also been shown to be superior to PPIs in clinical trials. It provides longer gastric acid control—approximately 21 h compared to 14 h with esomeprazole [40, 41]—and was shown to provide better healing and maintenance than lansoprazole in patients with erosive esophagitis [42]. Recent meta‐analyses have also shown that vonoprazan is more effective and safer than other options for healing [43, 44].

In conclusion, the pharmacologic properties of P‐CABs underline their high efficacy in both erosive and nonerosive reflux disease. They have more effective and longer‐acting antisecretory activity than PPIs, covering both day and night, and suppress acid independent of a meal and CYP450 activity [45].

Optimizing PPI and H2RA Use in Reflux Disease

Ellen Stein from Robert Wood Johnson Medical School discussed strategies to optimize PPI and H2RA dosing. Several of the recommendations for PPI and H2RA dosing can be difficult for patients to adhere to, and with the advent of P‐CABs, some of these strategies may become irrelevant. That said, defining appropriate treatment algorithms is important for more effective, timely symptom relief. The randomized controlled trial, OSCAR, showed that patients who receive instructions on how to correctly take PPIs experience better symptom relief than those who take the drug however they want [46].

Recommendations for PPIs include that they be taken no more than 30 min before the first meal of the day, which can be difficult for patients to predict. There are data to support dosing anywhere from 30 to 60 min prior to eating, which gives patients more flexibility [47].

There are several algorithms for adjusting PPI dosing based on symptom response. The AGA recommends starting with once‐daily PPI dosing for 4−8 weeks and de‐escalating therapy if symptoms improve. If not, they recommend escalating to twice daily dosing, which has been shown to improve healing of erosive esophagitis as well as heartburn relief, but Stein noted that it can be difficult for patients to time the dose with their eating schedule. If symptoms continue to persist after 1 year on PPIs, a more thorough evaluation for reflux is warranted to confirm GERD before committing to lifelong therapy [48].

Stein also described several populations who need special considerations when taking a PPI, as they can reduce the efficacy of several drugs, including anticancer agents [49] and therapies for HCV and HIV [48]. In addition, for patients with special anatomy, such as those who have undergone gastric bypass surgery, the PPI capsule can prevent proper absorption. Opening the capsule can improve PPI efficacy and healing [50, 51].

With regard to H2RAs, guidelines recommend they be taken in the evening to boost nighttime acid reflux control, but tachyphylaxis limits long‐term use and often necessitates drug holidays.

In summary, optimizing PPI and H2RA dosing requires adequate timing of dose and dose escalation or switching therapies if symptoms are not improving. Ultimately, the goal is to wean patients down to the lowest effective dose and identify patients who need other solutions, such as surgery.

When to Consider P‐CABs in Reflux Management

Ronnie Fass from Case Western Reserve University discussed current and potential applications for P‐CABs in reflux management. Despite the clinical advantages of P‐CABs, a 2024 AGA clinical update was less than enthusiastic about recommending them in clinical practice, citing concerns on cost, insurance coverage, lack of direct comparison with twice‐daily PPIs, and potential adverse events [52].

A key consideration is that current P‐CABs were developed and studied in Asia, primarily in homogeneous populations with different treatment paradigms. For example, in Japan, standard dosing of PPIs is half of that in the United States and Europe, and persistent GERD is defined as failure of once‐daily PPI, a lower threshold than typically used in the United States. These factors raise questions about whether clinical data from Asian countries are applicable to other populations.

Fass outlined several potential treatment opportunities for P‐CABs across different esophageal diseases, some with more data than others [53]. For example, in erosive esophagitis, data suggest that P‐CABs are as effective as PPIs in healing mild cases (Los Angeles grades A and B) but clearly outperform PPIs in more advanced grades (C and D) [54]. Switching to a P‐CAB is effective in esophageal healing in patients who failed PPI.

Treatment opportunities in nonerosive reflux disease (NERD) include on‐demand treatment and patients who need double‐dose PPI. Fass's group recently demonstrated that P‐CABs were more effective than placebo when used on‐demand, and another study demonstrated similar results when a P‐CAB was compared to a PPI [55]. Beyond NERD, P‐CABs may offer benefits in managing complex or refractory GERD, such as those occurring after POEM or sleeve gastrectomy procedures, or in patients with comorbidities like scleroderma, although they have yet to be studied in these populations [53].

Other opportunities for P‐CABs include using them to diagnose GERD instead of subjecting patients to invasive testing, better control of nighttime GERD, in which P‐CABs have been shown to be more effective than PPIs, NCCP due to GERD, and in patients who struggle with proper PPI timing and compliance [56].

Overall, while more diverse and U.S.‐based data are needed, P‐CABs appear to offer a valuable alternative in specific GERD scenarios where PPIs fall short.

Safety Profile and Side Effects of Acid Suppressants

Jose Remes‐Troches from Universidad Veracruzana discussed the safety profiles of P‐CABs and PPIs. Both PPIs and P‐CABs share mild short‐term side effects such as headache, diarrhea, nausea, and abdominal pain. However, these are rarely reasons for discontinuation. The more pressing issue is long‐term use of PPIs. This concern has been amplified by media coverage and patient awareness, complicating conversations about stopping or reducing PPI use. Evidence shows that primary care physicians are more likely than GI specialists to switch patients off a PPI instead of dose reducing [57]. Two important papers challenged many of these perceived risks, emphasizing that correlation does not imply causation [58, 59].

Many long‐term side effects attributed to PPIs—like fractures, osteoporosis, renal failure, and dementia—have weak causality when assessed using the Hill criteria for establishing causation (Figure 1). While certain risks, such as fundic gland polyps and possibly infections like spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and Clostridioides difficile (C. difficile), are more strongly supported, their absolute risk remains low, and much of the data arise from observational studies prone to bias.

Regarding P‐CABs, safety data are limited, and most studies have been conducted in Japan. Remes‐Troches noted that there is a significant amount of structural variability among the P‐CABs, so it may be difficult to generalize a class‐wide safety profile.

While there is evidence that the more intense acid suppression with P‐CABs could alter the microbiome, the clinical significance of this is unclear [60], as data show that the risk of C. difficile infection is similar between vonoprazan and PPIs [61].

One recent concerning report was a 2024 population‐based Japanese study that showed an association between high‐dose vonoprazan and increased gastric cancer risk following H. pylori eradication [62]. Remes‐Troches cautioned against reading too much into this study, given several limitations, such as including unverified H. pylori status and failure to control for key confounders like family history.

Another important observation from long‐term P‐CAB use, specifically vonoprazan, is increased gastrin levels. While 5‐year follow‐up data from the VISION study showed sustained hypergastrinemia, there was no link between elevated gastrin levels and neoplastic or dysplastic changes [63].

Remes‐Troches concluded that both PPIs and P‐CABs appear safe for short‐term use. For PPIs, long‐term safety concerns should be approached critically, while more data are needed to assess the long‐term effects of P‐CABs.

On‐Demand Versus Daily Acid Suppressants: When and Which Agents

Abraham Khan from NYU Langone Health discussed recommendations and data for on‐demand and daily acid suppressants.

Current treatment guidelines, including the ACG GERD guidelines and AGA clinical practice update, broadly recommend weaning patients off daily therapy if they experience symptom relief and considering on‐demand treatment [48, 64]. Systematic reviews suggest that on‐demand therapy is equivalent to continuous use and better than placebo for uncomplicated GERD and that dose reduction and discontinuation can be achieved for a significant portion of patients without symptom relapse [65, 66].

Given the lack of evidence on how to taper therapy, there are no recommendations on how to wean patients off daily treatment. Khan made the case for wireless pH monitoring to assess whether patients can safely transition off daily medication. Multiday data allow clinicians to correlate reflux patterns with lifestyle factors such as diet, weight, and sleeping habits. For example, if a patient who is overweight only experiences postprandial reflux, weight loss may facilitate discontinuation of daily acid suppression. A study of ∼100 patients showed that extended wireless pH testing can be used to guide patients off daily therapy [67].

Regarding which on‐demand therapy to use, H2RAs have several characteristics that make them particularly suited for on‐demand use. They have a short duration of action and can be used up to twice daily. While tachyphylaxis can occur, it is less concerning in an on‐demand context. In contrast, PPIs are less ideal due to their delayed onset and concerns about acid rebound when discontinued abruptly.

Emerging data on P‐CABs show promise for on‐demand treatment. Two recent studies have demonstrated superior performance of P‐CABs over PPIs in this setting, making them a potential future option for patients with uncomplicated GERD [55, 68].

Innovation: GERD Symptom Prompts: The Role for In‐Office Versus At‐Home Assessment of Disease Severity

Multiple studies have shown poor adherence to PPI therapy, which contributes to uncontrolled symptoms and unnecessary referrals to surgery [69]. Afrin Kamal Rahman from Stanford University discussed the development of a new mobile health (mHealth) application, or app, to help patients assess their GERD symptoms with the goal of improving patient adherence.

Rahman described low patient activation—the confidence, skillset, and knowledge to take care of one's health—as a key contributor to poor adherence. In a VA study in the United States, nearly half of patients with reflux disease had low activation. Rahman also showed that many clinicians are unfamiliar with the concept of patient activation and are not necessarily open to changing their communication style among low‐activation patients [70]. In busy clinics, it can be difficult to form a strong physician−patient relationship. Lack of trust and communication can undermine adherence if patients feel unheard or unsupported.

To address this, Rahman is developing digital health tools to improve patient activation. Mobile health applications can provide support outside of the clinic, incorporating gamified features like challenges and streaks to enhance engagement. In 2018, she developed a paper‐based question prompt list for patients with reflux to empower patients to ask targeted, disease‐specific questions during visits and foster dialogue and patient engagement.

She is now working on a mobile version. Reflux patients were involved throughout the development process to understand real‐life concerns and identify key themes that impact patients, such as the impact of reflux on social life. Next steps include usability testing and collecting data to prepare for a randomized controlled trial. Ultimately, Rahman will measure whether this mHealth app will improve medication adherence and disease management through personalized, patient‐driven digital interventions.

Session 2: Discussion

The panel discussion highlighted the evolving role of P‐CABs. Some argued for broad use of P‐CABs as first‐line therapy, noting that they offer all the benefits of PPIs with additional advantages, such as faster onset of action and sustained acid suppression. Other speakers stressed the need for a more personalized approach, given cost concerns and a lack of long‐term safety data.

Speakers agreed that P‐CABs are a powerful new tool but not necessarily a universal replacement for PPIs. For some diseases, such as eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE), PPIs may be preferable due to their non‐acid‐related anti‐inflammatory effects, which P‐CABs may lack. Speakers, therefore, underscored the importance of patient phenotyping and pH testing to avoid overprescription.

Long‐term safety also remains a key concern. While PPIs have been widely used for decades with reassuring safety profiles, P‐CABs lack such long‐term data. Due to their more potent acid suppression, P‐CABs have exaggerated acid suppression‐related side effects compared to PPIs. Of particular note is hypergastrinemia and its potential downstream effects. Fortunately, the various P‐CABs have distinct structures, which likely means they have unique safety profiles. Therefore, if a patient is intolerant to one P‐CAB, they may be able to switch to another.

Speakers also discussed whether P‐CABs should be part of a step‐up approach in GERD. Ultimately, the approach depends on the patient phenotype. In patients with grade C or D esophagitis, P‐CABs may be part of a step‐up approach. In those with less severe disease, clinicians and patients can weigh the pros and cons of P‐CABs as part of a shared decision‐making approach. While some may see P‐CABs as “killing a fly with a sledgehammer” in patients with nonerosive disease, the flexibility offered by on‐demand dosing may be attractive for patients. They cautioned against repeating past mistakes with PPIs, particularly over‐the‐counter availability, which led to overuse without formal diagnosis, and starting patients on high‐dose therapy without a clear exit strategy. Patient personality can also come into play. Some highly symptomatic or anxious patients may be hesitant to taper or discontinue medication.

In conclusion, P‐CABs offer significant therapeutic opportunities. They are most promising for severe erosive disease, partial PPI responders, or as on‐demand therapy. Thoughtful shared decision‐making approaches rooted in diagnosis, patient characteristics, and long‐term strategy are essential to avoid overuse and ensure optimal outcomes.

Session 3. Adjunctive Medical Management: Mucosal Protectants, Reflux Reducers, and Neuromodulators (Chairs: Marcelo Vela and Justin Wu)

Session 3 focused on adjunctive medical treatments for reflux disease. While acid suppression with PPIs and P‐CABs remains foundational, many patients continue to experience symptoms, highlighting the need for additional targets. Adjunctive therapies can improve esophageal acid clearance, support mucosal defenses, and increase gastric emptying. Targeting multiple components of the reflux pathway may ultimately enable physicians to tailor treatment to individual patient needs and improve long‐term outcomes.

Behavioral and Lifestyle Approaches

Kristle Lynch from The University of Pennsylvania discussed behavioral and lifestyle approaches to managing reflux, including tobacco use, diet, eating patterns, sleeping position, and weight management.

Regarding smoking, there are many reasons why tobacco use may exacerbate GERD symptoms. It can affect esophageal acid clearance, possibly due to reduced saliva and bicarbonate secretion, and reduce LES pressure. While some studies, including a large cohort study of 29,000 individuals, have demonstrated an improvement in reflux symptoms in those who stop smoking, data are inconsistent [71, 72]. For this reason, smoking cessation is not routinely recommended by the ACG or AGA to manage GERD symptoms [48, 64].

Dietary changes are common among patients with reflux, particularly the elimination of perceived trigger foods like caffeine, carbonated drinks, alcohol, and spicy foods. Possible mechanisms include increased gastric distension, TLESRs, reduced LES tone, and esophageal irritation, but robust causal links remain unproven. While some studies have shown that restricting trigger foods can lead to symptom relief, meta‐analyses and population‐based studies have shown no clear association between specific food triggers and GERD symptoms [73, 74, 75, 76, 77]. Neither the ACG nor AGA recommend global elimination of trigger foods to manage GERD symptoms [48, 64].

Other dietary changes used to ameliorate GERD symptoms include avoiding high‐fat, high‐carbohydrate, or calorie‐dense meals as well as late‐night eating (Figure 2). Fats and carbohydrates may reduce LES tone and/or gastric motility, while calorie‐dense meals can increase gastric distension and TLESRs. Observational studies focused on fat intake have been inconsistent. Regarding carbohydrates, several studies on replacing simple sugars with fiber have revealed a significant impact on GERD symptoms. Most prospective studies on meal timing show some benefit of earlier dinners and less calorie‐dense meals [78, 79, 80, 81]. Based on these data, avoiding late‐night eating is recommended by the ACG with a low level of evidence [64].

Mechanisms of action of meals with varied dietary compositions and eating patterns (courtesy of Kristle Lynch).

Sleeping position, specifically head‐of‐bed elevation, has been shown to reduce AET and improve reflux symptoms, including in patients with erosive esophagitis, and is recommended by the ACG with a low level of evidence [82, 83, 84].

Weight management is another important factor, as GERD and complications like Barrett's esophagus and esophageal cancer are linked to obesity. Randomized clinical trials and cohort studies have shown a decrease in AET and improvement in GERD symptoms in patients assigned to attempts at controlled weight loss [85, 86]. The ACG and AGA recommend weight loss for patients who are overweight to help manage GERD symptoms [48, 64].

Reflux Reducing Agents

Daniela Jodorkovsky from Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai discussed data for the nonacid suppression agents baclofen, bethanecol, prucalopride, and metoclopramide. While these agents lack robust clinical data in patients with GERD, there is a mechanistic rationale and limited evidence to support their use in symptom management.

Baclofen, a GABA‐B agonist, has been shown to reduce TLESRs and increase LES tone in healthy individuals. In small, randomized studies, baclofen decreased TLESR count and reflux events while improving symptoms like belching and regurgitation. However, effects on AET were inconsistent, and there was no improvement in symptoms such as heartburn or chest pain [87, 88]. A meta‐analysis showed that baclofen has a modest benefit, reducing GERDQ scores, nonacid reflux events, and DeMeester score [89]. Jodorkovsky concluded that baclofen may be helpful to reduce belching and regurgitation, nonacid reflux, postprandial reflux, and potentially reflux hypersensitivity, but its use is often limited by side effects of sedation.

Bethanecol, a muscarinic agonist used for urinary retention, has been shown to increase LES tone in small studies from the 1970s [90, 91]. Bethanecol may also improve esophageal clearance by improving esophageal motility or strength. In a study of seven patients with IEM, distal esophageal amplitude increased after a single dose, and the percent of ineffective swallows decreased [91]. Therefore, although mechanistic studies hint at a possible benefit in improving LES tone and esophageal clearance, there are no robust clinical data supporting its use in GERD management.

Prucalopride, a 5‐HT4 agonist approved in the United States for chronic idiopathic constipation, may also offer benefit through its effects on gastric emptying. In one study, prucalopride reduced AET in healthy individuals without affecting the total number of reflux events, suggesting a potential mechanism through reduced gastric acid volume [92]. Prucalopride may also help with esophageal clearance, as some studies suggest that it can enhance esophageal peristalsis, particularly secondary peristalsis [93]. In children, prucalopride has been demonstrated to improve foregut symptoms, including GERD [94].

Metoclopramide, a dopamine antagonist, has been shown to reduce heartburn and regurgitation while enhancing LES pressure and gastric emptying. However, it did not enhance esophageal acid clearance, and its benefits in reducing esophageal acid exposure or improving symptoms remain uncertain [95, 96]. Moreover, its use is limited by the risk of tardive dyskinesia with long‐term use, a rare but serious neurological adverse reaction.

Mucosal Protective Compounds for Treatment of GERD

Marcelo Vela from the Mayo Clinic discussed the emerging role of mucosal protective compounds in the treatment of GERD, focusing on approaches that enhance epithelial defense and repair. These approaches aim either to improve cytoprotection—the natural defense function of the esophageal mucosa—or to use bioadhesive compounds that physically coat and protect the esophageal mucosa from refluxate.

Data on agents that enhance cytoprotection are limited. Rebamipide, which increases prostaglandin production in the esophageal mucosa, has been shown to reduce symptom relapse when used in combination with a PPI in patients with mild esophagitis who initially responded to PPI therapy [97]. However, in a larger trial of patients with PPI‐refractory NERD, there was no benefit of rebamipide over placebo [98].

Topical mucosal protection is another promising area to maintain mucosal integrity. Alginate has shown potential in vitro, where it was found to reduce esophageal mucosal permeability by increasing transepithelial resistance in cell culture models [99].

Current work is focused on combining compounds like alginate with bioadhesives so that they stay in the esophagus longer. One such formulation is Esoxx, a compound containing hyaluronic acid and chondroitin sulfate in a bioadhesive carrier (poloxamer 407). In a randomized study of patients with NERD, the addition of Esoxx to PPI therapy resulted in greater symptom improvement and enhanced quality of life compared to PPI plus placebo [100]. A second bioadhesive, poliprotect, was shown to be noninferior to omeprazole in improving heartburn symptoms [101].

While these data are promising, they are preliminary. Neither the ACG GERD guideline nor the AGA clinical practice update on GERD mentions mucosal protectants. However, recently published Italian GERD guidelines do recommend esophageal mucosal protectants as an add‐on therapy for GERD, though this recommendation is weak and based on low‐level evidence [102].

The Status of Pepsin Inhibitors for PPI‐Refractory GERD and LPR

Alexandra Vandenberg from Nikki Johnston's lab at the Medical College of Wisconsin presented an update on the development of pepsin inhibitors as a novel therapeutic for PPI‐refractory GERD and LPR. PPIs are often ineffective in patients with LPR and a subset of patients with GERD. Nonacidic refluxate components like pepsin can damage the laryngopharynx.

Pepsin is a key enzyme present in all refluxate and strongly linked to LPR inflammation, tissue damage, and symptoms, making it a promising therapeutic target. Several drugs approved for HIV treatment also inhibit pepsin activity. These drugs are generally well tolerated, and the Johnston lab is working to repurpose these agents and develop both oral and inhaled formulations for the treatment of GERD and LPR [103].

Preclinical studies in cell and animal models of GERD and LPR have shown that fosamprenavir and similar drugs like darunavir can effectively inhibit pepsin activity. In an LPR mouse model, both oral and inhaled forms of fosamprenavir prevented pepsin‐mediated inflammation and tissue damage. Among the drugs tested, fosamprenavir demonstrated the most consistent efficacy and has the best safety profile and lower cost, making it the lead candidate for further development [103].

Inhaled delivery of fosamprenavir was particularly promising, demonstrating greater efficacy than oral administration at a reduced dose with no observed toxicity or inflammation [104].

Johnston's group is finalizing an IND application for phase 2 clinical trials to assess the efficacy of fosamprenavir for the treatment of LPR and PPI‐refractory GERD. They hope to start trials using generic fosamprenavir tablets for LPR in early 2026 and a sodium alginate formulation designed to improve esophageal delivery in patients with GERD in early 2027.

When Are Neuromodulators Needed in Symptomatic Reflux Disease?

Braden Kuo from Columbia University Vagelos College of Physicians and Surgeons discussed the use of neuromodulators in reflux disease. Neuromodulators are commonly used in clinical practice for challenging cases, particularly patients who remain symptomatic despite PPI therapy. These patients typically present with persistent symptoms such as heartburn or chest discomfort, even when endoscopy is normal or shows minimal changes.

For patients not responding to PPIs, pH testing is often the next step. When pH studies are normal, especially in those with persistent symptoms and normal mucosa, neuromodulation becomes a consideration. For patients with normal esophageal mucosa experiencing partial symptom relief from acid suppression, Kuo recommended conducting pH testing on therapy to determine what percentage of their symptoms is due to nonacidic causes.

Kuo stressed that patients who are candidates for neuromodulators often have other issues, such as sensitivity to medications, a history of drug intolerance, or multiple drug allergies, that can impact their ability to take therapy. Building trust and carefully explaining the nonpsychiatric use of these medications is, therefore, critical to manage sensation and motility, not mood. Depending on the patient type and tolerances, Kuo recommended considering starting at ultra‐low doses to build therapeutic confidence.

Choosing a neuromodulator requires a tailored approach that considers factors like comorbidities, cardiac sensitivity, autonomic sensitivity, other neurological issues, and chronic pain issues. Tricyclic antidepressants are effective but require caution due to anticholinergic effects and QT prolongation. SSRIs and SNRIs can affect GI motility and central nervous system function, potentially causing diarrhea or sleep disturbances. Gabapentinoids like gabapentin are favored for their titratability, especially in liquid form, allowing ultra‐low, tolerable doses that can be adjusted based on response. Prucalopride is another option for patients with significant constipation, targeting peripheral serotonergic pathways. In complex cases, stacking neuromodulators with different mechanisms may be appropriate.

In summary, for patients with persistent reflux symptoms unresponsive to PPIs and negative diagnostic findings, neuromodulation is a critical therapeutic pathway. Drug choice and dosing must be highly individualized, accounting for GI function, comorbidities, and patient sensitivity.

Innovation: What Are the Roles of Sodium Alginates and Amprenavir Against Pepsin‐Mediated Esophageal and Laryngeal Damage?

Pelin Ergun from Nikki Johnston's lab at the Medical College of Wisconsin presented preclinical research on sodium alginates and amprenavir to prevent pepsin‐mediated esophageal and laryngeal damage.

Sodium alginates form a physical antireflux barrier over the gastric acid layer, limiting the upward movement of refluxate into the esophagus and airways. This barrier can trap harmful refluxate components like pepsin and bile acids. Alginates can also provide topical protection to the sensitive esophageal mucosa, reducing inflammation caused by refluxate components. Preclinical studies showed that sodium alginate preserves epithelial integrity against refluxate in both esophageal and laryngeal cells. Specifically, sodium alginate was shown to prevent pepsin‐induced cleavage of E‐cadherin, a critical adhesion molecule, and to downregulate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), enzymes involved in tissue remodeling and inflammation.

In vitro RNA sequencing showed that pretreatment with sodium alginate rescued pepsin‐damage pathways involved in extracellular matrix repair, cell junction integrity, and inflammation in normal esophageal cells [99, 105, 106, 107, 108, 109].

Given the central role of pepsin in GERD and LPR, the Johnston lab is also interested in directly inhibiting pepsin activity. Ergun focused on amprenavir and its prodrug fosamprenavir, both of which are FDA approved for the treatment of HIV and have been shown to bind to and inhibit pepsin [103, 110].

In an established in vivo mouse model of LPR, amprenavir abrogated pepsin‐mediated laryngeal inflammation and mucosal damage [103]. RNA sequencing of esophageal and laryngeal cells revealed that amprenavir suppresses pepsin‐induced inflammatory pathways and activates structural integrity genes [111], while reducing E‐cadherin cleavage and regulating MMP activity [112, 113].

Johnston's group is developing a novel sodium alginate–based formulation of fosamprenavir designed to enhance mucosal adhesion and localized drug delivery. Future research will include studies in porcine esophageal models to assess mucoadhesion and drug conversion, as well as ex vivo human biopsy studies to evaluate mucosal permeability. Additional in vitro testing will use patient‐derived gastric juice to confirm efficacy in clinically relevant conditions. They hope that this combination may offer a safer and more effective therapy for patients with PPI‐refractory GERD or LPR.

Session 3 Discussion

During the panel discussion, panelists expanded on the diagnostic complexity of LPR and the challenges posed by the heterogeneity of GERD, thus necessitating an individualized approach. Unlike GERD, there are limited standardized diagnostic tools for LPR. While speakers highlighted the potential for pepsin as both a biomarker and therapeutic target, the story is far from simple. The mere presence of salivary pepsin may not be indicative of damage or disease, and methods to measure salivary pepsin have reproducibility issues. In terms of treatment, pepsin is thought to contribute to the inflammation that causes the LPR symptoms, supporting the work by Johnston's lab to develop pepsin inhibitors for the treatment of LPR. However, identifying suitable patients remains a challenge. Current trials focus on those with nonacid reflux, a group for whom PPIs offer limited benefit.

Speakers also highlighted the fact that GERD is not a single biological condition. Acid reflux, bile reflux, and reflux hypersensitivity represent mechanistically distinct entities that often respond differently to therapy. The current practice of focusing on acid suppression may miss these nuances. This may lead to continued long‐term manifestations of GERD despite symptom improvement and mucosal healing. Speakers were broadly in support of starting patients on PPIs. Many patients do well on acid suppression therapy. However, they noted the importance of early testing in refractory patients over cycling through PPIs to really get at the mechanism of disease and determine whether something like neuromodulators is appropriate.

In terms of weaning people off an adjunctive therapy like neuromodulators, speakers focused on making sure patients are symptom‐free and considering their comorbidities. Patients with a lot of comorbidities, such as anxiety, depression, or other chronic pain, may need chronic neuromodulator therapy and/or a very gradual taper. Another option speakers brought up is GI psychology. While this can be a great option for patients, not everyone has easy access to a GI psychologist. Additionally, patients might be hesitant to shift from a pharmaceutical therapy to a psychological approach. Establishing a trust‐based relationship is, therefore, important.

Ultimately, understanding the patient's personal goals and needs is key to effective therapy. For example, some patients may be unwilling to give up certain foods. For them, treatment may focus on a more optimized medication approach. For everyone, lifestyle changes are difficult, especially to commit to long‐term. Speakers advised focusing on short‐term changes to help motivate patients to incorporate them into their lives as their symptoms improve.

Session 4. Advances in Invasive Reflux Management (Chairs: Stefano Siboni and Edoardo Savarino)

This session focused on the surgical management of reflux. Speakers focused on various methods of anatomic reconstruction and restoration of the EGJ barrier. Techniques covered include new endoscopic approaches, TIF and concomitant TIF (c‐TIF), magnetic sphincter augmentation (MSA), RefluxStop, and the role of bariatric surgery to address reflux.

TIF and c‐TIF

Sigh Pichamol Jirapinyo of Harvard Medical School discussed recent advancements in TIF and its combination with surgical hernia repair, c‐TIF. TIF is an endoscopic procedure in which the EsophyX Z device and gastroscope are passed into the stomach and positioned at the GEJ to create a 270‐degree partial, 3‐cm wrap to reconstruct the antireflux barrier.

Four randomized controlled trials have demonstrated consistent efficacy and safety results for TIF. An initial study showed its superiority to a sham procedure. The RESPECT trial subsequently demonstrated its superiority to PPIs in treating regurgitation. Longer‐term data show durable results with up to 5‐year follow‐up in the TEMPO trial and 10 years in the Testoni trial. These studies show that the majority of patients experience improvement in regurgitation and atypical GERD symptoms, and many can discontinue PPI therapy.

From a practical standpoint, indications for TIF include BMI <35 kg/m2, Hill grade 1 or 2, hiatal hernia, and typical or atypical GERD symptoms. Patients who are unresponsive, intolerant to, or do not want to be on long‐term PPIs can also be suitable candidates. A typical workup includes an EGD to assess for esophagitis, Barrett's esophagus, and hernia size, along with FLIP and Bravo pH testing to confirm pathologic acid exposure and symptom correlation. Contraindications include severe esophagitis (grades C or D), dysplastic Barrett's esophagus, and achalasia.

For patients with larger hiatal hernias (>2 cm) or Hill grade 3 or 4, a c‐TIF is recommended. In these cases, the hernia is first repaired by the foregut surgeon. The endoscopist then performs the TIF during the same session. A randomized controlled trial comparing c‐TIF to laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication with hernia repair has completed enrollment.

Both TIF and c‐TIF are recommended in the recent ASGE guidelines on the diagnosis and management of GERD published in 2025 [114].

Magnetic Sphincter Augmentation

Emanuele Asti from Universita degli Studi di Milano presented an overview of MSA for GERD. The MSA device, known commercially as LINX, is a standardized, minimally invasive alternative to long‐term PPI use and traditional fundoplication. Since its introduction, over 50,000 devices have been implanted globally.

The efficacy and safety of MSA were initially established in a narrow patient population—those with abnormal pH monitoring, early stage disease, no obesity or large hiatal hernia, and mild esophagitis (grade A). Over time, the indication has broadened to include patients with more severe disease, larger hernias, advanced esophagitis (grades C/D), and those needing reflux treatment after bariatric surgery [115, 116, 117].

Several studies have shown durable responses with MSA, including improvement of GERD HRQOL score and discontinuation of PPIs [118].

Early implants were done with a tight device, potentially leading to complications, such as dysphagia, epigastric pain, and heartburn, that required explantation. Asti showed that placing a larger device can reduce the rate of explantation from 23% to 5%. However, dysphagia remains a persistent problem, with a recent meta‐analysis showing higher rates of dysphagia following MSA than fundoplication. Unexpectedly, upsizing does not change bolus clearance or dysphagia rates, and the reason for dysphagia following MSA remains unclear. In addition, approximately one‐third of patients do not have pH normalization [119].

MSA is a relatively new surgical device, and there are several questions about how to optimize its use. First, there is a balance to be struck between upsizing enough to reduce dysphagia but not too much to limit efficacy. In addition, patients with advanced disease and positive pH testing may need additional treatment.

RefluxStop

Salvatore Tolone from the University of Campania Luigi Vanvitelli discussed RefluxStop, a new surgical device consisting of an implantable silicone ball placed on the gastric fundus to anchor the esophagus in the abdomen [120]. The goal is to prevent acid reflux without compressing the food passageway, offering a dynamic and anatomical approach to reflux control.

Clinical data for RefluxStop are promising but limited. In a pilot study of 45 patients, all were pH‐negative 6 months after the procedure, with only 2% requiring daily PPIs [120]. Other studies reported significant improvements in GERD‐related QOL [121, 122, 123]. Surgical complications are similar to those of other antireflux surgeries, including esophageal tears and lateral breach. One key concern with RefluxStop is dysphagia and dilation, which is seen in approximately 15% of patients. Tolone noted this is likely due to the mispositioning of the device. Long‐term data are available up to 4 years of follow‐up, demonstrating good symptom control [124, 125, 126].

While current data are promising, Tolone highlighted several issues with available trials. In particular, it is unclear if all the patients included truly had GERD, as preoperative assessment did not always include pH monitoring or HRM.

Tolone noted that data supporting RefluxStop show a strong improvement in symptoms and QOL. There is a need, however, for more robust trials with standard, objective pre‐ and postoperative assessments, including physiologic evaluation, to better understand the impact of RefluxStop.

Bariatric Approaches to Managing Reflux Disease

Eric Sheu from Harvard Medical School discussed bariatric approaches to managing reflux disease. While there are many types of bariatric operations, the most relevant for primary reflux is the Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass.

Gastric bypass is highly effective for long‐term weight loss in patients with obesity. A 12‐year study demonstrated durable weight loss of approximately 27% of total body weight [127]. In addition, bypass surgery has positive outcomes on metabolic comorbidities: reversing type 2 diabetes, alleviating sleep apnea, and reducing hypertension.

Regarding reflux, studies of gastric bypass demonstrated symptomatic improvement and reduction in esophageal pH in patients with reflux [128]. There are several proposed mechanisms, including a reduction in acid volume and increased gastric emptying, thus limiting esophageal exposure. Importantly, unlike other procedures discussed, gastric bypass does not directly modify the structure or function of the GEJ. Its reflux benefits are largely secondary effects.

A 2020 white paper from the SAGES Foregut group called gastric bypass the gold standard for reflux in people with a BMI of 35 or higher [129]. Since then, professional societies have tempered their enthusiasm as newer studies show fundoplication may have better effects on abnormal pH and symptoms but poor longer‐term durability in patients with elevated BMI [130]. A recent task force from primarily surgical societies, as well as the AGA, now recommends considering a bypass in patients with a BMI over 35 [48, 131]. The appropriate cutoff for deciding between fundoplication and gastric bypass remains to be determined.

Gastric bypass may also have potential in patients who failed a fundoplication. A systematic review showed 93% improvement in symptoms with short‐term follow‐up in this population [132].

Sheu concluded that gastric bypass can effectively reduce GERD, at least in the short term. More data are needed on the durability of response as well as how gastric bypass compares to fundoplication. He recommended considering it in patients with a BMI over 35 and metabolic comorbidities or in those who have failed a fundoplication.

Complications of Invasive Antireflux Therapy

Renato Salvador from the University of Padova reviewed some of the complications of invasive antireflux therapies: laparoscopic fundoplication, MSA, RefluxStop, and TIF, focusing on intraoperative, postoperative, and long‐term concerns.

Laparoscopic fundoplication carries risks at each stage. Intraoperatively, esophageal or gastric perforation may occur, and there is a risk of mortality. Postoperative complications include dysphagia, gas bloating, and inability to belch [133]. These symptoms may persist if present early. Long‐term dysphagia can be addressed with dilation, although success can be limited [134].

Salvador was part of the group that developed the recent Padova Consensus, which provides guidance on incorporating HRM to postoperatively assess patients and better understand their symptoms [135]. The algorithm categorizes patients based on the separation between the LES and CD and the positioning of the high‐pressure zone. A high‐pressure zone above the CD may indicate a migrated wrap, while a high‐pressure zone below the CD may indicate a slipped wrap. This classification aims to guide clinicians in assessing the anatomy and function after fundoplication more accurately than traditional preoperative metrics.

Complications of MSA are similar to those of fundoplication. The major long‐term complications are dysphagia and gas bloat syndrome, which can affect more than 10% of patients [136].

For TIF, there is a higher risk of perforation than with fundoplication [137].

Finally, Salvador noted that RefluxStop is a new technique that requires a learning curve to optimally perform. However, longer‐term data and a better understanding of device migration will shed light on how to mitigate this risk.

Salvador concluded that these new antireflux procedures require careful revisions and optimizations to improve both functional results and complication rates. Assessing patients following procedures can be challenging and requires collaboration between the GI and surgeon. New tools, such as the Padova Classification, can help clinicians interpret HRM studies in the postoperative setting.

Innovation: Evaluation of the Esophagogastric Junction Barrier Before and After Intervention

Marco Sozzi from the University of Milan discussed methods to evaluate the EGJ barrier function and anatomy. Direct visualization of the EGJ via endoscopy can provide information on EGJ competence. Traditionally, the Hill classification has been used to grade the status of the EGJ; however, the recent AFS classification was developed to overcome its limitations via a more standardized evaluation of the EGJ components [31]. Recent work by Sozzi and colleagues has found that the AFS classification correlates well with pH testing results and manometry [138].

After antireflux procedures, endoscopy is used to distinguish between normal and altered anatomy. While there have been many attempts to standardize this description [139, 140], intersurvey variability remains high, highlighting the need to perform high‐quality endoscopies at referral centers.

Sozzi stressed the growing role of HRM in GERD analysis and management. The Padova Consensus emphasizes the role of HRM both preoperatively to evaluate EGJ barrier function and rule out conditions like achalasia as well as postoperatively, to assess anatomical and functional outcomes of antireflux surgery. The recently developed Milan score, which integrates four HRM variables, has been shown to predict abnormal pH exposure and correlate with GERD severity [27].

In addition, barium swallow can provide direct visualization of GEJ anatomy and function, but it suffers from low sensitivity and high interobserver variability [141, 142, 143]. Sozzi stressed this could be useful as an adjunct modality but should not be used as a standalone test.

Finally, FLIP is a newer technology to evaluate the EGJ. Results are inconsistent on the use of FLIP for GERD diagnosis. However, its role in intraoperative assessment of EGJ metrics is more established, with the potential to predict surgical outcomes.

In conclusion, there are multiple modalities to assess various aspects of the EGJ before and after surgery (summary in Figure 3). A high‐quality endoscopy and HRM can provide a comprehensive evaluation of EGJ anatomy and function. While barium swallows and FLIP can be useful as adjunct tools, there is a need for more evidence.

Session 4 Discussion

During the panel discussion, speakers discussed the potential future of some of the invasive antireflux procedures. In particular, there was skepticism about the utility of MSA, which has seen a sharp decline in the United States due to complications such as dysphagia and unanticipated tissue inflammation. While MSA may benefit select patients (e.g., young males with positive pH testing and high symptom burden), long‐term outcomes raise concerns. Dysphagia can persist even after symptom resolution of reflux, and device removal leaves scarring around the esophagus. Speakers anticipated that they would likely abandon the technique without stronger long‐term data. Regarding TIF, this is increasingly being used as a less invasive option, particularly in younger patients with severe esophagitis or Barrett's esophagus. Recent meta‐analyses show low complication rates, and proper technique, including esophageal dilation, can reduce risk.

The panel also discussed whether pH normalization is important if a patient's symptoms are controlled. Attendees argued that symptom relief is the main efficacy measure for PPIs, so why are surgical interventions held to a different standard? The counterpoint is that surgical procedures carry significant risk, and relieving symptoms is only one part of the equation. Objective acid control is also important, especially in patients at risk for complications like Barrett's esophagus, because acid exposure without symptoms can still damage the esophagus.

Session 5. Role of Reflux in Extra‐Esophageal Syndromes (Chairs: Daniel Sifrim and Nikki Johnston)

Reflux is often implicated in extra‐esophageal symptoms, such as hoarseness, chronic cough, and respiratory decline. Untangling the contribution of reflux to these symptoms from other possible etiologies and optimal management remains challenging. Traditionally, the framework for diagnosing reflux disease has been centered around GERD. While this has proven useful in guiding acid suppression therapy, it is less useful when symptoms are primarily laryngopharyngeal or pulmonary. This disconnect raises questions about the limitations of current diagnostic tools, the role of nonacid reflux, and the need for better characterization of reflux‐related syndromes across diverse populations. The session brought together experts in gastroenterology, otolaryngology, pulmonology, and pediatrics to explore the role of reflux in specific high‐complexity groups: adults with laryngopharyngeal symptoms, lung transplant recipients, and infants with brief resolved unexplained events (BRUE).

Key questions include when reflux truly contributes to upper aerodigestive symptoms, how to interpret impedance results when they do not match the clinical picture, and what constitutes sufficient evidence to justify medical, surgical, or behavioral treatment.

How Is Pharyngeal Reflux Diagnosed?

Inna Husain from Indiana University School of Medicine presented an overview of the challenges of diagnosing LPRD. This diagnosis is very common—approximately 30% of patients in ENT practices and 10% in GI settings are diagnosed with LPRD. That said, patients can struggle to get an accurate diagnosis of LPRD. Due to the long process of getting a diagnosis, the need to see multiple physicians, and suffering from symptoms, patients have high levels of anxiety and depression [144, 145, 146].

LPS, such as cough, throat clearing, and hoarseness, are nonspecific and represent protective mechanisms in the upper aerodigestive tract. They do not automatically equate to a diagnosis of LPRD or even reflux. As a result, defining and diagnosing LPRD is challenging and requires careful communication and consistent terminology among providers. As a result, the definition of LPRD has evolved and now includes not just the effects of direct refluxate on the laryngopharynx, but also indirect, vagally mediated responses [147, 148].

While acid suppression with PPIs is still used for some patients, Husain emphasized the need for nonacid suppression approaches, including alginate therapy, speech‐language pathology, neuromodulators, and superior laryngeal nerve blocks. Treatment should be guided by subtype and the underlying mechanism rather than reflexively assuming acid reflux.

Husain also stressed the need for better diagnostic tests. The current gold standard is hypopharyngeal‐esophageal multichannel intraluminal impedance‐pH testing (HEMII‐pH) [32]. However, this method is uncomfortable, expensive, and often poorly tolerated, limiting its utility in routine clinical practice.

New diagnostic tests are being developed, including ultrasound‐based tests to measure intraesophageal bolus noninvasively and in‐office laryngeal tissue biopsies to detect pepsin as a biomarker of reflux [149].

In conclusion, LPS are nonspecific and often attributed to LPRD. A true diagnosis and accurate subtyping can be time‐consuming, but are important for treatment and management. While HEMII‐pH remains the gold standard for the diagnosis of LPRD, new diagnostic methods are being developed.

Laryngo‐Pharyngeal Symptoms and Laryngopharyngeal Reflux Disease

Justin Wu from The Chinese University of Hong Kong discussed the complex relationship between LPS and LPR, emphasizing that while the terms are often used interchangeably, they represent distinct clinical entities.

LPR is defined as the retrograde flow of gastric contents above the upper esophageal sphincter (UES), causing LPS, which includes cough, throat clearing, globus, dysphonia, mucus sensation, and sore throat. While LPS can be indicative of LPR, there is poor correlation between LPS and LPR. A retrospective cohort study of patients with suspected LPR showed that those who tested positive for LPR via HEMII‐pH testing had similar symptoms to those who tested negative [150]. Up to 60% of patients with LPS have normal reflux monitoring, underscoring the diagnostic uncertainty that surrounds LPR [151].

Wu also highlighted the differences between LPRD and GERD. While GERD often involves LES dysfunction, occurs more when lying down, and is closely associated with obesity, LPRD typically involves UES dysfunction, is worse in upright posture, shows little correlation with obesity, and lacks classic GERD symptoms like heartburn [152, 153].

In terms of treatment, PPIs are largely ineffective in patients with persistent throat symptoms. In a randomized trial of patients with LPRD, there was no significant therapeutic benefit for PPIs over placebo [154]. However, some subgroups may benefit, such as patients with endoscopic esophagitis [155].

Due to the poor correlation between physiological studies and symptomatology, there is an emerging concept of a disordered interaction between the brain and larynx that results in laryngeal hypersensitivity [156]. Together with hypervigilance and anxiety, patients may have exaggerated coping responses that result in exaggerated laryngeal behaviors.

In summary, LPS are multifactorial, and LPR should not be assumed as the default cause. There is limited evidence supporting PPI use in patients with LPR, except in those with esophagitis. A growing body of research supports a neurosensory model, suggesting that behavioral therapies or neuromodulators may be more appropriate for managing LPS than traditional acid suppression.

Reflux and Voice Disorders

Anthony Hobson from The Functional Gut Clinic (London) discussed the complex relationship between reflux, the microbiome, and voice disorders, emphasizing the importance of the brain−throat−gut axis.

GERD is a potential contributor to voice disorders. While PPI therapy has not been shown to be effective at improving voice disorders, antireflux surgery has. Hobson argued that dysbiosis in the microbiome can contribute to many of the refractory symptoms seen in patients with GERD, such as belching, bloating, and regurgitation. In a study of patients undergoing antireflux surgery, 60% showed evidence of dysbiosis via a lactulose breath test, and those patients had a higher prevalence of persistent symptoms [157]. In healthy volunteers, subjects given highly fermentable sugars showed increased gas production and TLESRs, demonstrating a relationship between fermentation and gastroesophageal reflux [78].

Hobson proposed that bacterial fermentation in the gut may lead to weakly acidic refluxate, especially during the night, when swallowing is reduced, and neutralization of reflux is impaired. Nighttime drops in pH have been observed using LPR probes and advanced pH impedance monitoring. Hobson's group has identified a type of weakly acidic, weakly ionic reflux that comes up very quickly into the pharynx. During the day, this refluxate would normally be neutralized via swallowing, but at night, the refluxate remains and causes damage. Using VOC measurements in breath, Hobson's group has found that this refluxate consists of several short‐chain fatty acids and products of fermentation that could be potential targets for noninvasive assessment.

In summary, voice disorders related to reflux are multifactorial, involving a dynamic interplay of neurological, gastrointestinal, and microbial influences. Pharyngeal acid reflux is uncommon in voice disorders, and PPIs are largely ineffective and may worsen symptoms by promoting dysbiosis. Advanced diagnostics, including LPR probes, impedance monitoring, and breath analysis for fermentation markers, offer promising new directions for more accurate diagnosis and targeted treatment, which may include dietary or microbiome‐directed interventions.

Reflux and Pulmonary Disease

Nirmal Sharma from Harvard Medical School discussed the implications of reflux on pulmonary diseases, highlighting the gut−lung axis. Reflux can be an important contributor to lung disease via microaspiration, in which gastric contents enter the airways and damage the epithelium. This can trigger profibrotic inflammatory pathways, leading to fibrosis and potentially chronic lung disease. Additionally, vagal reflexes from distal esophageal acid exposure can cause bronchoconstriction, contributing to asthma and COPD symptoms. Medications used to treat these diseases (e.g., bronchodilators) may lower the LES, thereby worsening reflux. Treatment for reflux can also impact the lungs. It has been shown that PPIs change the upper airway and can cause downstream events that can lead to fibrosis and inflammation.

Clinical studies have shown a correlation between acid exposure and symptom severity and nighttime symptoms in asthma, COPD exacerbations, bronchiectasis, and chronic cough. Recently, a link between idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and reflux has also been seen, with up to 60% of patients with IPF showing abnormal pH‐impedance results, although this may be confounded by higher rates of smoking in this population [158, 159, 160].

Despite these correlations, data do not support routine treatment for reflux in patients with pulmonary disease if they do not have symptomatic acid reflux [161, 162]. Both asthma and IPF guidelines currently recommend against routine acid suppression or surgical treatment for reflux unless there is clear clinical or objective evidence of GERD [163]. Sharma emphasized a targeted, individualized approach—treating reflux when symptomatic and avoiding blanket therapy, especially considering potential adverse effects of long‐term PPIs, such as C. difficile infections, particularly in immunosuppressed patients.

Reflux and Lung Transplant Outcomes

Wai‐Kit Lo from Harvard Medical School discussed the impact of GERD on lung transplant outcomes. Lung transplant can increase the risk of reflux due to vagal nerve damage, impaired mucociliary function, delayed gastric emptying, and reduced esophageal clearance [164, 165, 166, 167]. These physiological changes raise concern for microaspiration, which can lead to airway inflammation, acute rejection, chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD), and graft failure.

Evidence from animal models on the impact of aspiration shows increased inflammatory responses in lung allografts. In humans, an increase in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) bile acids and pepsin post‐transplant has been associated with acute and chronic rejection [168, 169, 170, 171, 172].

Importantly, antireflux surgery, particularly fundoplication, may benefit lung transplant outcomes. A recent meta‐analysis of 20 studies confirmed that fundoplication improves allograft function and survival, with early intervention (within 6 months after transplant) yielding the best outcomes [173, 174, 175, 176].

Lo also showed that pretransplant measures of impedance and pH testing are linked to worse outcomes, including early rehospitalization, poorer post‐transplant pulmonary function, acute rejection, and CLAD [177, 178, 179, 180, 181, 182]. In addition to acid reflux, nonacid reflux has also been implicated in poor lung transplant outcomes. Impedance measures have been shown to be more predictive of allograft injury than pH measures alone, and nonacid reflux was found to contribute additional risk of CLAD in subjects with pathologic acid reflux [183, 184].

A recent national survey of U.S. transplant centers showed wide variability in their use of manometry and impedance pH monitoring in pre‐ and post‐transplant care [185]. Lo called for standardized, comprehensive assessments of esophageal function as well as acid and bolus reflux parameters to identify actionable reflux to improve lung transplant outcomes.

Innovation: Reflux Versus Oropharyngeal Dysphagia in Pediatric Aerodigestive Disease

Daniel Duncan from Boston Children's Hospital discussed the challenge of distinguishing GERD from oropharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration in infants and young children with aerodigestive symptoms. In pediatric practice, symptoms such as spitting up, crying, feeding refusal, and arching are commonly attributed to reflux, but many of these cases are due to other causes, such as aspiration [186, 187].

Unlike adults, infants feed frequently, meaning gastric contents are often nonacidic, and acid suppression therapies like PPIs are not effective. The approach to reflux in pediatric patients is, therefore, very different from that in adults. Pediatric guidelines recommend nonpharmacologic strategies first, including feed thickening and dietary changes, before considering acid suppression [188].

Common symptoms in young children often associated with reflux may not be due to reflux at all. pH‐impedance monitoring studies showed that symptoms such as gagging, arching, crying, and refusing to feed were not temporally associated with reflux events [189].

Several studies have also looked at symptom overlap between reflux and aspiration. Among young patients suspected of having reflux due to symptoms, a majority of them were found to have oropharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration. In this group, patients who had received PPIs for reflux were even more likely to have aspiration. Differentiating between these two conditions in infants can be difficult, as all infants have some amount of physiologic reflux, and aspiration in infants is often silent, with as many as 95–100% of cases occurring without overt symptoms like coughing [186, 190, 191]. However, getting the right diagnosis is important as it affects treatment and management [187, 192, 193, 194].

The condition BRUE—frightening episodes where infants turn blue or stop breathing—has often been misattributed to reflux. Multiple studies show no correlation between reflux and BRUE [195, 196, 197], while a large proportion of these infants have been found to have abnormal swallowing on further evaluation [198, 199, 200].

The fact that many symptoms are attributed to reflux in infants can be detrimental as it not only limits the ability to treat the actual underlying issue but also increases the likelihood of unnecessary acid suppression therapy, despite limited evidence of efficacy and known risks, such as increased hospitalization in aspirating infants [201, 202]. In contrast, thickening feeds has emerged as a safe, well‐tolerated, and effective intervention for infants with swallowing dysfunction. It reduces symptoms, improves intake, and decreases hospital readmissions [200, 203, 204, 205].

Session 5 Discussion

During the panel discussion, speakers discussed the role of pre‐ and post‐transplant reflux testing in lung transplant patients. Some noted that pretransplant testing may not be as informative, given that patients are so different after transplant. Some have also changed their preop protocol because universal testing became impractical in a setting where many patients do not advance to transplant. Pre‐ and post‐transplant manometry have shown similar predictive value for rejection risk, though pretransplant manometry can be useful to rule out candidates with absent contractility. Notably, post‐transplant improvements in esophageal function have been observed in patients who previously had poor motility, suggesting that lung health may positively impact GI motility. There was also hope that emerging BAL biomarkers, such as pepsin or bile acids, may be able to diagnose reflux in patients with lung disease and identify those at risk for more serious outcomes.

Speakers also expanded on the varying guidance provided by the new guidelines on diagnosing LPR. The recent Dubai classification and San Diego classification have different criteria for LPR, particularly in terms of the number of events needed for diagnosis. These differences highlight the need for a clear definition of LPR and the need for more data. Speakers admitted that the diagnostic criteria in the two consensus articles are not empirically driven but based on expert rationale.

Other topics of conversation included how EOE fits into the diagnostic protocol, the importance of treating comorbid lower GI diseases and how that may alleviate reflux symptoms, and the bidirectional relationship of cough and reflux.

Session 6: Modern Diagnosis of Esophageal and Laryngeal Reflux Syndromes (Chairs: Rachel Rosen and Ronnie Fass)

The last session of the meeting reviewed the modern diagnosis of GERD and LPRD, including the recent Lyon Consensus 2.0, behavioral syndromes in evaluating GERD, such as supragastric, belching, and rumination, and the role of mucosal impedance in diagnosing reflux.

Modern Diagnosis of GERD: Lyon Consensus 2.0

Prakash Gyawali from Washington University in St. Louis presented updates on the modern diagnosis of GERD, focusing on the Lyon Consensus 2.0. One key theme throughout the meeting was the spectrum of patients with suspected GERD—from clearly defined pathological reflux (e.g., erosive esophagitis and Barrett's esophagus) to esophageal hypersensitivity or functional gastrointestinal disorders. Diagnosing GERD is relatively straightforward at these extremes. However, there is less diagnostic certainty in the middle, where symptoms are present but objective evidence is limited.

To address this, the Lyon Consensus 2.0 provides a framework for interpreting esophageal testing results. For patients with typical reflux symptoms, an empiric trial of therapy is reasonable. However, in patients with atypical symptoms or lack of treatment response, physiologic testing via reflux monitoring (wireless pH monitoring or pH impedance monitoring off therapy), endoscopy, and/or manometry can help to confirm or rule out GERD [206]. The Lyon Consensus recommends endoscopy as the first diagnostic step for these patients. Conclusive evidence of GERD includes LA grade B‐D esophagitis, biopsy‐confirmed Barrett's esophagus, or peptic strictures. In the absence of these findings, further evaluation via reflex monitoring is warranted. Other tests, such as manometry and barium swallows, are considered adjunctive [206].

If testing remains inconclusive, scoring systems can provide more confidence in diagnosing GERD. Systems such as the Milan score, Lyon score, reflux metrics, and COuGH RefluX combine metrics that have little diagnostic value alone but, when combined, can provide more confidence in a diagnosis of GERD. For patients with a low probability of GERD, management approaches should focus on potential overlap with disorders of gut−brain interaction and/or hypervigilance [206].

Gyawali stressed that typical symptoms alone are not enough for actionable GERD, though they may be sufficient for empirical therapy. Upfront testing is more important in those with atypical symptoms and for escalation of management.

Modern Diagnosis of LPRD

Walter Chan of Harvard Medical School reviewed the modern diagnosis of LPRD, with a focus on the recent San Diego Consensus [147]. LPS may be related to a wide variety of underlying causes, both reflux and nonreflux‐related. However, many patients with LPS are given a diagnosis of LPRD even though they lack objective evidence of reflux [207, 208]. One of the key challenges in diagnosing LPRD, therefore, is distinguishing patients with LPS from nonreflux causes from those with LPRD who have objective evidence of reflux. This is complicated by the fact that many studies of patients with “LPR” include those who have not been tested for reflux, making data interpretation difficult. Furthermore, LPS are nonspecific and poorly predictive of reflux, and specialized PROs show limited diagnostic utility—though they can be useful to monitor symptom changes pre‐ and post‐treatment [150, 209].

Endoscopy can provide objective evidence of GERD, raising the suspicion of LPRD. In a large multicenter study of patients with LPS, 47% had endoscopic findings suggestive of GERD [210]. However, a negative endoscopy does not rule out reflux, as many patients have nonerosive reflux disease and require further testing. In‐office transnasal endoscopy may be helpful to detect esophagitis and Barrett's esophagus, but an experienced provider is needed.

Laryngoscopy is also an important tool for evaluating patients with LPS to rule out alternative diagnoses such as malignancies; however, it is not useful as a diagnostic tool for LPRD. In fact, studies show that many healthy individuals exhibit laryngeal signs often attributed to reflux, making laryngoscopy findings unreliable for diagnosis.

The management approaches used in typical GERD may not be useful in LPRD. Empiric PPI therapy—a mainstay in diagnosing and treating typical GERD—shows limited benefit in patients with LPS [154, 211]. However, this approach may be useful in patients with LPS and objective evidence of reflux, particularly esophageal symptoms [212]. For patients with isolated LPS, there is little/no benefit of PPIs over placebo [212, 213, 214]. In these patients, upfront testing to confirm reflux may be more cost‐effective than empiric treatment.

Reflux testing modalities include pH‐impedance monitoring (with Lyon Consensus 2.0 thresholds), combined hypopharyngeal‐esophageal pH‐impedance monitoring, wireless pH monitoring, which better captures day‐to‐day variability, and manometry. The COuGH RefluX Score, a novel risk stratification tool, emerges as a prediction score that can determine who should undergo upfront testing versus empiric treatment [215].

In conclusion, the San Diego consensus on diagnosing LPRD recommends that in patients with LPS, laryngoscopy is useful to rule out other nonreflux etiologies but not diagnose LPRD. Those with isolated LPS should undergo additional testing to confirm or rule out reflux before deciding on treatment. Empiric therapy can be effective in patients with LPS and esophageal reflux while considering additional testing [147].

Behavioral Syndromes in the Evaluation of GERD

Daniel Sifrim from Queen Mary University of London reviewed behavioral syndromes associated with GERD, focusing on belching disorders and rumination, which are increasingly recognized as overlapping contributors to reflux symptoms.

Gastric belching is a physiological process triggered by air swallowing, particularly during meals, which can lead to gastric distension and TLESRs and entry of air and/or liquid reflux (acid or nonacid) into the esophagus. Managing gastric belching includes reducing air intake during meals by avoiding carbonated beverages, behavioral modifications, and speech therapy. A practical tip shared by Sifrim involves minimizing air swallowing by having patients eat with their head upright and bringing the dish to their mouth.

In contrast, supragastric belching is a behavioral phenomenon that involves drawing air into the esophagus—not the stomach—through rapid diaphragmatic movement and opening of the UES. The air is expelled from the esophagus, producing a belch sound, but it never reaches the stomach. Although behavioral in origin, supragastric belching can provoke reflux, sometimes with liquid components. Treating supragastric belching involves cognitive behavioral therapy that focuses on diaphragmatic breathing and tongue positioning behind the upper teeth [216]. Sifrim noted that these interventions are simple to teach without the need for referral to psychologists. Many patients have both gastric and supragastric belching, necessitating tailored management approaches.

Sifrim also described rumination syndrome, a behavioral disorder involving effortless postprandial regurgitation without preceding nausea or retching. Primary rumination occurs when rumination is triggered by initial feelings of discomfort in the epigastric area, and the person tries to eliminate it. Secondary rumination occurs in response to reflux. Rumination can also be combined with supragastric belching. Treatment for rumination includes diaphragmatic breathing, though success rates are slightly lower than for supragastric belching [217].

Ultimately, behavioral syndromes such as supragastric belching and rumination are underrecognized contributors to refractory GERD symptoms. A better understanding and incorporation of behavioral therapies can help improve patient outcomes.

Mucosal Impedance Measurements in Diagnosing and Phenotyping Reflux

Edoardo Savarino from the University of Padua provided an overview of the role of mucosal impedance measurements in evaluating and phenotyping GERD. The correlation between mucosal impedance and impairment of the esophageal barrier was first described by Daniel Sifrim and Ricard Farre [218]. The study demonstrated that impaired esophageal barrier function correlates with reduced baseline impedance and mucosal damage. Subsequent studies confirmed that low impedance is associated with histological abnormalities and inflammation, providing a noninvasive marker for esophageal disease [219, 220, 221, 222].

In clinical practice, a widely used metric to measure baseline impedance is MNBI, which can be measured during 24‐h impedance‐pH monitoring and can identify esophageal mucosal damage even when acid exposure time is inconclusive [219, 223, 224]. MNBI has shown value in predicting symptom response in GERD, both on and off PPI therapy, and in patients with atypical symptoms or comorbid conditions such as IPF [225, 226, 227]. Moreover, it has demonstrated predictive utility for both pharmacologic interventions and antireflux surgery [227, 228, 229], supporting its inclusion in the Lyon 2.0 GERD classification.

However, catheter‐based impedance measurements are not without limitations [230]. The discomfort of transnasal catheters, variable mucosal contact, and potential artifacts from food, secretions, or non‐GERD inflammation can impact accuracy. In addition, MNBI readings require manual processing, which can be time‐consuming and subjective.

Direct mucosal impedance measurement via endoscopy is being investigated as an alternative to traditional catheter‐based measures. This technology allows real‐time, site‐specific assessment of esophageal mucosal health. A pilot study demonstrated lower mucosal impedance values in patients with erosive esophagitis or abnormal pH profiles compared to healthy controls or those with nonerosive disease [231]. Endoscopic‐based impedance also improves following PPI therapy, supporting the role of mucosal impedance as a marker of reversible mucosal inflammation [232].

Despite its potential, mucosal impedance measurement through endoscopy is not without drawbacks. There are limited data on the optimal protocol to adopt, and it does not provide information on standard reflux metrics, such as acid exposure time or reflux event counts. In addition, geographic and ethnic variations in normal impedance values further complicate interpretation.

Role of Endoscopy in Reflux Diagnosis

Nicola de Bortoli from the University of Pisa discussed the role of endoscopy in diagnosing GERD. Several international guidelines recommend upper endoscopy to evaluate the esophageal mucosa in patients with GERD‐related symptoms, particularly those with atypical symptoms, patients with partial response to PPI therapy, and those with risk factors for GERD, such as male sex, white race, age over 50, obesity, smoking history, or a family history of esophageal cancer [48, 64, 114].

In PPI responders with unconfirmed GERD, endoscopy within 12 months is recommended to evaluate for erosive esophagitis, Barrett's esophagus, or hiatal hernia. The diagnostic power of endoscopy can be limited in these cases if patients remain on acid suppression. Therefore, a washout period of 3–4 weeks is recommended to enhance diagnostic yield.

Endoscopic findings of Los Angeles grade B esophagitis or higher are considered conclusive evidence of GERD. Studies have shown significant overlap between grade B and grades C/D esophagitis in terms of acid exposure time, number of reflux events, and MNBI [233, 234]. Patients with grade A esophagitis or a normal Z‐line are considered unproven GERD and require further testing.

Endoscopy is also used to evaluate EGJ integrity. The new AFS classification offers a standardized grading system for hiatal hernias based on length, diameter, and flap valve appearance, enhancing diagnostic consistency [31].

Microscopic esophagitis has also emerged as a tool to distinguish NERD from functional heartburn, though current guidelines recommend esophageal biopsies only in cases of dysphagia or suspected EoE, not in patients with normal endoscopic appearance.

Upper endoscopy remains a cornerstone of GERD assessment and should be considered in patients with atypical symptoms, alarm features, or high‐risk profiles. It should be performed off acid suppression for optimal diagnostic value, and grade B esophagitis should be recognized as conclusive for GERD. New tools such as the AFS classification and evolving microscopic techniques continue to enhance the diagnostic utility of endoscopy.

Innovation: Artificial Intelligence for the Diagnosis of Supragastric Belching

Benjamin Rogers from Washington University in St. Louis presented research on developing a new AI‐based tool for diagnosing supragastric belching. Rogers recently published a review on the applications of AI in GI. Most current techniques use convolutional neural networks that rely on existing software, for example, HRM and FLIP [235].

Supragastric belching is well‐suited for new AI‐based detection tools. These events are frequently missed by commercial software, and proper diagnosis can affect patient management. In addition, interobserver variability when assessing ambulatory impedance measurements can lead to misdiagnoses, highlighting the need for an objective diagnostic measurement.

To address this, Rogers's group began with a complex decision tree model, which yielded high sensitivity at detecting supragastric belching. To improve diagnostic accuracy, they incorporated machine learning. They manually annotated impedance‐pH data from 20 patients across three groups—healthy controls, GERD patients, and those with supragastric belching. The data were processed through a custom‐designed neural network using three layers of convolution and multiple dense layers to refine learning. The neural network was trained using supervised learning, where machine‐detected episodes are manually adjudicated and fed back into the model to improve future predictions. This iterative learning process enabled the AI to distinguish patients with excessive supragastric belching from those with reflux and healthy controls. In a validation cohort, the model achieved sensitivity in the high 70% range. Results from a testing cohort show some variability and false positives, though with a good area under the curve, highlighting its ability to separate supragastric belching from other conditions.

Session 6 Discussion

Panelists highlighted the ongoing challenges in diagnosing LPRD, particularly regarding interobserver variability in interpreting proximal reflux episodes. Hypopharyngeal events in particular are often accompanied by artifacts from coughing or hypersensitivity and can be difficult to identify on impedance testing. Baseline impedance may ultimately be a more robust indicator of mucosal integrity than the number of events. One panelist advocated for simpler, short‐duration catheter‐based baseline impedance testing as a cost‐effective and clinically useful alternative to 24‐h pH monitoring.

Panelists also discussed the lack of breath testing and histology recommendations in the guidelines. Some brought up that this information could provide valuable insight into the mechanisms of disease. However, given that the data are not yet robust enough to guide treatment decisions, they are not included in Lyon 2.0 or San Diego consensus guidelines, given that the recommendations focus on actionable reflux and specific recommendations on de/escalating therapy. They agreed that breath testing may help to explain symptoms, but cannot currently be used to guide treatment and thus does not align with the current purpose of these guidelines. In addition, while there are robust data on the utility of esophageal biopsies to distinguish between GERD phenotypes, there is a lack of standardized biopsy protocols and variability in pathologist interpretation. Despite promising findings—such as resolution of microscopic abnormalities with PPI therapy—panelists questioned whether histology is necessary for everyday diagnosis, especially when clinical responses to empiric therapy are strong. The Lyon 2.0 guidelines acknowledge histopathology as a supportive tool but not a primary diagnostic criterion.

Panelists also discussed the definition of rumination and how it differs from regurgitation. While these two conditions may appear similar, rumination has a distinct physiological pattern observable via impedance manometry and is often characterized by postprandial symptoms without nighttime occurrences. Manometric features—such as a pre‐emptive effort to raise intragastric pressure—help distinguish rumination from passive reflux. In addition, secondary rumination may present as a learned behavior in patients with comorbid conditions, such as bulimia or functional dyspepsia. In such cases, rumination may develop as a maladaptive strategy to relieve postprandial distress. Recognizing this association led panelists to recommend routine screening for eating disorders or psychological factors in ruminators, with a potential referral to behavioral health specialists.
