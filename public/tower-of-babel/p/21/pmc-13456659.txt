Hydroxyurea Bridging Before Frontline Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitor Therapy in Newly Diagnosed Chronic-Phase Chronic Myeloid Leukemia: A Critical Appraisal
Authors: Mohammed Abdulgayoom, Awni Alshurafa, Abdulrahman F. Al-Mashdali, Shehab F. Mohamed, Mohamed A. Yassin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHydroxyurea-based cytoreduction before tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) initiation remains common in newly diagnosed chronic-phase chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), yet its contemporary value is uncertain.SummaryThis critical narrative review examines the historical rationale, biologic basis, clinical evidence, and guideline positions relevant to hydroxyurea use before or immediately around frontline TKI therapy. Hydroxyurea can lower leukocyte and platelet counts rapidly and may provide short-term symptomatic relief while diagnostic confirmation is pending or when definitive treatment access is briefly delayed. However, contemporary evidence does not support routine pretreatment in clinically stable chronic-phase CML. Observational studies have shown no improvement in European LeukemiaNet response milestones, no acceleration of molecular response kinetics, and possible disadvantages including delayed TKI initiation, greater hematologic toxicity, and more treatment interruptions. Randomized studies evaluating early hydroxyurea together with imatinib likewise have not demonstrated meaningful molecular or hematologic benefit over TKI therapy alone. Current guidelines consistently position hydroxyurea as a selective, time-limited bridging option rather than a standard pretreatment strategy.Key MessagesIn modern practice, the priority should be rapid initiation of definitive BCR::ABL1-directed therapy once the diagnosis is confirmed and access is secured. Hydroxyurea retains a limited role in selected situations such as symptomatic proliferative burden, suspected leukostasis, clinically important thrombocytosis, or short unavoidable logistical delay to TKI initiation.

Introduction and Scope of the Review

The therapeutic landscape of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) has been transformed by tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), which directly inhibit the BCR::ABL1 oncoprotein and have converted chronic-phase CML from a fatal leukemia into a chronic, highly manageable disease for most patients [1–4]. In parallel with these advances, several pre-TKI practices have been re-evaluated, including the use of hydroxyurea as initial cytoreductive therapy before definitive treatment is started.

Hydroxyurea is still frequently prescribed at diagnosis because it is familiar, inexpensive, rapidly available, and capable of lowering leukocyte and platelet counts over a few days. Clinicians may also reach for it when patients present with marked leukocytosis, thrombocytosis, splenomegaly, or constitutional symptoms, or when molecular confirmation and drug access are temporarily delayed [5–7]. The central question in current practice, however, is not whether hydroxyurea can reduce counts, but whether this transient cytoreduction improves clinically meaningful outcomes once effective targeted therapy is readily available.

This review focuses specifically on hydroxyurea use before or immediately around frontline TKI initiation in newly diagnosed chronic-phase CML. Rather than revisiting CML management broadly, it examines whether this persistent historical practice retains a defensible role in contemporary care by integrating the biologic rationale, available clinical evidence, and current guideline language [5–11].

Historical Background

Before the molecular era, CML management relied largely on palliative cytoreduction. Busulfan and hydroxyurea were widely used to control leukocytosis, thrombocytosis, and splenomegaly, but neither produced durable disease control. Busulfan is no longer used as routine cytoreductive therapy in contemporary CML management. Hydroxyurea, however, gained favor historically because of its oral administration, relatively predictable myelosuppressive effect, and more manageable toxicity profile compared with other cytoreductive agents [5, 12].

Interferon-alpha modestly improved outcomes in selected patients, but hydroxyurea remained important for early count control and symptomatic relief. With the introduction of imatinib and later second-generation TKIs, the treatment paradigm changed fundamentally. TKI therapy directly addressed the driver lesion of CML and became the standard first-line treatment for chronic-phase disease. As a result, hydroxyurea moved from a disease-directed therapy to a supportive bridging measure. The persistence of routine prescribing in some settings therefore reflects historical inertia as much as current evidence [1, 3, 5, 6, 12].

Pharmacologic Rationale and Why Its Routine Use Deserves Reappraisal

Hydroxyurea inhibits ribonucleotide reductase and suppresses DNA synthesis, thereby reducing proliferation of rapidly dividing myeloid progenitors [5]. In clinical practice, it can lower leukocyte and platelet counts over several days and may reduce discomfort related to splenic enlargement or proliferative disease burden. These properties explain why it remains attractive as an immediately available oral cytoreductive agent.

In newly diagnosed CML, the rationale for hydroxyurea usually falls into 4 categories: short-term control of marked leukocytosis or thrombocytosis while confirmatory testing is pending, temporary relief of symptomatic splenomegaly or constitutional symptoms, pragmatic use when immediate TKI initiation is not feasible, and concern about early count-related complications [5–7]. Yet these arguments are weaker in chronic-phase CML than they are often assumed to be. The circulating cells are predominantly more mature myeloid forms rather than rigid blasts, symptomatic leukostasis is uncommon, and tumor lysis syndrome is rare in otherwise stable chronic-phase disease [5–7, 13–15].

Clinical context therefore matters more than the absolute count alone. Marked thrombocytosis may be relevant when accompanied by vasomotor symptoms, thrombosis, bleeding, acquired von Willebrand syndrome, or other microvascular manifestations, but not every elevated platelet count requires urgent cytoreduction [5–7, 13]. Likewise, supportive measures such as hydration, urate-lowering therapy when appropriate, and close laboratory monitoring may be sufficient in many patients with a high proliferative burden [5, 6, 14, 15].

Another reason to reappraise routine pretreatment is that TKIs themselves usually induce rapid hematologic responses in chronic-phase CML. Hydroxyurea does not target BCR::ABL1, does not improve long-term disease biology, and may overlap with TKI-related myelosuppression. Prolonged or reflexive use may also distort baseline disease measurements, postpone definitive therapy, and complicate interpretation of early molecular kinetics in a disease where 3-month molecular milestones carry prognostic significance [2, 4, 8, 9].

Evidence from Observational Studies

The observational literature is limited but directionally consistent. It includes a large population-based TKI-era registry analysis, a smaller contemporary cohort focused on very early count and transcript kinetics, and older non-randomized data examining prolonged hydroxyurea exposure before imatinib [8, 9, 16]. Collectively, these studies do not demonstrate a clinically meaningful advantage for routine hydroxyurea pretreatment. The key observational studies and their limitations are summarized in Table 1.

Key observational studies evaluating hydroxyurea exposure before frontline TKI therapy in chronic-phase CML

In a Dutch population-based study of 402 patients with newly diagnosed chronic-phase CML treated with first-line TKIs, 175 (44%) patients received hydroxyurea pretreatment [8]. Those patients had higher leukocyte counts, less favorable ELTS/Sokal risk, and more constitutional symptoms or symptomatic splenomegaly at baseline, indicating clinician selection of patients with greater proliferative burden. Despite this, pretreatment was associated with a longer interval from diagnosis to TKI initiation (21 vs. 15 days) and more hematotoxicity after TKI start (25% vs. 11%), with more hematotoxicity-related dose reductions or interruptions [8]. No benefit was observed for complete hematologic response, partial cytogenetic response, BCR::ABL1 less than 10% at 3 months, complete cytogenetic response by 6 months, or major molecular response by 12 months [8].

A smaller 2025 cohort of 45 unselected patients reached a similar conclusion [9]. Twenty-one patients received hydroxyurea cytoreduction for 14 days before TKI therapy and 24 started a TKI directly. Serial assessments at baseline, day 7, and day 14 did not show a meaningful advantage for hydroxyurea with respect to early leukocyte cytoreduction, and no improvement was demonstrated in early hematologic or molecular response parameters [9]. The study also raised a methodological concern that pretreatment before formal baseline sampling may alter BCR::ABL1 transcript quantification and thereby complicate interpretation of early response kinetics [9].

Older observational data have raised a related concern regarding prolonged exposure before imatinib. In one retrospective cohort, hydroxyurea administration for more than 6 months before imatinib was associated with unsuccessful major molecular response achievement (RR: 1.60, 95% CI: 1.29–2.00) [16]. Although this scenario is not directly comparable to modern brief bridging, it reinforces the broader principle that non-targeted cytoreduction should not substitute for timely initiation of definitive BCR::ABL1-directed therapy [16].

Evidence from Randomized and Prospective Studies

Direct randomized comparisons of hydroxyurea pretreatment vs. immediate TKI initiation are scarce. Nevertheless, trials evaluating early hydroxyurea together with imatinib are informative because they test the broader hypothesis that hydroxyurea adds clinically relevant value when effective targeted therapy is already being delivered [10, 11].

In a randomized study from German cooperative groups, imatinib plus hydroxyurea did not improve major molecular response rates at 18 months compared with imatinib alone, and early molecular outcomes as well as cumulative adverse events favored imatinib monotherapy [11]. A more recent randomized trial comparing imatinib alone with imatinib plus structured hydroxyurea dosing likewise found no significant improvement in early molecular response or hematologic response with the combination strategy [10]. Taken together, the randomized data are limited but consistent with the observational literature: hydroxyurea does not improve response depth or speed in a way that justifies routine use before or alongside frontline TKI therapy [10, 11].

Selected Clinical Scenarios Where Short-Term Bridging May Still Be Reasonable

Although routine pretreatment is not well supported, hydroxyurea retains a limited role in selected patients when the aim is short-term symptom control or risk mitigation rather than improved leukemia-specific outcomes [5–7, 13]. The clearest indication is symptomatic hyperleukocytosis or suspected leukostasis. True leukostasis in chronic-phase CML is uncommon, but it can occur, particularly in very high-count states with neurologic, respiratory, or visual symptoms. In such cases, hydroxyurea is a reasonable temporizing intervention while definitive therapy is organized, and leukapheresis may be considered in extreme organ-threatening presentations [5–7, 13].

Short-term bridging may also be reasonable in patients with severe painful splenomegaly, marked early satiety from massive splenic enlargement, or clinically important thrombocytosis associated with thrombotic, hemorrhagic, or microvascular manifestations. In these situations, the justification is symptom control and stabilization, not expectation of better molecular response once the TKI starts [6, 13].

A final practical indication is a short unavoidable delay in TKI initiation, such as pending molecular confirmation, insurance approval, drug procurement, or other logistic barriers. Even in that setting, hydroxyurea should be framed explicitly as temporary bridging and should not become a default substitute for expedited access to definitive therapy [5–7, 13, 17].

Risks and Downsides of Routine Hydroxyurea Pretreatment

The most important drawback of routine hydroxyurea use is delay to definitive therapy. In modern CML management, prompt introduction of disease-directed treatment matters because early molecular response at 3 months has clear prognostic value. Any unnecessary postponement of TKI exposure therefore has potential clinical relevance [2, 4, 8].

Hydroxyurea may also contribute additional hematologic toxicity. Because it suppresses normal as well as leukemic hematopoiesis, pretreatment followed by TKI therapy can increase the likelihood of cytopenias, dose interruptions, and treatment modifications, particularly in patients with limited marrow reserve [8, 16].

A further concern is distortion of baseline assessment. Risk scores and baseline disease characterization are ideally derived from untreated values. Extended hydroxyurea exposure before formal treatment initiation may blur the initial disease profile and complicate interpretation of very early BCR::ABL1 kinetics [2, 4, 9].

Finally, the continued routine use of hydroxyurea may reinforce a habitual, nonevidence-based practice pattern. Because the drug is familiar and readily available, it may be started reflexively in clinically stable patients who could instead proceed directly to TKI therapy once the diagnosis is confirmed [5, 6, 8].

Guideline Recommendations and Practical Implications

Current major guidelines are broadly aligned. The European LeukemiaNet recommends TKI therapy as first-line treatment for newly diagnosed chronic-phase CML and allows a short course of hydroxyurea in symptomatic patients with high leukocyte or platelet counts while confirmatory testing is pending [6]. The ESMO guideline similarly emphasizes prompt initiation of TKI therapy after confirmation of BCR::ABL1-positive disease and recognizes hydroxyurea only as a temporary cytoreductive measure at presentation [13]. The British Society for Haematology centers management on timely TKI-based treatment and structured molecular monitoring rather than routine hydroxyurea pretreatment [7]. Older expert-practice statements and NCCN guidance are directionally similar, positioning hydroxyurea as a selective temporizing option rather than a mandatory preliminary step [5, 17, 18].

The practical implication across guidelines is consistent: hydroxyurea may be considered when symptoms, clinically meaningful proliferative burden, or unavoidable delays justify short-term bridging, but it should not delay definitive TKI initiation once diagnosis and access are secured. In other words, modern guidelines support restrictive, context-driven use rather than routine pretreatment [5–7, 13, 17, 18]. Contemporary Practical Approach

In the contemporary TKI era, the practical question is not whether hydroxyurea can transiently reduce leukocyte or platelet counts, but whether routine cytoreduction is necessary before starting definitive BCR::ABL1-directed therapy. In current practice, a clinically stable patient with suspected or confirmed chronic-phase CML should proceed to rapid diagnostic confirmation and prompt TKI initiation without routine hydroxyurea bridging. A pragmatic treatment pathway for selecting patients for short-term hydroxyurea bridging is shown in Figure 1 [6, 7, 13]. Hydroxyurea may be used briefly when there is symptomatic hyperleukocytosis, severe proliferative symptoms, clinically significant thrombocytosis, or a short unavoidable delay to definitive therapy [5–7, 13]. When it is prescribed, the plan should include explicit stop rules, supportive care when appropriate, and reassessment aimed at discontinuation as soon as TKI therapy is available [5, 6, 8, 14, 15].

Areas of Controversy and Unmet Needs

Several uncertainties remain. No large randomized trial has directly compared immediate TKI initiation with a fixed short course of hydroxyurea pretreatment under otherwise identical conditions. Such a study would help distinguish the true effect of cytoreduction itself from the consequences of treatment delay.

The optimal criteria for identifying the rare chronic-phase CML patient who truly benefits from early cytoreduction are also not well defined. Current decisions are based mainly on clinical judgment rather than validated thresholds, and most available evidence derives largely from imatinib-era cohorts. Whether all conclusions apply identically across broader first-line use of second-generation TKIs is likely, but not definitively proven [3, 8, 9, 11].

Similarly, the extent to which very early cytoreduction influences baseline and immediate post-diagnosis BCR::ABL1 transcript kinetics remains incompletely understood. Current evidence does not suggest clinically meaningful benefit, but the methodological implications for early response interpretation deserve further study [9].

Conclusion

Hydroxyurea remains an effective cytoreductive agent, but its role in newly diagnosed chronic-phase CML has narrowed substantially in the TKI era. Routine pretreatment before frontline TKI initiation is not supported by current evidence and does not appear to improve hematologic control, molecular response, or survival. Available data instead suggest that unnecessary pretreatment may delay definitive therapy and increase hematologic toxicity. Contemporary best practice is to initiate TKI therapy promptly once the diagnosis is confirmed and to reserve hydroxyurea for short-term, symptom-driven bridging in selected patients.
