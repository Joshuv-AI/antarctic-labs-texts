Drug and country characteristics associated with global approval and launch of FDA-approved novel therapeutics
Authors: Mengyuan Fu, Joshua D Wallach, Anthony D So, Harry Doernberg, Xiangyu Li, Xiaodong Guan, Reshma Ramachandran, Joseph S Ross
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGlobal availability of novel therapeutics varies widely across countries and is strongly associated with national income. However, whether drug characteristics are associated with global approval and launch remains unclear.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of therapeutics approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) between 2008 and 2019, examining global regulatory approval and market launch through February 2026 using data from Citeline Pharmaprojects.ResultsAmong 396 FDA-approved novel therapeutics, approval or launch of at least one therapeutic was identified in 119 countries outside the United States. Therapeutics were approved and launched in a median of 36 and 17 countries, respectively. In multivariable drug-level analyses, biologics and therapeutics developed by large sponsors were approved or launched in more countries, infectious disease therapeutics in fewer countries. Drug innovation status, orphan designation, unmet medical need, and safety risk were not associated with global approval or launch. At the country level, high-income level, regulatory maturity, larger populations, and greater healthcare resources were associated with more approvals and launches.ConclusionGlobal approval and launch of FDA-approved novel therapeutics were associated more strongly with country-level characteristics than drug-level characteristics. Improving global access may require addressing both regulatory and commercial barriers to approval and launch.Keywords: novel therapeutics, regulatory approval, market launch, global disparities

Introduction

Over the past 2 decades, advances in drug development and evaluation have led to the market launch of over a thousand novel active substances globally, substantially transforming clinical care across a wide range of diseases by expanding treatment options for many patients.1 However, regulatory approval and market launch of these newly developed drugs remain highly uneven across countries. Globally, approximately two-thirds of novel drugs first receive regulatory approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA).2 More than 80% are ultimately launched (ie, available for sale) in the United States, compared with approximately 65% in leading European countries (ie, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, United Kingdom) and 60% in Japan.1 In contrast, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have seen fewer regulatory approvals and market launches of novel drugs over the past decades, limiting patient access to these therapeutic advances.1,3

Evidence suggests substantial international disparities in timeliness of regulatory approval, market entry, and drug affordability. Studies consistently show that higher-income countries have more launches and shorter launch delays for novel therapies.4-6 Even medicines deemed essential by the World Health Organization (WHO) reached high-income countries (HICs) approximately 5 years earlier than low-income countries, with delays remaining largely unchanged over time.5 Countries participating in clinical trials supporting US FDA approval also do not achieve timely regulatory approval and market launch of the drugs they help evaluate, particularly in LMICs.7,8 Analyses of pricing and affordability further suggest that although nominal drug prices are generally higher in wealthier countries, drugs are often less affordable in poorer countries after accounting for purchasing power.9 As a result, even essential medicines can pose substantial cost barriers, often requiring multiple days’ wages for a single course of treatment,10 particularly for high-cost therapies such as cancer drugs, for which patient access often depends on robust universal health coverage systems.11,12

Although prior studies provide important aggregate evidence of global inequities in access to novel therapeutics, they have largely focused on the impact of country-level economic and health system characteristics and have offered limited insight into how attributes of individual drugs are associated with their subsequent global diffusion. For instance, whether innovative drugs, those addressing unmet medical needs, or those with safety concerns at the time of FDA approval are more or less likely to achieve broader global approval and launch remains unclear. Therefore, we examined the associations of drug and country characteristics with global regulatory approval and market launch for novel therapeutics approved by the FDA between 2008 and 2019.

Methods

Sample construction

We used the Drugs@FDA database, year-end records, published lists of approved therapeutics, and summary reports to identify all novel therapeutic products, including small-molecule entities and therapeutic biologics, approved by FDA between March 25, 2008 and December 31, 2019. We excluded diagnostic agents, vaccines, and cell- and gene-based therapies.

Drug-level characteristics

Consistent with our previous study,13 we used Drugs@FDA database, Federal Registry Notices, FDA press releases, and MedWatch to collect data on therapeutic type (small molecule or biologic), therapeutic area (autoimmune, musculoskeletal, and dermatology; cancer and hematology; cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia; infectious disease; psychiatry and neurology; and other), FDA first-in-class status, FDA orphan designation, receipt of any FDA expedited pathway designation (priority review, fast track, accelerated approval, or breakthrough therapy designation), and presence of an FDA boxed warning or safety-related postmarketing study requirements (PMR) at the time of FDA initial approval. We also identified the year and authority of first global approval, originator and licensee companies for each therapeutic using the Citeline Pharmaprojects database.14 Originator companies were classified as large if ranked among the world's top 50 pharmaceutical companies by global sales in 2024.15 Detailed variable definitions and classification methods are described in Appendix eMethods.

We constructed composite measures reflecting innovation, unmet medical need, and safety. Innovation status was classified into 3 mutually exclusive categories, including first-in-class (first-in-class designation at FDA initial approval), advanced-in-class (not first-in-class but received priority review at FDA initial approval), and addition-to-class.16 Unmet medical need was defined as receipt of ≥1 expedited pathway designation at FDA initial approval or no unmet medical need. Safety risk was classified as high if a boxed warning or PMR was present at FDA initial approval, and low if neither was present.

Country-level characteristics

For each country with ≥1 recorded approval or launch of study pharmaceuticals, we collected income level,17 population size,18 and physician density (physicians per 1000 people)19 from the World Bank, and geographic region20 and regulatory maturity using WHO classifications. Countries designated as WHO-Listed Authorities or operating at maturity levels 3-4 for medicines were classified as having a mature regulatory system.21,22 As our analyses estimated cumulative approvals and launches rather than modeling temporal dynamics, country-level characteristics were treated as time-invariant using the most recent data available as of February 15, 2026.

Global regulatory approval and market launch

For each novel therapeutic, we identified global regulatory approvals and market launches using Citeline Pharmaprojects,14 a commercial database tracking global pharmaceutical development in over 150 countries/territories that uses information from company press releases, academic presentations, and other public sources. Approval was defined, per Citeline, as whether a therapeutic was ever registered in a given country, irrespective of independent scientific review. Launch was defined as whether a therapeutic had ever been made available for sale in a given country, irrespective of national coverage determinations. For each therapeutic-country pair, we recorded whether it was ever approved or launched as of February 13, 2026, regardless of any subsequent withdrawal or discontinuation. For therapeutics approved through the European Medicines Agency (EMA) centralized marketing authorization procedure, approval was considered applicable to all European Union member states plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway; however, launches were determined at country level.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive analyses to summarize therapeutic-level and country-level characteristics and to describe the distribution of global regulatory approvals and market launches overall and by country. Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group differences were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum or Kruskal–Wallis tests. Associations between drug-level characteristics and the number of approving or launching countries were examined using negative binomial models. Similarly, associations between country-level characteristics and the number of therapeutics approved or launched were assessed using negative binomial models at the country level. These models are described in Appendix eMethods. Results are reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0.

Limitations

First, we examined therapeutics approved by FDA and did not include all novel therapeutics launched globally. Given that more than 80% of global novel active substances have been launched in the United States over the past 2 decades,1 our sample captures majority of global therapeutic innovations. However, FDA-approved therapeutics may reflect prevalent conditions and health priorities in the United States and other HICs and may underrepresent therapeutics developed primarily for conditions in LMICs. Second, approval and launch data were derived from the Pharmaprojects database, which relies on publicly available sources. If LMICs report this information less frequently or inconsistently; approvals or launches may be underreported, potentially exaggerating observed disparities with HICs. Third, approval processes and definitions vary across countries, and approval or launch does not necessarily equate to broad patient access, coverage, or affordability. Fourth, although we constructed composite measures for innovation, unmet medical need, and safety, these may not capture the multidimensional nature of these attributes across regulatory contexts. Treating drug- and country-level characteristics as time-invariant may obscure temporal changes over time. For instance, some countries shifted into higher World Bank income categories during 2008-2019, potentially leading to undercounting LMICs in analyses of earlier approvals. Fifth, we did not jointly model drug- and country-level characteristics because such approaches may be affected by data sparsity and nonindependence. Finally, we were unable to directly observe manufacturers’ strategic decisions regarding whether and where to file for approval. Future research incorporating stakeholder incentives will be important to better understand global disparities.

Results

Sample characteristics

Between March 2008 and December 2019, FDA approved 396 novel therapeutics, of which 297 (75.0%) were small-molecule drugs, and 99 (25.0%) were biologics (Table 1). Cancer and hematology (123 [31.1%]) and infectious disease (62 [15.7%]) were the most common therapeutic areas. A total of 280 (70.7%) therapeutics were first approved globally by FDA, while 55 (13.9%) and 32 (8.1%) were first approved globally by EMA and Japan Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA), respectively. Most therapeutics were developed by large sponsors (247 [62.4%]) and had a licensee company involved in commercialization (308 [77.8%]). Overall, 148 (37.4%) were classified as first-in-class and 112 (28.3%) as advanced-in-class; 167 (42.2%) were designated as orphan drugs. At FDA initial approval, 245 (60.9%) had received at least one expedited pathway designation, including priority review (219 [55.3%]), fast track (151 [38.1%]), accelerated approval (51 [12.9%]), and breakthrough therapy designation (98 [24.8%]). At initial approval, 254 therapeutics (64.1%) were classified as having high safety risk, including 119 (30.0%) with FDA boxed warning and 200 (50.5%) with safety-related PMR.

Global approval and launch

Among 396 FDA-approved novel therapeutics, 372 (94.0%) were approved, and 361 (91.2%) were launched in at least one country outside the United States. Overall, approval or launch of at least one study therapeutic was identified in 119 countries outside the United States (Table 2). These countries spanned all WHO regions; 65 (56.0%) were LMICs, 74 (62.2%) had immature regulatory systems, 58 (48.7%) had population <10 million, and 25 (21.0%) had <1 physician per 1000 people. Regulatory approval and market launch occurred in a median of 36 (IQR: 32-41) and 17 (IQR: 4-26.5) countries outside the United States, respectively (Table 1). Approvals and launches were concentrated in HICs, with therapeutics being approved and launched in a median of 34.5 (IQR: 31-37) and 16 (IQR: 3-23.5) HICs, compared with a median of 2 (IQR: 0-5) and 1 (IQR: 0-3) in LMICs (Appendix Table S1). Across countries, the median numbers of approved and launched therapeutics were 17 (IQR: 3-313) and 6 (IQR: 1-87), respectively. HICs approved and launched more therapeutics than LMICs (Table 2).

The EMA authorized 319 (80.6%) of the 396 therapeutics through its centralized procedure, followed by Canada (n = 269 [67.9%]), Australia (n = 257 [64.9%]), and Japan (n = 245 [61.9%]) (Appendix Table S2). Drug-level characteristics associated with global approval and launch

In multivariable analyses of drug-level characteristics (Table 1), biologics were approved in more countries than small-molecule drugs (IRR = 1.13, 95% CI, 1.02-1.25; P = .022), although no significant difference was observed in launches (IRR = 1.04, 95% CI, 0.86-1.26; P = .691). Compared with autoimmune, musculoskeletal, and dermatologic therapeutics, therapeutics for psychiatry and neurology were approved in fewer countries (IRR = 0.80, 95% CI, 0.65-0.99; P = .039) but there was no difference in launches (IRR = 0.81, 95% CI, 0.57-1.14; P = .228), whereas infectious disease therapeutics were approved (IRR = 0.74, 95% CI, 0.59-0.92; P = .008) and launched (IRR = 0.52, 95% CI, 0.36-0.73; P = .001) in fewer countries. Among FDA-approved novel therapeutics, those first approved globally by EMA (IRR = 1.28, 95% CI, 1.04-1.58; P = .021) and PMDA (IRR = 1.36, 95% CI, 1.02-1.83; P = .038) were launched in more countries than those first approved globally by FDA. Therapeutics developed by sponsors ranked among the world's top 50 pharmaceutical companies were approved and launched in significantly more countries than those from smaller companies (approvals: IRR = 1.19, 95% CI, 1.05-1.35; P = .006; launches: IRR = 1.31, 95% CI, 1.07-1.59; P = .007). Other therapeutic characteristics, including involvement of licensee companies, innovation status, orphan designation, unmet medical need status, and safety risk, were not significantly associated with global approval or launch.

In analyses stratified by country income level (Appendix Table S3), the associations observed in the overall analysis were largely preserved among HICs. However, in LMICs, biologics, EMA first approval, and originator company size were no longer significantly associated with approval or launch. Instead, therapeutics first approved globally in 2014 or later and therapeutics with FDA orphan designation were launched in significantly fewer LMICs.

Country-level characteristics associated with global approval and launch

In multivariable analyses of country-level characteristics (Table 2), HICs approved (IRR = 3.40, 95% CI, 1.51-7.64; P = .003) and launched (IRR = 4.29, 95% CI, 1.75-10.54; P = .001) substantially more therapeutics than LMICs. Countries with mature regulatory systems were associated with higher numbers of approvals (IRR = 4.15, 95% CI, 1.84-9.39; P = .001) and launches (IRR = 3.96, 95% CI, 1.60-9.78; P = .003). Compared with countries with populations under 10 million, those with populations of 10-49 million, 50-99 million, and ≥100 million approved and launched progressively more therapeutics, with IRRs ranging from 1.55 to 6.03 for approvals and from 1.75 to 7.96 for launches (P < .01). Countries with greater healthcare workforce capacity approved and launched more therapeutics, particularly in countries with physician density ≥3.5 per 1000 people (approvals: IRR = 4.78, 95% CI, 2.44-9.37; P < .001; launches: IRR = 5.26, 95% CI, 2.23-12.41; P < .001). By geographic region, countries in the African region (approvals: IRR = 0.29, 95% CI, 0.10-0.83; P = .02; launches: IRR = 0.26, 95% CI, 0.07-0.98; P = .047) and Eastern Mediterranean region (approvals: IRR = 0.36, 95% CI, 0.18-0.69; P = .002; launches: IRR = 0.36, 95% CI, 0.17-0.77; P = .009) approved and launched significantly fewer therapeutics than countries in the region of the Americas.

Discussion

Among 396 FDA-approved novel therapeutics, global regulatory approval or market launch of at least one study therapeutic was identified in 119 countries outside the United States; however, approvals and launches remained highly concentrated in countries with higher incomes, more mature regulatory systems, larger populations, and greater health care resources. At the drug level, therapeutics developed by large sponsors were approved and launched in more countries, whereas infectious disease therapeutics were approved and launched in fewer countries. Drug-level attributes associated with FDA regulatory decision-making, including innovation status, orphan designation, unmet medical need, and safety profile, were not associated with global approval or launch. These findings suggest that structural country-level factors and commercial incentives may play a more prominent role in shaping global regulatory approval and market launch of novel therapeutics than drug-level characteristics regarding their clinical importance and safety.

Our findings indicate that global approval and launch of novel therapeutics remain highly selective, with countries possessing greater economic capacity and regulatory maturity consistently approving and launching more novel therapeutics. Notably, although approval and launch have continued to expand in HICs, a diverging pattern was observed in LMICs. While these country-level findings align with prior research,4,5,7,8 our drug-level analyses further suggest that global approval and launch are strongly conditioned by drug sponsors’ organizational capacity and expected commercial returns. Biologics and therapeutics developed by the world's largest pharmaceutical companies were more likely to achieve approval across HICs, where affordability is generally higher,9,12 but not in LMICs. Sponsors’ commercial considerations may also explain the observed lower likelihood of launch for infectious disease therapeutics, even in high-income settings. Limited expected uptake, due to restricted use or therapeutic competition, may diminish launch incentives despite regulatory approval,23 resulting in a larger gap between approval and launch compared with other therapeutic areas, such as cardiovascular, diabetes, and cancer therapeutics.

Several drug-level attributes that commonly facilitate regulatory approval in the United States, including innovation status, orphan designation, addressing unmet medical need, and safety profile, were not associated with broad global approval or launch. This may reflect differences in how these attributes are defined and operationalized across regulatory systems. Taking orphan therapeutics as an example,24 the FDA permits orphan designation for subsets of more common diseases, particularly biomarker-defined cancer subgroups that represent the largest share of orphan-designated indications.25 In contrast, the EMA requires orphan drugs to target clearly distinct,26 life-threatening or chronically debilitating conditions and demonstrate significant benefit over existing therapies.27 The PMDA further requires clinical trial or development feasibility data showing superiority over existing alternatives.28 Consistent with these definitional differences, only about 40% of FDA-designated orphan cancer indications also received EMA orphan designation,29 and more than half of FDA-approved orphan drugs remain unapproved in Japan.30 In addition, many countries lack orphan designation or dedicated reimbursement mechanisms, limiting incentives for market entry.31,32 Similarly, what constitutes an unmet medical need or innovative therapy varies across regulatory systems. Many LMICs continue to struggle to access therapies addressing clinical needs that have already been met in high-income settings years earlier.5,10,11 As a result, regulatory designations assigned by one authority may not confer equivalent priority elsewhere.

Beyond differences in regulatory definitions and eligibility criteria across regulatory systems, variation in how national regulatory authorities balance speed and clinical value may also help explain why these attributes were not significantly associated with approval and launch in high-income settings. The FDA has increasingly adopted expedited review pathways based on more preliminary clinical evidence to accelerate patient access to innovative therapies that address unmet medical needs.33 While these pathways have shortened clinical development periods by approximately 2 years on average,34 they simultaneously introduce considerable uncertainty regarding drug safety and efficacy. In countries with mature regulatory systems that conduct independent scientific review, regulators may differ in how they evaluate and balance clinical need, innovation, and uncertainty during regulatory review and subsequent reimbursement processes.35 Future studies should examine how the accumulation of efficacy and safety evidence over time relates to the timing of approval and launch across countries, which may provide further insights into how different regulatory authorities weigh these competing considerations.

In countries with more limited regulatory capacity and less commercially attractive markets, the situation may be different. Following initial approval, many national regulatory authorities may leverage the regulatory work carried out by reference authorities, typically FDA or EMA, to inform their decision-making, a WHO-recommended practice known as regulatory reliance,36 to avoid duplicating work and accelerate patient access to new treatments. While these regulators maintain independence in making final decisions, FDA approval often serves as a critical reference,37 and may substantially streamline subsequent regulatory approval. For instance, 13 national authorities in Latin America38 and 5 in the Middle East and North Africa39 either directly recognize the FDA or EMA approvals or implement abbreviated review procedures if a product has already been approved by these authorities. As these reliance pathways generally apply to all therapeutics regardless of their specific regulatory characteristics at reference authorities, individual drug attributes may have less influence on subsequent approval decisions. However, it is notable that despite the proactive adoption of strong reliance mechanisms based on FDA approvals, we found that only a limited number of FDA-approved novel therapeutics reached these countries. Even in Mexico and Argentina, which had the highest approval rates among these countries, fewer than one-quarter of the 396 FDA-approved novel therapeutics achieved regulatory approval, and nearly half of those approved were not subsequently launched. These findings suggest that while regulatory reliance may reduce regulatory barriers to obtaining marketing approval, commercial considerations may continue to play a major role in shaping sponsors’ decisions to pursue market entry, ultimately determining whether novel therapeutics become available to patients. Efforts to improve global access to novel therapeutics may therefore require more efficient approval pathways, as well as policies that enhance the commercial viability of introducing new therapeutics into smaller and lower-income markets, such as regional regulatory and purchasing collaborations that create larger pooled markets.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of 396 novel therapeutics approved by the FDA between 2008 and 2019, global regulatory approvals and market launches varied substantially across countries and were associated far more strongly with country development characteristics and sponsor capacity than with drug-level characteristics that facilitate approval and launch in the United States. These findings suggest that improving global access to novel therapeutics will require both efficient regulatory pathways, including the appropriate use of regulatory reliance, and policies that address the commercial barriers to market entry.
