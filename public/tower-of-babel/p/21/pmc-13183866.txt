A power–exponential secant modulus model for the compression and settlement behavior of compacted loess fill
Authors: Zehui Li, Siyuan Ren, Anzhi Shen, Wankui Ni, Qixuan Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUneven foundation settlement is a primary cause of various issues in high-fill engineering projects, arising from differences in the compressibility of fill materials associated with their inherent heterogeneity. Accurately characterizing these properties is therefore essential for ensuring engineering stability. This study proposes a novel nonlinear secant modulus model based on a composite power exponential model (CPE model), designed to overcome the limitations of existing models in consistently characterizing the entire consolidation process. Compared with traditional secant modulus models, the proposed model is more concise, requires fewer parameters, and captures the nonlinear evolution of soil mechanical properties during compaction. Its applicability and modeling advantages were validated through one-dimensional consolidation tests on field-compacted loess. The results demonstrate that the power–exponential composite evolution law of the proposed model reproduces the characteristic evolutionary trend of the secant modulus: a rapid increase at low stress, steady growth at intermediate stress levels, and gradual deceleration under high stress. The underlying physical mechanism of the model corresponds to key processes such as pore closure, structural weakening, and skeletal rearrangement, thereby accurately reflecting the stress-dependent evolution of the secant modulus. This demonstrates the model’s strong applicability for analyzing compressive deformation and predicting settlement in high-fill foundations under complex stress conditions.Keywords: Compacted loess, Secant modulus, Settlement calculation, Nonlinear stress–strain relationshipSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

With the continuous advancement of regional economic development and transportation infrastructure construction, urbanization has progressively extended into hilly and deeply incised areas1,2. These regions are characterized by highly undulating terrain and complex geological conditions, with the availability of developable land severely constrained by multiple factors3. Consequently, large-scale terrain leveling and gully-filling projects have been carried out in recent years4,5. However, high-fill embankments typically comprise multiple layers of compacted soil. Significant variations in compaction degree, moisture content, and layered construction techniques lead to pronounced spatial heterogeneity in the soil properties within the fill. Under self-weight stress and subsequent consolidation, high-fill foundations are susceptible to considerable overall settlement and differential settlement. This has become a critical factor restricting the long-term stability and operational safety of projects in gully-reclaimed areas6–8. To effectively control and mitigate settlement issues in high-fill foundations, it is essential to conduct an in-depth investigation of the deformation characteristics of compacted soil under varying stress states and compaction conditions, and to develop computational models that accurately represent its intrinsic mechanical response9–11.

Researchers have systematically investigated the deformation characteristics of compacted soil under varying stress conditions and their influence on settlement behavior, with particular emphasis on stress–strain relationships to elucidate compression mechanisms. Zhang et al.12 reported through lateral confinement compression tests that the stress–strain relationship of compacted loess can be well fitted by a hyperbolic model within a limited stress range, demonstrating high fitting accuracy. However, as stress levels increase, the fitting stability of the hyperbolic model deteriorates markedly, hindering its ability to accurately capture nonlinear deformation behavior under high-stress conditions. To address this issue, Chen et al.13 employed a power function model to characterize the compression behavior at high stress levels. Although this approach improved fitting accuracy in the high-stress range, its high sensitivity to experimental data caused deviations in the low-stress range, leading to inadequate overall model stability. To further enhance the applicability of compression models across the entire stress range, Huang et al.14 proposed the Gunary model based on combined experimental observations and theoretical analysis. This model integrates the advantages of hyperbolic and exponential functions through a composite formulation, thereby capturing the nonlinear compression characteristics of compacted loess while improving curve continuity and adaptability to a certain extent. However, the Gunary model contains numerous parameters with unclear physical significance, which limits its stability in high-stress extrapolation and constrains its applicability in engineering practice. Consequently, there remains a need to develop a nonlinear model with a unified expression across the entire stress range and improved stability, in order to more accurately characterize the stress–strain behavior of compacted loess.

The deformation characteristics of compacted soil are reflected not only in the nonlinear form of the stress–strain curve but also, more importantly, in the progressive evolution of its physical and mechanical properties during loading15–17. Research has shown that the compression coefficient exhibits pronounced nonlinearity with respect to effective stress, whereas the relationship between soil stiffness and stress state is a critical factor governing foundation settlement and deformation rates18–22. Li et al.23 experimentally investigated the intrinsic relationships among compression index, compressive modulus, and secant modulus during compression, and confirmed that the secant modulus provides higher accuracy and broader applicability for characterizing vertically constrained deformation. Subsequently, Yang et al.24, Kong and Ding et al.25,26 developed modulus-based models using nonlinear functional formulations, including the power-function secant modulus model, the hyperbolic secant modulus model, and the Gunary secant modulus model. Although these models achieve satisfactory mathematical fitting of stress–strain relationships, they do not uncover the underlying physical mechanisms governing the stress-dependent evolution of compressive modulus. Consequently, their mechanical interpretation remains unclear, and their applicability is frequently constrained by specific experimental conditions. To address this limitation, Yang et al.27 further examined the variation of secant modulus with stress based on the Duncan–Chang hyperbolic model. However, because the formulation does not explicitly represent the asymptotic convergence of compressibility at high stress levels, it may yield nonphysical strain responses when extrapolated to the high-stress range, which is inconsistent with the actual deformation behavior of compacted loess. In summary, existing secant modulus models generally suffer from limited applicability, high parameter sensitivity, or insufficient physical justification, making it difficult to establish a robust formulation that can describe compressive behavior across the full stress range28. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a new model that is applicable across the full stress range and supported by a clear physical mechanism.

This study investigates the deformation characteristics of loess through one-dimensional consolidation tests, with particular emphasis on high-fill projects located in gully-developed loess regions. The objectives of this study are as follows: (1) to systematically examine the deformation behavior of compacted loess across a full range of stress levels based on compression tests; (2) to propose a novel nonlinear secant modulus model and establish a foundation settlement prediction framework using the layered-summation method; (3) to validate the accuracy and engineering applicability of the proposed model through comparisons with existing mainstream models; and (4) to analyze the sensitivity of the model parameters and elucidate their physical significance.

Materials and methods

Test materials

The loess samples used in this study were collected from a high-fill construction site in Tongchuan City, Shaanxi Province, China, as part of authorized geotechnical monitoring and engineering assessment activities. A borehole with a depth of 40 m was drilled at the site, and systematic sampling was conducted at 1 m intervals, yielding a total of 40 sampling depths. Two undisturbed soil samples were obtained at each depth, resulting in 80 undisturbed specimens in total. The sampling activities were carried out with the consent of the project owner and did not involve protected areas or regulated materials. Following sample collection, the borehole was retained and subsequently instrumented for long-term foundation stability monitoring, ensuring that the drilling activities did not result in adverse environmental impacts. Each specimen was trimmed into a cylindrical shape with a diameter of approximately 10 cm and a height of 20 cm, then immediately sealed using double layers of plastic film and aluminum foil and labeled according to sampling depth. All specimens were transported to the laboratory under low-temperature and vibration-free conditions for subsequent testing. Physical property tests were conducted in accordance with relevant ASTM standards29–32.

Test methods

This study employed a WG-type high-pressure consolidation apparatus to perform one-dimensional consolidation tests, with the laboratory temperature maintained at approximately 25 °C. Undisturbed samples from various depths were trimmed into consolidation specimens with a diameter of 61.8 mm and a height of 20 mm. The tests adopted a graded loading sequence of 12.5, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1600 kPa. Beginning with the second load level, each subsequent load was applied using the same loading ratio as the preceding level (load ratio = 1). The specimens were considered to have reached consolidation equilibrium after 24 h at each pressure level, or when the deformation rate fell below 0.01 mm/h. Consolidation deformation was continuously recorded throughout each loading stage. To minimize moisture loss during prolonged consolidation, the tops of the specimens were covered with double layers of moist filter paper and sealing film. The test procedures strictly followed the relevant ASTM 2435/2435M33, with appropriate adjustments to loading duration and moisture-retention measures based on specimen characteristics to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the test results. Because strains are very small at low stresses, deformation readings in this range can exhibit higher relative scatter; therefore, stable reading criteria were enforced and repeat measurements (replicate readings and replicate specimens) were used to verify repeatability in the low-stress range.

Layer L3, situated between 18 and 25 m, corresponds to a transitional contact zone between the artificial fill and the undisturbed loess. It shows a distinct combination of decreasing dry density and increasing moisture content relative to both the principal fill (L1–L2) and the natural loess (L4). From a mechanical standpoint, these physical-property patterns are consistent with an interface zone where compaction becomes less effective and the soil fabric is more susceptible to weakening. This indicates variations in material sources and differences in construction practices across different stages of filling. It also confirms that the study section developed pronounced multi-layered structural heterogeneity during the construction process. This spatial heterogeneity not only governs the compressibility sensitivity of soils at different depths but also directly influences the settlement behavior of high-fill foundations, thereby providing a necessary basis for subsequent analysis.

Model parameter sensitivity analysis

Based on the fitted relationships of the specimens, a sensitivity analysis was performed for parameters A, B and C in the CPE secant modulus model. To examine the influence of each parameter on model behavior, the fitting results of the L1-layer specimens were taken as representative, with the baseline curve shown as a solid black line. Under the condition that the remaining parameters were held constant, controlled perturbations were independently applied to A, B and C. By comparing the variations in the resulting simulated curves, the governing roles and internal response mechanisms associated with each parameter were identified.

Figure 6 illustrates the influence of each parameter on the nonlinear secant modulus curve. As shown in Fig. 6a, variations in parameter A primarily induce a vertical upward or downward shift of the entire curve, affecting the modulus magnitude across the full stress range without altering the curve shape. Figure 6b shows the effect of parameter B. A larger B accelerates the modulus increase in the intermediate pressure range, while the curve differences gradually diminish at high stresses, indicating that B mainly governs the amplitude of variation within the nonlinear transition zone. Note that the fitted B values may be small in magnitude because the controlling quantity is B which remains well scaled over the practical stress range. As shown in Fig. 6c, changes in parameter C primarily influence the curve behavior at high stress levels. A larger C accelerates the convergence of the modulus toward its stable value, demonstrating that C is the key parameter controlling the post-compaction stiffness evolution of the particle skeleton and the asymptotic behavior of the modulus under high stresses.

Model comparison and validation

Settlement prediction is a critical component of deformation control in high-fill engineering projects. In particular, for thick-layer fills and valley infill under pronounced lateral confinement, the soil’s nonlinear compressibility significantly affects the final settlement35,36. To further assess the engineering applicability of the proposed model, settlement predictions were conducted for representative field conditions and compared with conventional calculation methods. This comparison allows a comprehensive evaluation of the model’s performance in capturing the nonlinear deformation behavior of compacted loess.

Based on the aforementioned parameter calibration results, this study systematically compares three commonly used settlement prediction methods at a typical 40 m thick loess high-fill site: (1) the traditional layered summation method; (2) the secant modulus method based on the Gunary model; (3) the CPE secant modulus model proposed in this paper; (4) the compression index method. To ensure comparability, all three methods adopted the same layering scheme and stress increment settings, treating each soil layer as a one-dimensional confined compression element. Within the secant modulus framework, the settlement of each soil layer was calculated from the nonlinear strain increment, as expressed in Eq. (33).

Table 4 indicates that although the total settlement predictions from the three methods are generally consistent, the CPE secant modulus method exhibits superior predictive accuracy. Its total settlement of 74.36 mm lies between the layered summation method (75.29 mm) and the Gunary secant modulus method (73.39 mm), with relative deviations from both conventional methods below 2%. For reference, the compression index method yields a larger total settlement of 89.92 mm. This higher value is consistent with the inherently conservative nature of the compression index approach. From the prediction results, comparison with field-monitored data indicates that the predicted settlement magnitudes are reasonable at the engineering scale. More importantly, this value not only occupies an intermediate position but also effectively compensates for the simplifications inherent in the layered summation method when addressing the soil’s nonlinear compressive behavior.

Regarding model performance, the CPE secant modulus model attained correlation coefficients between 0.988 and 0.998 for all soil layers. It accurately captures the pronounced nonlinear consolidation behavior in the low-stress stage while consistently representing the modulus growth trends under high-stress conditions. Compared with conventional power-function models and the Gunary model, in which the secant modulus is parameterized directly as a function of stress, the CPE model characterizes soil deformation through an integrated stress–strain relationship, with the secant modulus obtained as a derived quantity. This formulation leads to a smooth and continuous evolution of stiffness over the full stress range and facilitates a straightforward implementation within one-dimensional layered settlement calculations. As a result, the CPE model exhibits stable numerical behavior when applied to multilayer profiles with heterogeneous properties and non-uniform stress distributions, without requiring additional empirical treatment.

In engineering applications, this model presents clear advantages: it does not rely on hard-to-obtain inputs such as initial porosity; instead, all model parameters are identified through calibration against conventional one-dimensional consolidation tests, without requiring independent parameter measurements. Its computational procedure is simplified, producing smoother depth-dependent settlement distributions and excellent convergence; it demonstrates superior stability and applicability in predicting the settlement of highly compacted fill materials.

Discussion

Theoretical analysis of the compaction–consolidation and hardening behavior of compacted loess

The consolidation–hardening behavior of compacted loess under loading can be reasonably explained by the microstructural characteristics of structural soils and classical compression mechanisms37–39. Compacted soils generally comprise remolded granular aggregates, fine-grained interstitial material, and minor secondary structural units. Their initial structure displays a certain degree of openness, albeit weaker than that of natural structural loess40–42. During the low-stress stage, particle arrangements retain their original loose configuration, allowing large-pore-dominated compressive deformation with minimal structural constraints. Consequently, the reduction in void ratio is limited, and the increase in secant modulus remains relatively small. As vertical stress increases, the original interparticle structural connections gradually weaken. Under localized stress, the fine interstitial particles redistribute, and some microstructures are reconfigured. This results in a marked decrease in void ratio and a rapid increase in secant modulus. This stage can be considered a transition from structural collapse to skeletal rearrangement, with deformation characteristics consistent with the typical yielding response of structural soils. During the high-stress stage, pore spaces approach near-complete compression, and the soil gradually transitions to a stress regime governed by the granular skeleton. The interstitial filling function of fine particles intensifies, and new load-bearing structures gradually form between particles, causing the secant modulus to stabilize. At this stage, soil deformation primarily relies on further consolidation of the skeleton framework and micro-scale particle displacements, reflecting a typical hardening behavior.

The compaction-hardening evolution patterns revealed by experimental results can be consistently explained through the microstructural adjustments of structural soils, the evolution of particle contact relationships, and classical compression mechanisms. This also demonstrates that the secant modulus-stress relationship more effectively captures the complete transition process from structure-controlled to skeleton-controlled behavior in soils than the simple stress-pore ratio relationship curve.

Model evaluation and limitations

Based on the aforementioned fitting results and settlement analysis, the CPE secant modulus model demonstrates stable and consistent performance across the full stress range of compacted loess. Parameters A, B and C provide clear guidance for soil deformation responses across different stress stages: Parameter A controls the scale strain, with its magnitude reflecting the compressibility potential of the soil under very high stress; Parameter B governs curvature changes in the low- to medium-stress range, regulating the rate of modulus increase during structural weakening in the medium-stress range and characterizing sensitivity to structural degradation; Parameter C determines the asymptotic trend of the secant modulus with increasing stress, where a larger C causes the curve to enter a gradual convergence phase earlier in the high-stress range, with reduced hardening, typically corresponding to soil layers with a relatively stable skeleton and well-developed particle contacts. Perturbing parameters A, B and C does not affect the continuity or monotonicity of the curve across the full stress range. This indicates that the typical compaction mechanism of compacted loess, involving pore closure, structural weakening, and skeleton reconstruction, remains consistent throughout all stress stages.

The settlement comparison results further confirm the effectiveness of the model in practical engineering applications. Compared with the Gunary secant modulus method, the CPE secant modulus model exhibits higher fitting accuracy across all soil layers, generates smoother settlement prediction curves, and ensures stable deformation control for highly compacted fill materials. Furthermore, model parameters can be directly obtained from standard one-dimensional confined consolidation tests, obviating the need for difficult-to-determine indicators such as initial porosity. This feature renders the model more readily implementable in practical engineering applications.

Meanwhile, it is worth emphasizing that the model form can also be used for remolded or reworked loess prepared under different compaction energies.However, the calibrated parameters should be regarded as state-dependent rather than universal constants, because compaction energy and construction procedures can alter the initial density, moisture condition, and fabric of the fill, thereby shifting the stress–strain evolution. For practical implementation, it is therefore more appropriate to calibrate the parameters for representative construction conditions and apply them within the corresponding range, updating the calibration only when the compaction state changes appreciably.

It should be noted that the model is developed based on one-dimensional loading tests, and its applicability is therefore constrained by specific stress paths and boundary conditions. In engineering scenarios involving pronounced three-dimensional stress states and variable lateral constraints, additional validation under controlled multiaxial stress paths is needed, and a natural extension is to express the governing stress variable in terms of stress invariants such as the mean effective stress to describe volumetric deformation. Moreover, the model currently does not incorporate environmental and temporal factors, such as humidity variations and loading rates. If time-dependent effects are to be considered, the constitutive form can be extended by introducing an explicit time-dependent component.

Conclusion

This study investigated the deformation behavior of compacted loess at multiple depths under different stress stages via one-dimensional consolidation tests. A novel secant modulus model based on a power-exponential composite (CPE) function was developed and validated for its capability to accurately characterize soil behavior across the entire stress range. The main conclusions of this study are summarized as follows:

Both the curves and the curves reveal a continuous evolution process: pore closure at low stress, structural failure at medium stress, and skeleton rearrangement at high stress. The secant modulus increases monotonically with effective stress, with significant differences among layers. These differences fundamentally reflect the influence of compaction degree, density structure, and particle composition on the deformation characteristics of the soil.

The settlement prediction method based on the power-exponential composite (CPE) secant modulus model exhibits excellent engineering applicability, effectively improving the reliability of settlement calculations for high-fill foundations. Compared with the layered summation method and the Gunary model, the total settlement predicted using the secant modulus approach under the CPE model shows a smoother distribution of layered deformation, higher computational stability, and a more realistic characterization of settlement response for highly compacted fills. Model parameters can be directly determined from conventional consolidation tests, further demonstrating the model’s strong applicability in engineering practice.
