Gender-Affirming Hormone Therapy and Cardiac Structure Among Transgender Women and Gender-Diverse Individuals
Authors: Mabel Toribio, Vencel Juhasz, Ria Talathi, Thiago Quinaglia, Melissa Wang, Matilda Delgado, Lidia S Szczepaniak, Tomas G Neilan, Markella V Zanni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKey words: cardiac structure, gender-affirming hormone therapy, gender-diverse, myocardial fibrosis, myocardial steatosis, transgender

As part of longitudinal care, transgender women are commonly prescribed gender-affirming hormone therapy (GAHT) consisting of estradiol coupled with hormonal therapy to suppress testosterone production. Retrospective data suggest that transgender women on GAHT may have a heightened risk of cardiovascular-specific mortality but data on the mechanisms underlying this increased cardiovascular risk are limited.1 GAHT may affect cardiovascular risk through its effects on myocardial structure. Myocardial structure has been shown to be influenced by estrogen therapy among cisgender women and by androgen deprivation therapy among cisgender men. There is, however, a paucity of data on how estradiol combined with testosterone suppression, as is prescribed longitudinally for gender affirmation, affects myocardial structure. We performed a prospective observational imaging study to characterize the effects of GAHT on cardiac structure to improve our limited understanding of the potential mechanisms underlying the heightened cardiovascular disease risk among individuals receiving GAHT.

What is the clinical question being addressed?

What are the effects of gender-affirming hormone therapy on cardiac structure among transgender women and gender-diverse individuals?

What is the main finding?

After 12 months, more testosterone suppression was associated with more myocardial fibrosis and less testosterone suppression was associated with less myocardial steatosis.

Methods

Twenty-four transgender and gender-diverse individuals (median [IQR]: age 25 [19-30] years) who were planning to initiate or who recently initiated GAHT with 17 beta (β)-estradiol and a testosterone suppressor through their clinician were enrolled. Participants underwent cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and cardiac proton spectroscopy (1H-MRS) at baseline and 12 months. Left ventricular (LV) mass, myocardial extracellular volume (ECV) (a surrogate for diffuse myocardial fibrosis), intramyocardial triglyceride levels, estradiol, and free testosterone were measured at baseline and 12 months. Increased stress has been postulated to be one of the mechanisms for heightened cardiovascular risk among transgender individuals.1 To ascertain potential relationships between stress and myocardial structure in our study cohort, we assessed perceived stress using the Perceived Stress Scale-10. Between-group differences in the change in myocardial structure parameters were assessed using a Wilcoxon rank sum test. Furthermore, associations between Perceived Stress Scale scores and myocardial structure parameters were assessed using a Spearman’s rho correlation coefficient. This study was approved by the Massachusetts General Brigham Institutional Review Board.

Results

Seventy-nine percent of participants identified as a transgender woman and 21% percent identified as either nonbinary or other. All participants were assigned male sex at birth. No participants were living with HIV and 4% were current smokers. Thirty-three percent self-reported as currently having depression and 42% self-reported as currently having anxiety. Sixty-three percent of participants were on oral or sublingual 17β-estradiol, 20% were on injection 17β-estradiol, and 17% were on transdermal 17β-estradiol. For their testosterone suppression, 92% of participants were on spironolactone, 4% were on leuprolide, and 4% were on bicalutamide. Final estradiol and testosterone levels among study participants were reflective of participant compliance with GAHT. Testosterone suppression is one of the primary objectives for this type of GAHT and routinely monitored clinically. The analytic cohort was divided into 2 groups (incomplete testosterone suppression vs complete testosterone suppression) based on median follow-up free testosterone levels (n = 12 in each group) to reflect varying levels of testosterone suppression. While the follow-up free testosterone level was significantly higher in the above-median group (“incomplete free testosterone suppression”) compared to the below-median group (“complete free testosterone suppression”; 7.8 [2.5-33.7] vs 1.3 [0.8-1.5], P < 0.001), the follow-up estradiol level did not differ between groups (Figure 1).

Over 12 months, indexed LV mass decreased among the complete free testosterone suppression group while increasing modestly among the incomplete free testosterone suppression group (between-group P for change = 0.04). Furthermore, myocardial ECV increased in the complete free testosterone suppression group while remaining relatively unchanged in the incomplete free testosterone suppression group (between-group P for change = 0.045). Intramyocardial triglyceride content remained relatively unchanged in the complete free testosterone suppression group while decreasing in the incomplete free testosterone suppression group (between-group P for change = 0.02) (Figure 1). At follow-up among the incomplete testosterone suppression group, perceived stress scores were directly related to LV mass (ρ = 0.58; P = 0.006) and indexed LV mass (ρ = 0.52; P = 0.02).

Discussion

The results from this exploratory analysis suggest that varying levels of testosterone suppression, when coupled with 17β-estradiol therapy, may have differing effects on the myocardium. In this regard, more testosterone suppression was associated with an increase in ECV (ie, more fibrosis) and less testosterone suppression was associated with a decrease in intramyocardial triglycerides (ie, less steatosis). These findings are of potential clinical importance, given that similar increases in the ECV are associated with incident heart failure and cardiovascular death, and increased myocardial steatosis may similarly also contribute to adverse cardiac remodeling and heart failure.2,3 Furthermore, a decrease in LV mass with GAHT, as observed in the complete testosterone suppression group, is plausible given lower LV mass among cisgender women vs cisgender men.

There are several limitations to this study. Our study included a small, relatively young population. The study’s young median age is reflective of enrollment of participants initiating GAHT, and could have influenced our findings, such that an older population could have exhibited a higher degree of myocardial fibrosis. Our findings that higher stress associates with higher LV mass highlight the need for future research on the role of stress on myocardial structure in this population. Future studies that include transgender women and gender-diverse individuals not receiving GAHT, for example, could provide additional insights on the role of stressors uniquely experienced by this population on myocardial structure. Future, larger studies may also permit investigation of differences in cardiometabolic effects across GAHT regimens, as there may be formulation-specific effects. Additionally, the lack of participants living with HIV in our study limits the generalizability of our findings given the disproportionate burden of HIV among transgender women. Studies examining how HIV affects myocardial structure among transgender and gender-diverse individuals are needed, particularly in light of the increased prevalence of HIV among transgender women and the known effects of HIV on myocardial structure in cisgender populations. Lastly, given the observational nature of our results, we cannot conclude that GAHT is causal to the changes in myocardial structure observed during our study.

Conclusions

This exploratory study highlights how the balance of testosterone and estrogen with GAHT may impact myocardial structure; however, larger studies are needed. Whether the subclinical changes observed are reversible, progress with prolonged GAHT, or ultimately translate into unfavorable cardiac outcomes, such as heart failure, remains unknown. Our results support the need for increased awareness of and further investigation of the effects of GAHT on myocardial function and structure, toward optimizing the cardiovascular health of those prescribed GAHT.
