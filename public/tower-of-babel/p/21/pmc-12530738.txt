Unilateral page kidney in end-stage renal disease patient presenting as recurrent flash pulmonary edema: A rare association
Authors: Vibhu Jain, Ashutosh Nagpal, Aditya Prakash Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPage kidney refers to the extrinsic compression of renal parenchyma causing renovascular hypertension. We present the case of a 58-year-old female with refractory hypertension and recurrent episodes of flash pulmonary edema following trivial trauma. Despite initial management with percutaneous drainage of the perinephric hematoma, she eventually required a nephrectomy due to persistent symptoms. The symptoms resolved post nephrectomy. The case highlights the importance of early diagnosis and timely intervention in Page kidney to prevent long-term complications.

INTRODUCTION

Extrinsic compression of the kidney causing compression of the intrarenal vessels, microvascular ischemia, and activation of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone (RAAS) leading to hypertension was first described by Irvin Page in 1939 and is known as Page kidney.[1] We present a case of a female presenting with refractory hypertension, and recurrent episodes of flash pulmonary edema days after trivial trauma, which was managed successfully with nephrectomy of the Page kidney.

CASE REPORT

A 58-year-old female, a known case of diabetes and hypertension, presented to the emergency with uncontrolled hypertension and dyspnea. She had a history of fall in the bathroom around 1 month back. On evaluation, she was found to have uncontrolled hypertension (blood pressure – 200/110 mmHg) for which nitroglycerine (NTG) infusion had to be started. Signs of fluid overload such as bilateral basal crepitations and bilateral pedal pitting edema were noted. Oxygen supplementation was needed, and she maintained a saturation of around 97% at high-flow oxygen with a nonrebreathing mask. Baseline blood investigations showed elevated serum creatinine levels (8 mg/dL). The coagulation profile was within normal limits, with a platelet count of 195 × 109/L and an International Normalized Ratio of 1.07. A two-dimensional echocardiogram suggested a left ventricular ejection fraction of 45%, concentric left ventricular hyperplasia, and grade 1 diastolic dysfunction. A contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) was planned under hemodialysis (HD) cover, as a noncontrast computed tomography done outside was suggestive of a perinephric hematoma along with some free fluid in the abdomen, raising suspicion of other solid organ injuries. The CECT showed a right perinephric hematoma causing compression of the renal parenchyma [Figure 1]. The contralateral kidney was small and atrophic, measuring 7.7 cm. No other solid organ injuries were noted.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography images in the (a) coronal plane and (b) axial plane showing the perinephric hematoma (red arrow) compressing the native kidney (white arrow)

She was admitted and underwent HD following which the shortness of breath (SOB) improved. A 10Fr pigtail catheter was placed by the intervention radiology team, which drained 200 ml of altered blood over 24 h. However, the next day she was intubated and started on mechanical ventilation because of the sudden onset of dyspnea. She was extubated subsequently after a single session of HD. The following day, she developed similar complaints in the form of sudden onset SOB, was intubated, and extubated post-HD within 12 h. In view of recurrent flash pulmonary edema due to page kidney, she was planned for nephrectomy 7 days after the day of admission. Intraoperatively, a large hematoma was noted around the right kidney, and a nephrectomy was performedtransperitoneally. The cut section of the nephrectomy specimen showed a large perinephric hematoma [Figure 2], and the histopathology confirmed the same along with changes of diabetic nephropathy in the native kidneys. Postoperatively the NTG infusion was stopped and the blood pressure was controlled on oral antihypertensives, there were no episodes of SOB and she was discharged on postoperative day 9. Currently, at 1-month follow-up, she is doing well with blood pressure controlled on three oral anti-hypertensives and on maintenance HD twice weekly.

DISCUSSION

Various etiologies for Page kidney have been described, however, there has been a recent shift in etiology, with trauma following contact sports or roadside accidents being superseded by postbiopsy hematoma (especially of transplanted kidneys) as the most common cause.[2] Page kidney usually follows trauma, however, there are cases of Page kidney reported with no apparent trauma or trivial trauma.[3] In this case there was only trivial trauma in the form of a fall in the bathroom leading to this condition. The causality in this case is explained based on a temporal association; hypertension worsening after a fall secondary to Page kidney due to the hematoma, leading to repeated episodes of flash pulmonary edema; and resolution of flash pulmonary edema, and well controlled hypertension after the nephrectomy.

Flash pulmonary edema is believed to be secondary to a sudden surge in RAAS activity, is characteristically sudden in onset, and resolves rapidly with adequate medical management. There are reported cases of unilateral renal artery stenosis manifesting as flash pulmonary edema, but not with Page kidney.[4] To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case of Page kidney manifesting as recurrent flash pulmonary edema.

Clinical presentation of Page kidney can vary from isolated hypertension, incidental detection during workup for hypertension, flank pain, and acute renal colic. Diagnostic modalities include an ultrasound and a CECT of the abdomen.[4] The age of the hematoma is a major deciding factor for treatment planning. Hematomas < 3 weeks old can be managed medically while waiting for spontaneous resolution. However, if the blood pressure is uncontrolled, the hematoma should be drained percutaneously. Hematomas more than 3 weeks old are less likely to respond with medical/percutaneous intervention because of a restrictive capsule that forms around the kidney causing its compression, and such hematomas usually need capsulotomy/nephrectomy.[5] A multidisciplinary team comprising nephrologists, urologists, intervention radiologists, and intensive and critical care physicians were involved in the decision-making process in this case. A step-ladder approach was followed. Conservative management was tried first, however, considering her persistent hypertensive state, a plan was made for decompressing the hematoma in the form of minimally invasive intervention (10 Fr pigtail placement) which drained 200 ml of hemorrhagic content initially. Despite this, the patient continued to have refractory hypertension requiring NTG support along with episodes of flash pulmonary edema. Hence, a multidisciplinary decision was made to go ahead with surgical intervention to relieve the compression. A debate for capsulotomy versus nephrectomy ensued further. However, in view of end-stage renal disease, small-sized kidneys, and nephrectomy being a definitive procedure, the decision for nephrectomy was taken.

CONCLUSION

A high index of suspicion must be present for diagnosing Page kidney. Persistent or refractory hypertension, especially post trauma or post-procedure should be considered a red flag and warrant thorough workup to rule out Page kidney. Timely diagnosis and prompt intervention, either medical or interventional, in the form of percutaneous or open drainage of the extrinsic compression is key to prevent long-term renal damage.
