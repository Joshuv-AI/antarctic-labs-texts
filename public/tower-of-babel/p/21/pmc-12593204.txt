Phase Ib study of xentuzumab and abemaciclib in patients with advanced solid tumors and in combination with endocrine therapy in patients with advanced breast cancer
Authors: D Yee, H Iwata, P LoRusso, M Oliveira, A Gonçalves, A Stradella, M Vidal, MP Sablin, MC Hardebeck, Y Fukuyama, N Forman, M Puig, RM Lorence
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis phase Ib trial assessed the insulin-like growth factor 1/2 neutralizing antibody, xentuzumab, plus abemaciclib ± endocrine therapy (ET) in patients with advanced/metastatic solid tumors, including hormone receptor (HR)-positive breast cancer.Patients and methodsIn part 1, patients with advanced solid tumors received escalating doses of xentuzumab + abemaciclib (cohort A). In part 2, dose-finding cohorts B-D had advanced/metastatic HR-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative breast cancer and received xentuzumab + abemaciclib plus letrozole, anastrozole, or fulvestrant. Part 3 included expansion cohorts assessing xentuzumab + abemaciclib plus fulvestrant in patients with HR-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative breast cancer with visceral (cohort D1) or non-visceral disease (cohort D2) who had progressed following ET, or non-visceral disease who had progressed following ET and a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor (cohort F). Primary endpoints were maximum tolerated dose (cohorts A-D), 18-month progression-free survival (cohort D1/D2) and disease control (cohort F). Comprehensive biomarker analyses were undertaken.ResultsA total of 133 patients were treated. The maximum tolerated dose was xentuzumab 1000 mg once weekly plus abemaciclib 150 mg twice daily (cohorts A-D). The most common grade ≥3 adverse event was decreased neutrophil count. Cohorts B-D demonstrated response rates of ≥25%. The 18-month progression-free survival rate in cohorts D1/D2 was 41.4%/78.5%. The disease control rate in cohort F was 40.0%. Biomarker analysis indicated target engagement. Prognostic biomarkers included total serum insulin-like growth factor 1 concentrations, expression of CCND1, and MCL-1 mutations.ConclusionsXentuzumab + abemaciclib + ETs demonstrated manageable tolerability and promising efficacy, especially in patients with breast cancer and non-visceral metastases.Key words: xentuzumab, abemaciclib, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF-2), hormone receptor-positive breast cancer

Introduction

The type 1 insulin-like growth factor receptor (IGF-1R) and cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) 4 and 6-retinoblastoma pathways are implicated in the pathogenesis and resistance mechanisms of several cancers, including hormone receptor (HR)-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative breast cancer,1, 2, 3, 4 and non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC).5,6 CDK 4/6 inhibitors plus endocrine therapy (ET) are standard of care first-line therapy for advanced HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer.7 Abemaciclib is an oral, continuously-dosed CDK 4/6 inhibitor that is approved in this setting and can be combined with an aromatase inhibitor as initial endocrine-based therapy, or combined with fulvestrant in patients with metastatic disease following ET, and as monotherapy in patients whose disease has progressed following ET and prior chemotherapy in the metastatic setting.8 Activation of IGF-1R increases cyclin D1 concentrations; therefore, dual inhibition of IGF and CDK 4/6 could decrease cell proliferation through disruption of cell cycle progression.9

Several categories of IGF-targeting drugs have been assessed in clinical trials, including IGF-1R monoclonal antibodies such as ganitumab, figitumumab, dalotuzumab, cixutumumab, and IGF-1R tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). Trials with IGF-1R antibodies plus antiestrogens, however, have been disappointing in breast cancer, perhaps due to inadequate inhibition of IGF signaling.10 Furthermore, both IGF-1R antibodies and TKIs can cause hyperglycemia, either by elevating concentrations of growth hormone and IGF binding proteins or by direct inhibition of insulin receptor (IR)-A and -B kinase, respectively.11

Xentuzumab is a humanized immunoglobulin G1 monoclonal antibody that binds with high affinity to IGF-1 and IGF-2 and neutralizes proliferative and pro-survival cellular signaling triggered by both proteins in tumor cells.12 This mechanism of action differs from IGF-1R antibodies and TKIs.12 On the one hand, xentuzumab inhibits the ability of both IGF ligands to interact with IGF-1R, and by neutralizing IGF-2, which has a high affinity for IR-A, it can potentially block compensatory IR-A signaling which has been implicated in resistance to IGF-1R antibodies.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 In cell-based assays, xentuzumab demonstrated potent inhibition of IGF-1R and IR-A signaling. In contrast, IGF-1R antibodies had no effect on IR-A signaling via IGF-2 and were less effective than xentuzumab at inhibiting IGF-1R signaling.12 In a model of metastatic prostate cancer to bone, an IGF neutralizing antibody was effective inhibiting receptor activation by both IGF-1 and -2 and decreased tumor growth.17 Also, unlike IGF-1R antibodies, xentuzumab is unlikely to disrupt growth hormone homeostasis,18, 19, 20, 21 and rarely induces hyperglycemia.21, 22, 23, 24 Xentuzumab monotherapy has demonstrated preliminary antitumor activity in two phase I studies in patients with advanced solid tumors.22 A phase Ib/II study in patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer demonstrated that xentuzumab combined with everolimus + exemestane was well tolerated. In the randomized part of this study, this triplet combination demonstrated evidence of progression-free survival (PFS) benefit versus everolimus + exemestane in patients with non-visceral metastases.23 In the subsequent randomized phase II XENERA-1 trial, however, which was undertaken specifically in patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer with non-visceral metastases,24 no PFS benefit was observed with the triplet combination versus everolimus + exemestane alone. In the phase II trial, very few patients were previously treated with a CDK 4/6 inhibitor (none in the xentuzumab arm) while in XENERA-1, about 75% of enrolled patients had previous treatment with this class of drugs. Accordingly, >4 years after the start of this trial, the development of xentuzumab in oncology was terminated by the sponsor. Nevertheless, before that decision was made, this phase Ib trial was initiated to investigate the safety and efficacy of xentuzumab plus abemaciclib with or without ET in patients with advanced solid tumors, NSCLC, or HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer. The emphasis of the trial was patients with breast cancer, who comprised most enrolled patients.

Additionally, a comprehensive biomarker analysis was undertaken analyzing serum biomarkers, tumor DNA, and tumor messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels to identify prognostic factors associated with PFS in patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer receiving xentuzumab-based combinations.

Patients and methods

Study design and patients

This prospective, open-label, non-randomized, multiple dose-finding, phase Ib trial (NCT03099174) was conducted in three parts (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105863). In part 1, cohort A aimed to determine the maximum tolerated dose (MTD)/recommended phase II dose (RP2D) of xentuzumab + abemaciclib in patients with advanced solid tumors. In part 2, cohorts B, C, and D (dose-finding cohorts) aimed to identify the MTD/RP2D of xentuzumab + abemaciclib on a background therapy of letrozole (cohort B), anastrozole (cohort C), or fulvestrant (cohort D) in postmenopausal women with locally advanced/metastatic HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer. Also in part 2, cohort E, an expansion cohort, was conducted to characterize the safety/tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and preliminary efficacy of xentuzumab + abemaciclib in patients with NSCLC. In part 3, cohorts D1, D2, and F were conducted as expansion cohorts to further explore the antitumor activity of the triplet combination of xentuzumab + abemaciclib + fulvestrant in patients with locally advanced/metastatic HR-positive breast cancer with: visceral disease, defined as lung, liver, pleural, peritoneal, malignant pleural effusion and malignant peritoneal effusion involvement (cohort D1), or non-visceral disease (cohort D2) that progressed following ET (aromatase inhibitor or selective estrogen receptor modulator); or non-visceral disease that progressed following ET (aromatase inhibitor) and prior CDK inhibitor treatment (cohort F). The focus on non-visceral disease was based on the findings of a previous phase Ib/II study.23

It was intended to recruit ∼12 patients in each of cohorts A-D, 20 in cohorts E and F, and 30 in cohorts D1 and D2. Eligible patients were aged ≥18 years (≥20 years in Japan); male or female in cohorts A and E, female in cohorts B, C, D, D1, D2, and F. Patients were required to have World Health Organization/Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status 0 to 1 at screening. The remaining inclusion criteria were specific to each cohort. Cohort A: patients with histologically or cytologically confirmed locally advanced or metastatic, measurable, non-resectable solid tumors who had failed on, were intolerant to, or were inappropriate for all available treatments known to confer clinical benefit; life expectancy ≥3 months. Cohorts B, C, and D: postmenopausal women with histologically/cytologically confirmed, measurable, non-resectable, locally advanced or metastatic HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer who had received 0 to 2 prior lines of chemotherapy for metastatic disease. Previous adjuvant and neoadjuvant chemotherapy was permitted. In each cohort, patients had to be eligible for the corresponding hormonal therapy. For cohorts B and C, previous treatment with fulvestrant or exemestane was allowed. For cohort D, prior therapy with non-steroidal aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, letrozole) or exemestane was permitted. Cohort E: patients with histologically/cytologically confirmed, measurable [Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1)] stage IV NSCLC who had progressed after platinum-based chemotherapy/immunochemotherapy and were ineligible for standard second-line chemotherapy. Patients with EGFR-activating mutations or ALK translocations should have previously received the appropriate targeted treatments. Cohorts D1, D2, and F: postmenopausal women with histologically or cytologically confirmed, measurable (RECIST v1.1), non-resectable, locally advanced or metastatic HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer. Cohort D1 and D2 only: relapsed while receiving, or within 1 year of completion of neoadjuvant or adjuvant ET, with no subsequent ET; or relapsed >1 year after adjuvant ET having received first-line ET (either an antiestrogen or an aromatase inhibitor) and no prior chemotherapy for metastatic disease; or presented with de novo metastatic disease and then relapsed after first-line ET for metastatic disease with no prior chemotherapy. Patients in cohort D1 must have had at least one documented visceral metastasis and patients in cohort D2 must not have had any visceral metastasis at enrollment. Cohort F: patients were resistant to prior therapy with an aromatase inhibitor and a CDK4/6 inhibitor (excluding abemaciclib) for locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer without any visceral metastases. Patients had received no more than one line of prior endocrine-based therapy and had not received any prior chemotherapy for advanced/metastatic disease. Key exclusion criteria were: prior anti-CDK agents (apart from cohort F), previous treatment with IGF-1R targeting compounds, known active uncontrolled or symptomatic central nervous system metastases, concurrent or a history of interstitial lung disease/pneumonitis including lung fibrosis, a history of major surgery involving the stomach or small bowel, or pre-existing Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis.

The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonisation Guideline for Good Clinical Practice. Approval was obtained from the Independent Ethics Committees or Institutional Review Boards of all participating sites. All patients provided written informed consent.

Treatments

All cohorts received xentuzumab i.v. 1000 mg once weekly and abemaciclib 150 mg orally (p.o.) twice daily (b.i.d.). Additionally, cohort B received letrozole 2.5 mg p.o. once daily and cohort C received anastrozole 1 mg p.o. once daily, while cohorts D, D1, D2, and F received fulvestrant 500 mg by i.m. injection once a month (with an additional 500 mg dose given 2 weeks after the first dose). Treatment could be interrupted, and doses of study drugs could be reduced for dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) or adverse events (AEs). Xentuzumab could be reduced to 750 mg/week and then 500 mg/week. Abemaciclib could be reduced to 100 mg b.i.d. and then 50 mg b.i.d. Patients could also discontinue one of the study drugs while continuing on the others.

Endpoints and assessments

The primary endpoints in cohorts A-D were the MTD/RP2D of xentuzumab + abemaciclib with or without ET. DLTs were assessed during the first treatment cycle to assess the MTD/RP2D (criteria provided in the Supplementary Appendix, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105863). In the event of the MTD not being reached, the RP2D dose would be determined based on safety data and other available information. The MTD was defined as the highest dose with <25% risk of the true DLT rate being >33%, based on a Bayesian Logistic Regression Model (BLRM) employing the escalation with overdose control.25 The primary endpoint in cohort E was objective response, comprising complete response (CR), and partial response (PR) per RECIST v1.1; in cohort F, it was disease control, defined as best overall response of CR, PR, or confirmed stable disease lasting for ≥24 weeks, or non-CR/non-progressive disease lasting for ≥24 weeks per RECIST v1.1; in cohorts D1 and D2, it was PFS rate at 18 months. Secondary endpoints were disease control (cohorts D1, D2, and E only), time to objective response, duration of objective response, duration of disease control, PFS, and objective response (cohorts D1, D2, and F only).

Biomarkers were assessed in a quantitative and qualitative manner in cohorts D1, D2 and E. For the quantitative analysis, three IGF-related serum biomarkers were evaluated: total IGF-1, free IGF-1, and dissociable IGF-2. The change in free IGF-1 over time is considered in cohort D1/D2 and cohort E as (considered patient cohorts of adequate size, i.e. the number of patients analyzed was >10 at baseline and at least one additional timepoint during the trial). In cohorts D1 and D2, blood samples for the assessment of serum IGF-1 (free and total) and IGF-2 concentrations were taken on day 1 (before drug administration) of cycles 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, end of treatment (within 7 days of receiving last treatment) and follow-up (42 + 7 days after last treatment). IGF-1 and IGF-2 concentrations were assessed with validated immunoassays. Cohort E followed a similar schedule except that blood samples were taken on day 1 of cycles 1-12, 15, and 18. Additionally, the baseline tumor mRNA expression levels of 48 genes were assessed. For each marker, patients were dichotomized into two subgroups—high and low expression—using cut-offs determined by maximizing the partial log-likelihood of a Cox proportional hazards survival model (for PFS at 18 months) across many possible biomarker cut-off values.26 Finally, the coding sequence of 395 cancer-related genes (and introns of 31 genes) were assessed in tumor tissue samples for single nucleotide variants, selected rearrangements and other genetic alterations using the FoundationOne®-based T7 hybid-capture assay27 in the clinically accredited laboratories of Foundation Medicine, Inc. (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105863). Genetic alterations were classified into 10 different pathways: RAS; DNA damage/repair; PI3K, PI3K and/or PTEN; apoptosis/cell death; genes that correlated with the efficacy of everolimus in patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer based on post hoc analysis of the phase III BOLERO-2 trial28; cell cycle proliferation; PI3K class I; PI3K class II and III; selected short variants of PIK3CA (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105863). Of note, for both tumor mRNA and DNA analyses, we only present results here where the number of specimens collected were of sufficient quantity/quality to ensure meaningful analyses.

Statistical analyses

For the dose-escalation cohorts, dose escalation was guided by a BLRM with overdose control that was fitted to binary toxicity outcomes. The toxicity probability at each dose level was calculated to determine the MTD. The estimated DLT rate for each dose level was continuously updated using the BLRM as data accumulated. The decision on the next dose-escalation step (dose level and cohort size) and the final decision on the MTD was the responsibility of a Steering Committee of independent experts and sponsor representatives and chaired by the Coordinating Investigator. All endpoints for expansion cohorts were analyzed using descriptive statistics. For biomarker analysis, hazard ratios (HRs) of PFS with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were generated for quantitative (high versus low expression) and qualitative (genetic alterations present versus absent) analyses, using a Cox proportional hazards model, adjusted for dichotomized biomarker and visceral involvement at screening. Only those potential biomarkers for which there were at least three PFS events and five patients in each subgroup were modelled. HRs where the upper or lower bounds of the CI did not cross 1, indicating the P value was <0.05, were of interest; of particular interest were those with extreme HR values of >5 or <0.2.

Results

Patients and treatment

Cohorts A-D were conducted at 11 centers in France, Japan, Spain, and the USA between 31 May 2017 and 6 November 2020. Cohorts E, F, D1, and D2 were conducted at 30 sites in France, Japan, Spain, USA, Denmark, Finland, and Korea. At the cut-off date of 31 March 2022, 7 patients in cohort F, 8 in cohort D1, and 14 in cohort D2 were still taking part in the trial (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105863). Overall, 28 patients entered the dose escalation part of the trial; 6 in cohort A, 7 each in cohorts B and C, and 8 in cohort D. In the dose expansion part, 105 patients were treated: 26 in cohort E, 15 in cohort F, 33 in cohort D1, and 31 in cohort D2. Baseline demographic and disease characteristics are shown in Table 1. Included patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer were generally representative of the disease as a whole (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105863). In all cohorts, the main reason for xentuzumab discontinuation was progressive disease.

DLTs and MTD

During the MTD evaluation period (defined as the first 28-day treatment cycle), two out of six DLT-assessable patients in cohort A (grade 3 decreased neutrophil count in both cases); one of six DLT-assessable patients in cohort B (grade 3 decreased neutrophil count); one of six DLT-assessable patients in cohort C (grade 4 thrombocytopenia); and one of six DLT-assessable patients in cohort D (grade 3 neutropenia) had DLTs (Table 2). The four MTDs were determined as follows: (i) xentuzumab 1000 mg once weekly plus abemaciclib 150 mg b.i.d. in all cohorts; (ii) for cohort B, letrozole 2.5 mg once daily was added; (iii) for cohort C, anastrozole 1 mg once daily was added; and (iv) for cohort D (and D1, D2, and F), fulvestrant 500 mg once a month (with an additional 500 mg dose given 2 weeks after the first dose) was added.

Safety and tolerability

A summary of AEs and dose reductions and discontinuations of xentuzumab and abemaciclib across cohorts is shown in Table 2. The number of patients requiring dose reductions of xentuzumab due to AEs ranged from zero (cohorts D1 and F) to three (42.9%; cohort B). Overall, across all cohorts, 17 patients (12.8%) required dose reductions. The most common reasons for dose reduction were neutropenia/reduced neutrophil count (n = 10) and thrombocytopenia/reduced platelets (n = 3). IGF function has been linked to normal hematopoiesis.29,30 Dose reductions of abemaciclib ranged from 26.9% (cohort E) to 100.0% (cohort C). The number of patients who discontinued xentuzumab due to AEs ranged from zero (cohorts A and D) to three patients (20.0%) in cohort F (due to headache, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and pulmonary embolism, respectively). Abemaciclib discontinuation rates due to AEs ranged from 0% (cohort C) to 25% (cohort D). Two patients discontinued abemaciclib due to diarrhea and one patient each discontinued due to decreased neutrophils and elevated aspartate transaminase.

The occurrence of serious AEs ranged from 0.0% (cohort A) to 57.1% (cohorts B and C). There were four fatal AEs; one each in cohorts D1 (respiratory failure, possibly related to abemaciclib but confounded by a concomitant respiratory syncytial virus infection), D2 (myocardial infarction; not considered drug-related), E (pulmonary embolism, not considered drug-related), and F (acute respiratory distress syndrome, not considered drug-related; Table 2). The most frequently reported AEs of any grade are shown in Table 2, and the most frequently reported xentuzumab-related AEs and abemaciclib-related AEs are shown in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105863, respectively. Overall, the most reported AEs of any grade included diarrhea, nausea, anemia, and asthenia; the most common grade ≥3 AE was decreased neutrophil count (Table 2). Two patients experienced mild xentuzumab-related hyperglycemia (one patient each in cohort D and D2; both grade 1).

Antitumor activity

Objective response rates (ORRs; RECIST v1.1) of 28.6%, 28.6%, and 25.0% were observed in cohorts B, C, and D, with disease control rates (DCRs) of 57.1%, 71.4%, and 62.5%, respectively (Table 3). In cohorts D1 and D2, the PFS rates at 18 months were 41.4% and 78.5%, with medians of 13.6 months (95% CI 7.2-25.9 months) and 33.1 months (95% CI 19.4-33.1 months), respectively (Figure 1). ORR was 60.6% in cohort D1 and 19.4% in cohort D2, with DCRs of 69.7% and 77.4%, respectively; there was one CR each in cohorts D1 and D2 (Table 3). Tumor waterfall and spider plots for cohorts D1 and D2 are shown in Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105863.

Biomarkers

Analysis of the three largest cohorts, D1, D2, and E, demonstrated that free IGF-1 was reduced while patients were on xentuzumab treatment, indicating target engagement (Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105863). Similar trends were observed in the smaller cohorts (data not shown). Median PFS was longer in patients with high total serum IGF-1 concentrations (>115 U/ml) than low total serum IGF-1 concentrations (31.0 versus 10.9 months; HR 2.32, 95% CI 1.13-4.76, P = 0.022; Figure 2A). Of 395 genes assessed for mutations, only MCL-1 was found to be potentially prognostic for PFS (HR 0.17, 95% CI 0.04-0.74, P = 0.0182). Median PFS was 13.5 months in patients with mutated MCL-1 versus 25.9 months in patients without mutated MCL-1 (Figure 2B). From quantitative analysis of baseline tumor mRNA dichotomized into high and low subgroups, high expression levels of the following five genes appeared to yield a better prognosis for PFS with HR values of >5.0: the cyclin D1 gene, CCND1 (HR 17.27, 95% CI 4.18-71.32, P < 0.0001); BCL-2 (HR 11.40, 95% CI 3.0-44.0, P = 0.0004); INSR (HR 6.05, 95% CI 2.00-18.37, P = 0.0015); MAPK3 (HR 5.69, 95% CI 1.93-16.81, P = 0.0016); and ErbB2a (HR 5.50, 95% CI 1.86-16.29, P = 0.0021; Supplementary Figure S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105863).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that xentuzumab and abemaciclib could be combined and administered with either letrozole, anastrozole, or fulvestrant. AEs requiring dose reductions of xentuzumab were relatively common during dose escalation, but less frequent during dose expansion. Fewer than 20% of patients discontinued xentuzumab or abemaciclib due to AEs; in general, dose reduction rates of abemaciclib appeared to be higher than xentuzumab. The most common AEs associated with xentuzumab + abemaciclib were diarrhea, nausea, asthenia/fatigue, and decreased neutrophils/neutropenia.

Overall, several observations suggest that xentuzumab may make a relatively minor contribution to the overall toxicity of the combinations assessed. Firstly, in prior studies, xentuzumab monotherapy was comparatively well tolerated.22 Secondly, the AE profile in the xentuzumab and abemaciclib plus fulvestrant cohorts in this study was similar to that of abemaciclib + fulvestrant in the MONARCH-2 trial.31 Notably, only two patients reported mild (grade 1) xentuzumab-related hyperglycemia; probably reflecting the fact that xentuzumab spares the interaction of insulin with the IR-B receptor. Overall, the safety profile of xentuzumab + abemaciclib (± ET) was manageable, and AEs were in a similar range as published for abemaciclib (± ET) although a low incidence of cytopenias might be aggravated when xentuzumab and abemaciclib were given together.31

Encouraging ORRs and DCRs were observed in cohorts B-D with the triplet combinations. In cohort E, the ORR was just 3.8% in NSCLC patients; however, almost half of patients achieved DCR, with a median duration of 5.8 months. In cohort F, the DCR was 40.0%, with a clinically relevant duration of 12.4 months (range 9.2-14.0 months). Outcomes in cohorts D1 and D2 were promising, with median PFS of 13.6 months and 33.1 months, respectively. Of note, PFS in cohort D2 was considerably higher than achieved with xentuzumab, everolimus, and exemestane in the XENERA-1 trial (median PFS of 12.7 months) and abemaciclib + fulvestrant in the MONARCH 2 trial (median PFS of 16.4 months), although there are limitations in making cross-trial comparisons, especially when considering differences in patient profiles. In MONARCH 2, for example, 56% of patients had visceral disease whereas cohort D2 only allowed non-visceral disease. In XENERA-1, only one prior line of CDK 4/6 inhibitor was allowed.24,32 The relatively small sample sizes in the current study also need to be considered.

In this study, we also assessed the prognostic value of several serum biomarkers, including free and total IGF-1 concentrations. Consistent with a previous phase Ib/II study in patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer, we observed that xentuzumab decreased free serum IGF-1 concentrations, indicating target engagement (Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105863; Boehringer Ingelheim, data on file).23 Moreover, we observed that higher total IGF-1 concentrations, and to a lesser extent higher free IGF-1 concentrations, demonstrated evidence of prognostic value for PFS, which, again, is consistent with the previous study. Baseline dissociable IGF-2 concentrations were not prognostic for PFS benefit.

Owing to a lack of a control arm, we were unable to determine whether IGF-1 concentrations were also predictive of xentuzumab benefit in this study. Data from the previous phase Ib/II trial, however, suggested that this may be the case, with both free IGF-1 concentrations and dissociable IGF-2 concentrations showing a predictive trend towards improved PFS in patients treated with the xentuzumab combination versus the control arm (Boehringer Ingelheim, data on file). Unfortunately, these findings were not replicated in the XENERA-1 trial. Nevertheless, further investigations are warranted to assess the predictive value of circulating IGF-1 biomarkers, as they might identify patients who could benefit from therapeutics targeting the IGF-1R pathway. Indeed, the potential role of IGF-1 as a predictive biomarker has been observed in clinical studies of other IGF-1R-targeted therapies such as dalotuzumab33 and ganitumab.34 A recent study in breast cancer neoadjuvant therapy suggests that phosphorylation of IGF-1R predicts benefit from ganitumab.35 It is possible that high IGF-1 concentrations with assessment of tumoral receptor activation could serve as a composite predictive biomarker for drugs of this class.

We also undertook comprehensive genomic analyses with the view of identifying novel biomarkers. One biomarker of interest was high mRNA expression levels of CCND1, which demonstrated strong prognostic potential (HR 17.3) versus patients with low levels of CCND1 expression. High CCND1 expression levels also showed strong predictive value for PFS in the previous xentuzumab phase Ib/II trial (interaction P value = 0.0002) but not in XENERA-1 (Boehringer Ingelheim, data on file). The IGF-1 and cyclin D1 genes have been identified as hub genes that interact heavily with other genes in the pathogenesis of breast atypical hyperplasia.36 Accordingly, our study incorporated a comprehensive analysis of cancer-related genes to investigate whether any mutations had prognostic significance. Only MCL-1 (an apoptosis regulator in the BCL-2 family) demonstrated potential prognostic value. Patients with an MCL-1 mutation had shorter PFS than those without (P = 0.0182), hinting that defects in apoptosis regulation may impede the activity of xentuzumab. This prognostic potential, however, was not supported by similar analyses of either the previous phase Ib/II trial or XENERA-1 (Boehringer Ingelheim, data on file).23,24 Previous studies have demonstrated that overexpression of MCL-1 has been linked to high tumor grade and poor survival in breast cancer patients.37 Interestingly in our current study, patients with high expression of BCL-2 had longer PFS than those with low levels (HR 11.40, P = 0.0004; Supplementary Figure S6B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105863), further supporting a possible link between defective apoptosis (particularly in the BCL-2 family) and reduced activity of xentuzumab. It should be noted, however, that BCL-2 expression has been identified as a favorable prognostic factor in breast cancer because it is tightly associated with estrogen receptor expression.38

With respect to the limitations of this study, it was an uncontrolled, open-label study. Due to the multiplicity of factors examined together with the small sample size, conclusions on the prognostic data should be drawn with caution. Also, with no control arm, firmer conclusions of the efficacy of various combinations could not be reached. Furthermore, as the study was terminated prematurely due the efficacy failure of XENERA-1,24 planned enrollment was not achieved and cohort size was limited.

Conclusion

Xentuzumab can be combined with abemaciclib and ETs in patients with advanced or metastatic solid tumors and breast cancer. The combinations had manageable tolerability and demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity particularly in patients with breast cancer and non-visceral metastases. The clinical development of xentuzumab in oncology has been terminated, however, due to lack of efficacy benefit in the randomized phase II XENERA-1 breast cancer trial.24 Nevertheless, the current study identifies important differences in toxicity and, potentially, activity between a ligand neutralization approach versus anti-receptor strategies.
