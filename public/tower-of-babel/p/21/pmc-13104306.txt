Infective endocarditis in an adult male patient with tetralogy of Fallot physiology secondary to double outlet right ventricle presenting with stroke: a complex presentation with multiorgan dysfunction—a case report
Authors: Archisha Kalra, Shaik Nafeez Javed, Mounika Cherukuri, Ganesh V. Shetty, Chandrashekar Udyavara Kudru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfective endocarditis presents unique challenges, particularly in individuals with congenital heart diseases, such as this case, where the patient had tetralogy of Fallot physiology secondary to double outlet right ventricle, and Fanconi anemia. This case underscores the importance of early recognition, multidisciplinary collaboration, and tailored therapeutic strategies in managing complex congenital heart diseases with infective endocarditis and other comorbidities.

Background

A double outlet right ventricle (DORV) is a distinctive and complex cardiac defect, showcasing a wide range of anatomical variations in the heart. In a DORV, one major artery and more than half of the other arise from the morphological right ventricle, following the “50% rule” [1]. The anatomical variations associated with this defect are extremely broad, with various atrial configurations, atrioventricular connections, ventricular structures, and spatial positioning of the great arteries. As a result, DORV can mimic the hemodynamics of ventricular septal defect (VSD), tetralogy of Fallot (ToF), transposition of the great arteries (TGA), or even a functional single ventricle [2].

ToF is a cyanotic congenital heart disease characterized by a tetrad of four anomalies, including ventricular septal defect, pulmonary stenosis, overriding of the aorta, and right ventricular hypertrophy, with a high incidence of infant and early childhood mortality and middle age survival of less than 5% [3]. Patients with ToF are considered at a high risk of developing infective endocarditis, more so in the pulmonary valve owing to turbulent blood flow across the stenosed valve [4].

Our patient also had features of Fanconi anemia, which is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by various developmental anomalies and bone marrow failure, resulting in pancytopenia, which increases the individual’s susceptibility to infections [5]. Here we present an extremely rare case of a combination of a DORV with ToF physiology and Fanconi anemia, both of which jointly increase the chances of infective endocarditis, as in this patient who also had an embolic stroke as a complication of infective endocarditis [6–8].

Case presentation

Patient information

A 45-year-old South Asian male individual presented to the emergency department with symptoms of progressive fatigue, fever of 101°F, left upper limb weakness, loose stools for 3 weeks, and decreased responsiveness since 5 days. He had a congenital heart condition diagnosed as tetralogy of Fallot. Past surgical history included surgery for a brain abscess in 2018, which had led to scar epilepsy. On physical examination (Fig. 1,2), he exhibited splinter hemorrhages, a short neck, a hypoplastic/rudimentary thumb, and low-set ears. Systemic examination revealed a pan systolic murmur (PSM) at the left parasternal region, an ejection systolic murmur (ESM) with a thrill at the pulmonary area, basal bilateral crepitations on lung auscultation, and splenomegaly. Initial laboratory investigations showed low hemoglobin (9.5 g/dL), thrombocytopenia (platelet count of 90,000/mm3), elevated total leukocyte count (14,000/mm3) with left shift, and deranged renal function tests (serum creatinine 2.8 mg/dL, blood urea nitrogen 48 mg/dL).Fig. 1Splinter hemorrhages in nails, grade 2 clubbing of nails, and hypoplastic thumb (arrow)Fig. 2Low set ear, narrow ear canal, short neck

Diagnostic assessment

Electrocardiogram (Fig. 3) demonstrated poor R-wave progression with upright R-waves in precordial leads. Transthoracic echocardiography (Fig. 4) revealed severe pulmonary stenosis with a large subaortic ventricular septal defect measuring 20 mm in diameter, showing bidirectional shunting, and a vegetation measuring 21 mm × 14 mm attached to the pulmonary valve, indicative of infective endocarditis. Ultrasonography of the abdomen and pelvis showed “horseshoe kidney.” Chest X-ray anteroposterior view (Fig. 5) revealed the presence of a “boot-shaped heart,” which was suggestive of ToF. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain (Fig. 6) showed bilateral cortical to subcortical infarcts, suggestive of an embolic phenomenon. Clinical course

Despite initial antibiotic therapy, the patient developed worsening acute kidney injury, necessitating nephrology consultation and initiation of hemodialysis. Concurrently, he developed signs of systemic embolization with new-onset redness and swelling over the distal aspects of the limbs. Bone marrow biopsy was performed in view of pancytopenia as he also had findings suggestive of Fanconi anemia, and such patients are prone to develop myelodysplastic syndrome or other hemato-oncological abnormalities. However, the biopsy revealed no abnormalities, and the patient had a normal reactive marrow. Laboratory investigations revealed a significant coagulopathy with low fibrinogen levels (120 mg/dL), elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels (1000 U/L), and a thromboelastography (TEG) profile consistent with a severe hypocoagulable state suggestive of early disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Management included cryoprecipitate transfusion to correct coagulopathy and platelet transfusions due to persistent thrombocytopenia.

Complications and management challenges

Genetic consultation was sought owing to suspicion of an underlying congenital anomaly such as Fanconi anemia, given the patient’s complex medical history and multiple anomalies consistent with Fanconi anemia such as horseshoe kidneys, low-set ears, hypoplastic thumb, and radial ray defect. The cultures identified Streptococcus mitis as the causative organism. Antibiotics were changed to ceftriaxone and gentamicin on the basis of sensitivity testing. However, despite the situation, the patient’s clinical status was still not improving owing to continuation of septicemia and multiple organ failure.

Outcome

After a family meeting discussing the patient’s prognosis and goals of care, a palliative care initiative was launched. The resultant clinical course of the patient was one of sudden bradycardia, resulting in cardiac arrest and subsequent death.

Discussion

This case presents a unique and complicated mixture of ToF physiology, DORV, infective endocarditis, Fanconi anemia, and embolic stroke, thus reiterating a daunting challenge with respect to diagnosis and management of such multifaceted problems.

Role of congenital heart disease in infective endocarditis

Patients with congenital heart defects such as ToF, especially those that have not been corrected, are predisposed to complications such as infective endocarditis. The turbulent blood flow across the malformed structures of the heart, especially the pulmonary valve, creates an environment conducive to vegetation formation, such as in this case. The presence of infective endocarditis was confirmed according to the modified Duke’s criteria. Timely diagnosis and investigations thus played a vital role in shaping management for this patient [7].

Previous history of temporal abscess

To this was added the patient’s history of a temporal abscess. In patients with uncorrected congenital heart disease, right-to-left shunting bypasses the pulmonary filter system, allowing septic emboli to migrate directly into the systemic circulation, including the brain. The history of a brain abscess may be illustrative of an early sign of such patients’ susceptibility to infections related to their congenital heart disease. This places a premium on prompt recognition and aggressive management of septic embolization in this cohort, to prevent the recurrent complications of embolic stroke or brain abscesses.

Impact of Fanconi anemia

In addition to congenital heart disease, the patient was diagnosed with Fanconi anemia, a genetic disorder manifesting itself as bone marrow failure, immune dysfunction, and increased susceptibility to infections. These two factors further worsened the prognosis of the patient by complicating the management of infections, pancytopenia, and coagulopathies, including DIC. Pancytopenia may have complicated infection control with the treatment of further sequelae, therefore still maintaining the emphasis on multidisciplinary management [9].

Role of Streptococcus mitis and prophylaxis in infective endocarditis (IE)

Streptococcus mitis, the pathogen responsible for the IE in this case, is not only uncommon in causing it but also among the rare cases found in patients with congenital heart disease. Typically present as part of normal oral flora, Streptococcus mitis can cause an infection, especially after dental procedures or in patients with poor oral hygiene. This case underscores the immense importance of infection prevention strategies, such as antibiotic prophylaxis in patients with congenital heart defects, in reducing the risk of IE [10].

Challenges of right-sided infective endocarditis

Right-sided IE, as seen in this case, is less common than left-sided IE and often presents with delayed symptoms. This makes diagnosis rather challenging because this valve is quite rarely a site of vegetation. To note, this patient had an embolic stroke owing to these vegetations becoming lodged in the cerebral vasculature, which shows the grave complications that can arise when diagnosis of an IE is delayed. This case demonstrates the importance of expeditious imaging and formal initiation of empiric antibiotics to prevent further complications such as embolization.

Multiorgan dysfunction and the need for multidisciplinary care

Acute kidney injury (AKI), DIC, and refractory sepsis, which occurred in this patient, indicate the management complexity of multiorgan dysfunction in advanced infective endocarditis. The patient was clinically judged to be deteriorating, despite aggressive treatment with intensive antibiotics and hemodialysis, along with supportive measures. This reminds one of the integrated and multidisciplinary management approach for the collegiality between specialists from cardiology, nephrology, hematology, and genetics in such complex management aspects. Joint interventions in collaboration with each other in such circumstances to address the conflating factors affecting successful patient outcomes is indeed the essence [12, 13].

Importance of early surgical intervention

The uncorrected ToF of the patient significantly aggravated the clinical condition. Although surgical correction of ToF in adulthood is possible, there is a potential for more operative risks and lower survival rates. In this case, other complicating factors, including infective endocarditis and multiorgan dysfunction, further precluded the possibility of surgical intervention. In addition, the patient had become very ill with refractory sepsis, DIC, and AKI, thus making it impossible to consider surgical intervention, indicating the importance of early corrective surgery in congenital heart disease to prevent long-term complications [14–16].

Conclusion

This case exemplifies the challenges of managing infective endocarditis in a patient with uncorrected congenital heart disease and Fanconi anemia. The combination of these disorders brought about a worse prognosis for the patient, highlighting the importance of early diagnosis, timely intervention, and a multidisciplinary approach to patient care. The history of temporal abscess and genetic disease further complicated the management of the situation. More research is needed for effective treatments and clearer guidelines for such complicated cases.
