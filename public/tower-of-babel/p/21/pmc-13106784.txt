Integrating EfficientNetV2 with guided filopic diffusion for enhanced rice leaf disease recognition
Authors: V. Vinoth Kumar, P. Rajesh, N. Krishnamoorthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rice production is integral to the agricultural sector of India; over 65% of the populations are dependent on rice as their major staple. The cultivation of rice sustains this important agricultural sector; yet, there are many challenges encountered by rice producers, one of which is several types of disease that negatively impact yield and quality. Due to the fact that rice leaf smut, brown spot and bacterial leaf blights are among the most important types of diseases that can significantly reduce the yield and quality of rice, it is important to be diligent when identifying these diseases using accurate and speedy methods on an annual basis for successful and sustainable production of rice crops. As technology advances there continue to be emerging technologies such as Deep Learning (DL) as applied in agriculture to identify diseases and therefore reshape the agricultural paradigm so as to address agricultural disease challenges more readily. This research proposes a previously undemonstrated approach for identifying Rice Leaf Disease using EfficientNetV2; a Diffusion Bounded Attention method for disease detection. The quality of the input imagery has been greatly increased using a Preceding Noise Reduction (PNR) using the Guided Filopic Diffusion (GFD) technique, retaining important characteristics of Rice Leaves (Leaf Texture) which are critical for disease classification within agricultural imaging. To evaluate the performance of our model we utilized the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC). This coefficient measures how much the predicted image areas representing disease overlap with the actual affected areas of the image. Therefore, DSC is a reliable way to evaluate model segmentation capability. The Rice Leaf Diseases Dataset we used to identify and classify Rice Leaf Diseases was very comprehensive. Our model achieved an accuracy rate of 98.92% and also attained the best recall, precision and F1 score.

Introduction

Rice serves as a fundamental dietary staple for over half of the world’s population, playing a crucial role in global food security and economic stability. Its cultivation spans more than 100 countries, with Asia dominating the sector; contributing approximately 90% of global rice production. This dominance highlights the critical need for efficient disease management strategies, as rice diseases pose significant threats to both yield and quality. The economic ramifications are profound, especially in vulnerable regions where rice is a primary food source. Managing these diseases is imperative to prevent potential nutritional deficits and mitigate food insecurity, which can be exacerbated by crop failures. Automated plant disease identification systems based on DL are rapidly advancing in order to support crop health monitoring and increase the use of precision agriculture. Several new CNN architectures have been created which are able to accurately identify fungal diseases found within heterogeneous crop datasets (i.e. datasets generated using different types of crops), even when these datasets were generated under different environmental conditions1.

Common rice leaf diseases

Bacterial Leaf Blight is caused by the bacterium Xanthomonas oryzae. It is one of the most severe bacterial diseases affecting rice. It can lead to significant yield losses, often ranging between 25 and 35%2, particularly in regions with high humidity and frequent rainfall. The disease manifests as water-soaked lesions that expand into yellowish stripes, reducing the photosynthetic capacity of the leaves and ultimately stunting plant growth. If left unmanaged, Bacterial Blight can spread rapidly, causing widespread damage to entire rice fields.

Leaf smut or rice blast, attributed to the fungal pathogen Magnaporthe oryzae, is another devastating disease that can decimate rice crops. It affects all parts of the rice plant, including leaves, stems, and panicles. The disease’s hallmark is the formation of diamond-shaped lesions with gray centers and brown borders. In severe cases, it can cause “rotten neck”, leading to complete crop failure. The pathogen thrives in warm, humid conditions, making it particularly challenging to control in tropical and subtropical regions. Effective management requires a combination of resistant varieties, cultural practices, and timely fungicide applications3.

Brown Spot, caused by the fungus Cochliobolus miyabeanus, has historical significance due to its role in the 1943 Bengal Famine, which led to widespread hunger and mortality4. Today, it remains a persistent threat due to its resilience and ability to thrive in diverse environmental conditions. Brown Spot primarily affects rice leaves, causing small, brown lesions that can merge and lead to premature leaf death. This reduces the plant’s photosynthetic efficiency and weakens overall crop health. The disease is often exacerbated by nutrient deficiencies; particularly a lack of potassium and nitrogen, making balanced fertilization essential for its management5.

The morphological variability of these diseases within phases of growth and environmental conditions poses major challenges for automated recognition systems, demonstrating the need for strong, generalizable DL models. Nevertheless, rice farming is often threatened by several foliar diseases including but not limited to bacterial leaf blight, brown spot, and leaf smut. These diseases, if they occur in the field and are undetected and uncontrolled may require severe reductions in yield. Traditional detection of these diseases and others depends heavily on visual monitoring in the field and expert identification in the lab. These methods are subjective, time-consuming, and prone to human error, especially if the field of agriculture is large. Therefore, there is increased interest in detecting plant disease using computer vision and DL in automated fashion as early, reliable, and scalable tools for precision agriculture. It has the ability to balance computational efficiency and performance, making it appropriate for application in agricultural monitoring systems with limited resources.

Design rationale and foundation of GFD and EfficientNetv2

Diffusion models have made strides regarding generating accurate representations of evolving, time-dependent image shapes, and allow users to restore images or enhance their geometric attributes, making it possible to achieve image level enhancement across many of the same domains that traditional methods utilize. Earlier research into diffusion models has determined that iterative diffusion processes can refine images by eliminating noise and by accentuating important image features26,27. Later works in this area have further explained that diffusion processes operate as an effective tool for overcoming limitations imposed by severe degradation while supporting the generation of high-resolution images, semantic image generation and image restoration, thereby validating using diffusion processes as an effective model for enhancing images through structure-aware models28.

GFD was inspired by the same ideas as Diffusion Based Refinement (DBR). However, the way we conceptualize GFD is different from how we conceptualize DBR and the goal of GFD is also different from the goal of DBR. DBR uses a probabilistic or generative diffusion approach, GFD is a non-probabilistic, non-generative diffusion method that has been developed for use as a tool for enhancing images of crops that have been infected by disease29. GFD uses a process that is similar to the image noise reduction process we see with generative diffusion methods to add features that are important for image interpretation of the lesion patterns that typically develop on rice plant leaves due to infections. GFD is designed specifically to help the practice of agriculture by providing an improved means of enhancing, preserving, and improving the accuracy of the final product, i.e., an enhanced image that represents the disease on the rice plant. Moreover, GFD provides a means of filtering out the effects of background noise, the effects of changes in light intensity that may be observed on the ground or through the canopy of rice plants, and provides enhanced separation of disease patterns from healthy rice crops before making decisions regarding classification of the data30. Thus, we see GFD as providing a means of constructing a light-weight version of the principles of diffusion that is adapted specifically for identifying and classifying agricultural diseases rather than generating images.

Simultaneously, in a complementary enhancement strategy, GFD was combined and utilized to emphasize fine-grained spatial and textural details in leaf images. GFD does not rely on traditional diffusion or attention-based image smoothing, but is based on Filopic guidance an approach to diffusion that emulates biology and preserves edge continuity while highlighting subtle features related to disease such as lesion boundaries, gradients in coloration, and spot morphology. Overall, guided diffusion aids in reducing noise and enhancing features for discrimination in the subsequent convolutional layers of the model. This led to improved sensitivity of the overall model for early stage disease symptoms that are not usually easily observable in the leaf images.

Recent developments in convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures have changed the way we classify diseases based on images by allowing networks to independently classify and extract hierarchical representations of varying levels of complexity from image input. However, in spite of their performance, conventional CNN architectures include challenges of computational expense, reduced generalizability across variable illumination and spatial backgrounds, and a level of redundancy of features all of which may affect the models robustness in practice. EfficientNetV2 provides a high-performance network based on a compound scaling approach that jointly scales the network depth, width and resolution, while improving performance through proved to be effective fused MBConv layers and progressive learning to facilitate improved accuracy and efficiency when training models.

We believe that this architecture will help to create an accurate, timely method for detecting leaf diseases in rice plants. In contrast to previous CNN methods that directly take advantage of the image data to characterize the disease, GFD is used to improve the resolution of the lesions by enhancing and overlaying the edge of the lesions on top of the image while reducing or filtering out background noise or artifacts in the original image. In addition to enhancing the ability of the model to more accurately locate the lesions, the parameter-efficient nature of EfficientNetV2, combined with a progressive learning methodology, further supports the ability of the proposed architecture to accurately separate visually similar types of rice leaf diseases like leaf smut, brown spots and blight caused by bacteria through the power of the GFD mechanism.

Motivation and societal importance

The basis for this research is driven by the pressing need for a system that automatically and affordably detects rice leaf diseases in their early stages, prior to visible symptoms being sufficiently severe. Furthermore, accurately and in a timely manner, identifying which disease is affecting a crop will allow growers to target chemical applications ultimately saving money and minimizing chemical overuse. These applications will also reduce the negative impacts of crop protection products on the environment in line with multiple United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 2: Zero Hunger and SDG 12: Responsible Consumption and Production).

Related work

Given the severity and economic impact of the diseases explained in the introduction part, early detection and efficient management are crucial. Advanced technologies, such as DL and precision agriculture, offer promising solutions. By leveraging ML models and high-resolution imaging, farmers can identify disease symptoms at early stages, enabling timely interventions. This approach not only improves crop yields but also enhances the quality of rice, contributing to better food security6. Moreover, sustainable management practices, including the use of resistant rice varieties, balanced fertilization, and integrated pest management, are essential for long-term disease control. Collaborative efforts between researchers, agricultural organizations, and governments can further strengthen disease management frameworks, ensuring the resilience and productivity of rice cultivation globally. Segmentation-based techniques have been developed to allow the user to better localize and predict where disease may occur. This allows a more accurate prediction of severity and also allows the user to extract more features before classifying crops7.

EfficientNetV2, a successor to EfficientNet, is renowned for its progressive learning mechanism, which enhances performance on visual tasks like plant disease detection. It adjusts regularization based on image size, allowing efficient scaling and faster training with fewer parameters than traditional CNNs like ResNet or InceptionV3. This model excels in tasks involving large, imbalanced datasets and variable image resolutions8. Its design allows it to excel in handling large, imbalanced datasets and varying image resolutions, making it particularly effective in agricultural applications where data diversity and imbalance are common challenges9.

EfficientNetV2 has demonstrated exceptional accuracy across multiple plant disease datasets, largely due to its faster convergence and better generalization capabilities compared to standard CNNs and Vision Transformers (ViT).Its performance may also degrade when applied to datasets with limited variability in background or disease symptoms.It often leverages transfer learning with pre-trained ImageNet weights, combined with progressive scaling and adaptive regularization strategies. This approach has proven effective in distinguishing visually similar diseases, particularly in crops like cassava and tomatoes. Studies report classification accuracies exceeding 97% for detecting various plant diseases, with results on datasets like PlantVillage reaching 97.5% and smaller, specialized datasets achieving up to 99.5% accuracy. These results highlight the model’s suitability for early disease detection tasks10. A study11 compared EfficientNetV2 against models like DenseNet and ResNet, achieving an accuracy of 98.3% on a custom rice leaf dataset. Pros include its adaptability to high-resolution images and reduced training time. However, it requires substantial computational power and fine-tuning for optimal results. This aims to incorporate a noise reducing mechanism before the input is fed in to the model.

Integrating EfficientNetV2 with attention mechanisms or hybrid CNN-RNN frameworks has shown to enhance detection robustness, particularly for rice blast disease, where accuracy reached 97.9%. These hybrid approaches improve feature extraction and disease classification but increase overall model complexity and inference time, which can be a limitation for real-time applications12. EfficientNetV2’s performance is highly dependent on dataset quality and diversity. While public datasets like PlantVillage and Kaggle provide useful benchmarks, some studies report reduced accuracy when models are tested on datasets with insufficient variability in disease symptoms or background conditions. This underscores the necessity for diverse and high-quality training data to ensure model generalization. Comprehensive studies that have been conducted on the application of artificial intelligence within the context of agricultural production demonstrate how deep learning methods can enable the integration of disease monitoring and crop management across all phases of crop production (i.e., pre-harvesting through harvesting through post-harvesting)13.

Some of the alternate approaches have also been utilized to detect plant leaf disease. Using SVMs, k-NNs, Random Forests, ResNets, DenseNets, MobileNets, and EfficientNets can help develop predictive models but require a certain amount of engineering and can suffer from scalability issues. Some available datasets are small, and SVM performs well with small datasets, while the competing methods tend to perform poorly with large datasets. Both k-NN and Random Forests are nonparametric and require significant engineering. ResNets and other variants allow for residuals, or deep networks, allowing for a larger number of layers. DenseNet and MobileNet are primarily based on feature reuse and mobile devices, respectively, while EfficientNet falls between them in accuracy and efficiency. Other limitations to models include increased memory usage, computational costs, and high noise sensitivity. All require a certain level of human intervention during model training, which limits their automated learning capabilities regarding complex patterns of disease development.

Recent studies demonstrate that EfficientNetV2 outperforms traditional models like ResNet and Inception-V3 due to its progressive learning strategy and parameter efficiency, achieving accuracy rates between 94 and 99%. However, these models still face limitations, such as sensitivity to noisy data, difficulties in distinguishing subtle disease symptoms, and reliance on extensive annotated datasets. Introducing GFD can address these challenges by enhancing feature extraction and interpretability. This diffusion technique improves the model’s ability to focus on fine-grained details, which is crucial for early-stage detection of rice diseases where symptoms may be subtle or visually similar. By integrating this method, the system can more effectively differentiate between closely related diseases and reduce misclassifications due to background noise.

Research challenges

Despite significant advances in DL, automated rice leaf disease detection has not been free of challenges. Intra-class variability of symptoms, differing by rice varieties and growth stages, inter-class similarities in diseases such as brown spot disease or bacterial leaf blight, and further noise introduced by field environmental factors including changes in illumination or occlusions, have led to model misclassification. The limited number of annotated datasets, especially for early infections, limits generalizability of models. To further confound this task, DL models are inefficient with respect to computational resources and in some cases, not computationally feasible for on-farm, real-time applications. Finally, the CNN often misses the finer textural or morphological clues that are critically important for identifying diseases as early as possible. Overall challenges highlight the need for an accurate, lightweight, and robust model, capable of reliable performance in highly variable and uncontrolled field scenarios.

Proposed methodology

Understanding diseases like leaf smut, brown spot, and bacterial leaf blight is the key factor for managing rice crop health. The proposed model improves the speed and accuracy of disease identification and aims to provide efficient and accurate identification of the three major rice leaf diseases. The model architecture is built with EfficientNetV2 as basic feature extraction backbone and a GFD module that enhances fine-scale textures and boundary representations of the diseased area. GFD is a task-specific diffusion framework developed in this paper to adapt the standard denoising diffusion to include a filopic structural prior and guidance loss in place of a new generative diffusion model. This combination allows the network to learn both high-level, semantic feature representations and low-level texture patterns of the lesions to discriminate between diseases that are visually similar. The architecture of the proposed system is illustrated in Fig. 2, providing details on the approach including: input image acquisition and preprocessing, feature extraction and diffusion refinement; and final classifications.Fig. 2Proposed architecture.

Proposed architecture.

EfficientNetV2 used in this approach analyzes rice leaf images through a sequence of convolutional and diffusion-guided layers to extract fine-grained features that are specific to disease features. Input images are resized (224 × 224), normalized, and denoised to maintain uniform illumination in the beginning of the process. The first convolutional (3 × 3 kernel and 32 filters) extracts fundamental spatial and vein features of the rice leaves. During the shallow extraction stage, convolutional layers extract features for discerning an early-stage diagnosis of the lesions and laying the foundation of texture. The deeper skip-connected convolutional layers in the model extract Multiscale contextual information into the learned representations, allowing unique forms of disease propagation to be detected in the multiscaled disease features. The deeper convolutional layers specialize in detecting complex spatial patterns, including spot distributions, streak formations, and lesion boundaries, which are crucial for differentiating between Leaf Smut, Brown Spot, and Bacterial Leaf Blight. In the final stage, a global average pooling layer compresses the extracted feature maps into a compact representation, preserving essential information while reducing computational complexity.

This condensed feature representation is then processed by a fully connected layer, which maps it to one of the three disease classes. Finally, a softmax activation function converts raw output scores into probability values, ensuring that the sum of probabilities equals 1, with the highest probability determining the predicted disease category. The final output includes the identified disease type, a confidence score (98%), feature maps highlighting the key disease regions. The GFD block enhances the detect ability of disease boundaries while reducing noise and noise artifacts.

Methods and materials

This section comprises the details of methods and materials used in proposed approach.

Dataset

This research employs the Rice Leaf Diseases Dataset14, which is composed of three predominant rice diseases.The dataset has a total of 4684 labeled images, in which 1,460 Leaf Smut, 1604 Bacterial Blight, and 1620 Brown Spot. All images are high resolution and were taken under different illumination and environmental conditions, along with sufficient variation in the images of each class to ensure diversity and robustness in training a model. This dataset is useful for a variety of DL purposes such as disease detection and image classification with environmental-based disease prediction in precision agriculture.

Guided filopic diffusion

GFD was chosen over traditional diffusion and noise reduction techniques, like Gaussian and Median Filtering, Anisotropic diffusion because GFD uses edge-aware and guidance-based diffusion processes that preserved tiny lesion boundaries while suppressing the unnecessary noise of the surrounding background. GFD cleans up an image gently while preserving key disease patterns. It reduces background noise but does not diffuse onto the edges of leaves, preserving clearly defined shapes and boundaries of disease spots. This enables the model to easily detect early and subtle disease symptoms, allowing for accurate classification.

Conventional smoothing processes generally blur the subtle diseases present. On the contrary, GFD maintains the structural continuity of lesions making low contrast or early stage symptoms more clearly identifiable. This is especially important for the visually similar diseases such as Leaf Smut, Brown Spot, and Bacterial Leaf Blight. GFD further improves the discriminality of EfficientNetV2 extracted features by reducing interference from the background noise, resulting in an increase in accuracy across all three diseases. GFD is thus the most effective process for detecting rice leaf diseases earlier and more robustly to date as it combines the advantages of region-specific enhancement with finer detail retention and compatibility with multi-scale feature extraction.

In GFD, the process involves gradually adding Gaussian noise to images and then learning to reverse this process to reconstruct a structured image. The “guided” aspect enhances this mechanism by introducing constraints or focusing the diffusion process on specific patterns. This approach allows the model to refine intricate details more effectively. For image classification tasks, especially those involving subtle visual differences, GFD provides enhanced feature extraction by ensuring that only the most relevant information is highlighted during each denoising step. This results in cleaner and more accurate feature maps, crucial for distinguishing between closely related classes such as different types of plant diseases15. Technically, GFD operates through a two-stage process as shown in Fig. 3: a forward diffusion that systematically corrupts the image with noise and a reverse process that reconstructs the image using a neural network. During reconstruction, the guided mechanism emphasizes critical features, making it particularly adept at handling noisy or low-quality inputs.Fig. 3Two stage diffusion process of GFD.

Two stage diffusion process of GFD.

This structured, iterative process offers robustness and precision, which traditional CNNs or simpler augmentation methods may lack when dealing with complex or noisy datasets. This capability is especially valuable in tasks like disease detection, where early symptoms may manifest as subtle changes in leaf texture or color. The method’s inherent robustness to noise and variability ensures consistent performance even in real-world conditions, where images often include background distractions or poor lighting. Another key advantage of GFD is its improved generalization. Because it reconstructs images through progressive denoising, the model creates a more comprehensive representation of the data distribution. This helps prevent overfitting to specific features, enhancing the model’s accuracy across diverse datasets. This characteristic is crucial for agricultural applications, where data variability is high and consistent performance is essential. EfficientNetV2

At its core, EfficientNetV2 it is designed using Neural Architecture Search (NAS), which automates the process of finding the most efficient network structure. This process fine-tunes the architecture across three critical dimensions: network depth (number of layers), width (number of filters per layer), and resolution (image size), ensuring balanced performance and resource utilization.

Unlike standard CNN architectures that rely on stacked convolutions to perform hierarchical feature extraction, EfficientNetV2 augments the feature representation process by providing a more efficient diffusion-guided refinement process that places specific emphasis on edge continuity, lesion boundaries, and minor dimorphic texture nuances evolving from high-resolution digital captures that are germane to plant disease classification. EfficientNetV2 introduces Diffusion Bound Convolutional Framework with GFD layers that are aimed at decreasing noise and increasing texture preservation that is not dependent on Mobile Inverted Bottleneck Convolutions (MBConv) and Fused MBConvs that are standard to existing architectures like EfficientNetV2. Architecture of EfficientNetV2 is shown in Fig. 5. Stage 0: Input phase and initial convolution phase

A 224 × 224 rice leaf image will first be prepped using normalization and then passed through a 3 × 3 convolutional layer, batch normalized and ReLU activated. This stage of exploration will be critical for extracting basic spatial and edge-level features against the background of a healthy sectional features like leaf texture, color tone, boundary outlines and also extract basic features needed to separate healthy from diseased regions and outputs when correctly identified. But it often relies on larger initial kernels (e.g., 7 × 7) risking, to a higher level than earlier flagging mechanisms, blurring fine disease textures by loosing distinguish features as veins and others mentioned above. It also applies a smaller 3 × 3 kernel to call out leaf edges and specific lesion and veins are preserved from the start of the pipeline.

Stage 1: Feature extraction phase

In this stage, to capture textural variation at the surface level, we apply two 3 × 3 convolutional blocks with ReLU activations to extract texture variations including color and shapes of early stage lesions. Next, we apply a 3 × 3 max-pooling layer, which helps reduce spatial information while retaining important textures.EfficientNetV2’s approach uses a 3 × 3 uniform convolution strategy that helps maintain high-resolution shallow contextualized features relevant for early detection of disease.

Stage 2: Diffusion feature encoding phase

In this stage, we incorporate GFD when encoding disease related regions to smooth edges sharper while suppressing background noise. The diffusion encoding process is able to maintain important venation features and lesion contrast while distinguishing infected from undeveloped tissue. Traditional CNN based DL methods do not have adaptive noise suppression and variable lighting background make performance very difficult, EfficientNetV2 with its fused-MBConv blocks that tend to emphasize efficiency over clarity.

Stage 3: Feature aggregation phase

EfficientNetV2 combines shallow and deep feature representations by utilizing multi-scale CNN layers, with skip connections. To promote model robustness and deter overfitting when data samples are limited, dropout regularization (p = 0.3) is implemented. State of the art (SoTA) networks, such as DenseNet typically over fit small datasets as they aggregate redundant parameters in deeper layers. EfficientNetV2 skip connections and controlled dropout reduce this redundancy, ensuring stability in feature re-use and convergence on limited data.

Stage 4: Adaptive refinement phase

The hybrid stage combines the features enhanced by diffusion with the deep aggregated maps using a Guided Dense Fusion layer, with the goal of maximizing spatial precision together with semantic abstraction. The fusion applies adaptive weighting of the features to achieve fine-grained disease localization and context-unaware reasoning. SoTA architectures traditionally feature a purely hierarchical usage of features, which diminishes bidirectional interaction of information between shallow and deep layers.

Stage 5: Classification phase

The condensed features are flattened and passed through a dense layer with 128 neurons using a ReLU activation and lastly through a Softmax layer that classifies the input images. The final output gives the probability distribution of each class and highlights the most likely disease class.

Experiment environment

The experimental setup for the models in this study is outlined as follows, with all algorithms tested under identical conditions. The Adam optimizer was chosen as it has an adaptive learning rate and is very fast to converge; therefore, a learning rate of 0.0001 was used to achieve stable gradient updates, resulting in smooth convergence and avoiding oscillations in the loss function during training. Using a batch size of 16 offered an ideal trade-off between convergence stability and the amount of memory used by the GPU while providing consistent improvements in accuracy across eras. Training was completed over 100 epochs; after this period performance metrics, including Dice Similarity Coefficient and accuracy, levelled off which is an indication that convergence occurred without overfitting. The cross-entropy loss decreased smoothly throughout training and had stabilised by the end of training, indicating that the model was optimised well. In addition, using four threads concurrently to load data helped improve the efficiency of loading data into the GPU, which reduced the overall time required for training. Results and notable observations

EfficientNetV2 stands out among CNN models due to its superior efficiency and performance, particularly when applied to complex tasks such as detecting rice leaf diseases. It achieves remarkable accuracy with fewer parameters and lower computational costs compared to traditional models. For instance, it is up to 6.8 times smaller than ResNet-152 while delivering comparable or superior performance. This efficiency translates to faster inference and reduced resource requirements, crucial for deploying models in agricultural settings where computational resources may be limited. The combination of fused operations and progressive learning significantly reduces training time. The advanced regularization and data augmentation strategies such as Mixup and CutMi help it handle imbalanced datasets effectively. This capability is particularly important in plant disease detection, where certain diseases may be underrepresented. Its integration with techniques like GFD further enhances its precision by refining feature extraction and lesion localization.

The evaluation of detection performance in this study is conducted using precision; accuracy, recall, and the F1 score16. The classification outcomes are defined as follows: True Positive (TP) denotes instances correctly classified as belonging to the positive class, while True Negative (TN) represents instances correctly identified as belonging to the negative class. False Positive (FP) refers to misclassified instances where the model incorrectly predicts a negative sample as positive, whereas False Negative (FN) pertains to cases where a positive sample is incorrectly classified as negative. Precision quantifies the fraction of correctly classified positive instances out of all instances predicted as positive, serving as an indicator of predictive reliability in positive class identification17. Accuracy (Eq. 17) represents the overall proportion of correctly classified instances across all classes, providing a holistic measure of model correctness. Recall18, also known as sensitivity or true positive rate, assesses the proportion of actual positive instances successfully identified by the model. To achieve a balanced assessment of classification performance, the F1 score is utilized, which is the harmonic mean of precision and recall.17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Accuracy = \frac{TP + TN}{{TP + TN + FP + FN}}$$\end{document}

Discussion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of various DL models for multi-class rice disease classification, with EfficientNetV2 integrated with GFD proving to be the most accurate and efficient approach. The proposed model outperformed state-of-the-art architectures such as DenseNet, ResNet34, and MobileNet variants, achieving the highest accuracy (98.2%) and F1 score (97.9%). While the proposed method offers improved feature localization and classification, few external factors may influence the data quality and cause discrepancies in the accuracy scores of results over time when assessing how well it performs. Furthermore, the use of diffusion preprocessing has added some additional processing time to an already time-consuming task; as such, future research activities regarding real-world deployment of models will benefit from optimizations in both data set collection methodologies and real-world application of newer technology.

The performance improvements found in this research are because of the cooperative relationship of GFD with the EfficientNetV2 feature extraction architecture that is used to create feature representations from images of rice leaf diseases. GFD enhances disease-specific visual cues by preferentially diffusing information along lesion aligned structures, while simultaneously filtering out background noise and other non-relevant textures. Most of the visible symptoms of these diseases present as thin, fragmented, or low-contrast lesions and therefore are often challenging to be seen in raw images. By enhancing the continuity of lesions and enhancing the local contrast, GFD creates more structurally coherent representations which lend themselves to improved downstream feature learning.EfficientNetV2 leverages this enhancement by analysing inputs with enhanced signal-to-noise properties. It uses two forms of convolution (Fused-MBConv & MBConv) to extract multi-scale features effectively and the synergy of GFD enhanced input with EfficientNetV2 leads to the learning of more discriminable and disease specific features while being less sensitive to background clutter and illumination variations. This synergy is demonstrated through consistently improved accuracy, F1 Score, and DSC measures, especially in reducing confusion between visually similar disease categories.

Conclusion and future enhancements

The impact that plant diseases have on agriculture exceeds a loss of numerous yields due to being a difficult task in maintaining a healthy and sustainable environment. This work reports on the development of an automated way to detect and classify the top three Major Rice Plant Leaf Diseases; Bacterial Leaf Blight, Brown Spot, and Leaf Smut through the utilization of a validated database of images that contain rice leaves infected with diseases, while emphasizing the importance of using a validated database. By providing the necessary tools and materials needed for the creation of this method, we demonstrated how GFD was used as part of a two-step process which includes a pre-processing stage focused on highlighting the infected areas and suppressing background noise followed by a segmentation stage where we utilized data from multiple scales. Validation of the efficacy of the GFD was done using the Dice Similarity Coefficient to provide quantitative support of our method’s accurate localization of disease infection. We then used EfficientNetV2 as our feature extraction and classification model to enable us to perform efficient multi-scale learning of the pattern of the disease.

Additionally, through a variety of experimental studies, we concluded that our approach provided superior results when analyzing diseased plants compared to traditional CNN architectures such as ResNet34, DenseNet, and MobileNet. The proposed approach surpasses conventional DL models, achieving a remarkable accuracy of 98.2% and an F1 score of 97.9%. The success of the proposed approach is attributed to its optimized compound scaling strategy, which effectively balances depth, width, and resolution, enhancing feature extraction efficiency while reducing computational overhead. An in-depth error analysis indicates that variations in model performance across different disease classes stem primarily from class imbalance and intra-class variability in symptom presentation.

The model at present solely identifies a single disease per image; therefore, it does not identify instances of multiple diseases appearing on a single leaf. Future studies will utilize larger, more diverse, and optimized datasets to expedite the diffusion process for quicker processing along with identifying how to develop a multi-disease detection capability, along with real-time field deployment. While the performance of the proposed framework on the Kaggle rice leaf disease dataset has been very strong, the experiments conducted for validation purposes only used this dataset as there is no other publicly available dataset for validation. Therefore, the differences between this dataset and the real world (e.g., differences in lighting, background clutter, camera type, and environmental conditions) may impact how well the framework generalizes to other agricultural environments. However, there is still a need for additional validation across multiple datasets and with real-world images before we can conclude that we can deploy the framework successfully. Additionally, improvements regarding the ability to adapt to new domains and perform field-level validation will be addressed in future research. Nevertheless, the proposed model exhibits strong feature learning capabilities, accurately distinguishing disease patterns while maintaining a minimal error rate of 1.8%.
