Adoption of the Reference Framework for Preventive Care for Older Adults: a study of primary care physicians
Authors: Claire Chenwen Zhong, Mingtao Chen, Zehuan Yang, Zhaojun Li, Chung Yi Lo, William CW Wong, Junjie Huang, Martin CS Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Reference Framework (RF) for Preventive Care for Older Adults in Primary Care provides evidence-based guidelines to enhance preventive care in Hong Kong. However, its adoption and feasibility among primary care physicians (PCPs) remain unclear. This study evaluates PCPs’ awareness, adoption, and perceived barriers and enablers to RF implementation.DesignA cross-sectional survey conducted among PCPs in Hong Kong. Sociodemographics, adoption of RF and perceptions of implementation barriers and enablers were collected. Logistic regression was used to identify key factors influencing RF applicability, acceptability and feasibility.SettingBoth private and public primary care institutions in Hong Kong.ParticipantA total of 485 PCPs who provide preventive care to older adults in primary care settings across both the private and public sectors in Hong Kong participated.ResultsAmong 485 physicians surveyed, most considered the RF applicable (71.3%), acceptable (78.1%) and feasible (65.6%), with significantly higher acceptability and feasibility reported by private physicians. Adoption of RF recommendations varied widely (17.5%–92.8%), with highest uptake for influenza vaccination and lowest for urinary incontinence screening. Private physicians more often viewed frequent RF updates as facilitators and market competition as a barrier, while public physicians more frequently reported incompatibility with clinical settings. Physicians seeing 30–59 patients per day were more likely to find the RF applicable (adjusted OR 2.123, 95% CI 1.201 to 3.753, p=0.010) compared with those who provided consultation for fewer than 30 patients per day.ConclusionWhile most PCPs perceived the RF as applicable, acceptable and feasible, significant variation in adoption across recommendations and between practice settings highlights implementation challenges. Key enablers included high-quality evidence and local clinical information, while barriers like time constraints and patient adherence highlight the need for regular updates, ongoing education and tailored strategies for wider RF adoption.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Previous research has underscored the essential role of primary care physicians (PCPs) in disease prevention among older adults and highlighted the significance of implementing clinical guidelines to alleviate the burden of disease. However, various factors in the clinical setting either facilitate or hinder the adoption of clinical guidelines by PCPs in their daily practice, which is crucial for delivering effective care. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have yet offered a comprehensive understanding of the adoption level as well as the facilitators and barriers to the implementation of the Reference Framework (RF) for Preventive Care for Older Adults in primary settings in Hong Kong.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study included 485 PCPs in Hong Kong, with an even split between public and private sector. Overall, the majority of PCPs considered the RF to be applicable, acceptable and feasible to adopt in daily practice. In addition, this study points to a tendency for physicians to adopt RF, for example, uptake of influenza vaccination, smoking cessation advice and hypertension screening were most frequently adopted, while urinary incontinence screening, hearing impairment screening and promotion of oral hygiene, which may be attributed to perceptions of disease priorities among PCPs. Additionally, we found that PCPs in the private setting are more frequently adopting the RF recommendations compared with PCPs in the public setting, possibly due to different resource availability in the clinical settings. The result indicated physicians with moderate practice sizes were more likely to find the RF applicable, while those in the public sector were less likely to perceive it as acceptable or feasible.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

First, this study points to a behavioural bias in the adoption of RF by PCPs in clinical decision-making, and may be attributable to inconsistencies in PCPs’ perception of disease priority and risk. This finding emphasises the awareness and education efforts should be focused on both healthcare providers and the public, particularly for conditions that may not be widely recognised as high priority. Second, given that we found workload may have hindered PCPs from applying RF, our findings suggest ways to better facilitate resource sharing by expanding public-private partnerships in primary care settings. Third, it is crucial to develop and test implementation strategies that facilitate the adoption of RF recommendations, and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research and the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change to understand the specific barriers and enablers in different healthcare settings.

Introduction

Population ageing is a global phenomenon with far-reaching implications for healthcare systems, driven by the rising prevalence of chronic conditions and multimorbidity among older adults.1 In Hong Kong, it is projected that by 2066, the elderly population (aged 65 years and above) will exceed 2.6 million, making up 37% of the total population.2 This demographic shift presents not only challenges for social services but also for healthcare systems, as age-related diseases, such as disability, impose a substantial burden on patients, healthcare professionals, caregivers and the system.3 A systematic review indicates that around 76.5% of the elderly population in China experiences comorbidities,4 which complicates disease management and increases the likelihood of medication-related problems, adverse events and more frequent healthcare visits.5 These co-existing health conditions elevate the complexity of care required by elderly patients and contribute to a higher burden on the healthcare system, particularly for primary care physicians (PCPs).1

PCPs play a key role as the first point of contact in the healthcare system, which is responsible for providing comprehensive care plans to prevent disease onset or progression.6 Disease prevention, particularly lifestyle interventions, can effectively delay the onset of frailty and improve health outcomes for the elderly.7 Nevertheless, the care provided to older adults extends beyond medical concerns; it must also emphasise enduring relationships and the overall well-being of both patients and their families.8 9 Evidence indicates that sustained physician-patient relationships and patient-centred service are essential to enhancing quality of life and well-being among older adults.10

To address potential public health crises and the adverse health risk among older adults in Hong Kong, such as lifestyles,11 adverse mental health,12 cognitive impairment,13 falls14 and visual impairment,15 the Hong Kong Primary Healthcare Office has developed a Reference Framework (RF) to guide preventive care for older adults in primary care settings.16 The RF aims to address the multifaceted health risks faced by older adults through a comprehensive, evidence-based approach. It covers six modules: (1) health assessment, (2) falls prevention, (3) dental healthcare, (4) visual impairment, (5) mental health and (6) cognitive impairment. The RF adopts a life-course approach, supported by population-based preventive programmes, and provides evidence-based recommendations for elderly care across different developmental stages.16

Numerous studies have demonstrated the benefits of guideline-directed preventive intervention on clinical outcomes, such as better prognosis, disease management and continuity of care.17 18 It is anticipated that the adoption of RF will provide more effective patient health management through lifestyle changes to prevent major health risks in older adults.16 However, failure to adopt the RF could exacerbate existing burdens, leading to increased workload for physicians and poorer outcomes for patients.19

The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) serves as a crucial framework for exploring the implementation of RFs.20 Numerous studies based on the CFIR demonstrate that PCPs’ decisions concerning the adoption of RFs are complex and multifaceted.21 The attributes of RFs, factors related to the healthcare system, individual perceptions and patient engagement have been recognised as pivotal determinants.21 22 However, despite the potential benefits of RF, no studies have comprehensively evaluated its adoption or the barriers and facilitators to its implementation in Hong Kong’s primary care settings. This study aims to (1) assess the level of adoption and awareness of the RF among PCPs in different practice settings and (2) explore the factors that enable or hinder its adoption.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a cross-sectional study to investigate the adoption level and the factors influencing the adoption of the RF for preventive care in primary care settings among PCPs in Hong Kong. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.23 As of recent estimates, there are approximately 15 298 medical doctors practising in Hong Kong, with 46% in the public sector and 54% in the private sector.24 Of these, an estimated 6000 PCPs were eligible to participate in this study. We included all PCPs providing preventive care to older adults in primary care settings across both sectors. As the RF had not been systematically evaluated before, we assumed a 50% adoption rate (p=0.50). To achieve a precision level of 0.05, a minimum of 385 valid responses were required based on the formula: [N=1.962 × p(1-p)/precision2].25

Sampling frame and recruitment

The survey was conducted using self-administered questionnaires distributed to physicians in both the private and public sectors. The research team compiled a database of over 5500 PCPs from both public and private settings by integrating registered physician lists from the Hong Kong Medical Association, the Hong Kong Doctors Union, university-affiliated physicians and the Hong Kong College of Family Physicians. Invitations were sent to 2502 PCPs (505 in public settings and 1997 in private settings) on the list via email, with a link to the online survey. To enhance response rates, regular reminders were issued to non-respondents. Ultimately, 485 responses were collected, yielding a response rate of 19.4%. Each physician who completed the survey received a coupon worth HK$100 and one Continuing Medical Education (CME) credit. The survey was designed to ensure anonymity, and all data were anonymised to protect participants’ privacy.

Survey instruments

Based on findings from indepth interviews in our previous study and literature search, a questionnaire was developed and tailored to the local context of primary healthcare in Hong Kong. The questionnaire underwent face validity by an expert panel comprising family medicine specialists, epidemiologists and researchers in implementation science. A pilot test was conducted with 20 physicians to evaluate the instrument’s validity and clarity. As no issues were identified during the pilot phase, no changes were made to the instrument. The survey instrument assessed the adoption level of each component of the RF, physicians’ perceptions regarding its implementation outcomes (including applicability, acceptability and feasibility) and potential enablers and barriers to its adoption (online supplemental table S1).

Perceptions regarding the RF’s applicability, acceptability and feasibility were measured using a four-point item, respectively. Physicians were asked whether they agreed that the RF was applicable, acceptable and feasible for use in clinical practice. Responses were categorised into two groups: scores of 0 (‘Strongly disagree’) or 1 (‘Disagree’) were categorised as ‘Disagree,’ while scores of 2 (‘Agree’) or 3 (‘Strongly agree’) were categorised as ‘Agree.’ This methodology aligns with prior research and enhances the comparability of the findings.26 The adoption level of the RF was assessed based on physicians’ self-reported frequency of use in clinical practice when caring for older adults. The RF was measured across 9 domains comprising: (1) vaccination (two items), (2) healthy lifestyle promotion (five items), (3) dental health (one item), (4) chronic disease management (five items), (5) cancer screening (three items), (6) functional disability (four items), (7) mental health (two items), (8) polypharmacy and adverse drug reactions (one item), and (9) social network and support assessment (two items). Each item was rated on a four-point Likert scale from 0 (‘never’) to 3 (‘very frequently’). Responses of 0 (‘never’) or 1 (‘rarely’) were categorised as ‘not frequently adopted,’ while responses of 2 (‘frequently’) or 3 (‘very frequently’) were categorised as ‘frequently adopted.’ Perceived enablers and barriers to RF adoption were assessed across five dimensions: (1) guideline-related enablers (seven items), (2) guideline-related barriers (four items), (3) patient-related barriers (six items), (4) physician-related barriers (four items) and (5) external factors (seven items), comprising a total of 28 items. Each factor was rated on a four-point Likert scale from 0 (‘strongly disagree’) to 3 (‘strongly agree’). For analysis, responses of 0 or 1 were categorised as ‘disagree,’ and responses of 2 or 3 as ‘agree.’ The survey also collected sociodemographic data to analyse adoption patterns across different practice contexts. This included age, gender, training status, years of practice experience, specialist qualifications, practice setting (public vs private) and practice size.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using R V.4.2.1. Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarise the data. χ2 tests were used to assess differences in adoption levels and implementation outcomes between public and private physicians. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression were employed to examine associations between sociodemographic characteristics and the acceptability, applicability and feasibility of the RF. The crude ORs, adjusted ORs (aOR) and 95% CIs were calculated. The goodness of fit for the logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer and Lemeshow test. For all analyses, a two-tailed p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. To assess the robustness of the results, ordinal logistic regression was employed as a sensitivity analysis.

Patient and public partnership

Given that this study was designed to explore PCPs’ adoption of and perspectives on RF, neither patients nor the public were involved in the study’s design, implementation, reporting or dissemination plans.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 485 physicians were included in this study, comprising 276 (56.9%) males and 209 (43.1%) females. Detailed sociodemographic characteristics are presented in table 1. Among the participants, 244 (50.3%) were practising in the public sector and 241 (49.7%) in the private sector. Most physicians were aged between 36 years and 50 years (46.8%), held the status of academy fellow (48.9%) and specialised in community and family medicine (60.8%). Approximately 36.9% (n=179) had less than 16 years of practice experience, and 37.5% (n=182) reported seeing 60 or more patients per day. χ2 analysis indicated significant differences between public and private physicians across several sociodemographic characteristics, including age, sex, years of practice, training status, specialist qualification and practice size (table 1).

Adoption level of RF for preventive care for older adults

The adoption level of recommendations in the RF for preventive care for older adults varied, ranging from 17.5% to 92.8% (online supplemental table S2; figure 1). Among all participants, the most commonly adopted recommendation was annual seasonal influenza vaccination (92.8%), followed by advising patients to quit smoking (88.7%) and annual screening for hypertension (88.5%). The least adopted recommendations were screening for urinary incontinence (17.5%), hearing impairment (19.8%) and promotion of oral hygiene (25.4%).

Among private physicians, the most commonly adopted recommendation was annual screening for hypertension (95.0%), followed by seasonal influenza vaccination (93.8%) and screening for hyperlipidaemia (90.9%). However, over 80% of private physicians reported rarely recommending urinary incontinence screening (adoption rate: 19.5%).

Among public physicians, the highest adoption rates were observed for seasonal influenza vaccination (91.8%), advising smoking cessation (91.4%) and provide medication review (84.0%). In contrast, over 80% of public physicians rarely recommended urinary incontinence screening (15.6%), hearing impairment screening (17.2%) and promotion of oral hygiene (19.7%).

χ2 analysis revealed significant differences between public and private sector physicians in the adoption of various RF recommendations (online supplemental table S2; online supplemental figure 1). Private physicians were significantly more likely to adopt the RF recommendation, such as key adoption gaps (p<0.001) were observed across annual hypertension screening (95.0% vs 82.0%), diabetes screening (83.8% vs 52.2%), diabetes screening strategy (83.4% vs 61.5%), colorectal cancer screening strategy (84.2% vs 63.5%), hyperlipidaemia screening (90.9% vs 59.4%) and hyperlipidaemia screening strategy (85.9% vs 61.5%). Except for the above, higher adoption was observed among private physicians for recommendations on hygiene promotion, cervical cytology screening for those not yet screened, a cervical cytology screening strategy and visual impairment screening (p<0.05). Conversely, a significantly higher proportion of public physicians assessed patients’ social support networks compared with their private counterparts (47.5% vs 34.9%, p=0.006).

Enablers and barriers associated with the adoption level of RF

Over 90% of physicians identified several enablers to RF adoption, including the potential role in enhancing patient knowledge (96.7%), inclusion of essential clinical information (96.5%), inclusion of high-quality evidence (96.3%), sufficient recommendations with high validity (95.9%), and inclusion of suffcient local contextual information (95.7%) (online supplemental table S3; figure 2). Key barriers to RF adoption included limited consultation time (94.2%), low patient motivation for lifestyle change (90.7%) and limited resources (90.1%). χ2 analysis revealed significant differences between public and private sector physicians in their perceptions of enablers and barriers (online supplemental table S3; online supplemental figure 2). A higher proportion of private physicians viewed frequent RF updates as a facilitator (91.7% vs 84.4%, p=0.02), while market competition was viewed as a limiting factor (53.1% vs 39.3%, p=0.003). In contrast, more public physicians noted the incompatibility of RF with their clinical settings (75.0% vs 60.6%, p=0.001).

Implementation outcomes of the RF

Most participants agreed that the RF was applicable (71.3%), acceptable (78.1%) and feasible (65.6%) for use in their daily practice to provide preventive care for older adults. Private physicians were significantly more likely than public physicians to perceive the RF as acceptable (82.2% vs 74.2%, p=0.044) and feasible (73.9% vs 57.4%, p<0.001) (online supplemental table S4).

Factor associated with the implementation outcome of the RF

The results of univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses for factors associated with the perceived applicability, acceptability and feasibility of the RF are presented in tables24. In the univariable analysis, physicians who had received basic specialist training (OR 2.038, 95% CI 1.125 to 3.693, p=0.019) and those working in practices with a daily patient volume of 30–59 (OR 1.987, 95% CI 1.158 to 3.411, p=0.013) were more likely to perceive the RF as applicable. Conversely, physicians aged 36–50 years were less likely to find the RF applicable (OR 0.543, 95% CI 0.322 to 0.916, p=0.022). In terms of acceptability, physicians working in the public sector were less likely to find the RF acceptable (OR 0.624, 95% CI 0.403 to 0.966, p=0.034). Feasibility was also negatively associated with working in the public sector (OR 0.476, 95% CI 0.325 to 0.699, p<0.001) and managing a high patient volume (≥60 patients/day) (OR 0.526, 95% CI 0.333 to 0.832, p=0.006). Conversely, greater feasibility was reported among physicians aged ≥50 years (OR 1.852, 95% CI 1.069 to 3.209, p=0.028), those with over 25 years of experience (OR 2.137, 95% CI 1.300 to 3.511, p=0.003), those not trained by the Hong Kong Academy of Medicine (HKAM) (OR 1.693, 95% CI 1.043 to 2.750, p=0.033) and those who had received only basic HKAM training (OR 1.713, 95% CI 1.008 to 2.910, p=0.047).

Discussion

Summary of findings

This cross-sectional study included 485 physicians, with an even split between public and private sectors. Overall, most physicians considered the RF applicable (71.3%), acceptable (78.1%) and feasible (65.6%). Private sector physicians reported higher levels of acceptability and feasibility compared with those in the public sector. Adoption of individual RF recommendations for preventive care in older adults varied substantially, with high uptake of influenza vaccination, smoking cessation advice and hypertension screening, but lower adoption of screening for urinary incontinence, hearing impairment and promotion of oral hygiene.

Significant differences in adoption were observed between public and private physicians across various recommendations, including those related to hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, cervical cytology, colorectal cancer, vision and social support assessment. Private physicians were more likely to report that frequent RF updates facilitated adoption, but also more frequently cited market competition as a barrier. In contrast, public physicians more often perceived the RF as incompatible with their clinical setting. Regression analysis further indicated that physicians with moderate practice sizes were more likely to find the RF applicable, while those in the public sector were less likely to perceive it as acceptable or feasible.

Possible explanations of the findings

Adoption level of different RF recommendations

While the overall adoption of the RF was high among physicians, there was variability in the uptake of specific recommendations. Physicians showed a clear preference for adopting recommendations related to common and high-burden conditions, such as seasonal influenza vaccination, hypertension screening and smoking cessation advice for older adults. In contrast, recommendations involving less prevalent or lower-priority issues, such as screening for urinary incontinence and hearing impairment, were seldom implemented. This pattern may reflect both the perceived relevance and clinical significance of certain conditions, as well as the strength of supporting evidence and public health priorities. Previous studies have suggested that physicians’ awareness of disease severity and prevalence significantly influences their clinical behaviour and decision-making patterns,27 28 as well as physicians’ biases about the severity of the disease may have led to a tendency towards clinical practice.28 In Hong Kong, hypertension is a prevalent and widely recognised chronic disease, affecting 57.4% of older adults in 2022.29 Major health authorities, including the Hong Kong Department of Health and the US Preventive Services Task Force, have actively recommended routine blood pressure screening for older adults to improve hypertension management and decrease the disease burden associated with it.30 31 Similarly, smoking cessation, the prevention of seasonal influenza and the prevention of pneumococcal disease are key priorities for the Hong Kong Department of Health. Disease surveillance, vaccination guidelines and promotional campaigns for vaccination and smoking cessation are well-established within Hong Kong.32 33

On the one hand, the robust evidence base supporting these preventive interventions—demonstrating reduced morbidity, mortality and improved quality of life—likely enhances physician confidence in their effectiveness and feasibility, thereby reinforcing routine adoption in clinical practice.27 On the other hand, the implementation of public health campaigns and subsidy policies may further enhance physicians’ awareness of these diseases and promote their adoption. In contrast, for conditions with lower prevalence or perceived risk, such as hearing impairment, despite posing a significant impact on patients’ quality of life,34 the lack of support and information within Hong Kong’s primary care system reflects the lower priority assigned to such conditions in clinical settings.35 36 Furthermore, the limited understanding of certain conditions by PCPs, such as symptoms associated with overactive bladder, coupled with diagnostic limitations within the primary care setting and embarrassment, undermines their capacity to manage these conditions proactively.37 This discrepancy highlights the importance of increasing awareness among healthcare providers and patients about the value of addressing uncommon or low-risk conditions. Improving awareness can lead to earlier interventions, better outcomes and enhanced adoption of RF recommendations for these conditions.

Enablers and barriers to RF adoption

As previous studies have indicated,21 our findings reveal that RF adoption involves multidimensional decision-making, encompassing RF characteristics, individual-level factors and inner setting-level factors.

Despite widespread acceptance of the RF in principle, several challenges hinder its full implementation. Physicians highlighted key enabling factors for adoption regarding the RF characteristics, including the availability of essential clinical information, high-quality evidence and local epidemiological data. When such information was lacking—especially for conditions less emphasised in public discourse—physicians perceived the guidelines as less relevant or applicable to their clinical context. Previous studies have pointed to similar findings, suggesting that physicians’ beliefs about the information and evidence presented in the RF influence their adoption decisions.38 Additionally, some guidelines may lack clarity in their design, hindering adoption by physicians, patients and healthcare providers.39 A Hong Kong qualitative study examining pneumococcal vaccination strategy implementation reported inconsistent clinical recommendations, with disagreement among physicians on whether to administer 13-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13) or 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPV23) first.39 This evidence indicates that continuously improving the content and design of RF to align with the local healthcare setting is crucial.

The barriers in the inner setting, including time and resource constraints, are also major barriers to RF adoption. Studies have shown that physicians often spend 5 min or less per consultation due to heavy workloads,40 which can lead them to neglect non-essential procedures such as vaccination promotion and smoking cessation.39 41 Similarly, evidence indicates that time limitations may lead PCPs to prioritise acute care over preventive strategies, as they are forced to concentrate on patients’ immediate symptoms and needs rather than on long-term health outcomes.42 This shift in focus could undermine the effectiveness of disease prevention, as decreased opportunities for early detection of health issues are due to the deferral of preventive action.41 Furthermore, as physicians rarely receive reimbursement for supporting health promotion programmes, they may lack the time and financial resources to undergo proper training, diminishing their motivation to participate in such programmes.41

Another barrier to adoption is the low motivation of patients to change their lifestyle, which may influence physicians’ willingness to adopt RF recommendations. The attitude of patients plays a crucial role in determining the adoption rate of healthcare interventions. Research has indicated that physician burnout impedes the adoption of innovations and that physicians’ perceptions of care outcomes and the meaning of their work are negatively associated with burnout.43 44

Differences between physicians in public and private settings

We observed that physicians in private settings reported a higher adoption rate of RF recommendations compared with their counterparts in public settings. This phenomenon can be attributed to several factors. A systematic review found that private physicians are more likely to provide additional tests and healthcare services due to financial incentives.45 Furthermore, the lower workload and resource availability in private settings may contribute to a higher adoption rate of RF recommendations.46 This also helps explain why more public physicians reported that the RF was not compatible with their clinical setting. Furthermore, the autonomy within workflows in public and private primary care institutions may explain this discrepancy. Previous research has highlighted substantial differences in organisational structures, with private PCPs benefiting from greater autonomy to develop targeted medical strategies, whereas increased non-clinical responsibilities and limited flexibility may constrain the decision-making capacity of PCPs in the public sector.47 An interesting finding was that private physicians were more likely to cite the high frequency of RF updates as a key reason for their adoption. Although the exact reasons for this are unclear, we believe that the role of quality of care in long-term patient relationships may be a contributing factor. RFs and clinical guidelines are widely recognised to improve care quality,18 and patient satisfaction is strongly influenced by the quality of care.48 In this context, access to up-to-date information is essential for private practitioners to update their professional knowledge and build long-term relationships with patients.48 On the other hand, our multivariable logistic regressions revealed that physicians in public settings were less likely to consider the RF feasible and acceptable in daily practice compared with those in private settings. This difference is likely due to resource constraints and heavy workloads in the public healthcare system.40 46

Implication for policy and practice

This study provides valuable insights into the adoption of RF recommendations by physicians across both private and public healthcare settings, revealing key enablers and barriers to their uptake. One critical finding is the lower adoption rate for recommendations related to diseases with lower prevalence or risk. This pattern suggests that awareness and education efforts should be focused on healthcare providers, particularly for conditions that may not be widely recognised as high priority. Targeted educational campaigns, as well as medical education initiatives focusing on less awareness diseases such as urinary incontinence, hearing impairment and oral hygiene among older adults, could play a pivotal role in enhancing the recognition and comprehension of these conditions.

Furthermore, it was observed that private physicians are more likely to adopt the RF compared with their public counterparts. Likewise, physicians working in public settings were less inclined to a positive view of implementation of RF in clinical settings. These disparities may be attributable to differences in workload and resource availability between the public and private healthcare systems. To improve the adoption of RF and enhance the use of preventive measures in primary care settings, expanding public-private partnerships (PPPs) could provide a viable solution. PPP programmes could help alleviate the pressure on public healthcare providers by facilitating resource sharing, thus creating more capacity for the implementation of RF recommendations. For example, the Primary Health Networks, established by the Australian government, provide funding and oversight to private healthcare organisations to facilitate their delivery of comprehensive primary healthcare services to the community.49 These partnerships can also promote the integration of new guidelines into practice, benefiting both patients and healthcare professionals by enhancing service delivery.50

Another key aspect emphasised by physicians is the importance of high-quality evidence and up-to-date RF content in driving adoption. This finding suggests that RF recommendations should be regularly reviewed and updated to ensure their continued relevance and to maintain physician confidence in their applicability. Ensuring that RF guidelines are based on the latest research and are tailored to local contexts will likely improve their integration into routine clinical practice.38

Strengths and limitations

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of RF adoption across both private and public healthcare settings, offering valuable insights into the factors influencing physician uptake. The inclusion of diverse healthcare settings enhances the generalisability of the findings, and the exploration of both enablers and barriers provides a balanced view of the challenges to RF adoption.

While this study provides valuable insights, there are some limitations. First, the data were collected via self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce bias due to participants’ subjective assessments of their practices and inflate estimates of RF adoption and perception. Second, the study’s findings are context-specific to the Hong Kong healthcare system, and caution should be exercised when attempting to generalise the results to other regions or countries with different healthcare infrastructures, especially the adoption rate of RF. However, the factors and phenomena identified, such as workload challenges, the importance of high-quality evidence, observed potential cognitive differences across various diseases and the role of PPPs, can inform other settings, particularly those with similar healthcare delivery models or challenges. Third, although face validation was performed by an expert panel and a pilot test was conducted, the instrument used for data collection has not undergone extensive validation, which may affect the reliability of the results. Fourth, the cross-sectional design of this study does not allow for the establishment of causal relationships between variables. Fifth, although providing CME credits and incentives was intended to increase participation, it may introduce potential selection bias, as PCPs who are inclined towards participation may be more likely to engage in this study. This could result in an overestimation or underestimation of the results. Finally, the study did not explore in depth the specific strategies or interventions that might facilitate RF adoption, leaving this area open for future research.

Conclusion

The findings from this study indicate a generally positive perception of the Hong Kong RF for preventive care for older adults among PCP, with most physicians considering it applicable, acceptable and feasible. High adoption was seen for recommendations on influenza vaccination, hypertension screening and smoking cessation, with private physicians reporting higher adoption due to differences in workload and resources. Enablers included high-quality evidence and local clinical information, while barriers such as time constraints and patient adherence were noted. The findings emphasise the need for regular updates, ongoing education and clear guidelines to sustain RF adoption. Future research should explore frameworks like CFIR and Expert Recommendations for implementing Change to develop tailored strategies and use Delphi studies to identify effective implementation methods.
