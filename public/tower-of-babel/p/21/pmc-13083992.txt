The effects of normal fault movement on the failure mechanism of water conveyance tunnels considering multi-field interaction
Authors: Zhang Xinwei, Chen Zhanxiang, Liang Weiheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTunnels of large water conveyance projects often cross active faults and are therefore exposed to permanent ground deformation induced by normal fault dislocation. In this study, a three-dimensional multi-field coupling model is developed to investigate the mechanical response and failure mechanism of a water conveyance tunnel crossing a normal fault. The model integrates surrounding rock, fault fracture zone, reinforced concrete lining, and internal pressurised water through an ABAQUS–FLUENT–MpCCI framework. The surrounding rock and fault zone are described by a Mohr–Coulomb elastoplastic model, the lining is simulated using the concrete damage plasticity model, and the internal water flow is governed by the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes equations with the RNG k–ε turbulence model. The numerical model is qualitatively validated against a scaled physical model test of a tunnel crossing a normal fault, focusing on the localisation of deformation and damage in the fault zone. A systematic parametric analysis is then conducted to examine the influence of fault displacement, fault dip angle, fault zone width, fault cohesion and lining strength. The overall lining damage in tension (OLDT) index is adopted to quantify the global damage state of the lining. The results show that normal fault dislocation induces strongly localised deformation and damage within the fault fracture zone, whereas tunnel segments far away remain essentially elastic. The crown and invert near the fault are confirmed to be the most vulnerable regions due to combined bending and shear. Increasing fault displacement significantly amplifies the lining damage in the fault zone, while variations in fault dip angle and fault width mainly affect the axial extent of the highly deformed region. Higher fault cohesion and higher lining strength both reduce the OLDT value and shrink the severely damaged zone. The internal water slightly modifies the stress distribution in the lining through fluid–structure interaction but does not change the fundamental failure mode. These findings provide a basis for performance-based design and mitigation measures for water conveyance tunnels crossing normal faults.Keywords: Normal fault, Water conveyance tunnels, Numerical simulation, Fluid-structure interaction, Damage characteristicSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Tunnels that cross active faults are highly vulnerable to permanent ground deformation induced by fault dislocation and strong earthquakes. Field observations and post-earthquake investigations have shown that tunnels intersecting fault zones may suffer severe damage or even collapse, despite being embedded in seemingly competent rock masses1–5. A recent review by Yao et al.6 systematically compiled more than one hundred case histories of tunnels crossing active faults over the past 120 years (1906–2025), and highlighted that damage is typically concentrated in the fault crossing zone, where differential displacement and shear deformation are most pronounced. Statistical studies further indicate that the degree of tunnel damage is strongly correlated with the characteristics of faulting and ground motion, and that mountain tunnels located in complex tectonic regions such as Southwest China are particularly exposed to such hazards5–8. These case histories underline the need for a better understanding of the mechanical response and failure mechanism of tunnels crossing active faults, especially for lifeline projects such as long-distance water conveyance tunnels.

In recent years, numerous experimental and numerical studies have been carried out to investigate the interaction between fault rupture and underground structures. Shaking table tests, centrifuge tests and 1 g model tests have been used to examine the deformation and damage patterns of tunnels or pipelines crossing normal and reverse faults under various geological and loading conditions1–3,9–13. These physical model tests have revealed that damage is usually concentrated near the intersection of the tunnel and the fault zone, and that the deformation mode of the tunnel is strongly influenced by the fault dip angle, burial depth and overburden conditions1,2,10–13. Complementary numerical studies have focused on the effects of fault geometry, tunnel–fault relative position, lining stiffness and ground conditions on tunnel response, providing valuable insights into the distribution of internal forces and the development of plastic zones in the surrounding rock14–21. More recent work has extended these analyses to complex loading scenarios, such as the combined action of fault dislocation and seismic loading21,22, and to adaptive or flexible tunnel structures in cross-fault sections14,23.

For water conveyance tunnels, the presence of internal water introduces additional complexities into the problem. On the one hand, the internal water pressure changes the effective stress state of the lining and surrounding rock, and may evolve during deformation. On the other hand, damage to the lining in the fault zone may lead to leakage, erosion and deterioration of hydraulic performance. Several recent studies have addressed aspects of this problem. Zhang et al.22 investigated the mechanical response of a cross-fault water conveyance tunnel under the combined action of faulting dislocation and seismic loading, while Zhang et al.24 analysed the damage evolution of a cross-fault water conveyance tunnel under the combined effects of creep and ground motion. Numerical studies based on specific water conveyance projects, such as the Xianglushan tunnel of the Central Yunnan Water Diversion Project, have further clarified the deformation and stress mechanism of water conveyance tunnels crossing strike-slip faults and evaluated the performance of adaptive anti-dislocation structures23,25. In addition, earlier research on the shear behaviour and failure mechanisms of surrounding rock-lining contact surfaces26, the application and verification of the concrete damage plasticity model for tunnel linings27, and the reduction of water pressure acting on tunnel linings using water-rock independent calculation methods28,29 has provided useful tools for modelling the coupled response of hydraulic tunnels. These works indicate that the interaction between hydraulic loads and structural response is an important factor in the safety assessment of water conveyance tunnels.

Nevertheless, several important gaps remain in the existing research. First, many studies on tunnels crossing active faults either neglect the internal water or treat it as a static, uniform pressure, so that the fully coupled interaction between surrounding rock, fault zone, lining and water is not captured4,21–24,28,29. Second, most experimental and numerical investigations have focused on traffic tunnels or specific project cases, while systematic parametric studies dedicated to large-diameter water conveyance tunnels crossing normal faults under different geological and lining conditions are relatively limited. Third, although previous research has proposed various qualitative descriptions of damage patterns, quantitative indices that can consistently characterise the global damage state of the lining under different fault and material parameters are still scarce4,20,21,26,29. Finally, the engineering implications and limitations of these findings for the performance-based design and mitigation of water conveyance tunnels crossing active faults have not been fully translated into practical guidelines.

To address these gaps, the present study develops a three-dimensional multi-field coupling model for a water conveyance tunnel crossing a normal fault, integrating rock–fault–lining–fluid interaction through an ABAQUS–FLUENT–MpCCI framework30–32. Compared with previous numerical studies on tunnels crossing active faults6,13,21–24, the main contributions of this work are as follows: (1) a coupled rock–fault–lining–water model is established, in which the surrounding rock and fault zone are described by an elastoplastic constitutive law, the reinforced concrete lining is modelled using the damage plasticity model, and the internal pressurised water flow is simulated by the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes equations with the RNG k–ε turbulence model; (2) the numerical model is validated against a scaled physical model test of a tunnel crossing a normal fault, focusing on the deformation pattern and damage concentration in the fault zone; (3) a systematic parametric analysis is conducted to investigate the influence of fault displacement, fault dip angle, fault zone width, rock mass cohesion and lining strength on tunnel response, and a global damage index (OLDT) is employed to quantify the lining damage state; and (4) based on the parametric results, engineering implications, advantages and limitations of the proposed approach are discussed, providing preliminary design recommendations for water conveyance tunnels crossing normal faults.

Among the available turbulence closures in FLUENT, the RNG k–ε model is selected because it provides a reasonable balance between accuracy and computational efficiency for internal turbulent flows with moderate streamline curvature and strain, such as those in water conveyance tunnels. Previous studies have shown that the RNG k–ε model can adequately capture the main hydrodynamic characteristics and pressure fluctuations in hydraulic tunnels and water conveyance systems, while keeping the computational cost acceptable for large-scale simulations. Therefore, the RNG k–ε turbulence model is employed for all simulations in this work.

Multiple physical field coupling method

The numerical software of ABAQ US31 and FLUENT32 are highly specialized tools, each excelling in its respective domain of solid and fluid analysis. ABAQUS is primarily used for addressing complex solid mechanics problems, particularly those involving material nonlinearity, geometric nonlinearity, and contact nonlinearity. It provides an extensive material model library and soil-structure contact model library, supporting simulations of materials and contact models such as concrete, metals, composites, and dynamic nonlinear interaction model, and offers customization of material behavior through user-defined subroutines like UMAT and FRIC. On the other hand, FLUENT is dedicated to fluid dynamics, excelling in the simulation of turbulence, laminar flows, multiphase flows, and fluid-thermal or fluid-particle interactions. It supports a wide range of turbulence models, flexible mesh generation, and user-defined functions (UDF) to handle complex fluid behaviors and physical phenomena. Overall, ABAQUS is best suited for solid structure analysis, while FLUENT is ideal for fluid dynamics. To fully leverage the strengths of ABAQUS and FLUENT in their respective domains, the MPCCI (Mesh-based Parallel Code Coupling Interface) platform can be utilized for direct coupling. This platform facilitates efficient data exchange and communication between the two software tools, enabling seamless integration of solid and fluid analyses30. MpCCI adopts the iterative calculation method of fluid-structure interaction to solve the fluid equation and structural equation, respectively, and then exchanges data through the serial algorithm to realize the direct communication between computational structural dynamics and computational fluid dynamics code, as shown in Fig. 1. Numerical model

Model description

Figure 3 presents a three-dimensional finite element model designed to investigate the interaction between a water conveyance tunnel, a geological fault, and internal fluid within the tunnel. The model dimensions are 290 m in length, 150 m in width, and 127 m in height, with a fault zone characterized by a dip angle of 60° and a thickness of 10 m. The water conveyance tunnel, buried at a depth of 60 m, features a horseshoe-shaped cross-section with a maximum height of 17.15 m and a width of 15.3 m, surrounded by a concrete lining to simulate reinforcement. The finite element mesh is carefully divided into different regions to ensure accurate representation and analysis: the hanging wall, located above the fault plane, consists of 19,200 C3D8R elements and 21,632 nodes, while the footwall, beneath the fault, also contains 19,200 C3D8R elements and 21,632 nodes. The fault zone, representing a mechanically weaker region, is discretized with C3D8R 7,680 elements and 9,152 nodes, and the internal fluid-filled tunnel region is modeled with 3,840 Fluid elements and 4,941 nodes to capture fluid-structure interactions. This model integrates structural, geological, and fluid components to simulate the effects of fault movement, internal fluid pressure, and rock mass deformation on the stability of the tunnel. By providing a detailed finite element discretization, the model allows for precise analysis of stress distribution, deformation patterns, and potential failure mechanisms, offering a comprehensive framework for understanding the coupled effects of geological faults and fluid-structure interactions on water conveyance tunnels.

Based on the above specific conditions of water conveyance tunnels across active faults, a three-dimensional numerical model of the surrounding rock-fault fracture zone-lining-internal water system is established by using the ABAQUS-MpCCI-FLUENT multi-field coupling numerical simulation platform. The overall simulation procedure is as follows. (1) In the first step, the degrees of freedom at the bottom of the surrounding rock are fully constrained and the normal degrees of freedom on the lateral boundaries are fixed. Gravity is then applied to the whole model and the initial geostress field of the surrounding rock is obtained through stress equilibrium. (2) In the second step, the tunnel excavation is simulated using the softening modulus method. The rock mass within the tunnel contour is removed instantaneously, which simplifies the excavation process while preserving the main mechanical response of the surrounding rock. (3) In the third step, the internal water domain is activated and the fluid-structure interaction between the lining and the internal water on the tunnel-water interface is established. This step simulates the real pressurised flow of internal water in the water conveyance tunnel. (4) In the fourth step, the fault fracture zone and the footwall are fixed, and a prescribed displacement parallel to the fault contact surface is applied to the hanging wall in order to simulate the normal fault dislocation. The overall operation flow and numerical model are illustrated in Fig. 5.

The operation flow.

In the coupled analysis, the surrounding rock, fault zone and lining are solved in ABAQUS, whereas the internal water flow is solved in FLUENT. The two solvers are linked through MpCCI in a staggered, partitioned manner. At each coupling step, ABAQUS first computes the structural response under the current fault displacement and fluid pressure, and passes the updated tunnel-water interface displacement to FLUENT. FLUENT then advances the flow field and returns the updated pressure and shear stresses on the tunnel-water interface. These interface quantities are mapped conservatively between non-matching meshes using MpCCI interpolation. Because the prescribed fault displacement in this study is monotonic and relatively slow, a quasi-static loading scheme is adopted for the structural domain, while the fluid domain is advanced with pseudo-time steps consistent with the coupling interval. This approach allows the essential fluid-structure coupling effects to be captured without introducing significant inertial effects in either domain. Contact along the fault plane is modelled using surface-to-surface contact elements with a Coulomb-type friction law and hard contact in the normal direction, allowing separation and sliding between the hanging wall and the footwall. This configuration enables simulation of the relative movement of the two fault blocks and the associated shear deformation in the fault zone, which are critical for reproducing the fault-induced deformation of the tunnel.

This classification provides a quantitative framework for assessing the progressive damage of tunnel linings under fault movement, supporting effective maintenance and repair strategies.

For the 60° dip angle normal fault, a sandbox model test was designed in11 with a geometric similarity ratio of 1:50 using a test box developed by Tongji University, as shown in Fig. 6. The movable base plate of the model box simulates the fault bedrock, with the footwall fixed and the hanging wall moving downward along the fault plane. On the basis of this sandbox test, a three-dimensional numerical model of the surrounding rock–fault fracture zone–lining system was established, as shown in Fig. 7. The hanging wall and footwall of the surrounding rock are modelled with the Mohr–Coulomb constitutive model, with a density of 1700 kg/m³, an elastic modulus of 0.3 GPa, a Poisson’s ratio of 0.2, an internal friction angle of 28°, and a cohesion of 0.1 MPa. The lining is modelled as a linear elastic material with a density of 2500 kg/m³, an elastic modulus of 1.13 GPa, and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.2.

A semi-quantitative consistency assessment was performed. First, the longitudinal extent of the localised deformation zone was extracted from both the numerical model and the physical model photographs in12. The numerical results indicate that the highly deformed region extends over approximately 1.1–1.4 tunnel diameters, which is consistent with the deformation band width identified from the experimentally observed settlement profile. Second, the centre position of the maximum curvature along the tunnel axis, which corresponds to the peak bending deformation, was compared between the numerical simulation and the physical model. The deviation in the peak position is within approximately 3–5% of the tunnel diameter, indicating a close agreement in the location of maximum structural demand. Third, the crack distribution obtained from the concrete damaged plasticity model exhibits a concentrated crack band within the fault zone, and the crack inclination angle and distribution width match those interpreted from the experimental crack patterns. These semi-quantitative comparisons demonstrate that the present numerical model captures both the deformation concentration mechanism and the dominant damage characteristics observed in the physical model.

Results and discussion

Across all parameter scenarios examined in this study, several common deformation and damage characteristics can be identified. First, deformation and lining damage are strongly localised within the fault fracture zone, while the tunnel segments far from the fault remain essentially elastic. Second, the crown and invert sections near the fault are more susceptible to tensile damage than the sidewalls, owing to the combined effects of bending and shear. Third, the internal water slightly modifies the stress state of the lining but does not change the fundamental failure mode, which is governed primarily by the relative stiffness of the fault fracture zone, surrounding rock and lining. In the following subsections, we therefore focus on the specific influence of each parameter (fault displacement, fault dip angle, fault zone width, fault cohesion and lining strength) on the magnitude and distribution of deformation, energy dissipation and lining damage, while avoiding repetition of the common trends.

The deformation localisation observed in this study is consistent with the crack concentration reported by Liu et al.1 and Shen et al.1, who also found that lining damage is dominated by tensile cracking at the crown and invert when tunnels intersect normal faults. The influence of fault dip angle on the extent of deformation agrees with the numerical findings of Qiao et al. (2022).Unlike traffic tunnels examined in previous studies, the present results show that internal water pressure slightly modifies the lining stress state but does not change the fundamental damage mechanism, highlighting a key difference for water conveyance tunnels.

Influence of fault displacement

Fault dislocation often causes earthquakes, and the magnitude of the fault momentum directly affects the seismic intensity, which causes structural damage to the underground buildings crossing active faults. Well et al.8 carried out magnitude-fault displacement statistical regression analysis in the form of fault dislocation. The fault displacement is 0.15 m, 0.20 m, 0.25 m, 0.30 m, and 0.35 m, and the magnitude is within 6–7. This paper studies the lining response under the above fault displacement, and the fault width, fault dip angle, fault cohesion, and lining strength remain unchanged, which are 10 m, 60°, 0.1 MPa, and 30 MPa.

The localisation of damage within the fault fracture zone and its gradual intensification with increasing fault displacement are consistent with previous numerical and experimental studies on tunnels crossing normal faults, which also reported that the most severe damage occurs near the tunnel–fault intersection while the segments away from the fault remain largely elastic1,2,11,21. This confirms that permanent ground deformation induced by fault dislocation is the primary control on lining damage in such configurations.

Vertical displacement of lining

Figure 11 shows the vertical displacement of the tunnel lining. The vertical displacement of the vault and the base of the hanging wall lining escalates with fault displacement. The escalation of fault displacement further amplifies the upward trend of vertical displacement in hanging walls. The displacement mutation of the lining is primarily focused on the fault fracture zone, with significant displacement deformation occurring between the hanging wall and the fault interface. These are due to the large unrecoverable deformation of the lining near the fault fracture zone controlled by the surrounding rock when the hanging wall rock slides along the fault dislocation surface.

Influence of fault dip angles

As one of the fault elements, fault dip angle will also affect the lining structure under fault dislocation. In this paper, the lining response at fault dip angles of 60°, 65°, 70°, 75°, and 80° is studied. The fault displacement, fault width, fault cohesion, and lining strength remain unchanged, which are 0.2 m, 10 m, 0.1 MPa, and 3 MPa.

The limited influence of fault dip angle on the global damage level, combined with its more pronounced effect on the axial extent of the highly deformed zone, is in line with recent findings for both traffic tunnels and water conveyance tunnels crossing active faults6,21. These studies also emphasised that steeper fault dips tend to localise deformation more strongly along the fault plane, while gentler dips distribute damage over a longer tunnel segment.

Influence of fault widths

The fault width is dictated by the mode of activity and the mechanical characteristics of the fault, which can indicate the impact of fault dislocation on subterranean structures adjacent to the fault. In this paper, 10 m, 15 m, 20 m, 25 m, and 30 m are taken as the research objects of fault width. At the same time, the fault displacement, fault dip angle, fault cohesion, and lining strength remain unchanged, which are 0.2 m, 60° MPa, 0.1 MPa, and 30 MPa, respectively.

Similar observations have been reported for tunnels intersecting wide fractured zones, where the widening of the weak zone mainly increases the length of the affected tunnel segment without dramatically changing the peak damage level of the lining 16,35.

Influence of fault cohesion

The cohesion of the fault fracture zone will affect the fault dislocation, and the soil cohesion at the fracture zone has an anti-shear effect on the fault dislocation. Therefore, this paper studies the lining response of cross-active faults with fault cohesion of 0.1 MPa, 0.3 MPa, 0.5 MPa, 0.7 MPa, and 0.9 MPa. Fault displacement, fault dip angle, fault width, and lining strength remain unchanged at 0.2 m, 60°, 10 m, and 30 MPa.

This behaviour agrees with previous studies showing that strengthening low-cohesion fault zones through grouting or other reinforcement measures can effectively reduce tunnel deformation and lining damage under fault dislocation6,26,28.

Influence of lining structure strength

The lining strength of a water conveyance tunnel can effectively inhibit the irreversible damage of cross-active fault lining structure caused by fault dislocation from the perspective of engineering safety protection, so it is necessary to study. In this paper, 15 MPa, 25 MPa, 30 MPa, 35 MPa, and 55 MPa are selected as the research objects of lining strength, and the fault displacement, dip angle, width, and cohesion are kept unchanged, which are 0.2 m, 60°, 10 m, and 0.1 MPa.

The diminishing benefit of further increasing lining strength beyond a certain level is also consistent with recent performance-based design studies for tunnels crossing active faults, which highlighted the need to balance safety and material consumption when selecting lining strength classes6,21,22.

Engineering implications

The parametric analysis in this study provides several implications for the design and mitigation of water conveyance tunnels crossing normal faults.

Fault displacement and dip angle. For the range of fault displacements considered in this study (0.15–0.35 m), the relative deformation and damage of the lining are strongly concentrated within the fault fracture zone, while the tunnel segments away from the fault remain in a low damage state. This suggests that design measures should focus on the fault zone and its adjacent sections, where the crown and invert are most vulnerable to tensile cracking. Variations in fault dip angle mainly affect the axial extent and distribution of the highly deformed zone, but the basic pattern of localised damage in the fault fracture zone does not change. In practice, tunnels crossing steep normal faults should therefore pay particular attention to the design of the lining in the immediate vicinity of the fault plane.

Treatment of low-cohesion fault zones. The results for different cohesion values show that low-cohesion fault fracture zones lead to larger vertical displacement of the lining at the fault section and more concentrated damage in the fault zone. Increasing the cohesion of the fault fracture zone reduces both deformation and damage demand on the lining. This indicates that, in engineering practice, grouting or other reinforcement measures which enhance the shear strength of the fault gouge can effectively improve the safety of water conveyance tunnels crossing active faults.

Role of internal water and fluid–structure interaction. The numerical results indicate that internal water slightly modifies the stress distribution in the lining through fluid–structure interaction, but does not change the fundamental failure mechanism, which is governed mainly by the relative stiffness of the fault fracture zone, surrounding rock and lining. However, once cracking occurs in the fault zone, internal water may aggravate leakage and erosion. Therefore, it is recommended that fluid–structure interaction be taken into account in the design and assessment of water conveyance tunnels crossing active faults, especially when the internal pressure is high or when transient hydraulic loads are expected.

Conclusions

This study establishes a coupled rock–fault–lining–water model for water conveyance tunnels crossing normal faults and validates it using a scaled physical model. The main findings are:

Fault-induced deformation is strongly localised within the fault zone, and tensile cracks at the crown and invert dominate lining failure.
