Nitrogen-based cationic copolymers as corrosion inhibitors for P110 carbon steel in 20% sulfuric acid investigated through experimental and theoretical studies
Authors: Ghadeer Mubarak, Chandrabhan Verma, Mohammad A. J. Mazumder, Imad Barsoum, Akram Alfantazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present study demonstrates the corrosion inhibition potential of three nitrogen-based cationic surfactant molecules (AMCs) for P110 carbon steel (P110 CS) in 20% H2SO4 solution. The corrosion inhibition efficiency (%IE) of the studied surfactants was demonstrated using electrochemical (OCP, PDP, and EIS), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and DFT-based computational studies. The outcomes suggest that their inhibition efficiencies increase with increasing hydrophobic character in the form of alkyl chain(s). The surfactant molecule (AMC-3), having two long alkyl chains, manifests the best inhibition efficiency, and AMC-1, without any such alkyl chain, manifests the lowest efficiency. Their %IE followed the sequence (PDP data at 100 ppm): AMC-3 (96.44%) > AMC-2 (93.39%) > AMC-1 (89.78%). Potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) study suggests that they retard anodic and cathodic Tafel reactions and serve as mixed-type corrosion inhibitors. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) study suggests the AMCs become effective by creating a barrier for the charge transfer process through their adsorption at the interface of metal and electrolyte. The adsorption mode of corrosion protection was also supported by SEM analyses, where a significant improvement in the surface morphology of the P110 CS surface was observed in the presence of AMCs, especially in the presence of AMC-3. Their adsorption on the P110 CS surface followed the Langmuir isotherm model. DFT-based quantum chemical calculations show that AMCs interact with the P110 CS surface through physicochemisorption mechanisms where long alkyl chains (or hydrophobicity) play a crucial role in their adsorption.

Introduction

Casing systems’ integrity is essential for ensuring safety, environmental protection, and operational efficiency in oil and gas operations1–3. The corrosion of these downhole casings and tubing, resulting from their exposure to harsh and corrosive environments during exploration and production4,5, poses significant challenges for the oil and gas industries. Moreover, the application of acidizing treatments in wells to dissolve components obstructing the flow of oil and gas involves the use of corrosive acids such as hydrochloric (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$HCl$$\end{document}), hydrofluoric (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$HF$$\end{document}), sulfuric (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{2}S{O}_{4}$$\end{document}), sulfurous (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{2}S{O}_{3}$$\end{document}), acetic (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C{H}_{3}COOH$$\end{document}), and formic (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$HCOOH$$\end{document}) acids, which can lead to rapid corrosion development6–9. The consequences of corrosion, including chemical leakage, pipeline and equipment breakdowns, and failures in metallic equipment, result in significant economic losses10–12.

Carbon steel, a common metal alloy known for its high hardness and strength, finds extensive application in various engineering fields, including the oil and gas industry, specifically the P110 carbon steel13,14. Due to its high strength and resistance to collapse under high pressure, P110 carbon steel is commonly employed in the casing and tubing of oil and gas wells. However, its susceptibility to corrosion in oilfield conditions poses a significant challenge15–17. Numerous strategies and practices have been developed and implemented to address P110 carbon steel corrosion in these wells. These may include corrosion-resistant materials, internal coating, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{2}S$$\end{document}and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${O}_{2}$$\end{document}scavengers and corrosion inhibitors3. One frequently employed method involves using corrosion inhibitors to create a protective layer on the steel surface3,11,18. Corrosion inhibitors function through inhibitory mechanisms, which include film-forming, scavenging, and neutralizing mechanisms, thereby reducing the concentration of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}^{+}$$\end{document} ions, depending on their chemical composition19. These inhibitors can be categorized as organic types, which are adsorbed onto the metal surface and decelerate corrosion, or inorganic types, which react with anodic and cathodic elements and are deposited, forming a barrier coating20,21. Effective corrosion inhibitors with strong inhibition efficiency typically contain heteroatoms (N, O, and P), aromatic functional groups, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document} electrons, which serve as active sites facilitating their adsorption onto the metal surface8,22. Surfactants are widely used as corrosion inhibitors due to their amphiphilic nature, enabling adsorption in interfaces23. They are commonly utilized in various industries given their advantageous features, including high inhibition efficiency, low toxicity, low cost, and simple synthesis and production24–27.

Although numerous organic and surfactant-based corrosion inhibitors have been reported for steel in acidic media, most studies focus on relatively mild acidic environments such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1M HCl$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3.5\%NaCl$$\end{document}, and only a limited number of investigations examine inhibitor performance in sulfuric acid solutions, despite their importance in industrial acid pickling and well-acidizing operations6,7. Previous studies on quaternary ammonium compounds have generally employed simple monomeric surfactants rather than copolymer architectures24,28 and therefore do not provide insight into how systematic modifications in molecular hydrophobicity affect adsorption and inhibition efficiency. Surfactant inhibitors with longer alkyl chains are known to form more compact protective films29, but this relationship has not been evaluated under the extremely aggressive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%{H}_{2}S{O}_{4}$$\end{document} conditions used in industrial practice.

In the present study, three organic surfactant molecules with varying hydrophilic and hydrophobic characteristics are synthesized, characterized, and tested as corrosion inhibitors for casing carbon steel P110 in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%{H}_{2}S{O}_{4}$$\end{document} solution. The ability of these quaternary ammonium copolymers (AMCs) to prevent corrosion was investigated at different concentrations and ambient and high temperatures in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%{H}_{2}S{O}_{4}$$\end{document} medium, representing the harsh conditions of a well during production. The findings of this research can aid the industry in identifying and formulating corrosion inhibitors based on surfactants most suitable for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%{H}_{2}S{O}_{4}$$\end{document} conditions. By gaining a deeper understanding of the mechanisms through which surfactants interact with metal surfaces in acidic conditions, more efficient and long-lasting corrosion inhibition techniques can be developed. In oil and gas operations where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%{H}_{2}S{O}_{4}$$\end{document} acidization is necessary; this understanding can lead to improved integrity, reduced maintenance costs, and enhanced safety.

Materials and methods

Material, electrolyte, and inhibitor concentrations

Industrial grade P110 carbon steel was used as the working material for all electrochemical (OCP, EIS and PDP) analyses. The chemical composition of P110 is evaluated with ICP-MS. Prior to ICP-MS analysis, the P110 carbon steel samples were digested using a microwave-assisted acid digestion method. Approximately 0.1 g of finely ground steel was placed in a Teflon digestion vessel, followed by the addition of a mixture of concentrated nitric acid (HNO3) and hydrochloric acid (HCl) in a 3:1 ratio. The vessels were sealed and subjected to controlled microwave heating to ensure complete dissolution of metallic constituents. After cooling, the digested solutions were diluted with deionized water to a known volume and filtered before being introduced into the ICP-MS system for elemental analysis. The synthesized AMC inhibitors belong to the class of quaternary ammonium–based cationic surfactants/copolymers, a chemical family known to be highly water-soluble even in strongly acidic media. Previous studies have demonstrated that such compounds remain fully soluble and chemically stable in concentrated acid solutions (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$15\%HCl$$\end{document} or acidic chloride solutions) and can be prepared as aqueous stock solutions for corrosion testing without use of co-solvents or heating. In our experiments, we dissolved the inhibitors directly in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\% {\mathrm{H}}_{2} {\mathrm{SO}}_{4}$$\end{document} and observed complete dissolution, consistent with the behavior reported in the literature28,30,32.

Density Functional Theory (DFT) was also utilized to perform comprehensive geometrical optimization of the examined inhibitors. The calculations employed Becke’s three-parameter exchange functional in conjunction with the Lee–Yang–Parr nonlocal correlation functional (B3LYP) and a 6-31G (d, p) basis set. These computations were executed using the Gaussian 03 program package. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) study

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is essential for understanding corrosion mechanisms and electrochemical reaction kinetics43. It also aids in extracting data about the interface between the metal surface and solution, as well as the impact of the inhibitor on the electric double layer. The electric double layer arises from species accumulation at the metal-solution interface during corrosion, influencing charge transfer between anodic and cathodic sites, thereby impacting corrosion mechanisms43. Figures 4 and 5 present the Nyquist and Bode (frequency and phase angle) diagrams for P110 CS corrosion in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%{H}_{2}S{O}_{4}$$\end{document}, both with and without AMCs at various concentrations. The Nyquist plots reveal a single semicircle in both conditions, indicating that the corrosion involves a single charge transfer process. Similarly, the Bode phase angle plots display a single maximum, further confirming a single charge transfer mechanism. The Bode frequency curves, with and without inhibitors, show the dynamic behavior of the corrosion processes.

Nyquist plots for P110 carbon steel corrosion in the absence and presence of different concentrations of a AMC-1, b AMC-2 and c AMC-3.

Corrosion inhibitors significantly impact Bode plots, especially in the low-frequency range, by altering corrosion mechanisms and promoting the formation of protective layers on the metal surface. Detailed analysis of the Bode phase angle graphs reveals that, in the absence of AMCs, the phase angle value is lower compared to when AMCs are present. A smaller phase angle typically indicates a rougher and more corroded surface. Without AMC molecules, the P110 CS surface appears more corroded and damaged due to attacks by free acids. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy data were analyzed using equivalent circuit fitting to extract charge-transfer resistance and constant phase element parameters that characterize the interfacial electrochemical behavior. The fitting results were used to assess relative changes in interfacial resistance and capacitive response with inhibitor concentration and temperature. The extracted parameters show consistent and physically meaningful trends across all tested conditions and are in good agreement with the corrosion behavior inferred from potentiodynamic polarization, adsorption analysis, and surface characterization.

Thermodynamic and kinetic studies

The corrosion inhibition of P110 carbon steel in sulfuric acid solution by inhibitors can be explained through molecular adsorption44. This process is influenced by the chemical structure of the organic compounds, the charge distribution within the molecules, the nature and surface charge of the metal, and the type of aggressive media involved45. Adsorption isotherms provide fundamental insights into the interaction between the inhibitors and the steel surface, as well as the interactions among the adsorbed molecules themselves and with the electrode surface46. In this study, the adsorption behavior of AMCs on the P110 CS surface in a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%{H}_{2}S{O}_{4}$$\end{document} solution was assessed using these isotherm models. The fitting parameters for these isotherm models, comprising intercept, slope, and regression coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}^{2}$$\end{document}) values, are tabulated in Table 4, and various tested isotherm models are depicted in Fig. 6a-d. To determine the most suitable isotherm model for the adsorption of AMCs on the P110 CS surface in a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%{H}_{2}S{O}_{4}$$\end{document} solution, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}^{2}$$\end{document} values were utilized. Given that the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}^{2}$$\end{document} values for this model were closest to unity, the results indicate that the Langmuir isotherm exhibited the best fit for all the investigated inhibitor compounds.

The Langmuir isotherm equation is given by the following formula33,435\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${K}_{ads}C=\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}$$\end{document}

Although the Langmuir adsorption isotherm provided the highest correlation coefficient among the tested models, the slope of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C/\theta$$\end{document}versus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C$$\end{document}plot deviates from unity, indicating non-ideal adsorption behavior. Such deviation suggests that the adsorption of AMCs on the P110 carbon steel surface does not strictly satisfy the assumptions of an ideal Langmuir isotherm, which include homogeneous surface sites, monolayer coverage, and the absence of lateral interactions between adsorbed species. In strongly acidic environments, adsorption of organic inhibitors is often influenced by surface heterogeneity, multi-site adsorption, and intermolecular interactions within the adsorbed layer, which can lead to apparent slopes significantly different from unity. Therefore, the Langmuir isotherm is used here as an approximate model to describe the dominant adsorption trend rather than as evidence of ideal Langmuir behavior. The results indicate predominantly adsorption-controlled inhibition, while acknowledging the non-ideal nature of the inhibitor–surface interactions.

Electrochemical studies were conducted at different temperatures to assess the impact of temperature on the corrosion inhibition potential of AMCs. Increasing the temperature can influence the kinetics of the interaction between corrosive species and inhibitor molecules by enhancing their mobility47. Some organic inhibitors may perform better at higher temperatures due to improved adsorption onto the metal surface, while others may degrade thermally or exhibit less efficient adsorption48. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of temperature-dependent behavior is essential to optimize the effectiveness of organic inhibitors and develop protective strategies for metal substrates under various operational conditions. DFT study and mechanism of corrosion inhibition

The frontier molecular orbital pictures (FMOs: optimized, HOMO, and LUMO) are presented in Fig. 9, and corresponding DFT-based parameters can be found in our previous publication30,31. This is well established that organic corrosion inhibitors become effective by adsorbing on the metal surface. Adsorption can proceed by chemisorption (sharing unshared electron pairs of heteroatoms and π-electrons of multiple bonds with metallic d-orbitals57,58 and/or physisorption (occurs by the electrostatic force of attraction between the charged metal surface and charged inhibitor molecules); our data indicate a predominantly mixed physicochemisorption mode.

Fig. 9Frontier molecular orbitals pictures of cationic nitrogen-based surfactant molecules evaluated in the presented study.

Frontier molecular orbitals pictures of cationic nitrogen-based surfactant molecules evaluated in the presented study.

The careful inspection of the chemical structure of the studied molecules suggests that the long alkyl chain contains quaternary nitrogen without any hydrogen. This observation shows that the side chain cannot be deprotonated in the electrolyte and will interact with the metallic surface only through physisorption or electrostatic force of attraction with the metallic substrate. Nevertheless, the quaternary nitrogen atoms of both rings in all three inhibitors contain at least one hydrogen that can easily undergo electrolyte-mediated deprotonation. The deprotonated nitrogen atoms with free unshared electron pairs can transfer their unshared electron pairs to the metallic d-orbital through chemisorption or coordination bonding. Therefore, the three surfactant molecules interact with the P110 CS surface through a physicochemisorption mode (Fig. 10). The order of inhibition efficiency, i.e., AMC-3 > AMC-2 > AMC-1, can be justified by increasing hydrophobic characteristics. The AMC-3 manifests the best hydrophobic character due to the presence of two long alkyl chains that protect from aggressive attacks. Conversely, the lowest effective protection of AMC-1 can be justified due to the lowest hydrophobic character.

Figure 10 shows the adsorption mechanism in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\% {\mathrm{H}}_{2} {\mathrm{SO}}_{4}$$\end{document}, the steel surface rapidly forms an anion-rich interface where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{HSO}}_{4}^{ - } {\mathrm{/SO}}_{4}^{{2 - }}$$\end{document} accumulate, and the positively charged AMC molecules are drawn to this layer and first anchor by electrostatic ion-pairing (physisorption), adopting an orientation that lays their heteroatoms and π-systems near the metal. At cathodic micro-sites, local alkalization during \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{H}}_{2}$$\end{document} evolution can partially deprotonate protonated nitrogen centers, generating neutral lone-pair donors that form Fe←N coordination bonds and, together with possible π–d interactions, convert the weakly bound state into stronger chemisorption. As adsorption proceeds, the AMC long alkyl chains pack laterally, creating a compact hydrophobic film that limits access of water and aggressive ions to the surface. This mixed physico-chemisorption layer reduces the number of electrochemically active sites and impedes charge transfer, which manifests experimentally as increased \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{ct}$$\end{document} decreased effective \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{dl}$$\end{document} (via CPE behavior), and a marked drop in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${i}_{corr}$$\end{document}; consequently, both anodic iron dissolution and cathodic hydrogen evolution are simultaneously retarded, consistent with a mixed-type inhibition mechanism.

Conclusions

In this study, three nitrogen-based cationic surfactant molecules (AMCs) for P110 carbon steel (P110 CS) in 20% H2SO4 solution are shown to have the ability to suppress corrosion. The following conclusions were reached:
