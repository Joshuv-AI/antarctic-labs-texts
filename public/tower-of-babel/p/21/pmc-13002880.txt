Current treatment algorithm: diffuse large B cell lymphoma
Authors: Stephen M. Ansell, Grzegorz S. Nowakowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is an aggressive B-cell malignancy and is the most common subtype of lymphoma. Treatment is administered with curative intent and approximately two thirds of patients are expected to have durable long-term survival. To achieve this, anthracycline-based chemotherapy in combination with rituximab is typically administered as initial therapy. Management is optimized based on the disease stage, prognostic clinical features, and histological or molecular subclassification. In patients with the activated B-cell subtype of DLBCL, polatuzumab vedotin is commonly included in the combination. For those with Myc and BCL-2 rearrangements, a more treatment intense approach is used. Despite this risk-adapted approach, at least one third of patients relapse. Those who relapse within 1 year, or are resistant to initial therapy typically receive chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy. For those relapsing more than a year post initial treatment, salvage chemotherapy followed by an autologous stem cell transplant is offered. In patients ineligible for cellular therapy, or those who progress after CAR T-cell treatment, management is palliative and includes administration of bispecific antibodies or antibody drug conjugate combinations. To further improve the outcome of DLBCL patients, incorporation of cellular and bispecific therapies into front-line treatment is currently being tested.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common subtype of B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) and accounts for approximately 30% of all lymphomas. DLBCL is made up of a variety of entities, with DLBCL-not otherwise unspecified (NOS) as the most common subtype [1–3]. For decades, chemoimmunotherapy with R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) has been the standard initial DLBCL treatment [4, 5]. Treatment with R-CHOP has resulted in durable long-term survival for approximately 60% of patients. In recent years, however, improvements in the molecular classification and subtyping of DLBCL [6–9], as well as the incorporation of a novel antibody-drug conjugate therapy into front-line treatment, has further improved the outcome of DLBCL patients [10].

Diagnostic considerations and risk stratification

Treatment selection for this disease is refined by clinical prognostic factors, as well as additional histological and molecular subclassification [6, 8, 9, 11]. The International Prognostic Index (IPI) identifies clinical factors associated with a poor outcome—including age > 60 years, advanced stage of disease, poor performance status, elevated serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and involvement of multiple extranodal sites—and incorporates them into a prognostic score [11]. Patients with a high IPI score are commonly considered for more aggressive therapy and possible participation in clinical trials, while those with a low score typically receive standard R-CHOP chemotherapy. It is also recognized that patients with rapid progression of disease that require urgent initiation of therapy have inferior outcomes [12].

DLBCL is also characterized by significant biological heterogeneity. Initial work using gene expression profiling identified specific subtypes of DLBCL related to the cell of origin [6, 7]. Based on genotyping of the normal counterparts in lymphoid cell development, a germinal center B-cell (GCB)-like subtype and an activated B-cell (ABC)-like subtype were identified. A third smaller population of cases had a gene expression profile that was not linked to either of these subtypes. Patients with the ABC subtype typically have inferior outcomes compared to the other two types [13]. While gene expression profiling remains the gold standard for identifying the subtypes, an immunohistochemical algorithm, the Hans algorithm, is widely used to identify these subtypes but may misclassify appropriately 20% of cases [14]. Additionally, a more aggressive subtype has been identified based on Myc and BCL-2 rearrangements and is typically identified by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis[15]. This lymphoma may exhibit more aggressive clinical behavior and is known as a “double-hit” lymphoma [16]. Recent studies have further identified an additional subtype derived from a cell of origin. The “dark zone” lymphoma is an umbrella term for lymphomas that phenocopy B-cells from the germinal center dark zone [17]. This includes DLBCL cases but also includes other high-grade lymphomas, including Burkitt lymphoma.

A more comprehensive analysis of molecular alterations in DLBCL has led to further molecular classifications of the disease [8, 9]. Using whole exome sequencing, DNA copy number analysis, and targeted amplicon sequencing, additional distinct genetic subclasses of DLBCL have been identified. This molecular classification has provided further information into the heterogeneous pathobiology of the disease and has identified subgroups with a poorer prognosis. Furthermore, these classifiers have identified signaling pathways that are unique to each subgroup, and which potentially could be targeted therapeutically. Recent clinical trials incorporating small molecule inhibitors or antibody drug conjugates have shown that certain of these unique molecular subtypes of DLBCL specifically benefit when targeted therapies are added [18, 19].

Practically, for the treating clinician, it is valuable to know whether a newly diagnosed patient with DLBCL has the GCB or ABC (non-GCB) phenotype. As outlined below (as shown in Fig. 1), most patients with DLBCL—GCB subtype, can be treated with standard R-CHOP chemotherapy with a good clinical outcome. Those with DLBCL—ABC subtype, are more typically treated with the antibody-drug conjugate, polatuzumab vedotin, added to R-CHP chemotherapy. Additionally, it is also important to do FISH analysis to identify DLBCL patients with Myc and BCL 2 rearrangements. These patients are commonly treated with more intensive regimens such as dose adjusted (DA) R-EPOCH (rituximab, etoposide, prednisone, vincristine, cyclophosphamide, and doxorubicin) or Burkitt-like regimen like CODOX-M/R-IVAC (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, methotrexate, ifosfamide, etoposide, cytarabine and rituximab) [20, 21]. Furthermore, the IPI score is helpful in selecting treatment. Patients with low-risk disease (IPI 0-1) typically have excellent outcomes when treated with standard R-CHOP chemotherapy, while those with high-risk disease (IPI 2-5) may benefit when additional agents, like polatuzumab vedotin, are added to R-CHOP. Initial therapy

Limited stage disease

Approximately a third of patients present with limited stage DLBCL, defined as stage I or II disease without substantial disease bulk [22]. The overall 5-year survival of these patients is approximately 85% and the goal of therapy has been to decrease the amount of treatment and thereby minimize toxicity associated with prolonged chemotherapy courses or the use of radiation treatment [23, 24]. In limited-stage, younger patients ( ≤ 60 years), with low-risk disease based on the IPI, the phase 3 FLYER trial showed that 4 cycles of R-CHOP chemotherapy followed by 2 additional doses of rituximab was sufficient therapy when compared to 6 cycles of R-CHOP chemotherapy [25]. Also, a National Clinical Trials Network (NCTN) trial of DLBCL patients with non-bulky (less than 10 cm) stage I and II disease tested the use of a PET scan to limit therapy [26]. In this study, patients who were interim PET negative received 1 additional cycle of R-CHOP and were then observed. Patients with a positive interim PET scan received involved field radiation therapy and radioimmunotherapy. The outcome in both groups was similar, and the study concluded that R-CHOP for 4 cycles, particularly in PET negative patients, was an acceptable standard approach.

Advanced stage disease

Over the past 20 years, R-CHOP chemotherapy for 6 cycles has been the standard of care for patients with advanced stage DLBCL [4, 5]. Multiple clinical trials have evaluated the addition of a new drug to the combination but have failed to show that the addition of these agents improved patient outcome [27–29]. Recently however, the POLARIX study demonstrated an improved progression-free survival (PFS) when polatuzumab vedotin was added to R-CHP chemotherapy (vincristine omitted due to overlapping toxicity with polatuzumab vedotin) [10]. The patients receiving Pola-R-CHP had an approximately 6% improvement in PFS compared to those treated with R-CHOP, however there was no improvement in overall survival. When patients in this trial were evaluated by cell of origin using gene expression profiling, those with the ABC phenotype appeared to benefit, while benefit was not seen in the GCB subtype. Recent data have suggested that molecular subclassification further identifies the patients most likely to benefit from Pola-R-CHP [30]. Patients with DLBCL in molecular cluster 5 (C5- also known as MCD and typically having MYD88 and CD79B mutations) saw a substantial improvement in PFS when treated with Pola-R-CHP, while the difference in outcome was not seen in the other molecular subtypes. In patients with Myc and BCL-2 rearrangements, conventional R-CHOP chemotherapy has been insufficient therapy, and it is not clear that Pola-R-CHP is the optimal approach either. In these patients, administration of a more intensive regimen such as DA-R-EPOCH or CODOX-M/IVAC-R is preferred [20, 21].

Practical approach to initial therapy

While enrollment in a clinical trial is always recommended, standard treatment outside of a clinical study should be based on the stage of disease, the presence or absence of clinical high-risk factors, and the pathological/molecular subclassification of the disease. For patients with limited-stage, non-bulky disease and low-risk by the IPI, 4 cycles of R-CHOP chemotherapy, provided the interim PET scan is negative, is sufficient treatment. For patients with bulky disease or those with a positive interim PET scan, 6 cycles of treatment should be given and consolidation radiation therapy considered. For patients with advanced-stage disease, those with low-risk DLBCL (IPI 0-1) and those with the GCB subtype have very good outcomes when treated with 6 cycles of standard R-CHOP treatment. The patients with the ABC subtype should get 6 cycles of Pola-R-CHP chemotherapy. Those with Myc and BCL-2 rearrangements, as well as those identified by the “dark zone” signature (if this analysis can be performed), should receive a more intensive regimen such as DA-R-EPOCH for 6 cycles (Fig. 1).

Relapsed disease

While approximately 60% of DLBCL patients will have long-term freedom from disease progression after initial treatment [31], those who relapse are at risk for a poor clinical outcome and death [32]. Historically, these patients typically received additional salvage chemotherapy followed by autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) if the disease was chemosensitive [33]. More recently, the use of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has been shown to be more effective than ASCT, particularly in patients who are primarily refractory to treatment or those who progress early [34, 35]. However, not all patients are able to proceed to CAR T-cell therapy or receive ASCT due to comorbidities or lack of access to specialized care [36]. In clinical practice therefore, it is important to determine goals of therapy—a curative versus palliative approach—and the clinical condition of the patient—fit versus frail. Additionally, the timing of disease progression also influences treatment choice (see Fig. 1).

Early progression (< 1year from diagnosis)

For patients with primary refractory disease or those who is disease progresses within 1 year of diagnosis, the treatment of choice is to utilize CAR T-cell therapy. Axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi-cel) and lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso-cel) are both approved treatments for these patients based on the ZUMA-7 and TRANSFORM clinical trials, respectively [34, 35, 37, 38]. In both studies, CD19 directed CAR T-cell therapy was compared to standard of care treatment with high-dose chemotherapy followed by ASCT. In these studies, the patients receiving CAR T-cell therapy had improved event free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS) compared to ASCT. However, manufacture of CAR T-cells may take several weeks and there is a risk that the disease will progress [39]. For these patients, bridging therapy is commonly given to maintain disease control. Bridging therapy may take the form of localized radiation therapy or combination chemoimmunotherapy utilizing antibody-drug conjugates and chemotherapy [40, 41]. The administration of CAR T-cell therapy in this context results in a complete response rate of approximately 75-80% with a 4-year PFS of approximately 40% [34, 38].

Late progression (> 1 year from diagnosis)

The randomized trials comparing CAR T-cell therapy to high-dose therapy and ASCT did not evaluate patients who progressed later than 1 year post diagnosis. These patients with a later relapse constitute a lower risk population, and high-dose therapy followed by ASCT remains the standard of care for this group [33]. These patients typically receive second-line chemoimmunotherapy regimens with the dual aim of reducing the disease burden prior to proceeding to ASCT, as well as determining sensitivity of the lymphoma to chemotherapy. Various salvage regimens have been used prior to ASCT but most are a platinum-containing regimen [42]. Typical combinations are dexamethasone, cytarabine, and cisplatin (DHAP) [43]; ifosfamide, carboplatin, and etoposide (ICE) [44]; gemcitabine, dexamethasone, and cisplatin (GDP) [45]; or gemcitabine and oxaliplatin (GemOx) [46]; each given in combination with rituximab. The overall response rates with these treatments range from 40 to 65%, with a complete response rate of 20–30%. No superiority has been identified for one regimen over the others, and institutional preference as well as the toxicity profile are typically used to select the salvage regimen. Lack of chemosensitivity is an established adverse prognostic factor for patients with DLBCL and so patients need to demonstrate at least a partial response to salvage therapy to receive an ASCT [33].

Third line strategies

CAR T-cell therapies, including axi-cel, liso-cel, and tisagenlecleucel (tisa-cel), are all approved therapies in patients who have received at least 2 prior lines of treatment [47–49]. Each of these products result in high response rates in patients with relapsed and refractory DLBCL and the responses are durable in a subset of patients. If a patient with DLBCL has not yet received CAR T-cell therapy after 2 prior lines of treatment, CAR T-cell treatment would be the treatment of choice. Patients who are ineligible for CAR T-cell therapy, as well as those who relapse after these treatments or after ASCT, have historically poor long-term outcomes [32]. However, new drugs have been recently approved for these patients, and these agents commonly result in durable clinical benefit and even an overall survival advantage when used in combination with other standard therapies. Selection of a third line treatment is driven by which treatment was given in earlier lines of treatment, potential lingering toxicities from prior therapy, tolerability of treatment, and expression of the target of the treatment in the tumor.

Antibodies and antibody drug conjugates

Multiple antibody-based therapies have been approved in the third line for patients with DLBCL. Tafasitamab is a glycoengineered monoclonal antibody targeting CD19 and is typically given in combination with lenalidomide resulting in responses in 60% of patients [50]. Loncastuximab teserine is a CD19-targeted antibody drug conjugate with a pyrrolobenzodiazepine payload that results in overall response rate of ~50% [51]. Polatuzumab vedotin is a monoclonal antibody targeting CD79b coupled to monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) that improves the overall survival of relapsed and refractory DLBCL patients when given in combination with bendamustine plus rituximab [52]. Similarly, brentuximab vedotin is a CD30 antibody-drug conjugate, also conjugated to MMAE, that improves the overall survival of DLBCL patients when combined with lenalidomide plus rituximab [53]. The choice between these therapies is largely driven by how many doses of treatment are planned, as tolerability may limit the duration of therapy.

Bispecific antibodies

Bispecific antibodies are a novel class of immunotherapy with substantial efficacy in relapsed and refractory DLBCL, including patients who relapse after CAR T-cell therapy. Currently approved bispecific antibodies combine two different monospecific antigen binding domains into a single antibody-derived molecule with dual specificity. Clinically approved bispecific antibodies in DLBCL target CD20 on B-cells and CD3 on T-cells, promoting an immune synapse and inducing T-cell cytotoxicity against malignant B-cells. Glofitamab is a bispecific antibody in a 2:1 configuration with bivalency for CD20 and monovalency for CD3. Fixed duration therapy with glofitamab results in durable responses, with a complete response rate of approximately 40% in DLBCL patients refractory to multiple lines of treatment [54]. Notably, a similar response rate was seen in patients who had previously received CAR T-cell therapy [55]. Epcoritamab is a full-length IgG1 bispecific antibody that also targets CD20 and CD3 and is administered via subcutaneous injection. Epcoritamab has also proven to be highly effective in relapsed and refractory DLBCL patients, including those previously receiving CAR T-cell treatment, with an overall response rate of 54% and a complete response rate of ~35% [56]. Bispecific antibodies can be given as monotherapy to maintain a remission in relapsed and refractory DLBCL or alternatively as a bridging to other therapy such as CAR T-cell treatment. The selection of which bispecific antibody to use is typically driven by the anticipated duration of treatment and the preferred route of administration.

Sequencing of therapies in relapsed and refractory DLBCL

Multiple factors need to be considered when choosing which treatment to give at what time, as treatments given in front-line and as salvage therapy will impact treatment choices offered to patients with relapsed and refractory DLBCL. For example, if the patient received polatuzumab vedotin as part of front-line therapy, use of an antibody-drug conjugate such as brentuximab vedotin, with the same MMAE payload, in the relapsed and refractory setting may be less attractive due to increased toxicity. Concern has also been expressed about utilizing agents such as tafasitamab or loncastuximab tesirine before or after CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy, as tafasitamab, loncastuximab tesirine and CAR T-cell therapy all target CD19 on malignant B-cells, and downregulation of CD19 expression after one treatment may result in lack of efficacy of the next agent [57]. However, data does not support this, as responses have been maintained when these agents have been used before or after the other. Similarly, there has been concern that utilizing two immune-mediated therapies such as bispecific antibodies and CAR T-cell treatment would be ineffective if these treatments resulted in T-cells were immunological exhausted [58]. Again, this has not been shown to be true as bispecific antibodies and CAR T-cell therapy have both been effective when used after the other modality. The decision, therefore as to which treatment to select should be based on the patient’s clinical condition and their ability to tolerate either more intense or more prolonged courses of treatment.

Specific situations

Primary mediastinal B-cell lymphoma (PMBL)

PMBL is an uncommon but distinctive subtype of lymphoma that has a distinct molecular profile that is more like Hodgkin lymphoma than DLBCL [59]. While PMBL was previously felt to be a subtype of DLBCL, it is sufficiently different histologically and molecularly that the World Health Organization now classifies this lymphoma as a unique entity. Patients typically present with a mediastinal mass and are treated with chemoimmunotherapy (such as dose adjusted R-EPOCH or R-CHOP) [60, 61], however R-CHOP may be associated with lower complete response rates [60]. Consolidation with radiation therapy is not required but can be considered [62, 63]. Patients with relapsed and refractory PMBL may uniquely benefit from PD-1 blockade and studies are in progress to test immune checkpoint inhibitors in front-line therapy [64].

Testicular involvement

Primary testicular lymphoma is a rare form of NHL that presents with a testicular mass and is typically diagnosed by orchiectomy. Similar to other types of DLBCL, patients receive R-CHOP chemotherapy. However, there is a significant risk of recurrence in the contralateral testicle and external beam radiation therapy is commonly given to the contralateral testicle [65]. There is also a substantial risk of central nervous system (CNS) recurrence and CNS prophylaxis is routinely given [66].

Central nervous system involvement

Central nervous system (CNS) involvement by DLBCL is associated with a poor prognosis and can either be the only location of the disease (primary CNS lymphoma) or the CNS can be involved secondarily to disease at other sites in the body [67, 68]. The CNS prognostic model (CNS-IPI), that includes advanced age and stage, the number of extranodal sites, elevated LDH and performance status, as well as kidney or adrenal gland involvement, is predictive of CNS involvement and stratifies patients into 3 categories with 2-year rates of CNS relapse of 0.6%, 3.4% and 10.2%, respectively [69]. Induction therapy for primary CNS lymphoma relies on using high-dose methotrexate-based regimens that penetrate the blood-brain barrier [70]. This is followed by consolidation treatment such as an autologous stem cell transplant, maintenance methotrexate at a lower dose, or whole-brain radiation treatment [71]. Even with optimal therapy, the long term event-free survival ranges from 39 to 67%. Secondary involvement of the CNS requires treatment to target the systemic disease with the addition of high-dose methotrexate to treat the CNS[72]. CNS prophylaxis using high-dose methotrexate to prevent secondary involvement of the brain is controversial [73] and is usually restricted to patients with high-risk DLBCL [69].

Elderly or frail patients

While most older patients tolerate R-CHOP reasonably well, some DLBCL patients cannot receive standard immunochemotherapy due to comorbidities or vulnerabilities linked to aging. Using a reduced intensity R-CHOP regimen, commonly referred to as R-miniCHOP, is common in this patient population. The 2-year PFS with R-miniCHOP is ~50% and this regimen offers a good compromise between efficacy and safety in very elderly or frail patients [74].

Conclusion

Therapy for patients with DLBCL continues to improve and practitioners have combination therapies available to them that are highly effective. Salvage therapy, including immunological approaches and cellular therapies have profoundly impacted the outcome of patients with relapsed and refractory disease. Current clinical trials are testing the use of effective second-line and 3rd line agents in combination with front-line therapy to further improve the patient’s outcome.
