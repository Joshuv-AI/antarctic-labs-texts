An in-depth survey of immunosuppressive myeloid cell heterogeneity in gliomas
Authors: Brandon Emanuel León, Defne Bayik
Published: 2025

While immunotherapies have become standard of care in many solid tumors, this success has not translated to gliomas, the most common malignant brain tumors.1 Notably, glioma-associated myeloid cells (GAMs) can constitute up to half of the tumor mass and confer treatment resistance.2 Thus, targeting the GAMs represents a promising strategy to overcome immunotherapy resistance. However, earlier single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies revealed significant heterogeneity in GAM expression signatures.3 A comprehensive understanding of this immunosuppressive landscape remains elusive—yet is critical for reprogramming myeloid cells to improve immunotherapy responses.

In their recent publication, Miller, Farran, Couturier, and colleagues set out to redefine myeloid populations based on immunomodulatory gene expression signatures.4 The authors initially identified 4 myeloid cell lineage-independent transcriptional programs from scRNA-seq of primary and recurrent gliomas: systemic inflammatory, microglial inflammatory, complement immunosuppressive, and scavenger immunosuppressive programs. By comparing mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations between GAMs and matched peripheral blood circulating monocytes, the authors were able to infer the developmental origin of GAMs and demonstrate that these programs exist in a spectrum of both brain-resident and blood-derived myeloid populations. Pertinently, they found a subset of GAMs with microglial inflammatory programming harbored mtDNA mutations that matched peripheral circulating monocytes indicating that developmental origin is not the sole determining factor in GAM immunomodulatory programming.

In defining the clinical relevance of GAM programming, the authors observed that scavenger immunosuppressive and microglial inflammatory programs were only detected in primary brain tumors and absent in peripheral tumors and brain metastases. These two programs also correlated with glioma grade, outcomes, and treatment response, unlike the systemic inflammatory and complement immunosuppressive programs, which were also present in circulating myeloid cells from glioma patients and/or noncentral nervous system tumors. Specifically, scavenger immunosuppressive GAMs were associated with worse clinical outcomes and resistance to neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade, while GAMs with microglial inflammatory programming were enriched in lower grade gliomas regardless of isocitrate dehydrogenase mutation status. These findings suggested that microglial and scavenger programs could be shaped by the tumor microenvironment (TME) and prompted the authors to interrogate the local and systemic molecular drivers of different GAM programs.

The assessment of the chromatin landscape revealed increased accessibility to AP-1 motifs in GAMs characterized by the scavenger program. IL-1β and TGFβ, previously implicated in GAM-cancer interactions,5,6 also increased scavenger immunosuppressive programming in glioblastoma organoids (GBOs) by activating AP-1. Since AP-1 activity was shown to induce the expression of co-inhibitory immune checkpoint molecules,7 this could be a potential mechanism by which scavenger GAM program drives immunotherapy resistance. Considering this clinical relevance, the authors sought to inhibit AP-1 in infiltrating myeloid cells and observed that a selective p300/CBP bromodomain inhibitor dampened immunosuppressive scavenging programming through chromatin remodeling and by reducing AP-1 accessibility. While these findings shed light on the molecular mechanics of scavenger programming, the ligands and transcription factors shaping microglial inflammatory programs remain unknown. Spatial analysis revealed that microglial GAMs localized to inflammatory and vascular niches—but not hypoxic regions—suggesting that further comparisons of these niches across tumor grades could yield insights into microglial programming and their role in low- versus high-grade glioma TMEs.

Using spatial transcriptomics, the authors also interrogated the interaction between glioma-specific and ubiquitous myeloid cell programs. They identified that systemic inflammatory programming was enriched in hypoxic and inflammatory niches. This program was additionally characterized by IFNγ response and chromatin accessibility to NF-κB motifs, a central transcription factor regulating inflammatory responses. Hypoxia-driven inflammation is characteristic of many TMEs,8 aligned with the activation of systemic inflammatory in other cancers. However, scavenger immunosuppressive GAMs also localized in hypoxic niches, in addition to vascular niches, prompting the investigation of the potential link between scavenger-systemic programs. High expression of AP-1 activating ligands by systemic inflammatory GAMs implied that these cells may drive scavenger immunosuppressive programming in hypoxic regions. While subsequent studies are warranted to delineate the potential lineage association between these two programs, targeting AP-1 shifted GAM tone from scavenging immunosuppressive to systemic inflammatory programming.

As opposed to the systemic inflammatory and scavenger programs, the complement immunosuppressive program was excluded from hypoxic niches and not informed by cytokine response. Complement immunosuppressive programming is not restricted to gliomas and linked to immunosuppression in many cancers, such as lung malignancies.9 Notably, glioma patients exhibited a dose-dependent enrichment in complement immunosuppressive programming with dexamethasone (Dex) usage. Dex treatment also primed circulating myeloid cells to an irreversible complement immunosuppressive program. Induced by glucocorticoid receptor (GR) signaling, this program was retained in Dex-treated GBO infiltrating-myeloid cells even when treated with IFNy, the driver of the systemic inflammatory GAMs. Importantly, earlier clinical evidence indicated that concurrent administration of Dex and PD-L1 blockade led to poorer survival in comparison to patients not treated with Dex.10 While the current study indicated that scavenger but not complement immunosuppressive program is linked to lack of anti-PD-1 response, these discrepancies indicate the immunotherapy agent (PD-1 vs PD-L1), and/or timing (neoadjuvant vs adjuvant) could be important considerations to determine the role of specific immunosuppressive myeloid programs in gliomas. Additionally, the current results suggest that targeting GR signaling could potentially overcome Dex-mediated immunosuppressive complement myeloid programs to improve the efficacy of immunotherapies.

Overall, this work provides a robust framework to further interrogate GAM heterogeneity and highlights the determinants of unique myeloid cell programs in gliomas. This study is limited to snapshots in GAM programming, and the analysis of the matched primary and recurrent tumors in future studies would be valuable to assess the reorganization of the niches and determine the effect of different GAM programs on cancer cells. These insights could guide the development of therapies that reprogram the TME, ultimately improving outcomes for glioma patients.
