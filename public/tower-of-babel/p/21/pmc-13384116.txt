Awareness, knowledge and attitudes towards artificial intelligence among nursing students: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Manal Nasser AL Ghazali, Muhammad Riaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInternationally, nursing students’ awareness and familiarity with artificial intelligence (AI) remain a challenge as evidenced by the current literature. Interestingly, the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) region has earned a strong standing for driving national digital transformation; however, this ambition has not been translated into research. Despite growing interest in AI-driven healthcare, empirical studies examining nursing students’ readiness in these countries to operate in healthcare environments remain limited, representing a critical gap in the literature. Our initial literature review found a high degree of heterogeneity among study designs, measurement tools and theoretical framing and highlighted an unequivocal need for a rigorous and systematic synthesis to uncover consistent patterns, methodological gaps and contextual factors that shape nursing students’ engagement with AI.This protocol aims to provide a structured plan for combining the current evidence on nursing students’ awareness, knowledge and attitudes regarding AI applications in nursing education.Methods and analysisThis protocol is prepared in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guidelines, and it is registered with the international Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO). A comprehensive and systematic search of the literature will be undertaken across four key electronic databases: PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus and Web of Science, using Boolean search strings constructed from Medical Subject Headings-controlled terms and free-text keywords encompassing six predefined thematic domains: AI applications in nursing education, student awareness, attitudes, technology acceptance, adoption, ethical considerations and regional context. All studies published in English between January 2020 and June 2026 including cross-sectional, cohort, quasiexperimental and qualitative studies will be included in this review. The primary outcome is nursing students’ awareness of and attitudes toward AI; secondary outcomes include AI-related knowledge, behavioural intention and ethical concerns. A 41-item standardised form will be used for data extraction across all included studies, systematically capturing study characteristics, instruments, theoretical frameworks, barriers, facilitators and the outcomes of interest. To assess the studies’ quality, we will use the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools for quantitative and qualitative studies, ensuring a comprehensive and methodologically consistent appraisal process across all included studies. Narrative synthesis will be performed complemented by meta-analysis where applicable, organised by the construct domains and geographic regions. This protocol provides an in-depth, systematic review plan that will report the most thorough synthesis to date regarding nursing students’ awareness, knowledge levels and perceptions of the utilisation of AI within nursing education. The review will identify validated instruments for cross-cultural adaptation, establish benchmarks and estimate prevalence of awareness, knowledge and attitude. We will describe the theoretical and contextual contrived factors associated with these constructs in nursing students.Ethics and disseminationAs this systematic review is based exclusively on published literature and does not involve the collection of primary data from human participants or animals, formal ethical approval is not required. Findings from this review will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at relevant national and international nursing and healthcare conferences. The review is expected to generate evidence-based insights that will inform nursing curricula, guide institutional policy on AI integration and highlight the critical evidence gap in the GCC region, including Oman, thereby contributing to the advancement of AI-ready nursing education internationally. A key focus will be mapping geographic variation, with particular attention to the GCC region where empirical evidence remains sparse.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420261320108.

Background

In recent years, the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) as a fundamental factor in healthcare delivery has become increasingly evident, reshaping the landscape across clinical and educational settings.13 AI has become an essential tool in nursing education in recent years, and it will definitely be incorporated in nursing education and training in the upcoming years.4 5 AI is used today for most professional and academic purposes and has become a useful tool for nurses in the healthcare environment.1 3 Internationally, it has become obvious that AI is deeply used in medical education in recent years.68 AI technologies will play a part in improving clinical reasoning, personalising learning pathways and strengthening training skills in high fidelity.9 10 Different AI platforms and agents are widely used in many nursing programmes.1115 Gemini, ChatGPT, Claude and others, along with clinical supporting systems and simulation platforms, are now widely used in nursing education and clinical practice.11 12 16

AI technologies are used across multiple healthcare settings and the outcomes have been promising such as using AI in clinical decision support systems, prognostication and documentation using various tools.17 18 There is evidence supporting the use of these technologies to help achieve accurate clinical diagnoses, facilitate daily operations and improve overall patient care.1921 In the nursing sector, the applications of AI are wide, including continuous patient monitoring, risk-level classification, medication administration management and simulation-based clinical training2022. As healthcare systems worldwide use AI-enabled tools, technological competency has become inseparable from the demands of contemporary nursing practice.1 While the use of AI in nursing education offers significant opportunities for innovation in teaching and learning, it also presents major challenges for academic organisations regarding preparedness, infrastructure and faculty development.4 20 22 While current evidence suggests a moderately high level of AI awareness among nursing students, with mutual estimates approximating 73%, the actual curricular incorporation of AI-related content remains disjointed and inconsistent, underscoring a persistent and significant gap in nursing education preparedness.22 23

The utilisation of generative AI in education is rapidly changing learning environments, creating both new opportunities and significant challenges in nursing education.24 The growing integration of AI across the full spectrum of healthcare delivery, from diagnostics and treatment planning to patient monitoring, decision-making, delivering patient education and supporting operational proficiency, improves digital literacy, particularly AI literacy, which is now recognised as an important competency in healthcare.162530 Effective integration of AI into healthcare systems requires nursing students and emerging healthcare professionals to demonstrate adequate knowledge, positive attitudes and well-defined perceptions regarding the application and implications of AI in clinical practice.25 30

The situation is more complex than technological adoption alone; it includes preparing nursing students to be proficient, critically engaged and ethically aware AI users rather than just passive technology consumers.31 32 Without systematic, structured curricular preparation, students risk entering practice as technically capable users of AI tools, yet critically underprepared as AI-literate practitioners proficient in navigating AI interfaces.31 32 This readiness gap risks producing graduates who are capable AI-device manipulators but insufficiently literate AI residents, who are able to click but not to critique.32 This concern is important given that AI applications in nursing range from diagnostic tools and patient care management to clinical decision-making and evidence-based practice support.24 As AI’s inspiration continues to increase across healthcare settings and educational fields, a thorough understanding of students’ acceptance of AI in academic settings is expected to become increasingly significant.27 This knowledge is important for developing evidence-based nursing curricula and ensuring that upcoming health professionals are prepared enough to function effectively within AI-integrated healthcare practice settings.1 With AI integration in healthcare and education, understanding students’ acceptance of AI in academic settings has become increasingly important.11 31 33 Nursing education is not fully ready to adapt to the rapid growth and development of AI technologies.25 32 Evidence indicates that current nursing curricula do not regularly include scientifically AI-based content, and many health workers lack the foundational competencies necessary to engage with AI despite its increasing adoption.28 30 34 This gap is particularly evident in developing contexts and the Middle East, where research remains limited and largely devoid of theoretical integration.25 27 34

The acceptance and anxiety of nursing students toward these technologies remain underexplored, mainly in Middle Eastern and developing settings.1 34 Despite its transformative potential, mounting evidence indicates a persistent and concerning lack of preparedness among nursing students to engage with AI technologies, with uneven preparedness across geographical, institutional and cultural contexts.4 35 Integrating AI into nursing education remains an obstacle due to the rapid advancement of AI technology.36 The present curricula for nursing students often do not systematically contain AI-related content.23 There are still variations and skill mixes in the use of AI technologies among healthcare workers, despite their widespread use.29 Therefore, the psychological dimensions of AI engagement among nursing students have received increasing scholarly attention, with emerging evidence from the Middle East highlighting the complex interplay between anxiety and attitudes as determinants of technology acceptance.37 This gap is particularly noticeable in developing settings and Middle Eastern regions, where research remains sparse and insufficiently grounded in theory.38

The interplay between knowledge, attitudes and perceptions toward AI is a complex process, and it is context-sensitive, shaped by factors such as learning environments, technological exposure and sociocultural backgrounds.2 23 25 Clarifying these relationships is essential for developing effective educational interventions directed at fostering readiness and motivation to integrate AI into practice.33 39 New technologies always face challenges in the initial stage, and this requires one’s attitude and willingness, which the technology acceptance model (TAM) dictates.29 The TAM is well-suited to synthesising evidence on nursing students’ AI engagement as it offers a coherent theoretical framework relating perceived usefulness, attitudes and behavioural intention constructs that are constantly operationalised and measured across the reviewed studies.40 Furthermore, several studies in this review extended TAM by incorporating additional constructs such as AI anxiety, self-efficacy and social influence, reflecting the model’s flexibility and adaptability to the specific demands of technology adoption in healthcare education contexts. This theoretical versatility makes TAM a uniquely appropriate and robust framework for organising and interpreting the heterogeneous evidence base that characterises this field. Furthermore, factors such as technology-related anxiety and perceived barriers can substantially influence these attitudes and subsequent adoption intentions.1 2 4 Barriers to AI adoption in nursing are not just about knowledge gaps they are also about worry.41 Consequently, addressing nursing students’ awareness, attitudes, worry and behavioural intentions toward AI is of critical urgency for forthcoming research, as these concepts are vital to understanding and estimating their actual behaviours in AI-integrated nursing education and practice environments. Although global research on AI in healthcare education is expanding, evidence from Arab nursing students remains scarce, particularly in multicounty investigations. Additionally, few studies have applied standardised and unified instruments to assess intention and its related determinants across different contexts.23

It is very important to understand the nursing students’ awareness, knowledge of AI and their attitudes towards AI. Although the volume of research examining AI in nursing education has expanded considerably in recent years, substantial gaps remain that hinder a thorough understanding of nursing students’ awareness of and attitudes toward AI.26 While some systematic reviews have examined AI literacy and competency across nursing populations,14 15 24 28 42 no comprehensive systematic review has specifically focused on the intersection of awareness and attitudes among nursing students as a distinct population. Nursing students represent a critical cohort whose formative educational experiences will shape the future nursing workforce’s capacity to engage with AI technologies.15 20 24 Therefore, a thorough systematic synthesis is essential to recognise consistent patterns, methodological gaps and contextual determinants that prompt nursing students’ engagement with AI. We made this protocol to guide a structured systematic review assessing nursing students’ awareness, knowledge and attitudes regarding AI use in nursing education and clinical practice.

This systematic review, with a possible meta-analysis of where adequate homogeneity exists among the studies that will be included, will comprehensively examine and synthesise the available evidence on nursing students’ utilisation of AI in education. While global evidence on AI in nursing education is expanding rapidly, no systematic review has specifically focused on nursing students in the Arab Middle East or Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, where national digital transformation strategies most notably Oman Vision 2040, Saudi Vision 2030 and the UAE National AI Strategy 2031 have not yet been matched by empirical research on workforce AI readiness. This disconnect between policy ambition and research evidence represents a critical and urgent gap that this review is uniquely positioned to address. This systematic review will address four questions: (1) what is the prevalence of AI awareness and knowledge among nursing students globally? (2) What are nursing students’ attitudes, acceptance levels and readiness towards AI in education and practice? (3) What barriers, facilitators and contextual factors influence their engagement with AI? (4) How do these findings vary by geographic region, particularly in the GCC and Middle East?

Methods

This protocol was established in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 statement,43 44 (online supplemental table 1), and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology45 for systematic reviews of prevalence and incidence. Our protocol is registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO: CRD420261320108).46

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies and setting

Studies using quantitative, qualitative or mixed research methodologies to examine knowledge, awareness and attitudes towards the application of AI-driven tools in nursing education will be considered for inclusion. This review imposes no geographic or contextual restrictions on study eligibility. Research conducted in any country may qualify for inclusion, provided it meets all pre-established inclusion criteria established prior to the initiation of the review. Notably, only English studies will be included. Study selection will follow the Population, Intervention/Interest, Context, Outcome and Study design framework.47 The step-by-step process of including and excluding studies covering all phases of screening and eligibility evaluation will be documented and illustrated in a systematic flowchart to maintain transparency and ensure reproducibility (figure 1).48

Studies will be included if they meet the following inclusion criteria:

Population

Studies will be eligible for inclusion if their participants are of any gender, aged 18 years or older. The population of interest comprises undergraduate and postgraduate nursing students enrolled in accredited nursing education programmes. Registered nurses enrolled in formal AI-focused continuing education or professional development programmes (eg, structured coursework, certificate programmes) will also be eligible for inclusion, provided their educational context mirrors that of nursing students. A pre-specified subgroup analysis will compare findings between nursing students and registered nurses in continuing education to assess potential population-level heterogeneity and ensure transparency in synthesis.

Interest/intervention

Studies that assess one or more of the following dimensions in the context of AI technologies: awareness, knowledge, literacy, attitudes, perceptions, acceptance, readiness, behavioural intention, anxiety or ethical awareness. The magnitude of AI technologies considered includes a broad range, including generative AI, machine learning applications, clinical decision-support systems, AI-driven simulations, natural language processing tools and intelligent tutoring systems.

Context

Worldwide, diverse nursing education settings comprise university academic programmes, clinical practice placements, simulation and skills-based training centres and continuing professional development platforms.

Study designs

This review will consider studies of all designs reporting quantitative and qualitative research such as cross-sectional surveys, cohort, randomised controlled trials and quasiexperimental studies. If inclusion criteria are met, studies reported in previous reviews will also be included. Editorials, conference abstracts and opinion papers will be excluded, as they do not provide primary or synthesised empirical evidence consistent with the objectives of this review.

Date range: January 2020–June 2026.

Language: English only.

Outcomes measure

The primary outcome of systematic review and meta-analysis will be nursing students’ AI awareness and attitudes towards AI technologies in nursing education and clinical practice. The secondary outcomes include nursing students’ AI-related knowledge and intention to adopt and use AI technologies in nursing education and clinical practice, as well as the directional and associative relationships among these constructs, collectively informing a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing AI engagement among nursing students.

Literature search methods

Electronic searches

A detailed and thorough review of existing literature will be carried out using four major databases selected for their applicability to nursing, healthcare and educational research: A systematic search will be conducted across six electronic databases: PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, ERIC and PsycINFO. PubMed and CINAHL were selected for their comprehensive coverage of nursing and health sciences literature; Scopus and Web of Science provide broad multidisciplinary indexing; ERIC was included to capture educational research on AI integration in nursing curricula and PsycINFO was included to capture studies examining psychological constructs including attitudes, behavioural intention and technology acceptance among nursing students. Studies published in English will be included regardless of geographic location or publication date. The search strategy employed Boolean operators combining Medical Subject Headings terms49 (online supplemental table 2) and free-text keywords across six thematic domains50: (1) AI in nursing education (eg, “Artificial Intelligence” AND “Education, Nursing”; (2) awareness and AI literacy; (3) attitudes, perceptions and acceptance; (4) readiness and adoption; (5) ethical considerations and (6) regional/cultural context (Gulf states, Middle East, Arab countries). Additional search strategies will include backward and forward citation chaining from key references, hand-searching of Nurse Education Today, BMC Nursing and JMIR Nursing, and grey literature searches of the WHO, Oman Ministry of Health and Sultan Qaboos University institutional repositories.

Manual search

In addition to the electronic database search, a comprehensive examination of the reference lists of all incorporated studies and pertinent published systematic reviews will be conducted manually to identify any additional eligible articles. All retrieved records will first undergo screening by revising their titles and abstracts to determine their eligibility for inclusion based on predefined criteria. In addition to the above-mentioned databases searches, key journals including Nurse Education Today, BMC Nursing and JMIR Nursing will be hand-searched to identify any relevant studies not captured through database searches. Hand-searching will cover the past 5 years (2020–2026) of each journal, encompassing all issues published within this period.

Screening and selection

Two independent reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts, followed by full-text evaluation of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements at the title/abstract or full-text screening stage will first be resolved through discussion between the two reviewers; if consensus cannot be reached, a third independent reviewer will arbitrate and make the final inclusion decision. Complete manuscripts of the potentially eligible studies will be retrieved and evaluated by the reviewers. The selection process will be documented using a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.51 Once eligible studies are identified, all citations will be imported into the bibliographic management software EndNote X9 to facilitate the detection and removal of duplicate records (EndNote X9). To ensure rigorous and transparent reporting, the study selection process will be comprehensively demonstrated using a PRISMA flow diagram (figure 2), as recommended in51, specifying the rationale for inclusion and exclusion decisions.48 In addition, a summary table outlining the characteristics of the included studies will be created.

Data extraction

Relevant and comprehensive data will be systematically extracted from studies considered eligible based on the defined inclusion criteria, guaranteeing robust and evidence-based information.52 Data will be extracted using a standardised 41-item form developed specifically for this review, covering study characteristics, population details, AI tools examined, outcome measures and key findings. Where critical data including effect sizes, psychometric properties or disaggregated subgroup findings are missing or insufficiently reported in published reports, corresponding authors will be contacted via institutional email with up to two follow-up reminders sent at 2-week intervals. If no response is received after the second reminder, the study will be retained in the review with the missing data clearly noted in the data extraction table and acknowledged as a limitation50 capturing: study identifiers (author, year, country, journal, DOI); study design and statistical methods; sample characteristics (size, demographics, academic year); regional and cultural context; AI applications studied; curriculum integration status; measurement instruments with psychometric properties (items, Cronbach’s α); theoretical framework; barriers and facilitators (individual, organisational, technical, ethical/legal, policy); intervention details where applicable; significant predictors with effect sizes (regression coefficient-β, OR, 95% CI, p values) and outcomes across four construct domains (awareness and knowledge, attitudes, behavioural intention, performance). The extraction form will be piloted on five studies and refined iteratively.53

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of all included studies will be assessed using validated, purpose-built appraisal instruments appropriate to each study design. Quantitative observational studies will be evaluated using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist, with applicable items, each item rated as: yes, no, unclear or not applicable. While qualitative studies will be appraised using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research.45 54 Quality scores will be categorised as high (≥75%), moderate (50–74%) or low (<50%) of total applicable items met. No studies will be excluded solely on the basis of quality assessment; however, quality ratings will systematically inform the synthesis of evidence, guide the interpretation of findings and be reported transparently throughout the review.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Included studies characteristics will be extracted and presented in a table. A structured narrative assessment will be done using the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guideline55 or with meta-analysis where applicable.52 56 We will focus on four main areas: (1) awareness and knowledge; (2) attitudes, acceptance and readiness; (3) behavioural intention and adoption and (4) ethical awareness and concerns. The findings for each area will be grouped by region (GCC, Arab non-GCC, international) and study design. Where applicable, meta-analyses will use the Der Simonian-Laird random-effects model with the inverse-variance method56 to combine effect estimates. Results will be presented as Forest plots. Statistical heterogeneity among studies will be assessed using the I2-statistic and Cochran’s Q-statistic,57 with values of 25%, 50% and 75%, indicating low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively. If there is high heterogeneity among studies and sufficient number of studies (>10) are available, meta-regression analysis58 will be used to identify the study and participants’ level factors associated with heterogeneity. In meta-regression, priori specified factors as potential sources of heterogeneity: geographic region (GCC vs non-GCC Middle East vs rest of world); academic level (undergraduate vs postgraduate nursing students) and measurement instrument type (validated scale vs researcher-developed tool vs adapted instrument) will be examined. Funnel plots59 60 and Egger’s test60 will be used for assessing publication bias. For outcomes with fewer than 10 included studies, the risk of publication bias will be discussed narratively, with reference to grey literature searches, trial registries and the distribution of effect sizes across included studies. Sensitivity analyses will look at how study quality affects the findings. Where mixed samples include nursing students alongside other health professions students or healthcare workers, inclusion will require that nursing student data are reported separately or that nursing students constitute at least 80% of the total sample. Studies where nursing students represent <80% of the sample and nursing-specific data cannot be disaggregated will be excluded to preserve population homogeneity and ensure the validity of the synthesis. Subgroup analysis will check differences by academic level (graduate vs undergraduate) and region (Gulf states, Middle East, Arab countries). Stata V.19 will be used for all statistical analyses in this review.61

Discussion

This protocol has been formulated for a systematic review that will represent the most comprehensive synthesis to date of the worldwide evidence concerning the awareness and attitudes of nursing students towards AI in nursing education. This systematic review protocol has been developed in response to the rapid growth of research examining nursing students’ awareness, attitudes and readiness toward AI. Notably, 31 of the 52 studies identified during the preliminary scoping search were published in 2025 alone, reflecting the accelerating pace of scholarship in this field. However, considerable methodological heterogeneity exists across studies, which will necessitate careful synthesis and interpretation of findings. The anticipated heterogeneity includes variation in sampling methods where convenience sampling predominates measurement instruments, several of which are unvalidated ad hoc scales developed without established psychometric testing, and theoretical framing, with studies variously employing TAM, Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology or no explicit theoretical framework. This heterogeneity will be systematically addressed through narrative synthesis guided by the SWiM framework, pre-specified subgroup analyses and meta-regression where sufficient studies are available.

This review is expected to establish benchmark estimates for AI awareness and attitude levels among nursing students, identify the most robust validated instruments for cross-cultural use (including the General Attitudes Toward AI Scale, the Scale Measuring Student Attitudes Toward AI and emerging domain-specific scales), and delineate the theoretical and contextual factors that shape student engagement with AI. Subgroup analyses by GCC region will be particularly informative for educational policymakers in nations where vision strategies mandate digital transformation, but empirical evidence on workforce preparedness remains sparse.

A key anticipated finding is the confirmation of a substantial evidence gap for the Middle East, Arab or Asian regions compared with the rest of the world, where only one study has been identified from the initial mapping. Similarly sparse evidence is anticipated in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia, where nursing education systems are expanding rapidly alongside national digitalisation agendas yet remain under-represented in the AI education literature. While the primary geographic focus of this review is the GCC and Middle East, the search strategy has been designed to capture any available studies from sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and other under-studied regions, ensuring that emergent evidence from these contexts is systematically identified, reported and considered in the comparative synthesis. This gap provides a clear and well-evidenced rationale for future primary research examining Omani nursing students’ AI awareness, attitudes, readiness and ethical awareness within the framework of Oman Vision 2040 and the country’s National Health Strategy. Specifically, the review will identify which validated instruments are most suitable for cross-cultural adaptation to Omani nursing students, provide benchmark prevalence estimates that can inform sample size calculations for future primary studies and highlight modifiable barriers involving perceived ease of use and AI anxiety that can be directly targeted in educational interventions and faculty development programmes. The findings of this review will be presented in a clear and practical recommendations table designed to support nursing educators and curriculum developers. This table will highlight validated tools that may be adapted for use across different cultural contexts, provide benchmark estimates of students’ awareness and attitudes toward AI for comparison across nursing programmes and identify key barriers that can be addressed in education. These may include issues such as anxiety about AI or limited understanding of its usefulness, which could be improved through thoughtful curriculum design and faculty development initiatives.

There are several potential limitations of this review that are acknowledged here. First, language bias may arise from the restriction to English-language publications. Relevant studies published in Arabic, Chinese or other languages particularly those from the GCC region, where Arabic-language nursing journals represent an important body of evidence, will not be captured by the current search strategy. This limitation is consistent with the broader challenge of conducting systematic reviews in regions where research is disseminated across multiple languages and will be explicitly acknowledged in the final review report.

Second, publication bias represents a potential threat to the completeness of the evidence base. The review will primarily include published peer-reviewed studies, and unpublished null or negative findings which are less likely to appear in indexed databases may therefore be under-represented. Although a grey literature search is planned, the sources originally specified (WHO, Oman Ministry of Health and Sultan Qaboos University repositories) were insufficient to comprehensively address this risk. The grey literature search has accordingly been expanded to include ProQuest Dissertations & Theses and Google Scholar, which index a broader range of conference abstracts, institutional reports, theses and dissertations that may contain relevant unpublished or informally disseminated findings. Publication bias will be formally assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test for outcomes where 10 or more studies are available; for outcomes with fewer than 10 studies, the risk of publication bias will be discussed narratively.

Third, cross-sectional design predominance is anticipated across the included literature, as the majority of studies examining nursing students’ AI awareness and attitudes employ cross-sectional survey designs. While cross-sectional studies provide valuable prevalence estimates and associations, they do not permit causal inference regarding the determinants of AI awareness, attitude formation or behavioural intention. This design limitation will be systematically acknowledged in the interpretation of findings and will be highlighted as the inclusion of studies with mixed health profession samples may introduce population heterogeneity, which will be addressed through subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

Ethics and dissemination

Since this is a systematic review protocol paper, it doesn’t require ethical approval because the systematic review will be fully grounded in the analysis of previously published literature and does not involve any direct participation by human subjects or animals. We will submit the results of this review for publication in peer-reviewed journals.
