Chronic expanding intracerebral hematoma: A case report
Authors: Jian Li, Chun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic expanding intracerebral hematoma is a special type of intracerebral hematoma. It is clinically rare and has a high misdiagnosis rate, requiring a combination of clinical and pathological analysis for definitive diagnosis. A middle-aged woman presented with right limb numbness. Neurological examination showed a Glasgow coma score of 15, the national institute of health stroke scale of 2, and a modified Rankin score of 3. The muscle strength was graded 4/5 (MRC grade). Cranial CT and MRI imaging’s revealed an irregular hematoma in the left basal ganglia and thalamus. Following surgical evacuation, pathology confirmed chronic expanding intracerebral hematoma. The patient recovered well and was discharged. Chronic expanding intracerebral hematoma is a rare disease with a high misdiagnosis rate. However, when the hematoma presents characteristic imaging findings, it can be helpful for diagnosis. Surgical resection is currently the definitive treatment.

Introduction

Chronic expanding intracerebral hematoma (CEICH), also referred to as chronic encapsulated intracerebral hematoma, is a rare clinical entity characterized by a high misdiagnosis rate [1]. It is frequently mistaken for conditions such as glioma, hemorrhagic brain tumor, or cerebral abscess, and establishing a definitive diagnosis requires correlation of clinical presentation with pathological findings [2]. The clinical course of CEICH is typically prolonged, predominantly manifesting as symptoms of progressive intracranial hypertension. We present a surgically confirmed case of a rare chronic expanding intracerebral hematoma, with the diagnosis established histopathologically.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old woman was admitted to an outside institution with numbness in the right limbs without obvious incentive for 2-week. No headache, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, fever, weakness of limbs, convulsions, incontinence, and other discomforts were reported. Noncontrast head CT showed hyper-attenuation in the left basal ganglion and thalamus (Fig. 1A). A midline shift of 6.1mm was observed. She reported no history of diabetes, hypertension, and cerebrovascular disease. After 2 weeks of conservative treatment, her limb numbness during hospitalization improved. She was presented to neurosurgery department in our hospital for further consultation. Neurological examination revealed a Glasgow coma score of 15, the national institute of health stroke scale of 2, and a modified Rankin score of 3. The muscle strength was graded 4/5 (MRC grade). The bilateral pathological signs were negative. Physiological reflexes were present, and bilateral pathological signs were not elicited. Finger nose test was negative. After 1 month, a follow-up CT scan showed that the attenuation of the mass was lower than that at admission with a midline shift of 7.3mm (Fig. 1B). Noncontrast brain MRI manifested an irregular hematoma in the left basal ganglion and thalamus with hypointense hemosiderosis around the lesion (Fig. 1C and D). After another month's hospitalization, her condition deteriorated with the muscle strength of the right limb of 3. The lesion was surrounded by a newly appeared crescent-shaped high signal intensity on noncontrast MRI, leading to an 8.2 mm midline shift (Fig. 1E and F). Surgery was performed, and pathology confirmed chronic expanding intracerebral hematoma. After 2-month’s rehabilitation, the patient was discharged with the muscle strength of the right limb graded 4 with positive pathological signs.

Discussion

CEICH also known as chronic encapsulated intracerebral hematoma, was first described by Hirsh et al. [3] in 1981. It is a condition with low incidence and remains poorly understood in clinical practice. The etiology of CEICH is most commonly attributed to occult vascular malformations [4]. CEICH most frequently occurs in the subcortical white matter of the parietal, temporal, and occipital lobes [5]. Its clinical course is characterized by slow progression, with progressive symptoms of intracranial hypertension and seizures being the most common presentations. The physical examination often reveals only papilledema in the absence of other focal neurological deficits. This lack of specific signs is a key reason why it is often misdiagnosed as a brain tumor. On noncontrast CT scans, the lesion exhibits variable density—high, low, or mixed—depending on the age of the hemorrhage, and is often surrounded by perilesional edema. When a well-formed capsule is present, contrast-enhanced CT may demonstrate ring enhancement without internal enhancement. MRI findings vary with the stage of the hematoma. A characteristic feature on MRI is a peripheral rim of low signal intensity, attributable to hemosiderin deposition, which can also exhibit ring enhancement on contrasted scans [6]. This signature finding is instrumental in diagnosing a chronic expanding hematoma. Hematoma growth is believed to result from repeated bleeding from newly formed blood vessels within the capsule [2,7]. Although imaging studies can be suggestive of a hematoma, surgical excision followed by histopathological examination remains the gold standard for a definitive diagnosis [[8], [9]]. Although no standardized guideline exists for CEIH [10], open craniotomy aiming for complete resection of the hematoma and its capsule remains the established curative standard [8].

CEICH needs to be differentiated from glioma, tumoral hemorrhage, and brain abscess.

Glioma: on CT: Low-grade gliomas typically appear as iso- or hypodense lesions with mild mass effect. High-grade gliomas show heterogeneous density, often with necrosis and cystic changes, accompanied by extensive peritumoral edema and significant mass effect. On MRI: Findings correlate closely with tumor grade. Low-grade gliomas are usually ill-defined, exhibit homogeneous high signal on T2WI/FLAIR, and most show no enhancement or peritumoral edema. In contrast, high-grade gliomas are characteristically heterogeneous in signal, demonstrate irregular ring enhancement (eg, “garland-like”), central necrosis, restricted diffusion, and are associated with marked peritumoral edema and mass effect.

Tumoral hemorrhage: on CT: Presents as an inhomogeneous hyperdense hemorrhagic focus surrounded by extensive hypodense edema with significant mass effect. On MRI: On T1WI, the lesion appears iso- or slightly hypointense; on T2WI, it shows mixed high and low signals. Enhancement typically reveals characteristic features, with the tumor parenchyma usually appearing slightly hyperintense. Irregular, nodular areas of marked enhancement may be seen within or at the margin of the hematoma, representing the solid tumor component.

Brain abscess: on CT: The lesion appears as a round or oval hypodense area. On MRI: On T1WI, it is hypointense; on T2WI, it is hyperintense. Postcontrast imaging shows ring enhancement, and the center of the abscess typically exhibits marked restricted diffusion, which is a characteristic finding.

Conclusion

CEICH is a distinct type of intracerebral hematoma associated with a high rate of misdiagnosis. This necessitates detailed clinical correlation and vigilant imaging follow-up. The present case illustrates a gradual increase in the hematoma's size over 2 months on noncontrast MRI, accompanied by the evolution of its characteristic signal intensity. These distinctive imaging features are valuable in supporting the diagnosis of CEICH.
