3C conjugates: a highly sensitive platform for antibody internalization assessment in ADC development
Authors: Changyan Chen, Yutian Zhang, Yadan Wu, Shuhui Luan, Xi Jiao, Jingli Liao, Shuai Wang, Yayuan Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) rely on antibody-mediated internalization to deliver cytotoxic payloads into tumor cells. Therefore, quantitative assessment of antibody internalization is essential for ADC development, particularly during early antibody screening stages. However, conventional internalization assays, whether direct or indirect, often face challenges such as low throughput, reduced sensitivity, and limited target specificity due to spatial hindrance. Here, we introduce a versatile 3C peptide conjugate platform that utilizes the high-affinity binding of IgG by the C1-C3 domains of streptococcal protein G. This platform includes 3C-toxin for cytotoxicity-based internalization detection and 3C-pHAb for pH-sensitive fluorescent tracking. By simply incubating these reagents with antibodies, effective labeling is achieved without complex modifications, enabling sensitive and high-throughput evaluation of internalization. We validated the platform across multiple tumor-associated targets, including HER2, CDH6, LIV-1, LYPD3, and GPC3, demonstrating a strong correlation between 3C-based assays and the cytotoxic efficacy of corresponding ADCs. Notably, 3C-toxin showed superior target promiscuity compared to traditional DT3C methods, expanding applicability to a broader range of antigens. This platform provides a scalable solution for antibody internalization analysis, positioned to accelerate ADC discovery by providing reliable early-stage screening metrics.KEYWORDS: 3C peptide, Antibody screening, Antibody-drug conjugation, Cytotoxicity assay, Internalization, pH-sensitive dye, Streptococcal protein G

Introduction

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) constitute a transformative class of biotherapeutics, combining monoclonal antibodies with cytotoxic payloads via engineered linkers. By 2024, 15 ADCs have gained marketing approval.1 To achieve selective cytotoxicity, ADCs must efficiently bind to tumor cells, undergo internalization, and traffic to appropriate intracellular compartment for payload release.2 Consequently, robust screening for antibody internalization is critical during ADC development. Current workflows prioritize sequential antigen-binding and internalization assays prior to ADC assembly, highlighting the need for sensitive, high-throughput methods to accurately predict ADC efficacy.

Traditional internalization assays are broadly classified as direct or indirect methods.3 Direct methods conjugate primary antibodies to cytotoxic payloads, radioisotopes4,5 or fluorophores.6–8 These approaches involve cumbersome conjugation steps and limited scalability. Indirect methods, such as secondary antibodies or peptide-based systems, streamline workflows and enhanced throughput, but introduce new limitations. For instance, Mab – ZAP (210 kDa) – a fusion of an anti-mouse antibody and ribosome inactivating protein, saporin – relies on post-internalization cytotoxicity.9 However, its large size (≥360 kDa when bound to the antibody) may hinder antibody internalization, and its specificity for mouse antibodies restricts broad applicability.10,11 Similarly, DT3C (70 kDa) is a recombinant protein combining diphtheria toxin (DT) with C1, C2, and C3 domains of Streptococcus protein G. DT3C was proved superior to Mab – ZAP in selecting antibodies internalized by cells.12 Despite its advantages, cell-type variability in DT/saporin sensitivity may limit translational relevance of DT3C/Mab-ZAP-based assays to actual ADC performance. Klussman et al.13 set up the secondary mAb-vcMMAE (~150 kDa), in which, the classical payload MMAE was used as toxin, but this approach still risks steric interference with antigen engagement due to toxin-labeled secondary antibodies. Taken together, these issues underscore the unmet need for a versatile, clinically predictive internalization platform.

We developed a 3C peptide-based platform leveraging the Fc-binding domain of streptococcal protein G.14 By coupling compact 3C scaffolds to cytotoxic agents (e.g., topoisomerase I inhibitor, tubulin inhibitor) or pH-sensitive dyes, our platform enables rapid, cost-effective antibody labeling with minimal steric hindrance. This system provides a reliable model for ADC behavior by mimicking key attributes. It bridges critical gaps in early-stage antibody screening, enabling efficient identification of candidates with optimal intracellular trafficking and drug-release potential.

Materials and methods

Expression and purification of 3C protein

Recombinant 3C protein (3C peptide-G4S-CCC) was expressed in Expi293F cells (Gibco, A14527) using a pTT5 expression vector (YouBio, VT2202) containing the gene encoding 3C protein. To facilitate purification, a C-terminal His tag was introduced. Affinity chromatography was performed with Ni Sepharose Excel (Cytiva, 17,371,202), and the protein was eluted using imidazole. The purified protein was stored in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.4).

3C-pHAb preparation

Twelve times molar equivalent of 10 mM tri(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine solution (TCEP, Invitrogen, T2552) was added to the 3C protein solution, which was reduced at 37°C for 3 h. About 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and seven times molar equivalent of pHAb Thiol Reactive Dye (Promega, G9835) were added to the reduced protein solution, and the reaction solution was coupled at 25°C for 3 h. The reaction product was dialyzed into PBS solution to remove unreacted free dye. All the above experiments were carried out under light-protected conditions. Size exclusion-high-performance liquid chromatography (SEC-HPLC) and LC-mass spectrometry (MS) were used to analyze the purity and the pHAb:3C ratio of the 3C conjugates.

3C-toxin preparation

After adding 3.5 times the molar equivalent of TCEP to the prepared 3C protein solution, the mixture was reduced at 37°C for 3 h. About 10% DMSO and 3.5 times the molar equivalent of mc-vc-PAB-MMAE or Camptothecin derivatives were added to the reduced protein solution, and the reaction solution was coupled at 25°C for 17 h. The reaction product was dialyzed into PBS solution to remove unreacted free toxins. The purity and the toxin:3C ratio value of the conjugate were analyzed by SEC-HPLC and LC-MS.

Antibody information

Trastuzumab (anti-HER2 antibody) was generated based on the patent US5821337A. Ladiratuzumab (anti-LIV-1 antibody) was generated based on the patent US20200165335A. Lupartumab (anti-LYPD3 antibody) was generated based on the patent WO2011070088. Anti-CDH6 Ab was generated based on the patent WO2024165049A1. Anti-FITC, GPC3-Ab-1, GPC3-Ab-2, GPC3-Ab-3 and GPC3-Ab-4 are novel Simcere Zaiming antibodies. The isotype and species of the above antibodies are human IgG1. The antibodies and fragments used in Figure 2(c) were novel Simcere Zaiming antibody VH/VL in human IgG1, human IgG4, mouse IgG1, mouse IgG2a, mouse IgG2b or mouse IgG2c.

Antibody expression and purification

All antibodies were expressed in Expi293F cells, and Protein A chromatography (Cytiva, 11,003,494) was used for antibody purification. If the antibody contains more than 5% aggregates, using a high-resolution size exclusion column such as Superdex 200 (Cytiva, 28,990,946) to improve antibody purity.

ADC conjugation

According to the required drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) value, 2–8 times molar equivalent of TCEP was added to the antibody solution, and the mixed solution was reduced at 4°C for 17 h. About 8–15 times molar equivalent of linker-payload compound was dissolved in DMSO and added to the reaction system, reacted at 25°C for 6 h. The reaction product was gradient eluted with Capto SP ImpRes cation (Cytiva, 17,546,855) to remove the unreacted free toxins. The final ADC was stored in 559 buffer (10 mM sodium acetate, 9% sucrose, pH 5.5) by overnight dialysis. The purity and DAR value of the antibody conjugate were analyzed by SEC-HPLC and LC-MS.

Cell lines and cell culture

SKBR3, a breast cancer cell line (ATCC, HTB-30), was cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium (Gibco, 16,600,082) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (ExcellBio, FSP500). NCI-N87, a gastric cancer cell line (SIBCB, SCSP-534), was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (MeilunBio, PWL015) supplemented with 10% FBS. OVCAR3, an ovarian cancer cell line (ATCC, HTB-161), was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 20% FBS and 10 μg/mL insulin (Yeasen, 40107ES60). Caov3, an ovarian cancer cell line (Cobioer, CBP60284), was cultured in DMEM medium (MeilunBio, PWL003) supplemented with 10% FBS. MCF-7, a breast cancer cell line (ATCC, HTB-22) was cultured in DMEM medium supplement with 10% FBS. NCI-H838, a non-small cell lung cancer cell line (Cobioer, CBP60146), was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. A549 (Cobioer, CBP60084) and MDA-MB-453 (Cobioer, CBP60386) were cultured in DMEM medium supplement with 10% FBS. All cells were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere and routinely sub-cultured. Cells were harvested in an exponential growth phase and used for the experiments described here.

Cytotoxicity assay

Cancer cells were seeded at 2000–4000 cells per well in a 96-well polystyrene plate (Kanovo, 062096) and culture at 37°C overnight. For indirect cytotoxicity assay, antibody and 3C-Tubi (Tubulin inhibitor), 3C-TopIi (Topoisomerase I inhibitor) or DT3C (PC-Biotech) were incubated at room temperature for 30 min to form antibody +3C-Tubi, antibody +3C-TopIi or antibody+DT3C complexes. The molar ratio of antibody to 3C reagent is 1:3 for 3C-toxin and 1:4 for DT3C, respectively. Cells were incubated with dilution series of complexes for 5 d. For direct cytotoxicity assay, cells were incubated with dilution series of ADC (antibody-Tubi or antibody-TopIi) for 5 d. The fluorescence was estimated using CellTiter-Meiluncell kit (MeilunBio, PWL111) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and % cytotoxicity = (Fluorescence blank-Fluorescence experimental group)/Fluorescence blank ×100%.

3C-pHAb internalization assay

Cancer cells were seeded at 10,000– 20,000 cells per well in a 96-well polystyrene plate and grown to confluence by overnight incubation at 37°C. The antibody and 3C-pHAb were incubated at room temperature for 30 min to form antibody +3C-pHAb complexes. The molar ratio of antibody to 3C-pHAb is 1:5. The complexes were added to cells at the desired concentration and then incubated for 0, 2, 4, 8, 24, 28 h to allow internalization. After washing with PBS twice, plates were read on Ensight (PerkinElmer, Ensight-HH3400) at Ex/Em: 532 nm/560 nm. At the same time, fluorescence images were captured on a microscope (Nikon, DS-Qi2).

Results

Preparation and mechanism of the 3C conjugates

The streptococcal protein G-derived 3C domain (3C peptide), with its amino acid sequence is shown in Figure 1(a), was used for antibody labeling based on its specific binding affinity for the Fc region of IgG antibodies.15 To enable conjugation, we introduced three cysteine residues into the 3C peptide via a G4S linker. Following reduction with TCEP, the cysteine thiol groups were activated to react with maleimide reactive group of toxin molecules and the pH-sensitive pHAb Thiol Reactive Dye. This reaction generated the respective conjugates: 3C-toxin and 3C-pHAb (Figures 1b,c).

(b) Schematic representation of the conjugation procedure and structure of 3C-pHAb and 3C-toxin. (c) Chemical structures of the toxin (camptothecin derivatives or mc-vc-PAB-MMAE) and pHAb (pHab thiol reactive dye). (d) Mode of action of 3C-toxin for assessing antibody internalization via detection of cell viability reduction. (e) Mode of action of 3C-pHab for assessing antibody internalization through measurement of pH-sensitive fluorescence signals.

For the 3C-toxin conjugate, we selected a Camptothecin derivative (TopIi) and mc-vc-PAB-MMAE (Tubi) as payloads, representing common classes of ADC toxin. Upon incubation with antibodies at room temperature, the 3C-toxin forms an antibody +3C-toxin complex. This complex binds to cell surface antigens, undergoes internalization, and traffics from endosomes to lysosomes. Subsequent release of free toxins induces cytotoxic effects. Thus, cell viability assays serve as a measure of antigen-specific internalization efficiency (Figure 1d).

For the 3C-pHAb conjugate, the conjugated pH-sensitive dye exhibits minimal fluorescence at neutral pH, but becomes highly fluorescent in acidic environments (e.g., endosomes or lysosomes), emitting fluorescence upon excitation at 532 nm16. The antibody +3C-pHAb complex facilitates quantitative, real-time tracking of internalized antibodies via fluorescence signal detection (Figure 1e).

Physicochemical properties and binding characteristics of the 3C conjugates

Under optimized conjugation conditions, 3C-TopIi, 3C-Tubi, and 3C-pHAb were successfully synthesized. Dialysis effectively removed unreacted toxin or dye, yielding conjugates with high purity (Figure 1b). SEC-HPLC analysis showed monomeric purity levels of 95.56%, 91.21%, and 98.56% for 3C-TopIi, 3C-Tubi, and 3C-pHAb, respectively (Table 1, Figure 2a). MS confirmed the molecular weights of the conjugates and the quantitative analysis determined average conjugation ratios of 2.79 for 3C-TopIi, 3.00 for 3C-Tubi, and 1.83 for 3C-pHAb (Table 1). Reducing SDS-PAGE analysis demonstrated that the molecular weight of the 3C conjugates (<30 kDa) was significantly lower than that of DT3C (~69.4 kDa) (Figure 2b).

To evaluate binding specificity, 3C-TopIi binding was assessed against various antibodies (Figure 2c). 3C-TopIi concentration (molar ratio to antibody)-dependent binding was observed for human IgG1 and IgG4, confirming the Fc-targeting capability of the 3C peptide. Notably, 3C-TopIi also bound to human IgG1 Fab and F(ab)2 fragments, consistent with prior reports of Streptococcal protein G interactions with Fab regions.16,17 Cross-species reactivity analysis demonstrated that 3C-TopIi also bound to murine IgG subtypes. These results reveal the versatility of the 3C conjugates for targeting diverse antibody formats in screening applications.

Application of 3C-pHAb in quantifying antibody internalization

As demonstrated in Figure 1(e), 3C-pHAb enables quantitative monitoring of antibody internalization via fluorescence intensity. To validate this application, we initially tested the system with the HER2-targeting antibody trastuzumab. Incubation of trastuzumab +3C-pHAb with HER2-high-expressing SKBR3 cells revealed a time-dependent increase in fluorescence signals that plateaued at 8 h (Figure 3a). Fluorescence microscopy further confirmed distinct intracellular signals in SKBR3 (Figure 3b).

Beyond HER2, 3C-pHAb effectively quantified internalization across diverse targets, including antibodies targeting CDH6, LIV-1, and LYPD3, in their respective cell lines (Figure 3c–e, Figure S1). Flow cytometry analysis measured antigen expression level based on antibody binding signals. We can find that the fluorescence intensity of 3C-pHAb correlated with antigen expression levels, and potentially also with the inherent internalization properties of the antibodies or target themselves. We also performed 3C-pHAb method in target-negative cell line and there was no significantly higher fluorescence signal value relative to the negative control (Figure S2), demonstrating the specificity of the 3C-pHAb method.

To explore broader utility, we evaluated 3C-pHAb with different GPC3-targeting antibodies in Huh7 cells. Internalization efficiency ranked as GPC3-Ab-1 > GPC3-Ab-3 > GPC3-Ab-4, while GPC3-Ab-2 showing negligible internalization over 24 h (Figure 4a). Notably, the cytotoxic potency of ADCs derived from these antibodies mirrored the internalization rankings: GPC3-Ab-1 ADC exhibited the strongest cell-killing activity, whereas GPC3-Ab-2 ADC was least effective (Figure 4b). Similar trends were observed in HepG2 cells (Figure 4c,d), reinforcing the predictive value of 3C-pHAb-based internalization assays for ADC efficacy. These findings highlight 3C-pHAb as a robust tool for early-stage screening of antibodies with optimal internalization profiles for ADC development.

3C-toxin-mediated indirect cytotoxicity as a surrogate for ADC direct killing in antibody screening

In addition to visualizing internalization using 3C-pHAb, we developed a complementary 3C-toxin strategy to indirectly evaluate antibody performance in ADC contexts by measuring cytotoxicity. This provides a comprehensive assessment of antibody potential (Figure 1d). Using trastuzumab as a model antibody, we validated this approach by comparing the cytotoxicity of its 3C-TopIi complex against HER2-positive SKBR3 and NCI-N87 cells. As shown in Figure 5, the Trastuzumab +3C-TopIi complex exhibited significant cell-killing activity, comparable to the cytotoxic effects of Trastuzumab-TopIi ADC. Similar efficacy was observed with 3C-Tubi, demonstrating the versatility of this method. These results indicate that 3C-toxin can effectively induce cytotoxicity through simple co-incubation with antibodies, without requiring direct covalent toxin conjugation. This approach not only streamlines early-stage antibody screening but also reliably predicts the cytotoxic outcomes of antibodies when conjugated to analogous toxins in ADCs.

Comparative analysis of 3C-toxin and DT3C demonstrates broader applicability for 3C-toxin

The 3C-toxin approach demonstrated broader applicability than the classical DT3C (diphtheria toxin-3C peptide) method across multiple targets. Although both systems utilize 3C peptide-mediated complex formation, DT3C’s larger molecular weight might cause significant steric hindrance upon antibody binding, limiting its functional utility (Figure 2b). While Trastuzumab+DT3C exhibited potent cytotoxicity against HER2-positive SKBR3 and NCI-N87 cells (Figure 5e,j), DT3C performance substantially declined for CDH6-targeted antibodies. Both antibody +3C-TopIi complexes and direct TopIi-conjugated ADCs outperformed DT3C-antibody complexes (Figure 6a). Notably, although complex formation between DT3C and both ladiratuzumab/lupartumab antibodies was successful (Figure S3), antibody+DT3C complexes failed to induce cytotoxicity in LIV-1 and LYPD3 models, whereas antibody +3C-toxin complexes replicated the cell-killing activity of corresponding ADCs (Figure 6b,cand S4). Collectively, these findings establish the 3C-toxin system’s superior versatility over DT3C, enabling indirect cytotoxicity evaluation across diverse targets and antibodies without payload size constraints or steric interference. We also tested the 3C-toxin method on target-negative cells and did not detect significant killing stronger than the negative control, demonstrating the specificity of the 3C-toxin method (Figure S5). Antigen binding site values, 3C-toxin cytotoxicity window and the sensitivity to free toxin were listed in the Supplemental Table S6. Our analysis indicates that the 3C-toxin cytotoxicity window is influenced by multiple factors beyond just antigen binding sites, including target characterization and toxin sensitivity.

Discussion

This study presents a novel platform leveraging the specific interaction between the streptococcal protein G-derived 3C domain (3C peptide) and IgG antibodies. Small-molecule toxins or pH-sensitive fluorescent dyes were conjugated to the 3C peptide, generating functional probes (3C-toxin and 3C-pHAb) that enable rapid antibody labeling via a simple 30-min incubation at room temperature. Following endocytosis of the antibody +3C conjugate complexes, the internalization efficiency is quantified through either toxin-mediated cytotoxicity or pH-triggered fluorescence activation. Experimental validation confirmed a strong correlation between this approach and the in-vitro potency of ADC.

Key advantages of our platform include: (1) Physiological relevance: Assay results accurately reflect actual ADC performance; (2) High throughput: Suitable for medium-to-large screening; (3) Broad applicability: Effective across diverse targets and antibody formats. Unlike bulky recombinant proteins, our minimal 3C-toxin and 3C-pHAb conjugates preserve native antigen recognition and internalization dynamics. This provides a more reliable model for predicting ADC behavior.

The widely used DT3C system – a fusion protein comprising the 3C peptide and diphtheria toxin – exhibits limitations due to its large size (69.4 kDa). This molecular weight may compromise DT3C-antibody binding and hinder target engagement during endocytosis. In contrast, our 3C-toxin uses ultralow-molecular-weight toxin payloads (e.g., Camptothecin derivatives [1.030 kDa] and mc-vc-PAB-MMAE [1.316 kDa]), which minimally disrupt 3C-antibody affinity while maintaining antigen recognition fidelity and internalization kinetics. As shown in Figure 6, 3C-toxin demonstrates superior sensitivity in indirect cytotoxicity assays and broader target applicability compared to DT3C, thereby more accurately recapitulating ADC-mediated cell killing.

The 3C-pHAb system utilizes pH-sensitive dyes (pHAb Thiol Reactive Dye) that remain fluorescently quenched at neutral pH (>7) but activate strong fluorescence in acidic environments (pH 5–6).18 Upon antibody-mediated endocytosis, the probe is transported to acidic compartments (endosomes/lysosomes),19 where fluorescence intensity – proportional to internalization efficiency – is triggered. We validated this assay’s robustness across multiple cell lines and targets (Figure 3). Unlike direct antibody labeling (e.g., CypHer5E Green NHS Ester conjugation, requiring per-antibody modification)20 or secondary antibody-based methods (which introduce >150 kDa reagents causing steric hindrance),21 3C-pHAb enables high-throughput screening without molecular interference. Fluorescence and confocal microscopy further permit real-time monitoring of endocytic dynamics, including lysosomal trafficking.

The 3C-toxin system exhibited broad compatibility with diverse antibody species, isotypes, and fragments (Figure 2d), suggesting its potential utility in early-stage murine antibody screening. However, suboptimal binding to mouse IgG2a subtypes-attributed to weaker 3C peptide recognition than human IgG – may lead to false-negative results. To mitigate this limitation, nanobodies such as TP117022 (specifically targeting murine Fab kappa chains) could replace the 3C peptide for enhanced murine antibody assays when conjugated with toxins or pH-sensitive dyes. Additionally, alternative commercial pH-sensitive dyes (e.g., pHAb Amine Reactive Dye,18,23 pHrodo Green/Red iFL STP esters,24 or CypHer5E NHS Ester)25 may be substituted in 3C-pHAb construction, though empirical validation remains required. In addition, the cytotoxic potency of the antibody +3C-toxin complex relative to its directly conjugated ADC counterpart is governed by parameters such as the DAR of the directly conjugated ADC and the affinity between the 3C-toxin moiety and the antibody. Because the antibody–3C interaction is sequence-mediated, the presence of a variable-region motif that mimics the 3C-binding epitope can promote augmented recruitment of 3C-reagent, thereby conferring enhanced cytotoxicity to the antibody +3C-toxin format versus the directly conjugated ADC. Nevertheless, comprehensive profiling of an extensive antibody panel indicates that the probability of encountering such sequence mimicry is low.

In summary, the 3C-based platform provides a sensitive, high-throughput solution for antibody internalization assessment, addressing critical limitations of conventional method. By enabling rapid screening of ADC candidates with minimal molecular perturbation, this technology offers significant potential for accelerating next-generation antibody development.
