Effectiveness of neoadjuvant chemotherapy with lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab in advanced endometrial cancer: a case report

Background

In Japan, the combination of lenvatinib, a selective multi-kinase inhibitor, and pembrolizumab, an anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody, has been covered by health insurance and is available for patients (since December 2021) with unresectable advanced or recurrent endometrial cancer that has progressed after chemotherapy. Guidelines recommend the combination of paclitaxel plus carboplatin (TC) or doxorubicin plus cisplatin for the treatment of advanced endometrial cancer [1]. Furthermore, lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab is an option for unresectable advanced endometrial cancer after treatment with platinum-based regimens. These recommendations are based on the results of the KEYNOTE-775/309 study [2]. The study investigated the efficacy and safety in patients with incurable endometrial cancer who have received one or two regimens of platinum-based chemotherapy; however, the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) remains to be elucidated. To our knowledge, there are few previous reports of lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab as NAC for advanced endometrial cancer. In the present case report, we describe a patient with advanced endometrial cancer who was successfully treated with neoadjuvant lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab, which enabled curative surgery. Uterine artery embolization was performed as an adjunctive hemostatic intervention to allow continuation of systemic therapy.

Case presentation

The patient was a 51-year-old Japanese woman with no family history of colorectal or endometrial cancer. She had experienced irregular genital bleeding for 1 year. She had previously visited a physician because of increased bleeding and difficulty with food intake. The patient was found to be severely anemic with a hemoglobin level of 1.8 g/dL. A transfusion of 10 units of concentrated red blood cells was administered. She was suspected of having advanced endometrial cancer on the basis of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings and was referred to our hospital (Fig. 1).

Magnetic resonance imaging findings. Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging shows an endometrial tumor with a 12.5 cm long diameter in the sagittal section (A). The coronal section shows a 4.7 cm left ovarian metastasis (arrow) and ascites (B)

Adequate biopsy of the tumor was challenging owing to the narrowness of the patient’s vagina and the deviation of the cervix caused by the tumor. Consequently, a small tumor fragment that had fallen into the vagina was submitted for pathological analysis. The histological diagnosis was adenocarcinoma not otherwise specified (Fig. 2). Computed tomography revealed paratracheal and para-aortic lymph node enlargement, ascites, and left ovarian metastasis. The endometrial cancer was classified as stage IVB (T3aN2M1) according to the FIGO 2008 classification and deemed unresectable. The decision was made to proceed with NAC.

Endometrial histology. A tubular-like structure is observed in some areas, which is considered to be an adenocarcinoma component with a substantial proliferative area. Hematoxylin and eosin staining viewed at 10× (A) and 20× (B)

The regimen for NAC was TC. We administered paclitaxel (175 mg/m2) in combination with carboplatin (area under the curve, 5 mg/mL/minute) intravenously every 3 weeks. The patient required further blood transfusions for anemia. The first three cycles of treatment were administered at 90% of the standard dose owing to anemia. After six cycles, the tumor size was reduced to 11 cm, the para-aortic lymph nodes had decreased in size, and ascites had resolved; however, the changes did not meet the RECIST 1.1 criteria for a partial response, indicating stable disease. Because hysterectomy was considered technically difficult owing to tumor invasion into the parametrial tissues, but the patient was stable, TC was continued. After eight cycles, the patient was urgently admitted to the hospital owing to heavy genital bleeding. MRI revealed a 20% increase in target lesion sum diameter, consistent with disease progression under the RECIST 1.1 criteria. Because of the observed disease progression, other options, such as doxorubicin-based chemotherapy or radiotherapy, were considered unlikely to be effective; therefore, lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab was selected on the basis of the results of the KEYNOTE-775 trial (Fig. 3). The patient had persistent massive genital bleeding requiring urgent hemostasis; therefore, uterine artery embolization (UAE) was performed prior to initiating combination therapy with lenvatinib and pembrolizumab (hemoglobin level of 7.0 g/dL). This embolization was intended as a supportive life-saving intervention to stabilize the patient’s condition and enable subsequent systemic therapy, not as a primary antitumor intervention. Bleeding was significantly reduced, and lenvatinib (20 mg orally daily) plus pembrolizumab (200 mg intravenously once every 3 weeks) was initiated after blood transfusion. During treatment, she developed National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events grade 2 diarrhea; therefore, we reduced the dose of lenvatinib to 14 mg/day. MRI after four cycles revealed a 33% reduction in tumor size compared with baseline, meeting the RECIST 1.1 criteria for a partial response and enabling surgical resection (Fig. 4). After completion of five cycles of pembrolizumab, surgery was performed. When the abdomen was opened, there were extensive film-like adhesions around the uterus. Abdominal total simple hysterectomy and bilateral adnexectomy were performed. Grossly, the hard yellowish-white tissue extended beyond the serosal surface on the right side of the uterus to the periureter (about 3 cm × 3 cm in size). This yellowish-white tissue could not be detached from the right ureter and remained in the pelvis. Pathological diagnosis of the excised uterus showed no residual cancer, consistent with pathological complete response (pCR) (Fig. 5). Histopathological examination confirmed that the yellowish-white tissue showed necrotic changes and no viable tumor cells. These findings were consistent with pCR.

After 14 cycles of pembrolizumab, lenvatinib was reduced to 10 mg/day owing to grade 2 nausea. After 22 cycles of pembrolizumab, the patient experienced prolonged grade 2 diarrhea. Colonoscopy and biopsy revealed lymphocytic infiltration and villous atrophy, consistent with immune-related enteritis. Oral steroids were started. As the intestinal symptoms persisted for more than 6 weeks despite corticosteroid therapy, lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab was discontinued. No recurrence was observed on imaging (Fig. 6). The decision was made to proceed with the same follow-up as for advanced cancer. At the latest follow-up, 4 years after initiation of treatment and 3 years after surgery, the patient remains alive with no evidence of disease recurrence. The patient reported significant relief from genital bleeding and abdominal symptoms following treatment. She expressed relief at being able to proceed with surgery after systemic therapy and felt reassured by the treatment outcome.

Discussion and conclusions

The present case study highlights three important findings. First, lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab may represent a feasible neoadjuvant option for advanced endometrial cancer. Second, UAE was successfully performed to control massive genital bleeding. Third, no residual cancer was detected in the resected uterus; however, induration was observed following lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab treatment.

Lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab may represent a feasible neoadjuvant option for advanced endometrial cancer. Lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab is approved as a treatment option for unresectable advanced or recurrent endometrial cancer after treatment with platinum-based regimens. This approval is based on the results of the KEYNOTE-775/309 study; however, its efficacy as NAC has not been studied. In the present case, lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab may have been effective as neoadjuvant therapy for advanced endometrial cancer. Previous studies have reported its use in renal cell carcinoma [3–5]. The present case report is a rare report of lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab as NAC for endometrial cancer.

UAE was performed to successfully control massive genital bleeding in advanced endometrial cancer. Although malignancy is generally considered a contraindication for UAE in the treatment of fibroids, arterial embolization has been performed and evaluated as a life-saving measure for uncontrolled bleeding in malignant conditions, particularly cervical cancer. A previous study reported the efficacy and safety of UAE for treating endometrial cancer [6]. Radiation for hemostasis is another treatment option; however, we chose UAE because endometrial cancer is less radiosensitive than cervical cancer and requires a longer time to achieve effective hemostasis.

There was no residual cancer; however, induration was observed in the removed uterus after lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab treatment. Several previous reports described residual cancer after lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab in renal cell carcinoma. In a previous study of clear cell renal cell carcinoma over 20 cm, radical nephrectomy was performed 23 weeks after initiation of lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab, with approximately 30% of cancers surviving [3]. In a previous case of local recurrence of dedifferentiated sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma after initial nephrectomy, radical resection was performed after completion of five cycles of lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab and no cancer remnant was found [4]. In clear cell renal cell carcinoma with tumor thrombus adherent to the inferior vena cava wall, nephrectomy and thrombectomy were performed 6 months after the start of lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab with no residual cancer [5]. In the present case, the yellowish-white indurated tissue surrounding the right ureter was difficult to separate during surgery. The tissue was initially suspected to be residual tumor; however, histopathological examination revealed that it consisted of necrotic tissue. Therefore, when surgery is performed after lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab, induration may represent post-treatment necrosis rather than viable tumor, a pattern commonly observed following effective immunotherapy [7, 8]. This necrotic tissue was interpreted as the post-treatment regression bed, consistent with the pathological changes reported following effective immunotherapy. After surgery, pembrolizumab was continued as adjuvant therapy because the disease was initially stage IVB, and the risk of microscopic residual lesions could not be excluded. This approach was consistent with the therapeutic strategy for advanced endometrial cancer, in which systemic treatment is maintained after resection in responders to prior immune-targeted therapy. Furthermore, in the KEYNOTE-775/309 study, treatment was continued as long as clinical benefit was observed.

The present study has several limitations. First, there was an unclear mismatch repair status owing to the small initial tumor volume, which made the Microsatellite instability (MSI) test unevaluable; thus, future cases should include biomarker assessment to guide therapy. Second, the absence of cancerous tissue in the removed uterus hindered further analysis. A sufficient tumor sample is necessary for determining an appropriate treatment strategy in the event of recurrence. This report describes a single case; therefore, the findings cannot be generalized.

Although the patient has remained disease-free for 4 years after initiation of treatment and 3 years after surgery, conclusions regarding the efficacy of lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab as neoadjuvant therapy should be interpreted with caution. This case should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of efficacy.

Lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab resulted in a marked reduction in tumor size, allowing surgery to be performed. No cancerous remnants were found in the removed uterus. This combination may represent a potential neoadjuvant strategy for advanced endometrial cancer.
