Status of emergency medical service use for acute ischemic stroke in China
Authors: Wenwei Qi, Ying Su, Jiarui Wu, Xin Shi, Xiaohong Wu, Feng Chi, Runyu Xia, Limin Qin, Liming Cao, Yan Yang, Qin Liu, Xiaoxiang Peng, Guobing Huang, Jinyan Chen, Yidong Xue, Wenbiao Guan, Dan Gao, Bin Ye, Tianxia Yu, Runxiu Zhu, Lijie Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe current status of emergency medical service (EMS) use among patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) in China is a concern. We aimed to understand the status of EMS use and influencing factors in patients with AIS in China. In this large-scale prospective multicenter hospital-based registry study, we evaluated the data of 10,856 patients with AIS admitted to 21 hospitals across different economic and geographic regions in China in 2022. The primary outcome was the proportion of patients arriving at the hospital via EMS. In patients with AIS who arrived independently and those who arrived via EMS, 79.9% and 47.1%, respectively, had an onset-to-door time > 3 h; 16.0% and 40.6%, respectively, received intravenous thrombolysis; and 4.0% and 15.5%, respectively, underwent thrombectomy. Factors promoting EMS use included older age, residence in a small city, wake-up stroke, cardioembolic stroke, and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores 5–15 and 16–42 after onset. Conversely, small-artery occlusion was associated with patients arriving to hospital independently. Hospital arrival via EMS significantly reduced prehospital delay and increased thrombolysis rate compared to arriving at the hospital independently. Improving public education regarding the advantages of using EMS is needed.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Prospective studies, Emergency medical services, Hospitals, Registries, ThrombectomySubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

The current situation of emergency stroke treatment is a major concern. Globally, stroke remains the second leading cause of death, accounting for 11.6% of all deaths annually; it was the third leading cause of death and disability in 20191. In China, the leading cause of death and disability is stroke2, with a reported 3.94 million new stroke cases, 28.76 million prevalent cases, and 2.19 million deaths due to stroke in 20193. Reperfusion therapies, such as intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) and thrombectomy, are used for managing acute ischemic stroke (AIS). However, these therapies should be administered or performed within the treatment window4; thus, the biggest challenge in managing AIS is prehospital delay. Shortening prehospital delay is critical for improving stroke survival rates5.

Activating the emergency medical service (EMS) is the most important factor in reducing the delay to hospital arrival among patients with stroke6. The use of EMS in patients with AIS is critical for ensuring timely and appropriate care. EMS plays a vital role in saving lives and reducing mortality and morbidity7. It is an independent predictor of good outcomes in patients with AIS8. The decision to seek medical assistance for acute stroke accounts for more than half of the prehospital delay9. In China, one of the main reasons for prehospital delays in patients with AIS is the failure of patients and their families to call EMS in a timely manner10. In 2016, China’s health management department released the “stroke emergency map” (SEM) and promoted its use nationwide to enable precise and rapid transport of patients with stroke, thereby reducing prehospital delays11. Clinical evidence has shown a positive influence of coordinated stroke centers and regional stroke networks on the clinical efficacy of IVT in patients with AIS5.

The utilization of EMS by patients with stroke is influenced by various factors, including public awareness, sex differences in symptom recognition, and the efficiency of EMS systems. The current status of EMS use among patients with AIS in China is a concern. The aim of this study was to understand the current situation regarding how patients with stroke arrive at the hospital and the factors influencing the use of EMS, which is crucial for formulating medical policies and guiding subsequent improvements in stroke treatment outcomes in China.

Methods

Design and setting

For this large-scale prospective multicenter hospital-based registry study, we collected data from patients with AIS who were admitted to any of the 21 participating hospitals in China between January 1, 2022, and October 31, 2022, from the China National Cerebrovascular Disease Prevention and Control Project Management Special Database. The participating hospitals are located in cities across different regions and economic levels in China and are grade A tertiary hospitals qualified to perform IVT and endovascular thrombectomy. Additionally, all relevant cities implemented an SEM.

Study participants

Patients aged ≥ 18 years, diagnosed with AIS, and with stroke onset ≤ 7 days before admission were included. Patients who were diagnosed with transient ischemic attack, in-hospital AIS, cancer-related stroke, iatrogenic AIS, or cerebral venous sinus thrombosis; with a life expectancy of < 3 months; Patients with transfer occurring during transport to the hospital; or diagnosed with severe mental disorders, dementia, or severe heart, liver, kidney, or lung failure were excluded. AIS was diagnosed based on findings from head computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging and diagnostic criteria outlined in the relevant guidelines12. The stroke subtypes were based on the Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) classification13.

Data collection and auditing

Data collection

A specialized online stroke case registration system was used for all eligible patients registered by dedicated stroke teams of the participating hospitals. A validated standardized questionnaire, which was completed by trained healthcare professionals, was used to collect basic patient information, details on stroke onset time and symptoms, and other related data. Within 24 h of patient admission, the research team interviewed patients and their families to gather information regarding symptoms before onset, location, and mode of transportation to the hospital.

In China, the classification of cities by population size is as follows: megacity, with a population of more than 10 million; super-large city, with a population of 5–10 million; large city, with a population of 1–5 million; medium-sized city, with a population between 500,000 and 1 million; small city, with a population of less than 500,00014. The number of research hospitals included in the study across different tiers of cities was as follows: one in megacities, six in super-large cities, seven in large cities, five in medium-sized cities, and two in small cities.

Data review

Data review standards stipulated that all registered cases must include all key data items, the answers recorded in the questionnaire should be crosschecked with data from electronic medical records to ensure information accuracy, and the data should be examined for outliers or logical inconsistencies and must meet inclusion and exclusion criteria. Outliers include timing errors (i.e., the recorded onset time of illness or medical visit exceeded reasonable limits), location data anomalies (i.e., errors in inputting a patient’s address, leading to incorrect geographic location), incorrect demographic information, logically inconsistent clinical assessment results (such as inaccurate National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, which can result in unusually high or low scores), and duplicate entries of the same patient’s data.

Classification of modified rankin scale (mRS) score and NIHSS score

The pre-stroke mRS score was categorized into three levels: 0–1 points (no or mild disability), 2–3 points (moderate disability), and 4–5 points (severe disability). This classification was mainly based on the clinical significance of the mRS score, research objectives, and mRS grading criteria from previous studies15, 16. It enables the differentiation of patients’ baseline functional status, facilitating the analysis of differences in EMS utilization among patients with different functional statuses.

The NIHSS score at admission was divided into three grades: 0–4 points (mild stroke), 5–15 points (moderate stroke), and 16–42 points (severe stroke). This grading referenced the stroke severity classification criteria used in studies by the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA), and other institutions17, 18, 19, and is consistent with the assessment practice for stroke severity in clinical settings.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (The R Project, Vienna, Austria). Normally and non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and median [interquartile range], respectively. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage). Between-group comparisons of measurement data were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test (non-normally distributed data) or t-test (normally distributed data), and between-group count data were compared using the chi-square test. The factors influencing EMS adoption were analyzed using univariate and multivariate logistic regression models. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Shenzhen University (project leader, no. 20200727003-FS01-XZ202103) and by the ethics committees of the participating hospitals. We confirm that all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The study is a prospective, low-risk observational study that involves no additional interventions beyond standard clinical care for patients with acute ischemic stroke. As no experimental procedures or invasive measures were administered, the ethics committees overseeing the study waived the requirement for individual patient informed consent. This waiver is consistent with institutional review board guidelines for observational research where risks to participants are minimal. This study is an analysis of aggregate clinical data and does not involve any experiments with human participants (including the use of tissue samples). Additionally, the manuscript does not contain any information or images that could lead to the identification of study participants. All data presented are de-identified and aggregated to ensure participant anonymity, in full compliance with ethical standards and publication guidelines.

Comparison of rescue efficiency between patients who arrived via EMS and those who arrived on their own

The onset-to-door time (ODT) of patients who arrived on their own was significantly longer than that of patients who arrived via EMS (P < 0.001). The proportion of patients with an ODT longer than 3 h was significantly higher for self-arrivals than for EMS arrivals (P < 0.001). Additionally, the rates of IVT and thrombectomy were significantly higher for EMS arrivals than for self-arrivals (P < 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively) (Table 2).

Analysis of factors influencing EMS use among patients with AIS

With EMS utilization as the dependent variable, the multivariate logistic regression model incorporated two categories of variables: (1) variables that showed statistical significance in the univariate analysis; and (2) factors that are professionally considered to be associated with the mode of hospital arrival. The results indicated that several factors promoted the use of EMS among patients, including older age (Odds Ratio [OR], 1.01; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1–1.02), residence in a small city (OR, 1.88; 95% CI, 1.17–3.00), WUS (OR, 1.46; 95% CI, 1.17–1.83), cardioembolic stroke (CES) (OR, 2.21; 95% CI, 1.74–2.80), and NIHSS scores of 5–15 (OR, 3.27; 95% CI, 2.72–3.93) and 16–42 (OR, 6.32; 95% CI, 4.91–8.13). Conversely, small–artery occlusion (SAO) (OR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.52–0.78) was associated with patients arriving independently (Table 3).

Discussion

This study revealed that the proportion of patients with AIS who utilize EMS to reach hospitals in China is relatively low. In addition, patients with AIS generally have lower educational levels and limited awareness of stroke emergency networks. Factors that promote EMS use include older age, residence in a small city, CES, WUS, and severe stroke. Conversely, SAO was associated with patients arriving to hospital independently.

EMS use among patients with AIS differs across countries

The proportions of patients with stroke who chose EMS for hospital transport significantly differ across countries. In this study, the utilization of EMS among patients with AIS in China was quite low, which concurs with reports indicating that only 3.2–15.4% of patients with stroke arrive at hospitals via ambulance20, 21. The utilization of ambulances in low- and middle-income countries is limited; in Pakistan, only 4.1% (n = 10,546) of patients arrive at emergency departments via ambulance22. Meanwhile, in the United States, 50%–59% of patients with stroke were reportedly transported to the hospital by EMS23, 24, whereas in Singapore, 8% of patients with AIS arrived via EMS as per the Singapore Stroke Registry25. Minority race, ethnicity, and residence in a rural community were associated with decreased odds of EMS use26. These substantial disparities are primarily influenced by various factors, including public health policies, public awareness, healthcare systems, cultural factors, economic conditions, and level of technical infrastructure. The effectiveness and accessibility of EMS are affected by construction of and investment in public health policies and emergency response systems.

Comparison of treatment outcomes for patients with AIS: EMS vs. self-arrival

In this study, patients who arrived via EMS had shorter ODT, lower rates of arrival delays, and higher rates of IVT and thrombectomy than those who arrived independently. The use of EMS can speed up the time to treatment and have a positive effect on minimizing treatment delays27. EMS transport is independently associated with earlier arrival (ODT ≤ 3 h), prompter evaluation (more patients with door-to-imaging time ≤ 25 min), faster treatment (more patients with door-to-needle time ≤ 60 min), and more eligible patients for IVT26. Priority dispatch and deployment are important aspects of stroke emergency response systems. Patients transported by ambulance benefit from specialized prehospital emergency care, including vital sign monitoring, stroke scale assessments, neurological examinations, early blood pressure management, blood glucose levels, and coagulation tests (and even portable computed tomography scans or IVT). The use of the EMS has been associated with reduced transport delays to the emergency department for acute stroke evaluation, higher thrombolytic therapy rates, and improved clinical outcomes28. In summary, EMS transportation can shorten ODT, accelerate imaging examinations, and reduce the door-to-needle time, ultimately improving stroke outcomes.

Influence of city size on EMS utilization

This study showed that small city size is a factor influencing the use of EMS for hospital transport by patients. The effect of city size on EMS use involves multiple factors, including population density, healthcare resources, traffic conditions, and cultural awareness. Large cities often have more healthcare facilities and specialized emergency services, making it easier for patients to access timely medical assistance. However, large cities may also face issues such as traffic congestion. The higher tendency of patients with AIS in small cities to use the EMS may stem from two main factors: residents there have relatively greater trust in the government-led emergency rescue system, and they often live farther from medical institutions with stroke centers—with family members also lacking the ability to transport patients with stroke quickly and safely—leading to greater reliance on EMS for transportation. Currently, there are relatively few comparative studies on the effect of different city sizes on EMS, and our research contributes to the understanding of this aspect.

Influence of culture and awareness on EMS utilization

Our study showed that the proportion of patients with AIS in China using EMS was very low, which may be closely related to the educational level (with 69.1% of patients having only a middle school or elementary school education and 7.6% being illiterate). The low awareness of SEMs (22.1%) indicate limited public interest in stroke emergency awareness. A low level of education is a significant factor contributing to insufficient EMS calls; patients with lower educational attainment tend to rely more on family members for decision-making, often preferring to wait for their family to return seeking hospital care. In incidents where “911” was called, 4.3% of the individuals activating EMS were the patients themselves, whereas 60.1% were family members or significant others6. The knowledge of prehospital EMS has been strongly associated with its utilization, highlighting the critical role of awareness and education in influencing EMS use29. Interestingly, knowledge of stroke symptoms did not correlate with increased EMS use (OR, 0.63)30. Currently, there is a lack of emphasis on promoting the benefits of EMS transportation for patients with AIS in China. Future efforts should focus on enhancing public awareness and underscoring the advantages of using EMS for timely hospital access in patients with stroke.

Influence of age on EMS utilization

Our study showed that older patients are more likely to use EMS for hospital transport. Individuals aged 60 years and older comprise one of the primary EMS user groups, and their EMS utilization rates increase with age22, 24, 31. Older adults often experience a higher prevalence of chronic diseases (such as heart disease, diabetes, and hypertension) and accidental injuries, which prompt them to seek EMS services more frequently32. Additionally, many older individuals may have decreased mobility, making EMS a preferable option to avoid the difficulties and risks associated with self-transportation to hospitals. Family members of older patients typically exhibit heightened concern for their health issues and may choose to call the EMS during emergencies to ensure that older adults receive timely medical assistance. Our findings underscore the vulnerability of older adults to AIS and the need for immediate medical care.

Influence of WUS on EMS utilization

WUS is a common presentation, accounting for 4%–28% of stroke incidence33. Functional outcomes are slightly worse in stroke-on-awakening patients34. In this study, we found that WUS is a significant factor driving patients to use EMS, likely due to the acute neurological deficits typically associated with waking up after a stroke, such as paralysis, speech difficulties, and limb numbness. Hence, patients and their families often quickly recognize the severity of their symptoms, which motivates them to seek prompt EMS assistance. Lower limb weakness (43.5%) was the most common symptom, followed by upper limb weakness (37.1%) and dysarthria (31.6%)35. Patients with WUS often present with more severe symptoms necessitating immediate medical attention36.

Influence of various stroke subtypes on EMS utilization

This study showed that patients with CES were more likely to utilize EMS for hospital transport, whereas those with SAO tended to arrive at the hospital on their own. The characteristics of different stroke subtypes, such as symptom presentation and onset, may influence patient decisions to seek EMS. CES is usually caused by thrombosis related to cardiac issues and presents with pronounced symptoms such as sudden weakness, speech difficulties, and loss of consciousness. Symptoms such as confusion/decreased levels of consciousness, aphasia, headache, and nausea/vomiting are associated with earlier hospital visits35. The symptoms that increased the odds of a 911 call were weakness, confusion/decreased levels of consciousness, speech/language, and dizziness/coordination/vertigo37. The acute onset often heightens the awareness of the seriousness of the situation, prompting patients and their families to call the EMS immediately. Patients experiencing altered consciousness or seizure symptoms (OR, 0.208; P < 0.01) are more likely to use EMS for medical care38. Dispatcher training is essential because misclassifying stroke cases can result in treatment delays, which negatively affect patient outcomes39. Currently, there is limited research on the relationship between different stroke subtypes and EMS use, and our study contributes to this understanding.

Influence of stroke severity on EMS utilization

This study showed that patients with more severe stroke (as measured by NIHSS scores) were more likely to use EMS for hospital transport. Stroke severity is associated with the use of EMS24, 37. The more severe the stroke symptoms, the more likely the patients and those around them were able to recognize the seriousness of the situation and seek EMS assistance quickly. Family members, paid caregivers, and coworkers play an important role in assisting patients with EMS, and patients rarely seek EMS assistance on their own6. More severe conditions may prompt family members or caregivers to opt for ambulances to ensure prompt hospital arrival. Moderate (OR, 6.31) and severe (OR, 8.44) symptoms were associated with calling EMS as the first medical contact9. Training dispatchers to recognize stroke symptoms can significantly improve the identification and triage of patients with stroke.

Other factors influencing the decision to choose EMS

Enrollment in private medical insurance significantly affects EMS40. Mexican–Americans have a significant stroke burden and are less likely to arrive at the hospital by EMS than non-Hispanic whites41. There is a pressing need to promote an urgent response to stroke symptoms, particularly among groups that experience the highest stroke burden. Racial-, ethnic-, and sex-based differences have been observed in EMS transport among hospitalized patients with stroke. White women are most likely to use EMS (62%), whereas Hispanic and Asian men and women have adjusted odds of using EMS that are 20% to 29% lower than their white counterparts22.

Implications of the study findings for advancing equity in stroke care services

First, regarding patient education and public awareness campaigns, targeted strategies should be tailored to populations with low educational attainment and limited knowledge of stroke emergency networks. Public education should specifically emphasize that even mild stroke symptoms (e.g., dizziness, mild limb weakness) require timely transport via the EMS42. Given that family members are the primary decision-makers when seeking care, these campaigns should also engage family caregivers—especially those in households with older adults or individuals at high risk of cardioembolic stroke. Second, strengthening EMS systems must prioritize underserved regions, particularly small cities. Small cities may face challenges such as insufficient ambulance coverage or longer response times, which are often attributed to the lower population density. Investments in these regions should focus on three key areas: expanding ambulance stations, integrating mobile stroke units, and telestroke diagnosis and treatment, which could break through geographical limitations and effectively shorten the treatment time43, 44.

Limitations

Although this study encompassed multiple cities across China with varying economic and cultural backgrounds, it did not reflect global trends or extend to rural areas. Despite our efforts to include numerous factors influencing transportation choices, some may have been inadvertently omitted. The findings of this study are limited to comprehensive stroke centers in urban areas. Therefore, the results of this study cannot represent the current situation in rural areas. When conducting international comparisons, full consideration should be given to differences in EMS systems, healthcare accessibility, and sociocultural backgrounds among different countries. Notably, the NIHSS scores were assessed at hospital admission rather than at the time of symptom onset. However, given the typically short onset-to-door time in patients with AIS, during which neurological deficits reflected by the NIHSS score remain largely stable, this assumption may not hold true for a small number of patients with rapidly progressive stroke.

Conclusions

In China, the proportion of patients with AIS who use EMS after the onset of symptoms is low. This may be related to cultural factors and a lack of awareness of EMS, highlighting the need for enhanced education regarding its use. Older age, residence in a small city, WUS, stroke subtype, and experiencing severe stroke are factors that encourage the use of EMS. Conversely, self-arrival at a hospital is influenced by the presence of SAO. Interventions should specifically target patients exhibiting these characteristics as well as aim to improve transfer practices.
