Prodrug Nanomedicine for Synovium Targeted Therapy of Inflammatory Arthritis: Insights from Animal Model and Human Synovial Joint Fluid
Authors: Zeynab Nosrati, Yun An Chen, Marta Bergamo, Cristina Rodríguez‐Rodríguez, Jonathan Chan, Kam Shojania, Raheem B. Kherani, Carson Chin, John T. Kelsall, Natasha Dehghan, Ann Marie Colwill, David Collins, Katayoun Saatchi, Urs O. Häfeli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany patients cannot tolerate low‐dose weekly methotrexate (MTX) therapy for inflammatory arthritis treatment due to life‐threatening toxicity. Although biologics offer a target‐specific therapy, it raises the risk of serious infections and even cancer due to immune system suppression. We introduce an anti‐inflammatory arthritis MTX ester prodrug using a long‐circulating biocompatible polymeric macromolecule: folic acid (FA) functionalized hyperbranched polyglycerol (HPG). In vitro the drug MTX is incrementally released through pH and enzymatic degradation over 2 weeks. The role of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) in site‐specific prodrug activation was verified using synovial fluid (SF) of 26 rheumatology patients and 4 healthy controls. Elevated levels of specific MMPs‐markers of joint inflammation‐positively correlated with enhanced prodrug release explained by acid‐catalyzed hydrolysis of esters by proteases. Intravenously administered 111In‐radiolabeled prodrug confirmed by SPECT/CT imaging that it accumulated preferentially in inflamed joints while reducing off‐target side‐effects in a mouse model of rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Added FA as a targeting vector prolonged prodrug action; prodrug with 4x less MTX applied every 2 weeks was as effective as weekly MTX therapy. The preclinical results suggest a prodrug‐based strategy for the treatment of inflammatory joint diseases, with potential for other chronic inflammatory diseases and cancer.

Introduction

Inflammatory Arthritis (IA) is a chronic disease caused by an overactive immune system inappropriately targeting connective tissues in the body. There are several different types of IA, including Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA), Gout, Psoriatic Arthritis (PsA), Ankylosing Spondylitis (AS) and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE).[ 1 ] RA is the most common IA causing synovial inflammation typically in a symmetrical pattern ultimately leading to destruction of cartilage and bone, and thereby disability.[ 2 ] For the purposes of this article, we will mainly be discussing RA, however other types of IA have similar features and treatment approaches. Synovitis (or synovial inflammation) is the hallmark of RA and its severity varies with disease progression. In RA, immune activation of macrophages leads to synovitis through a series of inflammatory reactions including inflammatory cell infiltration, angiogenesis that results in increased vasculature permeability, up‐regulation of cytokines, chemokines, and numerous inflammation‐associated receptors.[ 3 ] The inflammatory microenvironment is commonly characterized by an acidic pH (≈6.4),[ 4 ] overexpression of cell‐surface receptors (e.g., folate receptor),[ 5 ] reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (e.g., hydrogen peroxide and peroxynitrite),[ 6 , 7 ] enzymes (e.g., phospholipases and cycloxygenases),[ 8 ] and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs).[ 9 ]

While considerable progress has been made to develop new medicines to treat RA, there is currently no cure. Optimal treatment includes rapid diagnosis and initiation of disease‐modifying anti‐rheumatic drugs (DMARDs).[ 10 ] While awaiting DMARD response, non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and glucocorticoids can be used for symptomatic improvement. DMARDs can be divided into two major classes – conventional and targeted therapies.[ 11 , 12 ] Methotrexate (MTX) is a conventional synthetic DMARD and the initial drug of choice alone or in combination for most RA patients.[ 13 ] However, patients with inadequate response or poor tolerance to MTX are switched to or combined with biologics/targeted therapies (TNF α antagonists, IL‐6 antagonists, T‐cell modulating agents, etc.).[ 14 ] The main reason for discontinuing DMARDs is due to their adverse effects and/or lack of efficacy, as for example reported by a clinical study where 66% of patients (162/244) had discontinued MTX monotherapy after two years.[ 15 ] Other types of IA, including PsA, AS, SLE, as well as non‐specific (or as yet, undifferentiated) IA, may be also treated with MTX. For IA, MTX is administered weekly, orally or subcutaneously.[ 16 ] The oral formulation is limited to low doses, as the drug bioavailability at higher doses is constrained by the saturation of gastrointestinal (GI) transporters.[ 17 , 18 ] While the parenteral route avoids these transport problems, and may have reduced GI side effects, it still has the challenges of short blood circulation, poor aqueous solubility, and nonselective targeting, which hinder the therapeutic effectiveness of parenteral MTX.

Over recent years, various drug delivery systems (DDS) have been studied to address challenges associated with the efficient delivery of poorly water‐soluble anti‐inflammatory and anti‐rheumatic therapeutics through passive and/or active targeting.[ 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] Among those, environmentally responsive DDS are of great interest in RA, where the abnormal microenvironment of inflamed synovium is instrumental in the design and development of such systems.[ 24 , 25 , 26 ] Inflammation‐responsive DDS have been engineered to respond to pathologic stimuli such as oxidative stress,[ 27 , 28 , 29 ] acidic pH,[ 30 , 31 ] and inflammatory enzymes.[ 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 ] Due to the complexities and variations of an inflammatory microenvironment among patients, systems capable of responding to two stimuli have been more favorable than single‐response systems.[ 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 ] The integration of several responsive moieties within one polymer have made polymeric systems superior to other drug carriers with considerable applications in personalized medicine.[ 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 ]

Macromolecular polymers, including hyperbranched polymers and dendrimers have attracted great attention as drug delivery vehicles due to their high degree of surface functionality.[ 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 ] Advantages of hyperbranched polymers over dendrimers include short manufacturing time, easy scale up, and lower cost.[ 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 ] High molecular weight hyperbranched polyglycerol (HPG) is an unimolecular globular polymeric macromolecule with polyether polyol structure.

With its high water solubility (> 400 mg/mL), excellent biocompatibility (better than polyethylene glycol), non‐toxicity, hemocompatibility,[ 57 , 58 ] tunable blood circulation time (between 10 and 48 h based on their molecular weight), reliable biological half‐life, as well as multifunctionality, HPG is a highly promising vehicle in drug delivery applications.[ 59 , 60 , 61 , 62 ]

In the present work, we have proposed a novel therapeutic approach using HPG as a delivery vehicle to obtain selective synovium targeting and on‐demand drug release in response to multiple inflammatory stimuli present in RA. By i) turning a pharmacologically active small molecule drug into a hydrolyzable ester macromolecular prodrug, and ii) tethering folic acid (FA) to its surface, we aim to deliver more of the drug specifically to inflamed joints, achieve an extended‐release profile within the synovium, maintain an appropriate drug concentration at the site of inflammation for a longer period of time, improve effectiveness at lower doses, and avoid side effects in other organs. MTX with a well‐characterized pharmacokinetic profile was used as the example drug to validate the proposed therapeutic strategy for further development. Furthermore, we determined the level of MMPs in the synovial fluid (SF) of healthy donors and patients with RA, PsA, and AS and examined the correlation between the MMP levels and kinetics of drug release from our macromolecular prodrug system. As a proof of concept, we report this multi stimuli‐responsive macromolecular prodrug system as a targeted therapeutic agent for IA.

Results

Synthesis and Characterization of a Macromolecular Prodrug Platform

HPG was synthesized using anionic ring‐opening polymerization.[ 52 ] Analysis by multi‐angle laser light scattering (MALLS) coupled to gel permeation chromatography (GPC) confirmed a relatively monosized macromolecule (PDI = 1.008, MW = 840 kDa). HPG was amine modified to a short PEG‐NH2 linker using reductive amination and then tethered to FA by peptide coupling at five different HPG:FA molar ratios (19, 38, 57, 75 or 150 mol FA per mol HPG). The prodrug was constructed by conjugating MTX (and [3H]MTX) to HPG (and HPG‐FA) by esterification using the Mukaiyama reagent. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) confirmed the structures of HPG, HPG‐NH2, HPG‐FA, and MTX prodrug (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The structure of the folate‐functionalized macromolecular MTX prodrug is illustrated in Figure 1A.

A radioactive version of the prodrug was prepared by radiolabeling HPG with the radioisotope 111In prior to drug binding. This was achieved by initial HPG amination and further coupling the amines to a transcyclooctene linker (NHS‐PEG4‐TCO). After click chemistry with a tetrazine‐modified chelator (DTPA‐tzn) the nanoensemble was suitable for 111In3+ binding. Leftover amines were quenched by a short PEG linker during or after TCO modification. 1H NMR confirmed the presence of FA and the PEG linker and integration showed a HPG:FA:PEG molar ratio of roughly 1:57:30 prior to drug loading (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The 111In‐radiolabeled conjugates were further investigated for stability in the presence of excess EDTA and via apo‐transferrin challenge over the course of 2 weeks. After 2 weeks of incubation with a 100‐fold molar excess EDTA solution, more than 60% of 111In remained bound to both HPG and HPG‐FA conjugates (Figure S2A, Supporting Information). Apo‐transferrin competition experiments demonstrated excellent stability of 111In‐HPG at 2 weeks with over 85% bound intact to HPG. However, in HPG‐FA derivatives, higher FA molar ratios led to a decrease in radiolabeling stability to 73 ± 0.73%. This could be due to the binding affinity of FA to serum proteins.[ 63 ] that might affect the total formulation stability (Figure S2B, Supporting Information).

The DLS analysis of the HPG displayed an average particle size of approximately 10 nm, with a fairly low polydispersity (PDI = 0.162). A slight increase in particle size was observed with the grafting of FA to HPG (≈17 nm) regardless of the extent of FA conjugation (Figure 1B). Zeta potential measurements revealed that positively charged amino functionalized HPG (10.30 ± 4.92 mV) became neutrally charged upon FA‐conjugation and 111In radiolabeling. MTX loading caused an increase of hydrodynamic diameters to around 120–130 nm while the PDI and zeta potential did not change significantly (Table S1, Supporting Information).

UV/Vis spectroscopy was used to determine the average number of FA and MTX molecules conjugated per macromolecule (Figure 1C). Absorption spectra of HPG‐FA conjugates showed a gradual increase in absorption at 363 nm upon increasing the initial FA feeding molar ratio (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Furthermore, the number of MTX molecules per macromolecule was analyzed at the absorbance peak of 390 nm (Figure 1C) by spectral subtraction of samples before and after MTX loading. The number of MTX molecules loaded on straight HPG was highest and decreased as the number of conjugated FA molecules increased (Figure 1C).

Enhanced Intracellular Uptake of Folate Functionalized Biocompatible Carrier

Using qualitative live‐cell confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), we investigated the effect of FA modification on the cellular uptake of macromolecular carrier in LPS‐stimulated macrophages using HPG modified with Alexa 647. It was noted that macromolecular HPG was avidly phagocytosed by macrophages regardless of whether FA was conjugated or not (Figure 1D). However, increasing the number of conjugated FA from 1:19 mol:mol HPG:FA to 1:150, at a fixed HPG concentration (0.5 mg mL−1) resulted in a proportional increase of endocytosis, likely fueled by folate‐receptor activation.[ 64 , 65 ] The mean Alexa 647 fluorescence per cell was then obtained and plotted for each individual treatment (Figure 1E). It was found that FA increased the fluorescence intensity by about two‐fold when the FA concentration was increased from 19 to 150 mole equivalents per HPG. Compared to HPG without FA, the fluorescence signal of HPG‐FA (1:150) was even five‐fold higher. The relatively weak signal of HPG uptake can be explained by macrophage phagocytosis, while the increased entry of HPG‐FA complexes into LPS‐activated macrophages was the result of both phagocytosis and receptor‐mediated endocytosis.[ 66 ]

Enhanced nanoparticle uptake can contribute to greater cytotoxicity in macrophage cells.[ 67 ] M0‐ and M1‐ polarized RAW 264.7 cells were used to study the cytotoxicity of folate‐functionalized macromolecules. In non‐activated macrophages (M0), there was no change in the proliferation of cells treated with HPG‐FAs at all tested concentrations (0–5 mg mL−1) over the 120 h incubation time (Figure S4A, Supporting Information). LPS‐stimulated macrophages (M1) exposed to HPGs loaded with different ratios of FA were also not toxic at concentrations below 1 mg mL−1 over the same time. However, an increase in concentration to 5 mg mL−1 caused severe impairment of cell proliferation after 48 h of incubation (Figure S4B, Supporting Information). This can be explained by the fact that LPS triggers macrophage activation and leads to enhanced phagocytosis and folate receptor endocytosis. Since activated macrophages are more sensitive to cytotoxicity than non‐activated macrophages, 1 mg mL−1 HPG‐FA was selected as the highest concentration for further in vivo experiments.

Inflammatory Microenvironment Activates Macromolecular Prodrug

The release profile of MTX from the polymeric macromolecule was obtained at physiological blood pH (7.4) and a mildly acidic environment simulating endo/lysosomal compartments (pH 5.6). Both HPG‐FA‐MTX and HPG‐MTX prodrugs gradually released MTX over a period of 14 days (Figure 2A), showing a relatively good hydrolytic stability at pH 7.4 (<40% release after two weeks). By comparison (Figure 2B,C), a noticeably increased release of MTX was observed at acidic pH due to the accelerated hydrolysis of the ester linker (P < 0.05). At pH 5.6, MTX release was significantly increased to 71.2 ± 5.8% for HPG‐MTX and 82.5 ± 13.3% for HPG‐FA‐MTX, at the 2‐week time point. Free MTX was used as a control. More than 80% was measured in the receiving compartment within 24 h while pH had no effect on it.

In IA, the presence of many intracellular esterases favor the hydrolysis of ester‐type prodrugs and trigger drug release. To evaluate enzymatic hydrolysis, the prodrug was incubated in PBS (pH 7.4) with porcine liver esterase at 200 and 600 U mL−1. In the absence of esterase, MTX‐loaded conjugates demonstrated excellent stability to non‐specific hydrolysis in PBS, with less than 40% cumulative release of MTX over a period of two weeks (Figure 2D,E). Addition of esterase (200 U mL−1) increased cumulative drug release by over 1.5‐fold at each study time point. In addition, when prodrug was incubated with higher amounts of esterase (600 U mL−1), we observed a further significant increase (P < 0.05) in hydrolysis and MTX release to ≈2.3‐fold (Figure 2F).

A continuous release of MTX from HPG‐FA was achieved when incubated in SF of individual patients with RA (n = 14), PsA (n = 7), AS (n = 5), and pooled SF of healthy donors (n = 4). The MTX release rate was compared with release in PBS (Figure 2G). By integration of the area under the cumulative drug release curve, we found that the net drug release in SF of PsA, RA and AS patients were significantly higher compared to PBS. However, cumulative release did not differ significantly between PBS and healthy SF (Figure 2H). Furthermore, we showed that addition of the general MMP‐inhibitor GM 6001 to SF of RA patients significantly suppressed MTX release from the macromolecular conjugates, while it did not influence the release of free MTX (Figure 2I–L).

To further investigate the effect of MMPs in prodrug activation, MMPs levels of SF were assayed in PsA, RA, and AS patients and compared to SF levels of healthy human joints (Figure 3A–H). Results showed that the level of MMP‐1, MMP‐2, MMP‐3, MMP‐10, and MMP‐13 greatly increased in SF of patients with RA, PsA, and AS compared to healthy controls, while there was no significant alteration in MMP‐8. However, we found higher level of MMP‐9 and MMP‐12 in PsA and RA, respectively. The SFs with elevated MMP enzymes released more MTX over the two weeks of incubation (Figure 3I–P). Using the Pearson correlation method, we were able to demonstrate a moderate‐to‐strong positive correlation between the cumulative drug release and the levels of MMP‐1, MMP‐2, MMP‐3, MMP‐9, MMP‐10, and MMP‐13. However, no statistically significant correlation was observed with MMP‐8 and MMP‐12 levels (p > 0.05).

Macromolecular MTX Prodrug Targets Arthritic Joints In Vivo

We used single photon emission computed tomography combined with computed tomography (SPECT/CT) imaging to visualize the biodistribution and evaluate the pharmacokinetics of macromolecular carriers in vivo. For this purpose, 50 µL of 111In radiolabeled HPG or HPG‐FA was intravenously injected into arthritic mice, and HPG‐FA also into healthy mice. SPECT/CT images revealed the presence of the macromolecular carrier in the blood within the first 24 h in all mice, as evidenced by the high signal in the heart (Figure 4A–C). Time–uptake curves for the heart were produced with standardized uptake values (SUV mean) (Figure 4D). 111In activity was visible within inflamed knees and hind paws up to the last 10‐day time‐point (Figure 4A,B). As expected, there was no detectable 111In activity in the joint tissues of control mice administered with 111In‐HPG‐FA (Figure 4C). A two‐compartment model with a mean coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.9995 was applied to obtain quantitative parameters (Table S2, Supporting Information). Surface modification with FA significantly prolonged plasma half‐life and enhanced mean resident time (MRT) of HPG in arthritic mice (P < 0.05). The overall uptake of the folate‐targeting macromolecule in inflamed joints was not statistically different from the non‐targeting macromolecule (Figure 4E–G). However, our data clearly demonstrated increased retention of 111In‐HPG‐FA compared to 111In‐HPG (Table S3, Supporting Information). The longer retention of folate‐targeting macromolecule possibly indicates receptor‐mediated endocytosis, while the non‐targeting macromolecule are taken into the cell through phagocytosis.[ 68 ] Overall, our data confirm the significance of the folate targeting strategy as an effective clinical approach, as it offers greater drug retention and intracellular uptake.

We further evaluated the pharmacokinetics of the macromolecular MTX prodrugs (with and without FA targeting) in CIA mice over 14 days (Figure 4H,I). Findings showed the maximum uptake for HPG‐MTX at day 2 and for HPG‐FA‐MTX at day 3. In all joints of interest, both the maximum and overall uptake values were significantly higher for the folate‐targeting prodrug compared to the non‐targeting one (Figure 4J–L). Cumulatively, tethering folate to the surface of the macromolecular prodrug increased the overall accumulation in the inflamed joints significantly by more than 1.5‐fold (Figure 4M). The retention pattern of the folate‐targeting macromolecular prodrug in the inflamed joints allows for local and sustained delivery of MTX and maintains the therapeutic drug concentrations at the target joints for two weeks.

We further explored the distribution of both MTX and macromolecular vehicles simultaneously in the same animal through a dual‐radiotracer approach with 111In radiolabeled macromolecules conjugated with the 3H‐labelled MTX. Concentrations of 3H and 111In within inflamed joints reached their maximum at 48 h and gradually decreased to half and one‐third of their maximum uptake values on day 14, respectively (Figure 5A–C). This comparatively slower clearance of 3H than 111In from joints confirmed sustained unloading of MTX from the macromolecular carrier and prolonged retention of the cleaved drug within inflamed joints.

AUCs of the 3H and 111In radioactivity concentration curves indicated significantly enhanced uptake and retention of the folate‐targeted formulation compared to the non‐targeted one, which could be explained by the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, followed by folate receptor‐mediated endocytosis (Figure 5D,E). The pharmacokinetic patterns of 111In and 3H radioactivity in the blood and organs are shown in Figure 5F. The high concentration of both radiotracers in the blood at the initial time point and gradual reduction over time confirmed prolonged blood circulation of macromolecular carriers and the conjugated drug. This enabled the effective accumulation of targeted and non‐targeted formulations within inflamed joints, as evidenced by 111In and 3H concentrations. The macromolecular prodrug was primarily distributed in the liver, and spleen; the two major organs for macrophage cell uptake after systematic administration. The kidney uptake at early time points might be associated with large amounts (20–25% of cardiac output) of blood with radioactive content passing through in the renal blood flow.[ 69 ] The minimal radioactivity in the muscle and bone at all time points confirms that uptake by inflammatory joint tissue is not associated with surrounding bone and muscle uptake. Moreover, the agreement between the biodistribution of both radiotracers suggested that ex vivo biodistribution may accurately reflect macromolecular prodrug biodistribution in vivo.

Macromolecular MTX Prodrug Enhances Anti‐Inflammatory Efficacy

The in vivo therapeutic efficacy of the MTX prodrug was investigated in the same murine CIA model with mice having moderate‐to‐severe arthritis when scored with infrared thermal imaging (IRT) to have a temperature index ≥ 0.9 and paw thickness ≥ 2.5 for individual paws.[ 70 ] The treatment effects were assessed by measuring paw temperature, thickness, and clinical scoring (Figure 6A–F). MTX twice weekly reduced disease severity and caused a significant reduction in IRT, paw thickness, and clinical scoring AUCs (P < 0.05). Weekly and biweekly folate‐targeted HPG‐MTX, as well as weekly HPG‐MTX appeared to be equally effective as MTX twice weekly in reducing disease severity and progression. As expected, CIA mice receiving saline (Ctl +) exhibited a progressive increase in disease severity over time, while healthy mice (Ctl ‐), treated with saline, did not develop arthritis. The synovium of the ankle was also evaluated for histologic features by blinded pathologists, revealing severe inflammatory cell infiltration, synovial hyperplasia, and bone destruction in the saline‐treated arthritic animals (Figure 6G). As with the first assessment method, mice treated with MTX twice weekly displayed the most significant reduction (P < 0.05) in total histopathologic score compared to positive control (Figure 6H). However, there was no significant difference between CIA mice that received MTX twice a week and those that received HPG‐FA‐MTX once a week (P = 0.99). These results indicate that active FA targeting minimizes off‐target effects and allows for reduced drug dose while still achieving a therapeutic response equivalent to the current standard free MTX dosing regimen. Weekly MTX treatment did not reach therapeutic MTX levels since no significant changes were found between CIA mice treated with weekly MTX and saline (P > 0.05).

To assess the potential toxic effects of macromolecular prodrug formulation in a longer course of administration, we analyzed alanine transferase (ALT), aspartate transferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) liver enzyme activities to observe general liver function as well as serum creatinine for nephrotoxicity (Figure 6I–L). As expected, we observed an elevated level of serum liver enzyme (ALP and ALT) in CIA mice at baseline (48 h after LPS injection) which can be explained by the cytotoxic effect of LPS. Multiple intravenous administrations of MTX and the prodrug formulations (6 mg kg−1 weekly cumulative dose of MTX over 2 weeks) were well tolerated by the arthritic mice. Safety studies showed no significant effect on the function of liver and kidneys, supporting the safety of our macromolecular MTX prodrug.

Discussion

Inflammatory form of arthritis (including RA, PsA and AS) is a complex and progressive medical condition where inflammation occurs in several joints of the body. Here, based on the targeted‐prodrug approach, we developed a novel injectable polymeric drug delivery system for IA treatment. Our study revealed enhanced accumulation of prodrug in inflamed joints due to its extended circulation time as shown by SPECT/CT and ex vivo assessment with 3H‐MTX. Second, the drug stays for extended time periods at the site of action, especially if folic acid is conjugated to the prodrug as a targeting moiety.

Polymeric‐based prodrug systems have been extensively used in achieving site‐specific drug delivery, in which drug and polymer linked together via stimuli‐cleavable bond such as hydrazones, acetals, imines, and esters.[ 71 ] These systems are typically engineered to respond to exogenous and/or endogenous triggers at the target site. The presence of various stimuli within the inflammatory environment in arthritic joints, such as acidic pH, elevated temperature, oxidative stress, and inflammatory enzymes, can trigger the delivery system to release their cargo and achieve a therapeutic effect at the disease sites. These triggers are subject to variability between patients.[ 72 , 73 ] The more severe the inflammation and synovitis, the more pronounced the endogenous triggers.[ 74 ] Here, MTX was conjugated with HPG via the formation of ester linkage, which is sensitive to pH and can be hydrolyzed by esterases under acidic conditions.

MMPs, whose expression play an important role in pathological condition of cancer, are known as key enzymes in synovitis and contribute to excessive degradation of joint tissue in IA. Here we showed greater expression of MMP‐1, MMP‐2, MMP‐3, MMP‐9, MMP‐10, and MMP‐13 in SF samples of RA, PsA and AS patients compared to healthy SF samples. MMP‐responsive strategy has been used extensively in drug delivery and tumor targeting while there are limited studies in the area of IA.[ 75 ] To our knowledge, only two studies have reported an MMP‐responsive delivery system for RA treatment. Joshi et al. investigated a hydrogel‐based intra‐articular drug delivery system that was responsive after being exposed to MMP enzymes as well as synovial fluid from patients with RA.[ 76 ] In a more recent study, He et al. designed an MMP‐responsive PEGylated lipid nanoparticle for the treatment of RA.[ 77 ]

Here, we exhibited the controlled and sustained release of MTX from a polymeric prodrug through hydrolytic cleavage of esters in response to MMPs present in the inflamed SF. We also showed elevated levels of specific MMPs, positively correlated with enhanced prodrug release. This is consistent with the theory of acid‐catalyzed hydrolysis of esters by proteases.[ 78 ] The in vivo results demonstrated that several fold higher release of MTX in the arthritic joints, resulting in a much higher local drug exposure. Therefore, the MTX dose could be reduced by at least four‐fold when given as a prodrug, leading to the same suppression of inflammation over 2 weeks after just one injection versus the current standard weekly treatment dose.

The prodrug approach with our polymer is therapeutically effective, can significantly reduce the toxicity of the treatment, and could be used to treat a large variety of rheumatic conditions including rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, ankylosing spondylitis, cutaneous psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, sarcoidosis, and uveitis, to name a few. It also can potentially be used for many other applications and drugs, including passively targeted chronic inflammatory diseases and cancer with leaky tumor vessels, where the polymer will accumulate due to the EPR effect. However, due to the highly functionable surface of HPG, the prodrug could be modified with specialized targeting moieties capable of binding synovial cellular markers, making it more specific.[ 79 ] Highly effective – but because of that often also highly toxic – drugs that can be esterified with our polymer are further prime examples for the described successful prodrug strategy.

While certain aspects still need to be investigated, for example, studies of long‐term toxicity, potentially slower drug release, and especially clinical efficacy where we need to show in human trials whether this approach remains effective to maintain patient remission, our study suggests that a polymeric MTX prodrug is a nanomedicine with the potential to reduce IA pain, swelling and damage with fewer adverse effects, at much lower doses than currently used with a longer duration of action.

Experimental Section

Study Design

In this study, a prodrug nanomedicine platform was manufactured, for synovium targeted delivery of antirheumatic drugs and their target‐specific activation. In vitro cytotoxicity and cellular uptake studies were performed in LPS‐stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophage cell line using Incucyte Live‐Cell Analysis System and confocal laser scanning microscope. In vitro prodrug release mechanism was conducted in response to acidic pH, esterase and synovial knee joint fluids collected from 26 donors who were between 18–80 years old with clinical symptoms of knee joint synovitis and visited physicians across Vancouver, BC, Canada, between June 2019 and October 2020. The protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee of the University of British Columbia (protocol number H18‐02049), and all participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. The expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) in synovial fluids were assessed using multiplex bead‐based Eve Technologies Discovery assay. A correlation study was conducted to assess the statistical relationship between the two variables of MMP expression and the amount of enzyme‐triggered drug release in patient synovial fluids. In vivo pharmacokinetic, efficacy and toxicity studies involved an acute arthritis mouse model, in accordance with protocol (A16‐0178) approved by the UBC Animal Care Committee at the UBC Centre of Comparative Medicine (CCM).

Syntheses and Characterization of Methotrexate Prodrug

MTX was conjugated to the hydroxyl groups of HPG through esterification. Briefly, a mixture of MTX (7 mg, 15 µmol) and lyophilized 111In‐HPG (or 111In‐HPG‐FA; 20 mg) were dissolved in 3000 µL of DMSO under argon atmosphere with stirring until dissolved. For quantitative studies, 3H‐MTX was added together with the non‐radioactive MTX. Afterward, a solution of 2‐chloro‐1‐methylpyridinium iodide (CMPI, Aldrich, 97%; 7.5 mg, 29 µmol) and 4‐dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP, Acros Organics, 99%; 7.5 mg, 60 µmol) in anhydrous DMF (Vt = 1 mL) was added and stirred at room temperature for 2 d. The reaction mixture was quenched with water and dialyzed against PBS (2 L) for 1 d and water (2 L, daily change) for 2 d. The final solution was concentrated using Amicon Ultra® (100 kDa MWCO), washed twice and collected in water. Final compound was characterized by 1H NMR spectrometry (D2O, 400 MHz): δ 1.2 – 4.5 (m), 6.5 – 9.0 (m); UV–vis spectroscopy, dynamic light scattering (DLS), zeta potential, beta scintillation, and gamma counting.

Cell Culture

RAW264.7 cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and were used to mimic human macrophage cells for testing cytotoxicity and uptake. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Life Technologies) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO2 and 100% humidity.

Real‐Time Cell Proliferation

The effect of the macrophage‐activating agent lipopolysaccharide (LPS, from Escherichia coli 055:B5; Sigma‐Aldrich) on proliferation and doubling time of RAW 264.7 cells was tested. RAW 264.7 macrophages were plated in a 96 well‐plate (5000 cells per well) and cultured with or without LPS (100 ng mL−1) in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat inactivated FBS containing 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Using the IncuCyte™ ZOOM instrument (Essen Bioscience), cells were monitored over 120 h and phase‐contrast images were collected every 3 h. Exponential cell growth curves were obtained by graphing phase object confluence (%) versus time (h). Plotting the log of exponential growth curve over time resulted in a straight line where the slope was its growth rate constant (k). Cell population doubling times for both LPS and non‐LPS stimulated cells were determined by calculating ln(2)/k.

In Vitro Cytotoxicity of Polymeric Carrier

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in a 96‐well plate at 5000 cells per well in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat inactivated FBS containing 1% (v/v) penicillin/ streptomycin. To generate M1‐type macrophages (activated macrophages) cells were treated with LPS (100 ng mL−1) and cultured overnight. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium containing HPG or HPG‐FA at concentrations of 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1, and 5 mg mL−1. The plates were then placed in the IncuCyte™ ZOOM with a 10X objective in a standard cell culture incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Phase‐contrast images were collected every 3 h (four images per well) to determine proliferation rates in cells. This study was done in 6 replicates.

In Vitro Cellular Uptake of Polymeric Carrier

The cellular uptake of HPG and HPG‐FA was investigated in RAW 264.7 cells using confocal live‐cell imaging. Cells were harvested at 80% confluency and plated in 35 mm glass bottomed confocal dishes at concentration of 9 × 105 cells / dish in 1 mL folate‐free RPMI medium and allowed to attach overnight. To up‐regulate the surface folate receptor expression, cells were treated with 100 ng mL−1 LPS for 24 h. Afterward, the medium was discarded and replaced with fresh folate‐free RPMI medium (1 mL) with A647‐HPG or A647‐HPG‐FA at 5 mg mL−1 concentration or free Alexa 647 dye (2.5 µg mL−1, control). Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33 342 8 µm (Thermo Fisher) and the membrane with LysoTracker™ Green DND‐26 1 µm (Thermo Fisher) simultaneously for 10 min. Afterward, the cells were washed and visualized with the confocal microscope. Hoechst 33 342 was excited with a 405 nm laser and the emission measured from 420–480 nm. LysoTracker™ Green DND‐26 was excited with a 488 nm argon ion laser, and the emission measured from 505 to 535 nm.

In Vitro Release in Response to Environmental Stimuli

Float‐A‐Lyzer G2 devices with 50 kDa MWCO (Repligen) were used to investigate the cumulative release profile of MTX from HPG‐MTX and HPG‐FA‐MTX formulations in response to acidic pH and esterases. After filling the internal compartment of the Float‐A‐Lyzer with 1 mL formulation (HPG‐[3H] MTX or HPG‐FA‐[3H] MTX; 0.5 mg mL−1), it was placed into a 50 mL tube filled with 45 mL sink medium: i) PBS at pH = 7.4 or 5.6, and ii) PBS without and with esterase (porcine liver esterase; 200 or 600 U mL−1). The whole system was incubated at 37 °C with a shaking speed of 150 rpm. An aliquot (1 mL) of sink medium was removed at predetermined time points (from 0.5 h to 14 d) and replaced with the same volume of fresh PBS to ensure constant sink conditions and analyzed for free [3H]MTX with the beta‐liquid scintillation counter (Beckmann LS6500).

In Vitro Release in Response to Synovial Joint Fluid

SF used throughout this study was obtained from patients diagnosed with PsA (n = 7), RA (n = 14), and AS (n = 5) according to established criteria and normal donors (n = 4). SF from the affected knee of patients was kindly provided by a group of 8 rheumatologists with the patients’ informed consent regarding the study. Normal SF specimens were acquired from post‐mortem donors (Articular Engineering, Northbrook, IL, USA). The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee at the University of British Columbia (protocol number H18‐02049). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. IA and normal SF specimens were processed and quantified for eight MMPs (MMP‐1, MMP‐2, MMP‐3, MMP‐8, MMP‐9, MMP‐10, MMP‐12, and MMP‐13) using a human MMP multiplex assay (Eve Technologies). Patient characteristics are given in Table S4 (Supporting Information).

The in vitro drug release study was performed using a Float‐A‐Lyzer dialysis bag with MWCO of 50 kDa. A volume of 0.5 mL HPG‐[3H]MTX (or HPG‐FA‐[3H]MTX; 0.5 mg mL−1) was mixed with 0.5 mL individual SF from patients, pooled SF from healthy donors or PBS, enclosed in a dialysis bag and incubated in 45 mL PBS media at 37 °C while shaking at 150 rpm. Furthermore, a general MMP‐inhibitor GM6001.[ 80 ] was added to SF of both RA patients and healthy control samples at the required concentration (100 µm). At predetermined time points (from 0.5 h to 14 d), 1 mL sink media was withdrawn and analyzed for free [3H]MTX with the beta‐liquid scintillation counter. The same volume of fresh media was added to the sink media after each sampling.

Animal Model

All studies were conducted on the UBC Animal Care Committee approved protocol A16‐0178 in accordance with the Canadian Council on Animal Care (CCAC). DBA/1J female mice (6‐7 weeks) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories and acclimatized for 7 days prior to the start of the experiments. Mice were housed 4 per cage, maintained in a temperature‐controlled (24 ± 1 °C) room, 30 to 50% humidity, on a 12/12 h light/dark cycle, and provided with breeder diet, food and water during the whole study. Prior to immunization, mice were housed in cages with soft bedding and a soft diet on the cage floor. Mice were randomly selected to be either in the healthy control group or immunized with collagen type II (CIA). As previously described,[ 70 ] CIA was induced in mice by intradermal injection of 100 µL collagen II (CII)/complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA) emulsion containing 100 µg of CII at the tail base, followed 21 days later by a booster immunization with 100 µL collagen II (CII)/incomplete Freund's adjuvant (IFA) emulsion. On day 24, each mouse was injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with lipopolysaccharides (LPS; 2 mg kg−1, diluted with 100 µL saline).

In Vivo SPECT/CT Imaging and Systemic Kinetics

SPECT/CT imaging experiments were carried out to visualize the biodistribution of the 111In‐labeled HPG (n = 3) and HPG‐FA (n = 3) in CIA mice following a single intravenous tail vein injection. The control group (healthy mice; n = 3) was intravenously administered with 111In‐labeled HPG‐FA. A separate study was conducted to compare the biodistribution of the 111In‐labeled HPG‐MTX (n = 4) and HPG‐FA‐MTX (n = 4) in CIA mice. Directly after the injection, SPECT/CT imaging was performed on a VECTor system (MILabs) equipped with an animal bed mounted on a motor‐controlled stage for accurate positioning in three dimensions. SPECT imaging was performed for each individual mouse at different time points (HPG and HPG‐FA: 0, 6, 24, 72 h, 7, and 10 d; HPG‐MTX and HPG‐FA‐MTX: 1, 6, 24, 48, 72 h and 7, 10, and 14 d) using an energy window width of 20% centered around the 171 keV photopeak for 111In. Following each SPECT scan, a whole‐body CT scan was acquired at 50 kV and 615 µA for anatomical information. Anesthesia during imaging was induced with 5% isoflurane at 2 L min−1 oxygen flow and maintained with spontaneous breathing with 1–3% isoflurane during scanning. Compartmental analysis was carried out using a two‐compartment model and elimination from the central compartment using Origin 9.5 software (OriginLab Corporation). SPECT‐derived radioactivity concentration–time data of the heart volume of interest (VOI) was used as a substitute for plasma concentration–time data[ 81 , 82 ] to measure clearance of radioactivity from the central compartment. Pharmacokinetic parameters were subsequently derived using Origin 9.5 software.

SPECT/CT Image Reconstruction and Processing

Scans were reconstructed with U‐SPECT Rec2.5li software (MILabs) using a pixel‐ordered subset expectation maximization (POSEM) algorithm[ 83 ] using 16 subsets, 10 iterations, and an isotropic 0.4 mm voxel grid. The CT projection data was reconstructed using SkyScan NRecon software (Microphotonics) to generate a 3D image with a 0.169 mm3 voxel size. Scatter correction was performed with the Triple Energy Window (TEW) method during the iterative reconstruction.[ 84 ] Two adjacent narrow sub‐windows (10% width) were set on each side of the 20% wide photopeak window. Reconstructed images were post‐filtered with 1 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel to minimize the effect of noise. Images were post‐processed and analyzed using AMIDE, a Medical Image Data Examiner software (UCLA, CA, USA).[ 85 ] Spherical VOI were manually drawn on the sequential images for the liver, spleen, and heart. Due to the irregular shape of the knees and paws, a 3D‐isocontour threshold method was used to draw VOI and delineate areas of high activity (inflamed) from the surrounding low activity (healthy) regions. Using quantitative VOI analysis, the total radioactivity (MBq) was determined for each VOI and decay corrected to generate time activity curve expressed as the mean standardized uptake value (SUV) or mean percent injected dose (%ID). Once activity uptake reached its maximum value (Amax), the percentage of activity retention into the joints was calculated by dividing the remaining activity in the arthritic joints by the Amax value and multiplying by 100.

Ex Vivo Assessment

A quantitative ex vivo study was performed to assess the pharmacokinetic of the two different formulations in CIA mice. Two different radioactive tracers were used to track the distribution of MTX and its polymeric nanocarrier in an individual animal. 3H‐labeled MTX and 111In‐labeled HPG were used to prepare 111In‐HPG‐[3H]MTX and 111In‐HPG‐FA‐[3H]MTX. Forty‐two CIA mice were randomly assigned to 2 groups (n = 21) and administered with a single intravenous tail vein injection of 111In‐HPG‐[3H]MTX or 111In‐HPG‐FA‐[3H]MTX (1.85 MBq 111In‐labeled conjugates and 4.8 kBq of [3H]MTX in 100 µL PBS). At 1, 6, 24, 48, and 72 h, 1 and 2 weeks, groups of 3 mice were humanely sacrificed; whole blood (collected from cardiac puncture) and tissues of interest were collected for further analysis. Post blood/tissue collection, tissues were weighed, and measured for tracer concentrations. Samples were assessed for 111In content using the 2470 Wizard 2 Automatic Gamma Counter (Perkin Elmer).

For 3H quantification, blood and tissue samples were weighed, and placed in a 20 mL glass scintillation vial. Afterward, 1 mL SOLVABLE was added to tissue samples and incubated at 60 °C until completely dissolved. Blood and fully digested tissues were decolorized by adding 0.2 mL 30% hydrogen peroxide (60 °C, 30 min). To reduce foaming in blood samples upon addition of hydrogen peroxide, 0.1 mL of 0.1 m EDTA‐di‐sodium salt solution was added. After cooling to room temperature, 10 mL of Ultima Gold was added to each vial, temperature and light adapted for 1 h prior to counting in a liquid scintillation counter. The counting efficiency was applied, disintegrations per minute (DPM) were obtained and the final results expressed as the percent injected dose per organ (%ID/organ) or gram of tissue mass (%ID/g). For one‐step digestion of bone and joint samples, a solution of perchloric acid (0.3 mL) and hydrogen peroxide (0.3 mL) was used. After solubilisation, 10 mL Hionic‐Fluor was added, and 3H activity was measured by liquid scintillation counter (Tri‐carb 3110 TR, PerkinElmer). The counting efficiency was applied, disintegrations per minute (DPM) were obtained and the results expressed as the percent injected dose per joint (%ID/joint) or gram of tissue mass (%ID/g).

Therapeutic Efficacy

CIA mice (n = 32) with fully developed RA were randomly divided into 8 groups (n = 4) and received treatment at different dose frequencies (2 times weekly, once weekly and/or once every other week) up to 4 weeks. Mice were individually weighed and received intravenously either free MTX (3 mg kg−1), HPG‐MTX or HPG‐FA‐MTX (3 mg kg−1 formulated MTX), or saline (positive control; Ctl +). Healthy mice (n = 4) used as a negative control (Ctl ‐) received weekly saline injections. Disease progression was examined before treatment (baseline) and every 3 (or 4) days during treatment independently with caliper measurements, clinical arthritis scoring, and IR imaging. After 4 weeks of treatment, animals were euthanized by CO2 inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation. Hind paws of mice were harvested, fixed, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4‐µm thick sagittal sections. For histology assessment of ankles, slides stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) were scored by two certified pathologists at HistoWiz Inc. (histowiz.com) on a 4‐point scale (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe), with respect to degree of inflammation, synovial hyperplasia, cartilage damage, and bone erosion.

Toxicity Study

The CIA mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 4) to receive 4 injections of HPG‐MTX, HPG‐FA‐MTX, or MTX, all at a dose of 3 mg kg−1 MTX, or vehicle over a period of two weeks. A group of healthy mice (Ctl ‐) received vehicle twice weekly for 2 weeks. At day 0, day 7, and one day after final injection, blood samples were withdrawn from mice through the saphenous vein or by cardiac puncture. After coagulation of blood for 1 h at room temperature, serum was separated following centrifugation at 2000 g for 15 min at 4 °C and stored at −20 °C until biochemical analyses. Serum sample analyses were performed by a commercial diagnostic laboratory (IDEXX Laboratories Inc) to measure the serum levels of ALT, AST, and ALP, and serum creatinine to estimate liver and renal toxicity.

Statistics

All measurements were performed in triplicate, with results being reported as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using advanced statistical tests in OriginLab 9.6 Pro software. Results were expressed as mean ± SD. The significance between the two experimental groups was determined by Student's t test or, for comparison of 3 or more groups, by one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc analysis. Significant differences between positive control and experimental groups were determined using the Dunnett test; a P value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
