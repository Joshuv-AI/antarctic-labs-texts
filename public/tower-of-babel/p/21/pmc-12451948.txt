Outcomes of single kidney transplantation from small pediatric donors aged <5 years: comparative analysis between pediatric and adult recipients
Authors: Runtao Feng, Bingzhou Zhong, Haoxin Liang, Jianan Chen, Jianmin Hu, Song Zhou, Guorong Liao, Jun Liao, Weiwei Jiang, Siqiang Yang, Yongguang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of kidneys from small pediatric donors (< 5 years) for single kidney transplantation (SKT) may expand the donor pool. In recent years, many national allocation policies have given priority to pediatric recipients. However, comparative studies involving pediatric and adult recipients of single kidney transplants from small pediatric donors are limited. All patients who underwent SKT from small pediatric donors aged < 5 years at our center between January 1, 2015 and June 30, 2024 were included in this study. The outcomes included graft survival, patient survival, and post-transplant complications, which were compared between pediatric and adult recipients. The patient cohort included 83 adult recipients and 55 pediatric recipients from 48 and 40 small pediatric donors (aged < 5 years), respectively. Three-year death-censored graft survival and patient survival in pediatric recipients were 81.7% and 100%, respectively, which were not significantly different from those in adult recipients (89.0% and 95.8%). The leading cause of graft loss was vascular thrombosis (6 cases), followed by rejection (5 cases). No significant differences were observed between pediatric and adult recipients regarding primary nonfunction, the incidence of proteinuria, vascular and urinary complications, or the cumulative incidence of acute rejection. However, adult recipients experienced a significantly higher incidence of delayed graft function than pediatric recipients. SKT from small pediatric donors achieved comparable outcomes in adult and pediatric recipients, validating that current policies prioritizing pediatric recipients are clinically appropriate. Addressing risks of vascular thrombosis and rejection may further improve outcomes.

Introduction

Kidney transplantation is the preferred therapy for end-stage kidney disease, providing a superior prognosis and quality of life compared to dialysis [1–3]. However, the shortage of deceased donor kidneys remains an ongoing concern despite expanding use of kidneys from pediatric donors, expanded criteria donors, and living donors [4–7].

According to data from the Australian and New Zealand Organ Donation Registry, pediatric donors account for over 10%, and the general prognosis is satisfactory, despite concerns about the increased risk of early complications [8–12]. Graft function is known to adapt to the recipient’s growth over time; therefore, long-term glomerular filtration rates may potentially be higher in pediatric donor kidneys than in adult donor kidneys [13,14].

However, some centers hesitate to use kidneys from small pediatric donors (aged <5 years) due to concerns about vascular thrombosis, technical complications, insufficient nephron mass and high filtration injury [9,15,16]. Utilization of pediatric donor kidneys is usually categorized into single kidney transplantation (SKT) from older pediatric donors and en bloc kidney transplantation (EBKT) from younger pediatric donors [17]. En bloc involves extracting both kidneys along with the main blood vessels, followed by connecting the donor’s aorta and inferior vena cava to the recipient’s vessels [18]. EBKT is thought to maximize graft outcomes by doubling nephron mass, and studies have shown that its long-term survival is comparable to standard deceased donor or even living donor kidneys [19–21]. In contrast, SKT can maximize the use of scarce donor organs. In addition, the SKT surgical technique for pediatric kidneys is similar to that for adult donors, yielding a shorter learning curve. Currently, the optimal body weight or donor age for splitting en bloc pediatric kidneys remains undefined. According to Eurotransplant guidelines, kidneys from donors aged < 2 years were mandatorily, and those from donors aged 2–5 years are advised to be transplanted as EBKT [12]. However, to maximize the number of recipients, many kidneys from small pediatric donors aged <5 years have undergone SKT procedure [12,22,23].

To date, there are no established standards for allocating kidneys from pediatric donors. Practices vary globally, and many transplant systems give priority to pediatric recipients under national policies [24–26]. In China, more families are willing to donate organs of their deceased children if pediatric recipients receive priority [27]. At our transplant center, SKT from small pediatric donor was initially performed primarily for adult recipients; however, since August 2018, policy changes have prioritized allocating pediatric donor kidneys to pediatric recipients [28]. However, comparative studies between the pediatric and adult recipients receiving SKT from small pediatric donors are limited, and it remains unclear whether pediatric recipients achieve better outcomes than adults. In this retrospective single-center observational study, we compare outcomes in pediatric and adult recipients of SKT from small pediatric donors (< 5 years) to inform optimization of allocation strategies.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a single-center retrospective study involving all patients who received SKT procedure from deceased small pediatric donors (aged <5 years) between January 1, 2015, and June 30, 2024. Patients with less than three months of follow-up were excluded.

This study received ethics approval from the Ethics Committee of Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University. Because of the retrospective and non-interventional design, written informed consent was waived. All donor organs were donated after donor death to the Red Cross Society of China and distributed by the China Organ Transplant Response System, in accordance with national laws. All study procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and Istanbul.

Transplant procedure and immunosuppression

En bloc pediatric kidneys were separated in the operating room, using a vena caval extension graft for the right renal vein, a vena caval cuff for the left renal vein, and a donor aortic patch for both renal arteries. Transplantation was achieved through external iliac vascular anastomoses outside the peritoneal cavity using 7-0 or 6-0 polypropylene running sutures. The graft ureter was then anastomosed to the recipient’s bladder using the Lich-Gregoir technique with a ureteral stent placement.

The vast majority of patients received either a lymphocyte-depleting agent or basiliximab for induction therapy, while a few received both agents or no biologics. The initial maintenance immunosuppressive regimen included tacrolimus or cyclosporine, steroids, and mycophenolic acid.

Perioperative management

Papaverine hydrochloride (15 mg/mL, 2 mL) was injected into the adipose tissue around the renal graft hilum before reperfusion and then continuously infused intravenously at 4 mL/h (0.6 mg/mL) for 2 to 3 days after transplantation. In addition, unfractionated heparin (1–2 mg/h) was administered for 3 to 5 days. Postoperative bedside ultrasound was used to monitor the diameter and variability of the inferior vena cava to assess circulatory status. The fluid management protocol aimed to maintain fluid, electrolyte, and acid–base balance and to avoid both circulatory volume underload and overload. Fluid infusion was adjusted based on the recipient’s urine output and bedside ultrasound assessment.

Data collection and variable definition

Since 2015, our center has routinely gathered post-transplant follow-up data, and additional data were collected via retrospective chart review. Baseline characteristics were collected and compared between pediatric and adult recipients, including donor body weight, sex, age, cold ischemia time (CIT), warm ischemia time (WIT), and donor type, as well as recipient body weight, sex, age, induction therapy, number of HLA mismatches, number of previous transplants, type of pretransplant dialysis, and pre-transplant panel-reactive antibody (PRA). The deceased donor types included donation after circulatory death (DCD) and donation after brain death (DBD) [29].

Delayed graft function (DGF) was defined as the need for dialysis in the first week post-transplant, excluding primary nonfunction (PNF) and graft removal within that period [30,31]. Failure to restore graft function due to non-immunological causes was defined as PNF [12]. Patient death was defined as all-cause mortality, and graft loss was defined as return to permanent dialysis or need for retransplantation. Acute rejection (AR) was defined as clinically diagnosed or biopsy-proven. Clinical diagnosis required the following conditions: recipient exhibits clinical symptoms such as elevated serum creatinine, oliguria, graft enlargement, or graft tenderness; Doppler ultrasound examination rules out renal vascular abnormalities and urinary tract obstruction; and other possible factors causing renal dysfunction, such as infection, volume depletion, and drug nephrotoxicity, are ruled out. Biopsy-proven AR included acute T-cell-mediated rejection and acute antibody-mediated rejection according to the Banff 2013 classification of renal allograft biopsy [32,33]. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Schwartz formula for pediatric recipients and the 2009 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula for adults. Proteinuria was defined as a result of at least 1+ for dipstick analysis of a midstream specimen or more than 300 mg urinary protein per 24 h.

Study outcomes

All patients were followed up until death, graft loss, or the end of the study period (December 31, 2024). The primary outcome was 3-year death-censored graft survival. Secondary outcomes included DGF, PNF, AR, vascular and urinary complications, and patient death. Additionally, eGFR was compared at 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 months post-transplant between pediatric and adult recipients.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were summarized as mean ± SD and compared using the Student’s t-test. Continuous variables that were not normally distributed were reported as median (range) and compared using the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages) and compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. In addition, patient and graft survival, along with the cumulative incidence of complications, were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves and compared via the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards model was fitted to determine hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for factors associated with 3-year graft loss. Two-tailed P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Summary statistics of postoperative complications are shown in Table 3. During follow-up, AR occurred in 14 adult recipients and 7 pediatric recipients, comprising 4 patients with antibody-mediated rejection and 17 with T-cell-mediated rejection. No significant difference was observed in the cumulative incidence of AR at 1 year post-transplant between the two groups (10.3% for adult recipients and 12.5% for pediatric recipients, p = 0.082). By 3 years, the cumulative incidence of AR was 16.1% in adult recipients and 16.3% in pediatric recipients (p = 0.820, Figure 3(A)).

All 6 cases (6/138, 4.3%) of vascular thrombosis (2 venous and 4 arterial) resulted in graft removal; these occurred in 4 adult and 2 pediatric recipients (p = 1.000). Of these 6 patients, 5 cases occurred within one week of transplantation, and 1 on day 14 post-transplant. Fifteen patients presented with renal artery stenosis and were subsequently treated with balloon dilatation or stent implantation. In terms of urinary tract complications, 2 adult recipients developed urinary leakage at 1 and 3 weeks post-transplant, respectively, and both were managed surgically via ureterocystostomy.

Graft and patient survival

The death-censored graft survival rates at 1 and 3 years post-transplant were 87.7% and 85.8% in all 138 recipients, respectively. During follow-up, 11 pediatric recipients (11/55, 20%) lost grafts due to vascular thrombosis (n = 4), PNF (n = 3), recurrence of primary nephropathy (n = 2), thrombotic microangiopathy (n = 1), and rejection (n = 1). Similarly, 11 adult recipients (11/83, 13.3%) lost grafts due to rejection (n = 4), vascular thrombosis (n = 2), recurrence of primary nephropathy (n = 2), graft infection (n = 2), and PNF (n = 1). No significant difference was observed in the 1-year death-censored graft survival rate between the two groups (81.7% for pediatric recipients and 91.6% for adult recipients, p = 0.082). By 3 years, the death-censored graft survival rates were 81.7% and 89.0% for pediatric and adult recipients, respectively (p = 0.160, Figure 4(A)). Overall, the primary cause of graft loss was vascular thrombosis (6 cases, 27.3%), followed by rejection (5 cases, 22.7%).

Kaplan-Meier Curves comparing death-censored graft survival (A) and patient survival (B) between pediatric and adult recipients.

Overall, patient survival rates at 1 and 3 years post-transplant were 99.2% and 97%, respectively. During follow-up, 5 adult recipients died due to severe pneumonia (n = 2), malignancy (n = 2) and ruptured aneurysm (n = 1). Three years and four months after transplantation, one pediatric recipient died from hypovolemic shock due to severe diarrhea. The 3-year patient survival rate was 100% for pediatric recipients and 95.8% for adult recipients, and this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.253, Figure 4(B)).

Figure 5 illustrates the incidence of proteinuria following transplantation in pediatric and adult recipients. Within the first month post-transplant, proteinuria was observed in 64.6% (31/48) of pediatric recipients and 58.2% (46/79) of adult recipients. Subsequently, the incidence of proteinuria declined progressively over time in both groups. Collectively, these findings indicate that proteinuria, mediated by hyperfiltration injury in small pediatric donor kidney transplantation, is reversible.

Predictive parameters for graft survival

To further analyze the association between recipient group and 3-year death-censored graft survival, univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed. Compared with adult recipients, the HR for graft loss in pediatric recipients was 1.91 (95% CI, 0.77–4.71, p = 0.162). After adjusting for established covariates affecting graft survival (DCD donors and donor weight < 10 kg) in the regression model, the adjusted HR for pediatric recipients was 2.60 (95% CI, 1.00–6.75; p = 0.050). DCD donors independently predicted inferior graft survival (adjusted HR, 3.96; 95% CI, 1.49–10.54; p = 0.006) in this model.

Discussion

Renal transplantation is the optimal treatment for kidney failure because it offers improved patient survival and quality of life over dialysis. It also improves growth potential and neurocognitive development in pediatric patients [34,35]. However, the widening disparity between demand and organ supply has restricted access to transplantation, particularly for pediatric patients [36,37]. Efforts have been made for many years to broaden the deceased donor pool through the use of single kidneys from small pediatric donors (aged < 5 years). However, standardized criteria for the optimal allocation and utilization of small pediatric donor kidneys remain undefined, and many national allocation policies have recently prioritized pediatric recipients. Whether pediatric recipients are more suitable candidates for SKT from small pediatric donors than adult recipients remains unclear.

Therefore, we evaluated the outcomes of pediatric and adult recipients undergoing SKT from small pediatric donors (aged <5 years). In this retrospective single-center observational study, we found that pediatric recipients did not have significantly different outcomes than adult recipients in terms of 3-year death-censored graft survival and postoperative complications.

In this study, the death-censored graft survival rates of all 138 recipients at 1 and 3 years after transplantation were 87.7% and 85.8%, respectively, which were comparable to or better than previously reported results [9,38–40]. Moreover, the overall patient survival rates at 1 and 3 years post-transplant were also satisfactory (99.2% and 97%, respectively). Concerns exist that the nephron mass provided by single small pediatric donor kidneys may not be adequate for adult recipients. However, graft function significantly improved in both pediatric and adult recipients within the first year post-transplant and remained stable thereafter. The eGFR at 3 years post-transplant was 80.49 ± 23.5 and 80.38 ± 22.7 mL/min/1.73m2 in pediatric and adult recipients, respectively (p = 0.985). These clinical data further support the effectiveness of SKT from small pediatric donors aged < 5 years.

The elevated risk of vascular thrombosis is a significant concern contributing to some transplant centers’ hesitancy to utilize kidneys from small pediatric donors for SKT [9]. In our cohort, the overall incidence of vascular thrombosis was 4.3% (6/138, 2 cases of venous thrombosis and 4 of arterial thrombosis), which is lower than previously reported [41,42]. One reason for this lower incidence is meticulous handling during surgery, which minimizes intraoperative and perioperative vascular irritation, along with the routine use of papaverine to prevent vasospasm and maintaining higher postoperative blood pressure. Nevertheless, this rate remains higher than that seen with adult donor kidneys [43,44]. All thrombotic events occurred within the first two weeks after transplantation, consistent with other reports [12]. These results suggest that perioperative factors, such as fluid balance and surgical technique, play an important role in thrombosis development; therefore, clinical practice should focus on postoperative management and close follow-up during this high-risk period. Notably, pediatric recipients in our study did not have an increased susceptibility to postoperative thrombosis compared to adult recipients.

Another common early complication was DGF, found in 60 recipients (46.9%) in our cohort, a similarly high incidence to that reported in another study (5/11, 45.5%) [45]. Interestingly, the incidence of DGF was significantly lower in pediatric recipients than in adult recipients (24.5% versus 60.8%, p < 0.001). One possible reason for this disparity is the higher proportion of DCD donors among adult recipients, as DCD status is an important independent risk factor for DGF in both pediatric and adult recipients [46,47]. In addition, inadequate functional kidney mass during the early post-transplant period may be another important factor, as evidenced by the relatively low incidence of DGF in pediatric single-kidney recipients from adult donors [44,46,48].

Rejection is increasingly recognized as a critical factor influencing poor post-transplant outcomes [23,49]. In general, pediatric recipients are more susceptible to rejection than adults, which may be related to autoimmune characteristics, poor medication adherence, and smaller tolerated doses of antimetabolites due to faster metabolism of tacrolimus. According to the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network/Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (OPTN/SRTR) 2017 Annual Data Report, the cumulative incidence of AR at one year after transplantation was 10.7% in pediatric recipients compared to 6.8% in adults [50]. However, there were no significant differences in the cumulative incidence of AR at 1-year and 3-year post-transplant between pediatric and adult recipients in our study. Among the 21 patients with AR, 5 experienced graft loss during follow-up. Greater emphasis should be placed on early diagnosis and prompt treatment of rejection to further optimize prognosis.

The primary limitations of this study include its single-center retrospective design, small sample size, and lack of long-term follow-up data. Differences among transplant centers in donor acceptance criteria, surgical technique, perioperative management, and long-term immunosuppression regimens may affect transplant outcomes. Nonetheless, we believe that our results encourage the design of prospective, large sample clinical studies to further evaluate our conclusion. Larger prospective clinical studies will also be essential to more comprehensively evaluate risk factors affecting outcomes of kidney from small pediatric donors aged < 5 years. Additionally, future studies will compare pediatric recipients of kidneys from deceased donors aged <5 years with those receiving grafts from donors aged >5 years or standard criteria adult donors. Such comparative analyses will comprehensively evaluate the clinical benefits of prioritizing allocation of kidneys from small pediatric donors to pediatric recipients.

In conclusion, this study suggests that SKT from small pediatric donors (aged < 5 years) is justified and that pediatric recipients can achieve intermediate-term outcomes comparable to those of adult recipients. Therefore, it is not contrary to clinical practice to prioritize the allocation of kidneys from small pediatric donors to pediatric recipients in accordance with national policy.
