A self-sufficient nanoreactor orchestrating calcium overload and sonodynamic/chemodynamic-amplified ferroptosis for oral squamous cell carcinoma therapy
Authors: Lei Wu, Chuiyin Wang, Qian Chen, Aiqi Wang, Meiying Peng, Gengming Zhang, Mingxin Cao, Zhaoyang Guo, Xiaoying Yang, Yinsong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacteria-derived components hold great promise for cancer therapy owing to their versatile biological properties. We previously reported that Porphyromonas gingivalis cell walls (PgCW), rich in intrinsic µ-oxo bisheme ([Fe(III)PPIX]2O), can effectively mediate both sonodynamic therapy (SDT) and chemodynamic therapy (CDT). More recently, we identified PgCW’s inherent potential to trigger ferroptosis. However, its therapeutic efficacy is limited by the hypoxic tumor microenvironment (TME) and insufficient endogenous hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) required for catalytic reactions. Herein, a self-sufficient nanoreactor (DCP) was developed by depositing calcium peroxide (CaO2) within dendritic large-pore mesoporous silica nanoparticles (DLMSNs), followed by the electrostatic adsorption of PgCW. Under ultrasound irradiation, DCP combines SDT and CDT with continuous glutathione (GSH) depletion to trigger ferroptosis. In the acidic TME, the decomposition of CaO2 generates O2 to enhance PgCW-mediated SDT, supplies H2O2 for Fenton-like CDT, and releases Ca2+ to induce calcium overload. The abundant reactive oxygen species (ROS) from CaO2-supplemented SDT and CDT, together with GSH exhaustion, amplify ferroptosis, driving severe lipid peroxidation (LPO) and cell membrane rupture to release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) for primary tumor regression. Furthermore, intracellular calcium overload synergistically promotes this ferroptosis-mediated immunogenic cell death (ICD), which, combined with the innate adjuvanticity of PgCW, drives robust systemic immune activation to suppress distant tumors. In summary, this nanoreactor overcomes TME limitations through self-sufficient substrate supply and efficiently combines calcium overload with amplified ferroptosis to suppress the growth and metastasis of oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). Introduction

Bacterial cancer therapy, leveraging natural or engineered live bacteria and their structural derivatives, has emerged as a promising strategy [1–3]. Particularly, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) on bacterial cell walls endow these systems with immune adjuvant properties [4, 5]. Beyond such universal immunological benefits, our previous investigations identified Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg) cell walls (PgCW) as an exceptional biomaterial [6, 7]. Due to its intrinsic µ-oxo bisheme ([Fe(III)PPIX]2O) structures, PgCW can act as a sonosensitizer. When activated by ultrasound-induced acoustic cavitation, it undergoes energy transfer with ambient molecular oxygen, generating abundant reactive oxygen species (ROS) and thereby mediating sonodynamic therapy (SDT) [8, 9]. Meanwhile, Fe(III) within the PgCW framework depletes intracellular glutathione (GSH) and is reduced to Fe(II), which subsequently drives the Fenton-like decomposition of endogenous H2O2 to generate highly cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH), enabling chemodynamic therapy (CDT) [10, 11]. These processes are accompanied by the typical characteristics of ferroptosis, including ROS generation to trigger membrane lipid peroxidation, and depletion of reduced GSH leading to glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) inactivation [12–14]. The persistent accumulation of lipid peroxides causes plasma membrane rupture, thus releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) to activate antitumor immune response [15–17]. However, the tumor microenvironment (TME) limits the therapeutic efficacy of PgCW. Specifically, tumor hypoxia restricts O2-dependent SDT, and endogenous H2O2 (10–100 µmol/L) is insufficient to sustain long-term CDT, so exogenous substrate replenishment is needed [18, 19].

Calcium peroxide (CaO2) is attractive for its biocompatibility and continuous oxygen-evolving capability in aqueous environments [20, 21]. This characteristic renders it a potent O2 generator, extensively employed to alleviate hypoxia and enhance SDT [22, 23]. Moreover, CaO2 undergoes pH-responsive decomposition within the TME to provide a steady supply of H2O2 and Ca2+ [24, 25]. Compared to conventional monofunctional O2 or H2O2 generators, CaO2 is capable of concurrently potentiating both SDT and CDT [26]. Furthermore, localized Ca2+ accumulation induces calcium overload, which triggers oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to cell death [27, 28]. However, CaO2 is unstable under physiological conditions and decomposes prematurely before reaching the tumor site. To address this challenge, CaO2 is often encapsulated in nanomaterials such as mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) or lipid-based nanocarriers [21, 29]. These nanomaterials provide physical protection to CaO2, preventing its early leakage. Upon exposure to the acidic TME, the encapsulated CaO2 decomposes, releasing O2 and H2O2. The release of CaO2 can be precisely controlled, thus enhancing its targeted delivery to tumor sites and minimizing off-target effects [30].

Dendritic large-pore mesoporous silica nanoparticles (DLMSNs) are biocompatible carriers characterized by a high specific surface area and large pore volumes, making them suitable for physical loading of therapeutic agents [31, 32]. In this study, DLMSNs serve as a carrier for both CaO2 and PgCW, developing a self-sufficient nanoreactor (DCP) with TME-responsive behavior and enhanced stability, thereby enabling their controlled release within the TME. As illustrated in Scheme 1, DCP is prepared by the in situ deposition of CaO2 nanoparticles within DLMSNs via the reaction of CaCl2 with H2O2, followed by the electrostatic adsorption of naturally derived PgCW. After intratumoral administration in an oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) model, DCP is activated by the dual-stimuli of the acidic TME and ultrasound (US) irradiation. Specifically, PgCW integrates a dual SDT/CDT process and continuously depletes intracellular GSH, thereby establishing the biochemical foundation for ferroptosis. This initial activity is significantly potentiated by the acid-responsive CaO2, which functions as a triple depot to ensure a sustained supply of O2, H2O2, and Ca2+. This influx of these substrates not only alleviates hypoxia to augment PgCW-mediated SDT but also sustains Fenton-like reactions, while concurrently inducing calcium overload. The resulting multimodal oxidative stress and ionic interference synergistically amplify the ferroptotic response, culminating in profound primary tumor regression. Furthermore, this therapeutic cascade drives immunogenic cell death (ICD), which, coupled with the innate adjuvanticity of the PgCW, evokes durable immunological memory and facilitates the systemic eradication of distant metastatic lesions.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF), and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Triethanolamine (TEA) was obtained from Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent (Tianjin, China). Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), (3-glycidyloxypropyl)trimethoxysilane (GPTMS), [3-(trimethoxysilyl)propyl]trimethylammonium chloride (TMAPC), and glutathione (GSH) were purchased from Tianjin Xienns Biochemical Technology (Tianjin, China). Deferasirox (DFS), 2’,7’-Dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), calcein-AM, propidium iodide (PI), 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), and citric acid were obtained from Meilunbio (Dalian, China). Tris(4,7-diphenyl-1,10-phenanthroline) ruthenium(II) dichloride ([Ru(dpp)3]Cl2) and calcium chloride (CaCl2) were purchased from Aladdin Chemistry (Shanghai, China). 3,3’,5,5’-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) was purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology (Beijing, China). IL-4 and GM-CSF were obtained from ABclonal Technology (Wuhan, China).

Cell lines and animals

Bacterial strain Pg (code BNCC337441) was sourced from BeNa Culture Collection (Beijing, China) and cultured in sheep blood agar plates supplemented with 0.5% chlorhematin and 0.5% vitamin K1 at 37 °C in an anaerobic chamber (80% N2, 10% H2, and 10% CO2). The SCC-7 murine squamous cell carcinoma cell line and RAW 264.7 murine macrophage line were purchased from Haodi Huatuo Biotechnology (Shenzhen, China) and BioVector NTCC (Beijing, China), respectively. SCC-7 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium, while RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cell lines were incubated in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Female C57BL/6 mice and Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (4–6 weeks old) were provided by SPF Biotechnology (Beijing, China). All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of YiShengyuan Gene Technology Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China; Protocol No. YSY-DWLL-2024409) and performed in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.

Preparation of PgCW and DCP

DLMSNs and citric acid-modified DLMSNs (DLMSNs-CA) were synthesized according to our previously reported methods, with minor modifications [33, 34]. Briefly, TEA (400 mg) and CTAB (304 mg) were dissolved in deionized water (20 mL) at 80 °C for 1 h, followed by the sequential addition of DFS (91.2 mg, 3 h) and TEOS (3.2 mL, 20 min). The mixture was quenched with ethanol, centrifuged, and the template was removed via reflux extraction in methanolic HCl (10:1, v/v) for 6 h. For carboxylation, DLMSNs (100 mg) were reacted with GPTMS (100 µL) in anhydrous acetonitrile (50 mL) containing TEA (125 µL) at 80 °C for 24 h. The resulting epoxy-intermediates were subsequently treated with citric acid (100 mg) and acetic acid (100 µL) at 80 °C for 12 h to yield DLMSNs-CA.

According to a modified protocol reported previously [35], DLMSNs-CA (20 mg) along with CaCl2 (10 mg) were equilibrated in ethanol (15 mL) at 40 °C for 20 min, followed by the addition of H2O2 (30%, w/w, 30 µL) and ammonium hydroxide (28%, w/w, 1 mL), and the mixture was reacted under stirring for 5 min. The intermediate products were harvested via centrifugation and redispersed in ethanol (20 mL). To facilitate the formation of a protective silica shell, ammonium hydroxide (400 µL) was added, followed by the dropwise addition of TEOS (400 µL) under constant stirring for 3 h. The resulting products, designated as DC, were subsequently quaternized using TMAPC (100 µL) in ethanol at 40 °C for 12 h to obtain cationic DC+. To optimize the loading efficiency, DC were prepared with different weight ratios of DLMSNs-CA to CaCl2 (ranging from 1:0.1 to 1:1).

PgCW was extracted from Pg cells following our previously reported protocol [6, 7]. Briefly, Pg colonies were harvested and incubated with lysozyme (2 mg/mL) in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) containing 1 mol/L sucrose for 3 h. After centrifugation, the PgCW-rich supernatant was fractionated using a 50 kDa MWCO centrifugal ultrafilter (Merck KGaA, Germany). The concentration of hemin within PgCW was determined using UV–Vis spectrophotometry (U-3310, Hitachi, Japan) based on a standard calibration curve. For DCP assembly, as-prepared DC+ (0.5 mg/mL) was stirred with the PgCW extract at various concentrations (60, 80, 100, and 120 µg/mL, hemin equivalent) for 10 min. The resulting DCP was recovered by centrifugation, washed with deionized water, and resuspended for subsequent experiments.

Characterization of PgCW and DCP

The morphology of PgCW, DLMSNs, DC, and DCP was characterized via transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi, Japan), with PgCW negatively stained with 3% phosphotungstic acid. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) imaging, along with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping, were performed on a JEM-2100F microscope (JEOL, Japan). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted using an ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms were recorded using an Autosorb-iQ analyzer (Quantachrome, USA) to determine the BET surface area and BJH pore size distribution. The hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and Zeta potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern, UK), and the colloidal stability of DCP was monitored in deionized water and 10% FBS over 14 days at 4 °C and 25 °C. For freeze-thaw stability, the DCP solution underwent three consecutive cycles of freezing at −20 °C for 12 h and thawing at room temperature, followed by DLS measurements. Finally, the UV–Vis absorption spectra of DLMSNs, DC, DCP, free hemin, and PgCW were recorded to verify component loading.

LPS and protein analysis of Pg, PgCW and DCP

The LPS levels in Pg cells, PgCW, and DCP were quantified using an LAL Endotoxin Assay Kit (GenScript, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For protein characterization, total proteins were extracted and quantified via a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Meilunbio, China). These samples containing 30 µg of protein were then resolved by 12% SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. Protein bands were finally photographed for comparison.

Evaluation of O2, H2O2, and Ca2+ released from the DCP

O2 generation triggered by DC and DCP was evaluated by dispersing each sample solution (1 mg/mL) in deoxygenated Tris-HCl buffer (pH 6.5), and the dissolved oxygen concentration was monitored in real time every 30 s using a dissolved oxygen meter (JPBJ-608, Rex, China). H2O2 production was quantified using the Ti(SO4)2 spectrophotometric assay. Briefly, DCP (1 mg/mL) was incubated in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 6.5) at 37 °C, and the supernatants collected at predetermined intervals were reacted with Ti(SO4)2, followed by absorbance measurement at 407 nm. For Ca2+ release profiles, DCP was incubated in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 6.5 or 7.0) at 37 °C under constant stirring (200 rpm). The supernatants were harvested at scheduled time points, and Ca2+ concentrations were analyzed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (ICAP Q, Thermo Scientific, USA).

Evaluation of SDT and CDT performance of DCP

The SDT performance was evaluated by detecting the production of singlet oxygen (1O2) using DPBF as a fluorescent probe. Briefly, DLMSNs, DC, DCP (100 µg/mL, normalized to the DLMSNs concentration) and PgCW (20 µg/mL, calculated as hemin equivalent) were mixed with 50 µL of DPBF (1 mg/mL) and further processed with or without US irradiation at an intensity of 1.0 W/cm2 for 0 to 5 min. Absorbance at 425 nm was recorded immediately after each interval to monitor DPBF degradation. The CDT performance was assessed by measuring the generation of hydroxyl radicals (·OH) through the oxidation of TMB. Briefly, each sample solution (1 mg/mL) was incubated with 40 µL of TMB (1 mg/mL) and subsequently processed with or without US irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 1.0 MHz, 5 min). The generation levels of ·OH were quantified via UV–Vis spectroscopic analysis, based on the characteristic absorption of oxidized TMB at 652 nm. For electron spin resonance (ESR) analysis, the sample solutions were mixed with TEMP (50 mmol/L) or DMPO (50 mmol/L). The mixtures were then treated with or without US irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 1.0 MHz, 5 min). All ESR spectra were acquired using an EMXplus spectrometer (Bruker, Germany).

GSH consumption capacity of DCP

The GSH consumption capability of DCP was evaluated by detecting thiol concentrations using Ellman’s reagent. Specifically, DCP at varying concentrations was incubated with GSH (0.25 mmol/L) in PBS at 37 °C for 1, 2, 3, and 4 h, respectively. Afterwards, the mixtures were centrifuged, and the supernatants were reacted with 0.05 mmol/L of Ellman’s reagent (DTNB, MedChemExpress, USA). The remaining thiol concentrations were quantified by measuring the absorbance at 412 nm according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1, GraphPad Software, USA). Student’s t-test was used for comparison between two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was employed for comparison among multiple groups. p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Extraction, characterization, and ferroptosis potential of PgCW

PgCW was extracted from Pg cells and systematically characterized according to our established protocols [6, 7]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image revealed that the PgCW fragments were well-dispersed, exhibiting the characteristic bacterial cell wall fragments and membrane structures (Fig. 1A). According to our previous report, PgCW possesses the capability to mediate both CDT and SDT owing to the presence of µ-oxo bisheme structures, while simultaneously exhibiting GSH depletion ability [6, 7]. Since these combined features satisfy the biochemical prerequisites for initiating ferroptosis, we hypothesized that PgCW could serve as a potent inducer of this cell death pathway. To validate this hypothesis, we first evaluated the intracellular ROS generation in mouse squamous cell carcinoma-7 (SCC-7) cells using DCFH-DA as a fluorescent probe. As presented in the confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images (Fig. 1B), cells treated with PgCW alone exhibited marginal green fluorescence. This observation was attributed to the scarcity of endogenous H2O2 to drive the Fenton-like reaction. Upon US irradiation, PgCW was effectively activated by sonoluminescence, triggering a marked increase in intracellular ROS. This elevation in ROS levels was further quantified and confirmed by flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 1C and D). Furthermore, intracellular GSH concentrations were quantified using the Ellman’s reagent. A significant decline was observed after treatment with PgCW (Fig. 1E), driven by the intrinsic Fe3+-induced redox depletion of GSH. Many studies have demonstrated that the synergy between CDT/SDT-mediated ROS generation and concurrent GSH exhaustion accelerates the accumulation of lipid peroxides (LPO) [36]. Consequently, we monitored intracellular LPO levels using BODIPY 581/591 C11 as a fluorescent probe. In contrast to the control group, cells treated with PgCW followed by US irradiation displayed a pronounced accumulation of LPO (Fig. 1F), indicating that the combined treatment successfully initiated the lipid peroxidation process. The resulting severe lipid damage in turn induced the marked downregulation of the GPX4 protein levels (Fig. 1G and S1). These findings collectively verify that the combination of PgCW and US irradiation can successfully trigger ferroptosis in SCC-7 cells. Finally, we employed the MTT assay to evaluate the therapeutic outcome, which revealed that this ferroptosis achieved only a limited growth inhibition of approximately 40% in tumor cells (Fig. 1H). This suboptimal therapeutic efficacy is presumably restricted by the TME, where CDT lacks sufficient endogenous H2O2 substrates and SDT is compromised by severe tumor hypoxia. Therefore, it is essential to supplement these therapeutic substrates to overcome these limitations.

Preparation and characterization of DCP

DCP, a self-supplied nanoreactor, was developed to achieve enhanced antitumor efficacy and circumvent the TME substrate limitations. Specifically, DLMSNs were first synthesized via a modified dual-template strategy and functionalized with GPTMS. Subsequently, citric acid was grafted via a ring-opening reaction to introduce carboxyl groups (-COOH), obtaining DLMSNs-CA. These negatively charged sites effectively chelated Ca2+, facilitating the in situ deposition of CaO2 nanoparticles within the dendritic pores of DLMSNs-CA through the reaction between calcium chloride and H2O2. Subsequently, a thin silica shell was grown on this intermediate via the hydrolysis of TEOS to improve the structural stability, thereby obtaining the DC. The surface of DC was modified with TMAPC to introduce positive charges (DC+). Afterwards, PgCW was coated onto the surfaces of DC+ via electrostatic interactions under mild stirring to obtain DCP. Initially, TEM characterization was employed to evaluate the morphology of DC and DCP fabricated under varied parameters to optimize the formulation. For the CaO2 loading, the feeding mass ratio of DLMSNs-CA to CaCl2 was optimized at 1:0.5, as higher ratios induced undesirable extra-pore precipitation and premature burst release (Fig. S2). For the PgCW adsorption, the feed concentration of PgCW was restricted to 100 µg/mL, whereas exceeding this threshold resulted in unadsorbed fragments and interparticle aggregation (Fig. S3). Under these optimized parameters, TEM images revealed that the DLMSNs displayed a highly uniform spherical morphology with well-defined, center-radial dendritic pores, while the finalized DCP nanoreactors maintained excellent monodispersity with a distinct membrane-like shell encapsulating the core (Fig. 2A). Zeta potential and DLS measurements further confirmed the above preparation process. The zeta potential reversed from +27.2 ± 3.7 mV (DC⁺) to −15.5 ± 3.2 mV (DCP) upon PgCW surface coating (Fig. 2B), a shift attributed to the inherently negatively charged components (e.g., LPS and proteins) on the bacterial cell walls. Concurrently, the hydrodynamic diameter exhibited a stepwise increase from 135 ± 5.1 nm (DLMSNs) to 186 ± 7.8 nm (DC), and ultimately to 280 ± 13.5 nm (DCP) (Fig. 2C). Moreover, DCP maintained a low PDI of 0.112 ± 0.015 (Fig. S4), indicating excellent monodispersity and colloidal stability. Altogether, these results demonstrate the successful stepwise construction of the DCP nanoreactor.

HAADF-STEM imaging and EDS elemental mapping revealed that Si, O, Ca, and Fe were uniformly distributed throughout DCP (Fig. 2D and E). The XPS survey spectrum revealed the characteristic peaks of Si, O, and Ca from the inorganic core, alongside the distinct signals of C, N, and Fe derived from the organic PgCW, which was consistent with the EDS mapping results (Fig. S5A). Notably, the deconvolution of the N 1s peak resolved two distinct chemical states at 399.9 eV (C–N bonds in the amide/protein backbone) and 398.2 eV (imine/tertiary amine residues) characteristic of the PgCW (Fig. S5B). These results verified the successful deposition of CaO2 and the subsequent adsorption of the iron-rich PgCW. HRTEM analysis displayed distinct lattice fringes with interplanar spacings of 0.29 nm and 0.25 nm, corresponding to the (002) and (110) crystal planes of crystalline CaO2 (Fig. S6). ICP-MS analysis demonstrated that the CaO2 loading capacity of DCP was approximately 19.5 wt%. N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms and pore size distribution curves of DLMSNs, DC, and DCP further confirmed the successful stepwise preparation (Fig. 2F and S7). DLMSNs exhibited a characteristic type-IV isotherm, featuring a high specific surface area (SBET) and a large pore volume (Vp). After CaO2 deposition and subsequent PgCW surface coating, both parameters underwent a progressive decline. Specifically, SBET decreased from 542.3 to 205.2 and 16.1 m2/g, while Vp dropped from 1.95 to 0.78 and 0.057 cm3/g, respectively. UV–Vis absorption spectra (Fig. 2G) revealed a characteristic absorption peak at approximately 400 nm for the isolated PgCW, corresponding to its intrinsic heme components, the key moieties responsible for its catalytic and sonodynamic activities. Crucially, this signature peak was efficiently preserved in the DCP spectrum, confirming the successful integration of PgCW and suggesting the retained bioactivity of the incorporated heme moieties. Coomassie brilliant blue staining further confirmed consistent protein components in Pg, PgCW, and DCP (Fig. 2H). LPS quantification demonstrated the effective retention of essential bacterial PAMPs, with no significant differences among Pg, PgCW, and DCP (Fig. 2I). To evaluate the colloidal stability of DCP, the nanoparticles were incubated in water and 10% FBS at 4 °C and 25 °C over 14 days. DLS measurements showed no significant fluctuations in size and PDI (Fig. 2J and S8A). Furthermore, three consecutive freeze-thaw cycles did not alter the size or PDI of DCP (Fig. S8B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the DCP nanoreactor possesses sufficient stability for subsequent storage and transportation.

Substrate-supplying capacity and ROS generation of DCP

To evaluate the function of the self-sufficient nanoreactors (DC and DCP), we first investigated the capacity of the encapsulated CaO2 to act as a triple-substrate generator (i.e., O2, H2O2, and Ca2+). Upon dispersion in an aqueous environment at pH 6.5, DC and DCP underwent steady decomposition, triggering a continuous release of dissolved O2 and H2O2 (Fig. 3A and B). Notably, DCP exhibited a higher O2 generation level but a lower H2O2 accumulation level than DC. This discrepancy was attributed to the intrinsic catalase-like activity of the PgCW, which catalyzed the decomposition of the self-generated intermediate H2O2 into O2. DCP was dispersed in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 6.5 and 7.4) and Ca2+ release was monitored (Fig. 3C). At pH 7.4, the cumulative Ca2+ release was only 23.7% over 24 h. In sharp contrast, under mildly acidic conditions (pH 6.5), DCP degraded rapidly, releasing nearly 90% of Ca2+ within the first 4 h. These results indicate that DCP remains stable in neutral environments but degrades responsively in the weakly acidic TME.

Driven by these continuously generated substrates, the capacity of DCP to initiate SDT and CDT was assessed. We first evaluated the production of 1O2 during SDT using the DPBF probe (Fig. 3D and E). PgCW exhibited a significant decrease in DPBF absorbance with US irradiation, indicating its potent SDT performance owing to the abundant [Fe(III)PPIX]2O. Remarkably, under US irradiation, DCP exhibited an even greater decline in DPBF absorbance compared to PgCW. This superior SDT efficacy is attributed to the self-supplied O2, which continuously replenishes the oxygen consumed during the sonodynamic process. To provide direct evidence of 1O2 generation, ESR spectroscopy with the TEMP probe was performed. Consistent with the DPBF results, the DCP with US group triggered the most characteristic 1:1:1 triplet signal of TEMP-1O2 (Fig. 3F and G). Meanwhile, the generation of highly cytotoxic ·OH via the CDT pathway was monitored using the TMB probe (Fig. 3H, I and S9). Even without US irradiation, DCP exhibited a distinct increase in the characteristic absorption peak of oxidized TMB at 652 nm, whereas PgCW displayed negligible changes. This contrast demonstrates that PgCW lacks the essential H2O2 substrate, whereas CaO2 within DCP decomposes to generate H2O2, thus driving the PgCW-catalyzed Fenton-like reaction. Furthermore, ESR measurements using DMPO as the spin trapper were conducted to verify ·OH production. The DCP group exhibited the characteristic 1:2:2:1 quartet pattern of the DMPO-·OH adduct, which was significantly augmented upon US irradiation (Fig. 3J and K). This enhancement was likely due to US-induced cavitation, as previously reported [9, 37]. These results suggest that DCP functions as a self-sufficient nanoreactor capable of both SDT and CDT. We further assessed the GSH-depleting capability of DCP using the Ellman’s reagent. As shown in the UV–Vis spectra, a rapid attenuation of the characteristic absorption peak of NTB2− at 412 nm was observed upon DCP exposure (Fig. 3L and S10). This time- and concentration-dependent GSH depletion confirms the capacity of DCP as a self-sufficient nanoreactor to impair cellular antioxidant defenses.

In vitro multimodal anticancer effects of self-sufficient nanoreactor

The multimodal antitumor effects of DCP were evaluated in SCC-7 cells. First, the cellular internalization of DCP was assessed using CLSM and flow cytometry. The results demonstrated the successful time-dependent internalization of FITC-labeled DCP (Fig. 4A and S11). Because the fluorescence intensity reached a plateau at 4 h and no significant difference was observed at 6 h, we selected the 4 h time point for US irradiation in subsequent cell experiments. Upon cell entry, the hypoxia-alleviating capacity was evaluated using the specific probe [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2. CLSM images (Fig. 4B) and fluorescence quantification (Fig. 4C) revealed a decrease in hypoxia-associated red fluorescence in the cells treated with DC and DCP, confirming continuous O2 self-supply from CaO2. Next, the intracellular ROS generation was evaluated using the fluorescence probe DCFH-DA (Fig. 4D and S12). Even without US stimulation, the DCP-treated cells exhibited distinct ROS generation, validating the spontaneous self-supplied CDT mediated by PgCW. US irradiation further amplified DCP-mediated ROS generation, demonstrating the synergy between self-supplied substrates and US-triggered SDT. Additionally, we investigated the ionic interference capability of DCP by monitoring intracellular Ca2+ concentrations using the fluorescence probe Fluo-4 AM. As shown in Fig. S13, DC and DCP both induced a robust increase in Fluo-4 AM green fluorescence, confirming the efficient acid-triggered Ca2+ release from the CaO2.

According to previous reports [11], Ca2+ overload and oxidative stress induced by SDT/CDT induce cellular damage by impairing mitochondrial function. Therefore, we used the fluorescent probe JC-1 to evaluate the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm). As shown in Fig. 4E, the cells treated with DCP followed by US irradiation exhibited the lowest ΔΨm, as evidenced by the transition of JC-1 from red-fluorescent J-aggregates to green-fluorescent monomers, demonstrating severe mitochondrial dysfunction. Subsequently, the amplification of ferroptosis was evaluated to confirm the therapeutic advantage of the nanoreactor. As quantified in Fig. S14, DCP significantly reduced intracellular GSH levels compared to PgCW, which was attributed to oxidative GSH consumption by H2O2 generated from CaO2. Intracellular LPO levels were subsequently monitored using the C11-BODIPY fluorescent probe. Notably, DCP alone caused more pronounced LPO accumulation than PgCW with US irradiation, whereas the DCP with US elicited the strongest ferroptotic response, as evidenced by maximal LPO fluorescence (Fig. 4F). Correspondingly, the most significant down-regulation of GPX4 expression was observed in the cells treated with DCP followed by US irradiation (Fig. 4G and S15). These findings validate that CaO2 within DCP promotes intracellular ROS generation by providing requisite substrates and synergistically facilitates GSH depletion, ultimately amplifying the ferroptotic cascade.

To validate the antitumor efficacy of calcium overload and amplified ferroptosis, we evaluated the synergistic cytotoxicity in SCC-7 cells. MTT assay revealed that DC and DCP, when used alone, both inhibited cell growth in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 4H). Specifically, at a concentration of 200 µg/mL, DC reduced the cell viability to approximately 69.5%. However, pre-treatment with BAPTA-AM eliminated this cytotoxicity and successfully inhibited the loss of ΔΨm (Fig. S16A and B). These results demonstrate that Ca2+ overload serves as the primary driver directly inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent cellular damage. At the same concentration, DCP decreased the cell viability to 46.8%, driven by ROS-mediated ferroptosis. Notably, upon US irradiation, DCP simultaneously mediated SDT to further trigger ferroptosis, augmenting cytotoxicity as evidenced by the declined cell viability of 13.4% (Fig. 4I). Subsequently, Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), a typical ferroptosis inhibitor, was employed to validate the therapeutic contribution of ferroptosis. In the DCP combined with US treatment group, Fer-1 administration significantly restored the cell survival rate to 63.0%, confirming that ferroptosis operates as the major execution pathway driving the severe cytotoxicity (Fig. S17). Finally, Live/Dead cell staining was performed to visually assess these antitumor effects. The control cells exhibited ubiquitous green fluorescence indicative of live cells, whereas DCP with US irradiation killed almost all the cells, as evidenced by red fluorescence, directly reflecting synergistic induction of cell death (Fig. 4J and S18). Consistent results were obtained in apoptosis evaluation. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that DCP with US irradiation induced the cellular eradication, exhibiting a total apoptotic rate up to 80.4% (Fig. S19).

In vitro antitumor immunity activated by self-sufficient nanoreactor

Many investigations have shown that calcium overload and ferroptosis are potent inducers of ICD in tumor cells [38, 39]. This process triggers the release of DAMPs, which subsequently stimulate antitumor immune responses. Herein, the ICD-inducing effect of DCP with US irradiation was evaluated by assessing the surface exposure of calreticulin (CRT), the translocation of high-mobility group box-1 (HMGB1), and the extracellular secretion of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Using CLSM and flow cytometry, the characteristic surface exposure of CRT was monitored in SCC-7 cells (Fig. 5A, B and S20). Even without US irradiation, DC and DCP both significantly elevated the cell surface expression of CRT, confirming their ICD-inducing effects, likely attributable to calcium overload triggered by CaO2. In contrast, DCP exhibited a more potent ICD-inducing effect than DC, indicating that PgCW-mediated and H2O2-amplified Fenton-like CDT also contributed to this response. Upon US irradiation, DCP further markedly promoted the cell surface exposure of CRT, owing to PgCW-initiated and O2-amplified SDT. Similarly, consistent results were obtained for the extracellular secretion of ATP (Fig. 5C) and the nucleocytoplasmic translocation of HMGB1 (Fig. 5D). These findings demonstrated the greatest ICD-inducing effect of DCP with US irradiation among all treatments.

The release of DAMPs, in tandem with the intrinsic PAMPs of the PgCW, provides potent “eat-me” and “danger” signals to the immune system [40]. We therefore investigated whether this synergistic immunostimulatory environment could effectively trigger the activation and maturation of professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs). First, we evaluated the polarization of macrophages towards the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, which can exert direct tumor-killing effects and orchestrate subsequent immune cascades [41, 42]. Mouse macrophage RAW264.7 cells were incubated with the supernatants derived from SCC-7 cells with various treatments and then analyzed using flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 5E and S21A, RAW264.7 cells in the treatment group of DCP with US irradiation exhibited the highest expression level of CD80, a biomarker for M1 macrophages [43], indicating its potent ability to polarize macrophages toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were isolated from C57BL/6 mice and then incubated with the supernatants derived from SCC-7 cells with various treatments to assess the initiation of adaptive immunity. Flow cytometric results revealed that BMDCs in the treatment group of DCP with US irradiation elicited the most robust maturation, characterized by the significant upregulation of the co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 (Fig. 5F and S21B). Theoretically, these matured APCs cross-present tumor antigens, enabling the activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). Therefore, we established a co-culture system consisting of matured BMDCs, splenic T cells isolated from C57BL/6 mice, and SCC-7 cells to evaluate this antitumor immune response. Flow cytometric results revealed that the CD8+ T cell population was dramatically increased in the treatment group of DCP with US irradiation compared to the control group (Fig. 5G and S21C), demonstrating the efficient activation of cytotoxic T cells. Finally, the viability of SCC-7 cells in the co-culture system was assessed to evaluate the antitumor efficacy. Cell viabilities corresponding to the treatment groups of DCP alone and DCP with US irradiation decreased to approximately 54.2% and 28.5%, respectively (Fig. 5H), demonstrating robust antitumor immunity activated by this self-sufficient nanoreactor.

In vivo antitumor effects of self-sufficient nanoreactor

A mouse OSCC model was established through subcutaneous injection of SCC-7 cells into the C57BL/6 mice to evaluate the in vivo antitumor efficacy. These tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into 8 groups and treated according to the schedule illustrated in Fig. 6A. Specifically, the mice were intratumorally injected separately with PBS (control), DLMSNs, DC, and DCP at an equivalent DLMSNs dose of 37.5 mg/kg. US irradiation (2.0 W/cm2, 1.0 MHz, 5 min) was applied at the tumor site of mice in the relevant groups 4 h post-administration. Intratumoral ROS generation was first assessed using the fluorescent probe DHE. CLSM images of DHE-stained tumor frozen sections are presented in Fig. 6B. Even without US irradiation, DCP evidently promoted ROS generation, which is primarily attributed to PgCW-mediated and H2O2-amplified Fenton-like CDT. Upon US irradiation, DCP further enhanced the DHE fluorescence signal, confirming synergistic ROS generation from SDT and CDT induced by this self-sufficient nanoreactor. Subsequently, immunofluorescence staining was used to evaluate ferroptosis by assessing GPX4 expression. CLSM images displayed that DCP with US irradiation notably downregulated GPX4 expression, whereas the control tumors retained robust GPX4 positivity (Fig. 6B). These findings collectively demonstrate that DCP-mediated SDT/CDT combination therapy effectively triggers ferroptosis in vivo.

Driven by ROS-exacerbated oxidative stress leading to ferroptosis, this combination treatment achieved substantial antitumor efficacy. Tumor growth was continuously monitored and the results are shown in Fig. 6C and S22. Tumors in the PBS and DLMSNs groups grew rapidly, whereas DC and DCP, even without US irradiation, significantly inhibited tumor growth. This is primarily attributed to nanoreactor-mediated ionic interference and H2O2-amplified CDT. Upon US irradiation, DCP exhibited the strongest antitumor efficacy and almost completely suppressed tumor growth, indicating the contribution of SDT induced by this nanoreactor. Photograph and quantitative weights of ex vivo tumors further demonstrated the strongest antitumor efficacy of DCP with US irradiation (Fig. 6D and E). Histological and immunohistochemical analyses were next performed on the excised tumors. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that DCP with US irradiation induced obvious structural disintegration and extensive tumor necrosis. Immunohistochemical staining of Ki-67, a marker for cell proliferation [44], demonstrated that the mice treated with DCP followed by US irradiation exhibited the lowest expression of this proliferation marker (Fig. 6F). These histological alterations are consistent with the cellular collapse induced by calcium overload and ROS-mediated ferroptosis.

Throughout the treatment period, the body weights of these mice were detected as an important indicator of overall health status. No significant body weight fluctuations were observed across the control and treatment groups (Fig. S23). At the end of treatment, the major organs and blood were harvested for comprehensive histological and hematological examinations. H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidneys) (Fig. S24), along with blood routine and serum biochemistry panels (Fig. S25), revealed no noticeable inflammatory lesions, systemic toxicity, or hepatic/renal dysfunction. Furthermore, a hemolysis assay was performed to evaluate blood compatibility. As shown in Fig. S26, DCP resulted in negligible hemolysis, indicating excellent hemocompatibility. These results collectively suggest the acceptable biocompatibility of the combination treatment strategy based on the self-sufficient nanoreactor DCP. Concurrently, it should be noted that the presence of bacterial cell wall components within the PgCW poses potential risks of systemic inflammation and long-term immunogenicity. Consequently, comprehensive safety evaluations remain imperative prior to clinical translation, which necessitate extended immunotoxicity profiling, granular tracking of immune cell subsets, and longitudinal cytokine monitoring.

In vivo antitumor immunity activated by self-sufficient nanoreactor

Given the potent ICD induced by the self-sufficient nanoreactor DCP and the innate adjuvanticity of PgCW, we further evaluated the activation of in vivo antitumor immunity. SCC-7 tumor-bearing mice were sacrificed 48 h post-treatment, and their tumors and spleens were harvested to prepare single-cell suspensions for flow cytometric analysis. First, the maturation of DCs and the infiltration of T cells within tumor tissues were assessed. Compared to the control, DCP even without US irradiation significantly increased the proportions of CD80+ CD86+ DCs (Fig. 7A and B) and effector CD3+ CD8+ T cells (Fig. 7C and S27A). This distinct upregulation was attributed to a complementary dual mechanism. On one hand, the PAMPs derived from PgCW directly stimulate DCs through specific pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), triggering innate immune activation and subsequent T cell infiltration [45]. On the other hand, the DCP-mediated calcium overload and ferroptosis cause a substantial release of DAMPs, propagating adaptive antitumor immunity. Upon US irradiation, this synergistic effect is further amplified, culminating in a robust and localized immune response. Consistent results were obtained in flow cytometric analysis of splenic tissues (Fig. 7D, E, S27B and S27C). Given that the spleen is the major secondary lymphoid organ responsible for systemic lymphocyte homing and maturation, these pronounced splenic immune alterations successfully confirmed the induction of a systemic immune cascade rather than a localized response [46]. The serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-6, were subsequently detected using the ELISA kits. Notably, DCP with US irradiation boosted the serum levels of these pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. S28), further demonstrating systemic immune activation. We also examined the generation of immunological memory by evaluating the memory T cell populations in spleen tissues. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that DCP with US irradiation prominently enhanced the proportion of memory CD3+ CD44+ CD62L− T cells (Fig. 7F and G). This robust immunological memory provides essential systemic surveillance against tumor recurrence and metastasis.

Finally, a bilateral OSCC mouse model was established to simulate distant metastasis, thus to validate this systemic antitumor efficacy (Fig. 7H). Primary tumors received the treatments as described above, while the distant untreated tumors were monitored for abscopal effect. As shown in the growth curves of distant tumors (Fig. 7I), DCP with US irradiation exerted the strongest suppressive effect. This was visually confirmed by the ex vivo photographs of the distant tumors (Fig. 7J). To investigate the immune mechanism underlying this distant inhibition, CD8+ T cells infiltration in the distant tumors was evaluated. Staining results showed that the DCP with US irradiation group significantly promoted the infiltration of CD8+ T cells within the tumor tissues (Fig. S29), demonstrating the activation of a systemic immune response against distant tumors.

Conclusion

In summary, we successfully developed a self-sufficient nanoreactor, DCP, that effectively overcomes therapeutic challenges posed by the hypoxic TME. CaO2 is loaded within DLMSNs as an autonomous triple-action depot, supplying O2, H2O2, and Ca2+ for multimodal therapy in response to the acidic TME. PgCW extracted from Pg cells is next coated onto DLMSNs to obtain DCP. This coating serves as a therapeutic agent for both SDT and CDT, and also acts as a consumer of GSH. O2 and H2O2 generated from CaO2 significantly amplify PgCW-mediated SDT/CDT combination therapy, producing a massive ROS burst. Together with endogenous GSH depletion, the generated ROS induces extensive lipid peroxidation and irreversible ferroptosis. Intracellular Ca2+ accumulation triggers calcium overload, which synergizes with ferroptosis to maximize ICD induction. With its ability to induce the ICD and its inherent adjuvanticity derived from PgCW, DCP elicits a robust systemic immune cascade and establishes immunological memory to effectively suppress both primary and distant metastatic tumors. Although this systemic immune evaluation was primarily reflected by the holistic immune status of the spleen, a more granular tracking of lymphocyte fluxes in peripheral blood and the dynamic activation of draining lymph nodes remain to be captured, which will be highly prioritized in our future outlook to facilitate its clinical translation. Ultimately, this work presents a therapeutic strategy that combines localized ionic interference with ROS-driven ferroptosis, and provides an effective approach against hypoxic malignancies. Despite the promising antitumor efficacy, we acknowledge that intratumoral administration may limit the clinical applicability of this platform, especially for metastatic OSCC, and future efforts should focus on developing systemically deliverable formulations.
