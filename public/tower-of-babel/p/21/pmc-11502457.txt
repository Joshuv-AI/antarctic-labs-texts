Quantitative cervicovaginal fluid fetal fibronectin: A liquid biopsy for intra‐amniotic inflammation
Authors: Puntabut Warintaksa, Roberto Romero, Waranyu Lertrat, Nutnaree Yuenyongdechawat, Paninee Mongkolsuk, Supakorn Chaiyakarn, Rapeewan Settacomkul, Pisut Pongchaikul, Pornpun Vivithanaporn, Piya Chaemsaithong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntra‐amniotic inflammation is causally linked to spontaneous preterm labor. The gold standard for the diagnosis of intra‐amniotic inflammation is the determination of an amniotic fluid profile obtained from transabdominal amniocentesis, which is invasive. Cervicovaginal fluid fetal fibronectin (fFN) is a widely‐used predictive biomarker for spontaneous preterm labor. The aims of this study are to determine (1) whether a quantitative cervicovaginal fluid fFN test can be used to identify the presence of intra‐amniotic inflammation; and (2) an appropriate cut‐off value of a cervicovaginal fluid fFN concentration for the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation.Material and MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 78 patients with preterm labor and intact membranes who had a sample collected for quantitative cervicovaginal fluid fFN measurement and underwent transabdominal amniocentesis. Intra‐amniotic inflammation was defined as an amniotic fluid interleukin‐6 concentration ≥2.6 ng/mL. Clinicians were masked from the results of cervicovaginal fluid fFN and amniotic fluid interleukin‐6 concentrations. Logistic regression analysis was used to determine which factors were significant predictors of intra‐amniotic inflammation. The diagnostic indices of the cervicovaginal fluid fFN test for the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation were calculated.Results(1) Frequency of intra‐amniotic inflammation was 26.9% (21/78); (2) the higher the cervicovaginal fluid fFN concentration, the greater the risk of intra‐amniotic inflammation (p < 0.001); (3) cervicovaginal fluid fFN concentration ≥125 ng/mL had an area under the curve of 0.91 (95% confidence interval: 0.83–0.96) for the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation with 100% sensitivity, 100% negative predictive value, 82.46% specificity and a positive likelihood ratio of 5.7; and (4) cervicovaginal fluid fFN cut‐off of 125 ng/mL had a significant higher predictive performance than the traditional cut‐off (50 ng/mL) for the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation.ConclusionsQuantitative cervicovaginal fluid fFN with a cut‐off of 125 ng/mL had a high sensitivity and a negative predictive value as well as a positive likelihood ratio for the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation. Its high sensitivity and negative predictive value can be used to decrease an index of suspicion of intra‐amniotic inflammation. This test may be useful as an initial assessment test to select appropriate patients for amniocentesis to determine intra‐amniotic inflammation.Keywords: cervicovaginal fluid, fetal, fibronectin, interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), intra‐amniotic infection, intra‐amniotic inflammation, liquid biopsy, prediction, preterm labor with intact membranes

Key message

The cervicovaginal fluid fetal fibronectin concentration with a cut‐off of 125 ng/mL had a high sensitivity and a negative predictive value the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation. This test can be used to decrease an index of suspicion of intra‐amniotic inflammation and could serve as an initial test to select appropriate patients for amniocentesis to determine intra‐amniotic inflammation.

INTRODUCTION

Preterm birth is the leading cause of perinatal morbidity and mortality worldwide. 1 , 2 The estimated global preterm birth rate is 10.6% with 10.4% of the regional preterm birth rate concentrated in Asia. 3 Preterm infants are at an increased risk for chronic respiratory illness, 4 blindness, and intellectual disability, 5 , 6 , 7 including cerebral palsy. 5 , 8 , 9 Preterm labor is a syndrome with multiple causes that may include intra‐amniotic infection, intra‐amniotic inflammation, vascular disorders, decidual senescence, uterine overdistension, cervical disease, breakdown of maternal–fetal tolerance, or stress. 2 , 10 Among such causes, intra‐amniotic infection and/or intra‐amniotic inflammation is a proven cause of spontaneous preterm labor. 2 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

Intra‐amniotic inflammation occurs in approximately one‐third of pregnancies with preterm labor, 2 , 11 , 12 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 and its presence is associated with adverse perinatal outcomes such as early preterm birth, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 18 , 20 neonatal morbidity, 14 , 18 , 19 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 clinical and acute histologic chorioamnionitis 14 , 15 , 16 , 18 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 as well as funisitis. 29 , 30 , 31 Importantly, pregnant women with intra‐amniotic inflammation even without the presence of microorganisms in the amniotic cavity (also called “sterile intra‐amniotic inflammation”) have similar adverse outcomes as do those with detectable microorganisms in the amniotic cavity (intra‐amniotic infection) 14 , 28 , 30 , 32 in the context of preterm labor with intact membranes, 14 , 28 , 33 preterm prelabor rupture of the membranes, 16 , 30 threatened mid‐trimester miscarriage, 34 asymptomatic patients with a short cervix, 32 and clinical chorioamnionitis at term. 35 , 36 Thus, intra‐amniotic inflammation is a frequent and important disorder. Therefore, rapid, accurate identification of an inflammatory status in the amniotic cavity is essential to guide clinical management and to minimize the potential adverse outcomes for both mother and fetus.

The gold standard for the diagnosis of intra‐amniotic inflammation is the determination of the amniotic fluid profile obtained from transabdominal amniocentesis. 12 However, this method is considered invasive. Several noninvasive methods such as cervicovaginal fluid fetal fibronectin (fFN) 37 , 38 and cervicovaginal fluid cytokines [ie, interleukin (IL)‐6, IL‐8] 39 , 40 , 41 have been evaluated for the identification of intra‐amniotic infection and/or intra‐amniotic inflammation. Among these, a concentration of cervicovaginal fFN is the most widely used biomarker for the prediction of spontaneous preterm birth. 42 , 43 , 44 Fetal fibronectin is an extracellular matrix protein found in the choriodecidual interface, 45 which is not normally present during 22 to 37 weeks of gestation; therefore, the detection of such protein is an indicator of labor. 46 An elevated concentration of cervicovaginal fetal fibronectin test with the traditional cut‐off of 50 ng/mL has a high negative predictive value for the prediction of spontaneous preterm birth, 47 , 48 and it is a risk factor for intra‐amniotic inflammation and acute inflammatory lesions of the placenta. 37 , 49 A quantitative cervicovaginal fFN test has recently been developed. 38 , 43 , 50 , 51 , 52 Hence, the objectives of this study were to determine: (1) whether the quantitative cervicovaginal fFN test can be used to identify the presence of intra‐amniotic inflammation; and (2) the appropriate cut‐off value of a cervicovaginal fFN concentration for the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation in women presenting with spontaneous preterm labor and intact membranes.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and population

This prospective cohort study comprised pregnant women diagnosed with preterm labor and intact membranes, who had been admitted to the Labor and Delivery Unit at Ramathibodi Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand, between January 2022 and October 2023.

Inclusion criteria were defined as singleton pregnant women aged ≥18 years with the diagnosis of spontaneous preterm labor with intact membranes between 20 and 366/7 weeks of gestation who were admitted to the Labor and Delivery Unit. We excluded patients who (1) refused amniocentesis; (2) had underlying infectious diseases with potential fetal transmission after invasive procedures such as Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection or active hepatitis B; (3) had sexual activity within the previous 24 h; (4) had an intrauterine fetal death; (5) had oligohydramnios (amniotic fluid index <5 cm); (6) had placenta previa and active bleeding; and (7) had emergent obstetrical conditions such as unstable vital signs, eclampsia, placental abruption, fetal distress, and/or imminent delivery (cervical dilation >5 cm). The processes of the study as well as risks and benefits were discussed with the participants. Written informed consent was obtained prior to the collections of samples.

Cervicovaginal fFN collection

Cervicovaginal fluid was collected before vaginal digital examination. A sterile, dry speculum was inserted into the vagina and a Dacron polyester fiber swab was placed into the external cervical os and left in place for 10 s. 43 Bedside quantitative cervicovaginal fFN measurement was performed using a commercially available enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Hologic, Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Physicians were masked to the results of the cervicovaginal fFN test, which were not used for clinical management. In addition, researchers who determined the fetal fibronectin and IL‐6 concentrations were masked from one another.

Cervical length measurement

Pregnant women were asked to empty their bladder and lie in the lithotomy position. A 3–12 MHz transvaginal transducer with a condom cover and lubricant was gently inserted into the anterior fornix of woman's vagina. The mid‐sagittal view of the cervix was obtained and magnified to occupy 75% of the screen. The transducer was withdrawn until no pressure was exerted on the cervix. The cervical length was measured from the visualized full length of the endocervical canal from the internal os to the external cervical os. The cervical length was measured three times and the shortest length was selected. 53 Cervical length measurement was performed by a maternal–fetal‐medicine fellow or a maternal–fetal‐medicine specialist.

Amniotic fluid collection and analysis

Ultrasound‐guided transabdominal amniocentesis was performed by a maternal–fetal‐medicine fellow or a maternal–fetal‐medicine specialist prior to the administration of prophylactic antibiotics, tocolytics, or an antenatal corticosteroid. Amniotic fluid (10 cc) was collected in a sterile fashion and immediately transported in a capped, sterile syringe to the clinical laboratory where it was cultured for aerobic bacteria. Evaluation of the white blood cell (WBC) counts, glucose concentrations, and Gram stains of amniotic fluid was performed shortly after collection in the clinical laboratory. Amniotic fluid not required for clinical assessment was centrifuged for 10 min at 4°C shortly after the amniocentesis, and the supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −70°C until analysis. The presence of intra‐amniotic inflammation was evaluated by determining the amniotic fluid IL‐6 concentration with the ELISA method (Biotek Synergy Neo2, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), characterized with a sensitivity of <1.0 pg/mL. 37 Intra‐assay and inter‐assay coefficients of variation were <10%. Amniotic fluid IL‐6 concentrations were determined for research purposes and such results were not used for patient management. Intra‐amniotic inflammation was characterized by an amniotic fluid IL‐6 concentration ≥2.6 ng/mL. 14 , 32 , 54

Clinical management

A cervicovaginal fFN test and an amniocentesis were performed at the time of the diagnosis of preterm labor and prior to the administration of antibiotics and antenatal corticosteroids. Patient management was guided by an amniotic fluid white blood cell count or the presence of bacteria (Gram stain or culture) result. Specifically, if there was intra‐amniotic inflammation (defined as an amniotic fluid WBC count ≥50 cells/mm3) or intra‐amniotic infection (i.e., detectable bacteria in the amniotic cavity by Gram stain or culture with the presence of intra‐amniotic inflammation), we administered triple antibiotics (ceftriaxone, metronidazole, clarithromycin) for 14 days in women with preterm labor <32–34 weeks of gestation. These patients were delivered if clinical chorioamnionitis developed, or if a nonreassuring fetal status or advanced labor occurred. By contrast, delivery was considered in those with intra‐amniotic infection if the gestational age was ≥34 weeks, given an unknown Group B Streptococcus status. In those without intra‐amniotic infection/inflammation, clinical standard management with the administration of antibiotics (ampicillin 2 g intravenously q 6‐h) for 48 h was performed. For corticosteroids, we administered dexamethasone 6 mg intramuscular for 4 doses to induce lung maturity in cases with a gestational age <37 weeks of gestation.

Clinical definitions

Spontaneous preterm labor was defined as persistent uterine contractions accompanied by progressive cervical dilatation and effacement or the initial presentation with regular uterine contractions and cervical dilatation at least 2 cm for a gestational age between 206/7 and 366/7 weeks. 55 Intra‐amniotic inflammation was diagnosed if the amniotic fluid IL‐6 concentration was ≥2.6 ng/mL. 56 Intra‐amniotic infection was defined as the presence of intra‐amniotic inflammation with demonstrable microorganism (detected by cultivation or 16S Sanger sequencing) in the amniotic cavity. 56

Acute inflammatory lesion of the placenta includes acute chorioamnionitis and acute funisitis. 57 , 58 Acute chorioamnionitis is characterized by a histologic lesion associated with neutrophils that enter the chorio‐amnion via decidual venules in the membranes and the chorionic plate from the intervillous space. Acute funisitis is defined as a lesion associated with neutrophils that enter the chorionic plate and the Wharton's jelly by migrating through the walls of large chorionic and umbilical vessels. The staging and grading of acute inflammatory lesions of the placenta has been defined by the criteria proposed by Redline et al. 59

Statistical analyses

The sample size calculation was calculated based on the screening performance of cervicovaginal fFN for the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation from a previous study that showed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.80. 38 The incidence of intra‐amniotic inflammation in women with preterm labor and intact membranes is 20%–30%. 15 In order to achieve an AUC of 0.81 with an alpha being 5% and a power of 90%, a minimum of 66 women with preterm labor and intact membranes was required.

The normality of the data distribution was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Numerical data were presented as numbers, mean [standard deviation (SD)] or median [interquartile range (IQR)] depending on the data distribution. Categorical data were presented as numbers and percentages. The comparison of categorical data and continuous data was assessed by the Chi‐square test and by a t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U‐test, respectively. Logistic regression analysis was used to determine which of the factors among the maternal characteristics, ie, cervical parameters (cervical dilatation and cervical length), cervicovaginal fluid fibronectin concentration, and amniotic fluid profile, were significant predictors of intra‐amniotic inflammation. With forward selection, predictors with a p‐value <0.1 were included in a multivariate logistic regression analysis to determine which factors were independent predictors of intra‐amniotic inflammation. Spearman's correlation analysis was used to determine the correlations between cervical parameters, amniotic fluid WBC counts, and concentrations of glucose, cervicovaginal fFN, and amniotic fluid IL‐6. The performance of cervicovaginal fFN was estimated by receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curves and area under the ROC curve (AUC). Predictive performance indices (sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values as well as likelihood ratios) of the quantitative cervicovaginal fFN test for the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation were calculated. The DeLong test was used to compare the differences of the AUC between models, ie, different cervicovaginal fetal fibronectin concentrations. A probability value (p value) <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed with SPSS statistical software version 18 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Armonk, NY, USA) and Medcalc (Medcalc Software, Mariakerke, Belgium).

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics and pregnancy outcomes of the study population

A total of 98 women with spontaneous preterm labor and intact membranes were eligible for this study during the study period. Twenty pregnant women were excluded due to the following reasons: decline to participate (n = 14), imminent delivery (n = 5), and an invalid fetal fibronectin result (n = 1). Seventy‐eight pregnant women who had cervicovaginal fluid fibronectin and amniotic fluid samples collected were included for analysis. Clinical characteristics of excluded and recruited participants were not different (Supplementary Table S1).

Characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1. The median (IQR) maternal age was 32.5 years (28.0–38.0) and prepregnancy body mass index was 22.9 kg/m2 (19.1–25.7). The frequencies of nulliparous women and multiparous women with a previous history of spontaneous preterm birth were 35.8% (28/78) and 20.5% (16/78), respectively. The median (IQR) gestational age at amniocentesis was 31.9 weeks (27.2–34.0) while the median (IQR) gestational age of delivery was 35.0 weeks (31.2–37.6). Spontaneous early preterm delivery (<34 weeks of gestation) occurred in 66.6% (52/78). The frequency of intra‐amniotic infection and intra‐amniotic inflammation was 10.2% (8/78) and 26.9% (21/78), respectively (Figure 1). Women with intra‐amniotic inflammation had a significantly lower gestational age at amniocentesis, cervical length and gestational age at delivery than those without intra‐amniotic inflammation (Supplementary Table S2). However, higher amniotic fluid WBC and IL‐6 concentrations as well as frequency of delivery within 7 days of amniocentesis were observed in women with intra‐amniotic inflammation. The median (IQR) of the cervicovaginal fFN concentrations was significantly higher in patients with intra‐amniotic inflammation than in those without intra‐amniotic inflammation [449 (285.50–500) vs. 22 (4.50–86.50); p < 0.001] (Figure 2).

Diagnostic performance of cervicovaginal fFN in the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation

Quantitative cervicovaginal fFN had an AUC of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.87–0.98) for the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation (Figure 3 ). We then determined the optimal cut‐off value of cervicovaginal fFN concentrations based on the diagnostic performance of highest sensitivity and specificity. Cervicovaginal fluid fibronectin at the cut‐off of 125 ng/mL had 100% sensitivity, 82.46% specificity, 67.74% positive predictive value, and 100% negative predictive value for the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation (Table 4 ).

Clinical characteristics and pregnancy outcomes according to a cervicovaginal fFN cut‐off of 125 ng/mL

Patients whose concentration of cervicovaginal fFN was ≥125 ng/mL had a significantly shorter cervical length but greater cervical dilatation at admission (p = 0.01 and 0.04, respectively). In addition, a significantly higher median amniotic fluid WBC count and amniotic fluid IL‐6 concentration, but a lower median gestational age at delivery, were observed in those with fetal fibronectin ≥125 ng/mL (p < 0.05, for all). The frequency of intra‐amniotic inflammation and acute inflammatory lesions of the placenta was also significantly higher in women who had cervicovaginal fFN ≥125 ng/mL compared to those with cervicovaginal fFN <125 ng/mL (67.7% vs. 0% and 22.6% vs. 0%; p < 0.05 for both). DISCUSSION

The Principal findings of the study are: (1) The frequency of intra‐amniotic inflammation in women with spontaneous preterm labor and intact membranes was 26.9% (21/78); (2) the higher the cervicovaginal fFN concentration, the greater the risk of intra‐amniotic inflammation; (3) the cervicovaginal fFN concentration had an AUC of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.83–0.96) in the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation. This test with a cut‐off of 125 ng/mL had a 100% sensitivity, 100% negative predictive value, 82.46% specificity and a positive likelihood ratio of 5.7 for the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation; and (4) cervicovaginal fFN with a cut‐off of 125 ng/mL had a significantly higher predictive performance than the traditional cut‐off (50 ng/mL) for the prediction of intra‐amniotic inflammation in pregnant women presenting with preterm labor with intact membranes. The association between cervicovaginal fFN and the presence of intra‐amniotic inflammation might be due to an activation of the decidual membrane component of the common pathway of parturition, 10 which could be a consequence of intra‐amniotic inflammation. 60 , 61

Previous studies have reported an association between cervicovaginal fFN and intra‐amniotic inflammation. 37 , 38 The first study was reported by Yoon et al. who determined the amniotic fluid inflammatory status in asymptomatic women at 23 to 31 weeks of gestation with positive cervicovaginal fFN (>50 ng/mL) (n = 58/1709). 37 The frequency of intra‐amniotic inflammation (IL‐6 >2.5 ng/mL) was 5.3% and, importantly, all patients with a positive fetal fibronectin concentration and intra‐amniotic inflammation subsequently delivered preterm neonates. These observations suggested an association between positive cervicovaginal fFN and intra‐amniotic inflammation as well as preterm delivery. 37

Subsequently, Oh et al. evaluated whether the quantitative cervicovaginal fetal fibronectin test can predict intra‐amniotic inflammation in 180 patients with preterm labor and intact membranes. 38 In this study, intra‐amniotic inflammation was defined by an elevation of matrix mellatoproteinase‐8 >23 ng/mL, and the prevalence of intra‐amniotic inflammation was 30%. The authors concluded that the higher cervicovaginal fFN concentration, the greater the risk of intra‐amniotic infection/inflammation (relative risk, 16.8, 95% CI: 5.8–48.5). An elevation of cervicovaginal fFN (≥150 ng/mL) had a sensitivity of 86.2%, a specificity of 71.3%, a positive likelihood ratio of 3, and a negative likelihood ratio of 0.19 for the diagnosis of intra‐amniotic inflammation. In addition, the authors have shown that a cervicovaginal fFN concentration ≥150 ng/mL had a better diagnostic performance than a cervicovaginal fFN ≥50 ng/mL in the identification of intra‐amniotic infection/inflammation. 38 Our findings are consistent with those reported by Oh et al., 38 as we reported a similar cut‐off for fetal fibronectin, although with slightly higher predictive performance. Of note, the frequencies of intra‐amniotic inflammation in the Oh et al. and in our studies are approximately similar (30% vs. 26.9%), and this observation is consistent with previous reports. 14 , 28 , 33 , 54 , 62 Interestingly all pregnant women with cervicovaginal fFN concentration <125 ng/mL had no intra‐amniotic inflammation. Ten patients (10/57) had a fetal fibronectin concentration ≥125 ng/mL but had no intra‐amniotic inflammation [i.e., a false positive fetal fibronectin test; 17.5% (10/57)]. The reason for a false‐positive fibronectin test might be due to minimal bleeding from placenta previa (n = 2) and a mucous bloody show with advanced cervical dilatation (n = 8). The latter is indicative of the activation of the common pathway of parturition.

Lastly, van der Krogt et al. have also demonstrated an association between a high cervicovaginal fFN test (>50 ng/mL) and acute inflammatory lesions of the placenta in women who delivered preterm (n = 66). 49 Cervicovaginal fFN was determined during antenatal care in asymptomatic women at risk for spontaneous preterm birth. Cervicovaginal fFN had a sensitivity of 65.6%, a specificity of 85.4%, a positive likelihood ratio of 4.48, and a negative likelihood ratio of 0.4 for the identification of acute inflammatory lesions of the placenta. Of note, cervicovaginal fFN determination in this study was performed a long time prior to the onset of preterm labor. Hence, the temporal relationship of high cervicovaginal fFN and the occurrence of intra‐amniotic infection could not be evaluated.

Intra‐amniotic infection and/or intra‐amniotic inflammation is causally linked to spontaneous preterm delivery. 2 , 10 , 11 Recent evidence suggests that intra‐amniotic infection as well as sterile intra‐amniotic inflammation can be successfully treated by using a new regimen of antimicrobial agents. 63 , 64 , 65 , 66 , 67

Previous reports demonstrated that the antibiotic regimen consisting of ceftriaxone, metronidazole, and clarithromycin (also called “triple antibiotics”) was effective in treating proven intra‐amniotic infection and intra‐amniotic inflammation in women presenting with preterm labor with intact membranes, 68 preterm prelabor rupture of membranes, 69 acute cervical insufficiency, 67 , 70 or mid‐trimester miscarriage. 34 , 71 Specifically, 50 women presenting with preterm labor and intact membranes with intra‐amniotic infection and/or intra‐amniotic inflammation (determined by cultivation and polymerase chain reaction for Ureaplasma spp. and IL‐6 concentrations) received triple antibiotics for 14 days. Amniocentesis was repeated at 1 week after antibiotic treatment to evaluate the responsiveness of treatment. Resolution of intra‐amniotic infection and/or intra‐amniotic inflammation was demonstrated in 79% (15/19) of patients. Overall treatment success, defined as a resolution of intra‐amniotic infection/intra‐amniotic inflammation or delivery at a gestational age >37 weeks, was 84% (16/19). 68 This study demonstrated the effectiveness of triple antibiotics to treat intra‐amniotic infection/inflammation as well as to improve maternal and fetal outcomes in women with proven intra‐amniotic infection/inflammation. However, amniocentesis was considered an invasive tool. According to the results of previous 37 , 38 and our studies, we proposed that the cervicovaginal fFN test can be used as a test to assess the likelihood of intra‐amniotic inflammation in patients presenting with spontaneous preterm labor and intact membranes. Specifically, cervicovaginal fluid fibronectin had a high sensitivity and a negative predictive value; therefore, if the test result is negative (<125 ng/mL), the patients are unlikely to have intra‐amniotic inflammation, hence, an amniocentesis might not be necessary. Importantly, all patients with intra‐amniotic inflammation had a high cervicovaginal fFN (≥125 ng/mL) concentration; however, not all patients with an elevated cervicovaginal fluid fibronectin concentration had intra‐amniotic inflammation. Altogether, this test may be useful as an initial assessment to select appropriate patients for amniocentesis to determine intra‐amniotic infection and/or intra‐amniotic inflammation. This would have decreased the number of amniocenteses. Collectively, the cervicovaginal fFN test had the optimal properties of a point‐of‐care test or a liquid biopsy for the identification of the intra‐amniotic inflammation status, namely: (1) high sensitivity and specificity for the determination of intra‐amniotic inflammation; (2) simple to perform (bedside test); (3) inexpensive to set up; (4) operator independent; and (5) rapid in obtaining the result (within 10 min). A larger study is required to replicate these findings.

The strength of our study was that we collected several variables associated with intra‐amniotic infection and intra‐amniotic inflammation including maternal clinical characteristics, cervical examination, cervical length, cervicovaginal fFN concentration, and amniotic fluid profile data. Our limitation was related to the small sample size.

CONCLUSION

Quantitative cervicovaginal fFN with a cut‐off of 125 ng/mL had a high sensitivity and a negative predictive value as well as a positive likelihood ratio for the identification of intra‐amniotic inflammation in women presenting with spontaneous preterm labor and intact membranes. Its high sensitivity and negative predictive value can be used to decrease an index of suspicion of intra‐amniotic inflammation; therefore, this test may be useful as an initial assessment test to select appropriate patients for amniocentesis to determine intra‐amniotic infection and/or intra‐amniotic inflammation. Results can be available within 10 min—this has important clinical implications and opens avenues for early diagnosis as well as treatment of intra‐amniotic inflammation and intra‐amniotic infection.
