Blueprint for building a patient-centered hereditary cancer program based on stakeholder perspectives
Authors: Tamar Nicole Soussana, Michelle Primiano, Muhammad Danyal Ahsan, Carolyn Baron, Lauren Mitchell, Ravi N. Sharaf, Melissa K. Frey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Program structure, educational offerings, and staff roles are key considerations in establishing cancer genetics services.•New programs should consider incorporating a comprehensive care model to offer surveillance, follow-up, and cascade testing.•Patient and provider education is important to enable genetics referrals and a full understanding of testing results.•Assigning patient-facing versus administrative responsibilities to distinct roles may help to promote clinical efficiency.

Introduction

Cancer genetics services have become an extraordinarily important resource to identify and reduce hereditary risk for numerous cancers, including breast, ovarian, and endometrial cancers. More than 10% of cancers are likely associated with an underlying germline pathogenic variant in a single cancer susceptibility gene (Mandelker et al., 2017, Yap et al., 2022). For ovarian and pancreatic cancer, universal genetic testing is currently recommended due to high rates of underlying hereditary cancer syndromes (Stoffel and Carethers, 2020, Daly MB, Pal T, Maxwell KN, Churpek J, Kohlmann W, AlHilli Z, et al. NCCN Guidelines(R) Insights: Genetic/Familial High-Risk Assessment: Breast, Ovarian, and Pancreatic, Version 2.202J Natl Compr Canc Netw., 2023). Genetic testing provides critical information that can influence cancer treatment and allow for personalized precision cancer care (Russo and Giri, 2022, Cortesi et al., 2021). However, it is equally important to identify hereditary cancer syndromes in cancer unaffected individuals, as genetic testing results can prompt screening and risk-reducing interventions that decrease cancer-associated morbidity and mortality (Kauff et al., 2008, Koornstra et al., 2009). Over the past decade, the demand for clinical cancer genetics services has steadily increased, with projections indicating substantial growth in the coming years (Schienda and Stopfer, 2020, Dragojlovic et al., 2023). Therefore, it is necessary to understand factors that may support the development of successful hereditary cancer programs. Our objective was to synthesize the perspectives of stakeholders surrounding these facilitators.

Materials and methods

A single internal consultant conducted key informant interviews with 34 individuals from NewYork-Presbyterian Hospital/Weill Cornell Medicine Manhattan, NewYork-Presbyterian Queens, NewYork-Presbyterian Brooklyn Methodist Hospital, and Columbia University Medical Center during the summer of 2021 to gather recommendations for a clinical cancer genetics program in its developmental stage. Participants were identified by genetics program leadership and included six genetics providers, six genetic counselors, eight administrative staff members, and 14 referring providers. Interviews were conducted via Zoom and ranged from 15 min to one hour in length. The data were aggregated and organized by topic and theme.

Results

Roles and responsibilities of genetics program staff

The provider that refers patients to genetics services is often responsible for implementing recommendations made by genetics providers. Our referring providers expressed that this responsibility is difficult, with many indicating a preference for a genetics program to coordinate and monitor downstream patient care, including cancer surveillance, risk-reduction interventions, and cascade testing (Fig. 1). Stakeholders also requested that genetic counselors with expertise in cancer genetics be available to consult with patient-facing clinicians. In several states, including New York, genetic counselors cannot adequately bill for the time and complexity of patient-facing interactions. To address this challenge, stakeholders recommended that genetic counselors see patients together with a billing provider rather than individually. Stakeholders also indicated that employing genetic counselors with same-day availability may promote a more seamless patient experience, as patients who communicate genetic concerns to referring providers can be seen without needing to return for a separate appointment (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Summary of stakeholder recommendations for the development of clinical cancer genetics programs.

Administrative and clinical efficiency

Stakeholders noted significant administrative burden associated with providing genetics services. Tracking referrals, coordinating with laboratories, communicating with insurance providers, and following up on patient-related questions can occupy a large share of a provider’s time. Our stakeholders suggested that much of this work could be delegated so that providers could focus on patient-facing tasks. Employing genetic counseling assistants is one potential strategy to help address administrative demands (Fig. 1). The use of genetic counseling assistants has been shown to contribute positively to the overall functioning of a cancer genetics clinic by boosting efficiency of pre-appointment activities, reducing the length of counseling appointments, and increasing patient volume (Hallquist et al., 2020). In addition, stakeholders advocated for the incorporation of algorithms to facilitate referrals to genetics providers. This recommendation is supported by a prior study in which standardized electronic medical record referrals sent directly to a cancer genetics program significantly increased referral rates (Bednar et al., 2022).

Care access

Stakeholders believed that cancer genetics programs should be structured to maximize access and foster a patient-centered experience (Fig. 1). Medical translators and stipends or grants for populations with financial barriers were recommended to facilitate equitable access. Additionally, stakeholders recommend both video and in-person visit options to promote accessibility, but also note that the consent process is easier conducted in person. Some participants proposed offering an option for genetic testing at chemotherapy infusion suites rather than through a separate phlebotomy visit. Similarly, there is potential to complete informed consent for genetic testing during existing appointments for treatment or phlebotomy. Stakeholders also suggested a prioritized appointment system to allow expedited genetic testing for patients with time-sensitive medical concerns (e.g., patients whose cancer treatment or surgery could be impacted by testing results, or patients considering pre-implantation genetic diagnosis during in-vitro fertilization who have a concerning family history for hereditary cancer).

Program scope and continuity of care

Stakeholders requested that clinical cancer genetics programs be involved in all aspects of patient care relating to hereditary cancer. Beyond initial risk assessment and genetic testing, stakeholders advocated that genetics programs manage cancer-related follow-up care and cascade genetic testing, which enables identification of genetic carriers (Fig. 1). Previous research found that provider- or clinic-initiated contact of relatives significantly improved the rate of cascade testing as compared to patient-mediated contact (Frey et al., 2022). Stakeholders also suggested establishing tumor boards for identification of patients in need of genetic counseling, which has been shown to increase genetic testing rates (Bednar et al., 2022). Regular follow-up visits were recommended for patients with a pathogenic variant or variant of unknown significance. Further, stakeholders supported the operation of clinical cancer genetics programs as standalone entities within the hospital setting (Fig. 1). This design contrasted with previous service structures at this institution whereby a genetics clinician was embedded within a specific department. Of note, prior research suggests that embedding genetic counselors within non-genetics clinics effectively increases access to genetic counseling (Bednar et al., 2022). However, a standalone program may allow for implementation of the suggestions described above, as well as additional benefits, such as a program-specific call center and website and a designated space for clinical and administrative tasks.

Patient and provider education

Stakeholders noted a paucity of cancer genetics-focused educational tools and opportunities for both patients and providers (Fig. 1). Referring oncology providers may lack an understanding of cancer genetic counseling, benefits of genetic testing, and the complex and dynamic eligibility criteria for genetic testing (Ha et al., 2018). As a result, providers may be uncertain about when to refer patients for genetic evaluation, and those implementing mainstream genetic testing may lack the training or resources to provide comprehensive pre- and post-test counseling. Educating providers, especially those in primary care and oncology, can improve cancer genetics referral rates (Bednar et al., 2022, Ha et al., 2018). Clinical cancer genetics providers may also benefit from educational opportunities, such as case conferences and continuing medical education activities. Patient education on topics ranging from genetic testing limitations to results implications is critical, especially among historically underserved patients or those with low health literacy (Joseph et al., 2017). Supplementation of information covered during the genetic counseling appointment, such as decision aid tools and knowledge questionnaires, has been found to decrease appointment times and increase genetic counseling efficiency (Bednar et al., 2022). Completion rates of genetic testing also improve after replacing pre-test genetic counseling with educational videos (Bednar et al., 2022, Swisher, 2023). Stakeholders proposed that providing comprehensive educational resources and information offers the potential to increase efficiency and patient satisfaction at cancer genetics programs.

Conclusions

There is an urgent need to meet the ever-increasing demand for clinical cancer genetics services. Cancer genetics programs can identify and reduce risk across a variety of hereditary cancer syndromes, with breast and gynecologic cancers as major areas of focus. It is important that programs are designed in a way that supports providers and enables the delivery of high-quality patient care. Through stakeholder interviews, we identified recommendations for a clinical cancer genetics program’s development, which centered on the role of providers, program structure, and educational offerings. Stakeholders emphasized the importance of clear roles for genetic counselors and providers and the potential to reduce administrative burden through delegation of non-clinical tasks to support staff. Key structural considerations surrounded the design of programs that promote access, convenience, and comprehensive patient care beyond initial genetic testing. Stakeholders also supported the operation of programs as standalone entities with centralized resources to streamline referrals and care continuity. Furthermore, a focus on education can help to equip patients and providers with the knowledge necessary to optimize referrals and understand genetic testing implications. It is important to note that the stakeholder recommendations provided here are limited to a single hospital system in New York City, and therefore applicability may be limited. However, collectively, these findings provide a valuable framework for institutions aiming to establish or improve clinical cancer genetics programs. These stakeholder recommendations aim to promote a patient-centered, efficient, and accessible model that addresses the diverse needs of patients and providers alike. Future research efforts may involve the representation of patient perspectives on this clinical framework, and additional ways in which the cancer genetics clinic may be improved.
