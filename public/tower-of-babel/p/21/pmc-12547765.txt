Establishment of a Modified Ferric Chloride-Induced Canine Superficial Femoral Artery Thrombosis Model
Authors: Wang Song, Yingwen Wang, Shuting Li, Chunmei Long, Xiang Han, Xingqi Fang, Zhen Li, Yuwei Yang, Shihao Zhu, Xuanyin Chen, Qi Wang, Baolai Zhang, Li Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ferric chloride (FeCl3) is a common agent for inducing thrombosis in laboratory animals. However, there is no sufficient data regarding the conditions of model preparation in dogs. We aimed to identify the optimal concentration and solvent of FeCl3 for inducing canine superficial femoral artery thrombosis. We applied 30, 40, or 50% FeCl3 in 1 M hydrochloric acid or water topically on the superficial femoral artery for 30 min to induce thrombosis, respectively, with the distal end of the exposed artery clipped for 15 min. Then the thrombus size and blood flow were assessed by the High-Resolution Ultrasonic Photoacoustic Multimode Imaging System, and pathological changes were evaluated with HE staining. Regardless of the concentration or solvent, FeCl3 solutions successfully induced stable and dense thrombi. At day 28, 30% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl demonstrated greater peak systolic velocity (PSV) ratios of normal and recanalization rates than 40% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl. Except for day 14, 40% FeCl3 in H2O had lower PSV ratios and recanalization rates compared to 50% FeCl3 in H2O. The 40% FeCl3 in H2O demonstrated greater blood flow recovery than the 40% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl. Compared to the water solution, the acidic solution caused more pronounced endothelial cell damage, with no differences between varying concentrations in the same solvent. FeCl3 application combined with blood flow blockage effectively induced stable thrombi in the superficial femoral arteries of dogs. Hydrochloric acid solutions demonstrated better thrombus formation efficacy than water.

Introduction

Vascular thrombosis leads to the occurrence of conditions such as myocardial infarction, venous thromboembolism, and ischemic stroke. Current thrombolytic treatments, which include antiplatelet and thrombolytic medications like urokinase, alteplase, and tenecteplase, face limitations such as a narrow therapeutic window and bleeding risks. Consequently, there is an ongoing clinical demand for safer, more potent, and more convenient drugs, which remains a central focus in thrombolytic drug research.

To explore the mechanisms of thrombosis formation and evaluate thrombolytic drugs in preclinical studies, establishing applicable thrombosis models in animals is essential. Currently, the methods of vascular thrombosis induction include the injection of autologous thrombus, vascular ligation, artery-vein bypass thrombosis, photochemically induced thrombotic occlusion, electrolysis-induced vascular injury, and ferric chloride (FeCl3) thrombotic induction. Among them, the topical application of FeCl3 to blood vessels is one of the most widely used approaches for inducing thrombosis. Its advantages are that it more closely resembles the pathophysiology of human thrombosis formation, and special equipment is not required. The mechanism behind this method is that FeCl3 penetrates into the vascular intima, causes vascular endothelial cell detachment, and exposes the basement membrane components to the circulating blood flow. Then a large number of iron ions accumulate on the surface of endothelial cells, promoting platelets and tissue factors to form aggregates that adhere to the surface, which eventually leads to thrombus formation in the lumen of the vessel. Under the dissecting microscope, the formed thrombus resembles the arterial thrombus of humans.

Researchers who use FeCl3 to induce thrombosis often focus on small animals such as rats, mice, rabbits, and guinea pigs, studying blood vessels like the carotid artery, femoral artery, mesenteric vessel, and inferior vena cava. Small animals have the advantages of being easily genetically manipulated and having low purchase prices and low per diem housing costs. In contrast, the experimental costs of larger animals like dogs and pigs are high but characterized by platelet physiology that is closer to humans.

In thrombosis research, fewer studies focus on larger animals like dogs. Huttinger et al. and their colleagues have detailed the method of inducing thrombosis in the common carotid artery of dogs using FeCl3. In their experiments, deionized water was selected to dissolve FeCl3. However, there is currently no consensus on the optimal induction time, concentration, or solvent for establishing stable and reproducible thrombosis models via FeCl3 thrombotic induction in dogs. In water, ferric ions can react with water molecules to form iron­(III) hydroxide (Fe (OH)3) and hydrochloric acid (HCl). Given the critical role of ferric ions in thrombus formation, it is convincing that hydrolysis can diminish the thrombosis effect. Thus, it would be better to use a hydrochloric acid solution for preparation to ensure that the concentration of iron ions in the FeCl3 solution is clear and stable by inhibiting hydrolysis. In addition, some studies have successfully established animal venous thrombosis models by combining FeCl3-induced thrombosis with blocking blood flow or creating vascular stenosis. ,

In this study, we induced thrombosis in the superficial femoral artery of dogs using FeCl3 with different concentrations or solvents in combination with clipping the distal end of the exposed artery.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Ethical Statement

All beagles (aged 12 to 14 months, half male and half female) were provided by the Sichuan Yangshe Institute (Chengdu, China) and fed adaptively in the experimental environment for 2 weeks. The room temperature was 20–22 °C and the light/dark cycle was 12/12 h (lights on from 6 A.M. to 6 P.M.). The animals had free access to food and tap water. All studies were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the School of Basic Medical Sciences of Lanzhou University (Approval Number: Lzujcyxy20241102).

Induction of Femoral Artery Thrombosis in Dogs

For the purpose of exploring the most suitable solvent and optimal treatment concentration to form thrombi by the induction of FeCl3, all dogs were fasted for 24 h and randomly assigned to four different groups. FeCl3 (Shanghai Jinshan Chemical Plant; Product Batch Number: XK13–0010807101 H) was diluted in deionized water or 1 M hydrochloric acid solution, and finally four different solutions were applied: 30%(w/v) FeCl3 solution in 1 M HCl, 40%(w/v) FeCl3 solution in 1 M HCl, 40%(w/v) FeCl3 solution in water, 50%(w/v) FeCl3 solution in water. The cotton pieces were soaked in FeCl3 solutions for 10 min before the establishment of the thrombi.

Preceded by a subcutaneous injection of atropine­(Sui Cheng Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Product Batch Number: 62312312 42, Specification: 1 mL: 0.5 mg) 15 min earlier to prevent excessive salivation, Zoletil 50 (VIRBAC, Product Batch Number: BN 968A) was administered via intramuscular injection­(7.5 mg/kg) to induce the anesthetic state in dogs, while propofol (Xi’an Libang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Product Batch Number: 12311162 II, Specification: 50 mL: 0.5 g) was delivered intravenously (4–6 drops/s) to maintain anesthesia during the surgical procedure.

Under anesthesia, the dogs were fixed on the operating table in the supine position, with the skin from the groin to the knee prepared and disinfected using an iodophor. The location of the femoral artery was identified by palpating the area where the pulse was the most prominent. A skin incision was made, and the subcutaneous and muscle tissues were carefully separated to expose 2 cm of the superficial femoral artery. An artery clamp was then applied to the distal end of the exposed artery at its bifurcation for 15 min. Meanwhile, presoaked cotton pieces were wrapped around the artery and secured with a plastic film to protect the surrounding tissues. To prevent desiccation during the procedure, 0.02 mL of FeCl3 solution was added to the cotton every 10 min. After 30 min, the clamp and cotton pieces were removed, and the incision was closed with sutures and disinfected.

Observation of Thrombi Formation of the Superficial Femoral Artery and the Image Record

Prior to thrombus induction, the first ultrasonographic evaluation of the superficial femoral artery was performed in all animals, with subsequent examinations conducted immediately postoperatively and on postoperative days 7, 14, 21, and 28.

Following standardized anesthesia with Zoletil 50 (Virbac, France), the surgical site and transducer were coated with an acoustic coupling gel. Hemodynamic and morphological data were acquired by using the High-Resolution Ultrasonic Photoacoustic Multimode Imaging System (VEVO F2 LZAR-X; FUJIFILM, Japan) before and after surgical intervention. B-mode ultrasonography (29 MHz, 1–27 mm) enabled anatomical identification of the superficial femoral artery and intraluminal thrombi with transverse and longitudinal vascular images archived for further analysis.

Subsequent hemodynamic evaluations using Color Doppler Mode quantified critical parameters, including peak systolic velocity (PSV), velocity time integral (VTI), peak velocity (Peak Vel), mean velocity (Mean Vel), pulsatility index (PI), and resistance index (RI). Pulsed-wave (PW) Doppler spectral analysis involved marking three consecutive peaks with parameter means calculated to minimize physiological variability. Thrombus length was determined by manual tracing of thrombi in longitudinal B-mode images, while transverse sections provided measurements of the vascular diameter and recanalization rate.

Measurements of Thrombi

On the 28th day, the animals were anesthetized following the standardized protocol established during thrombus induction. After the final ultrasound observation, the blood vessels were excised, and photographs were taken, during which the length and wet weight of the blood vessels and thrombi were measured.

HE Staining of Blood Vessels

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was utilized to observe the pathological changes in the blood vessels. Briefly, the samples were fixed via 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at a thickness of 3 μm. HE staining was performed on blood vessel slices, and the images were obtained by Research-grade Upright Microscopic Imaging System (Nikon-ECLIPSE 80i/DS-Ri2/NIS-Elements D, Japan).

Statistical Analysis

In the following section, the analysis of the experimental data was performed using SPSS 26.0 software and Prism 10.1.2. One-way ANOVA was used in blood flow analysis. All results were represented as Mean ± SEM, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The Blood Flow in the Superficial Femoral Artery Significantly Decreased after FeCl3 Treatment

Following FeCl3 induction and subsequent ultrasonographic evaluation, blood flow data within the superficial femoral artery was acquired, as shown in the longitudinal views of Pulse-wave Doppler Mode (Figure ). A significant reduction in blood flow was observed compared with the normal state. Detailed analysis using Pulse-wave Doppler Mode revealed marked declines in key blood flow parameters, including PSV, VTI, Peak Vel, and Mean Vel. These findings indicate that FeCl3 treatment induced thrombus formation in the superficial femoral arteries of dogs across different concentrations and solvents, leading to vascular obstruction and significant alterations in hemodynamics.

Thrombosis Induced by a Higher Concentration of FeCl3 Is More Stable than the Lower One Dissolved in 1 M HCl

We also observed differences between solutions of the same solvent but different concentrations. When 30% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl was compared with 40% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl, the former showed a more pronounced increase in blood flow in the embolized vessels after modeling. The PSV of 30% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl recovered to 32.02 ± 4.20% and 42.99 ± 12.55% of normal levels on the 14th and 28th days postmodeling, respectively, while the PSV of 40% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl recovered to 16.73 ± 5.75% and 33.34 ± 12.71% (Figure a,b).

Similar differences were noted in the recanalization rate and the thrombus length. On the 14th, 21st, and 28th days, thrombi induced by 40% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl were longer (Figure a,c), while the recanalization rate of 30% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl was higher than that of 40% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl at all postmodeling time points, reaching 30.81 ± 12.82% and 30.40 ± 3.65% on the 28th day, respectively (Figure b,d). However, no significant changes in PI and RI were observed for either solution before or after modeling (Figure b). Thrombosis Induced by a Lower Concentration of FeCl3 is More Stable than the Higher One Dissolved in Water

For 40% FeCl3 in H2O and 50% FeCl3 in H2O, our findings indicate that higher concentrations do not necessarily enhance thrombus formation efficacy. In ultrasound detection, except on day 14, the PSV of 50% FeCl3 in H2O was higher than that of 40% FeCl3 in H2O on days 7, 21, and 28 postmodeling (Figure a,b). Similar trends were observed for the VTI, Peak Vel, and Mean Vel (Figure b). No significant differences were found in the PI and RI (Figure b).

Thrombogenic Effect of FeCl3 in 1 M HCl Is Better than That of FeCl3 in H2O

In order to explore the impact of solvents on thrombi induced by FeCl3, we compared the thrombogenic effect of a 1 M hydrochloric acid solution and deionized water. As shown in Figure a,b, on the seventh day after modeling, the blood flow velocity of both solutions began to increase as observed from PSV, Peak Vel, Mean Vel, and VTI. The increase in blood flow velocity for the 40% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl was lower than that of the 40% FeCl3 in H2O. On the 28th day, the PSV of the former recovered to 33.34 ± 12.71% of the normal state on the 28th day after modeling, while the latter just recovered to 16.73 ± 5.75%. The thrombus length of 40% FeCl3 in HCl was larger at all time points (Figure a,c). However, the recanalization rates showed no trend as described above (Figure b,d). Moreover, during measurement, it was found that the average lengths of the thrombi formed in the 40% FeCl3 in H2O were lower than those of 40% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl (Table ). Referring to the thrombus weight (Figure e), the thrombus weight of 40% FeCl3 in H2O was smaller.

Varying Degrees of Injuries of the Superficial Femoral Artery

HE staining was performed on the obtained thrombi. From the images under the 40× lens (Figure ), it can be observed that the infiltration of inflammatory cells, proliferation of fibroblasts and fibrocytes, and the formation of granulation tissue were evident in all solutions, but the sections exhibited damage of varying degrees to the intima.

Pathological changes in the superficial femoral artery of dogs treated with FeCl3 were observed by optical microscopy. (a) HE staining of the superficial femoral artery induced by different concentrations and solvent solutions of FeCl3. Scale bar = 500 μm. (b) The enlarged images above are in each part of each group. Scale bar = 100 μm.

In the acid solutions, notably, 40% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl exhibited substantial blood clots in the proximal, middle, and distal sections, encircled by uniformly proliferating granulation tissue. The 30% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl featured smaller clots centrally in the proximal and distal sections, accompanied by extensive granulation tissue and dense white luminal channels within the newly formed tissue. The middle section was reminiscent of 40% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl, with significant clot presence. In contrast, the water solution showed newly formed tissue covering most of the central field of view, with minor blood clot areas. The 50% FeCl3 in H2O had a larger clot area compared to the 40% FeCl3 in H2O. Upon closer examination with a 100× lens (Figure ), more intricate damage was evident with endothelial cells lining the white luminal channels, suggesting the formation of new blood vessels.

Discussion

FeCl3 induction is widely used in the preparation of thromboembolic models, and it is considered a method that more closely resembles the pathophysiology of human thrombosis compared to mechanical, photochemical, or laser-induced injuries; however, the mechanical injury approach is more invasive, requires greater operator skill, presents challenges in standardization, and tends to produce inconsistent results. While photochemical and laser-induced methods are minimally invasive and offer high spatial precision, both suffer from relatively high experimental costs. Moreover, while most researchers have only focused on arterial and venous thrombosis in small animals such as mice, rats, and rabbits, there is no established induction condition for the construction of a canine arterial thrombosis model.

Huttinger et al. established a carotid artery thrombosis model in canines using an FeCl3 solution of 50% (in water). Angiography, blood flow velocity measurements, and pathological section results showed that the target vessels were completely occluded. Based on this, we determined the maximum concentration of FeCl3 used in our experiments to be 50%. However, in our previous studies, it was observed that 50% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl resulted in the formation of excessively stable thrombi, exceeding the typical pathological range and potentially obscuring the efficacy of thrombolytic agents with established effectiveness. Therefore, taking into account the above finding, the significant costs associated with large animal experiments, and existing literature, the concentrations of ferric chloride in aqueous solutions and hydrochloric acid aqueous solutions were selected for this study. Carlsson et al. combined FeCl3 with inducing vessel stenosis in the rat caval vein thrombosis model. This combination reduced the concentration of FeCl3 necessary to conduct and shortened the duration of induction, most importantly, it leads to a high success rate for the FeCl3 thrombosis model. Therefore, we made several refinements to the model to enhance its reproducibility, specifically by occluding the distal end of the superficial femoral artery.

However, many studies fail to specify the preparation method for FeCl3 solutions. Some studies suggest using physiological saline or deionized water for preparation, but FeCl3 can hydrolyze when dissolved in water, and its preparation requires the addition of hydrochloric acid to inhibit its hydrolysis. Thus, in this experiment, four solutions were designed to observe their effects on thrombus formation by using different concentrations of FeCl3 prepared with water or hydrochloric acid solutions. When ferric chloride is dissolved in pure water, heterogeneity during dissolution can cause locally elevated pH, leading to rapid hydrolysis and the formation of ferric hydroxide colloids, which compromise solution stability. In contrast, the use of 1 M HCl (theoretical pH = 0) as a solvent effectively suppresses colloid formation during dissolution. Accordingly, ferric chloride solutions prepared with 1 M HCl remain clear and transparent and exhibit a light yellow color, as opposed to the reddish-brown hue characteristic of extensive hydrolysis. Notably, results from ultrasound imaging and histopathological evaluation (HE staining) revealed that exposure to 30% and 40% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl did not induce significant vascular pathology compared to that in the aqueous solution. In addition, it was found that 50% FeCl3 prepared with deionized water resulted in poor absorption by the filter paper, with a slag point in it, likely due to the instability of it. Correspondingly, this instability also led to a more pronounced blood flow recovery trend compared to 40% FeCl3 in water after modeling, indicating less effective thrombus formation. The other solutions did not exhibit these issues.

In comparison to the recommended protocol, which specifies an induction time of 45 min for thrombosis formation, our study utilized a shorter duration of 30 min. Furthermore, the common carotid artery lies deep and is in close proximity to critical nerves, making it susceptible to severe injury during manipulation. In contrast, the superficial femoral artery presents a simpler anatomical structure, allowing for easier surgical procedures and greater efficiency. Practically, the use of the superficial femoral artery may result in reduced tissue trauma, higher postoperative survival rates, and extended survival times in animal subjects, thereby facilitating longer-term investigations into thrombolytic agents.

When comparing different solvents, 40% FeCl3 in H2O showed a smaller thrombus size and better blood flow recovery over time than FeCl3 in 1 M HCl. Pathological observations revealed uniform granulation tissue proliferation in acid solutions, likely due to even iron ion distribution, causing uniform vascular intima damage and hindering intimal hyperplasia. In contrast, water solutions showed irregular proliferative tissue, indicating an instability. Given the differences in ferric complexes between FeCl3 solutions prepared with hydrochloric acid and water, we think that the concentration of thrombus-associated ferric complexes, which may be the cause of vascular injury in the aqueous solution, is lower than the former. This then leads to reduced absorption of FeCl3 and diminished vascular endothelium damage, ultimately resulting in less stable and more dispersible thrombi compared with acid solutions. As for which specific iron complex plays a key role in thrombus formation, existing research has not extensively addressed this question. Future studies will be required to further investigate this aspect.

A comparison also was made between solutions using the same solvent but with different concentrations. The 40% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl not only had lower blood flow recovery and larger mean obtained thrombus length than 30% FeCl3 in 1 M HCl. In the case of the aqueous solutions, 50% FeCl3 did not demonstrate superior thrombogenic effects compared to 40% of FeCl3. This finding suggested that increasing the concentration beyond 40% did not necessarily enhance the thrombus formation. Hydrolysis in water solutions destabilized the concentration, leading to higher blood flow recovery in higher concentration solutions postmodeling, which also explains the superiority of acid solutions. Additionally, during the dissection in the experiment, it was found that some dogs in 40% FeCl3 in H2O had thrombi protruding in a spherical shape. It was considered that the stability of the front end of the canine thrombi was poor, and it fell off due to the blood flow impact during the period, and squeezed together with the middle part of the thrombi, causing local vascular dilation and severe blockage.

In summary, our experimental results confirm that the hydrochloric acid solution used as a solvent for the preparation of FeCl3 induces thrombus formation more effectively than that in deionized water. And acidic solution with higher concentrations yields better thrombus formation results.

The establishment of the thrombus model in the superficial femoral artery of dogs provides more references for developing embolism models using experimental animals other than rats and rabbits. Simultaneously, the outcomes also provide data support for future studies. However, the reactivity of this model to different thrombolytic drugs and its potential application in other blood vessels still need further validation.

Conclusions

In this study, we combined the induction of FeCl3 with unilateral vascular occlusion to induce thrombosis in the superficial femoral artery, demonstrating that this method can form relatively stable and uniform thrombi within blood vessels. On the other hand, by comparing the results of FeCl3 dissolved in different solvents, it is believed that 1 M hydrochloric acid solution used to prepare the induction solution yields better results than water, leading to improved thrombosis effect.
