“Differentiated care is not a matter of feasibility but a necessity”- service providers’ perception on initiating a comprehensive tuberculosis care package (CCp-K) to reduce deaths in Kerala, India
Authors: Raman Swathy Vaman, Kalaiselvi Selvaraj, Dina Nair, Mathew Joseph Valamparampil, Anjali Vannadil, Jose Vincent, Reshma Rajan Sudha, Serah Mamachan, Greshma Chandrasekharan Nair, Athira Kavundezhath Radhakrishnan, Jeen Melfha, Annadurai Jeyakumar, Hemant Deepak Shewade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKerala reports one of the highest case fatality rates and high prevalence of comorbidities and risk factors among persons with tuberculosis (PwTB). We developed a comprehensive care model (CCp-K) focusing on triaging persons with tuberculosis for severe illness, uncontrolled diabetes, alcohol and nicotine dependence at diagnosis, and ensuring inpatient care and follow-up. In the pre-implementation phase, we explored the barriers, facilitators, and perceived suggestions in the design and implementation of CCp-K from various interest holders for sustainable implementation.MethodsWe conducted an interest holder mapping and using the Power-Interest matrix of Mendelow, we purposively selected key interest holders at the district and state level. In-depth interviews were conducted using an interview guide by trained interviewers until data saturation was achieved. The audio recordings were transcribed, translated, and analysed using a framework-guided thematic analysis approach.ResultsTwelve interest holders participated in the interview, which included nine men and three women. The interviews lasted for a mean duration of 74 min (SD = 22). A total of 33 codes were generated and categorized under five themes: (i) perception on TB deaths (ii) relevance of CCp-K differentiated TB care model, (iii) perceived feasibility and enablers of implementing CCp-K, (iv) perceived barriers, and (v) perceived suggestions for the model and its delivery. The interest holders perceived that while delayed diagnosis contributes to TB mortality, the CCp-K model can bridge critical gaps in post-diagnosis care, particularly through early triaging and comorbidity management to reduce TB deaths. The robust healthcare infrastructure of Kerala, local governments and community involvement, and cross-adaptation opportunities from previous successful models were perceived as facilitators. The lack of nodal treatment centres, stigma among healthcare workers, social vulnerability, and competing priorities were perceived as major barriers.ConclusionsThe interest holders perceived the CCp-K model to be relevant and feasible. However, Kerala should overcome the barriers of inadequate inpatient referral centers by establishing designated TB care facilities, leveraging existing collaboration with private partnerships, and strengthening the social support system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13413-1.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is the world’s leading cause of death from a single infectious disease agent. In 2023, an estimated 10.8 million people fell ill with TB, and 1.3 million died from TB [1]. India contributes to 28% of deaths globally, with an estimated 0.3 million deaths in 2023 [2]. Kerala, the southernmost state of India, notifies almost three times lower TB than the national average (62 vs. 179 per 100,000 population) [3]. However, the reported case fatality among persons with TB (PwTB) in Kerala is more than twice the national average (8.4% vs. 3.6%) [3].

Early triaging of severely ill PwTB, followed by prioritization of inpatient care, effective chemotherapy, and comorbidity management, is crucial in reducing TB mortality [4, 5].

India’s national TB elimination programme (NTEP) in January 2021 recommended severity assessment for all notified PwTB at diagnosis and referral of severely ill patients for inpatient care [6]. Most of the TB deaths are early deaths (within two months), and half of the early deaths happen within the first two weeks of diagnosis, this mandates a quick triaging for severe illness at the time of diagnosis [7, 8]. Since April 2022, a differentiated TB care strategy has been successfully implemented in routine programme settings in the neighbouring state of Tamil Nadu (TN-KET) [9]. TN-KET uses a triage tool with screening for BMI, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, and ability to stand without support, and if triage positive, ensuring referral to inpatient care and follow-up. TN-KET has also set district-level targets, which are explicitly monitored at the state level [10].

Currently, the PwTB is screened for the presence or absence of diabetes mellitus (DM), alcohol use, and tobacco use. In Kerala, the quality of pre-treatment evaluation and clinical follow-up is ensured using a biweekly clinical follow-up up which is attached to the treatment cards of all PwTB. However, the referral under this system is exclusively based on the clinician’s discretion. Further, this system also lacks a monitoring mechanism at higher levels. TB deaths are often influenced by a confluence of factors that span multiple health system building blocks - delays in service delivery, unaddressed comorbidities, weak referral pathways, limited inpatient care linkage, and inadequate integration with social protection mechanisms. Addressing these interrelated issues demands a health systems approach grounded in the design and delivery of complex interventions that bring together multiple components across clinical care, public health, and community support. While Kerala has ongoing strategies to enhance early detection, including active case finding and vulnerability mapping, our implementation project focuses specifically on post-diagnosis interventions. Accordingly, a Comprehensive Care Package (CCp-K) was developed by the research team to address severe illness, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, alcohol dependence, and nicotine dependence, contextualized to the high burden of non-communicable diseases and associated risk factors in Kerala.

Though the differentiated TB care has been emphasised since 2021, only very few states have initiated it. Further, considering the contextual differences in health-seeking behaviour and access to the public health care system, involvement of private partnership, the implementation feasibility and effectiveness need to be established in the state of Kerala. Hence, we aimed to assess the situation and systematically explore the barriers, facilitators, and perceived suggestions in the design and implementation of the CCp-K from various interest holders for sustainable implementation.

Methods

Study design

This was a descriptive qualitative study utilising framework-guided thematic analysis. This study adhered to the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) guidelines for reporting [11] (Supplementary appendix 1).

Study duration

The study was conducted between October 2024 and January 2025 as part of the pre-implementation phase of the CCp-K model in Kerala.

Study setting

We conducted our study in Kerala, the southernmost state of India. Kerala ranks top in the human development index, gender development index, and is a consistent performer in NITI Aayog’s Sustainable Development Goals Index and Multidimensional Poverty Index. Kerala’s local self-governance model, a three-tier system of Panchayat Raj Institutions and Urban Local Bodies, is a widely recognized example of decentralized governance, empowering local bodies with resources and administrative powers to implement development programs at the grassroots level. The state had a presumptive TB case examination of 1453 per 100,000 population in 2023. 34% of tested PwTB were diagnosed with diabetes mellitus, 16% had tobacco usage, and 16% had alcohol use among those screened. Among the cohort of PwTB who started treatment in 2022 in Kerala, 16,772 (82.7%) had treatment success, and 1686 (8.3%) died [3].

The state NTEP infrastructure includes 14 NTEP districts. Each sub-district level unit has a senior TB treatment supervisor, a Senior Treatment Laboratory Supervisor & Medical Officer for TB Control. The programme in each district is supervised by the District TB Officer (DTO) and the programme management unit at the District TB centre (DTC). Every district has a dedicated pulmonologist at the DTC. There are other dedicated key staff, namely TB-health visitors in urban areas and drug drug-resistant TB coordinator. Currently, PwTB and DM are managed according to the Non-Communicable Diseases (NCD)-TB collaborative guidelines by the Central TB Division (CTD). There is at least one tobacco cessation clinic and deaddiction centre (“Vimukthi” meaning liberation in the Malayalam language) at the district headquarters. PwTB requiring admission are usually admitted at medical colleges or dedicated respiratory disease hospitals available in a few districts only.

The comprehensive care Package-Kerala (CCp-K) model

The project team has developed a CCp-K model that included a set of parameters for triaging severe illness at diagnosis for all PwTB (Body mass index (BMI) less than or equal to (≤) 14.0 kg/m2, BMI 14.1 to 16.0 kg/m2 with leg swelling, respiratory rate more than (>) 24 per minute, oxygen saturation less than (<) 94%, not able to stand without support), screening for diabetes mellitus (DM) and inpatient care for uncontrolled DM (FBG ≥ 250 mg/dl), assessing for nicotine dependence using heaviness of smoking index and alcohol dependence using CAGE questionnaire and referring to tobacco cessation centres and “Vimukthi” de-addiction centres. The scope of the CCp-K pathway is shown in Fig. 1. The triage tool was developed through consultative workshops involving pulmonologists, programme officers, and public health experts, and aligned with the operational guidelines available in the NTEP differentiated TB care guidelines. Study population

We conducted a mapping to identify the key interest holders for the CCp-K project. Interest holders were classified into the four quadrants of Mendelow’s Power- Interest matrix by assessing their operational authority (power) and level of engagement or influence in shaping new care models (interest) [13]. We use the term “interest holders” in place of “stakeholders” to align with recent scholarship that critiques the colonial connotations of the latter and recommends more inclusive language in global health research [14]. Interest holder classification was conducted through structured consultation meetings involving the research team and senior program staff from the State TB Cell. For each potential participant, their level of power was assessed based on formal programmatic authority (e.g., ability to allocate resources, issue directives), and interest was determined by their degree of involvement in decision-making, implementation, or advocacy for new TB care models. Interest holders were then categorized into the matrix quadrants through consensus. We acknowledge that such classification is dynamic, as the level of power or interest may vary depending on the individual occupying a post, their engagement with the issue, and contextual organisational or political factors. The mapping therefore reflected the current realities of the TB control ecosystem in Kerala at the time of the study. We ensured diversity across roles (e.g., policymakers, program managers, clinicians) and levels of influence (state vs. district) to capture a comprehensive range of perspectives.

Data collection

The interviews were conducted after several meetings with the interest holders to explain the scope of the CCp-K model. We conducted one-to-one in-depth interviews with key interest holders by trained interviewers (MJV, AV, AKR, JV, RRS, SM) using an interview guide developed for the study (Supplementary appendix 2). Of the six interviewers, four were female and two were male. MJV is a medical officer with postgraduate qualifications (MD) in public health. Interviewers AV, AKR, JV, RRE, and SM are from academia with postgraduate qualifications (MD) in public health and previous experience in the TB programme. The interview guide included open-ended questions based on the review of literature and inputs from the team of experts who had experience in implementing health programmes, especially in the context of TB. We conducted interviews both in person and online through the Zoom application. The interviews were conducted in a private, comfortable room in the interviewee’s office or their home. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, assessed separately within the high-power-high-interest and high-power-low-interest groups. Saturation was determined through an iterative process in which the coding team monitored the emergence of new codes and themes after each interview. Once two or more consecutive interviews within a subgroup yielded no new substantive insights, saturation was considered achieved for that group. With the consent from interest holders, the interviews were audio recorded and kept in password-protected computers accessible only to the Principal Investigator (RSV) and Co-Investigators (KSS, DN, HDS). The transcripts were sent back to the participants for validation.

Data analysis

The audio recordings were transcribed and translated into English within three days of the interview. The analysis of transcripts was done simultaneously, and these initial transcripts guided further prompts in the interview guide. The interview transcripts were coded and thematically analysed using Taguette qualitative data analysis tool [15]. We followed a five step framework analysis approach to analyse the transcripts made from interviews namely (1) data immersion (iterative reading by the independent trained coders (RSV, KSS)) (2) identifying the thematic framework (in line with the issues specified under research question/objective a priori identification of domains) (3) coding (relevant segment aligned to objectives were coded using the a priori term which can describe best) (4) synthesis (organising similar codes across different interest holders) (5) identification of pattern and interpretation (concepts and issues emerged across different interest holders and linkage across different domains). The coding discrepancies were arbitrated by a senior third coder (DN) and resolved through consensus.

Researcher reflexivity

To enhance reflexivity and minimize potential biases arising from some researchers’ affiliation with the health system, the following strategies were employed: (1) Interviews were conducted by trained external qualitative researchers who were not involved in the design or implementation of the CCp-K model (2) Interviews were guided using open-ended, non-leading prompts to elicit unfiltered responses from participants (3) Transcripts were prepared using word-for-word verbatim, including fillers, pauses, and emotional reactions, rather than using edited or paraphrased content, to preserve the authenticity of participants’ narratives (4) Except for one researcher, all coders were independent of the data collection process and not affiliated with the health system, reducing the risk of interpretive bias (5) Coding discrepancies were resolved through collaborative discussions to reach consensus (6) Regular team debriefings were conducted to reflect on researcher positionality, surface implicit assumptions, and ensure analytic transparency. These steps helped ensure the trustworthiness and credibility of the qualitative findings.

Perceptions of deaths due to TB in Kerala

Burden of TB deaths in Kerala

Service providers in Kerala expressed concern over the rising TB case fatality ratio, which increased from 5 to 9%, surpassing the national average. They attributed this to improved reporting and a broader definition of TB deaths, including unrelated causes, as noted by a senior pulmonologist.

“But in the last 2–3 years, the reported mortality in Kerala has been around 8%, which is almost the highest in India. So, it’s a very disheartening thing for people who have worked in TB for such a long time that mortality has gone up to this extent, and to our understanding, nothing much has been done for that. Also, mortality is something that is not discussed or addressed in Kerala.”

“But in the last 2–3 years, the reported mortality in Kerala has been around 8%, which is almost the highest in India. So, it’s a very disheartening thing for people who have worked in TB for such a long time that mortality has gone up to this extent, and to our understanding, nothing much has been done for that. Also, mortality is something that is not discussed or addressed in Kerala.”

Perceived reasons for TB deaths

Interest holders attributed TB deaths to delayed diagnosis, comorbidities (DM, CKD, COPD), substance use, poor adherence, adverse drug reactions, stigma, and inadequate follow-up. Economic hardship, lack of social support, and gaps in preventive care (absence of government-provided pneumococcal and influenza vaccination) further compounded risks, with vulnerable groups like tribal populations, migrants, and destitute individuals facing higher mortality due to poor access and nutrition.

Perception of the relevance of a differentiated care model (CCp-K) in Kerala

The service providers perceived that this model of early triaging at the time of TB diagnosis to assess the patient’s risk of death and initiate appropriate care at the nearest facility could significantly reduce mortality and post-TB sequelae.

High burden of NCDs and risk factors

The interest holders recognized Kerala as India’s non-communicable disease capital, with a very high prevalence of DM, CKD, CLD, COPD, which are contributors to poor TB treatment outcomes, as shared by a pulmonologist.

“Yeah, differentiated TB care is the need of the hour because, as I already mentioned, TB deaths it’s mainly related to comorbidities.”

“Yeah, differentiated TB care is the need of the hour because, as I already mentioned, TB deaths it’s mainly related to comorbidities.”

They also acknowledged that the use of alcohol and tobacco can lead to liver and lung injury and may also result in treatment interruptions and deaths.

Gaps in the existing programme

Interviewees noted that while Kerala’s TB program includes pre-treatment checkups and fortnightly reviews, better integration is needed to manage TB with comorbidities, especially alcohol and nicotine dependence.

Value addition

The interviewees opined that the proposed model aligns well with Kerala’s existing NTEP framework, particularly in enhancing the management of diabetes, tobacco use, and alcohol consumption. Hence, they felt the proposed model CCp-K as a value addition to achieve the target without straining the health system resources to a larger extent.

“Because, as I already told you, the systems are in place; they need to be structured only. So I am hopeful that if implemented, this model will be a revolutionary thing in reducing the mortality related to tuberculosis.”

“Because, as I already told you, the systems are in place; they need to be structured only. So I am hopeful that if implemented, this model will be a revolutionary thing in reducing the mortality related to tuberculosis.”

Perception of the feasibility and enablers for the CCp-K model

The interest holders perceived the CCp-K model to be feasible. Kerala has a robust primary health system with well-connected public and private sectors, with a previous history of successfully implementing various models for diseases and disasters, as explained by a senior programme officerIn Kerala itself, one of the first publications regarding the role of diabetes in tuberculosis and the need for addressing it was there. Kerala has also done the vulnerability mapping very early. One of the first models regarding the role of nutritional supplementation in control of tuberculosis, especially in MDR tuberculosis, has also been done from Kerala.

In Kerala itself, one of the first publications regarding the role of diabetes in tuberculosis and the need for addressing it was there. Kerala has also done the vulnerability mapping very early. One of the first models regarding the role of nutritional supplementation in control of tuberculosis, especially in MDR tuberculosis, has also been done from Kerala.

Healthcare infrastructure

Interest holders noted that Kerala’s strong public health network and robust private sector can support CCp-K. The existing STEPS (System for TB elimination in the private sector) program, which is organized as a single window for notification, linkage for public health actions, and treatment adherence support in private hospitals by hospital consortia and NGO support, can also be leveraged for CCp-K.

Health workforce

Interest holders noted that NTEP staff in Kerala consistently demonstrate a patient-centered, non-stigmatizing approach. This is supported by a well-established public health workforce, adequate doctor-patient ratio, specialist availability at CHCs and above, and effective community outreach through ASHAs.

Existing NCD/COPD clinics

The interest holders highlighted the importance of well-established NCD and COPD (SWAAS) clinics from the health sub-centre level onwards, with strong bidirectional screening for TB and comorbidities. They considered the availability of tobacco cessation services at district tobacco cessation centres and primary care level as part of the “SWAAS” programme, including nicotine gums and buprenorphine as a major enabler for cessation services.

We have very good screening happening at the grassroots level in the NCD clinics. We have given the 4-symptom seal to all the NCD clinics. Whenever an NCD patient comes for follow-ups, they ask these 4 symptoms complex and put the seal on the book. If the patient is having symptoms, they will send the sputum for testing.

Whenever an NCD patient comes for follow-ups, they ask these 4 symptoms complex and put the seal on the book. If the patient is having symptoms, they will send the sputum for testing.

Local self-government and community involvement

The interest holders opined that in Kerala, high literacy, patient health-seeking behavior, and strong community involvement will create a favorable environment to implement the CCp-K model. The local governments in Kerala are already implementing various projects for nutrition support, alongside programmatic solutions that address TB care, including free diagnostics.

Cross adaptation

Interest holders highlighted Kerala’s past success with initiatives like palliative care and CKD management, suggesting these experiences can inform the adaptation of the CCp-K model.

Perception of barriers and challenges to implementing CCp-K

Inadequate referral facility/nodal treatment centre

All the interest holders were concerned regarding the non-availability of a referral facility for inpatient care. They opined that Kerala has inadequate inpatient infrastructure for TB care, with only four districts having dedicated TB admission facilities. The decline in IP care facilities, partly driven by the shift to domiciliary care and perceived TB elimination progress, has resulted in reduced TB ward availability, impacting timely care for severely ill patients. Limited TB beds, particularly in taluk and district hospitals, and the absence of dedicated centres contribute to delays, confusion, and patient deaths during transfers, as shared by a senior pulmonologist.

Some medical colleges also do not have a TB Ward. The Taluk hospital referred to the medical college, saying we don’t have a TB ward. So why would you refer to a place that does not have a TB ward? They sent the patient back, and the patient died in the ambulance. We have had such shocking incidents in Kerala as well.

Some medical colleges also do not have a TB Ward. The Taluk hospital referred to the medical college, saying we don’t have a TB ward. So why would you refer to a place that does not have a TB ward? They sent the patient back, and the patient died in the ambulance. We have had such shocking incidents in Kerala as well.

Also, they raised a concern that the existing TB hospitals do not have adequate infrastructure to provide care for severe TB. Additionally, they felt that challenges in infection control, managing bystanders, and rural patient referrals would further strain the system.

Competing priorities

Interviewees expressed concern that competing programmatic priorities, especially from other communicable and non-communicable diseases, have reduced TB’s focus and overwhelmed frontline workers like multipurpose health workers and ASHAs.

Fear of transmission among healthcare workers

The interest holders voiced that the fear of TB transmission among healthcare providers is causing reluctance to admit or manage PwTB. They shared that de-addiction clinics often refuse to admit infectious patients, delaying care, and when they are admitted to crowded care homes, increasing transmission risks, as detailed by a pulmonologist.

Stigma is still there about tuberculosis, even among the staff. One patient, I referred to a psychiatrist, and then they (the staff) just sent him back, saying that it is an open case. Either I will go, or the patient should go.

Stigma is still there about tuberculosis, even among the staff. One patient, I referred to a psychiatrist, and then they (the staff) just sent him back, saying that it is an open case. Either I will go, or the patient should go.

Social vulnerability

The interest holders opined that PwTB, without caretakers, often face neglect, especially destitutes and elderly individuals, with families unwilling to support them due to past grievances or busy schedules. They also highlighted the difficulties in following up PwTB going abroad and other guest workers coming to Kerala.

Geographical and financial access to services under CCp-K

Interest holders explained how the distance and narrow scope for TB services under health insurance can act as significant barriers. Lack of beds in the closest facility to the patient, especially in remote and rural areas, leads to delayed referrals due to travel challenges of 70–80 km and the need for a bystander at the referred facility. They were also concerned about the fact that TB management is not fully covered by insurance, such as OPD-based care and ancillary support like oxygen and nebulizers for home-treated PwTB.

Patient-related factors

Interviewees noted that persistent TB stigma leads to test refusal, while delays in financial support, exacerbated by difficulties in opening bank accounts, impede care. Gaps in IEC efforts result in limited public understanding of the importance of TB treatment.

Programme priority

Interest holders perceived reduced top-level prioritization of TB, with repeated strategies lacking evaluation and weakened oversight following the discontinuation of TU-level performance reporting and detailed mortality reviews. Poor monitoring of comorbidity tracking and neglect of TB death analyses in district reviews have further limited accountability, as noted by a state program manager.

I would say that the TB programme is still a low priority in the state, because if you see the minutes of the state meeting, I’m not criticizing, but the TB program is run by an additional director, one of the 14 additional directors in the state. But this program is reviewed after multiple small programs, which have been supervised by deputy directors. So, what I personally feel is that this program lacks attention at the highest level.

I would say that the TB programme is still a low priority in the state, because if you see the minutes of the state meeting, I’m not criticizing, but the TB program is run by an additional director, one of the 14 additional directors in the state. But this program is reviewed after multiple small programs, which have been supervised by deputy directors. So, what I personally feel is that this program lacks attention at the highest level.

Health workforce

The interest holders perceived that frequent transfers and promotions of DTOs (District Tuberculosis Officers) are disrupting the TB program, as they need at least two years to effectively learn and manage the program. There is perceived overburdening by the existing HCWs due to existing vacancies and multiple parallel programmes. They were also doubtful whether overburdened OPDs at peripheral health centres with limited consultation time may affect implementing CCp-K.

Competence of HCW

The interest holders acknowledged that HCWs are identifying comorbidities, but technical issues and a lack of adequate knowledge about using the Ni-kshay system hinder proper notification and data entry. Proper tobacco care requires both pharmacological and non-pharmacological management, but the HCW is currently not equipped to handle this effectively. Many doctors, especially at the periphery, are unaware of the recent TB guidelines and management strategies, which hinder proper patient care.

Infrastructure gaps

The interest holders identified that the limited availability of inpatient beds for PwTB is complicated by the occasional supply chain issues in insulin and other ancillary drugs availability. Most of the districts lack a well-established tobacco cessation centre and deaddiction centres with adequately trained staff, and professionals like psychologists, psychiatrists, and paramedics are lacking.

Perceived suggestions for implementing the model

Infrastructure strengthening

The interest holders suggested that creating a centre of excellence for TB clinical care at each district with ICUs and airborne infection control practices alongside strengthening peripheral health systems, can enhance referral pathways and ensure PwTB receive specialized care at dedicated centers of excellence. As detailed by the state-level programme manager.

All PwTB who are coming from the districts need at least one apex center that can cater to all their difficulties. So, if we can come up with a very robust district nodal center for tuberculosis in every district, that is going to be a game changer.

All PwTB who are coming from the districts need at least one apex center that can cater to all their difficulties. So, if we can come up with a very robust district nodal center for tuberculosis in every district, that is going to be a game changer.

They opined that collaboration of the private sector through MOUs can facilitate infrastructure sharing with the public sector, enhancing overall TB care. While resources are available, the primary challenge lies in ensuring adequate bed capacity at nodal centers. To manage this, they suggested that establishing two or three decentralized nodal centers per district, based on case load and available resources, such as one in a medical college, one in a district hospital, and potentially another in a strategic location, would help address this gap effectively. They mentioned that strengthening existing de-addiction centres and tobacco cessation centres by manpower pooling and availing adequate drugs and consumables is important.

Private sector engagement

They suggested that the private sector can significantly contribute by ensuring optimal treatment of comorbidities using newer therapies, which can improve disease control and reduce TB-related mortality. They indicated the need for modification in the CCp-K framework to suit the private sector’s capabilities, allowing them to use public facilities only when necessary while still supporting the core framework.

Local self-government and community engagement

The interest holders perceived the key role of local government institutions and “Kudumbashree” (self-help groups in Kerala) in early case detection, nutrition and pension support, identification of sick PwTB, mobility support, and bringing back lost to follow-up PwTB into the system. NGO networks can play a vital role in supporting TB care by enhancing outreach, providing resources, and strengthening patient support systems. The intersectoral coordination with various departments is said to be vital in implementing the CCp-K model. They also opined that the CCp-K framework may be modified to address specific issues of destitutes and migrants.

Leveraging technology

They suggested that utilizing telemedicine services such as “eSanjeevani” for patient follow-up can relieve the pressure on nodal treatment centres. Many interest holders suggested that strengthening the monitoring system through a digital solution, such as a dashboard, would allow real-time tracking of patient progress and improve follow-up across all levels of the TB program.

Training and capacity building

The interviewees emphasized timely training for PHC staff on triaging, clinical assessment, and documentation, suggesting the use of existing TB program e-learning platforms. They also recommended improved IEC strategies to raise awareness in the community about severe illness requiring admission.

Workflow/Standard Operating Procedures (SOP)

Most of the participants suggested that the practical implementation of the triage system, especially at the field level, requires clear responsibility allocation with a micro-plan/SOP giving the responsibilities to each staff specified at each level of health facilities to ensure effective execution.

Discussion

In the pre-implementation phase of a structured, complex intervention aimed at reducing TB deaths in Kerala, we engaged a diverse range of interest holders to understand the feasibility and acceptability of the CCp-K model. The interest holders acknowledged the model to be relevant and feasible, considering the robust health infrastructure, health literacy, and community involvement in Kerala. However, barriers in terms of lack of designated TB care facilities, human resources, and linkage with specialized centres for de-addiction and tobacco cessation were identified.

The role of early diagnosis is undeniably important in reducing TB mortality; however, our study focused on the equally critical domain of care after diagnosis. Interest holders in our study highlighted that, despite a well-structured program, a high proportion of deaths occur even after patients are diagnosed and initiated on treatment, highlighting a need for more intensive inpatient and comorbidity-focused care. We also documented interest holder concerns regarding delayed diagnosis, especially among vulnerable populations, which are addressed through separate public health initiatives in Kerala.

High prevalence of comorbidities and risk factors, older age, social vulnerabilities, and poor follow-up were highlighted as main causes of TB deaths by the interest holders, which is similar to many studies conducted in India earlier [7, 16–18]. Considering the paradoxical nature of high risk for acquiring TB among disadvantaged populations (street dwellers, destitutes, and the elderly) with problems related to treatment adherence, substance use, and comorbidities makes this group vulnerable to developing adverse TB treatment outcomes [19]. Though actions should be implemented to reduce the case fatality, it shows the robust reporting system of Kerala compared to other parts of India, which is a key indicator for programme management. Another study conducted in Kerala using verbal autopsies identified two two-thirds of the causes of death were diseases other than TB, which calls for prompt management of comorbidities associated with TB [20]. In addition to biomedical factors, the social vulnerabilities influencing the TB deaths as reported in this study and previous literature emphasize the need for integrating a socio-cultural model when such differentiated TB care is planned [21].

The high burden of NCDs and risk factors in Kerala makes it ideal to implement the differentiated care model [22–26]. The already well-established public health care system, complemented by the private sector [27], existing STEPS programme [28], completed vulnerability mapping, and supportive local governments driven by the Kerala TB elimination mission can boost the CCp-K [29, 30]. LSGs in Kerala have a track record of supporting health initiatives through flexible resource allocation and community-based welfare mechanisms [31, 32]. In the context of CCp-K, they can support transport, nutrition, coordination with healthcare staff for timely referral and follow-up, facilitation of access to welfare schemes, and community engagement to reduce stigma and promote adherence. However, variation in engagement and capacity across different LSGs may influence implementation fidelity and will need to be addressed during scaling up.

Though gaps exist in the programme [33], Kerala can cross-adapt from its various health sector initiatives, such as the palliative care programme, diabetic retinopathy screening programme, etc., to implement programmes to reduce TB deaths [34–38]. The pre-existing “SWAAS” clinic for COPD [39] and NCD clinics can be incorporated into the CCp-K model without additional resources.

The major barrier to implementing the CCp-K model will be the availability of a nodal treatment centre with ICU and specialist services. Strengthening existing Sub-district and District hospitals with dedicated earmarked beds for managing severely ill PwTB, pooling specialists and other HCWs available at the district, and establishing referral linkages through the primary care system can address the issues until full-fledged NTCs are established. Utilization of existing isolation beds in the infectious disease department of various hospitals may be considered before the establishment of NTCs. Till the designated facilities for severe TB care become operational from the public health sector, the larger buy-in from the private sector, and mission hospitals may catalyze the process of implementation. However, engagement private sector involves several challenges [40]. The role of existing platforms such as STEPS needs to be explored to meet these needs. To overcome the nonavailability of specialists at all levels, telemedicine has been suggested as a potential strategy to seek support. The role of telemedicine in fulfilling the specialist role needs to be explored during the pilot phase of the CCp-K model.

HCW stigma toward PwTB can notably lower the quality of care, leading to poor treatment outcomes [41]. A recent scoping review has identified fear as a major stigma driver and lack of infection control as a major stigma facilitator among HCWs [42]. Ensuring adequate infection control practice in TB treatment centres and addressing the fear of HCWs can reduce the stigma. Another study identified that adherence is profoundly influenced by how individual healthcare providers interact with patients on treatment and address their needs [43]. The healthcare providers should be competent in understanding these influences on patients undergoing treatment. To address the social vulnerabilities identified during the inquiries, a pro-poor model of patient-centered care and prevention with nutritional, financial, and psycho-social support, as suggested by Bhargava et al., can be incorporated into the CCp-K to address gaps in universal health coverage and social protection [44].

Emerging evidence underscores the importance of differentiated TB care, particularly in high-burden settings. Previous evidence from complex TB interventions in South Africa has highlighted the value of early triaging and inpatient care for patients with severe forms of TB or comorbidities [45]. Previous studies conducted in South India found that differentiated TB care was both feasible and acceptable, provided that healthcare workers received adequate training and upskilling [46, 47]. The integration of comorbidity management, such as diabetes and alcohol use disorders, aligns with global calls for person-centred TB care under the WHO End TB Strategy [48].

The CCp-K model exemplifies a complex intervention in health services, as it integrates several interacting components, including clinical triaging, comorbidity management, intersectoral coordination, and community engagement. A key learning from our study is that even in a well-performing health system like Kerala’s, implementing differentiated TB care poses significant operational and structural challenges. The findings highlight the importance of role clarity, streamlined referral pathways, interdepartmental coordination, and data integration to support complex care models. The CCp-K model, although developed in the Kerala context, provides a flexible framework that can be adapted to other settings based on their existing infrastructure, resource availability, and burden of comorbidities. Successful replication will depend on the extent to which local health systems can support inpatient care, integrate services across vertical programs, and provide support for patients with social vulnerabilities.

Strengths and limitations

The in-depth interviews were conducted by trained researchers who are familiar with the health system of Kerala. This helped to develop better prompts in the interview guide. However, our study had certain limitations. The current study didn’t capture the perceptions of interest holders who will use the model in the field, such as peripheral medical officers, staff nurses, and field workers. For this group, we felt it would be challenging to hypothetically ask their views about a ‘yet-to-be’ implemented model and get their opinion. We also acknowledge that the perspectives of persons with TB and families of those who have died from TB were not included in this phase. As this study was focused on assessing the feasibility, relevance, and system-level preparedness for CCp-K, we engaged health system actors and programme managers as the primary interest holders. However, during the pilot, we plan to include their perceptions to explore the actual implementation challenges. Lastly, although our model does not directly address delayed diagnosis, which remains a key contributor to TB mortality, it complements Kerala’s existing diagnostic strategies by targeting improved outcomes in the immediate post-diagnosis phase, a critical period during which most TB deaths occur. Finally, while the findings are likely applicable to high-performing health systems such as Kerala, their generalizability to lower-resourced settings may require further contextualization and adaptation based on local infrastructure, workforce capacity, and health system organization.

Policy and practice implications

The inputs from these interviews will help to translate the prototype CCp-K model and guide its initiation in Kerala. The CCp-K model provides a structured approach to enhancing TB care through risk-based triaging, inpatient management, and continuous follow-up. Other states in India and countries with high TB burden can customize this model based on local epidemiology, health system capacity, and socio-cultural contexts. By integrating lessons from this study, policymakers can design sustainable, patient-centered TB care strategies that align with global TB elimination goals.

Conclusion

During the pre-implementation phase of the CCp-K, we engaged the interest holders and explored their perceptions on TB deaths and the feasibility of implementing the model in Kerala. The interest holders perceived the model to be relevant and feasible. However, Kerala should overcome the barriers of inadequate inpatient referral centers by strengthening existing facilities and leveraging collaboration with the private sector. Based on the insights generated, the CCp-K model is now being prepared for pilot implementation in selected districts, where its feasibility, acceptability, and fidelity will be evaluated under routine programmatic settings. The model will be integrated into existing NTEP workflows, leveraging established mechanisms such as district TB coordination committees and digital reporting platforms. The model can offer a scalable approach for embedding TB care within broader chronic disease management frameworks, promoting convergence across vertical programmes.
