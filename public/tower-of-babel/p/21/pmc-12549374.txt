Comparative Evaluation of Biocompatibility between Conventional and Computer-Aided Design/Computer-Aided Manufacturing Dental Polymers Using a Golden Hamster Model
Authors: Xia Wei, Huakun Shangguan, Ling Wu, Linjuan Gao, Yu Pan, Yiling Cheng, Hui Cheng, Yinghui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsComputer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) dental polymers exhibit superior mechanical properties compared to conventional ones, but their biocompatibility remains controversial. This study aimed to systematically compare in vivo biocompatibility of conventional and CAD/CAM dental polymers using a golden hamster model.MethodsSix dental polymers were evaluated: conventional (VAR) and CAD/CAM (OP) polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) denture base materials; conventional bis-acrylic (PT) and CAD/CAM PMMA (DM) interim restorative materials; pressed (PB) and CAD/CAM (CB) polyetheretherketone (PEEK) prosthetic framework materials, with polypropylene as control. Disk specimens (n = 6) were sutured in golden hamster buccal pouches for 14- and 28-day mucosal irritation tests. Systemic and local biocompatibility was assessed through hepatic/renal function tests, histopathology (HE staining), apoptosis detection (TUNEL), and apoptosis-related molecular analysis (RT-qPCR and Western blot) of buccal pouch mucosa, liver, and kidney tissues.ResultsAll groups showed normal physiological conditions throughout the study period. Histological examination revealed normal tissue morphology in all tested organs, with only mild or no mucosal irritation observed. Transient fluctuations were observed in ALB, A/G, BUN and TP at 14 days, but the differences between groups tended to stabilize at 28 days. The results of TUNEL and RT-qPCR demonstrated comparable results across all groups. The expression of Bax and Bcl-2 proteins in buccal pouch mucosa of VAR group were elevated at 28 days compared to 14 days, while only Bax protein was elevated in PB group (P < .05). The expression level of pro Caspase-3 protein in the liver tissue of VAR group and PT group decreased at 28 days (P < .05).ConclusionsBoth conventional and CAD/CAM dental polymers exhibited acceptable in vivo biocompatibility, but CAD/CAM dental polymers showed superior temporal stability.Clinical RelevanceThe superior temporal stability in the in vivo biocompatibility profile of CAD/CAM dental polymers supports their clinical preference for long-term prosthodontic applications.Key words: CAD/CAM, Dental polymers, In vivo, Biocompatibility, Apoptosis

Introduction

Polymers have become indispensable in modern dental practice, serving as denture bases, interim restorations, and prosthetic frameworks. Among these materials, polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA), composite resins, and polyetheretherketone (PEEK) are the most widely employed.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Recent advancements in computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technologies have revolutionized dental polymer fabrication.6,7 Compared to conventional methods, CAD/CAM dental polymers exhibit superior mechanical stability8 and enhanced dimensional accuracy,9 driving their widespread adoption in prosthodontic applications.10,11

Despite these technological advantages, the clinical deployment of dental polymers remains contingent upon rigorous biosafety evaluation.12 A critical concern involves the leaching of processing residuals, particularly unreacted monomers and polymerization by-products, which have demonstrated dose-dependent cytotoxicity in oral tissues.13, 14, 15 Several studies have demonstrated that both conventional and CAD/CAM dental polymers exhibit comparable levels of residual monomers and show no significant differences in terms of cell proliferation capacity.16, 17, 18 However, Our prior in vitro studies have confirmed that CAD/CAM dental polymers exhibit superior cytocompatibility compared to conventional materials.19,20 This superior cytocompatibility is likely due to the high-temperature and high-pressure conditions during the CAD/CAM processing, which enhance monomer conversion rates and thereby reduce the risk of leaching of residual substances.21 Therefore, comparative studies on the biocompatibility of conventional and CAD/CAM dental polymers remain controversial.

Furthermore, existing studies are exclusively based on in vitro experiments, and no in vivo studies have been published yet. Consequently, this leads to some major experimental limitations: one is in vitro models fail to recapitulate features of the oral physiological environment, including masticatory force, enzymatic degradation, and immune responses.22, 23, 24, 25 Moreover, the in vitro models lack the ability of in vivo models to comprehensively assess both local tissue responses and systemic biological effects.26 Importantly, there are no standardized in vivo assessments have been conducted to compare these material systems under clinically relevant conditions. To address these gaps, this study aimed to employ the golden hamster buccal pouch model for systematic evaluation of histomorphological changes in direct-contact tissues, and apoptotic markers in both local (oral) and distant (hepatic/renal) organs, based on established methodologies from previous literature.27 The findings of this study will contribute valuable insights to the unresolved biocompatibility discussion surrounding conventional versus CAD/CAM dental polymers, with practical implications for clinical material selection.

The null hypotheses were tested: (1) there was no difference in hepatic/renal function tests, apoptosis detection (TUNEL), and apoptosis-related molecular analysis (RT-qPCR and Western blot) between conventional and CAD/CAM dental polymers; (2) temporal factors would not significantly influence the aforementioned biological parameters.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation

Disk-shaped specimens with standardized dimensions (5.0 mm diameter × 0.5 mm thickness) were fabricated for this study. According to ISO 10993-12: 2012,28 polypropylene was selected as the control material (CON), while six different dental polymers were evaluated for comparison (n = 6). Six dental polymers were evaluated in this study:

1)conventional compression-moulding PMMA denture base material (Vertex Acrylic Resin, Vertex, Netherlands; VAR),

conventional compression-moulding PMMA denture base material (Vertex Acrylic Resin, Vertex, Netherlands; VAR),

2)CAD/CAM PMMA denture base material (Organic PMMA eco Pink, Organical CAD/CAM GmbH, Germany; OP),

CAD/CAM PMMA denture base material (Organic PMMA eco Pink, Organical CAD/CAM GmbH, Germany; OP),

3)conventional bis-acrylic composite interim restorative material (Protemp™ 4, 3M ESPE, Germany; PT),

conventional bis-acrylic composite interim restorative material (Protemp™ 4, 3M ESPE, Germany; PT),

4)CAD/CAM PMMA interim restorative material (Die Material, Aidite Technology Co., Ltd, China; DM),

CAD/CAM PMMA interim restorative material (Die Material, Aidite Technology Co., Ltd, China; DM),

5)pressed PEEK prosthetic framework material (BioHPP, Bredent GmbH, Germany; PB),

pressed PEEK prosthetic framework material (BioHPP, Bredent GmbH, Germany; PB),

6)CAD/CAM PEEK prosthetic framework material (breCAM.BioHPP, Bredent GmbH, Germany; CB).

CAD/CAM PEEK prosthetic framework material (breCAM.BioHPP, Bredent GmbH, Germany; CB).

The detailed fabrication procedure of the specimens has been thoroughly described in our previous studies.1,19,20 Following fabrication, all specimens underwent sequential wet polishing with 500- and 1200-grit SiC sandpapers. Subsequently, all specimens underwent ultrasonic cleaning in deionized water and ethylene oxide gas sterilization.

Construction of the golden hamster model

The experimental protocols were performed in strict accordance with the animal welfare guidelines authorized by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (Approval No.: IACUC FJMU 2023-0021). Following our previously established experimental protocol,27 we maintained a sample size of 6 golden hamsters per group. This study employed a two-phase design with 14- and 28-day observation periods, comprising 7 groups per time point (n = 6), totalling 84 SPF-grade male golden hamsters (49∼55 days old, 120 ± 5 g). Following a one-week adaptation period under ISO 10993-2: 2006,29 the hamsters were received intraperitoneal injections of sodium pentobarbital (2% solution, 40 mg/kg) to achieve surgical anaesthesia. Six dental polymers and control material were surgically sutured into the buccal pouch according to our previous study.27 All experimental and control animals were housed in standardized laboratory environments with routine monitoring of body weight parameters.26 Upon completion of the experiments, the hamsters were humanely euthanized under anaesthesia (Figure 1A, B).

(B) The specimens were sutured to the buccal pouch mucosa of golden hamster. (C) The weight growth of the golden hamsters after 14- and 28-day of specimen stimulation.

Collection of blood, buccal pouch mucosa, liver and kidney tissues

Following humane euthanasia of the golden hamsters, blood was collected from the medial canthus vein of each group to obtain approximately 200 µL of serum, which was subsequently stored at −20 °C. The buccal pouch mucosa in contact with the sutured specimens was carefully excised after the removal of the specimens and sutures. The collected mucosal tissue was processed as follows: 1) immediate fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, and 2) cryopreservation at −80 °C. Following the same protocol, liver and kidney tissues were similarly divided into two aliquots for either fixation or frozen storage.

Hepatic and renal function tests

Serum biochemistry profiling was performed using a Roche Modular P800 automated analyser (Roche, USA) to evaluate hepatic and renal function through quantification of: aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum creatinine (CREA).

H&E staining

The fixed buccal pouch mucosa, liver, and kidney tissues were subjected to dehydration through a graded alcohol series, followed by clearing, paraffin embedding, and sectioning. Following standard protocols, tissue sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Solarbio, China) for comprehensive histological evaluation of potential pathological alterations.

TUNEL assay

Tissue sections from buccal pouch mucosa, liver, and kidney tissue sections were processed for apoptosis detection using the Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labelling (TUNEL) kit (Elabscience Biotechnology, China). Subsequently, the sections were observed, photographed, and documented under a fluorescence microscope. For quantitative analysis, six randomly chosen high-magnification fields (400×) per histological section were examined, with positive cells enumerated relative to the total cells in each field. The average percentage of positive cells across the six fields was taken as the result for that tissue section.

RT-qPCR

Tissue blocks stored at −80 °C were ground into powder using RNAiso Plus lysis buffer in a low-temperature high-speed grinder at 5.5 m/s for 3 cycles of 1 minute each at 4 °C. After the tissue was completely ground, RNA was extracted, precipitated, dissolved, and its concentration was measured. The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA according to the instructions of the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan). Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was conducted using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa, Japan) on the LightCycler/LightCycler 480 System (Roche, Germany). The relative mRNA expression levels of apoptosis-related genes (Bax, Bcl-2, and Caspase-3) were determined using the 2−△△CT method, normalized to the housekeeping gene Gapdh. Western blot

Tissue blocks stored at −80 °C were ground into powder RIPA lysis buffer in a low-temperature high-speed grinder at 5.5 m/s for 3 cycles of 1 minute each at 4 °C. Following tissue homogenization, total protein was extracted and quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). For immunoblotting, protein samples (20 μg/lane) were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were probed overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-Bax (Proteintech, China), anti-Bcl-2 (Proteintech, China), anti-Caspase-3 (Proteintech, China), and anti-β-actin (Proteintech, China). Following incubation with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, protein signals were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Beyotime, China) and analysed using ImageJ software (V1.48, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 software. Data normality was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test, with variance homogeneity assessed through Levene's test. When data were normally distributed and variances were homogeneous, results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (X ± S). Two-way ANOVA was conducted to analyse the hepatic and renal function indexes, TUNEL positivity rates, RT-qPCR and Western blot results in buccal pouch mucosa, liver, and kidney tissues among different groups. Post hoc analyses were employed using the Turkey test among the different groups, including different materials or the same materials but with different processing methods. For datasets violating normality or homogeneity of variance, variable transformations were applied before conducting two-way ANOVA, or non-parametric rank-sum tests were used instead. All statistical thresholds remained at P < .05.

Results

General performance of the golden hamsters

After the specimens were sutured, the clinical manifestations of the golden hamsters in each group were observed. No significant changes were noted in the skin, fur, eyes, mucosa, respiratory system, circulatory system, nervous system, limb movement, or behaviour patterns. The body weight of the golden hamsters was gradually increased (Figure 1C).

Hepatic and renal function tests

The results of hepatic and renal function tests are illustrated in Figure 2. The interaction between material type and stimulation time had no significant effect on the levels of ALT, AST, AST/ALT, GLB, A/G, CREA and BUN/CREA in golden hamsters (P > .05). In contrast, the interaction between material type and stimulation time had a significant effect on the levels of ALB, BUN, and TP in golden hamsters (P < .05).

The hepatic and renal function in the golden hamsters after 14- and 28-day of specimen stimulation. #Represents the statistical difference at different time points in the same group (P < .05), *Represents the statistical difference between groups at the same time point (P < .05).

After 14 days of stimulation, the ALB levels were higher in the VAR, OP, PT, DM, and CB groups than in the control group (P < .05), and the A/G ratio was higher in the DM group than in the control group (P < .05). Additionally, the BUN levels were lower in the CB group compared to the control group (P < .05), the BUN levels were higher in the VAR group compared to the PT and CB group (P < .05), the BUN levels were also higher in the OP group compared to the CB group (P < .05). While the TP levels were higher in the VAR, OP, and PT groups compared to the control group (P < .05). After 28 days of stimulation, there were no significant differences in hepatic and renal function indicators among different materials or among the same materials with different processing methods (P > .05).

The time effect analysis showed that from 14 to 28 days of stimulation, ALT levels increased in the OP group, and AST levels rose in the VAR and OP groups (P < .05). ALB levels increased in the VAR, PT, PB, CB and control groups, and GLB levels increased in the CB and control groups, the A/G ratio elevated in the control group (P < .05). BUN levels rose in the PT, DM, and CB groups, and CREA levels were higher in the PT, DM, PB, CB, and control group, the BUN/CREA ratio elevated in the DM group (P < .05). Additionally, TP levels were higher in the VAR, PB, CB, and control groups (P < .05).

Histopathological evaluation of buccal pouch mucosa

No cell degeneration or flattening, no tissue deformation or erosion, no white blood cell infiltration, no or minimal vascular congestion, and no or minimal tissue edema were observed in the buccal pouch mucosa of each group after 14- and 28-day of stimulation. There was no significant difference in the morphology of buccal pouch mucosa between the sutured sides and the opposite buccal pouch mucosa in each group, no matter whether the specimens were stimulated for 14 days and 28 days. Discussion

The first null hypothesis was rejected based on observed variations in hepatic and renal function indices among material groups at 14 days, despite comparable TUNEL positivity rates and apoptosis-related molecule expression across groups. The second null hypothesis was also rejected as temporal analysis revealed there were significant time-dependent changes in the expression of apoptotic proteins in conventional dental polymers.

All materials investigated in this study have been clinically used for a certain period and have received clearance from regulatory authorities (NMPA/FDA) based on standard biocompatibility tests. However, it is important to note that these regulatory assessments primarily focus on basic safety thresholds rather than comparative evaluations across different manufacturing techniques or in-depth biological performance analyses. But these are precisely the key points of this study. This study was designed to provide a comprehensive evaluation of biocompatibility between conventional and CAD/CAM dental polymers that extends beyond standard certification requirements.

Existing literatures suggested that residual monomers leaching from dental polymers may induce dose-dependent cytotoxicity in oral tissues and have been linked to apoptotic pathways.13,14 CAD/CAM dental polymers are polymerized under high temperature and pressure. Theoretically, they may have lower residual monomer elution and better biocompatibility due to their higher degree of conversion in comparison to conventional polymers.16,17,30 But the existing researches fail to resolve the controversy of biocompatibility of conventional and CAD/CAM dental polymers. To address this concern, our previous studies19,20 conducted a comprehensive in vitro evaluation using human gingival fibroblasts. Through CCK-8 assays (cell viability), flow cytometry (apoptosis quantification), RT-qPCR and Western blot (apoptosis markers), we demonstrated that CAD/CAM dental polymers show undetectable residual monomers and superior cytocompatibility, especially in temporary and denture applications. Additionally, bis-acrylic composite resins were found to modulate mitochondrial apoptosis pathways, directly impacting cell proliferation. To bridge the translational gap between in vitro findings and clinical applications, and given the lack of reported in vivo biocompatibility data for CAD/CAM dental polymers, this study developed the golden hamster buccal mucosa irritation model, based on the references31, 32, 33 and previous work by our research group,27 to critically assess the in vivo biocompatibility of CAD/CAM dental polymers. Studies indicate that the golden hamster buccal pouch—an immunologically privileged site resistant to foreign-body rejection—is ideal for tissue culture and microcirculation studies, making it highly suitable for oral mucosal irritation tests.34,35

Our experimental design involved direct suturing of material specimens to the buccal mucosa, enabling comprehensive evaluation of tissue responses through histomorphological and apoptotic analyses. The results showed that both conventional and CAD/CAM dental polymers had good local biocompatibility, as the mucosal tissue morphology in the experimental group was normal and no obvious apoptosis was observed. Notably, the VAR group exhibited increase in Bax and Bcl-2 proteins at 28 days, suggesting progressive material degradation that may correlate with potential monomer leaching from aging polymer matrices. This observation aligns with previous findings by Cakırbay Tanış et al,36 who demonstrated that water storage duration significantly affects the cytotoxicity of denture base materials, with MTT assays revealed cell viability decreased after 15-day of immersion compared to 24-hour storage periods. These collective findings underscore the necessity for extended-duration studies evaluating the biological consequences of long-term material degradation.

In addition, dental monomers may also cause liver and kidney toxicity.37 Therefore, this study also detected the histomorphological and apoptotic analyses of the liver and kidneys of golden hamsters. This study found that both conventional and CAD/CAM dental polymers had good systemic biocompatibility, as all material groups exhibited normal liver/kidney histopathology and no obvious apoptosis was observed. Notably, transient biochemical changes (eg, ALB, A/G, BUN and TP fluctuations) were observed, but these stabilized at 28 days with no lasting alterations. These changes may represent adaptive physiological responses (eg, acute-phase protein synthesis38) rather than toxicity.

Furthermore, the results of this study indicate that CAD/CAM dental polymers showed superior temporal stability in the in vivo biocompatibility compared to conventional materials. This conclusion is supported by the expression of apoptosis-related proteins Bax and Bcl-2 was increased in the VAR group at 28 days, and pro Caspase-3 was decreased in the VAR and PT groups, indicating potential long-term toxic risks associated with conventional materials. Additionally, the protein-level observations suggest that conventional materials exhibit time-dependent apoptotic protein accumulation, which may indicate progressive polymer degradation and possible monomer leaching from aging matrices. In contrast, CAD/CAM dental polymers exhibited superior temporal stability throughout the experimental period. The differential apoptotic responses observed between material groups originate from their distinct physicochemical properties. On the one hand, our previous high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analyses have demonstrated no detectable residual monomers in CAD/CAM dental polymers, while residual monomers and by-products were detected in the VAR and PT groups.20 These findings align with the current in vivo observations of changes in apoptotic proteins, as residual monomers and by-products can affect cell proliferation through the intrinsic mitochondrial apoptosis.20 Moreover, residual monomers in conventional dental polymers demonstrate significantly more pronounced time-dependent accumulation compared to CAD/CAM dental polymers.39 On the other hand, the relative apoptotic stability in CAD/CAM dental polymers may reflect their manufacturing advantages including higher conversion efficiency and more consistent polymer morphology compared to conventional processing methods.8,21 While in vitro studies have demonstrated superior cytocompatibility of CAD/CAM dental polymers compared to conventional ones, the present in vivo investigation revealed both of them had acceptable in vivo biocompatibility, and CAD/CAM dental polymers only showed superior temporal stability at the protein level. This discrepancy may be attributed to the complex interplay of physiological factors in living systems, including metabolic processing and immune responses.

Previous comparative investigations of the biocompatibility of conventional and CAD/CAM dental polymers have primarily relied on in vitro models.30,40,41 In contrast to these studies, the current research employed the golden hamster oral mucosa stimulation model, evaluating histomorphological changes, apoptotic responses in oral mucosa, and potential systemic effects on distal organs (liver and kidney). However, the 14- and 28-day observation periods may not fully capture long-term biological effects. Moreover, post-hoc evaluation confirmed suboptimal statistical power for some measurements, implying these specific data were conditional under the present experimental conditions. Future studies should incorporate extended observation periods and increased the sample size to evaluate material degradation dynamics. Additionally, while our findings highlight the superior temporal stability of CAD/CAM dental polymers in terms of in vivo biocompatibility, represent only one aspect of material performance. Future studies should incorporate physical and chemical properties assessments to fully characterize CAD/CAM dental polymers.

Conclusions

Within the study's experimental framework, our findings demonstrate that both conventional and CAD/CAM dental polymers exhibited acceptable in vivo biocompatibility, but CAD/CAM dental polymers showed superior temporal stability, supports their clinical preference for long-term prosthodontic applications.
