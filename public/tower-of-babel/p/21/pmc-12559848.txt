Postprandial Hypoglycemia Associated With Insulin Receptor Gene Variant G1146R
Authors: Lauraliz Delacruz, Lindsay J Adamczak, Olga Astapova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypoglycemia in people without diabetes can be caused by a variety of factors and may become life-threatening if compensatory mechanisms are overwhelmed. We present a case of postprandial hypoglycemia associated with a heterozygous insulin receptor (INSR) gene variant, G1146R, in an 82-year-old Caucasian man with glucose intolerance and an extensive family history of similar symptoms. His hypoglycemia worsened with advancing age and eventually required a continuous blood glucose sensor with alarms to avoid severe hypoglycemia. This INSR variant is predicted to disrupt the autophosphorylation function of the receptor and was previously reported in an individual with severe insulin resistance presenting in childhood, but who did not experience hypoglycemia. This case highlights the clinical variability of INSR gene variants and may lead to reclassification of G1146R INSR, currently a variant of uncertain significance, as a likely pathogenic variant.

Introduction

Hypoglycemia in people without diabetes is typically defined by a blood glucose level below 55 mg/dL (SI: 3.1 mmol/L) (reference range, 60-99 mg/dL [SI: 3.3-5.5 mmol/L]) accompanied by typical symptoms that are promptly relieved by administration of glucose (Whipple triad). In hypoglycemia, adrenergic symptoms of sweating, tachycardia, tremors, and anxiety are observed due to counterregulatory hormones, including cortisol and adrenaline. If hypoglycemia worsens, neuroglycopenic symptoms such as altered mental status, confusion, agitation, lethargy, and/or loss of consciousness can develop. If not treated promptly, this condition can be fatal [1, 2].

Hypoglycemia occurs when glucose homeostatic mechanisms, including hepatic gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis, are overwhelmed or dysfunctional, signaling a defect in these pathways or the hormone systems that regulate them [3]. If exogenous insulin or its secretagogues are excluded, the etiology of hypoglycemia may be elucidated in the context of concurrent insulin level. In cases where insulin is appropriately suppressed, insufficient glycogen stores or counterregulatory hormones, as in adrenal insufficiency, excessive glucose demand resulting from critical illness, or liver and/or renal failure are often the culprit. However, if insulin is elevated or inappropriately normal during hypoglycemia, this points to abnormal insulin secretion, action, or clearance. Most cases of noniatrogenic insulin-mediated hypoglycemia are due to excessive and/or delayed physiologic insulin secretion (reactive hypoglycemia) or an insulin-secreting neuroendocrine tumor.

Genetic causes of hypoglycemia are rare and are mainly due to inborn errors of metabolism and congenital hyperinsulinism [4]. Here, we present a case of recurrent postprandial hypoglycemia associated with a heterozygous genetic variant in the insulin receptor gene (INSR) that was previously classified as a variant of uncertain significance.

Case Presentation

An 82-year-old Caucasian man with a medical history of hypothyroidism was referred to the endocrinology clinic with the chief complaint of hypoglycemia. He reported experiencing approximately 1 hypoglycemic event per year for the past 30 years with symptoms of sweating, tremors, tunnel vision, and slurred speech that resolved promptly after consuming hard candy or orange juice. Symptoms were most frequent approximately 2 to 3 hours after breakfast, which was typically high in sugar (donuts, pastries), but occasionally occurred after late-night snacks as well. He experienced occasional presyncope and 1 episode of loss of consciousness that resolved after treatment with oral glucose gel by emergency medical technicians. He noted that in the 2 years before the presentation, his symptoms had increased in frequency and reported increasing unawareness of their early stages. Fingerstick glucose checked at the time of symptoms often reported low glucose below the level of detection. Serum glucose drawn at the time of neuroglycopenic symptoms (speech defects) was 38 mg/dL (SI: 2.1 mmol/L). He denied recent changes in his weight. Insulin was not measured at the time of symptoms.

Daily medications included levothyroxine 25 mcg, fluticasone 100 mcg nasal spray, cholecalciferol 2000 units, folic acid 1 mg, and cyanocobalamin 500 mcg daily. As-needed medications included acetaminophen 500 mg every 6 hours and cyclobenzaprine 10 mg 3 times daily. He consumed fewer than 1 alcoholic drink per week, which was not associated with the presenting symptoms.

The patient had no history of bariatric or other gastrointestinal surgery. He had no medical history of malabsorptive disorders.

Family history was notable for 4 of the patient's 5 children and several of his grandchildren experiencing similar postprandial symptoms and having diagnoses of hypoglycemia.

Diagnostic Assessment

Physical examination revealed a well-appearing, lean elderly man. Weight was 59.4 kg, height was 1.727 m (body mass index, 19.92 kg/m²), blood pressure was 134/78 mm Hg, and heart rate was 65 beats per minute. There was no acanthosis nigricans and no skin rash. The rest of the examination was unremarkable.

The patient underwent fasting blood glucose monitoring hourly at 9 through 15 hours of fasting without symptoms of hypoglycemia. The lowest fingerstick glucose level was 81 mg/dL [SI: 4.5 mmol/L] at 11 hours of fasting. A sulfonylurea panel test did not detect tolbutamide, glipizide, glyburide, nateglinide, repaglinide, or glimepiride.

Hemoglobin A1C was 5.7% (SI: 38.8 mmol/mol) (reference range, ≤ 5.6% [SI: ≤ 38 mmol/mol]) and subsequently increased to 5.9% (SI: 41.0 mmol/mol). Insulin level was 33 µIU/mL (SI: 198 pmol/L) (reference range, 3-25 µIU/mL [SI: 18-150 pmol/L]) with a concurrent serum glucose of 70 mg/dL (SI: 3.9 mmol/L). TSH level was 3.09 µIU/mL (SI: 3.09 mU/L) (reference range, 0.27-4.20 µIU/mL [SI: 0.27-4.20 mU/L]). Glomerular filtration rate was 79 mL/min/1.73 m2. Over the following several years, low postprandial serum glucose was documented 3 times (38-48 mg/dL, or 2.1-2.7 mmol/L). Fasting insulin was 22 µIU/mL (132 pmol/L) with concurrent glucose of 93 mg/dL (5.2 mmol/L). Postprandial insulin levels ranged from 33 µIU/mL (198 pmol/L) to 57 µIU/mL (342 pmol/L) (Table 1).

The patient underwent sequence analysis and deletion/duplication testing of a panel of 120 genes associated with hypoglycemia, which identified 2 variants of undetermined significance: heterozygous INSR c.3436G > A (p.Gly1146Arg, or G1146R) and heterozygous PCK2 c.461-7T > C (Intronic).

Treatment

Targeted genetic testing for the INSR gene variant was recommended for affected individuals in the patient's family. The patient was instructed to follow a low glycemic index diet and check his blood glucose before driving. Despite the recommended dietary modifications, he continued to have frequent symptomatic hypoglycemic events. Acarbose was initiated and titrated up to 50 mg 3 times daily with meals but was discontinued because of gastrointestinal side effects. The patient then started using a continuous blood glucose monitor with alarms for glucose below 70 mg/dL (SI: 3.9 mmol/L).

Outcome and Follow-up

The symptom severity and degree of hypoglycemia were dramatically improved thanks to the sensor alarms and prompt treatment with juice. Review of the sensor data at the end of the follow-up period of approximately 6 years revealed that the patient was spending 85% of time with blood glucose in the range of 70 to 180 mg/dL (SI: 3.9-10.0 mmol/L), 4% in the range of 55 to 69 mg/dL (SI: 3.1-3.8 mmol/L), 0% below 55 mg/dL (SI: 3.1 mmol/L), and the remainder of the time with blood glucose above 180 mg/dL (SI: 10.0 mmol/L). He did not experience syncope or presyncope again since implementing the continuous glucose monitor.

Discussion

Reactive hypoglycemia is a type of insulin-mediated hypoglycemia that occurs due to exaggerated and/or delayed insulin secretion after carbohydrate ingestion and may be caused by β-cell hyperplasia [5], or nesidioblastosis. Patients with reactive hypoglycemia often have a history of malabsorptive bariatric surgery [6], rapid weight loss, and/or insulin resistance [5]. Reactive hypoglycemia is suspected when hypoglycemic episodes occur within 2 to 4 hours after meals in patients with suggestive history. In this case, reactive hypoglycemia was associated with glucose intolerance and prediabetes, as evident by elevated hemoglobin A1C, elevated fasting serum glucose, and frequently elevated fasting insulin levels despite concurrent normal serum glucose (Table 1). Although our evaluation was limited by the lack of insulin level measured concurrently with hypoglycemia, we believe that this is a case of insulin-mediated hypoglycemia. The clinical history and the lack of hypoglycemia during a 15-hour fast were not consistent with insulin-secreting neuroendocrine tumor. Symptoms were initially mild and became more severe in advanced age in this patient with low body mass index, suggesting that depletion of glycogen stores and/or age-related dysfunction of compensatory mechanisms contributed to hypoglycemia. The implementation of a continuous glucose sensor with alarms for hypoglycemia was instrumental in reducing the risks of life-threatening events and hospitalization.

Because of strong family history of hypoglycemia, genetic testing was performed and revealed a heterozygous variant of uncertain significance in INSR c.3436G > A (p.Gly1146Arg or G1146R). An insulin receptor is a transmembrane hetero-tetramer consisting of 2 α subunits that make up the extracellular insulin-binding domain and 2 β subunits that comprise the transmembrane domain and the intracellular tyrosine kinase function [7]. Insulin binding leads to conformational changes in the intracellular domains, which allows the 2 tyrosine kinase moieties to autophosphorylate each other and initiate a tyrosine phosphorylation cascade of intracellular second messengers [8]. Glycine 1146 is located within the tyrosine kinase domain of the β subunit.

The heterozygous G1146R variant has been previously reported in an 11-year-old child with insulin resistance, obesity, and severe acanthosis nigricans; hypoglycemia was not reported in that case [9]. In silico modeling predicted that the G1146R variant would disrupt the folding of the tyrosine kinase domain of INSR, and in vitro experiments confirmed that autophosphorylation of INSR was abrogated by this mutation [9], suggesting that this variant would disrupt intracellular signaling downstream of the insulin receptor.

Insulin can activate the IGF1 receptor [10], albeit with lower affinity than its cognate receptor, and both insulin and IGF1 receptors can combine to form hybrid heterotetramers that can be stimulated by either ligand [11]. Although the G1146R variant severely affects INSR function when both β subunits are mutated in vitro, its molecular effect in a heterozygous individual with 1 functional INSR allele and 2 functional IGF1R alleles is difficult to predict. Although this case differs in presentation from the prior reported case of this INSR variant, other pathogenic mutations within the tyrosine kinase domain of the receptor have been reported to cause familial hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia [12-14] presenting with postprandial hypoglycemia similar to this case. The spectrum of INSR mutations is quite broad, ranging from severely debilitating diseases such as leprechaunism, to milder forms of diabetes. Thus, available evidence suggests that INSR mutations can have variable presentations that may depend on other factors associated with insulin signaling, such as the IGF1 receptor and intracellular signaling molecules.

The PCK2 gene encodes the mitochondrial form of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, a mitochondrial enzyme involved in gluconeogenesis [15]. Although a theoretical link between PCK2 mutations and hypoglycemia is mechanistically plausible, the PCK2 variant, c.461-7T > C, identified in this patient, has not been reported to be associated with hypoglycemia or other glucose metabolism disorders, nor have other variants in the PCK2 gene. Based on available data, it is difficult to conclude whether this gene variant contributed to the phenotype of this patient.

In conclusion, we present a case of familial hypoglycemia following the reactive hypoglycemia pattern and associated with an INSR gene variant predicted to disrupt its tyrosine kinase activity, that was previously described in a patient with severe insulin resistance. Our findings may lead to re-classification of this variant from a variant of uncertain significance to a likely pathological variant.
