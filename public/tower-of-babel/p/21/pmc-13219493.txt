Differences in metabolic profiles of lupin-based beverages produced with various fermentation techniques
Authors: Jasmin Raita, Hany Ahmed, Malviina Nikola, Retu Haikonen, Niina Kelanne, Oskar Laaksonen, Baoru Yang, Ville Koistinen, Kati Hanhineva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant-based beverages made from protein-rich raw materials are gaining popularity due to the increasing demand for sustainable food production. However, these beverages may suffer from undesirable sensory or nutritional properties. To address these challenges, fermentation could be employed in their production. We employed lactic acid bacteria, spontaneous fermentation, and kombucha fermentation, followed by LC–MS metabolomics analysis, to investigate the changes in the biochemical composition of fermented beverages using lupin, a less-utilized protein-rich plant, as the raw material. After fermentation with individual lactic acid bacteria strains, as well as with spontaneous and kombucha fermentations involving various microbial species, we observed distinct differences among these fermentations. For instance, the relative abundances of biogenic amines and raffinose family oligosaccharides (RFOs) varied depending on the fermentation technique used. Additionally, the relative abundances of lactic acid derivatives increased in all fermentations. The results of this study demonstrate that fermentation can effectively modify the biochemical composition of lupin-based beverages. By selecting the appropriate fermentation technique, lupins could be used to produce sustainable protein-rich beverages with possibly improved nutritional content.

Introduction

The consumption of plant-based beverages as alternatives for dairy products has steadily increased due to health-related interests, environmental sustainability, and concerns regarding animal welfare1,2. For example, allergies associated with cow’s milk3 have steered the focus of consumers towards plant-based beverages, which are commonly made from various nuts, seeds, legumes, and cereals. Soybeans are one of the most popular raw materials used in the production of plant-based beverages, and the amount of protein in soy-based beverages is similar to the one in cow’s milk4,5. However, other raw materials used in the production of plant-based beverages, such as lupin, are emerging6,7. Despite this, there are still concerns related to the nutritional quality of plant-based alternatives to cow’s milk, as their content of protein and other nutritional components is often different8.

Lupins are known to have a protein content similar to soy, emphasizing their potential as a raw material for meat and dairy product alternatives9. Depending on the lupin species, protein content can vary from 28–40% on a dry basis10. However, some lupin species contain high levels of toxic alkaloids, such as lupinine and sparteine, which can hinder their safe consumption11. Narrow-leafed lupins (Lupinus angustifolius) are known to contain lower amounts of these alkaloids, although variations can exist due to cultivation conditions11,12. Besides their high protein and dietary fiber content9, narrow-leafed lupins can be grown in northern Europe, whereas the majority of soy needs to be imported13. However, like many pulses, lupins can also have unwanted odors and flavors that may reduce their acceptance among consumers14,15. To address these challenges, fermentation has been used to improve the sensory properties of lupins, while also potentially enhancing their nutritional properties6,16,17. For instance, fermented lupin seeds can contain higher amounts of phenolic compounds compared to raw seeds17,18.

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are often used in the production of dairy products and likewise can be used in the production of plant-based foods19. For instance, some LAB are able to modify the raffinose family oligosaccharides (RFOs), which are known to cause abdominal discomfort associated with the consumption of pulses, including lupin20,21. Additionally, LAB are able to remove antinutritional factors, such as phytic acid, and improve the availability of proteins21–23. Spontaneous fermentation, which relies on microorganisms naturally present in the raw materials, fermentation environment, or equipment, is a traditionally used fermentation method24. Spontaneous fermentation can improve the nutritional quality of plant-based foods by increasing the bioavailability and digestibility of various nutrients25. Indeed, LAB and yeasts have been isolated from spontaneously fermented legumes26. Furthermore, kombucha, a tea-based drink fermented with a Symbiotic Culture of Bacteria and Yeast (SCOBY), is typically made with sweetened black or green tea, but variations such as soy-based kombucha have also been developed27–30. Kombucha fermentations were found to improve the sensory profiles of soy-based beverages by reducing the levels of unwanted compounds and enhancing desirable attributes, such as sourness29,31. During the fermentation process in kombucha, the bacteria present form a biofilm composed of microbial cellulose on the surface of the liquid, which can then be used for future kombucha fermentations32.

Here, we studied the effect of various fermentation techniques (two LAB strains, kombucha, and spontaneous) on the biochemical composition of lupin-based beverages with metabolic profiling using liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LC–MS). Existing research has focused on specific compounds or nutrients in lupin-based beverages, but limited research about the comprehensive biochemical composition after fermentation exists. Furthermore, to our knowledge, kombucha prepared from lupin has not been studied before. We hypothesize that different fermentation techniques could have specific effects on the biochemical composition of lupin-based beverages by modulating the existing compounds and creating new ones.

Results

Impact of fermentation on the biochemical composition of lupin beverages

Comprehensive metabolomics analysis was performed to determine the impact of various fermentation techniques on the biochemical composition of lupin-based beverages. Fermentation techniques included lactic acid fermentation with two different bacteria strains (LAB 10492 and LAB 20174), spontaneous fermentation, and kombucha fermentation. To monitor the success of fermentations, pH values (Fig. 1b) were measured throughout the fermentations. The 24 h endpoints for both single-strain LAB fermentations were chosen based on preliminary tests where fermentation was continued in total for 48 h, and existing literature6. The endpoint of kombucha fermentation was chosen to be at a similar pH value as in the single-strain LAB fermentations, but the pH of the spontaneous fermentation did not decrease from the observed values, even when the fermentation was continued for additional days. Therefore, it differs from the pH value of approximately 4.0, which was used as the endpoint in the single-strain LAB and kombucha fermentations. The biochemical composition of the beverages changed during fermentation, as demonstrated with principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 1a). The single-strain LAB fermentations separated clearly from spontaneous and kombucha fermentations. The two single-strain LAB fermentations resembled each other strongly, whereas spontaneous and kombucha fermentations resulting from various microbial species both had unique fingerprints, evidenced by separation in the PCA (Fig. 1a). In addition to the fermentation type, the duration of fermentation also had an impact on the biochemical composition of these lupin-based beverages. While the single-strain LAB fermentations again exhibited similar patterns throughout the fermentation, spontaneous and kombucha fermentations started to separate after 6–8 h (Fig. 1a) indicating differences within their biochemical compositions (Fig. S1). This difference between the used fermentation techniques can result from autoclaving of the lupin beverages, which was done before single-strain LAB fermentations to ensure the growth of only the inoculated bacteria.

Impact of fermentation on various metabolite classes

Quinolizidine alkaloids, such as lupinine, hydroxylupanine, and oxymatrine (Fig. S2), were found at the beginning of spontaneous fermentation in cluster 1, but their abundances seem to decrease after 24 h. Similarly, in kombucha fermentation, their abundances decreased after 48 h. Given the potential toxicity of these alkaloids at certain quantities, their presence in plant-based beverages is undesirable33. However, their exact quantities were not analyzed in this study as our approach was non-targeted metabolomics analysis using relative abundances. Their abundances seemed to decrease towards the end of the fermentation, except for LAB 10492 fermentation (Fig. 2a). Other compounds behaving similarly in each fermentation include sucrose, trehalose, and melibiose, present in cluster 3. The abundances of compounds present in cluster 3 decrease towards the end of the fermentations, while the abundances of some compounds in cluster 2 increase (Fig. 2b). Compounds distinguishing the single-strain LAB fermentations from kombucha and spontaneous fermentations include flavonoids, such as apiin, nicotinic acid, pyroglutamic acid, and ectoine found in cluster 6 (Fig. 2a, b). In addition, flavonoids camelliaside b and apigenin-digalactoside are found in cluster 1 in single-strain LAB fermentations, while their abundances have decreased towards the end in spontaneous and kombucha fermentations.

Amino acids, including leucine, isoleucine, and proline, are present in kombucha after 24 h of fermentation in cluster 4, while they seem to be absent in other fermentations. Succinic acid and putrescine (cluster 2) appear after 24 h of spontaneous fermentation, while the abundance of indolelactic acid (ILA) increases at 24 h of fermentation in each of the techniques. Compounds distinguishing kombucha and spontaneous fermentations from the single-strain LAB fermentations include linoleic acid and monolinolenin, found for instance in cluster 5. The abundance of the flavonoid chrysoeriol (cluster 5) increased after 24 h in kombucha and spontaneous fermentations, but then proceeded to decrease.

The abundances of aspartic acid and glutamic acid decreased in the single-strain LAB fermentations, while their abundances increased in kombucha and spontaneous fermentation (Fig. 2a and Fig. 2S). However, their abundances started to increase after 24 h, which might be the reason why such an increase was not observed in the single-strain LAB fermentations, as that was their endpoint. From organic acids, the abundance of succinic acid increased only in spontaneous fermentation, while the abundance of lactic acid increased in other fermentations more than in the spontaneous one. In the single-strain LAB fermentations, the abundances of pyroglutamic acid were higher than in spontaneous and kombucha fermentations, but the levels remained similar throughout the fermentations (Fig. 2a and Fig. 2S). Additionally, citric and aconitic acid abundances (Fig. 2a and Fig. 2S) remained similar in the single-strain LAB fermentations, while their abundances decreased towards the end with kombucha and spontaneous ones. In kombucha fermentation, the abundance of aconitic acid suddenly increased at 48 h followed by a decrease.

Raffinose family oligosaccharides, lactic acid derivatives, and biogenic amines

When focusing on the annotated compounds (n = 155), RFOs were found to be one group affected by the fermentation technique. The relative abundances of stachyose and raffinose (Fig. 3) decreased in spontaneous and kombucha fermentations after 24 h, but until that their levels remained similar in each fermentation technique. The relative abundances of raffinose show a decrease in both of the single-strain LAB fermentations, and further decreases could have possibly been observed if the fermentations had been continued longer. These compounds are often considered as antinutritional factors as they can’t be digested in the small intestine and might therefore cause flatulence34,35. The abundances of phenyllactic acid (PLA), ILA, and lactic acid increased in each of the fermentations. The relative abundances of biogenic amines increased during some of the fermentations, which might indicate the spoilage of the beverage (Fig. 3). Biogenic amines are formed from free amino acids during the fermentation process, and are unwanted compounds due to their adverse health effects in high concentrations36,37. During kombucha and single-strain LAB fermentations, the abundance of spermidine increased, while the abundance of tyramine was found to increase in spontaneous and kombucha fermentations. The abundance of putrescine, which can be associated with unpleasant odors such as the smell of death38, increased after 8 h of spontaneous fermentation. In addition, the abundance of phenylethylamine started to increase in kombucha fermentations after 24 h. Even though sensory analysis was not included in this study, a putrid smell was observed during the spontaneous fermentation towards the end. At the 24 h timepoint, where sampling was done for all of the different fermentation techniques, the abundances of phenylethylamine and tyramine are similar for each fermentation. However, as seen with the standard deviations, spontaneous and kombucha fermentations have differences between the three replicate fermentations (Fig. 3).

Differences in the abundances of vitamin B compounds during varying fermentation techniques

The relative abundances of Vitamin B compounds nicotinic acid (Vitamin B3), its derivative niacinamide, and thiamine (Vitamin B1) were dependent on the fermentation time and type (Fig. 4). Additionally, the impact of pre-treatment was observed with niacinamide and thiamine, as their abundances in the single-strain LAB fermentations differ from kombucha and spontaneous fermentations which did not have autoclaving as a pre-treatment. The abundance of niacinamide decreased during the first 30 min in both single-strain LAB fermentations, and after 8 h in spontaneous and kombucha fermentations. On the other hand, the abundances of nicotinic acid increased during the first hours of the single-strain LAB fermentations, followed by a decrease towards the end. In spontaneous and kombucha fermentations, the abundances increased only after 8 h, followed by a decrease after 24 h towards the endpoint. This difference most likely reflects the higher abundance of LAB in the single-strain fermentations already during the early hours of fermentation. Thiamine abundances decreased in each of the fermentations towards the end. However, the abundance of thiamine decreased more steeply in LAB 10492 than it did with LAB 20174. In previous lupin fermentation experiments, the content of vitamin B12 has increased39, but this could not be confirmed in this study as that compound was not identified from the LC–MS data. Discussion

Using liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis, we comprehensively characterized the biochemical composition of lupin-based beverages produced using four different fermentation techniques. We found that the relative abundances of compounds such as lactic acid and its derivatives increased, while the abundances of RFOs decreased. Some species of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum have been shown to produce indolelactic acid (ILA) from tryptophan40, whereas phenyllactic acid (PLA) can be produced by lactic acid bacteria (LAB) from phenylalanine and tyrosine41. Both PLA and lactic acid contribute positively to food preservation and safety due to their antimicrobial properties42,43. However, the presence of RFO’s is not entirely negative, as they can have beneficial effects, including prebiotic properties34. In lupins, certain cultivars can contain higher concentrations of RFOs than others35,44, but fermentation can decrease their content45. Similar results are observed here with spontaneous and kombucha fermentations, as their relative abundances decreased.

The formation of biogenic amines is often associated with fermentations without a starter culture46, as also shown here in the case of tyramine, putrescine, and phenylethylamine. In this context, using pure LAB starters could serve as a better option instead of mixed starters such as symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast (SCOBY), as tyramine is one of the biogenic amines with toxic properties47. It has been associated with the rise of blood pressure and migraines for some individuals48. Previously, the content of nicotinic acid has been found to increase during LAB fermentation of plant-based beverages49 and cow’s milk50. However, the abundance of nicotinic acid decreased towards the end of the fermentation, indicating that shorter fermentation time could be optimal for producing a lupin-based beverage still containing this bioactive compound; although then some of the beneficial effects of fermentation would be lost, such as the reduction of oligosaccharides.

Legume-based products can often have unwanted attributes, such as beany and “green” flavors and odors, which can negatively impact their acceptance. Some of the compounds formed during these fermentations have been associated with possible sensory properties. For instance, succinic, lactic, citric, aconitic, and pyroglutamic acids are associated with sourness51, which can be characteristic for fermented foods6. In addition, citric and lactic acids can provide astringency52, while aspartic, succinic, and glutamic acids have been associated with umami taste52,53, and their abundances increased in kombucha and spontaneous fermentations. Although acetic acid is typically produced during kombucha fermentations due to the presence of acetic acid bacteria30,31, it was not found within the compounds with available MS/MS data in this study.

Based on the results, fermentation time should not be continued for a prolonged time, as the relative abundances of compounds with possible unwanted nutritional or sensory properties can increase. In addition, the abundances of vitamin B compounds decreased towards the end of fermentations, and the abundances of flavonoids camelliaside B and apigenin-digalactoside decreased after 24 h of fermentation. In previous studies, these flavonoids have been associated with potential health benefits, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-bacterial properties54,55. Therefore, pH values were not the most suitable option to decide the endpoints of the fermentations, as exemplified with biogenic amines in kombucha fermentation.

The limitations in this study include that we only used one SCOBY, and both of the used LAB strains were L. plantarum. To ensure the impact of certain compounds on the sensory profile of these beverages, sensory analysis would have been needed in addition to analysis focusing on the volatile compounds present in these beverages, including compounds like acetic acid and hexanoic acid. Another relevant point is that the lupin-based beverages in the single-strain LAB fermentations and kombucha and spontaneous fermentations had different pre-treatments, including heating, which likely has an impact on the end results. Moreover, non-targeted metabolomics analytics focuses on relative abundances of the signal intensities in the study setup, rather than on exact concentrations of any particular compounds. In future studies, the quantification of organic acids, such as acetic acid, citric acid, and lactic acid, will provide valuable information required for implications regarding the nutritional quality of these beverages. Additionally, comparing the three fermentation techniques proved challenging due to their differing kinetics and final pH values. Fermentations with the two LAB strains occurred more rapidly, which can be observed with their steeper pH decreases. The inoculated bacteria count was likely higher in those fermentations than in kombucha and spontaneous ones. This difference further complicates the comparison between these fermentation techniques. In addition, metagenomics would provide valuable information regarding the microbial composition of the final product, including its safety. Therefore, further examinations are needed before these beverages can be deemed safe for consumption.

In conclusion, we showed that fermentation has the potential to modify the biochemical composition of lupin-based beverages, and each of the employed fermentation techniques had specific effects on the composition. These findings include novel information regarding the kombucha fermentation of a lupin-based beverage. In addition, the information produced in this study could be further employed in the development of lupin-based beverages.

Methods

Materials for lupin-based beverages

Lupin flour (Sweet lupin flour) was obtained from local farm Koivunalhon Luomutila (Lieto, Finland). Two strains of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum from in-house collection LAB 10492 and LAB 20174 were used in the fermentations, and they were originally obtained from Deutsche Sammlung von Mikroorganismen und Zellkulturen (DSMZ, Braunschweig, Germany). Kombucha starter kit (Kombucha Starter Kit, Good Guys Oy) was obtained from local store. Flavored black tea (Forsman tea, Keisarin malja tee (Fairtrade black tea 87%, pineapple pieces 6%, orange pieces 3%, natural flavor 2.5%, lily 1.5%), Aaro Forsman Oy) for kombucha was purchased from the local market.

Preparation of lupin-based beverages

Lupin-based beverages were prepared by mixing lupin flour with tap water in a 1:10 (w/w) ratio and blending it for 30 s at maximum speed (Chef XL Titanium with a blender attachment, Kenwood Limited, Havant, UK). After blending, the beverages were centrifuged (10 min, 5000 g, RT) to collect the supernatant, and the remaining solid materials were discarded. Lupin beverages for lactic acid bacteria fermentations were stored in a freezer (–20 °C) until the experiments, and autoclaved within the bioreactor before inoculating the bacteria. For spontaneous and kombucha fermentations, the lupin beverages were prepared in the same way, except without autoclaving and freezing.

Single-strain LAB fermentations

Lactic acid bacteria strains (LAB 10492 and LAB 20174) had been stored in MRS glycerol stock at –80 °C from previously conducted studies56, and they were preincubated for 24 h in MRS broth (Accumedia Neogen, USA). The bacteria cells were washed twice by centrifuging (3500 rpm, 20 min, +8 °C) and suspending the remaining pellet to 0.9% saline. The viable cell count was determined by plating dilution series onto MRS plates and incubating them at +30 °C for 48 h.

The single-strain LAB fermentations were conducted for 24 h in a bioreactor in 1.5 L vessel containing 500 mL of lupin beverages (Minifors 2, Infors HT, Switzerland). The whole system, including the beverage, was autoclaved, as it was found to be the most efficient way. The concentration of the inoculated bacteria was approximately 1 × 108 CFU/mL. The temperature of the bioreactor was kept at +28 °C with stirring of 100 rpm/min. Throughout the fermentation, the pH was measured, and samples for the LC–MS analysis were collected at the start after inoculation (0 h), after 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, and finally at 24 h. The samples were stored at –20 °C. The fermentations for both bacteria strains were conducted in triplicate.

Spontaneous fermentation

For the spontaneous fermentation, the lupin beverages were prepared as previously explained, and the bioreactor was autoclaved for sterilization without the beverages. The bioreactor was kept at +28 °C with 100 rpm/min stirring, and the pH was measured through fermentation. Samples were collected similarly as with the single-strain LAB fermentations and continued with every 24 h until the end of the fermentation after 96 h. Spontaneous fermentations were also conducted in triplicate. Samples were stored at –20 °C until further analyses. The end of the fermentation was determined by the pH value, which did not continue to decrease after 96 h of fermentation, and therefore, that was determined to be the endpoint. These spontaneous fermentations had the longest fermentation times, which was expected, as they are based on the existing microorganisms in the raw material instead of inoculating bacteria as was done in the single-strain LAB and kombucha fermentations. The pH value at the end point was close to the one in LAB fermentations.

Kombucha fermentation

Kombucha starter was prepared from commercial SCOBY by boiling 2.7 L of tap water and adding 15 g of black tea, and letting it simmer for 20 min. After 20 min, the tea leaves were removed, and 180 g of regular white sugar was added and dissolved. Once the tea was cooled down to +28–30 °C, the SCOBY was added with the started liquid included in the packaging. The kombucha starter was left in a dark, static place at RT for 11 days, and it was covered with cheesecloth. The kombucha starters for the other two fermentations were prepared in the same way, except using the previously prepared kombucha as the acidifying starter liquid for the new kombucha batch.

Lupin kombuchas were prepared in triplicate by adding 5% v/v of the kombucha starter to the lupin beverages and let to ferment in beakers covered with cheesecloths under static conditions at +30 °C for 72 h in total. Samples were collected from the same time points as in the single-strain LAB fermentations, continuing every 24 h until the end of the fermentation. The endpoint of the fermentation was determined to be at approximately the same pH value as in the single-strain LAB fermentations. Final pH values for the three replicate fermentations were 3.57, 5.53, and 3.94. During sample collection, pH values were measured (LLG-pH meter 5) to monitor the fermentation process. Samples were stored at –20 °C until further analyses.

Metabolomics analysis : LC–MS sample preparation

Frozen samples from various time points from the LAB, spontaneous, and kombucha fermentations were thawed before further preparations. The samples (200 µL) were extracted with 80% methanol (800 µL) (MeOH, Honeywell, Charlotte, NC, USA) in ultrapure water (Elga, Veolia Water Solutions and Technologies, Woodridge, IL, USA) and vortexed with full speed (5 s, RT). After vortexing, the samples were centrifuged (10 min, 18,000 g, +4 °C) and the collected supernatants (200 µL) were diluted with 80% methanol in water (800 µL), vortexed with full speed (5 s, RT), and filtered with 0.2 µM PTFE filters (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) into HPLC-vials with inserts. 20 µL of each diluted sample was filtered and pooled to be used as a quality control.

LC–MS analysis

Metabolomics analysis was performed using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC–QTOF–MS) using hydrophilic interaction (HILIC, Acquity UPLC BEH Amide, 1.7 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; Waters Corporation) and reversed-phase (RP, Zorbax Eclipse XDB-C18, 1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; Agilent Technologies) chromatographies. The instrument used in this study was Agilent 1290 Infinity UPLC coupled with a 6546 LC–QTOF. For HILIC, mobile phases consisted of 1:1 acetonitrile in water (solution A) and 9:1 acetonitrile in water (solution B), both containing 20 mM ammonium formate (Sigma-Aldrich). The gradient was 0–2.5 min, 100% B; 2.5–10 min, 100% B to 0% B; 10–10.01 min, 0% B to 100% B; 10.01–12.5 min, 100% B with 0.6 ml min−1 flow rate. For RP, mobile phases were water (solution A) and methanol (solution B), with both containing 0.1% v/v formic acid. The gradient used was 0–10 min, 2–100% B; 10–14.5 min, 100% B; 14.5–14.51 min, 100–2% B; 14.51–16.5 min, 2% B with 0.4 ml min−1 flow rate. For both chromatographies, the injection volume was 2 µl, and the sample tray was kept at +4 °C, and electrospray ionization was used with positive and negative modes. The following source parameters were used: drying gas flow, 10 l min−1; temperature, 325 °C; sheath gas flow, 11 l min−1; temperature, 350 °C; nebulizer pressure, 45 psi; capillary voltage, 3,500 V; and nozzle voltage, 1000 V. In the full scan mode, the scan range was set to 50–1600 m/z, scan time to 1.67 Hz, and abundance threshold to 150. Quality controls were injected at the beginning of the analysis to prime the system and after every 12 samples to monitor the stability of the LC–MS analysis. Samples were analyzed in a randomized order. Collision energies of 10, 20, and 40 eV were applied in the separate MS/MS scans for the quality control and selected samples. Parameters for MS/MS scans were an abundance threshold of 200, target of 25,000 counts per spectrum, scan rate of 3.33 Hz, scan range of 50–1600 m/z, maximum of 4 precursors per cycle, precursor isolation width of 1.3 Da, active exclusion after 2 spectra and release after 0.25 min. The data acquisition software used was MassHunter Workstation Acquisition 11.0 (Agilent Technologies).

Data analysis

MS-DIAL57 (ver. 4.80) was used for automated peak picking and alignment of the raw data. The peak picking was done by using 0.01 Da for MS1 tolerance and 0.025 Da for MS2 tolerance, m/z range 0–2000 Da, minimum peak amplitude 2000 signal counts, and mass slice width 0.1 Da, smoothing level 3 scan, and minimum peak width 5 scan. For the peak alignment, retention time tolerance was 0.2 min and MS1 tolerance 0.015 Da; Gap filling by compulsion was also used. The adduct ions for positive mode were [M + H]+, [M + NH4]+, [M + Na]+, [M + CH3OH + H]+, [M + K]+, [M + ACN + H]+, [M + H-H2O]+, [M + H-2H2O]+, [2 M + H]+, [M-NH4 + H]+, and for negative mode [M − H]−, [M − H2O − H]−, [M + Cl]−, [2 M − H]−, [M + HCOOH-H]−. After alignment, the results from positive and negative modes of RP and HILIC were exported into Microsoft Excel. Combined modes were pre-processed using R-studio version 2023.06.2 with the notame package58 for data drift correction and flagging low-quality features59.

Metabolite annotation

Maximum value of the sample abundance to blank average ratio was calculated for all of the molecular features, and values below five were discarded to eliminate possible contaminations. All of the remaining molecular features were considered to be of good quality, and the ones containing MS/MS data were used for annotations. The metabolite annotations were made by comparing m/z, retention time, and MS/MS fragmentation patterns with in-house and publicly available databases in MS-DIAL. Identification level 1 was used for compounds matching authentic standards, ID level 2 for putatively annotated compounds, and ID level 3 for putatively annotated compound classes60.

Statistical analyses

Principal component analysis for four fermentation techniques using 35,996 molecular features obtained from the LC–MS/MS analysis was prepared using ggbiplot (v.0.6.2) in R-studio with log transformation and z-score normalized data. k-means clustering analysis with six clusters was made with ComplexHeatmap (v.2.18.0) with z-score normalized data. The number of clusters was chosen based on visual examination to represent clearly differential patterns in the metabolite abundances, without resulting in clusters holding only a few metabolite features. The data consisted of average signal abundances of 155 annotated compounds at each fermentation time point, for each fermentation technique. The number of clusters was selected based on visual examination. k-means clustering lineplots were prepared with ggplot2 (ver. 3.5.0). Lineplots for annotated compounds were prepared with ggpubr (ver. 0.6.0).
