Estimating the Joint Probability Density for Index Construction: Some Simplifications Using the TWEAK as Example
Authors: Maureen Murdoch, Barbara Ann Clothier, Siamak Noorbaloochi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIndex construction using the joint probability density (JPD) method is extremely robust and, unlike confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) or item response theory (IRT), puts few restrictions on underlying data. When input variables' numbers are large, however, JPD estimation can be difficult.ObjectiveTo assess two simplifications of JPD estimation using the TWEAK, a well validated screener for problematic drinking, and to compare these against the fully estimated JPD, the conventionally scored TWEAK, and TWEAK scores estimated through CFA and IRT.MethodsMailed survey of a nationally representative panel of 410 gender‐stratified, post‐9/11 Veterans with pending disability claims for posttraumatic stress disorder.ResultsSummary statistics for the TWEAK's fully estimated JPD and the two simplifications were very similar, and Spearman's correlations were 0.97–1.00 (ps < 0.001). Spearman's correlations across the remaining scoring approaches were −0.84 to −0.90 (ps < 0.001). All 6 scoring approaches identified differences in men's TWEAK scores by alcohol use diagnosis (ps ≤ 0.02); the 3 JPD approaches did not reach statistical significance in the women. IRT analysis identified local dependence issues.ConclusionsOur simplified estimations of JPD score were very similar to the fully estimated JPD and much easier to calculate. All 3 JPD estimations were highly concordant with more traditional scoring approaches.

Introduction

Any collection of variables has a joint probability distribution (or probability mass function) that provides the probability of a particular combination of observed values occurring relative to all the other possible combinations of values. On net, rarer combinations will have a lower joint probability than more common combinations. When the collection of variables are attributes with at least ordinal properties, the individual probabilities of having any one combination of observed values relative to all the other combinations can be rank‐ordered to create a scalar, as long as all the variables or indicators target the same construct (unidimensionality), have approximately the same scoring direction—that is, higher (lower) numbers correspond to “more” (“less”) of the construct being assessed (unidirectionality), and demonstrate codirectionality—that is, the resulting scalar's scores agree with higher (lower) values of all the underlying variables or indicators (Noorbaloochi et al. 2024).

This approach to index creation, known as the Joint Probability Density (JPD) method (Noorbaloochi et al. 2024), has several advantages over more traditional scaling methods, such as principal component analysis, factor analysis, or item response theory. First, the JPD scalar is always unidimensional. Thus, the JPD method allows one to avoid problems with rank ordering observations when more than one latent variable explains covariance between indicators. Second, because the JPD method summarizes the entire joint distribution of its underlying inputs, it discards less information than principal component and factor analysis. The latter two are based on the joint distribution's variance or covariance only. The higher informativeness of the JPD method relative to methods that discard other moments of the joint distribution may lead to greater granularity and finer distinctions between observations. Third, the JPD method imposes far fewer restrictions on the underlying indicators. Indictors are allowed to have different scales (e.g., nominal, counts, continuous) and extreme skewness or kurtosis, all of which can cause problems when using principal component or factor analysis. The JPD method also does not require local independence among indicators, an important requirement for item response theory.

Given JPD's minimal requirements, perhaps the method's biggest barrier to scalar creation is estimating the joint probability density itself. This is especially true when there are large numbers of input variables. In our previous work, following Bayesian Network specification paradigm, we used all possible conditional factorizations of the joint probability density and their geometric mean to estimate an index (Noorbaloochi et al. 2024). Because there are p! possible conditional factorizations under this paradigm (where p = the number of input variables), this approach becomes computationally time‐intensive when there are more than 5 or 6 inputs. Our experiments with simulations of joint probability density estimation suggested that a smaller, random selection of joint probability density conditional specifications—or even a backward selection of a single conditional specification—might provide a satisfactory approximation of the underlying joint probability density.

In the present study, we had two broad aims: first, using a well‐validated screening measure for problematic drinking (the TWEAK, described below), we tested whether a smaller, random sampling of conditional specifications or the backward selection of a single conditional specification were sufficient estimators of the TWEAK's JPD relative to the “gold standard” JPD estimator derived from all p! conditional factorizations. To assess this, we compared the two alternative estimators' JPD scores to our gold standard JPD score using distribution moments, Shannon's H, Spearman's correlations, Bland‐Altman limits of agreement graphs, and validity performance. For our second aim, to confirm that JPD scalars perform similarly to more traditional scoring methods, we compared the TWEAK's conventional scoring algorithm (a sum‐score) to all three JPD scoring approaches and to TWEAK scores derived from confirmatory factor analysis and item response theory. To assess our second aim, we used Spearman's correlations and criterion‐related validity checks.

The TWEAK is an acronym for Tolerance, Worry, Eye opener, Amnesia, K[c]ut down and was initially developed to screen for problematic drinking in pregnant women (Russell 1994). It was later extended to other populations. The TWEAK is ideal for comparing the JPD method against more traditional scaling methods, because it has extreme skew and uses different response metrics for different items. These characteristics are potentially problematic for factor analysis. Widely differing sensitivities for the TWEAK across gender (Cherpitel 1995) and sociodemographics (e.g., female Veterans (Bush et al. 2003) versus civilian women (Chan et al. 1993)) suggest that the TWEAK may not meet assumptions for local independence, a problem for item response theory.

Methods

Study Design and Human Studies Oversight

The design is a prospective, nationally representative panel study. The panel was foundation for a gender‐blocked, 3 × 2 × 2 factorial trial that examined the impact of cover letter modifications on survey non‐response bias (Murdoch et al. 2022). The Minneapolis VA Health Care System's Internal Review Board for Human Studies reviewed and approved the study protocol (#4495‐B). This is a post‐hoc analysis.

Participants and Setting

Participants were United States Veterans from Operations Enduring Freedom, Iraqi Freedom, and New Dawn (OIF/OEF/OND) who had pending Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) disability claims for posttraumatic stress disorder as of November 18, 2015. From the national sampling frame of 14,630 men and 2945 women with pending claims, we used simple randomization without replacement to select 480 men and 480 women to receive mailed surveys. 410 panel members returned completed surveys for a response rate of 42.7%. Survey participants were recruited between February and August 2016. We collected VA administrative diagnoses for alcohol use between August 5, 2015 and February 1, 2016.

Overall, respondent's median age was 33.0 years (mean = 35.2, SD = 8.9, range = 19–67); 53% were white, 27% were black or African American, and 10% were Hispanic.

Survey Protocol

We used a mailed survey to collect Veterans' information on several topics, including the TWEAK. As described elsewhere (Murdoch et al. 2022), Veterans received a survey pre‐notification letter, followed by a postcard reminder and up to 3 copies of a mailed questionnaire at 2‐week intervals. Returning a completed questionnaire signified consent to participate in the survey. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Measures

All administrative data were extracted from the VA Corporate Data Warehouse. We used ICD‐9‐ and ICD‐10‐CM codes to extract any alcohol‐related diagnoses of alcohol use, summarized as a “yes/no” variable, in the 180 days prior to survey. Administrative gender values were extracted from Veterans' self‐report at the time of their enrollment into VA and were categorized into mutually exclusive categories: “male” or “female.”

The TWEAK was derived from earlier CAGE (acronym for Cut down, Anger, Guilt, Eyeopener) (Ewing 1984) and Michigan Alcoholism Screening Test (MAST) (Selzer 1971) questionnaires for problematic drinking. The TWEAK's developers did not follow traditional scaling methods (e.g., factor analysis) but rather hand‐selected items from existing instruments to optimize sensitivity and specificity in their population of interest (Russell 1994). The TWEAK is comprised of 5 items: (1) Tolerance: “How many drinks can you hold before you pass out or fall asleep?” (2) “Have close friends or relatives Worried or complained about your drinking in the past year?” (3) Eye‐Opener: “Do you sometimes take a drink in the morning when you first get up?” (4) Amnesia: “Has a friend or family member ever told you about things you said or did while you were drinking that you could not remember?” and (5) “Do you sometimes need to C[K]ut down on your drinking?” Per the conventional scoring algorithm, Item 1 responses are recategorized to 0 if < 6 and to 2 if 6 or more. Thus, while respondents are traditionally asked to write in the number of drinks they can hold, we offered them a forced choice of “< 6” or “6 or more.” TWEAK sum‐scores greater than 2 have pooled sensitivity of 0.85 and pooled specificity of 0.86 for alcohol use disorder (Santos et al. 2020).

In the present paper, “TWEAK” refers collectively to any scalar derived from the 5 input variables. Specific TWEAK scalars emanating from a specific scoring approach, described below, are referred to as “TWEAK‐X,” where X is the abbreviated scoring method, for example, TWEAK‐CFA for the TWEAK score derived using confirmatory factor analysis.

Scoring Approaches

To compare the three JPD scoring approaches (aim 1), we first estimated the TWEAK's JPD‐based summarization using all 120 possible conditional factorizations of the joint probability density and treated this as our gold standard. As simplifications, we then estimated the TWEAK's joint probability density using a random sample of the 120 conditional chain rule factorizations followed by backward selection of a single conditional specification. For aim 2, we used the TWEAK's conventional scoring algorithm, confirmatory factor analysis, and item response theory to create 3 alternative scores based on traditional scaling methods.

JPD Estimation Using All 120 Possible Conditional Factorizations (‐JPDall120)

Our fully estimated JPD used chain rule factorization, fx1,x2,…,x5=fx1fx2|x1…fx5|x1,…,x4 where fx1,x2,…,x5 is the joint probability density, and x1,x2,…,x5 represent the 5 TWEAK input variables. The chain rule can be applied in 5! (=120) ways, each of which captures a dependency structure. To utilize the information captured by all 120 dependency structures, we estimated each specification separately and averaged them to create the TWEAK‐JPDall120 score. For all the JPD estimations, the most probable combination of scores was “0” on all 5 items. Therefore, lower scores signify severer drinking problems.

JPD Estimation Using a Random Selection of the 120 possible Conditional Factorizations (‐JPDrand4)

In prior work estimating joint probability distributions, we noticed that the bootstrapped standard errors for each person's JPD score was very small (Noorbaloochi et al. 2024). This suggested that a smaller, random sample of dependency structures might adequately approximate the JPD. Calculating a smaller number of potential dependency structures would be considerably faster and easier than estimating all p! alternatives.

To assess this in a progressive forward manner, we randomly selected one of the 120 possible dependency structures and calculated its mean squared distance (the averaged squared difference between individuals' scores and the sample mean). We then compared the mean squared distance of that structure to one created from the average of two randomly selected dependency structures, then three randomly selected dependency structures, and so on, until the difference between subsequent structures' mean squared distances dropped below 5%. We used ChapGPT (www.chatgpt.com) to number and list all 120 unique, chain factorizations and selected among them using a computerized random number generator. Results showed that the mean squared distance of four averaged, randomly selected dependency structures was within 4.2% of three randomly selected dependency structures. JPD Estimation Using Backward Selection (‐JPDback)

In another approach to simplify the JPD's estimation, we used backward selection with maximum likelihood logit analysis to identify the best fitting alternative between fx1|x2,…,x5, fx2|x1,x3,x4,x5, fx3|x1,x2,x4,x5, fx4|x1,x2,x3,x5, or fx5|x1,…,x4 for the 5th term in the chain rule factorization. This was followed by finding the best fitting alternative for the fourth term in the chain rule factorization, and so on, until the best fitting option for all 5 terms had been identified. The best‐fitting JPD estimation was: JPDback=fˆx5fˆx3|x5fˆx4|x3,x5fˆx2|x3,x4,x5fˆx1|x2,x3,x4,x45

Conventional (‐Conv)

Following Russell (Russell 1994), item 1 (Tolerance) was re‐categorized to 0 if the response was less than 6 and to 2 if the response was 6 or more. Item 2 (Worry) was scored as 2 = yes, 0 = no. Items 3–5 were scored as 1 = yes, 0 = no. Responses were then summed to a score ranging from 0 to 7.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (‐CFA)

We used confirmatory factor analysis based on maximum likelihood with missing values and robust standard errors to estimate the latent variable, “high‐risk alcohol use,” where the latent variable = the TWEAK‐CFA score. Bartlett's χ 2 test for sphericity was 834.645, p < 0.0001, and the Kaiser‐Meyer‐Olkin test for sampling adequacy was 0.7934, indicating that the 5 TWEAK inputs were “middling” for factor analysis.

Item Response Theory (‐IRT)

We used a 2‐parameter binary logistic model to estimate each individual's latent trait (theta) based on the TWEAK items' difficulty (a) and discrimination (b), where theta = the TWEAK‐IRT score. This analysis required us to re‐score the Tolerance and Worry items from 2 to 1. Further, the initial 5‐item model failed to converge; inspection showed that this was because responses to item 5 (“Do you sometimes need to cut down on your drinking?”) were highly dependent on responses to items 2 (Worry) and 3 (Eye‐opener)—a violation of local independence. We repeated the analysis with just items 1, 2, 3 and 4. The 4‐item model converged after 5 iterations, with an improvement in fit from the fixed effects model to the full model of ΔLog Likelihood χ 2 = 151.689, p < 0.001. The TWEAK‐IRT score reported here is based on 4 items only.

Statistical Analysis

We used R 4.2.1 and STATA version 18.0 for analyses. To assess the underlying assumptions for the JPD method, we examined the five TWEAK input variables' inter‐item correlations using Spearman's correlation (unidirectionality), Cronbach's alpha (“parallelism” or unidimensionality), and item‐total correlations (co‐directionality). To compare distribution moments for the three JPD approaches, we report summary statistics rounded to the third decimal place. We used Shannon's H to assess for possible information loss across the three JPD scoring approaches. Maximum entropy (H) for the conventionally scored TWEAK sum‐score is 3 bits, and a difference of 0.3 bits or more represents a potentially meaningful reduction in uncertainty. Inter‐score Spearman correlations were used to describe concordance among the three JPD scoring approaches, and non‐parametric Bland‐Altman graphs, to visually examine the limits of agreement among them.

To examine comparability between the three JPD methods and three traditional approaches, we provide two‐way scatterplots and inter‐score Spearman correlations between the conventionally scored TWEAK, the 3 JPD scoring approaches, and the CFA and IRT approaches. As a validity check to ensure all 6 scoring approaches performed as expected, we compared TWEAK scores by any administrative alcohol diagnosis verse no diagnosis within and across gender. In this analysis, to account for skewness, we used Kruskal‐Wallis χ 2 tests. To test for structural dependency in the IRT analysis only, we used the Mantel–Haenszel χ 2 test for gender‐based differential item functioning (DIF). We used a conventional p‐value < 0.05 to denote statistical significance.

Results

JPD Assumptions

All correlations between individual TWEAK items were positive and ranged from 0.37 to 0.76, supporting evidence of unidirectionality. Cronbach's alpha for the 5 TWEAK items was 0.78, suggesting acceptable equivalence of items (unidimensionality). Item‐to‐total score correlations ranged from 0.62 to 0.85, indicating evidence of co‐directionality.

Concordance of the Three TWEAK‐JPD Scoring Approaches

Summary statistics and H for the three JPD scoring approaches are shown in Table 1. For convenience, we also show summary statistics for the three traditional scoring approaches. In terms of the present paper's hypotheses, Table 1 shows that summary statistics for the three TWEAK‐JPD approaches were very similar. Identical H for each JPD approach indicates there was no loss of information across them. Non‐parametric Bland‐Altman graphs (Figure S1) present the limits of agreement, showing that the median difference across JPD approaches was zero with narrow 95% limits of agreement. The 95% limits of agreement were much smaller between the TWEAK‐JPDall120 and TWEAK‐JPDrand4 (0.001 to −0.002), however, than between the TWEAK‐JPDall120 and TWEAK‐JPDback (0.15 to −0.16) and between the TWEAK‐JPDrand4 and TWEAK‐JPDback (0.17 to −0.17). Table 2 (first 3 rows and columns) show that the three TWEAK‐JPD scoring approaches correlated 0.97 to 1.00 (all ps < 0.001).

Criterion‐Related Validity

15.0% of men respondents and 6.0% of women respondents had at least one alcohol‐related diagnosis in their medical record, and 37.4% of men and 24.0% of women had TWEAK‐Conv scores > 2. For men, TWEAK‐Conv scores > 2 had 75.0% sensitivity and 69.3% specificity for any administrative alcohol diagnosis. For women, the TWEAK‐Conv's sensitivity and specificity for any administrative alcohol diagnosis was 50% and 77.7%, respectively.

Table 3 shows that, as hoped, TWEAK‐JPDall120 and TWEAK‐JPDrand4 gender‐specific scores were very similar to one another and remained so after stratifying by alcohol use diagnosis. Compared to their TWEAK‐JPDall120 and TWEAK‐JPDrand4 scores, men's median TWEAK‐JPDback scores tended to be higher. For women with any alcohol use diagnosis, median TWEAK‐JPDback scores were likewise higher than their TWEAK‐JPDall120 and TWEAK‐JPDrand4 scores.

The three TWEAK‐JPD approaches and the three traditional approaches identified statistically significant differences in TWEAK scores by any alcohol use diagnosis in men. None of the JPD approaches identified statistically significant differences in TWEAK scores by alcohol use diagnosis in the women, though the traditional approaches performed as expected.

For men and women with any alcohol use diagnosis, TWEAK scores did not differ significantly by gender (all ps ≥ 0.98). However, among those without an alcohol use diagnosis, men's scores did differ significantly from women's (ps ≤ 0.02 for TWEAK‐Conv, ‐JPDall120, ‐JPDrand4, and ‐JPDback; p = 0.05 for TWEAK‐CFA; and p = 0.005 for TWEAK‐IRT). To see if differential item functioning might explain this finding, we performed a Mantel–Haenszel χ 2 test for DIF. Table 4 shows that men had more than 2 fold higher odds of reporting “≥ 6” to the Tolerance item than did women after controlling for total TWEAK‐IRT scores; however, the result did not quite reach statistical significance (p = 0.07).

Discussion

Results showed that JPD scores generated through a random selection of 4 dependency factorizations or through backward selection of a single factorization produced results very similar to those produced from estimating and averaging all 120 permutations of the chain rule factorization. Obviously, these simplifications are far easier to calculate and make the JPD method much more accessible for index creation, particularly when there are large numbers of input variables.

The high correlation between the three TWEAK‐JPD scoring approaches, the conventional TWEAK scoring algorithm, and the confirmatory factor analysis and item response theory approaches support using the JPD method as an efficient, informative alternative for creating scales or indices. The JPD can be estimated regardless of varying scales or of items' underlying conditional dependencies, making it a more robust approach to some data structures. The JPD also preserves all the information in the data, because it is based on the underlying indicators' total distribution. In contrast, factor analysis scores are based only on items' covariances (which are the second moments of the distribution), and item response theory modeling heavily depends on assuming local independence between items. In the present study, the original, 5‐item TWEAK could not converge using item response modeling, because responses to the “C[K]ut” item depended locally on responses to “Worry” and “Eye‐opener” items.

Although backward selection of a single JPD factorization was our easiest method for estimating a JPD score, there appeared to be some improved correspondence between the TWEAK‐JPDall120 and TWEAK‐JPDrand4 compared to the TWEAK‐JPDback. Progressive, random selection of JPD factorizations may be superior to a single, backward factorization, even if the latter were optimized. In the present paper, to create the TWEAK‐JPDrand4 score, we first identified all 120 potential dependency factorizations of the 5 TWEAK inputs, then randomly selected among them. This approach would not be feasible for indices with larger numbers of inputs. For example, there are more than 2.43 × 1018 potential factorizations for an index with 20 input variables. In such cases, we recommend randomly selecting the order of each chain factorization term. For example, in a 20‐variable index, randomly select among the 20 inputs for the first term, fxa, then randomly select among the 19 remaining inputs for the second term, fxb|xa, and so on.

While our main aim was to identify simpler, easier ways to estimate a joint probability density for index construction, results also shed light onto the TWEAK's test performance. Sensitivity and specificity were considerably lower in this sample relative to the pooled sensitivity and specificity reported by (Santos et al. 2020). This could reflect the different criterion used in others' research. Diagnoses related to alcohol use, as we used, are decidedly under identified in the medical record compared to the true prevalence of alcohol use disorder (Abidi et al. 2018). The TWEAK has much higher sensitivities when mapped against diagnostic interviews, such as Diagnostic Statistical Manual criteria (Chan et al. 1993) or the Composite International Diagnostic Interview for alcohol dependence (Cherpitel 1995; Cremonte et al. 2010). Prior results showing differing test performance across unique populations (e.g., Cherpitel 1995; Bush et al. 2003; Præstegaard et al. 2018) have hinted at structural local dependence in the TWEAK. Although not quite reaching statistical significance, our Mantel‐Haenszel χ 2 test suggested that item 1 (Tolerance) may perform differently by gender. We also identified surface local dependence between items 2, 3 and 5, which was resolved by removing item 5. The 4‐item TWEAK‐IRT appeared to perform as well as all the other scoring approaches and, indeed, correlated 1.00 with the conventionally scored TWEAK.

All the scoring approaches demonstrated criterion‐related validity in the men, but the three JPD approaches did not reach statistical significance in the women. Possibly, this was a Type II error, since effect sizes were in the direction expected, and we had only 12 women with an alcohol use diagnosis. The three traditional approaches performed as expected under the same power considerations; however, the three JPD scores were highly skewed compared to our more traditional approaches, and this latter property may have also increased the risk of Type II error.

The participants in this study are likely representative of other OEF/OIF/OND Veterans filing posttraumatic stress disorder disability claims, but this sample's generalizability to other populations, including pre‐9/11 Veterans, Veterans who are not filing claims, and—especially—non‐Veterans is suspect. Local dependence of TWEAK items may not pertain in other populations and should be explored in future research. The present study emphasized internal validity, which should not have been compromised.

Conclusion

As with prior work showing that the JPD method returns indices highly concordant with those created through CFA and IRT (Noorbaloochi et al. 2024), we showed here that the TWEAK‐JPD versions were highly concordant with the original, conventional TWEAK and with indices created through CFA and IRT. The simplifications we report here should make it easier to adopt the JPD method for future studies. Our data suggest a slight preference for using progressive, random selection of JPD factorizations over an optimized, single, backward factorization, though this should be confirmed in future studies.
