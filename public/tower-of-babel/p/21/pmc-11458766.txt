RF NEMS switches based on graphene for low pull-in voltage and excellent RF performance
Authors: Laifang Zheng, Junsheng Zhang, Huajie Wang, Jijun Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces a novel graphene RF NEMS capacitive switch and conducts an extensive analysis of its RF performance using CST and COMSOL Multiphysics software. The switch’s characteristic impedance matching is accomplished through a coplanar waveguide (CPW) structure, incorporating a dielectric layer topped with conductive graphene beam electrodes. Simulation results reveal that the monolayer graphene RF NEMS switch maintains an insertion loss of 0.003 ~ 0.015 dB and an isolation greater than 40 dB across an ultra-wideband (UWB) frequency range of DC ~ 140 GHz. Moreover, the switch demonstrates a switching time of 4.03 ps at a pull-in voltage of 1 V. The performance of multilayer graphene RF NEMS switches was also evaluated. The study further investigates the operational mechanism of the graphene RF NEMS switch and performs finite element analysis on the proposed design. The graphene RF NEMS switch discussed herein offers significant advantages, including minimal size, lightweight, cost-effectiveness, low pull-in voltage, rapid response time, excellent RF performance, and reduced space consumption in circuitry. Its potential applications span a range from L to F bands, encompassing data storage, communication systems, sensors, remote sensing and military uses.Subject terms: Nanoscience and technology; Optics and photonics; Electronics, photonics and device physics

Introduction

Over the past few decades, significant advancements have been made in silicon integrated circuit technology, primarily driven by the miniaturization of Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistors (MOSFETs)1. This advancement has spurred research across various applications, including sensors, Nanoelectromechanical Systems (NEMS), actuators, and new material technologies2–4. In the communications sector, the burgeoning development of low Earth orbit satellite constellations has escalated research in phased array antennas5, intensifying the demand for high-performance miniaturized devices in RF applications. Notably, RF Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems (MEMS) switches are crucial in these antennas due to their high-frequency (HF) efficacy, offering low insertion loss, minimal power consumption, and superior isolation compared to PIN and FET switches6–8. Compared to PIN and FET switches, RF MEMS switches have advantages such as low insertion loss, low power consumption, and high isolation9. However, metal beam-based RF MEMS switches face challenges with stiction and mechanical stability at HF10. Additionally, their typical pull-in voltage (> 10 V) exceeds the operating voltage of contemporary integrated circuits, limiting their suitability for a range of applications, from mobile communications to advanced radar systems11. The semiconductor industry’s success hinges on balancing device miniaturization with reliable performance12. Consequently, RF NEMS switches have gained popularity in RF and HF microwave communication systems. Their advantages include negligible power consumption, low insertion loss, compact size, lower pull-in voltage, high ON/OFF ratio, minimal leakage current, rapid switching, reduced resistive losses, and enhanced RF performance13–15. These attributes render RF NEMS switches ideal for use in data storage, communication, sensors, and military defense applications16–20.

Current RF NEMS switches are hindered by several drawbacks, including large size, high pull-in voltage, slow switching time, and instability. To address these challenges, two primary strategies have been identified. firstly, optimizing geometric designs through the use of perforations or serpentine structures21,22. secondly, selecting new materials with a low elastic modulus and a high dielectric constant for the suspended beam and dielectric layer, respectively23. In this context, graphene, a carbon-based 2D material, has gained significant interest due to its exceptional electron mobility, ultra-thin profile, substantial current carrying capacity, and low resistivity24–26. When used as the top actuation electrode beam material in RF NEMS switches, graphene not only reduces pull-in voltage and accelerates switching times but also mitigates switch failure due to fracture27,28. Furthermore, high dielectric Hafnium oxide(HfO2) has been proposed as a switch dielectric material, known to enhance RF performance at high frequencies by reducing impurity scattering in graphene and potentially achieving a high capacitance ratio for the switch29,30. Thus, incorporating these materials into RF NEMS switches presents a promising avenue for enhancing their overall performance.

In 1995, Goldsmith et al. pioneered the first generation of RF NEMS switches, characterized by high pull-in voltages and suboptimal RF performance31. Subsequent research has rapidly evolved; notably, in 2009, Milaninia et al. developed a NEMS switch utilizing two polycrystalline graphene membranes, functioning as both the suspended beam and the bottom electrode, with an actuation voltage of 5 V. However, this model exhibited contact failures after limited usage17. In 2013, Mafinejad et al. demonstrated that graphene-based RF M/NEMS switches, with a lower pull-in voltage of 7.7 V and faster actuation, outperformed their metal counterparts which required 35 V32. By 2014, Pankaj et al. had advanced monolayer and multilayer graphene RF NEMS capacitive switches, achieving pull-in voltages below 2 V and switching times between 240 ~ 430 ns. These switches showed insertion losses of 0.01 ~ 0.3dB and 0.01 ~ 0.2dB, and isolations of > 10 dB and > 20 dB for monolayer and multilayer graphene, respectively, across a 1 ~ 60 GHz frequency range27. Moldovan et al. in 2015 conducted comprehensive research on the structure and electrical properties of multilayer graphene capacitive switches, noting an actuation voltage of 10 V and a switching time of 1.56 us. At 6 GHz, the insertion loss was − 1.6 dB, and the isolation − 1.75 dB33. In 2018, Anjum et al. simulated a cantilever beam-style NEMS switch using graphene and molybdenum disulfide, achieving an isolation of -62dB at 7 GHz and an insertion loss of -0.03 dB at 14 GHz, actuated at less than 1 V34. More recently, in 2020, Chaudhary and Mudimela proposed a novel parallel NEMS switch using GO/graphene beams, obtaining insertion losses of 0.03-0.06dB and 0.03 ~ 0.05 dB for monolayer and multilayer GO beams, respectively, and isolations of > 70 dB and > 60 dB, within the 1 ~ 60 GHz range35. Despite these advancements, current RF NEMS switches still grapple with challenges like high drive voltages, slow switching times, large sizes, complex structures, low isolation, and high insertion losses. Furthermore, to date, no RF NEMS switch has been successfully implemented in the ultra-HF communication domain, significantly constraining their potential in the high-demand wireless communication sector for high-performance, miniaturized, broadband devices.

This paper presents the inaugural design and simulation of a graphene RF NEMS capacitive switch, tailored for the DC ~ 140 GHz UWB frequency range, and evaluates its RF performance. The switch employs the CPW structure to ensure impedance matching throughout the entire microwave band, demonstrating robust RF performance across this spectrum. This versatility renders it suitable for a wide array of applications spanning the L to F bands, including data storage, communication systems, sensors, remote sensing and military technology.

Structural design

Figure 1a, b illustrate the 3D schematic and top view of the proposed graphene RF NEMS capacitive switch. Among various feed methods, the CPW feed is particularly favored for designing active integrated circuits due to its coplanar configuration36. Consequently, a CPW transmission line was chosen for the RF NEMS switch structure to optimize RF application compatibility. As depicted in Fig. 1a, the switch comprises a high-purity silicon substrate, a CPW, an HfO2 dielectric layer, and a graphene top electrode conductive film. The high thermal resistance of silicon makes it an ideal substrate, enhancing RF performance in high-temperature settings. The CPW consists of a center conductor and a ground conductor, where the conductivity is σAu = 4.56 × 107 S/m and is considered a perfect conductor for full-wave simulation in a switch. The conductors, with thicknesses of d2 = 0.05 μm and d3 = 0.02 μm, are positioned atop the substrate at a distance of d1 = 1 μm from the bottom, with dimensions P/W/S/W = 1/0.3/0.6/0.3 μm. The HfO2 dielectric layer, situated above the central conductor, has a thickness of d4 = 0.001 μm and a width of W = 0.3 μm, possessing a dielectric constant of 25 for DC isolation of the switch from the CPW central conductor. The graphene top electrode conductive film, acting as a suspended beam, is positioned at a height of g0 = 0.02866 μm above the bottom dielectric layer, anchored on both sides to the ground conductors. Application of a DC voltage across the graphene beam and the central conductor with an RF signal induces electrostatic forces, causing contact and separation. The distinctive feature of graphene beams lies in their electric field effect37, allowing for conductivity adjustment by applying a lateral electric field within the gate structure. This paper explores the relevance of the proposed RF NEMS switch, as depicted in Fig. 2. Figure 2a illustrates the inactive state of the switch, where the beam remains suspended in its original position, maintaining inherent conductivity (σup), thus allowing signal transmission with zero loss. Conversely, Fig. 2b demonstrates the activated state: the electrostatic force pulls the beam downwards, establishing contact with the RF transmission line and partially with the bottom dielectric. In this configuration, a segment of the graphene beam [region 2 in Fig. 2b] is influenced by the vertical electric field from the bottom electrode, altering its conductivity (σdown). The conductivity in regions 1 and 3 remains approximately equivalent to the inactive state (σup). This mechanism resembles a graphene Field-Effect Transistor (FET), where the gate modulates the channel current in “ON” and “OFF” states, with the key distinction that the gate does not entirely encompass the channel.

Electromagnetic modeling

The parallel capacitor switch can be modeled through simulation using two short segments of a transmission line, coupled with a lumped CLR to represent the suspended beam. The shunt impedance of the switch is defined by Eq. (8)46.

The capacitance C in the equation varies based on the switch’s position, representing either the up-state or down-state capacitance. The LC series resonant frequency of the switch is approximated using Eq. (9)46.

The CLR model functions as a capacitor below the resonant frequency and transitions to behaving like an inductor above it. Precisely at the resonant frequency, the model operates as a resistive circuit. In the up-state position of the switch, the inductance has minimal impact on performance; however, its role becomes significant in the down-state.

The fundamental operation of the parallel capacitor switch involves controlling signal switching by manipulating the coupling capacitance between the dielectric layer and the electrodes. Figure 3 illustrates the equivalent circuit diagram depicting this RF NEMS switch’s working principle. Without any drive voltage, the switch remains in an “ON” state, as depicted in Fig. 3a. Here, the coupling capacitance remains inactive, allowing the signal to flow from input to output, predominantly characterized by insertion loss. Conversely, as Fig. 3b demonstrates, activation of the coupling capacitor transitions the switch to a “OFF” state. In this state, the dielectric layer contacts the beam, forming a coupling capacitance and directing the signal flow from input to ground47. This configuration is characterized by its ability to transmit AC signals while isolating DC signals. As the RF signal is channeled through the coupling capacitor to the ground, minimizing signal leakage or interference is crucial, assessable by the degree of isolation. Consequently, the magnitude of the coupling capacitance critically influences both isolation and insertion loss.

Experiment and discussion

In the study of RF NEMS switches, isolation and insertion loss are key performance metrics. Accurate assessment of these metrics is essential for gauging switch performance. This research analyzed the monolayer graphene RF NEMS switch structure across a DC ~ 140 GHz frequency range using S-parameter analysis in CST software, as depicted in Fig. 4. Our simulations indicated that the insertion loss ranged between 0.003 and 0.015 dB, and isolation was greater than 40 dB (Fig. 4a), demonstrating the switch’s effective RF performance. Additionally, the impact of graphene thickness on switch performance was explored. Comparative analysis of multilayer and monolayer graphene RF NEMS switches (Fig. 4b, c) revealed that multilayer switches exhibit a similar insertion loss range (0.003 to 0.017 dB). However, the lower insertion loss in monolayer switches correlates with their reduced up-state capacitance. Moreover, the multilayer switch’s maximum isolation reached only 27 dB, significantly lower than the monolayer, attributable to the thicker graphene layers and lower down-state capacitance in the latter, resulting in higher isolation. Overall, comprehensive evaluations suggest superior performance of the monolayer graphene RF NEMS switch.

To elucidate the operation of the monolayer graphene NEMS switch, Fig. 5 depicts the electric field and surface current distributions in its “OFF” state. The electric field, polarized along the y-axis, is represented in intensity by the color map. Figure 5a offers a top view of the switch, highlighting the electric field’s maximum intensity at one side of the central conductor, diminishing along the graphene suspended beam. The electrostatic force draws the suspended beam to the RF transmission line, transferring the electric field from one side of the central conductor downward through the dielectric layer, perpendicular to the beam, and reducing the field on the conductor’s opposite side. Figure 5b provides a side view, showing an increasing electric field strength from bottom to top, focused beneath the central conductor and beam. Figure 5c displays a top view of the switch’s surface current distribution, offering an intuitive perspective on current flow. The results show an even current distribution through the switch, with minor variations due to the RF signal’s varying paths across the beam. In the off state, as the electron beam moves downward, the majority of the current follows suit, spreading toward the edges as depicted in Fig. 5d. This ensures the switch’s stable operation.

During extended use, graphene films in RF NEMS switches may experience fatigue and stress concentration, to evaluate the reliability and practicality of the proposed structure, finite element analysis was performed on the NEMS switch, utilizing a monolayer graphene beam in COMSOL Multiphysics software. This process entailed both mechanical and electrical modeling of the switch, along with the simulation of surface Von Mises stress and total surface displacement, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Research indicates that graphene possesses a fracture strength exceeding 100 GPa, qualifying it as an ideal material for the upper electrode. Consequently, applying a pull-in voltage of 1 V to the graphene beam generates an electrostatic force, resulting in its downward movement. The data reveal that the beam’s average stress is 30 GPa, and the maximum stress reaches 70 GPa, remaining below the fracture threshold and thus confirming the beam’s feasibility, as shown in Fig. 6a. Figure 6b displays the strain tensor in the x-y component of the beam, with the highest values at its sides. Moreover, applying a 13 nN upward force to the switch electrode causes a 0.03 μm displacement, as demonstrated in Fig. 6c, This indicates that the switch can operate continuously and stably.

The simulated performance of the RF NEMS switch under ideal conditions has been previously discussed. In practice, however, dimensional variations during production can significantly affect the switch’s performance. This analysis focuses on the impact of the length and width of the monolayer graphene beam, a critical component, on the mechanical and electrical performance of the switch. To ensure clarity, each dimension was analyzed separately, holding other parameters constant. Figure 7a demonstrates that an increase in the beam’s width (W) from 0.3 to 0.7 μm results in diminished isolation performance within a DC ~ 140 GHz frequency range. Figure 7b explores the relationship between switching time, pull-in voltage, and beam width, indicating that the switch with a 0.3 μm width has a switching time approximately 10 µs faster and requires 0.5 V less pull-in voltage than the 0.7 μm variant. Conversely, Fig. 7c shows that the beam’s length (S) has a negligible impact on isolation. Nonetheless, a length of 0.6 μm provides the best switching response time and drive voltage, as Fig. 7d illustrates. Consequently, given the complexity and cost of the fabrication process, the optimal dimensions for the graphene beam are W = 0.3 μm and S = 0.6 μm.

In both capacitive and cantilever configurations, constructing a suspended structure is pivotal for fabricating graphene RF NEMS switches due to its ability to diminish residual impurities and enhance RF performance. This suspended structure of graphene is fabricated via a transfer method. A crucial step in this process is the transference of CVD graphene from a Cu substrate to a perforated SiO2 substrate, facilitated by polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). Figure 8 illustrates the schematic process of creating the graphene suspended structure using this method. The fabrication involves a six-step procedure. Initially, singlelayer graphene is formed on a pretreated Cu foil within a quartz tube furnace, utilizing a 2:1 methane/hydrogen mixture at 400 mTorr pressure for 20 min, as depicted in Fig. 8a, b. Subsequently, the graphene undergoes patterning into stripe-like configurations through ultraviolet lithography-oxygen plasma etching (UVL-OPE), presented in Fig. 8c. The third phase involves applying a nitrocellulose coating, then removing Cu with N-Methyl 2-pyrrolidinone (NMP), as shown in Fig. 8d. In the fourth phase, the graphene is transferred to PDMS, with the nitrocellulose layer being eliminated using ammonium persulfate (APS), as seen in Fig. 8e. The penultimate step entails positioning the PDMS with graphene onto a 1 cm² perforated SiO2/Si substrate, as illustrated in Fig. 8f. The final stage involves gently detaching the PDMS on a transfer stage to yield the graphene suspended structure, detailed in Fig. 8g.

Schematic diagram of the process for preparing the graphene suspended structure using the transfer method. (a) CVD graphene on Cu base, (b) Patterning of graphene, (c) Coating with a layer of nitrocellulose, (d) Removal of Cu and placement on PDMS, (e) Placement on the target substrate, (f) Peeling off PDMS.

Figure 9 illustrates the five-step bottom-up fabrication process of the proposed graphene RF NEMS capacitive switch. The initial step involves depositing a silicon (Si) substrate, depicted in Fig. 9a. The next phase includes electroplating the center conductor of the coplanar waveguide (CPW), employing DC magnetron sputtering (DC MS) to apply a 2 nm titanium (Ti) and 18 nm gold (Au) seed layer onto the Si substrate. This is followed by spin-coating a uniform photoresist layer (AZ5214), then exposing and developing it using a photomask and developer solution (DS). Figure 9b–e show the patterning of the Ti/Au seed layer with oxygen plasma etching (OPE) and subsequent removal of the photoresist in acetone to form the CPW center conductor. The third step involves depositing a hafnium dioxide (HfO2) dielectric layer using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD), as shown in Fig. 9f. For the fourth step, the process continues with DC MS to sputter a 30 nm Au layer onto the Si substrate, followed by spin-coating and developing AZ5214. The Au layer is patterned using OPE and the photoresist is removed in acetone, forming the CPW ground conductor as illustrated in Fig. 9g–j. The final step transfers the graphene suspended structure detailed in Fig. 8 onto the CPW, culminating in the graphene RF NEMS capacitive switch.

Due to the similarity in driving methods and structures between RF MEMS and RF NEMS switches, NEMS can be considered as an advancement of MEMS switches. Consequently, the testing equipment used for MEMS switches is also applicable to NEMS switches. Therefore, we introduce the testing of RF performance, driving voltage, and response time for these switches.

The primary testing equipment for NEMS switches includes a probe station, voltage amplifier, vector network analyzer, constant current source, digital signal generator, and oscilloscope. The principle of testing insertion loss and isolation involves amplifying the constant current source’s voltage with a voltage amplifier and applying it to the NEMS switch via the DC probe of the probe station, thereby controlling the switch’s on and off states. Figure 10 presents the schematic and actual diagrams of the test system. The methods for testing the switch’s driving voltage and RF performance are essentially the same. The principle of testing response time is based on a square wave signal generated by a digital signal generator, amplified by a voltage amplifier, and applied to the driving electrode pad of the NEMS switch through a DC probe. The constant current source’s output end is connected to the switch’s input end, with the output end grounded. The oscilloscope’s CH1 and CH2 respectively monitor the electrical signals of the digital signal generator and the constant current source, as shown in Fig. 11.

Conclusion

This paper introduces a novel graphene RF NEMS capacitive switch and presents an extensive analysis of its RF performance within the UWB frequency range of DC ~ 140 GHz. The monolayer graphene RF NEMS switch is characterized by its low pull-in voltage, rapid switching time, and superior RF performance, contrasting with the comparatively inferior performance of multilayer graphene RF NEMS switches. The investigation into the working mechanism reveals that the electric field strength is highest near the central conductor, diminishing on either side of the suspended graphene beam. In the “OFF”state, the current flows downward through the dielectric layer of the beam, ensuring the switch’s stable operation. Finite element analysis indicates that the beam experiences an average stress of 30 GPa, and a force of 13 nN applied to the switch electrode results in a displacement of 0.03 µm. The graphene RF NEMS switch proposed here is versatile, applicable to a range of uses from L to F bands, including data storage, communication, sensors, remote sensing and military applications.
