An interpretable multimodal machine learning model for predicting malignancy of thyroid nodules in low-resource scenarios
Authors: Fuqiang Ma, Fengchang Yu, Xinyu Gu, Lihua Zhang, Zhilin Lu, Lele Zhang, Herong Mao, Nan Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThyroid nodules (TNs) represent a prevalent clinical issue in endocrinology. The diagnostic process for malignant TNs typically involves a three-stage detection: the function detection, color ultrasound (CU) detection and biopsy. Early identification is crucial for effective management of malignant TNs.MethodsThis study developed a multimodal network for classifying CU images and thyroid function (TF) test data. Specifically, the PubMedClIP model was employed to extract visual features from CU images, generating a 512-dimensional feature vector. This vector was subsequently concatenated with five indicators of TF tests, as well as gender and age information, to construct a comprehensive representation. The combined representation was then fed into a downstream ML classifier, where we evaluated seven models, including AdaBoost, Random Forest, and Logistic Regression.ResultsAmong the seven ML models evaluated, the AdaBoost classifier demonstrated the highest overall performance, surpassing other classifiers in terms of area under the curve (AUC), F1, accuracy, and coordinate attention (CA) metrics. The incorporation of visual features extracted from CU images using PubMedCLIP further enhanced the model’s performance. Feature importance analysis revealed that laboratory indicators such as free thyroxine (FT4), free triiodothyronine (FT3), and clip_feature_184 were the most influential clinical variables. Additionally, the integration of PubMedCLIP significantly improved the model’s capacity to accurately classify data by leveraging both clinical and imaging information.ConclusionThe proposed PubMedCLIP-based multimodal framework, which jointly utilizes ultrasound imaging features and clinical laboratory data, demonstrated superior diagnostic performance in differentiating benign from malignant TNs. This approach offers a promising tool for individualized risk assessment and clinical decision support, potentially facilitating more precise and personalized protocols for patients with TNs.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Thyroid nodules, Thyroid function, Thyroid cancer, Multimodal machine learning, PubMedCLIP

Background

Thyroid cancer (TC) represents the most common endocrine malignancy [1]. Globally, TC incidence has shown a consistent increase across North America, Europe, Asia, and Australia [2]. Research indicates that 7%−15% of TC cases originate from thyroid nodules (TNs) [3], which are detectable by palpation in only 4%−7% of the affected individuals. With advances in ultrasound technology, TNs are now identified in 50%−70% of the general population [4]. This underscores the clinical significance of accurate risk stratification for malignant TNs [5]. Currently, diagnostic approaches encompass clinical evaluation, laboratory tests, imaging studies, and biopsy [6]. However, these methods remain subject to interobserver variability influenced by clinicians’ experience, cognitive bias, and health conditions.

Recent years have witnessed growing clinical interest in applying machine learning (ML) to thyroid disease (TD) studies using electronic medical records (EMRs), with promising results [7–11]. Meanwhile, artificial intelligence (AI) has become a key tool for TD prediction. However, current studies predominantly focus on imaging analysis [12–15], often neglecting clinically relevant data like thyroid function (TF) tests and nodule characteristics [16–18]. Notably, only 12.35% of studies incorporate both data types [19], despite the standard clinical practice in most parts of China requiring both laboratory tests and imaging examinations for comprehensive evaluation.

In clinical practice, acquiring diagnostic data is often challenging due to multiple factors such as ethical restrictions, patient privacy concerns, and limited case availability. Even when adequate data exists, traditional supervised learning models demand expert annotation, a time-consuming and labor-intensive process that impedes diagnostic model development. What’s more, obtaining data for certain rare diseases remains rather difficult. It is, therefore, particularly crucial to enhance the performance of diagnostic models in low-resource settings.

The contrastive learning paradigm provides a promising solution for developing robust diagnostic models under data constraints. This approach utilizes publicly available data and their corresponding descriptions to autonomously learn data-description relationships. A prominent example is OpenAI’s CLIP model [20], which employs dual encoders: a visual encoder (ResNet or Vision Transformer) for images and a textual encoder (Transformer) for text. It learns to connect images with text through training on extensive image-text pairs, CLIP optimizes feature embeddings by maximizing similarity for matched pairs while minimizing it for unmatched pairs [21]. After training, CLIP can be used to extract a feature vector from images. This is done by passing the image through the model’s image encoder, which generates a compact representation capturing what is in the image. This feature vector can then be used for tasks like image classification, search, or comparison-without need for additional training. This architecture demonstrates exceptional multimodal understanding and zero-shot learning capabilities [22].

In medical applications, CLIP has achieved state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance in answering medical questions [23, 24], offering new possibilities for medical image representation learning. Our study adapts this framework by developing a medical CLIP variant that integrates CU images to improve the TC detection performance.

Although the ML models are effective with their complicated but powerful algorithms, limitations still exist due to the difficulty of direct interpretation that hinders the integration of AI technologies with clinical practice [25]. Therefore, the ability to appropriately interpret the output of prediction models is so significant to generate proper user trust, give insights on how to improve these models, and reinforce understanding of the modeling process [26].Being able to characterize how the parameters contribute to models, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) can be used to enhance the trust of medical professionals [27]. To date, few studies have adopted clinical data and images to predict the benign or malignant nature of TNs, and the adopted SHAP method has exhibited its unique superiority in interpreting the prediction models.

This study aims to develop a CLIP-based model for predicting malignant TNs by integrating patients’ clinical data and color ultrasound (CU) images at their first visits. The model is designed to assist in TD diagnosis and support clinical decision-making, with a potential to enhance the prediction accuracy while reducing healthcare costs.

Methods

Participants of the study

The study cohort comprised 672 patients diagnosed with TNs at Hubei Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine between January 2018 and June 2022, including 348 surgically confirmed TC cases and 324 randomly selected TN patients. Participants had a mean age of 44.26 ± 12.47 years (range:13–78) with a male-to-female ratio of 1:3.94 (136 males, 536 females). The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee (HBZY2023-C27-02) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, to strictly maintain the patients’ confidentiality. However, participants’ consents are unnecessary, since the data we utilized in this study were anonymous and all patients’ information was de-identified at the point of collection. The overall study design is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram of the model design

Inclusion criteria

The included patients should meet the diagnostic criteria of TNs and TC designated by “Contemporary Management of Thyroid Nodules” [28], and ensure an absence of other serious internal diseases.

Exclusion criteria

The excluded patients are the pregnant and lactating women, and those with acute infections, complicated with the immune system and nervous system disorders, and incomplete follow-up data.

Data collection and processing

Firstly, patients’ EMRs were retrieved from the Information Department of Hubei Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Then, 672 patients’ clinical data were collected, including their demographic characteristics (gender, age), five TF tests at first visits (FT3, FT4, TSH, TgAb, TPOAb), CU images of thyroid, and pathological reports of surgery. The data validity assessment was performed as follows: the original data were cross-checked by two individuals, and researchers randomly selected 5% of the processed data for comparison afterwards.

Model development in low-resource scenarios

Due to the limited availability of the patient data and relatively low incidence of malignancy among patients with TD, acquiring large-scale, representative datasets remains a major challenge in clinical research. Additionally, ethical review processes often impose further constraints on data collection. These factors place our study within the context of low-resource scenarios. In such situations, making full use of multimodal data becomes both a practical and effective strategy.

Although ML models can achieve acceptable performance on structured numerical data such as TF test results, this success does not extend to visual modalities like conventional ultrasound images, even with limited sample sizes. Both convolutional neural network (CNN)-based and transformer-based architectures typically require large amounts of training data to achieve robust generalization, and their performance tends to degrade significantly when trained on limited CU image datasets.

To address this limitation, we employed the PubMedCLIP pretrained [23] model to extract visual features from CU images. PubMedCLIP is an adaptation of the CLIP framework [29], pretrained on a large collection of medical images sourced from PubMed publications. It has demonstrated strong zero-shot classification capabilities across various medical imaging tasks. In this study, we used PubMedCLIP image encoder (the structure is defined in [30])as a fixed feature extractor by removing its final classification layer and extracting features from the last hidden layer.

Specifically, as shown in Fig. 2, each CU image was passed through the PubMedCLIP model to generate a 512-dimensional feature vector. Meanwhile, the patients’ TF test results, along with age and gender, were encoded into a 7-dimensional numerical vector. These two vectors were then concatenated (joining two vector end-to-end to form a new one without overlap) into a single 519-dimensional feature vector, which served as input to the downstream ML classifier for predicting malignancy (cancer vs. non-cancer).

Diagram of the model design

Model validation

Data were randomly split into a trained dataset (324 cases) and a validated dataset (348 cases). Using the 519-dimensional feature vectors, we developed prediction models with binary classification labels (1 for TC, 0 for TN) based on surgical pathology. Missing TF values were imputed using median values, while gender was coded categorically. Seven ML algorithms were utilized to predict the nature of TNs, including Gradient Boosting, Neural Network, Random Forest, Logistic Regression (LR), Naive Bayes, AdaBoost, and SVM. To assess the reliability of these models, several common evaluation metrics were employed, such as area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), Accuracy, F1, Precision and Recall. Moreover, a five-fold cross-validation was conducted to verify the prediction model [31].

Features selection and model interpretation

ML model interpretability still remains challenging. To address this, we employed SHAP to rank feature importance and improve model transparency to solve the “black box” problem [26]. The SHAP values facilitated feature selection by ranking their predictive contributions [25, 32]. Additionally, we utilized CLIP to jointly encode textual and visual data, optimizing their feature alignment and enabling analysis of CLIP’s influence on multimodal representations.

Statistical analysis

All data in this study were statistically analyzed using SPSS (Version 25.0). Categorical data were described as case numbers (%), and comparisons between groups were made using the Chi-square test. Continuous data of normal distribution were analyzed using the t-test and presented as “mean ± standard deviation” (X ± S), while those deviating from the normal distribution were presented as median and interquartile range (M(IQR)), using the Mann-Whitney U test to make comparisons between groups. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The final model selection

The adding of 512-dimensional CLIP features enhanced the model performance, with AUC values ranging from 0.548 to 0.732 (Table 3). Notably, the CLIP+LR model showed significant improvements over LR alone: 9.8% increase in AUC, 9.6% in accuracy, 8% in F1, 9.2% in Precision, and 6.5% in Recall. These results demonstrate CLIP’s ability to enhance algorithmic performance. The CLIP+LR combination achieved the highest AUC (0.732) and F1 among all tested models, so it was selected as the ultimate prediction model. Corresponding AUC curves are displayed in Fig. 3C.

The selection of AUC as the primary evaluation metric in Table 4 warrants clarification. In clinical deep learning applications, AUC offers distinct advantages over threshold-dependent metrics (Precision, Recall, F1) for three key reasons: its robustness to class imbalance, comprehensive assessment across all classification thresholds, and better reflection of clinical utility where misclassification costs vary. Furthermore, AUC enables direct model comparisons and provides a more complete representation of discriminative ability.

Model interpretation

To gain insights into the relative contribution of each feature in the proposed model, we employed the SHAP values, a game theory-based method for interpreting ML models, to quantify and visualize feature importance. In Fig. 3, subfigures a and b depict the model performance and feature contributions solely based on TF test data, whereas subfigures c and d illustrate the results after incorporating visual features extracted from CU images using the CLIP-based model.

As shown in subfigures a and c, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve demonstrates improved discriminative performance following the integration of CLIP-derived image features. This observation aligns with the quantitative results presented in Table 2 (model without CLIP features) and Table 3 (model with CLIP features).

Further comparison of subfigures b and d reveals changes in feature importance distribution. Prior to the inclusion of CLIP features, there was greater variability in the influence of individual TF parameters on the model’s predictions, as evidenced by the larger differences in bar widths representing the SHAP values. In other words, the model placed disproportionately higher weight on certain laboratory indicators.

However, after integrating the image-derived features, the importance of FT4 and FT3 remained relatively unchanged, while contributions of the remaining features became more evenly distributed (i.e., reduced variation in the SHAP value magnitudes across other variables). This pattern suggests that the addition of CU image features provides the model with complementary diagnostic information, thereby reducing its dependency on a limited number of biochemical markers and enhancing the overall classification robustness.

Discussion

TC has emerged as a significant global public health concern in recent decades. Effective TC prevention requires integrated clinical and public health strategies to reduce unnecessary patient risks. Accurate differentiation between benign and malignant TNs is crucial for optimal management of TD, with clinical consensus serving as a key benchmark for early malignant TNs detection [34].

In China, TNs diagnosis typically combines blood tests and imaging examinations, consistent with the clinical diagnostic procedures of our study. Current predictions regarding the nature of TNs are primarily based on the unimodal imaging data, such as CU images [35], computerized tomography (CT) images [36], and single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) images [37]. Only few studies would utilize the clinical data, and even fewer would make a combined use of CU images and TF data [14]. Despite the impressive capabilities of machine visual functions for medical image interpretation, a significant shift that should be considered in the future is how to transcend the limitations of the unimodal tasks, such as the image-based tasks, and how to elevate the machine capability so that input modes like texts will be included to lay a solid foundation for the multimodal AI [38].Previous studies have shown that multimodal ML (MMML) model outperforms the unimodal ML model, and the more complete modal types would contribute to better performances [39].

However, current mainstream multimodal models for medical image processing still primarily rely on supervised learning based on the labeled data, requiring a large number of labeled data for training, which is difficult to meet in the medical field due to hard-won annotations and rare diseases. The self-supervised learning techniques, including contrastive and generative learning, indicate hopes of overcoming the challenge faced with the limited labeled datasets in medical AI [40].

The CLIP model, trained on a dataset of 400 million image-text pairs collected from the Internet using natural language description of images, has trained a neural network model with a profound understanding of both images and texts [29]. Studies have demonstrated that training with text-image pairs can generate models with excellent transfer and generalization capabilities based on traditional visual tasks, which can achieve favorable performance on different downstream tasks with minor adjustments [41].

Collecting the training data used for the CLIP model is a significant cost for most healthcare institutions. This study leverages the pre-trained contrastive learning model to incorporate the limited imaging data into tabular data-driven ML models commonly used by most medical institutions, and demonstrates effectiveness of the approach in experiments. More importantly, the CLIP model’s training data is not limited to specific diseases, indicating that the proposed method in this study can also be effective in disease prediction in other low-resource scenarios.

The results of our study demonstrated that the AdaBoost algorithm achieved superior predictive accuracy for TC detection, and incorporating CLIP features significantly modified the model computations compared to that of using TF data alone. Notably, the LR model with CLIP integration showed optimal performance in both AUC and F1 metrics, consistent with our preliminary findings.

This study investigates multimodal data integration to enhance the ML model performance for TNs classification in resource-constrained settings. Our MMML approach combining CU images and clinical data achieved superior performance (AUROC=0.732) compared to unimodal models using either data type alone, or with significant improvements in F1 scores. To our knowledge, this represents the first application of CLIP-enhanced ML models for TN and TC differentiation.

Apart from the CLIP model for predicting the nature of TNs, our study also used the model to analyze interpretability. Since it is difficult for clinicians to accept a prediction model that cannot be directly interpreted and understood, we used SHAP to explain outputs of the final model. Based on the SHAP values, feature importance allows researchers to further explore the “black box” of many ML algorithms and understand how they work and which features are crucial in providing accurate predictions [26, 42]. The pooled SHAP analysis in this study showed that FT4, FT3, and TSH were the most important factors in the LR model. Similar to our findings, some studies reported that lower FT3 and FT4 levels are associated with a higher risk of TNs [43, 44], while some considered no correlation between FT3, FT4, and TC [17], or a nonlinear relationship between them. Some other studies found that patients with higher TSH levels suffered a significantly higher rate of TC [45]. Through adding the CLIP features, results showed that they affected the model’s computation, and FT4, FT3, and clip_feature_184 were the pivotal contributing factors.

Our proposed method has the following advantages: First, it aligns with the current TC detection process, utilizing TF tests and CU imaging data. Second, by incorporating the CLIP pretrained model, it overcomes the challenge of acquiring data for building specialized models, thereby saving the time spent on manual annotation. Third, the comparative experiments demonstrate that the proposed method outperforms traditional ML methods and image classification algorithms in terms of AUC, indicating that the provided classifier in this study exhibits superior overall performance across different thresholds, which is crucial for medical diagnosis as it allows doctors to flexibly adjust the threshold based on specific situations. It also indicates that the classifier is more robust against the imbalanced datasets, making it particularly suitable for the low prevalence of cases in the population. Moreover, interpretation based on the importance of the SHAP value features can help medical professionals better understand decision-making and then strengthen their trust in the ML models. Last but not the least, by providing a tool for predicting the malignancy of TNs, this study supports patients in expressing their preferences and values, and may promote shared decision-making between patients and physicians [46].

This study is also subject to several limitations. Firstly, while our contrastive learning model demonstrates robust zero-shot capabilities comparable to CLIP, its performance after fine-tuning slightly trails proprietary models trained on larger, curated datasets. Secondly, despite achieving superior results to the existing contrastive methods, our evaluation was limited by dataset scale. Thirdly, the absence of established sample size standards for the ML-based prediction models presents challenges, as our single-center cohort from Hubei Province, though rigorously validated, may not fully represent broader populations. These factors warrant future multi-center validation to assess generalizability.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the multimodal integration of PubMedCLIP with clinical parameters significantly enhanced the classification accuracy of TNs. The model demonstrated robust predictive performance, with TF markers (FT4, FT3) and imaging features (clip_feature_184) identified as the key determinants. These findings may have suggested the potential clinical value of this integrated approach for personalized risk assessment and real-world decision-making in the future management of TNs.
