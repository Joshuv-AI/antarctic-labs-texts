Aptamer-functionalized tetrahedral framework nucleic acid delivery of siBhlhe22 for repairing osteoporotic bone defects via dual modulation of PI3K-Akt signaling and purine metabolism
Authors: Qiaonan Ye, Long Bai, Yue Gao, Shiyi Zeng, Zhiyuan Zhang, Yong Li, Qianke Tao, Maorui Zhang, Yunfeng Lin, Jingang Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoporosis is characterized by an imbalance between bone formation and resorption, and the dysregulated differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) plays a central role. Our previous study identified Bhlhe22 as a key negative regulator of osteogenic differentiation; here, we further validate its role in osteoporotic BMSCs (OP-BMSCs) and investigate its therapeutic potential. Knocking down Bhlhe22 enhanced osteogenesis by upregulating key osteogenic markers (RUNX2, ALP, OPN) and suppressing the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. To enable targeted delivery of siBhlhe22, we designed an aptamer-functionalized tetrahedral framework nucleic acid-based nanocarrier (Apt19S-tFNA-siBhlhe22, ATS). The ATS system exhibited well-defined nanostructure, excellent biocompatibility, and high cellular uptake efficiency in OP-BMSCs. In vitro, ATS-mediated Bhlhe22 knockdown significantly promoted osteogenic differentiation and matrix mineralization. This effect depended on the inhibition of PI3K-Akt signaling, as demonstrated by rescue experiments using the agonist Recilisib. Non-targeted metabolomics revealed that osteoporosis was associated with disruption of nucleotide and purine metabolism, which was effectively reversed by ATS treatment. Functional studies confirmed that intact purine metabolism, which was essential for ATP production and redox balance, was required for the pro-osteogenic effect of ATS. In an osteoporotic rat bone defect model, ATS delivered via GelMA hydrogel promoted bone regeneration. This was accompanied by in vivo suppression of PI3K-Akt signaling and upregulation of OCN and OPN. Our findings establish a novel gene therapy strategy that targets Bhlhe22 to simultaneously modulate pro-osteogenic signaling and metabolic reprogramming, offering a promising anabolic approach for osteoporosis treatment.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04706-6.Keywords: Tetrahedral framework nucleic acids, Osteoporosis, Bone regeneration, Bhlhe22, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, Purine metabolism, Osteogenic differentiation

Background

Osteoporosis (OP) is a common skeletal disease characterized by systemic bone loss and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, presenting a significant global public health challenge, especially among elderly and postmenopausal women [1, 2]. Pathological fractures caused by osteoporosis have a limited self-repair capacity, and the clinical reconstruction of large bone defects remains a major unsolved problem [3, 4]. Currently, autologous and allogeneic bone grafts are the primary methods for repairing bone defects, aiming to create a microenvironment that supports cell proliferation and differentiation to facilitate bone healing [5–9]. However, these approaches are associated with limitations such as limited donor supply, trauma from secondary surgeries, risk of disease transmission, immune rejection, and potential contamination, all of which significantly impact patient compliance [10–13].

Bone tissue engineering, which combines cells, bioactive factors, and scaffold materials, offers a new direction for developing biomimetic bone substitutes. Nevertheless, the transplantation of exogenous cells still encounters challenges including complex procedures, contamination risks, and potential tumorigenicity [14–17]. Given the limited number of endogenous stem cells at bone injury sites, there is a pressing need to develop ideal bone repair materials that can efficiently recruit and induce endogenous stem cells to promote in situ osteogenesis [18–21]. Although various targeting molecules, such as antibodies, chemokines, growth factors, and peptides, have been used for stem cell recruitment, the limited number of in situ homing mesenchymal stem cells and nonspecific chemotaxis remain key bottlenecks restricting bone repair efficacy [22–32].

In recent years, aptamers, short oligonucleotide strands targeting extracellular ligands, have become ideal tools for specific cell recognition and recruitment, due to their high affinity, strong specificity, low molecular weight, good stability, low immunogenicity, and ease of chemical modification [33, 34]. They have demonstrated a wide range of potential in areas such as tumor diagnosis and treatment, cell imaging, and drug delivery [35, 36]. In tissue regeneration, aptamers are designed to specifically recognize and capture marrow mesenchymal stem cells [37, 38]. Among various aptamers, DNA aptamer 19 S (Apt19S) has emerged as a promising candidate for bone regeneration applications owing to its specific affinity for alkaline phosphatase (ALPL), a critical marker prominently expressed on the surface of osteogenically-primed mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) [39]. This distinct property positions Apt19S as a valuable tool for overcoming the significant challenge of inadequate endogenous stem cell homing to sites of bone injury. Thus, using Apt19S to improve the ability of scaffold materials to recruit MSCs in situ is a promising way to promote bone regeneration [40, 41].

The primary pathological mechanism behind osteoporosis is an imbalance between bone formation by osteoblasts and bone resorption by osteoclasts, leading to net bone loss [42–44]. Bone marrow stem cells (BMSCs), which are precursors of osteoblasts, are crucial in bone regeneration. However, under osteoporotic conditions, BMSCs exhibit a pathological differentiation bias toward adipogenesis rather than osteogenesis, thereby impairing bone formation and contributing to bone marrow fat accumulation [45–47]. Therefore, effectively recruiting MSCs and guiding their differentiation to the osteogenic lineage is crucial for successful bone regeneration. Developing new therapeutic strategies to precisely regulate osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs represents a major clinical challenge.

Gene therapy, especially RNA interference technology, is a powerful tool for precisely regulating cellular function and treating a wide range of diseases [48]. In the context of osteoporosis, knocking down key negative regulators of osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs is seen as a highly promising therapeutic approach [49, 50]. We have confirmed in previous studies that Basic Helix-Loop-Helix Family Member E22 (BHLHE22), a basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factor, is significantly upregulated in BMSCs and negatively regulates osteogenic differentiation by inhibiting PI3K-Akt signaling pathway [51]. The PI3K-Akt signaling pathway is a key regulator in cell survival, proliferation, and differentiation, and its role in determining the fate of BMSCs is intricate and context-specific [52]. Some studies suggest that activating PI3K-Akt might lead to a pro-osteogenic effect, while others indicate that its dysregulated or excessive activity in pathological conditions could impair osteogenic differentiation, thus emphasizing the need to clearly define its role in specific microenvironments [53, 54]. Concurrently, cellular differentiation is an energy-demanding process that requires metabolic reprogramming to provide biosynthetic precursors and energy [55]. Purine metabolism, a core part of nucleotide metabolism, provides building blocks for DNA/RNA synthesis and generates ATP, the direct energy currency for cellular activities [56]. Metabolic dysregulation, especially in energy metabolism, is a hallmark of age-related diseases like osteoporosis and directly affects BMSC function [57]. However, it is currently unexplored whether Bhlhe22 influences BMSCs’ osteogenic differentiation by modulating the PI3K-Akt pathway or cellular energy metabolism in the context of osteoporotic bone defect repair.

Despite their potential, clinically implementing siRNA therapies—exemplified by siBhlhe22—faces numerous delivery obstacles, including structural instability, susceptibility to enzymatic degradation, insufficient targeting precision, and limited cellular uptake efficiency [58, 59]. Thus, there’s a significant need to develop multifunctional carriers that can protect siBhlhe22 from nuclease-mediated degradation and achieve controlled release. DNA nanotechnology offers an innovative solution [60]. Tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs), a class of self-assembled DNA nanostructures, have garnered significant attention due to their exceptional biocompatibility, high cellular uptake efficiency, structural programmability, uniform molecular weight, and controllable size and morphology [61]. These tFNAs are typically constructed using Watson-Crick base pairing principles in a one-pot annealing method, demonstrating strong performance in tissue regeneration and inflammatory disease treatment [62]. They can enter mammalian cells without transfection reagents and regulate cell proliferation and migration [63]. Furthermore, tFNAs promote stem cell proliferation and enhance osteogenic differentiation by modulating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [64, 65]. Additionally, tFNAs exhibit the capacity to scavenge reactive oxygen species and modulate macrophage polarization, thereby facilitating bone injury repair [66]. Notably, tFNAs can transport nucleic acid drugs through embedding, vertex attachment, or charge adsorption mechanisms [67].

This study aims to develop and comprehensively analyze a new delivery system termed ATS, which consists of tFNAs modified with the targeting aptamer Apt19S and loaded with siBhlhe22. This complex has a well-defined nanostructure, suitable size, and a high siRNA loading capacity. More importantly, aptamer modification greatly improves the cellular uptake efficiency of the complex in OP-BMSCs, providing a solid foundation for targeted therapy. We encapsulated the ATS complex in gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel to establish a localized controlled-release system for sustained bone repair and defect filling. GelMA has good biocompatibility, degradability, non-toxic properties, and antibacterial and anti-inflammatory activities [68]. The main part of tissue repair is achieving in situ cell regeneration at the pathological site. GelMA inherently contains RGD sequences that facilitate cell adhesion; however, these sequences are prone to degradation by matrix metalloproteinases, thereby diminishing their ability to support cell adhesion. Covalently conjugating the stem cell-targeting aptamer Apt19S to the GelMA scaffold surface can effectively prevent enzymatic degradation and enhance the recruitment of endogenous BMSCs to the bone defect area, which is a crucial early step initiating bone repair [32, 69, 70].

Building upon this foundation, the present study aims to construct a new dual-functional engineered system by combining aptamer-mediated cell targeting and recruitment with tFNA-mediated gene therapy delivery. Our hypothesis is that this system can effectively and sequentially address the challenges of cell homing and fate regulation, thus aligning with the natural healing mechanism involved in osteoporotic bone repair. We will also investigate the mechanism of Apt19S-mediated cell recruitment and the osteogenic effects of siBhlhe22, as well as the potential role of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and metabolic reprogramming (especially in purine metabolism) in the osteogenic context, systematically revealing the molecular events involved in osteogenesis and metabolic processes coordinated by engineered tFNAs. This study will evaluate the effects of this engineered system on cellular behavior in vitro and bone regeneration efficacy in osteoporotic animal models, to provide a promising targeted therapy platform for osteoporotic bone defects and offer new insights into precise regulatory mechanisms of bone formation.

Methods

Animal model and cell isolation

All animal procedures were conducted according to the protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Southwest Medical University (Approval No. 20230703-016). Female Sprague-Dawley rats (8 weeks old) were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Southwest Medical University. An OP model was established via bilateral ovariectomy (OVX), with sham-operated rats serving as controls. At 12 weeks post-surgery, the successful induction of osteoporosis was confirmed by Micro-CT analysis of the distal femur, assessing bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular bone separation (Tb.Sp). Primary osteoporotic bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (OP-BMSCs) were subsequently isolated from the tibial and femoral bone marrow cavities of OVX rats. Cells were cultured in α-MEM complete medium supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco). OP-BMSCs were characterized by flow cytometry for surface markers (negative for CD34, CD31, CD14; positive for CD29, CD90, CD44) and their multi-lineage differentiation potential was verified through osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic induction assays.

Cell transfection and grouping

To knock down Bhlhe22, small interfering RNA targeting Bhlhe22 (siBhlhe22) and its negative control (siNC) were transfected into OP-BMSCs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). The relevant sequences are shown in Table 1. For subsequent nanoparticle treatment experiments, the following groups were established: Blank control (Blank), tFNA vehicle group (T), free siBhlhe22 group, tFNA-siBhlhe22 complex group (TS), and Apt19S-aptamer-modified TS group (ATS). For rescue experiments, the PI3K-Akt pathway activator Recilisib (10 µM, MedChemExpress) or the purine metabolism disruptor xanthine/xanthine oxidase system (100 µmol/mL X and 25 mU/mL XO, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to cells treated with ATS [71].

Preparation and characterization of tFNAs, TS, and ATS nanocomplexes

The single-stranded oligonucleotides used are listed in Table 2. Single-strand DNAs, including S1, S2, S3, S4, S2-Apt19S, and S4-b, were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. Following established protocols, four single-strand DNAs (S1, S2, S3, and S4 for tFNA; S1, S2, S3, and S4-b for TS; S1, S2-Apt19S, S3, and S4-b for ATS) were mixed at equimolar ratios in TM buffer, denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by annealing at 4 °C for 20 min to form tFNA or Apt19S-modified tFNA, respectively. Subsequently, siBhlhe22 bearing complementary sequences was conjugated to Apt19S-tFNA via DNA annealing at 37 °C for 1 h, yielding the final ATS complex. The solutions of tFNA, TS, and ATS nanocomplexes were subjected to full-wavelength scanning analysis using an ultra-micro spectrophotometer (UV5 Nano, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). The assembly process was verified by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. The purity of the complexes was carried out by High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC, Agilent 1260 Infinity II). The morphology and size of the nanoparticles were characterized using Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM, Bruker Dimension Icon) and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM, Hitachi HT7800). Hydrodynamic diameter and Zeta potential were determined via Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS, Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS).

Structural stability analysis, cellular uptake observation, and cell migration assay

The structural stability of ATS was assessed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. Cells were cultured with ATS in fresh αMEM medium supplemented with either 2% or 10% FBS. Samples were collected at specific time points (0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 h) for subsequent analysis. To assess cellular uptake efficiency, OP-BMSCs were treated with 250 nM Cy5-labeled siBhlhe22, TS, or ATS in fresh αMEM containing 2% FBS for 12 h. After incubation, the medium was removed, and cells were trypsinized, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS for flow cytometry analysis. For imaging, cells were cultured in confocal dishes, washed, fixed, and stained with FITC-labeled phalloidin and DAPI after treatment. Images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope. For cell migration assay, OP-BMSCs were seeded in 6-well plates, scratched with a sterile pipette tip, and treated with 250 nM free siBhlhe22, TS, or ATS in αMEM containing 2% FBS. Images were captured at 0 and 48 h post-treatment, and wound closure rates were calculated.

Osteogenic differentiation induction and staining

When cells attained 70%-80% confluency, the culture medium was replaced with osteogenic induction medium (complete medium supplemented with 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, and 100 nM dexamethasone). The induction medium was refreshed every 3 days. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining was performed after 7 days of induction using a BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (Beyotime). Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining was performed after 14 days of induction using 2% Alizarin Red S (pH 4.2) to assess mineralized nodule formation.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular protein extracts were obtained using RIPA lysis buffer. Equal amounts of protein samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Following blocking with 5% skim milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific for BHLHE22, Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), Osteopontin (OPN), ALP, PI3K, p-PI3K, AKT, p-AKT, and GAPDH (Table 3). Subsequently, membranes were incubated with corresponding HRP-linked secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were detected with an ECL chemiluminescence kit (Millipore) and quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin. Subsequently, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting RUNX2 or OPN. Following extensive washes, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 or 594 for 1 h at room temperature, protected from light. Cell nuclei were subsequently visualized by DAPI counterstaining. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA isolation was conducted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), followed by reverse transcription to generate cDNA. qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio 6 Flex instrument using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate Biology). Gene expression levels were normalized to the internal control Gapdh and analyzed via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. The specific primer sequences are listed in Table 4.

Metabolomic analysis

BMSCs from the control (CON), osteoporosis model (OP), and ATS-treated (OPATS) groups were collected. Untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled with a Fourier transform mass spectrometry system (UHPLC-Exploris240, Thermo Fisher). Raw data were processed and analyzed with Progenesis QI software (version 3.0, Waters Corporation, USA). Multivariate statistical analysis, including Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA), was conducted in SIMCA-P software. Metabolic pathway annotation was performed based on the KEGG database, and significantly altered pathways were identified using GSEA.

ATP and reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

Intracellular ATP concentration was quantified with a chemiluminescence-based ATP assay kit (Beyotime). The level of ROS was determined using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (Beyotime). Fluorescence intensity was detected by laser scanning confocal microscopy, spectrophotometry, and flow cytometry, respectively.

In vivo bone defect repair experiment

The hydrogels were prepared by mixing ATS nanocomplexes with a 10% GelMA solution containing 0.25% photoinitiator at designated volume ratios. Each mixture was transferred into a 96-well plate and photopolymerized to form solid hydrogels. AFM and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) were employed to analyze the morphological characteristics of the hydrogels. BMSCs were then seeded onto the surface of the corresponding hydrogels and co-cultured for 48 h. SEM was used to observe the morphology of BMSCs cultured on hydrogels with different ATS-to-GelMA volume ratios. The viability of BMSCs on ATS-loaded GelMA hydrogels was assessed by live/dead staining. For the in vivo bone repair experiment, osteoporotic rats were randomly assigned to five groups. Each animal underwent the creation of a critical-sized calvarial bone defect. The ATS nanocomplex-GelMA hydrogel constructs were implanted into the bone defect sites. The experimental groups included: Blank (GelMA hydrogel only), T, siBhlhe22, TS, and ATS. The animals were euthanized at 4 and 8 weeks post-surgery. Calvarial specimens were collected and analyzed using micro-CT scanning. Quantitative analysis was conducted to assess the BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Sp.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis

Samples were decalcified, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned to be stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome. For immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis, tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval and then incubated with primary antibodies targeting p-PI3K, p-AKT, Osteocalcin (OCN), or OPN. This was followed by treatment with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualization with DAB chromogen. CD29 and CD90 were also subjected to immunofluorescence staining. All the stained sections were examined and digitally captured under a microscope. The immunofluorescence staining for CD29 and CD90 was performed at 7 days post-operation. The staining and immunohistochemical analyses were performed at 4 and 8 weeks post-surgery.

Statistical analysis

All cell-based assays were independently repeated at least three times, and all animal experiments were repeated no fewer than five times. For metabolomics analysis, a minimum of six biological replicates per group were included. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical evaluations were performed with GraphPad Prism software (version 9). For comparisons involving two groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was applied. When comparing three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Tukey’s test for post hoc comparisons. A probability (P) value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v.9).

Results

Bhlhe22 knockdown enhances osteogenic differentiation of osteoporotic BMSCs

We first established an osteoporotic rat model via OVX. Micro-CT analysis showed a significant loss of femoral bone mass in this model (Fig. 1A). BMSCs derived from this model, termed OP-BMSCs, demonstrated the capacity for multi-lineage differentiation (Fig. 1B) and displayed a surface marker profile that aligned with the established characteristics of BMSCs (Fig. 1C), thereby establishing a dependable cellular model for further investigations.

Key experimental results showed that Bhlhe22 knockdown effectively promotes the osteogenic differentiation of OP-BMSCs. qPCR analysis at the molecular level revealed elevated mRNA expression levels of crucial osteogenic markers—Runx2, Alp, and Opn—in the siBhlhe22 group relative to the negative control (NC) group (Fig. 1D). Consistently, western blot analysis showed that the protein levels of the same markers (RUNX2, ALP, OPN) in the siBhlhe22 group were significantly upregulated compared to the NC group (Fig. 1E-F). Additionally, functional assays corroborated these molecular alterations: ALP staining and ARS staining both demonstrated that interference with Bhlhe22 substantially enhanced the early osteogenic activity and the capacity for late-stage mineralization nodule formation in OP-BMSCs (Fig. 1G). Further cellular-level validation revealed that immunofluorescence staining demonstrated increased expression of both RUNX2 and its downstream target OPN following Bhlhe22 knockdown (Fig. 1H).

Collectively, these results are consistent with our previous findings and provide further evidence that Bhlhe22 is a major negative regulator of osteogenic differentiation in OP-BMSCs [51]. The knockdown of this factor activates RUNX2, leading to the coordinated upregulation of crucial factors involved in both early and late stages of osteogenesis. This process effectively restores the impaired osteogenic capacity in OP-BMSCs. In osteoporosis, the bone marrow microenvironment frequently disrupts the balance of osteogenic-adipogenic differentiation in BMSCs [72]. This study shows that targeted inhibition of Bhlhe22 can reverse this imbalance, effectively directing OP-BMSCs towards osteoblastic differentiation and promoting matrix mineralization. Bhlhe22 emerges as a new therapeutic target, offering a vital theoretical foundation for the development of anabolic strategies aimed at treating osteoporosis through the promotion of bone formation.

Preparation and characterization of ATS

In this study, we initially constructed conventional tFNAs through controlled self-assembly (Fig. 2A). By extending the S4 strand with a sticky end complementary to that of siBhlhe22, tFNA could further self-assemble under specific conditions into TS, as shown in Fig. 2B. To achieve efficient and targeted delivery of siBhlhe22 to OP-BMSCs, we designed and constructed an aptamer-functionalized nucleic acid nanocarrier (Apt19S-tFNA-siBhlhe22, ATS). As illustrated in Fig. 2C, ATS was prepared via a multi-step self-assembly process: First, Apt19S was conjugated to the S2 strand, termed S2-Apt19S. Four specific single-stranded DNAs (S1, S2-Apt19S, S3, S4-b) were self-assembled through annealing to form the structurally defined tetrahedral framework nucleic acid (Apt19S-tFNA-b). Subsequently, the Bhlhe22-targeting siRNA was conjugated to the Apt19S-tFNA via an extended sticky end, forming the final ATS complex.

UV-vis absorption spectra showed that T, TS, and ATS all exhibited characteristic DNA absorbance peaks around 258 nm, and the absorbance ratios (A260/A280) were within the expected range for pure nucleic acid structures, indicating successful assembly without significant contamination by free nucleotides or proteins (Fig. 2D). The agarose gel electrophoresis was used to verify the assembly process. Compared to individual DNA strands or intermediates, the successfully assembled ATS had a significantly reduced band mobility, suggesting the formation of a larger, more intricate structure, which supports the successful integration of tFNA, aptamer, and siRNA (Fig. 2E). HPLC analysis showed that different components (T, TS, ATS) have distinct retention times, which confirms the successful synthesis and high purity of ATS (Fig. 2F).

We did a thorough analysis of the physical properties of ATS. AFM and TEM images visually confirmed that the ATS nanostructures possessed a uniform tetrahedral morphology and the expected nanoscale dimensions (Fig. 2G-H). DLS results show that ATS has a hydrodynamic diameter of roughly 25 nm, which is in line with the design, and it demonstrates its structural stability (Fig. 2I). Further evidence confirming the successful synthesis of the targeted materials was provided by Zeta potential measurements, which revealed the anticipated surface charge properties (Fig. 2J).

Next, we analyzed the serum stability and cellular uptake efficiency of the ATS. The serum stability assays indicate that ATS degraded significantly and released siBhlhe22 after 6 h of co-incubation in 10% serum, while in 2% serum, substantial degradation and release of siBhlhe22 occurred after 12 h (Fig. 2K). This release behavior is a key prerequisite for the functional delivery of ATS. ATS acts as a protective carrier to deliver siRNA to target cells, followed by structural dissociation and release of free siBhlhe22. These results show that ATS has better stability in 2% serum, and the optimal co-incubation time was found to be 9–12 h. Moreover, flow cytometric analysis showed that in the free siBhlhe22, the percentage of siRNA-positive cells was 48.9%, and in ATS, it was 99.8% (Fig. 2L). More importantly, quantitative and microscopic imaging analyses of siRNA uptake showed that the ATS treatment group had a significantly improved cellular uptake efficiency of siRNA compared to the free siBhlhe22 group, which aligns with the flow cytometry data (Fig. 2M). Free siBhlhe22 alone, being negatively charged and highly hydrophilic, hardly crosses the cell membrane to enter the cytoplasm, whereas ATS is taken up by cells via an Apt19S-mediated endocytosis pathway, efficiently delivering siRNA into the cells [41]. Upon entering the cytoplasm, the siRNA duplex unwinds, and the antisense strand binds to the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), which then recognizes and cleaves the complementary Bhlhe22 mRNA, ultimately reducing BHLHE22 protein expression levels. Furthermore, a wound healing assay was performed to evaluate the effect of different treatments on cell migration ability. As shown in Fig. 2N, after 48 h of treatment, the ATS group exhibited the highest wound closure rate, followed by the TS group, while the free siBhlhe22 group showed the lowest migration ability. These results indicate that the targeted delivery system ATS significantly promotes cell migration compared to non-targeted TS or free siBhlhe22, and the incorporation of Apt19S plays a critical role in enhancing the migratory capacity of OP-BMSCs. These data collectively indicate that Apt19S facilitates targeted recognition and efficient uptake of the nanocarrier by OP-BMSCs.

In summary, we successfully constructed a well-defined nucleic acid nanocomplex, ATS, which demonstrates good biocompatibility and high efficiency for delivering siBhlhe22. Its Apt19S has a high cellular uptake efficiency, which is mainly because of its targeting function, also enhances cell migration, providing a strong foundation for efficient gene therapy at both cellular and animal levels to reverse the osteoporotic phenotype.

ATS-Mediated Bhlhe22 knockdown promotes osteogenesis in OP-BMSCs via activating PI3K-Akt signaling

To evaluate therapeutic effectiveness of the ATS nanosystem, we conducted a systematic analysis of its effects on Bhlhe22 expression, osteogenic differentiation potential, and relevant signaling pathways in OP-BMSCs. The experiment included five groups: a blank control, tFNA carrier alone (T), free siBhlhe22, tFNA-delivered siBhlhe22 (TS), and aptamer-modified ATS. qPCR and western blot results clearly showed key trends (Fig. 3A-C). Firstly, compared to the control group, the siBhlhe22, TS, and ATS groups all effectively reduced Bhlhe22 mRNA and protein levels, with the ATS group having the most potent effect, thereby confirming its high gene silencing efficiency. More importantly, Bhlhe22 was downregulated, so the expression levels of key osteogenic markers (Runx2, Alp, Opn) showed an increasing trend from siBhlhe22 group to ATS group, suggesting that our delivery system, especially the targeted ATS, most effectively rescued the osteogenic gene expression program in OP-BMSCs.

To explore the molecular mechanisms, we further analyzed the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. The results showed that the levels of phosphorylated PI3K and Akt (p-PI3K, p-AKT) followed a completely opposite pattern to osteogenic markers, with the ATS group showing the most significant reduction. This indicates that Bhlhe22 knockdown might suppress the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway to exert a pro-osteogenic effect (Fig. 3D).

The outcomes of the functional assays fully supported the molecular observations. ALP and ARS staining revealed a progressive increase in early osteogenic activity, as indicated by ALP staining intensity, and late mineralization potential, evidenced by the count of ARS red nodules, progressing from the control group to the ATS group (Fig. 3E). The ATS treatment group formed more mineralized nodules compared to other groups. Concurrently, immunofluorescence staining visually demonstrated that the fluorescence intensities of RUNX2 (primarily in the nucleus) and OPN were strongest in the ATS group, which further confirmed at the single-cell level that ATS had the highest potency for promoting osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 3F).

Overall, these results showed that our engineered ATS nanosystem can efficiently and precisely deliver siBhlhe22 to OP-BMSCs. Successful Bhlhe22 knockdown not only upregulated the expression of osteogenesis-related genes, but more importantly, inhibited the key PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. Inhibition of this pathway is regarded as a vital signal that promotes osteogenic differentiation [51, 73, 74]. Consequently, ATS effectively reverses osteogenic dysfunction in OP-BMSCs by employing a synergistic mechanism involving gene silencing and signaling pathway inhibition, thereby showcasing considerable promise for osteoporosis therapy.

Activation of PI3K-Akt signaling inverses the osteogenic effects of ATS

Analysis of the signaling pathway revealed a pattern of “inhibition followed by activation.” The Western blot analysis demonstrated that ATS treatment led to a significant reduction in the levels of p-PI3K and p-AKT. Conversely, the introduction of Recilisib reactivated this signaling pathway, as shown in Fig. 4A-B.

The results offer strong support for the role of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway in regulating osteogenic differentiation. ATS treatment alone elevated both the mRNA levels (Fig. 4C) and protein expression (Fig. 4D) of crucial osteogenic genes, including Runx2, Alp, and Opn, when compared to the control group. When Recilisib was administered alongside ATS, the pro-osteogenic effect induced by ATS was significantly counteracted, resulting in osteogenic marker expression levels close to or even below those of the control. This “increase then decrease” trend was apparent in the protein quantification analysis (Fig. 4E). This implies that artificially overactivating PI3K-Akt during ATS treatment suppresses osteogenic differentiation.

The results from the functional assays fully corroborated these findings. Figure 4F-G showed that ALP and ARS staining (the ATS group) had the strongest osteogenic activity and most calcium nodule formation, while the osteogenic differentiation capacity was significantly inhibited in the ATS+Recilisib group. Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed this trend in RUNX2 and OPN protein expression, revealing the most intense signal in the ATS group, which was notably diminished after agonist treatment (Fig. 4H-I).

This section gives evidence via gain-of-function rescue experiment, showing that the osteogenic promotion mediated by ATS via Bhlhe22 knockdown is dependent on the inhibition of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. When this pathway is excessively activated by an exogenous agonist, the pro-osteogenic effect of ATS is greatly reduced, showing that PI3K-Akt is a regulatory hub in this process. A balanced, not hyperactive, PI3K-Akt signaling environment is probably the optimal condition for Bhlhe22-mediated regulation of BMSC osteogenic differentiation. This discovery enhances understanding of the downstream molecular processes governed by Bhlhe22 and indicates that future therapeutic approaches should precisely regulate the intensity of signaling pathways to avoid unintended adverse effects.

The PLS-DA score plot demonstrated a clear distinction among the CON, OP, and OPATS groups, suggesting that both osteoporosis and its subsequent treatment have resulted in significant metabolic reprogramming, as illustrated in Fig. 5A. A key finding was the recognition of nucleotide and purine metabolism as the primary pathways affected by osteoporosis and intervened by ATS. The plot of differential abundance scores indicated that nucleotide metabolism represented the most significantly enriched pathway in the comparison between OP and CON groups, with purine metabolism also exhibiting notable changes (Fig. 5C). GSEA further confirmed that these two pathways were significantly suppressed in the osteoporotic state (Fig. 5D). More importantly, in the comparison between OPATS and OP, nucleotide metabolism was identified as the most significantly altered pathway, but its change trend was reversed compared to the osteoporotic model, suggesting that ATS treatment effectively counteracted metabolic disturbances caused by OP (Fig. 5E-F). Venn diagram analysis found 165 core differential metabolites linked to the treatment response, which collectively point to the key metabolic pathways (Fig. 5G).

To further validate the alterations in purine metabolism, we quantified the levels of key purine metabolites, including Adenosine Monophosphate (AMP), Inosine, Hypoxanthine, and Xanthine, across the three groups (Fig. 5H). Compared with the CON group, the OP group exhibited decreased levels of AMP, Inosine, and Xanthine, while Hypoxanthine showed no significant difference. Notably, ATS treatment partially restored these metabolic changes, with the OPATS group showing levels intermediate between those of the CON and OP groups. This pattern indicates that osteoporosis leads to a generalized suppression of the purine metabolite pool, and ATS treatment effectively counteracts this suppression.

To confirm the metabolomics results, we performed a qPCR assay on the mRNA expression levels of key enzymes within the nucleotide metabolism pathway, with a focus on purine metabolism, as depicted in Fig. 5I. The results indicate that the expression of the nucleoside salvage enzyme Deoxycytidine kinase (Dck) was significantly upregulated in the OP group compared to the CON group, whereas the expression of the purine catabolic enzymes Purine nucleoside phosphorylase (Pnp) and Xanthine dehydrogenase (Xdh) was significantly downregulated. Cytidine deaminase (Cda) expression showed a similar downward trend, but the change was not statistically significant. This pattern indicates that BMSCs actively attempt to maintain purine reserves during osteoporosis. Importantly, the treatment with ATS restored the expression levels of Dck, Pnp, and Xdh to those seen in the CON group, showing its regulatory effect on this dysregulated metabolic pathway.

In summary, this section shows a new pathological feature of osteoporosis from a metabolic viewpoint: a severe dysregulation of nucleotide and purine metabolism. Nucleotides are the basic building blocks for cell proliferation and anabolic processes (like DNA/RNA synthesis), and their metabolic impairment directly affects the activity and function of bone-forming cells such as BMSCs. ATS treatment effectively reversed the metabolic abnormality, offering a new systems biology mechanism to explain its pro-osteogenic effect: ATS could remodel the metabolic state of the bone marrow microenvironment, providing essential raw materials and energy for bone repair, thus activating the proliferation and differentiation capacity of BMSCs. This discovery shows that Bhlhe22’s gene regulatory function is linked to downstream metabolic reprogramming, which reveals the potential mechanism by which ATS treats osteoporosis via multi-dimensional, systemic regulation.

Disruption of purine metabolism abrogates the osteogenic effects of ATS

Based on the previous finding that ATS significantly reverses osteoporosis-associated purine metabolic disturbances, we further conducted a loss-of-function experiment to directly validate the necessity of purine metabolism in the pro-osteogenic effect of ATS. We added the purine metabolism disruptor xanthine/xanthine oxidase (X/XO) to OP-BMSCs treated with ATS, to interfere with the intracellular normal purine metabolic process [71].

The results show that disrupting purine metabolism reversed the pro-osteogenic effect of ATS. From the results of qPCR (Fig. 6A) and Western blot (Fig. 6B-C), it was shown that compared to the ATS-alone treatment group, the ATS + X/XO group had significant downregulation in the expression of key osteogenic markers (Runx2, Alp, Opn) at the mRNA and protein levels. Functional assays corroborated this trend, showing that the addition of X/XO significantly reduced the strong ALP activity and the extensive formation of mineralized nodules induced by ATS, as demonstrated by ALP and ARS staining (Fig. 6D). Immunofluorescence staining at the single-cell level further supported the conclusion that the protein expression intensity of RUNX2 and OPN was reduced in the ATS + X/XO group compared to the control group (Fig. 6E-F). The data consistently demonstrate that normal purine metabolism is crucial for ATS to exert its bone-forming effects.

To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we identified two primary downstream outputs in purine metabolism: energy supply and oxidative stress levels. From Fig. 6G, it’s clear that ATS treatment increased the intracellular ATP content in OP-BMSCs but introducing X/XO significantly reduced cellular ATP levels. Meanwhile, X/XO treatment caused a significant increase in intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 6H-J). This shows that disrupting purine metabolism with X/XO depletes ATP, which is the essential energy currency for biosynthetic processes such as osteogenic differentiation, and its metabolic process might produce excessive ROS, causing oxidative stress damage to cells.

This section provides clear causal evidence indicating that ATS drives osteogenic differentiation through the regulation of purine metabolism, highlighting it as a fundamental functional mechanism rather than just a correlated occurrence. Normal purine metabolism generates adequate ATP to support bone formation and sustains proper redox homeostasis. When this metabolic pathway is exogenously disrupted, even with ATS, its pro-osteogenic effect is negated because of an energy crisis and oxidative stress. This shows the essential role of purine metabolism in bone metabolic homeostasis. Based on earlier findings, our data establish a mechanistic framework for the action of ATS: ATS, by delivering siBhlhe22, modulates two potential pathways—inhibition of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and promotion of purine metabolism. The latter offers the bioenergetic support (increased ATP) and redox support (reduced ROS) essential for osteogenic differentiation. This finding gives a new theoretical basis to understand the metabolic causes of osteoporosis and develop metabolism-targeted therapies.

Discussion

Effective reconstruction of bone defects in osteoporosis presents a significant clinical challenge, mainly because the pathological bone marrow microenvironment hinders the recruitment and osteogenic differentiation of endogenous MSCs [75–77]. This study suggests a new and integrated therapeutic approach to tackle these two bottlenecks. We identified Bhlhe22 as a key negative regulator of osteogenesis in OP-BMSCs, and engineered a sophisticated nanotherapeutic platform, ATS, for its targeted delivery. Our findings indicate that ATS facilitates bone regeneration via coordinated dual mechanisms: it suppresses the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and restores dysregulated purine metabolism, thereby suggesting a strong “signaling-metabolism” co-regulation strategy for the treatment of osteoporosis.

The bHLH family is recognized for its role in regulating cell fate decisions; however, the precise function of Bhlhe22 in bone metabolism had not been fully investigated [78–80]. In our study, we found the PI3K-Akt pathway to be a key downstream effector. The observation that Bhlhe22 knockdown inhibited the p-PI3K and p-AKT, combined with the reversal of its pro-osteogenic effects by Recilisib (a PI3K-Akt agonist), establishes a direct causal link. This indicates that Bhlhe22 functions upstream of PI3K-Akt in OP-BMSCs, suggesting that its knockdown may alleviate inhibitory signaling on osteogenesis through modulation of upstream regulators or interacting partners of the PI3K-Akt axis. Although basal BHLHE22 signaling is relatively weak, this low abundance remains pathologically significant in the osteoporotic microenvironment—it is sufficient to sustain aberrant activation of the PI3K-Akt pathway, thereby exerting a significant biological impact on osteogenic differentiation; this explains why targeted knockdown of Bhlhe22 effectively relieves osteogenic inhibition, as BHLHE22 resides at an upstream node of network pathological amplification loop in bone metabolism regulation. This finding introduces a new dimension to the previously context-dependent and intricate role of PI3K-Akt in bone biology and highlights its hyperactivation as a key contributor to osteoporotic pathogenesis [53, 81, 82].

Our untargeted metabolomics revealed a new pathological aspect of osteoporosis, which is a severe disruption in nucleotide and purine metabolism. The observation that these pathways were significantly suppressed in OP-BMSCs and were effectively reversed by ATS treatment shifts the perspective from a purely signaling deficit to a bioenergetic and biosynthetic crisis. Purine metabolism plays a crucial role by providing ATP, the universal energy currency, and precursors necessary for nucleic acid synthesis. These components are in high demand for cellular proliferation, matrix synthesis, and mineralization during the process of osteogenesis [83, 84]. Rescue experiments employing the xanthine/xanthine oxidase system have provided clear functional evidence: the disruption of purine metabolism in the system prevented the osteogenic benefits of ATS, with a significant decrease intracellular ATP and a rise in ROS. This shows that ATS-induced metabolic reprogramming is not a secondary effect, but a necessary and permissive condition for osteogenic differentiation. It guarantees cellular energy homeostasis and reduces oxidative stress, creating a favorable metabolic environment for bone formation.

More importantly, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and purine metabolism are not independent or parallel but exhibit close functional crosstalk. Activated PI3K-Akt signaling can support de novo purine nucleotide synthesis by enhancing the activity or upregulating the expression of key enzymes involved in nucleotide synthesis, such as phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate synthetase (PRPS), thereby meeting the high demand for energy and macromolecules during rapid proliferation and differentiation [85–88]. Conversely, intracellular levels of purine metabolites (such as ATP) can also influence the activation state of PI3K-Akt via feedback regulatory mechanisms [89]. In this study, Bhlhe22 knockdown inhibited the hyperactivation of PI3K-Akt; we speculate that this disrupts the pathological driving of purine metabolism by this pathway, while ATS simultaneously corrects purine metabolic disturbances, further cutting off the metabolic basis that supports aberrant signal transduction. However, whether Bhlhe22 directly regulates the transcription of purine metabolism-related enzymes and which transcription factors (such as c-Myc and HIF1α) downstream of PI3K-Akt mediate this crosstalk requires further experimental validation.

The core of translating mechanistic insights into therapeutic effectiveness lies in the design of the ATS nanocarrier. The tFNA core has several distinct advantages: it has inherent biocompatibility, can be efficiently taken up by cells without needing transfection reagents, and has a programmable structure for precise functionalization [61, 90–92]. The conjugation of Apt19S is a strategic choice; this aptamer has high affinity for ALPL on the surface of osteogenically-primed MSCs [39]. It provides active targeting specificity, which is clearly shown by the superior cellular uptake of ATS compared to the non-targeted TS complex. This targeting strategy ensures a high concentration of therapeutic siRNA at the intended site of action, thereby enhancing efficacy while reducing off-target effects [93]. Additionally, the integration of ATS into a GelMA hydrogel resulted in a localized, sustained-release reservoir at the bone defect site, as observed in histological studies [69].

The in vivo findings robustly supported the proposed mechanisms and confirmed their therapeutic promise. Osteoporotic rats treated with ATS-loaded GelMA hydrogel showed strong and long-lasting bone regeneration, which was significantly better than all control groups. Importantly, immunohistochemical analysis of the regenerated tissue matched the in vitro findings: areas of new bone formation showed concurrent inhibition of p-PI3K/p-AKT and upregulation of OCN and OPN expression. The spatial and temporal correlation observed offers compelling in vivo proof that inhibiting PI3K-Akt, through Bhlhe22 knockdown, directly contributes to the activation of osteogenic gene expression during bone healing. The efficacy gradient shows that each component contributes to the overall therapeutic outcome.

In the current literature, our work advances the field in several aspects. First, unlike most studies focusing on a single signaling pathway such as BMP or Wnt, or a single metabolite such as calcium or phosphate, our work builds upon our previous identification of Bhlhe22 by revealing that signaling abnormalities and metabolic disorders are not independent events. We have transformed this target into a “signaling-metabolic” synergistic therapeutic strategy for the osteoporotic microenvironment, expanding the research perspective from a single signaling pathway to metabolic reprogramming. This provides a more comprehensive way to correct the osteoporotic phenotype and represents a shift from single-target intervention to systemic network regulation [51]. Second, this study designed the ATS nano-targeted delivery system to overcome the bottleneck of in vivo osteogenic-specific delivery. Compared to traditional viral vectors and lipid nanoparticles, the ATS system exhibits superior biocompatibility, precise nanostructure, and ease of functionalization, and its degradation products are non-toxic nucleotide fragments, which offers a significant advantage in scenarios such as bone defect repair that require long-term or repeated administration [41, 94]. The Apt19S modification in this study significantly enhanced binding affinity and specificity for osteogenically-primed BMSCs compared to non-targeted nanocarriers [41]. Although under osteoporotic pathological conditions, the osteogenic priming capacity of BMSCs is inherently impaired, potentially leading to delayed or insufficient upregulation of ALPL and thereby reducing the early targeting efficiency of Apt19S, the ATS indeed exhibited superior cellular uptake in vitro compared to non-targeted TS complexes. Third, integrating ATS into the GelMA hydrogel for localized sustained release creates a high concentration gradient at the bone defect site. This local enrichment effect can partially compensate for potential deficiencies in targeting specificity, ensuring that the therapeutic siRNA reaches an effective concentration at the intended site of action. However, whether the hydrogel itself possesses osteoinductive activity, for instance whether its degradation products can buffer the local acidic microenvironment, and whether this activity produces synergistic effects with ATS, are questions worth further decoupling [41, 95].

However, this study has limitations. The therapeutic platform is based on tFNA and is still in the preclinical stage. Although the ATS system shows significant efficacy in animal models, its clinical translation still faces challenges, including the need for further improvement in serum stability, immature large-scale production processes, cost control, and stringent regulatory approval requirements [96]. Additionally, a long-term biosafety assessment and potential immunogenicity of tFNA in large animal models upon repeated administration are needed. Although the rat ovariectomy model is widely used, assessing the effectiveness of ATS in alternative osteoporosis models, such as glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis, could increase its clinical applicability.

Conclusions

The study identifies Bhlhe22 as a key negative regulator in osteogenic differentiation in osteoporosis and introduces ATS, an aptamer-functionalized nucleic acid nanocomplex for the targeted delivery of siBhlhe22. Knockdown of Bhlhe22 enhanced osteogenesis by suppressing the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, while concurrently correcting osteoporosis-associated purine metabolic dysfunction. This metabolic reprogramming restored cellular ATP production and reduced oxidative stress, thereby creating a favorable microenvironment for bone formation. Notably, in an osteoporotic rat model, ATS treatment resulted in substantial bone regeneration, which was verified through immunohistochemical analysis, showing that the PI3K-Akt pathway was inhibited, and the expression of osteogenic markers was enhanced in newly formed bone. Our findings reveal a comprehensive mechanistic axis where ATS coordinates “gene silencing-signaling inhibition-metabolic reprogramming” to guide BMSCs towards osteogenesis, thus presenting Bhlhe22 as a promising therapeutic target and ATS as an effective anabolic strategy for osteoporosis treatment, which addresses both signaling and metabolic aspects of bone formation.
