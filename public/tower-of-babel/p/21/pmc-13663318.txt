Extensin-enriched host cell wall specialization restricts fungal invasion while sustaining symbiosis in the mycoheterotrophic orchid, Gastrodia elata
Authors: Yi-Tse Wang, Bodil Jørgensen, Chieh-Yun Cheng, Chih-Hsin Yeh, Yung-I Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFully mycoheterotrophic orchids depend entirely on fungal partners for carbon and mineral nutrition, yet the structural mechanisms that regulate fungal accommodation within host tissues remain poorly understood. This question is particularly relevant in the Gastrodia elata-Armillaria symbiosis, where the fungal partner is a wood-decaying basidiomycete capable of aggressive host colonization and responsible for root rot disease in many tree species. Here, we examined whether host cell wall remodeling and extensin deposition contribute to the establishment and regulation of the host-fungus interface in G. elata tubers.ResultsUltrastructural analyses revealed extensive host-derived cell wall remodeling during fungal colonization of G. elata tubers, including localized wall thickening and the formation of hypha-encasing wall domains within fungal-colonized cells and adjacent boundary cells. Comprehensive Microarray Polymer Profiling identified extensin epitopes recognized by JIM20 as the most strongly enriched hydroxyproline-rich glycoproteins component following colonization. Immunogold labeling localized extensin-enriched HRGPs specifically to modified host cell walls and interfacial matrices surrounding intracellular hyphae. Quantitative comparison demonstrated that the interfacial matrix surrounding intracellular hyphae was substantially thicker than that reported for arbuscular or typical orchid mycorrhizal associations. Chemical inhibition of HRGPs deposition using 3,4-DHP disrupted interfacial wall formation, resulting in uncontrolled fungal spread into normally restricted tissues, cortical necrosis, and reduced tuber growth.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that extensin-enriched host cell wall remodeling establishes a reinforced symbiotic interface that spatially restricts fungal invasion while maintaining potential routes for nutrient exchange. Our results reveal a structural mechanism enabling a fully mycoheterotrophic orchid to accommodate an invasive fungal symbiont, indicating that spatial compartmentalization of host-fungus interactions represents an important mechanism contributing to symbiotic stability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09502-w.Keywords: Armillaria, CoMPP, Boundary cells, Hydroxyproline-rich glycoproteins, Mycorrhiza, symbiosis

Summary statement

This study reveals that extensin-enriched host cell wall remodeling forms a specialized interface that spatially restricts fungal invasion while retaining potential transport capacity in the fully mycoheterotrophic orchid Gastrodia elata.

Background

Plant cell walls are intricate, dynamic structures composed primarily of polysaccharides and other biopolymers that form a highly organized network surrounding the protoplast. These walls serve as fundamental mediators of plant morphology, contributing to cell shape, tissue integrity and developmental patterning [1]. Despite their compositional diversity across taxa and tissues, plant cell walls fulfill roles analogous to those of the animal extracellular matrix, linking cells into cohesive tissues and participating in the regulation of growth and division [2]. In addition to providing mechanical strength and structural support, cell walls also act as a frontline barrier against biotic stress, playing a central role in plant defense responses [3]. Notably, the cell wall is not a static structure. Its composition and architecture can be dynamically modified in response to developmental cues and environmental interactions.

Such plasticity is especially evident during interactions with mycorrhizal fungi, where the plant cell wall serves as both a point of contact and a site of active remodeling. In mycorrhizal associations, the plant cell wall is not only a structural barrier or substrate but also the primary interface for establishing contact with fungal partners [4, 5]. In ectomycorrhizal (ECM) symbioses, the fungal partner first envelops the root tip to form a dense hyphal mantle, followed by the development of the Hartig net within the root cortex. In this Hartig net region, plant and fungal cell walls come into direct contact, forming a specialized interface for communication and nutrient transfer. Although fungal hyphae penetrate the root cortex, host cell walls generally remain intact, exhibiting only subtle and localized modifications, such as wall loosening, in several ECM systems [5, 6]. By contrast, in arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbioses, fungal hyphae traverse the plant apoplast and invaginate the host plasma membrane to form arbuscules within cortical cells. This process is accompanied by extensive remodeling of the host cell wall and the formation of a specialized interface compartment that facilitates bidirectional solute exchange [7]. In AM symbioses, this interface comprises both fungal and plant membranes, often separated by an apoplastic matrix enriched in plant-secreted materials [8, 9]. This interfacial matrix contains a variety of cell wall related components, including cellulose, pectins, β-glucans, and proteins such as expansins and hydroxyproline-rich glycoproteins (HRGPs). Importantly, the composition of this matrix is dynamically regulated during arbuscule development and degeneration, reflecting the extensive cell wall remodeling required to sustain symbiotic function.

Given the dynamic and context-dependent nature of cell wall remodeling during plant-fungus interactions, approaches that allow comparative and semi-quantitative analysis of cell wall composition are essential. Comprehensive Microarray Polymer Profiling (CoMPP) is a high-throughput glycomic technique that enables semi-quantitative mapping of defined glycan epitopes across diverse biological samples by combining the specificity of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs) [10]. In this approach, cell wall polymers are sequentially extracted and spotted onto microarrays, where they are probed using specific sets of mAbs to reveal detailed glycan signatures. CoMPP has been applied to various interactions between plants and other living organisms to reveal dynamic changes in cell wall composition. For instance, it was used to examine how structural differences in host cell walls influence susceptibility to parasitism by Cuscuta reflexa [11]. In mycorrhizal symbioses, CoMPP has been employed to detect changes in cell wall composition during AM symbioses in tomato, both locally in roots and systemically in fruits [12], and to investigate cell wall remodeling in Corylus avellana roots during ECM colonization by Tuber melanosporum [13].

While arbuscular mycorrhiza represents the most widespread form of endomycorrhizal symbiosis in land plants, orchid mycorrhizas constitute a distinct yet related system that shares certain structural features while differing markedly in interface organization [7]. Orchid mycorrhizas are characterized by the intracellular colonization of root or protocorm cells by coiled fungal hyphae known as pelotons [14]. The symbiotic interface formed in orchid protocorms is structurally and functionally comparable to that of other endomycorrhizal systems [4], yet it also exhibits unique features reflecting the strong dependence of orchids on fungal partners during early development, particularly in mycoheterotrophic species.

In most green orchids, pelotons are periodically digested by the host cell through a process termed tolypophagy, during which fungal cytoplasmic contents are released and absorbed by the host. By contrast, a distinct strategy known as ptyophagy occurs in some fully mycoheterotrophic orchids [15, 16]. In this system, fungal colonization involves specialized peg-like hyphae that intrude into host cortical cells and are frequently confined by localized host cell wall outgrowths. Upon rupture of the hyphal tip, fungal contents are thought to be directly released into the host cytoplasm [17]. Although the cytological features of ptyophagous interactions have been described [18], the structural organization and composition of the host-fungus interfacial matrix in this system remain poorly characterized.

Among the components of the symbiotic interfacial matrix, HRGPs are thought to play important roles in regulating cell wall structure and function during plant-fungus interactions [19]. This diverse protein family includes extensin, arabinogalactan proteins (AGPs), and proline-rich proteins (PRPs), each contributing differently to cell wall architecture, signaling, and symbiotic accommodation [20]. In AM systems, extensin has been implicated in reinforcing the periarbuscular interface, whereas AGPs are involved in symbiotic signaling and developmental processes [5, 21, 22]. Emerging evidence suggests that HRGPs also play important roles in orchid mycorrhizas. In Dendrobium officinale, extensin-enriched HRGPs accumulate at the cell walls of colonized cells and are localized within the interfacial matrix surrounding pelotons [23], indicating a potential role in maintaining the integrity of the symbiotic interface.

Gastrodia elata is a fully mycoheterotrophic orchid that forms an intimate and highly specialized symbiosis with Armillaria [24]. Throughout its life cycle, G. elata depends entirely on its fungal partner for carbon and nutrient acquisition. Notably, Armillaria is a wood-decaying fungus widely recognized as an aggressive causal agent of root rot disease in forest trees [25], implying that G. elata must have evolved strategies to accommodate and constrain fungal invasion in a manner that promotes symbiosis rather than pathogenesis. Previous anatomical studies have shown that Armillaria establishes a highly organized symbiotic architecture within G. elata tubers [18]. During symbiotic establishment, Armillaria rhizomorphs invade tubers from the basal region and colonize a discrete outer cortical cell layer. Within these cells, intracellular hyphae are enveloped by a host-derived interfacial matrix, forming characteristic hypha-encasing wall domains, while fungal spread is spatially restricted by a distinct boundary cell layer separating colonized tissues from the inner cortex. Despite detailed cytological observations, the composition and spatial organization of cell wall-derived components within the host-fungus interface of G. elata tubers remain largely unexplored. Characterizing this specialized interface therefore provides an opportunity to gain insight into the structural adaptations underlying stable ptyophagous interactions and into how plants can establish mutualistic relationships with fungi that are pathogenic in other contexts.

In this study, we investigated cell wall remodeling during the establishment of the mycorrhizal association between G. elata and Armillaria by combining immunohistochemical analyses and CoMPP, together with monoclonal antibodies recognizing specific cell wall components, was used to profile changes in cell wall composition following fungal colonization. To assess the contribution of HRGPs to the organization of the ptyophagous host-fungus interface, 3,4-DHP was applied to disrupt HRGPs deposition.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and symbiotic cultures

For the production of mycorrhizal tubers, non-mycorrhizal tubers were first derived from G. elata callus cultures following the procedure described by Yeh et al. [26]. These were then used to establish symbiotic cultures with the wood-decaying fungus-Armillaria. The plant and fungal materials used in this study were cultivated samples maintained by the Taoyuan District Agricultural Research and Extension Station, Ministry of Agriculture, Taiwan. Since the samples were sourced from an official agricultural research institution and grown under controlled conditions, no field collection permissions or licenses were required. A laboratory-maintained Armillaria tabescens strain was cultured on the potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium, and actively growing mycelia from the colony margins were used as fungal inoculum. Prior to initiating the symbiotic culture, the fungal isolate was transferred onto sterilized timber blocks of Liquidambar formosana in 500 ml glass flasks and incubated in the dark at 20 ± 1 °C. After 30 days of culture, the mycelium had completely colonized the timber blocks. The non-mycorrhizal tubers were then placed onto these colonized timber blocks and cultured under the same conditions. After 2 months of culture, A. tabescens rhizomorphs had infected the basal region of the tubers, promoting their growth. Developing mycorrhizal tubers approximately 1 cm in length were collected at this stage for further analysis.

Histochemical study

Sections of non-mycorrhizal and mycorrhizal tubers were fixed in a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution (0.1 M phosphate buffer at pH of 6.8) for 12 h at 4 °C. After fixation, the samples were washed three times for 10 min each with the same buffer at room temperature, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series. The dehydrated samples were then embedded in Technovit 7100 resin (Kulzer & Co., Wehrheim, Germany), following the protocol described by Yeung and Chan [27]. Resin sections, 3 μm thick, were cut using a glass knife on a Leica RM2135 rotary microtome. To detect total insoluble carbohydrates, sections were stained using the periodic acid-Schiff reaction and then counterstained with either 0.05% (w/v) toluidine blue O in benzoate buffer for general histology or 1% (w/v) amido black 10B in 7% acetic acid for protein detection. Stained sections were observed and imaged under a Carl Zeiss Axioskop microscope equipped with a digital camera (Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany).

Comprehensive microarray polymer profiling

CoMPP analysis was performed following the protocol of Kračun et al. [28]. For both non-mycorrhizal and mycorrhizal tubers, three biological replicates were collected from independent tubers grown in separate flasks and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Approximately 10 mg of each sample was precisely weighed (to one decimal place) and transferred to individual wells of an 8-strip tube (CLS4413, SIGMA; Merck Life Science, Darmstadt, Germany). Homogenization was carried out using a Tissuelyser II (Gentec Biosciences, Columbia, USA) with glass beads prior to extraction. A two-step sequential extraction series was utilized. For each sample, the volume of extractant was adjusted based on sample weight. Glass beads were retained to facilitate mixing. The first extraction was performed with 50 mM CDTA (trans‐1,2‐Diaminocyclohexane‐N, N,N’,N’,‐tetraacetic acid monohydrate, pH 7.5, Merck Life Science, Darmstadt, Germany). Samples were then shaken at room temperature for 27 s− 1 for 2 min, followed by 6 s− 1 for 2 h. After centrifugation at 4,000 rpm (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 10 min, the supernatant was transferred to labeled 0.5 ml Eppendorf tubes (Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany) and stored at 5 °C to minimize enzymatic or chemical degradation. CDTA forms stable coordination complexes with calcium ions, disrupting cross-links in the cell wall and releasing pectin-associated polysaccharides. The second extraction used 4 mM NaOH, again following the same procedures as for the previous step. The alkaline conditions disrupted hydrogen bonds, enhancing the solubilization of hemicelluloses. Following extraction, samples were transferred to a 384-well plate (CLS3342, SIGMA; Merck Life Science) and subjected to four serial dilutions. Arrayjet buffer (55.2% glycerol, 44% water, and 0.8% Triton X‐100) was used as the diluent. The plates were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min and printed onto 0.45 μm pore-size nitrocellulose membranes (Whatman, Maidstone, UK) using an Arrayjet Marathon piezoelectric microarrayer (Arrayjet Ltd., Edinburgh, UK). Three independent biological replicates were analyzed, and each dilution series was printed in duplicate as technical replicates on the microarray. Printed arrays were blocked with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 5% milk powder overnight at 5 °C. Then the arrays were incubated with the primary monoclonal antibodies (mAbs, see Table S1) for 2 h and then washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, membranes were incubated for 2 h with alkaline phosphatase-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rat or anti-mouse), depending on the primary antibody used, washed three times with PBS and once with water. Signal detection and data analysis were conducted following the procedures described by Ahl et al. [29]. Data were visualized as heatmaps, with the highest mean signal normalized to 100% and a 5% threshold applied to remove background noise.

Transmission electron microscopy

Tubers from non-mycorrhizal and mycorrhizal samples were fixed by a high-pressure freezing (Leica EM PACT2) method and subsequently subjected to freeze substitution in ethanol with 0.2% glutaraldehyde and 0.1% uranyl acetate (Leica Automatic Freeze-Substitution System). Afterward, the samples were embedded in LR White resin. Ultrathin sections, 90 nm in thickness, were cut using a diamond knife on a Leica Reichert Ultracut S microtome and placed on formvar-coated nickel grids. These sections were subsequently stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Observations were conducted using a Philips CM 100 transmission electron microscope (FEI Company) at 80 kV.

Immunogold labeling

Immunogold labeling was performed as described by Mravec et al. [30], using the monoclonal antibody JIM20 (PlantProbes, Leeds, United Kingdom), which recognizes extensin epitopes to investigate extensin distribution. Samples were prepared by the high-pressure freezing and subsequently embedded in LR White resin. Ultrathin sections, 90 nm in thickness, were cut using a Leica Reichert Ultracut S Ultramicrotome and mounted on nickel grids. Grids were blocked in PBS for 15 min, followed by bovine serum albumin blocking. Primary antibody incubation (1:20 dilution) was performed at room temperature for 1 h, followed by PBS washes. Grids were then incubated with secondary antibody, 18 nm colloidal gold AffiniPure™ Goat Anti-Rat IgG (H + L) (EM Grade) (1:20 dilution, Jackson ImmunoResearch), for 1 h at room temperature. After labeling, sections were stained with 0.5% uranyl acetate for 5 min and rinsed with distilled water. After overnight drying, the grids were ready for observation under a Philips CM 100 transmission electron microscope (FEI Company) at 80 kV.

The distribution of antibody labeling was compared between non-mycorrhizal and mycorrhizal tubers. For each cell layer, three fields of view per section were selected for quantitative analysis. Cell wall area and the number of gold particles in each field were quantified using ImageJ. Label density was calculated as the number of gold particles per unit area of the target structure and expressed as gold particles per total cell wall area. Data are presented as boxplots. Prior to one-way ANOVA, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. For the data analyzed in this study, residuals did not significantly deviate from normality, and variances were homogeneous among groups. Statistical differences among groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s HSD test for post hoc comparisons (P < 0.05). All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1).

Treatment with 3,4-Dehydro-L-Proline

The role of HRGPs in the symbiotic interaction of G. elata was investigated using 3,4-DHP, a prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor that alters the structure of HRGPs in the cell wall. For symbiotic cultures, small mycorrhizal tubers of G. elata (approximately 0.14–0.18 g fresh weight) were placed on PDA medium supplemented with 200 µM 3,4-DHP (Sigma-Aldrich Co.) in a 6-cm glass Petri dish. The concentration of 200 µM was selected based on preliminary dose-response assays, which demonstrated a clear treatment effect without affecting fungal growth (Tabel S2). The Petri dish was then placed inside a Magenta GA-7 culture box, with sterilized L. formosana wood chips inoculated with the fungal isolate used as a filling layer between the Petri dish and the bottom of the box. Petri dishes without 3,4-DHP treatment were used as controls. After two months of culture, growth was assessed as the increase in fresh weight per tuber. In parallel, the distribution of the JIM 20 epitope in 3,4-DHP-treated cultures was examined by immunogold labeling, as described above. Experiments were conducted using a completely randomized design with three biological replicates per treatment. Statistical significance between two independent groups was evaluated using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test.

Results

Developmental anatomy of the mycorrhizal tuber in Gastrodia elata

A. tabescens colonization significantly enhanced the growth of G. elata tubers. In the absence of symbiosis, tuber clumps remained small, and although some tubers had elongated, they failed to further enlarge (Fig. 1a). After 60 days of inoculation with A. tabescens on wood blocks, the tubers enlarged and formed lateral buds (Fig. 1b). A. tabescens rhizomorphs penetrated the tuber through the basal region, resulting in a light brown discoloration at the base (Fig. 1b).

The tuber was composed of parenchyma cells of varying sizes. In the uninoculated tuber, the outermost six cell layers adjacent to the epidermis were smaller than the internal cells and formed a cortex-like zone surrounding the inner tissue (Fig. 1c). After inoculation with A. tabescens, the epidermis of the basal tuber region became withered and deteriorated. Beneath the epidermis, three distinct cell types were identified: the fungi-colonized cells, the boundary cells, and the inner cortical cells (Fig. 1d). The fungal hyphae penetrated and colonized cells in the cortex-like outer layers, forming what we refer to as the ‘fungi-colonized layer’. Adjacent to this layer, the boundary cells were significantly enlarged. These cells, defined by their anatomical location between the fungal colonized layer and the inner cortex, exhibited vesiculated cytoplasm, contained numerous starch grains and protein bodies, and possessed large, highly lobed nuclei (Fig. 1d). On the inner side of the boundary, the inner cortical cells remained free of fungal hyphae but accumulated abundant starch grains. Notably, fungal colonization was confined to the region bounded by the boundary cells.

Structural changes of cell walls following colonization

One of the major anatomical changes associated with A. tabescens colonization was the reinforcement of host cell walls (see Figs. 1e-h and 2, Table S3), especially in fungus-colonized cells and boundary cells. In non-mycorrhizal tubers, parenchyma cells were characterized by uniformly thin cell walls, with a median thickness of approximately 0.5 μm (Fig. 1e). In contrast, mycorrhizal tubers exhibited pronounced spatial heterogeneity in cell wall thickness. The inner cortical cells were characterized by relatively thin cell walls, with a median thickness of approximately 0.8 μm (Fig. 1f), whereas fungus-colonized cells showed marked wall thickening, reaching a median thickness of approximately 2 μm (Fig. 1g). Localized wall thickenings were frequently observed at sites of fungal penetration, forming conspicuous hypha-encasing structures composed of host-derived wall materials. The most pronounced cell wall modification was observed in boundary cells, where the median wall thickness reached approximately 4.5 μm. Boundary cell walls displayed conspicuous and uneven thickening, with non-uniform deposition patterns (Fig. 1h). This heterogeneity was most evident on tangential walls facing the fungus-colonized cell layer, indicating a spatially localized structural modification specifically associated with the host-fungus interface.

CoMPP profiling of cell wall proteins in Gastrodia elata tubers

Compositional changes of cell wall proteins of G. elata in response to A. tabescens colonization were detected using CoMPP. Among the eight antibodies targeting epitopes on the major cell wall proteins, five yielded detectable signals (Fig. 3). The strongest extensin signal was detected by JIM20 in the CDTA extraction from both non-mycorrhizal and mycorrhizal tubers, with significantly higher levels in mycorrhizal samples. In contrast, the other extensin specific antibody, LM1 produced only a weak signal, only in the mycorrhizal tubers. Although three AGPs specific antibodies, JIM13, LM2 and MAC207 generated detectable signals, they gave weak responses in both CDTA and NaOH extractions from non-mycorrhizal and/or mycorrhizal tubers.

In situ distribution of Gastrodia elata cell wall proteins

To complement the ultrastructural analysis of cell wall thickening, immunogold labeling experiments were performed to examine the in situ distribution of cell wall proteins in both mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal G. elata tubers. Among the extensin-specific antibodies tested in CoMPP, JIM20 produced the most robust and reproducible labeling signals in both sample types and was therefore selected for detailed immunolocalization analysis.

In non-mycorrhizal tubers (Fig. 4a), JIM20 labeling was detected along cortical cell walls, as shown in the representative TEM micrograph (Fig. 4c), consistent with the strong signals detected in non-mycorrhizal tubers by CoMPP analysis. In mycorrhizal tubers (Fig. 4b), strong JIM20 labeling was observed in the fungus-colonized cell layer, particularly along disrupted host cell walls (Fig. 4d). These disrupted wall regions corresponded to localized wall thickenings at sites of fungal penetration. JIM20 gold particles were detected at interfaces between intracellular fungal hyphae and host-derived wall and membrane materials (Fig. 4e). In areas characterized by conspicuous hypha-encasing wall structures, high densities of JIM20 gold particles were observed in the interfacial matrix (Fig. 4f). In the boundary cells, JIM20 labeling was associated with the unevenly thickened cell walls and was detected in both thickened and unthickened wall regions (Fig. 4g). Notably, the interface between G. elata and A. tabescens was characterized by a conspicuously thick interfacial matrix, as revealed by ultrastructural observations (Fig. 5a, Table S4). Quantitative analysis of JIM20 labeling density (Fig. 5b, Table S5) confirmed significantly higher labeling in the fungus-colonized cell layer compared with the boundary and inner cortical cell layers.

Effect of 3,4-DHP on symbiotic culture

To assess the effect of 3,4-DHP on tuber growth of G. elata, 3,4-DHP was added to PDA medium in the presence of the fungal symbiont. Following two months of symbiotic culture, clear differences in tuber growth were observed between treatments. Control tubers were generally larger and more vigorous, whereas tubers on 3,4-DHP-treated cultures were noticeably smaller and exhibited reduced growth (Fig. 6). Consistent with these morphological differences, tubers grown in the untreated control culture showed a substantial increase in fresh weight (405.0 ± 131.3 mg), whereas tuber growth was strongly suppressed in 3,4-DHP-treated cultures, with an average increase of only 85.0 ± 7.8 mg (mean ± SD, n = 3; Fig. 7).

In the 3,4-DHP-treated cultures, enhanced fungal colonization was frequently observed. Fungal hyphae extensively penetrated the basal region of G. elata tubers and were associated with increased necrosis of cortical cells and disruption of host cell wall integrity (Fig. 8a). To further examine the effect of 3,4-DHP on the distribution and localization of HRGPs during tuber development under symbiotic conditions, immunolabeling with the JIM20 antibody was performed on developing tubers cultured on the 3,4-DHP-supplemented medium. In control cultures, strong JIM20 labeling was detected in the interfacial matrix between G. elata cells and fungal hyphae (similar to Fig. 4). In contrast, following 3,4-DHP treatment, only weak JIM20 signals were observed at the symbiotic interface (Fig. 8b, d). In parallel with the reduced JIM20 labeling, fungal hyphae were commonly observed penetrating and occupying enlarged boundary cells in the cortical region of 3,4-DHP-treated tubers (Fig. 8b and c).

Discussion

As a fully mycoheterotrophic orchid, G. elata depends entirely on Armillaria for carbon acquisition ultimately derived from the decomposition of woody substrates, a process for which Armillaria is widely recognized as an aggressive wood-decaying pathogen in forest ecosystems [25]. The establishment of a mycorrhizal symbiosis between G. elata and such a fungus therefore presents a striking biological paradox: a plant host must permit fungal colonization to secure nutrition supply, while simultaneously preventing uncontrolled fungal invasion and tissue destruction.

Ultrastructural specialization of the G. elata-Armillaria interface

In this study, our ultrastructural observations demonstrate that colonization by A. tabescens is accompanied by pronounced remodeling of host cell walls in G. elata tubers. In particular, fungal colonization is associated with localized wall thickening, the formation of hypha-encasing wall regions, and marked structural differentiation between fungus-colonized cells, boundary cells, and inner cortical cells (Fig. 1d). Quantitative comparison of host-fungus interface thickness revealed a pronounced structural distinction between the G. elata-Armillaria association and other mycorrhizal systems, including Rhizoctonia-associated orchid mycorrhizal fungi (OMF) and AM symbioses (Fig. 5a, Table S4). Our measurements demonstrate that the interfacial matrix surrounding intracellular A. tabescens hyphae in G. elata tubers is substantially thicker than the periarbuscular or peri-peloton interfaces characteristic of AM and typical OMF systems. In AM symbioses, the interfacial matrix remains relatively thin and membrane-associated, facilitating efficient bidirectional nutrient exchange [5]. By contrast, the interface in G. elata is characterized by a massive host-derived matrix (Fig. 4e, f), reflecting a fundamentally different architectural investment. This structural intensification suggests that G. elata prioritizes the spatial containment of the highly invasive hyphae, even as it retains potential transport connectivity through specialized transport mechanisms.

The marked increase in interface thickness in G. elata likely reflects the unique ecological and physiological constraints imposed by a fully mycoheterotrophic lifestyle. Given that G. elata depends entirely on A. tabescens for carbon ultimately derived from wood decomposition, the host must permit extensive fungal colonization while simultaneously preventing uncontrolled hyphal expansion and tissue degradation. A thickened interface may therefore function as a physical and spatial buffer, separating metabolically active fungal hyphae from host protoplasts and limiting the extent of fungal intrusion. These features are consistent with a spatially regulated host response that both accommodates fungal ingress and constrains its expansion, thereby safeguarding host tissue integrity in the presence of an otherwise pathogenic fungus.

Extensin enrichment as a key biochemical feature of the symbiotic interface

To investigate the biochemical associated with A. tabescens colonization, CoMPP was employed to quantify the relative abundance of specific cell wall epitopes in both mycorrhizal and non-mycorrhizal tubers. Our CoMPP analysis reveals a pronounced and selective increase in JIM20-reactive extensin epitopes within colonized tubers (Fig. 3). Notably, a remarkable contrast was observed between the strong JIM20 signal and the weak LM1 signal, despite both antibodies recognizing HRGPs-associated epitopes. JIM20 recognizes highly arabinosylated extensin motifs, whereas LM1 detects a distinct HRGP epitope [31, 32]. This divergence likely reflects differences in epitope specificity and the modification status of extensins within the cell wall. The weak LM1 signal suggests that the epitope recognized by LM1 may become less accessible during cell wall remodeling, potentially as a consequence of extensin cross-linking or epitope masking within the complex wall matrix. These results indicate that Armillaria colonization is associated with substantial changes in extensin epitope composition and accessibility within host cell walls. It should be noted that the present study primarily relied on the extensin-specific antibody JIM20 together with a limited number of AGP-specific antibodies. Therefore, our conclusions mainly reflect the distribution and potential function of extensin-enriched HRGP domains. The potential contributions of other HRGP subclasses, such as proline-rich proteins (PRPs), were not investigated and therefore remain to be determined.

Immunocytochemical labeling further demonstrated that these epitopes are highly enriched at wall modification sites, specifically within the hypha-encasing domains of colonized cells and the walls of adjacent boundary cells (Fig. 4). This localized accumulation suggests that extensin deposition and cross-linking are strategically deployed at mechanical hotspots to reinforce host cell walls against fungal colonization. Extensin-enriched HRGPs increase cell wall recalcitrance and reduce extensibility through oxidative cross-linking with other wall polymers [33]. In AM symbioses, HRGPs deposition has been proposed to include both the limitation of microorganism spread within host tissues and the de novo synthesis of cell wall-like material at the host-microbe interface [34]. Similarly, in Rhizobium-infected nodules, localized HRGPs accumulation is associated with interface differentiation rather than the activation of a generalized defense response [35]. Consistent with these interpretations, extensin-enriched HRGPs have also been shown to accumulate at fungal entry sites in germinating orchid seeds and in the basal region of protocorms of Dendrobium officinale [23]. Such localized enrichment suggests that extensin-enriched HRGPs contribute to defining and maintaining the symbiotic region by reinforcing compartmental structure and spatially restricting fungal proliferation. Notably, the accumulation of extensin-enriched HRGPs in the basal protocorm, where active colonization occurs, contrasts with their absence in the apical meristem, supporting a role for extensin-enriched HRGPs in establishing a polarized symbiotic zone that preserves meristem integrity.

Extensin-mediated interface differentiation enables structural control while retaining potential transport capacity

In mycorrhizal symbiosis, the interface serves as a functional apoplastic compartment where essential nutrient and signal exchange occurs between plant and fungal cells [4, 7]. In orchid mycorrhizal symbioses, however, the biochemical composition of this interface remains poorly understood. Available studies indicate that the interfacial matrix is dynamically modified in relation to hyphal status. For instance, in Rhizoctonia-associated orchids, such as Platanthera hyperborea, Spiranthes lacera and Spiranthes sinensis, pectic and cellulose-related epitopes are notably absent at interfaces involving active hyphae, appearing only within the matrix surrounding collapsing pelotons [36]. Furthermore, direct evidence for glycoproteins within the orchid interfacial matrix is limited. To date, Li et al. [23] reported that JIM11-reactive HRGPs are localized to the matrix of both active and degenerating hyphae in Dendrobium. In this study, we observed a distinct spatial and quantitative distribution of extensin-enriched HRGPs in G. elata. Our findings reveal a massive and localized accumulation of JIM20 epitopes within the markedly thickened cell walls surrounding active A. tabescens hyphae (Fig. 4e, f). Notably, such pronounced deposition of JIM20 epitopes in the interfacial matrix has not been reported in autotrophic, Rhizoctonia-associated orchids. This divergence suggests that mycoheterotrophic hosts may require more rigorous structural control to manage the highly invasive nature of Armillaria, a fungus characterized by its potent pathogenicity [25].

HRGPs are well established components of plant defense, where their accumulation restricts pathogen invasion at the site of entry and is followed by rapid insolubilization through oxidative cross-linking within the cell wall [33, 37, 38]. Although HRGPs are classically associated with plant defense, accumulating evidence indicates that their deployment is highly context dependent and not restricted to antagonistic interactions. In symbiotic systems, host cells actively remodel the interface through de novo synthesis of cell wall-like materials In AM symbioses, HRGPs and cellulose are deposited at the interface between intracellular fungal structures and the host plasma membrane [6, 21]. Notably, HRGPs accumulation in these symbiotic studies does not necessarily align with a classical defense response. While Gianinazzi-Pearson et al. [39] observed that defense responses in AM symbioses are often transient, other studies indicate that genes typically associated with plant immunity remain only weakly expressed during successful colonization [40, 41]. Similarly, transcriptomic analyses of protocorms of chlorophyllous orchids associated with rhizoctonia-group fungi revealed gene expression patterns consistent with compatible symbiotic interactions, characterized by moderated defense activation alongside enhanced metabolic and transport functions [42]. However, such developmental compatibility may not fully explain the G. elata-Armillaria association, where the fungal partner exhibits invasive growth characteristics uncommon among typical orchid mycorrhizal fungi.

In G. elata, this persistent structural reinforcement represents a specialized adaptation rather than a failed defense. Despite the physical barrier formed by JIM20-rich extensins, these sites remain metabolically functional, as demonstrated by the localized up-regulation of the sucrose transporter GeSUT4 [43]. The co-localization of structural reinforcement and active transport machinery suggests that G. elata employs a finely tuned strategy to modulate host immunity. By coupling structural compartmentalization with active nutrient transport, G. elata effectively delimits fungal spread while simultaneously maintaining a high-capacity interface for nutrient acquisition. This balanced response allows G. elata to exploit Armillaria as a carbon source without triggering a self-destructive systemic defense response.

Functional validation of HRGPs using chemical perturbation

Chemical perturbation using 3,4-DHP has been widely used to investigate the structural role of HRGPs in plant cell walls [44]. As a potent proline analogue, DHP selectively inhibits proline hydroxylation, resulting in the synthesis of structurally aberrant HRGPs and impaired cell wall assembly [45, 46]. In Dendrobium symbiotic germination, 3,4-DHP treatment led to reduced expression of HRGP epitopes and extensive fungal invasion throughout protocorms [23]. This loss of structural control results in developmental arrest and the eventual death of the protocorm, indicating that extensin-enriched HRGPs contribute not only to cell wall structure but also to the spatial regulation of host-fungus interactions during orchid development. Our results demonstrate a conserved functional role for extensin-enriched HRGPs in the G. elata-Armillaria symbiosis. Disruption of extensin-enriched HRGPs accumulation led to significantly reduced tuber growth (Figs. 6 and 7), enhanced fungal colonization, and extensive cortical necrosis accompanied by the collapse of host cell wall structures (Fig. 8a). Concomitantly, JIM20 labeling at the symbiotic interface was markedly reduced following 3,4-DHP treatment (Fig. 8b, d). Together, these observations indicate that HRGP-rich wall domains are essential for maintaining the integrity of the symbiotic interface and enforcing the spatial restriction of fungal growth, thereby preventing the transition from symbiosis to pathogenesis.

Conclusion

Our study reveals that extensive cell wall remodeling and the localized accumulation of extensin-enriched HRGPs are central to the establishment and maintenance of the G. elata-Armillaria symbiosis. By integrating ultrastructural analyses, glycomic profiling, immunolocalization, and functional perturbation, we demonstrate that extensin-enriched wall domains form a specialized host-fungus interface that spatially constrains fungal growth while preserving potential transport capacity. The formation of this reinforced interface reflects a finely tuned mechanism that limits excessive fungal intrusion while maintaining a functional symbiotic state. This localized wall reinforcement, together with extensin deposition, is consistent with a coordinated remodeling process associated with controlled fungal accommodation and the absence of a deleterious systemic defense response. Collectively, our findings indicate the importance of interface architecture in fully mycoheterotrophic systems and provide insight into the role of host-controlled wall specialization in balancing accommodation and restriction during plant-fungus interactions.
