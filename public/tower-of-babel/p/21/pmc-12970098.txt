Strategies for assessing posthospitalisation outcomes in trauma survivors and their feasibility of implementation: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Marylie Laberge Sévigny, Lynne Moore, Belinda J Gabbe, Juanita Haagsma, Elizabeth Wake, Barbara Haas, Diana Skaff, Robin Green, Colleen Trevino, Jeanette Finstad, Marc-Aurèle Gagnon, Pierre-Luc Deziel, Mélanie Bérubé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEach year, physical traumas affect over one billion people worldwide, generating a substantial burden in terms of mortality, disability and productivity loss. The period following hospital discharge, encompassing the transition to home, short-term stays in rehabilitation facilities, as well as outpatient and community-based follow-up care, represents a critical phase in the recovery process for trauma injury patients. Yet, this phase remains poorly documented. We aim to (1) Map data on assessment strategies for various outcomes among trauma survivors after hospital discharge, including the tools used and how they are administered, as well as the resources required, and the barriers and facilitators to their implementation, and (2) Compare the feasibility of implementing the assessment strategies while considering response rates, resource use, costs and sustainability.Methods and analysisWe will conduct a scoping review using the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology. We will search MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus and PsycINFO for studies published since 1990 to reflect the evolution of contemporary follow-up practices, including the emergence of Patient-Reported Outcome Measures and digital tools. There will be no language restrictions. We will consider all studies involving trauma survivors, focusing on the evaluation of postdischarge health outcomes. Two independent reviewers will screen studies and extract data on population characteristics, assessment strategies and feasibility. Results will be analysed thematically and presented narratively. We will present counts and percentages for each assessment strategy, along with its characteristics and associated barriers and facilitators. Subgroup analyses will also be conducted based on clinical and social determinants and contextual factors.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required for this review. The results of this scoping review will be shared through publication in a peer-reviewed journal, conference presentations and our network of knowledge users.Open science framework registration numberDOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/KZHS4

Introduction

Each year, more than one billion people experience traumatic injuries worldwide.1 Although recent progress in trauma care has significantly reduced post-trauma mortality, survivors still face a substantial ongoing burden.2 Traumatic injuries are among the leading causes of disability1 3 4 and account for the highest number of productive years of life lost.4

The period following hospital discharge, including the transition to short-term stays in rehabilitation facilities, the return home and the long-term outpatient or community-based follow-up care, represents a critical phase in the recovery process for individuals who have experienced traumatic injuries. Yet, this continuum of care remains poorly documented.58 Trauma patients are at risk of developing long-term complications such as decreased function, persistent pain and reduced quality of life.9 These risks may be exacerbated in the absence of appropriate, coordinated postdischarge support, increasing the strain on health systems and society.10 In this context, the development of integrated, patient-centred models to assess posthospitalisation outcomes, which are distinct from the provision of long-term care, is essential to better capture the functional, psychological and social recovery of survivors.1 4 11

Current quality of care assessment methods, such as trauma registries, typically focus on health outcomes during hospitalisation or in the short term, neglecting the patients’ long-term continuum of care.12 13 Furthermore, posthospitalisation assessments rely primarily on objective health outcomes (eg, mortality and re-admission rates) or administrative outcomes (service use, costs).14 15 While these outcomes are important, they capture only a limited part of the patient’s long-term lived experience.13 16 In this regard, Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) can be valuable complementary tools, enabling patients to assess their own health status at specific time points.17 When combined with objective health outcomes, they offer a more comprehensive and nuanced view of recovery trajectories.18

Despite their documented value, using PROMs as part of trauma follow-up remains inconsistent across jurisdictions. Some trauma systems, such as those in Australia19 and the Netherlands,13 have successfully integrated longitudinal PROMs into routine practice. However, many other countries still face significant challenges to their implementation, leading them to focus on isolated outcome assessment strategies rather than a comprehensive or integrated approach.18 Likewise, variability in administration methods (eg, self-administration vs professional-led, timing of assessments, type of personnel involved), heterogeneity in the tools used and multiple barriers associated with data collection processes (eg, required human and material resources, low patient response rates) have been noted.20 21 Nonetheless, several facilitating factors have been identified, including the integration of outcome assessment strategies into routine care, the involvement of patients and their families, and the use of new technologies, namely digital tools like mobile health applications, web-based monitoring platforms and electronic symptom trackers.717 19 2224

To date, knowledge syntheses on the long-term follow-up of trauma patients have focused on describing the most frequently assessed outcomes and the measurement tools being used.25 26 However, none of these syntheses have addressed the processes involved in implementing these measurement strategies. To develop more coherent, accessible and sustainable long-term outcome assessment strategies in trauma survivors, it is crucial to describe the resources needed for their implementation after hospital discharge. It is also important to identify the barriers and facilitators to their feasibility, in order to inform future outcome assessment models.7 27 28 Ultimately, the effective implementation of such models can improve the continuity of care and support the long-term recovery of trauma survivors.

Methods and analysis

We will conduct a scoping review based on the six-step methodological framework developed by Arksey and O’Malley: (1) Identifying the research question, (2) Identifying relevant studies, (3) Study selection, (4) Data extraction and analysis, (5) Collating, summarising and reporting the results, and (6) Stakeholder consultation to enrich or validate the findings.29 30

This protocol was developed in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines for scoping reviews,31 32 and the results will be reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist.31 33 We started the review in November 2025, with the aim of completing it by February 2027. This protocol has been preregistered on Open Science Framework (DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/KZHS4).

Step 1: Identifying the research question

This scoping review aims to map the nature and extent of available data on strategies used to measure outcomes in trauma injury survivors following hospital discharge. It will focus on outcome measurements such as PROMs and other types of clinical, functional, psychological, social or administrative indicators, from routinely collected data or directly from patients. This review will not address follow-up care delivery, but rather how outcomes are collected and assessed postdischarge.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria are based on the population/concept/context framework for scoping reviews, as recommended in the methodological guidelines of the JBI.34

Population

We will include all studies reporting outcome assessment strategies for adult or paediatric patients who have sustained traumatic injuries (ie, traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, visceral organ injuries, orthopaedic injuries, burns). Patients will need to have been admitted to a hospital or acute care facility for treatment of a traumatic injury, regardless of mechanism.

Concept

We will focus on structured strategies that assess the postdischarge period following hospitalisation for trauma. Studies must address concrete modalities of health data collection after hospital discharge, whether administered in person, by telephone, electronically or through alternative patient-centred approaches (eg, mailed surveys, proxy interviews, community-based assessments). We will include studies that report proxy PROMs (eg, completed by caregivers or family members) when the patients are children or adults physically or cognitively unable to complete them themselves, whether due to injury-related impairment (eg, severe TBI) or pre-existing conditions (eg, dementia, intellectual disability).3538

Context

We will include all studies conducted after hospital discharge, across various postdischarge settings, whether clinical (eg, outpatient visits, specialised trauma clinics, rehabilitation centres), community-based (eg, community support services, home care organisations, trauma survivor support groups) or home-based (eg, home visits by healthcare professionals, telephone follow-up, telemedicine or digital self-assessment platforms). All geographical locations and models of care will be considered, as long as posthospital outcomes are documented. We will exclude studies without a follow-up period after hospital discharge, conducted exclusively in military settings with no plan for generalisation, and reporting on a follow-up conducted solely as part of a research project, with no intention of integration into a trauma care programme or system.

We will include publications from 1990 onwards to reflect the evolution of contemporary follow-up practices, including the emergence of PROMs and digital tools, namely mobile health applications, web-based monitoring platforms and electronic symptom trackers.40 There will be no language restrictions. Articles published in languages other than that of the research team members, that is, English, Arabic, Dutch, French, Italian, Norwegian, Portuguese, will be translated using the DeepL Translator tool.41

Source of evidence selection

All study designs will be accepted, including quantitative, qualitative and mixed-methods studies, provided they clearly describe how they assessed the results relevant to a healthcare system. We will exclude single case studies and conference abstracts (ie, abstracts without a full article available) and research protocols.

Step 2: Identifying relevant studies

Databases

We will search for relevant studies in the following databases: PubMed (National Institute of Health), Excerpta Medica database (Embase) (Ovid), Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Literature (CINAHL) (EBSCOHost), Scopus, PsycInfo (Ovid). Additionally, we will explore dissertations, theses and academic reports via ProQuest. To ensure comprehensive coverage, we will also screen the reference lists of all included articles, dissertations and theses to identify any additional eligible studies. Our goal is to document how strategies are implemented and their feasibility, rather than simply identifying which measurement tools are used. Therefore, only peer-reviewed literature providing this contextual information will be eligible.

Search strategy

In accordance with the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies guidelines,42 a rigorous systematic search strategy will be developed in collaboration with an information specialist. The strategy will then undergo an independent peer review by a librarian. Our search strategy will be based on a combination of free-text terms and controlled vocabulary, including terms such as: trauma, injury, follow-up, PROMs, feasibility, barriers and facilitators.

We will begin with a pilot search in Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE) (via PubMed) and Embase (via Ovid) to identify additional relevant keywords. Key terms extracted from the titles, abstracts and full texts of selected studies will be collected and integrated into the final strategy. This enriched strategy will then be applied across all selected databases to ensure comprehensive coverage.

To ensure the sensitivity, specificity and overall effectiveness of the search strategy, we will use a predefined set of eligibility criteria to select a group of sentinel studies.7 9 18 20 24 27 39 43 These studies will represent a benchmark to evaluate the search strategy’s performance. Sensitivity will be assessed by confirming that all sentinel studies meeting the criteria were successfully retrieved, and specificity by examining whether the strategy avoids retrieving an unmanageable volume of irrelevant citations, which could compromise the feasibility of the screening process. Step 3: Study selection

We will use EndNote (V.X9.3.3, New York City: Thomson Reuters, 2018) for citation management. Duplicate records will be identified and removed using both automated and manual methods. Two reviewers (MLS, M-AG) will independently screen the titles, abstracts and full texts of the studies. To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the selection process, they will follow a systematic and iterative approach, testing the screening process on a random sample of 250 citations. This process will be refined until an acceptable level of inter-rater agreement is achieved, based on methodological standards (kappa >0.8).44

In the case of disagreement, a senior member of the research team (MB) will be consulted to resolve the conflict. Finally, we will document the search strategy, selection process and reasons for exclusion in detail using a PRISMA flow diagram45 to ensure transparency and traceability throughout the study selection process.

Step 4: Data extraction

We have developed standardised data extraction forms using the JBI template and Microsoft Excel (V.16.0, Microsoft Corporation, 2018, Redmond, Washington, USA), which are available in onlinesupplemental digital files 1 2. These forms will be iteratively tested by two reviewers (MLS, M-AG) with both methodological and subject-matter expertise. The test will be conducted on a random sample of five studies representing the scope of included studies, and the form will be refined until an acceptable level of inter-rater agreement is reached, in line with methodological standards (kappa >0.8).44 Data will be extracted from the original publications by two reviewers (MLS, M-AG). Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third team member (MB or LM), as needed.

Regarding the study context, the following information will be collected: country, year of publication, research design, funding and implementation site (eg, trauma system, provincial or national health authority, hospital-based, community-based). As for the study population, reviewers will extract the total number of participants and their distribution across various variables: type and severity of trauma, sex, gender, age group, ethnocultural background, socioeconomic status and place of residence (urban or rural). We will also document the outcome assessment strategies used after hospital discharge. This will include the nature of the indicators assessed, the PROMs or other measurement tools used, the mode of administration (eg, in person, virtual, hybrid), any specific adaptations made to meet patients’ particular needs (eg, questionnaires filled by a proxy, family members or with support from healthcare professionals), the timing and the frequency of assessments and where the questionnaires were administered (eg, home, outpatient clinic).

In addition, the interventions’ feasibility analysis will be guided by the Proctor’s Implementation Outcomes framework. Accordingly, the following elements will be extracted: response rate (the proportion of eligible patients who completed the questionnaires), identified barriers and facilitators, as well as associated direct costs (eg, staff salaries, equipment purchases, software licences, training expenses) and indirect costs (eg, time required from clinical personnel, workflow disruptions or productivity losses related to implementation). The sustainability of the implemented assessment strategies will also be examined.46 We will extract data in line with the conceptual domains identified in recent sustainability models, including the integration of assessment strategies with broader systems (eg, use of data for auditing practices and providing feedback) and the persistence of outcome assessments over time.47 We will also collect information on patient engagement strategies and any equity-related considerations regarding the implementation of the assessment strategies.48 The data extraction process will include the documentation of ethical concerns (eg, the lack of an ethics statement or recruitment and data collection issues). These will be reported transparently in the synthesis.49 Where available, we will examine stratified response rates to identify differential participation across subgroups. Particular attention will be given to populations at increased risk of being excluded from follow-up care, such as individuals with no fixed address or those living with mental health or substance use disorders. This will help highlight the barriers faced by these groups and potential solutions to address these intersecting determinants.

Any disagreements between reviewers will be resolved through consensus-based discussions. In the event of persistent disagreement, a senior member of the research team (MB or LM) will be consulted to arbitrate the decision. If essential information is missing or unclear, we will contact the first, second and last authors of the corresponding study, sending up to three emails to request clarification.

Step 5: Collating, summarising and reporting the results

The findings of this scoping review will be compiled, synthesised and presented using both descriptive and analytical methods, in accordance with JBI guidelines34 and the PRISMA-ScR checklist.50 As previously mentioned, once study selection is complete, data will be extracted and organised into a table summarising the type of outcomes and measures used, follow-up modalities, implementation strategies and feasibility elements.

The results will be synthesised using a descriptive and thematic approach, highlighting the main trends, variations observed across the included studies and research gaps. To improve the interpretability of the findings, we will construct a mapping table that links the types of outcomes assessed (eg, PROMs, clinical, functional, psychological, social, administrative) and the other modalities involved (eg, mode of administration, frequency of assessments) with key feasibility elements such as response rates, timing of follow-up, resources mobilised and reported costs. This cross-classification will support the identification of patterns between various posthospitalisation assessment strategies and help compare the feasibility of their implementation.

In addition to this mapping and thematic analysis (qualitative analysis), we will record the frequency of specific characteristics related to the outcomes/tools and the barriers and facilitators associated with their implementation using counts and proportions. This will improve our understanding of the key issues to consider in the implementation process.

Subgroup analyses will be conducted, data permitting, to explore variations in the posthospitalisation assessment strategies and feasibility dimensions across different participant-related and contextual factors. These factors will include the type of trauma, participant age group, mode of administration of the assessment tools (eg, in-person, online, hybrid) and healthcare system context (eg, low-income, middle-income and high-income countries). This stratified synthesis will help account for heterogeneity across injury types and clinical contexts, identifying which strategies are more feasible, acceptable or sustainable depending on the setting and population characteristics. This will strengthen the contextual relevance and transferability of the findings.

The results will be presented in various formats to support interpretation and dissemination. Descriptive tables will summarise the characteristics of the studies and strategies, as well as their feasibility and key findings, while figures and graphs will provide a clear representation of trends and comparisons.

Step 6: Stakeholder consultation

An advisory committee was consulted to design this protocol, to ensure the relevance and applicability of the findings. This committee included experts from high-income, middle-income and low-income countries, representing various disciplines involved in trauma care and postdischarge follow-up, such as epidemiology, health data protection and enhancement, trauma surgery, nursing, rehabilitation, psychology, social work and primary care. Furthermore, individuals with lived experience, as well as healthcare decision-makers and administrators, were also involved.

The consultation process aimed to: (1) Identify any additional references that could enrich the analysis; (2) Gather feedback on which elements to include, and how to present and interpret the findings; and (3) Explore opportunities for knowledge mobilisation and how to apply the results in clinical practice and health policy.

To optimise collaboration, we will schedule periodic meetings (in person or virtual) with the members of the advisory committee at key milestones of the review process. These meetings will include structured group discussions and guided feedback sessions. In addition, we will use online tools such as questionnaires, shared documents and electronic surveys to systematically collect comments, suggestions and evaluations. This mixed-methods approach will help capture both individual and collective perspectives and ensure their integration at different stages of the scoping review. This approach is aligned with best practices in stakeholder engagement for knowledge syntheses, which recommend combining synchronous and asynchronous data collection methods to ensure broad participation and the integration of varying perspectives.32 51 52

Discussion

The results of this scoping review will provide an up-to-date and structured overview of strategies to assess outcomes in trauma survivors after hospital discharge. By identifying the strategies employed, their characteristics, the resources mobilised, as well as the barriers and facilitators to their implementation, this synthesis will offer an essential knowledge base to support the development of more integrated, accessible and sustainable long-term assessment strategies within trauma care systems.

The expected impacts extend to both research and clinical practice. This review will help inform methodological choices for future evaluation and interventional studies, while also guiding clinicians and decision makers on favourable conditions for the integration of outcomes and assessment tools into post-trauma care pathways. The findings will also highlight current gaps in the literature, helping us establish research priorities.

This scoping review presents certain limitations. In accordance with the recommendations for scoping reviews29 31 34 45 and due to the wide variety of study designs, we will not assess the methodological quality of the included studies. We do not plan to conduct a meta-analysis, as this scoping review is descriptive and exploratory. We also expect to find discrepancies in the measurement tools used and the data available on the feasibility of implementing the reported assessment strategies. Hence, we will not be able to determine the overall strength of the evidence. However, this scoping review could help us identify strategies specific to the assessment of posthospitalisation outcomes that could be suitable for a future meta-analysis. Third, the anticipated variability in the assessment strategies may limit comparability across studies and the conclusions that can be drawn. Fourth, given the variability in assessment strategies across healthcare contexts and systems, some results may not be generalisable. However, we plan to conduct subgroup analyses, when the data are available, based on variables such as World Bank country income categories. In addition, an advisory committee with healthcare professionals, policymakers and individuals with lived experience from high-income and middle/low-income countries will be involved throughout the review process. This will ensure that the findings are relevant and applicable, thereby enhancing their transferability to clinical practice and health policy.

Ethics and dissemination

As no primary data will be collected, ethics approval is not required as per the Research Ethics Board of the CHU de Québec-Université Laval (ethiquedelarecherche@chudequebec.ca). Although this review does not require ethics approval, we will document and report on whether the included studies obtained ethics approval and informed consent, in line with recommendations from the JBI31 32 and the Committee on Publication Ethics.49 53

The results of this scoping review will be shared through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presentations at conference meetings.
