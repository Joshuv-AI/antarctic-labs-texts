Arthrodesis using Iliac Crest autograft in revision surgery of the first metatarsophalangeal joint total arthroplasty for the treatment of Hallux Rigidus: a retrospective study
Authors: Ahmet Yurteri, Numan Mercan, Ahmet Fevzi Kekeç, Ahmet Yıldırım
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe treatments for hallux rigidus (HR) encompass a wide spectrum, including conservative methods and surgical approaches such as arthroplasty and arthrodesis. This study aims to evaluate the outcomes of revision with arthrodesis following the failure of the first metatarsophalangeal joint total arthroplasty (MTPJ1TA).Materials and methodsPatients who had surgery at two advanced orthopedic centers between January 1, 2020, and January 1, 2024, were reviewed. Those with at least 6 months of postoperative follow-up were assessed for demographics (gender, age, side), reoperation rates, complications, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores, Foot and Ankle Disability Index (FADI) scores, and American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society Hallux Metatarsophalangeal and Interphalangeal Scale (AOFAS-HMI) scores. Radiological evaluations included hallux valgus angle (HVA), intermetatarsal angle (IMA), dorsiflexion angle (DFA), and first ray length (FRL) in anteroposterior and lateral views.ResultsOf 27 patients, 4 were male (15%) and 23 female (85%), with an average age of 56.18 ± 7.49 years (range 41–72). Arthrodesis was performed in 26 patients, with one requiring reoperation due to implant failure. Average VAS scores were 7.14 preoperatively and 3.55 postoperatively (p < 0.05). Average FADI scores were 50.51 preoperatively and 71.51 postoperatively (p < 0.05). Average AOFAS-HMI scores were 51.22 preoperatively and 70.59 postoperatively (p < 0.05). Average HVA was 19.7° preoperatively and 6.29° postoperatively (p < 0.05). Average IMA was 10.66° preoperatively and 11.37° postoperatively (p = 0.406). Average DFA was 34.14° preoperatively and 22.33° postoperatively (p < 0.05). Average anteroposterior FRL was 10.17 cm preoperatively and 10.77 cm postoperatively (p < 0.05). Average lateral FRL was 10.12 cm preoperatively and 10.42 cm postoperatively (p < 0.05). None of the patients in the study exhibited postoperative donor site complications or transfer metatarsalgia, while a 100% rate of bone union was observed.ConclusionWe think that revision of MTPJ1TA with arthrodesis is a safe treatment option. Additionally, the use of an iliac crest autograft can be a viable option to restore bone stock and create a biological environment conducive to fusion. This study evaluates revision surgery with arthrodesis in the largest patient group with failed MTPJ1TA, highlighting its significance in the field. However, further studies are needed to determine the ideal surgical procedure.Keywords: Hallux rigidus, Metatarsophalangeal, Arthroplasty, Arthrodesis, Hallux valgus

Introduction

With the aging global population, complaints related to osteoarthritis are increasing, and arthroplasty is becoming increasingly prevalent in the daily practice of orthopedics. Studies have reported high patient satisfaction in arthroplasty operations performed for the osteoarthritis of major weight-bearing joints such as the hip, knee, and ankle. Given this the patient satisfaction achieved through arthroplasty, orthopedic surgeons are also interested in employing this surgical procedure for the osteoarthritis of the first metatarsophalangeal joint (MTPJ1), also known as hallux rigidus (HR) [1–5]. Although arthroplasty for MTPJ1 has been used for decades and continues to be used, the literature presents mixed outcomes, indicating both good and poor results for this method, suggesting a potential inconsistency [6–8]. Additionally, some reports have documented unacceptably high failure and revision rates [9–11].

There is insufficient data in the literature regarding the appropriate treatment for failed MTPJ1 arthroplasty [12, 13]. The situation is particularly challenging in cases of first metatarsophalangeal joint total arthroplasty (MTPJ1TA), where primary application already significantly reduces bone stock and aseptic loosening can further resorb the remaining bone, complicating revision surgery. Arthrodesis, a commonly used method for the primary treatment of HR, can also be applied as a revision surgery for failed MTPJ1TA.

In MTPJ1 arthrodesis, to ensure proper first ray length (FRL) and replace diminished bone stock, surgeons may need to use autografts or allografts. Given the complexity of revision surgery for MTPJ1TA, our study will also examine whether the iliac crest autograft, known for its osteoinductive and osteoconductive properties and abundant availability, is a suitable graft option. This study aims to evaluate the clinical and radiological outcomes obtained after revision of failed MTPJ1TA with arthrodesis.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and planning of the study

This study was approved by the Necmettin Erbakan University Ethics Committee under decision number 2024/4902, and informed consent was obtained from all patients included in the study. Between January 1, 2020, and January 1, 2024, patients who underwent total arthroplasty of the MTPJ1 and subsequently developed aseptic loosening, leading to a transition to MTPJ1 arthrodesis, were retrospectively reviewed at Konya City Hospital Orthopedics and Traumatology Clinic and Necmettin Erbakan University Faculty of Medicine Hospital Orthopedics and Traumatology Clinic. The inclusion criteria were defined as having undergone MTPJ1TA (TOEFIT-PLUS™, Plus Orthopedics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland), experiencing pain complaints, being diagnosed with aseptic loosening, and undergoing MTPJ1 arthrodesis for the first time. Patients who had non-metallic arthroplasties, hemiarthroplasty applied to either the metatarsal or phalangeal side of the joint, a history of periprosthetic infection or septic loosening, or neuroarthropathy were excluded from the study. Hemiarthroplasties and non-metallic arthroplasties were excluded to maintain standardization within the study group, as these procedures require less bone resection compared to MTPJ1 total arthroplasties.

Surgical technique and postoperative rehabilitation

It was observed that all patients in the study underwent revision surgery with arthrodesis under spinal anesthesia. Additional anesthetic techniques such as popliteal nerve block or peripheral nerve block around the ankle can be beneficial for both intraoperative and postoperative anesthesia and analgesia, and it was noted that peripheral nerve block was used in conjunction with spinal anesthesia in 4 patients. In the supine position with a high thigh tourniquet, the entire leg, including the iliac region, was prepped for surgery. The dorsal incision line on the toe (which was the case for all patients included in the study) was used. The extensor hallucis longus tendon was retracted laterally, while the terminal branch of the superficial peroneal nerve, the dorsal cutaneous nerve, was explored and preserved medially. The MTPJ1 capsule was opened longitudinally. The implants with aseptic loosening in both the metatarsal and the phalanx were removed in all patients using a thin osteotome. The fibrous tissues at the bone-implant interface were resected to prevent interference with fusion. For all patients, the required graft size was larger than 1 cm², and the iliac crest was chosen as the donor site for the autograft. There was no addition of artificial bone grafts to the donor location. Once the autograft was placed between the metatarsal and phalanx bones, a Kirschner wire was inserted from the toe tip to the metatarsal shaft to ensure proper alignment in the dorsoplantar axis (Fig. 1). Subsequently, a dorsal 2.0–2.4 mm miniplate, such as those from the Variable Angle LCP® Foot System by DePuy Synthes (a part of Johnson & Johnson MedTech, headquartered in New Brunswick, New Jersey, USA) or the Arthrex Low Profile Straight Plate series (headquartered in Naples, Florida, USA), or a midfoot plate was placed according to the surgeon’s preference. If the stability was deemed insufficient by the surgeon, a secondary plate was added.

(A) Removal of the prosthesis containing the polyethylene insert (B) Debridement of fibrotic tissues from the metatarsal and phalanx (C) Filling the gap with an iliac crest autograft and temporary fixation of the bones with a Kirschner wire (D) Application of arthrodesis with a plate

Postoperatively, patients were advised to use a short leg splint for the first 4 weeks and to avoid weight-bearing on the foot. After 4 weeks, patients were recommended to start partial weight-bearing using a flat, rigid-soled orthopedic shoe and to progress to full weight-bearing as tolerated.

Clinical and radiological assessment

The demographic characteristics of the patients included in the study (gender, age, side), reoperation rates, and postoperative major and minor complications were recorded. Clinical and functional scores of the patients were assessed using the Visual Analogue Score (VAS), the Foot and Ankle Disability Index (FADI) [14], and the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society Hallux Metatarsophalangeal and Interphalangeal Scale (AOFAS-HMI) [15]. Radiological evaluations included measurements of the hallux valgus angle (HVA), intermetatarsal angle (IMA), dorsiflexion angle (DFA), and first ray length (FRL). The HVA was measured as the angle between the longitudinal axes of the first metatarsal and the proximal phalanx in the anteroposterior plane. The IMA was measured as the angle between the longitudinal axes of the first and second metatarsals [16]. The DFA was measured as the angle between the longitudinal axes of the first metatarsal and the proximal phalanx in the lateral plane [16]. The first ray length (FRL) was measured in both anteroposterior (FRL-AP) and lateral (FRL-L) radiographs as the distance between a line drawn from the distal point of the distal phalanx of the first toe and a line at the base of the longitudinal axis of the first metatarsal (Fig. 2) [17, 18].

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) was used for data analysis (Graph Pad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA). Skewness and kurtosis values were examined to determine whether continuous variables (VAS, FADI, AOFAS-HMI, HVA, IMA, DFA, FRL-AP, and FRL-L) fit a normal distribution. Repeated Measures ANOVA test was used for comparing the postoperative values of the groups with the preoperative values. The minimum, maximum, median, mean, and standard deviation (SD) values of these parameters were recorded, and the level of significance was determined as a p value < 0.05 in all tests.

Results

Of the 27 patients who met the inclusion criteria, 4 were male (15%) and 23 were female (85%), with a mean age of 56.18 ± 7.49 years (range 41–72 years). The right foot was affected in 15 patients (56%), and the left foot in 12 patients (44%). Patients were followed for an average of 16.07 ± 8.78 months (range 6–34 months). Arthrodesis with single-stage revision was achieved in 26 out of 27 patients (96.2%), while one patient (3.7%) who had single-plate arthrodesis developed implant failure and subsequently underwent re-arthrodesis with dual plates. It was observed that a single plate was applied to 24 patients, while a secondary plate was added to 3 patients due to insufficient stabilization with a single plate. Patients were followed for an average of 16.07 ± 8.78 months (range 6–34 months). All patients received an autograft from the iliac crest as the bone graft. Arthrodesis with single-stage revision was achieved in 26 out of 27 patients (96.2%), while one patient (3.7%) who had single-plate arthrodesis developed implant failure and subsequently underwent re-arthrodesis with dual plates. Major complications included reoperation due to implant failure in this single patient (Fig. 3). In this patient, the failed 2.0 mm single miniplate was removed and revised with two 2.4 mm plates, resulting in successful fusion upon follow-up. Minor complications included implant irritation in one patient and serous discharge from the iliac crest bone graft donor site lasting 10 days in another patient (7.4%). At the final follow-up, all patients demonstrated successful fusion. All patients returned to their daily activities and work.

X-ray imaging of a patient with implant failure (A) Anteroposterior view (B) Lateral view (C) Oblique view

In this study, none of the patients required specific sets for revision surgery. The implants were removed using a thin osteotome. Signs of metallosis were observed in three patients from whom implants were removed (Fig. 4). In these patients, aggressive debridement was performed to excise the metal debris. The surgeons reported that significant shortening of the first ray occurred intraoperatively following implant removal. To address the potential postoperative complications such as transfer metatarsalgia due to this shortening, physiological length was restored by placing an autograft from the iliac crest into the bone defect. No instances of transfer metatarsalgia were observed during patient follow-ups. Another intraoperative finding was petechial bleeding on the metatarsal bone surfaces after implant removal and debridement, while the proximal phalanx bone surfaces exhibited less bleeding and more fibrotic soft tissue. This suggests that greater care may be needed to ensure union between the proximal phalanx and the iliac crest autograft, highlighting a potential risk of nonunion. None of the patients included in the study experienced donor site complications.

(A) Appearance of the prosthesis causing metallosis before opening the joint capsule (B) Appearance of metallic debris after capsulotomy (C) Post-debridement appearance after removal of metal debris (D) Application of arthrodesis with a plate

The average preoperative VAS, FADI, and AOFAS-HMI scores were significantly improved postoperatively from 7.14 to 3.55, 50.51 to 71.51, and 51.22 to 70.59, respectively (p < 0.05). Radiologically, the preoperative IMA, HVA, DFA, FRL-AP, and FRL-L values were 10.66°, 19.7°, 34.14°, 10.17 cm, and 10.12 cm, respectively. Postoperatively, the values for HVA, DFA, FRL-AP, and FRL-L significantly improved to 6.29°, 22.33°, 10.77 cm, and 10.42 cm, respectively (p < 0.05), while IMA showed no significant change (p = 0.406). Discussion

MTPJ1 arthrodesis has become a more effective and reliable surgical treatment for HR and other forefoot disorders, both in primary and revision surgeries. This study examines the revision of 27 patients who underwent MTPJ1TA with iliac crest bone block fusion and evaluates their clinical and radiographic outcomes. It was observed that 26 out of 27 patients achieved fusion with an iliac crest autograft in a single stage and were able to return to their daily activities.

Despite the high interest in MTPJ1 arthrodesis, complications such as loss of joint motion, transfer metatarsalgia, and interphalangeal arthritis have led surgeons to explore alternative treatment methods [19, 20]. To preserve joint movement, resurfacing hemiarthroplasty and MTPJ1TA have been attempted. However, both arthroplasty methods, which are still in use today, have been reported to have high complication rates, raising significant concerns. Therefore, surgeons performing MTPJ1 arthroplasty must also be well-equipped for revision surgeries. The literature lacks a specific consensus and sufficient data on this subject. In our research, the most comprehensive study we found was a review by Eric So et al., who analyzed six studies involving 76 patients [21]. This review reported a high nonunion rate of 16.5%. However, the study’s standardization was compromised by several factors, including patients’ diverse surgical histories (silicone implants, hemiarthroplasty, total arthroplasty), different implants used for arthrodesis stabilization (screws, Steinmann pins, plates), and varying graft types (allografts, iliac crest autografts, calcaneal autografts, femoral head autografts, distal tibia autografts). In our study, the consistency of these parameters across all patients and the higher fusion rates make our findings potentially more valuable. Our study demonstrates that using similar parameters for all patients can lead to higher fusion rates, emphasizing the potential benefits of standardized surgical techniques and graft choices.

The main challenge in revision surgery for failed MTPJ1TA is the limited amount of available bone stock compared to other arthroplasty revisions (such as hemiarthroplasty or silicone arthroplasty). In cases where MTPJ1 hemiarthroplasty has failed and an arthrodesis is planned for revision, sufficient bone stock may allow for direct fusion of the proximal phalanx to the metatarsal without the need for a graft. However, in MTPJ1TA or when there is significant loss in the length of the first metatarsal, restoring the FRL necessitates the use of an autograft or allograft. Bone allografts, though relatively costlier and with lower fusion rates, provide an alternative. Autografts are typically sourced from the iliac crest, but they can also be harvested from the proximal metaphysis of the tibia. In their study, Gross et al. examined 12 patients with failed MTPJ1 arthroplasty who underwent arthrodesis using allografts and autografts (from the iliac crest, calcaneus, and metatarsal head). They reported nonunion in 16.7% (2 patients) and delayed union in 41.7% [22]. Myerson et al. evaluated 24 patients who had various prior surgical interventions (silastic arthroplasty, bunionectomy, distal metatarsal osteotomy, Keller resection arthroplasty, and MTPJ1TA) and subsequently underwent revision arthrodesis with either autografts or allografts, reporting a fusion rate of 79% [23]. Compared to these studies, our study demonstrated a higher fusion rate. Achieving arthrodesis in the MTPJ1 can be challenging due to its distal location and relatively low perfusion. This anatomical disadvantage is further compounded in MTPJ1TA, where greater bone excision occurs, necessitating larger bone grafts for revision surgery. Despite these adverse conditions, the high fusion rate observed in our study may be attributed to the use of iliac crest autografts. Iliac crest autografts are effective in bone healing and regeneration due to their osteoinductive and osteoconductive properties. Osteoinductive features initiate new bone formation, while osteoconductive properties provide a conducive environment for the growth and establishment of these new bone cells. This dual mechanism makes iliac crest autografts a preferred choice in orthopedic and dental surgeries [24–27]. Brodsky et al., in their study involving 12 patients with insufficient bone stock who underwent MTPJ1 arthrodesis using iliac crest autografts, reported a fusion rate of 92.5% [16]. Similarly, our study observed a high fusion rate, indicating that the use of iliac crest autografts may be an excellent option for such procedures.

Despite the effectiveness of iliac crest autografts, reports indicate complication rates as high as 63% at the donor site. This high rate of complications has prompted some surgeons to explore other donor sites for autografts. Usuelli et al. performed arthrodesis using calcaneal autografts in 12 patients with failed MTPJ1 arthroplasty and reported a nonunion rate of 25% (3 patients) [18]. They noted several advantages of using calcaneal autografts: the proximity of the MTPJ1 to the calcaneus, which eliminates the need for additional surgical site preparation; the sufficiency of regional anesthesia without requiring additional anesthesia; and reduced postoperative pain at the donor site. However, despite these advantages, their fusion rate of 75% was lower compared to the 96% fusion rate observed in our study. Also, no patients experienced donor site complications in our study. This could be attributed to the relatively small size of the autografts required for toe arthrodesis, which may not be substantial enough to cause significant complications at the donor site. This lower risk of complications and high fusion rates suggest that using iliac crest autografts remains a viable and effective option for MTPJ1 arthrodesis, particularly in the context of revision surgery where substantial bone stock restoration is critical.

The primary objective of radiological imaging in this context is to optimize the physiological gait mechanism and prevent biomechanical complications such as transfer metatarsalgia. Ensuring the appropriate FRL is crucial for these purposes. An excessively long FRL can lead to excessive tension in the soft tissue components, potentially causing wound problems due to ischemia. In our study, we observed a significant increase in the FRL compared to the preoperative FRL. This adjustment is essential to re-establish a more natural foot structure and function, which helps in avoiding complications associated with improper alignment and length discrepancies. By achieving this optimal length, we aim to facilitate better weight distribution and minimize the risk of secondary issues such as metatarsalgia.

This finding is consistent with other studies where arthrodesis was performed using grafts [16, 17, 23, 28]. An advantage of our study is the choice of the iliac crest as the donor site, which allows for obtaining more bone graft compared to the calcaneus and proximal tibia, thereby achieving the desired length. We think that the absence of metatarsalgia complaints in the postoperative period may be attributed to the adequate FRL achieved. Furthermore, our study observed a decrease in the postoperative HVA and DFA compared to preoperative measurements, which is consistent with previous studies [18, 21]. This consistency can be explained by the fact that the patients in our study had aseptic loosening following MTPJ1TA, which means they lacked the healthy capsule, ligament, and soft tissue balance necessary to maintain physiological coronal and sagittal plane alignment. As a result, their toes were likely deformed in the medial-lateral or dorsal-plantar directions preoperatively. We are of the opinion that the surgeon corrected the distorted angles through intraoperative adjustments with simple maneuvers.

The primary strength of this study is that all patients had the same indication (aseptic loosening following total MTPJ1TA) and the same surgical technique (using iliac crest autograft with plate and screw osteosynthesis) was applied. To our knowledge, this study provides valuable insights into hallux MTP arthrodesis in a relatively large patient group following failed MTPJ1TA. Among the limitations of our study, the retrospective nature of the study stands out. The inherent characteristics of retrospective studies (such as the collection of data in hindsight and the inability to control for all influencing factors) diminish the study’s strength. Another limitation is the absence of a control group for comparison. Additionally, the surgeries were performed by multiple surgeons using different brands of plates. However, we think this factor did not adversely affect the study’s outcomes due to the surgeons’ substantial orthopedic experience (each having at least five years of experience) and the use of the same surgical technique.

Conclusion

The treatment of failed MTPJ1TA remains underexplored, and there is no consensus on the optimal surgical procedure. Given the susceptibility of MTPJ1TA revisions to complications, we think that arthrodesis with using an iliac crest autograft is a reliable surgical option for restoring bone stock and achieving fusion. The functional scores and radiological measurements obtained in this study support this assertion. This study evaluates revision surgery with arthrodesis in the largest patient group with failed MTPJ1TA, highlighting its significance in the field. However, further studies are needed to determine the ideal surgical procedure.
