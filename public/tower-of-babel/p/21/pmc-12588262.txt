Drivers of canyon incision in the Peruvian Andes: Tectonics, precipitation, and drainage basin capture
Authors: Jennie Plasterr, Nadine McQuarrie, Eitan Shelef, Sarah Falkowski, Paul R. Eizenhöfer, Todd A. Ehlers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The drivers behind the marked 2- to 3-km relief canyons carved into the ca. 3.7-km-high Andean Plateau remain debated, with proposed triggers ranging from plateau surface uplift to precipitation-enhanced erosion. We integrate a surface processes model with published cross-section–derived estimates of structural geometry, kinematics, and shortening rates, as well as precipitation estimates from global atmospheric circulation models, to assess whether the geometry, sequential fault motion, and resulting surface uplift can reproduce plateau morphology. Endorheic basins form where uplift on faults exceeds incision. A decrease in shortening after ~10 Ma allows rivers to compete with uplift and incise into endorheic basins at the plateau edge. Two modeled basin capture events at ~7 to 5 Ma and ~3.5 to 0.5 Ma, coupled with climate-driven discharge fluctuations, markedly increase rivers’ discharge, erosion, and sediment transport, facilitating canyon incision and highlighting the importance of basin captures in canyon formation along the northeastern edge of the Andean Plateau.

INTRODUCTION

The relative controls of climate and tectonics in shaping the morphology of the Andean Plateau in South America remain a subject of persistent debate [e.g., (1–11)]. Recently, the marked 2- to 3-km-deep canyons carved into the ca. 3.7-km-high Andean Plateau (Fig. 1) have become a focal point of this debate, with proponents arguing for climate enhanced erosion (9, 10, 12) versus tectonically driven uplift (11, 13–15). A change in climate at ca. 4 to 3 Ma that increases moisture and precipitation at the eastern mountain front (Fig. 2B) is argued to have increased rainfall along the high and dry eastern plateau edge (10, 16–18), increasing rivers’ discharge, erosion, sediment transport, and, thus, incision (19). Alternatively, arguments in support of tectonic drivers rest on the relationship between surface uplift and steepened streams, which increase the capacity of rivers to incise [e.g., (19–21)]. Modes of proposed tectonic uplift include plateau-wide surface uplift (Fig. 2A) due to the rapid removal of dense lithosphere [e.g., (22)], or more localized uplift of the Eastern Cordillera over an active ramp (23) and/or at the steep mountain front via an active, potentially surface breaking fault (Fig. 2C) (11). Counter to an increase in uplift rates over the past 5 to 10 Myr (due to surface uplift or localized uplift), recent thermo-kinematic models of the Eastern Cordillera of Peru (23, 24) suggest a marked decrease in shortening (and thus structural uplift rate) from ~10 Ma to the Present is required to replicate published thermochronometric cooling ages and encompasses the proposed timing of incision along the plateau’s eastern margin (10). In conjunction with decreased uplift rates is the potential for rivers along the plateau edge to capture endorheic basins within the plateau [e.g., (25, 26)] (Fig. 2D), resulting in sharp increases in discharge, stream power, sediment transport, and erosion (27–29). To evaluate the potential drivers of river incision along the eastern margin of the Andean Plateau in Peru, we integrate available cross-section–derived estimates of fault geometry, kinematics, and shortening rates that best replicate measured thermochronometric ages [e.g., (23)] with a surface processes model (CASCADE) (30, 31) that simulates topographic evolution over geologic timescales. We explore how variations in shortening rates, precipitation patterns, and fluvial erodibility influence modeled landscape development and evaluate the resulting model fit by comparing simulated and observed topographic and drainage metrics.

Topographic and erosional characteristics of the San Gaban region are illustrated by a digital elevation model (Fig. 1B) and a 50-km swath topographic profile (Fig. 1C) across southeastern Peru. These images highlight the northeastward extent of the low-relief plateau surface, which in this region encompasses elevations above 4 km, as well as a sharp elevation transition from 4 to 1 km over a distance of ~30 km. The plateau surface is cut into by a series of deep canyons spaced 50 to 80 km apart (10, 32), and the largest of these is the San Gaban Canyon (Fig. 1B). At the plateau edge, a pronounced change in elevation is colocated with a suite of observations that show focused erosion in the region across multiple time scales (Fig. 1C). This focused erosion along the plateau edge from ~4 to 3 Ma to present may be driven by tectonic uplift [either of the plateau itself (22) or faulting at the plateau edge (11)], a change in climate that increased precipitation, erosion and sediment transport (9, 10, 12), drainage capture of endorheic basins (25), or a combination of these factors (Fig. 2).

For example, apatite (U-Th)/He (AHe) ages [nominal closure temperature of ~68°C (33)] at the base of the canyon and near the northeastern edge of the plateau are as young as 2 to 3 Ma and align spatially with the highest cosmogenic radionuclide catchment-averaged erosion rates (Fig. 1C). In addition, patterns of mean KsnQ, a rainfall-weighted variant of the normalized channel steepness index, also show the highest values in this region (Fig. 1C). The alignment of young cooling ages, high catchment-averaged erosion rates, and high KsnQ values suggest that topography, rainfall, and erosion are in quasi-equilibrium (34) and have been for potentially millions of years (35).

Metrics that evaluate drainage divide stability [Gilbert metrics (36) and χ] highlight two regions of high relief and high gradient: one at the plateau edge and the other within the middle reaches of the San Gaban Canyon [Fig. 1 (black outlined boxes) and fig. S1]. Discrepancies across the divide in χ values in the middle reaches of the San Gaban catchment argue for the outward migration of the eastern and western drainage divides. This response is consistent with the regions of high Gilbert metrics, such as gradient and relief within the canyon (fig. S1) (36, 37). The outward migration of drainage divides below the prominent knickpoint in the upper San Gaban channel is an anticipated response to all of the proposed mechanisms of canyon incision (Fig. 2), including a marked increase in elevation of the plateau (Fig. 2A) and/or a change in climate that increases incision and relief, and promotes outward drainage migration (Fig. 2C).

The first-order physiography of the Central Andes in southern Peru is formed by the active volcanic arc along the western edge of the plateau and the folded and faulted rocks of the Eastern Cordillera. Between these high peaks lies the internally drained Altiplano Basin, and, to the east of the Eastern Cordillera, topography abruptly decreases into the actively deforming Subandean Zone (Figs. 1 and 3). Early deformation in the Eastern Cordillera created topography, focused exhumation, and brought rocks to the surface between ~50 and 30 Ma (23, 38) and propagated westward until ~20 Ma (23, 38, 39) (Fig. 3A). This early deformation and exhumation period set the geometry of the surface structures observed through the Eastern Cordillera and the resulting topographic high (23) would have transferred sediments eastward through paleo-rivers and hillslope processes.

The structural and topographic uplift of the modern Eastern Cordillera initiates at ~19 Ma, with motion occurring along the easternmost basement thrust sheet, which is linked to shortening in the Subandean Zone (23, 39) (Fig. 3B). The age of fault initiation (19 Ma) and the slow shortening rates (<0.5 mm/year) from ~10 Ma to the Present are required to replicate older (e.g., 15 to 36 Ma) measured cooling ages from high-elevation interfluves (23). In contrast, measured cooling ages from the canyon are all notably younger {e.g., ~4 Ma AHe (Fig. 1C) and ~8 Ma apatite fission track [AFT; nominal closure temperature ~ 110°C (40)] (10, 38)}. The initiation of faulting and uplift over a ca. 10-km-high basement ramp, and its eastward propagation and associated erosion, set the primary control on the pattern of predicted cooling ages (23) and is anticipated to influence the first-order signal of topography along the eastern plateau edge. In addition, an order-of-magnitude slowdown in shortening rates from 4.0 to ~0.4 mm/year should also result in a notable change in topography by limiting surface uplift and allowing erosional processes to have a greater influence on the landscape.

RESULTS

Integrating fault displacement and landscape evolution

Cross-section estimates of fault geometry, kinematics and rates of shortening (23), are inputs in the surface processes model CASCADE (30, 31, 41). The model integrates vertical and horizontal displacement trajectories based on cross-section geometries and simulates surface erosion through both transport- and detachment-limited fluvial processes. CASCADE uses a fluvial erodibility constant (Kf) as proxy for bedrock strength, where precipitation and channel geometry are accounted for through separate parameters (detailed in Materials and Methods and Eq. 1). Kf and rock strength are inversely proportional, with lower Kf values indicating stronger rocks and higher Kf values indicating weaker rocks (41). Changes in the climate are represented by variations in magnitude and spatial patterns of precipitation derived from global atmospheric circulation models (42) for Pliocene and modern precipitation values (Fig. 2B). These changes affect modeled discharge and the associated fluvial erosion (34, 41, 43).

To assess the sensitivity of the landscape to variations in shortening rates, we test two end-member scenarios: constant shortening and a marked decrease in shortening rates at ~10 Ma (table S1). We also evaluate the sensitivity of model results to five different climate scenarios that are represented by variations in the magnitude and patterns of precipitation through model time and space: [1] Pliocene precipitation, which is lower in orographic magnitude and amplitude compared to modern values (42), [2] modern precipitation, characterized by a pronounced orographic curve (Fig. 1C), and [3 to 5] a marked transition from Pliocene to modern precipitation values at 2.5, 3.0, and 4.0 Ma. The simulated topography is compared with modern topographic features, including the location of the drainage divide, the magnitude and location of elevation, relief, channel shape, and the eastward extent of high-elevation interfluves (Fig. 1). We quantify these differences by calculating a percent fit for maximum, minimum, and mean topography based on a normalized root mean square error (NRMSE). In addition, we calculate a normalized drainage basin area ratio to quantify plan-view agreement between modeled and observed drainage basins configuration. Both metrics are detailed in Materials and Methods.

Best-fit model

The best-fit model incorporates a marked decrease in shortening rates from 4 to ~0.35 mm/year at ~10.6 Ma (table S1), a change in precipitation from Pliocene to modern values (Fig. 2B) at 2.5 Ma, and a fluvial erodibility constant of 1.01 × 10−4 (Fig. 4). The resulting topography has the highest relief of 3.2 km occurring across an 11-km swath (from 93- to 83-km model space), with a peak relief of 3.9 km located at 92 km (Fig. 4A). In comparison, the measured plateau topography maintains a 2.5-km relief from 95 to 76 km, with the maximum relief of 2.8 km located at 77 km, defining the eastern edge of the plateau. The model’s eastward extent of the plateau edge is situated at 82 km, where the relief is 2.8 km. Because of limited upstream incision (movie S1), the canyon is under-eroded by 25 to 800 m between 100 km and the model’s drainage divide at 115.5 km, which is located 4.5 km east of San Gaban’s modern drainage divide (Figs. 1B and 4A).

The geometry and sequential motion of faults, along with the resulting surface uplift locations (23, 39), create a series of endorheic basins as the plateau propagates northeastward (Fig. 4B) due to the rate of uplift outpacing the river’s ability to incise (44, 45) (movie S1). The northeastern drainage basin (~90 to 70 km) is captured at 5.5 Ma, while the southwestern drainage basin (~120 to 90 km) is captured at 1.5 Ma. The capture of the southwestern basin provides the impetus for river incision and the resulting high relief that defines the eastern edge of the plateau (Fig. 4B and movie S1).

The profile percent fit of our best-fit model is 75.9%. The normalized basin area ratio [e.g., the area of the largest modeled drainage basin divided by the model area from 120 to 50 km in Fig. 4C (a total area of 70 × 50 km)] is 0.60, compared to the San Gaban basin value of 0.68 (Fig. 5).

Model limitations

Our modeling approach is most sensitive to the location, geometry and rate of uplift imparted by the geologic cross section (Figs. 3 to 6). It is also sensitive to small changes in Kf (Fig. 5), which is applied uniformly across the entire model space. Rock strength across the study area is naturally heterogeneous due to marked changes in lithology and erodibility (34). This variability is not included in our models. While replicating detachment-limited transport, CASCADE follows the common stream-power model with m = 0.5 and n = 1, imparting a reference concavity of 0.5 (41). This reference concavity of 0.5 is used for all calculations (χ, Ksn, and KsnQ) to maintain internal consistency. We acknowledge that concavity indices cluster near 0.4 to 0.6 in simple settings, with 0.45 frequently used as a theoretical mid-range value (46, 47). Similarly, while n = 1 is commonly used in simple detachment-limited stream power models, higher values of n (i.e., n > 1), representing nonlinear dependency of erosion on topographic slope, alters equilibrium relief and fluvial response time (19, 48) in a way that may influence model results.

Constant shortening models.

Measured (black lines) and modeled (red line and shading) swath topography for four constant shortening models where fluvial erodibility varies from 130 x 10−6 (A), 150 x 10−6 (B), 170 x 10−6 (C), and 200 x 10−6 (D). Mean topography across the 110- to 70-km region (the plateau) decreases in elevation from ~3 (B) to ~2 km (D) as the fluvial erodibility constant increases from 150 × 10−6 (B) to 200 × 10−6 (D).

Beyond the parameter choices explored here, additional model limitations stem from the structure of CASACADE. To inhibit extreme distortion of the triangulated irregular network (TIN), which CASCADE uses to calculate surface processes, models undergo dynamic remeshing every 2500 model year intervals (41). This remeshing process can initiate small irregularities in the TIN due to the random redistribution of nodes maintaining the same average grid resolution that translate into topographic roughness and can cause minor changes in river catchment geometry. Consequently, the inherent TIN remeshing process of CASCADE results in slight variations in topography and incision magnitudes for each model run (fig. S2). Last, during the Last Glacial Maximum, the equilibrium line altitude in the study area was approximately at 4.4 km (49), with preserved outermost moraines at ~3.6 km (50). Glacial processes are not implemented in the models and would have contributed additional erosion (51–53). The presence of glacially carved valleys could have helped facilitate drainage basin capture, in particular the southwestern basin which occurs at 1.5 Ma at ~90-km model space (Fig. 4B). Persistent differences between measured and modeled topography occur in the best-fit model (Fig. 4) and in simulations with different modeled rock strength and/or climate scenarios (Figs. 7 and 8). Potential causes of the misfits undoubtedly result from both the above limitations and lateral variations in structures, which we elaborate on in Discussion.

Model sensitivity to tectonics

The sensitivity of the modeled landscape to the rate of shortening, especially the decrease in rate at ~10.0 Ma, is assessed by evaluating landscape models with constant shortening rates (3 mm/year), varying fluvial erodibility constants (ranging from 130 × 10−6 to 200 × 10−6), and a change in precipitation from Pliocene to modern values at 2.5 Ma. All models produce a narrow, high peak (6.5 to 8.5 km) at the drainage divide (115.5 km), with a maximum topography decreasing in elevation toward the plateau edge at 75 to 70 km (Fig. 6). A complex, internally drained plateau is formed between 115 and 75 km, which includes three sub-basins. The continuous uplift from 7.0 to 2.0 Ma, focused around 67 km, exceeds the stream power required to incise bedrock and capture the internally drained sub-basins (Fig. 6 and movie S2).

The average topographic response to constant shortening rates results in a lack of incision and high peaks (~7.5 km+) at the drainage divide. While the plateau edge and drainage divide are located within 5 to 8 km of modern positions, there is a lack of lower canyon relief (between 85 and 65 km) for low fluvial erodibility constants (130 × 10−6 to 150 × 10−6) and low plateau elevations (~2 km) at high fluvial erodibility constants (170 × 10−6 to 200 × 10−6). For all constant shortening models, there are no capture events of the endorheic basins between 115 and 75 km. This absence of drainage basin captures results in no incision and low percent fits to observed topography, with all but three simulations below a 50% profile fit (Fig. 5). In addition, all constant shortening models produce low drainage basin ratios <0.1.

Model sensitivity to precipitation

Five precipitation scenarios were modeled with a fluvial erodibility constant of 102 × 10−6 (Fig. 7): persistent Pliocene precipitation, persistent modern precipitation, and a marked change from Pliocene to modern precipitation at 2.5, 3.0, and 4.0 Ma. Pliocene precipitation values are characterized by a lower amplitude orographic gradient, with rates reaching up to 1.97 m/year in the foreland and tapering to ~1.1 m/year in the hinterland (Figs. 2B and 7). In contrast, modern precipitation exhibits a steeper orographic profile, with higher average rates of ~3.5 m/year in the foreland and ~0.5 m/year in the hinterland (Figs. 1B, 2B, and 7). Landscape models with only Pliocene precipitation do not promote incision at most evaluated fluvial erodibility constants (Kf: 95 × 10−6 to 107 × 10−6) and, instead, result in plateaus extending to 85 to 80 km with a low relief endorheic basin (Fig. 7A). However, at fluvial erodibility constant values of 108 × 10−6, Pliocene precipitation models initiate incision at ~1.3 Ma, creating a landscape that matches 71.8% of the observed topography. In general, models with a change from Pliocene to modern precipitation at 2.5 Ma produce the highest-fit models, particularly for fluvial erodibility constants between 101 × 10−6 and 105 × 10−6 (Figs. 4A; 5, A and C; 7; and 8, B and C). These models best replicate the location of the plateau edge and canyon relief, with incision occurring between 0.3 and 2.6 Ma.

Models with modern precipitation initiating at 3.0 Ma limit incision at low fluvial erodibility constants (95 × 10−6 − 102 × 10−6), while concentrating precipitation and erosion at the mountain front between 85 and 50 km (Fig. 7C). As a result, the modeled plateau edge retreats ~10 km to the southwest, and the canyon is about 600 m deeper than observed from 90 to 50 km (Fig. 7C). Incision initiates at 0.9 Ma with fluvial erodibility constants of 102 × 10−6, at 1.4 Ma with Kf = 103 × 10−6, and 2.1 Ma with Kf = 104 × 10−6. The best-fit model that incorporates a shift to modern precipitation at 3.0 Ma achieves a 69.4% fit to observed topography with a fluvial erodibility constant of 103 × 10−6.

Lengthening the time of modern precipitation to 4.0 Myr limits erosion at the drainage divide, which promotes higher elevations, and focuses increased precipitation and resulting erosion at the mountain front (Fig. 7D). Similar to the modern precipitation at 3.0 Ma scenario, the modeled plateau edge retreats by ~10 km to the southwest and drainage basin capture occurs between 0.1 and 3.4 Ma. The best-fit model for modern precipitation at 4.0 Ma is a 74.4% fit to observed topography, with a fluvial erodibility constant of 102 × 10−6.

Simulations imposing modern precipitation throughout the entire modeled run produce the highest topography (7.5 to 8.0 km) in the southwest and a plateau edge located about 15 km west of its modern locations (Fig. 7E). At low fluvial erodibility constants (Kf = 95 × 10−6.), the modeled topography results in an internally drained basin at 4.7 to 5.0 km in elevation. Drainage basin capture occurs over a narrow time interval between 1.4 and 1.8 Ma. The plateau edge retreats from ~77 to ~90 km (Fig. 7E), with canyon incision between 100 to 70 km being as much as 0.8 to 1.4 km greater than observed.

Model sensitivity to fluvial erodibility

The effect of the fluvial erodibility constant (Kf, Eq. 1) on the modeled topography is assessed using the climate scenario where precipitation changes from Pliocene to modern values (Fig. 2B) at 2.5 Ma (Fig. 8). At a high rock strength (Kf = 95 × 10−6), the modeled topography forms a 4.0- to 4.5-km-high plateau with no incision along the eastern edge (Fig. 8A). The drainage divide between the initial two structurally controlled, endorheic basins defines the plateau edge at 80 km, creating an ~40-km-wide internally drained basin. Increasing the fluvial erodibility constant (Kf > 100 × 10−6) initiates drainage basin capture, with higher values promoting earlier incision in the modeled evolution (Fig. 8, B to D).

As the modeled fluvial erodibility increases from 100 × 10−6 to 110 × 10−6, the drainage capture age of the southwestern basin increases from <1.0 to 4.6 Ma. The initial drainage basin capture of the northeastern basin also occurs earlier (~8.0 Ma), over-eroding the plateau edge. This increased erosion caused by higher fluvial erodibility constants results in over-incision compared to the modern channel profiles (up to an additional ~2 km of incision) and greater relief (4.5 km). Models with fluvial erodibility constants that inhibit incision (<100 × 10−6) produce percent fits to topography ranging from 66 to 69% (Fig. 5). In contrast, models with fluvial erodibility constant values that promote early incision (generally >105 × 10−6) produce percent fits to topography between 55.6 and 71.8%.

DISCUSSION

Patterns of erosion across the San Gaban Canyon

The model results enable evaluation of both the location and magnitude of incision that produces the best-fit modeled topography (Fig. 4). Measured erosion rates from catchment-averaged cosmogenic radionuclide data range from 0.2 to 1.2 mm/year (34), reflecting an apparent timescale of a few to tens of thousands of years (54). However, samples were collected from catchments without slope-break knickpoints, indicating they are in quasi-steady-state (34) and thus may represent erosion rates over longer, potentially million-year timescales (35). Modeled canyon erosion rates, averaged over 200 kyr, 1.0 Myr, and 3.0 Myr, show two prominent peaks in erosion at ~78 and ~94 km (Fig. 9A). These peaks are associated with the two locations of drainage basin capture that occur at 5.5 and 1.5 Ma (Fig. 4B). Although the first capture event of the northeastern drainage basin occurs at 5.5 Ma, erosion rates at ~78 km remain elevated across all three timescales (0.8 to 1.2 mm/year) with the highest rates (1.2 mm/year) averaged over 1.0 Myr (Fig. 9A). This is because incision propagates upstream of the capture point along channel segments that run parallel to the basin axis (at ~80 to 78 km) and structural strike (Fig. 4 and movie S1), resulting in increased channel slopes that drive sustained erosion well after the initial capture event. Both capture events and their resulting incision only occur in models that incorporate a decrease in shortening rates after ~10 Ma.

Erosion rates averaged over 1.0 Myr are the highest within the canyon (155 to 60 km) due to the southwestern drainage basin capture at 1.5 Ma (Fig. 4B) and a slight increase in shortening and uplift rates from 0.35 to 0.5 mm/year at 1.0 Ma (table S1). The pattern of modeled erosion rates, averaged over 1.0 Myr, correlates with the highest measured catchment-averaged cosmogenic radionuclide erosion rates (34), in particular the northeastern peak at ~78 km (Fig. 9A). The cosmogenic radionuclide data are limited to the region between 90 and 60 km, preventing direct comparisons of the southwestern peak in modeled erosion rates at ~94 km with measured data.

In contrast to the catchment-averaged cosmogenic radionuclide data, which reflect short-term erosion over the past ~102 to potentially 106 years (35, 54, 55) and illustrate a marked increase in erosion at ~78 km, the AHe cooling ages (Figs. 1 and 9A) record exhumation integrated over longer time scales of ~4 Myr. These AHe ages are uniformly young (3.5 to 4.5 Ma) across a broad region of the San Gaban, from 100 to 50 km (Fig. 9A), suggesting a more uniform exhumation over this longer time window. The youngest AHe cooling ages (2.8 and 1.8 Ma) are located at 100 and 88 km, proximal to the southwestern peak in modeled erosion at ~94 km, where modeled erosion rates reach 1.6 mm/year (1.0 Myr average) and 1.2 mm/year (3.0 Myr average) (Fig. 9A). In general, young AHe ages spatially coincide with peaks in modeled erosion rates. 3.6 km of modeled exhumation from 3.0 Ma to present is consistent with previous estimates of ~2.9 to 5.6 km of exhumation since ~5 Ma (38).

Modeled erosion rates from our best-fit constant shortening model (Fig. 6B) show marked differences in erosion magnitude and pattern (fig. S3) compared to the decreased shortening model. In the constant shortening model, uplift and translation of topography (41) concentrate erosion within the internally drained basin (fig. S3 and movie S2). This creates a peak in erosion rates that reaches ~6 mm/year at 90 km, with a more moderate peak (1 to 2.5 mm/year) at 50 km. Such high erosion rates extended over 3.0 Myr are inconsistent with measured ~8 Ma AFT ages, which place limits on the magnitude of erosion that can occur after this time (38). The pronounced differences between measured and modeled erosion rates resulting from the constant shortening model supports the assertion that shortening rates decrease at ~10 Ma (23) and that this decrease facilitated regional scale incision and drainage reorganization.

The spatial patterns of Ksn (46, 56) and KsnQ (32, 34, 43) have been identified as key metrics of focused exhumation. KsnQ is particularly valuable as it incorporates the effect of precipitation (Eq. 4), which can vary spatially and influence discharge (32, 34, 43). Because the best-fit topography results from two distinct precipitation patterns, modeled KsnQ is calculated for both modern patterns of precipitation (applied from 2.5 Ma to the present) and inferred Pliocene patterns of precipitation (applied from model start to 2.5 Ma). In the San Gaban region, both measured Ksn and KsnQ values show a peak at ~78 km coincident with a peak in modeled erosion rates (Fig. 9). Measured Ksn values also exhibit two additional peaks: the largest at ~95 km and the westernmost at 110 km before declining to the southwest (Fig. 9B). In contrast, both measured and modeled modern KsnQ values decrease rapidly from the peak at ~78 km to the southwest due to the sharp decline in precipitation (Figs. 1B, 2B, and 9B). The largest measured Ksn peak correlates with the highest peak in modeled erosion rates at 98 to 94 km (Fig. 9). Modeled Ksn and Pliocene KsnQ values are also high in this region with peaks of 256 and 289, respectively.

Estimated Pliocene precipitation magnitudes remain elevated southwest of the plateau edge (Fig. 2B), contributing to higher Pliocene KsnQ values across that area (Fig. 9B). This difference in precipitation over the largest period of model time (19 to 2.5 Ma) helped to increase discharge, erosion, and sediment transport, ultimately, facilitating the first drainage basin capture at ~7 to 5 Ma. Uniformly young AHe cooling ages located along 100 to 60 km continue southwest of the pronounced peak in KsnQ measurements, but are colocated with high modeled and measured Ksn values (Fig. 9) and are proximal to the location of the active basement ramp (Fig. 3B). These cooling ages reflect a longer-term erosion signal that aligns more closely with Ksn and inferred Pliocene KsnQ values than modern KsnQ values. Although differences between measured and modeled values for Ksn and KsnQ remain (as discussed in the following section), the first-order match in the prominent peak centered at 80 km (Fig. 9B) supports a nonequilibrium mechanism (e.g., drainage basin capture) for the spatial alignment of young cooling ages, high catchment-average erosion rates, and high KsnQ values at the northeastern plateau margin (Fig. 1).

The mobility or stability of drainage divides is another proxy used to interpret climatic, tectonic, or drainage basin change (36). Metrics that evaluate drainage divide stability, such as Gilbert metrics (relief, gradient, and channel head elevation) (36, 37), χ, and knickpoints are also helpful in recognizing recent drainage basin capture (25, 26, 36, 57). The upper San Gaban Basin contains two adjacent prominent knickpoints (Fig. 4 and fig. S1). Downstream of these knickpoints, in the middle reaches of the San Gaban catchment, across drainage divide differences in χ values argue for the outward migration of the eastern and western drainage divides. Drivers of canyon incision, including fault uplift (Fig. 2C) or plateau-wide surface uplift (Fig. 2A), are expected to create southwestward migrating knickpoints with outward migrating drainage divides in their wake, as highlighted in maps of χ (fig. S1). The location of the knickpoints, with one notably at the confluence of the two major tributaries that define the upper San Gaban (Fig. 4) may reflect a coincidental position of a southwestward migrating knickpoint formed through such uplift scenarios. Alternatively, this knickpoint location is an expected result of recent drainage basin capture and is replicated in our best-fit model (Fig. 4B).

Persistent misfits between measured and modeled topographic metrics

While the surface processes models replicate first-order topographic features, such as the plateau edge, drainage divide location, and general patterns of relief (Figs. 1 and 4), persistent misfits remain. The first and most obvious discrepancy is the maximum and mean elevations between 65 and 30 km in model space (Figs. 4, 7, and 8). In this region, measured maximum elevations reach ~1.6 km, compared to ~0.6 km in the models, and the measured mean elevations are ~1.0 km, compared to ~0.3 km in the models. These topographic discrepancies highlight limited uplift in this region across all model scenarios. In addition, somewhat higher model elevations between 65 and 60 km steepen the downstream gradient between 60 and 50 km. Because Ksn and KsnQ are sensitive to slope (Eqs. 3 and 4), this results in high modeled Ksn and KsnQ values at ~55 km that are both higher in magnitude and shifted to the NE compared to measured values (Fig. 9). The pronounced difference in measured and modeled topography between 60 and 30 km is most likely a result of lateral changes in structures and related changes in uplift not present in the model. This can most readily be seen in higher measured topography on the northeastern edge of the model space (Fig. 4B) compared to topography along the San Gaban channel and to the west. This difference in maximum predicted elevations across 60 to 40 km is amplified by an increase in uplift at 60 km at 1.0 Ma (movie S1). Minor differences in model kinematics and how very small amounts of shortening (0.5 km; table S1) are partitioned across structures between 60 and 30 km (Fig. 3B, blue arrow) could modify the modeled topography in this region and better replicate measured topography.

In addition, all models over-erode the plateau edge (~75 km) via plateau retreat (Fig. 4 and movie S1). The eastern edge of the plateau aligns with a change in stratigraphy from the Ordovician San Jose Group in the east to the Sandia Formation in the west (23). Although both formations are identified as resistant lithologies (34), a potentially more resistant Sandia Formation may help in supporting the plateau edge. Our results show that modeled topography is highly sensitive to minor changes in rock strength (represented by changes in the fluvial erodibility constant, Kf) (Figs. 5 and 8). Incorporating spatial variability in Kf to reflect both potential and identified (32, 34) variations in rock strength is anticipated to yield more accurate representations of the erosive and topographic history.

Although not a persistent misfit, several of the highest percent-fit models show under-erosion in the upper reaches of the San Gaban Basin (Figs. 4, 7, and 8 and fig. S2), which is potentially due to the model’s uniform Kf. This under-erosion also contributes to a clear difference in modeled vs measured Ksn and KsnQ values observed between ~118 and 104 km (Fig. 9). Although the spatial differences in modeled versus measured Ksn and KsnQ values persist at ~110 and 55 km (Fig. 9), the models do generally reproduce key landscape features, including elevation, relief, and the approximate location of the plateau edge and high-relief canyon, and geomorphic parameters such as Ksn and KsnQ between 105 and 70 km (Fig. 9). These marked similarities are compelling and suggest that basin capture is a key process that drives the erosion history of the eastern plateau margin.

Drainage basin capture in the Central Andes and canyon incision

Basin capture as a driver of incision along the eastern edge of the Central Andes has been previously recognized. Notable examples, such as the Ayacucho Basin in central Peru (58) and the Paruro Basin in southern Peru (59) were internally drained and active sedimentary depozones until 6.8 and 7.4 Ma, respectively. These endorheic basins were later captured by the Mantaro and Urubamba rivers (25, 58). Recent thermochronometric work from the Urubamba River to the northwest of the San Gaban Basin (Fig. 10), records rapid exhumation (~1.3 mm/year) since ~4.0 Ma (25). The Urubamba River’s capture of an endorheic drainage system in the Altiplano at ~4.0 Ma is interpreted to have triggered river incision and increased exhumation rates.

The structural geometry and evolution of the San Gaban region focus uplift across a series of surface-breaking and subsurface faults that are separated by intervening, internally drained basins (23, 39) (Fig. 4B and movie S1). At shortening rates of 4.0 mm/year and relatively arid conditions, the high uplift rate exceeds incision rate above ramps, preventing the formation of channel networks (45) that connect landscapes across the ramp. It is only following the substantial decrease in shortening at ~10 Ma that rivers begin to incise into the drainage divides and overcome localized uplift (movie S1). Across the suite of surface processes models we evaluated (Fig. 5), two key drainage basin capture events occur: one between 7.0 and 5.0 Ma, and a second, more critical capture, between 3.5 and 0.5 Ma. In the best-fit model, the captures occur at 5.5 and 1.5 Ma, respectively.

Observations along the northeastern margin of the plateau highlight the length scale (~50 to 75 km) of the structures (such as linked sets of folds, faults, and ramps) that create these basins, providing insights into both past and potential future drainage basin capture sites (Fig. 10). Sub-basins adjacent to and directly southeast of the San Gaban basin currently drain into the Altiplano [Fig. 10B, between (iii) and (iv)]. These sub-basins have a similar shape and geometry to the upper San Gaban drainage basin and, thus, show potential precapture basin geometries (Fig. 4B). χ analysis of this region highlights the marked difference in χ across the Eastern Cordillera drainage divide, supporting a potential future capture (fig. S4). In addition, the shape and geometry of the upper Consata [Fig. 10, (iv)] and La Paz [Fig. 10, (v)] river systems reach behind the high peaks of the Eastern Cordillera and have a shape consistent with structurally controlled, potentially internally drained sub-basins that have undergone river capture. These upper reaches of east and west draining river systems, which stretch along the Eastern Cordillera of this portion of the Central Andes, highlight how the marked canyons that carve into the northeastern Andean Plateau are linked to drainage basin capture.

Increasing evidence and a growing body of literature from other regions underscores the importance of drainage basin capture as a key mechanism for triggering increased river incision and exhumation (26–29, 60, 61). Knickpoint propagation analysis and associated celerity models in the Waipaoa River (New Zealand), Roan Plateau (Colorado), and Sutlej River (Himalayas) (26, 27) demonstrate that changes in base level can drive incision into river channels. One such base level change can result from large drainage basin capture, which can cause abrupt erosion of the capturing basin, lowering the base level of the captured basin and causing incision and upstream knickpoint migrations into this basin. Likewise, numerical modelling of drainage basin capture events and resulting river reorganization of the Rhine/Aare River can account for a substantial portion of the overall incision in the Swiss Alpine Foreland, attributing 400 to 800 m of exhumation to drainage basin capture over the last ~4.0 Myr (29). While basin capture and the resulting incision may be relatively rare events, when they occur, the result can be marked and has a prolonged influence on the landscape. We suggest that the prominent canyons carved along the northeastern edge of the Andean Plateau are also key examples of incision driven by basin capture that occurs when decrease in shortening and uplift rates enable capture through incision across structural barriers.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Surface processes model, CASCADE: Subsurface

Fault geometry, kinematics and rates of shortening, derived from an integrated geologic cross-section and thermal kinematic model (23), serve as structural subsurface inputs into CASCADE. CASCADE (30, 31) allows for the integration of structurally driven uplift and translation, surface erosion, and sediment transport processes to replicate topographic evolution on an orogenic time scale (41). A modified version of CASCADE (41, 62, 63) tracks the horizontal translation, or lateral advection rate, and vertical uplift trajectories of surface nodes, spaced every 1.0 km, in response to displacement along discrete faults modeled using the kinematic software MOVE.2019.1 (PE Ltd.).

The model space extends across a region from 5.0 km east of the easternmost frontal fault (Fig. 3B) to 150 km west, with a width of 50 km. This region encompasses both the extent of the San Gaban Canyon and the area that is actively shortening and uplifting over the 19.0 Myr window being modeled. Although a marked decrease in shortening is necessary to replicate measured thermochronometric cooling ages, to evaluate the sensitivity of canyon incision to shortening rates, landscape evolution is simulated under two tectonic scenarios: a constant shortening rate of 3.0 mm/year and a decrease in shortening from 4 to 0.35 mm/year at ~10.6 Ma. The model start times are set at 12.3 Ma for the constant shortening simulation and 19.0 Ma for the decreased shortening simulation (table S1).

Surface processes model, CASCADE: Surface

CASCADE simulates surface erosion through both transport- and detachment- limited fluvial processes by utilizing erosion-deposition length scales (30, 31, 41). In CASCADE, erosion is proportional to topographic parameters as well as bulk erodibility (K). Bulk erodibility is a function of a fluvial erodibility constant (Kf) [unitless], precipitation (P) [L/t], an erosion-deposition length scale (lf) [L], and a coefficient controlling channel width as a function of discharge (c) [(t/L)1/2] (41). This relationship is given byK=(KfP1/2)/(lfc)(1)

To determine the sensitivity of model results to rock strength under different precipitation and shortening scenarios, Kf values ranging from 0.95 to 2.0 × 10−4 were tested. While CASCADE governs how surface processes respond to climatic and material parameters, approximating realistic erosion patterns also depends on incorporating preexisting topographic conditions. Thermochronometric data from the San Gaban region, including zircon (U-Th)/helium (ZHe) and zircon fission track [nominal closure temperature of ~240°C (64)] cooling ages (~15 to 36 Ma), record a pre–15 Ma period of deformation and erosional exhumation (Fig. 4B) (23, 38) and suggests the existence of preexisting topography and paleo-river networks. A boundary condition is imposed on the western edge of the model space to replicate preexisting elevated topography, which remains at high elevations today [e.g., (23)] (Fig. 1). The 4-km-high elevations at the western margin of the model space are replicated by an imposed uplift rate of 1.5 mm/year with a maximum elevation of 4.5 km, set only along the western boundary. The model initiates at 19.0 Ma with a low-relief, east-sloping fluvial megafan surface [e.g., (65)] representing a depositional environment east and in the foreland of the mountain front that exists outside of the model space (Fig. 3). The megafan surface slopes at 0.1° to the east and facilitates eastward river drainage. To simulate the preexisting river networks, a red noise function is applied that creates a 2.0-km-wide and 50-km-long zone of lower topography imposing an east draining channel along the center of the model space from 150 to 70 km. The local relief of this lowered zone is set by randomly lowering the initial elevation of the model nodes within it by 0 to 10 m.

Surface processes model, CASCADE: Precipitation

Orographic precipitation curves for the Pliocene and present were extracted along a 50-km-wide swath of the cross-section transect (Fig. 1B) from a global atmospheric circulation model, ECHAM5 (42), which is based on modern topography. We compared ECHAM5 modern precipitation values to measured modern precipitation data extracted from the WorldClim2 database (66). The adjustment in precipitation magnitude needed to align the ECHAM5 modern precipitation to the measured modeled precipitation was applied to the Pliocene ECHAM5 modeled values.

The adjusted precipitation curves for both modern and Pliocene values are used as precipitation inputs for the model. Because of global atmospheric circulation models focusing on the most recent (3.0 Ma to the present) changes in precipitation (42), uncertainty in Miocene precipitation values, and proposed impacts of climate change from 5.0 Ma to present (9, 10, 16–18, 42); for simplicity, we apply the modeled Pliocene precipitation values as a proxy for all time periods before the transition to modern precipitation conditions (at 4.0, 3.0, or 2.5 Ma). Observed relationships between elevation and precipitation show a rapid decline in precipitation magnitude with increasing elevation (Fig. 1). Plotting this relationship for eastern Peru shows an intersection of mean precipitation and mean topography at ~2 m/year of precipitation and ~2 km of elevation (Fig. 1C). This first-order observational relationship is maintained through each time step as the deformation front and uplifted topography propagates eastward through time. For the Pliocene precipitation values, where the maximum precipitation reaches 1.97 m/year, the model maintains 1.75 m/year of precipitation at an elevation of 2 km as deformation and elevation continue to migrate eastward (fig. S5).

Evaluating best-fit models

The final topography of the modeled landscapes is compared to modern topographic values extracted from a 30-m-resolution ASTER GDEM along a 50-km-wide swath across the cross section to assess the accuracy of the model’s landscape in relation to observed topography. To evaluate variations in the model’s landscape response to key geomorphic features such as the location of the plateau edge, the location of the drainage divide, and depth and extent of canyon incision (Fig. 1C), a percent fit for maximum, minimum, and mean topography is calculated. The percent fit is determined using a NRMSE, where the normalization is based on the maximum range in elevation. Percent fit is then calculated as (1 − NRMSE) × 100%, so that higher values indicate a closer match between modeled and observe topography. It is calculated at each 0.5 km over the model space from 120 to 0 km to avoid incorporating the imposed boundary condition at the western edge of the model space (150 km). In addition, the maximum elevation in the model is capped at 5.5 km, reflecting the average maximum topographic values in the Peruvian Andes to ensure that variations in the percent fit are indicative of variations in model incision magnitude and the eastward extent of the plateau edge, rather than the difference in the overall elevation.

To complement the swath-based topographic comparison, we also calculated a drainage basin area ratio to evaluate plan-view drainage structure. This metric is defined as the area of the largest modeled basin that drains to the north, toward 0 in model space, and is analogous with the San Gaban divided by the total area of the domain. We define the domain between 50 and 120 km as shown in Fig. 4C (a total area of 70 × 50 km). The abbreviated domain allows us to isolate the main San Gaban trunk stream and the representative modeled trunk stream. This allows for a direct comparison between the modeled drainage network and the observed San Gaban basin to assess how well the model captures basin configuration in map view.

For the CASCADE model results, we calculate KsnQ across the modeled topography using both modern precipitation value and Pliocene precipitation values. The mean values of Ksn and KsnQ are extracted along the cross-section encompassing a 50-km-wide swath (Fig. 1B) and then smoothed to twice the resolution (~8.4 km2) of the TRMM 2b31 precipitation resolution using a moving mean average. The reference concavity for all calculations is 0.5 to maintain internal consistency between modeled and natural landscapes.

Knickpoints both in the San Gaban and modeled channel profiles were initially identified using the knickpoint finder function within MATLAB TopoToolbox and applied to a 30-m-resolution ASTER GDEM. This tool uses a curvature-based algorithm to identify breaks in slope along the channel profile. To reduce over-identification and focus on prominent knickpoints, only knickpoints exhibiting a pronounced change in elevation (>200 m) over a horizontal distance of ~1 to 3 km were retained. These thresholds were selected based on typical relief patterns observed in the study area and served to distinguish prominent knickpoints from minor slope irregularities.

Three erosion rate curves are calculated from the CASCADE model results by extracting erosion rates for each 100-kyr interval and averaging the rates over the last 0.2, 1, and 3 Myr. These time intervals are chosen to reflect the average timescales of erosion rate proxies within the San Gaban Canyon [AHe thermochronometers (10, 38) and cosmogenic radionuclide catchment-averaged erosion rates (32, 34)] and allow for comparison between model predictions and observed data (Figs. 1 and 9). The resulting erosion rate curves along the model domain are smoothed using a moving mean window whose size is set to twice the resolution (~8.4 km2) of the TRMM 2b31 precipitation data, to maintain consistent smoothing scales across all metrics.
