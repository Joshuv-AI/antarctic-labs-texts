The safety and efficacy of posterior scleral reinforcement with bovine pericardial patch in progressive high myopia: a retrospective study
Authors: Yuting Gao, Yuzhu Luo, Yunhui Zhao, Xinchen Wang, Tongcheng Xu, Jingjing Ding, Xueyan Li, Peng Ding, Heting Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo observe the safety and efficacy of posterior scleral reinforcement (PSR) with a bovine pericardial patch in patients with progressive high myopia over a 1-year follow-up period.MethodsThirteen eyes of 11 patients with progressive high myopia who underwent the PSR surgery were followed up with measurements of axial length (AL), spherical equivalent (SE), best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), and intraocular pressure (IOP) at baseline, 1 and 3 months, and 1 year postoperatively.ResultsThe preoperative AL (29.55 ± 1.43 mm) was decreased to 28.95 ± 1.48 mm at 1 month post-PSR surgery and elongated to 29.68 ± 1.45 mm at the end of the follow-ups. The SE decreased from − 15.13 ± 3.43 diopters (D) preoperatively to − 14.43 ± 4.07D at 1 month post-PSR surgery and increased to − 15.25 ± 3.08D at the end of the follow-ups. BCVA remained relatively stable, with no statistically significant differences observed during the 1-year follow-up. The postoperative BCVA improved in eight eyes (62%). No serious postoperative complications were observed.ConclusionsThe modified Snyder-Thompson PSR surgery utilizing a bovine pericardial patch has the potential to delay AL elongation over a one-year postoperative period.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04393-1.Keywords: High myopia, Posterior scleral reinforcement, Bovine pericardial patch, Axial length

Background

Myopia, a widespread and increasingly concerning health issue, has garnered global attention [1]. Notably, its prevalence has risen sharply over the past few decades, particularly in the developed regions of East and Southeast Asia [2, 3]. Among young adults, the prevalence of high myopia ranges from 10%−20% [4]. Moreover, high myopia is a significant contributor to visual impairment and blindness, owing to its associated ocular conditions, including myopic maculopathy, glaucoma, and retinal detachment [5, 6].

Consequently, the pursuit of therapeutic interventions to mitigate the progression of high myopia remains a top research priority. Posterior scleral reinforcement (PSR) is a surgical technique designed to halt the further elongation of the axial length (AL) and prevent fundus complications associated with high myopia by reinforcing the posterior pole of the eyeball. The PSR surgery was first proposed by Shevelev in 1930 [7] and then modified and simplified by Thompson [8] and Snyder [9]. However, this surgery has not yet gained widespread acceptance as a clinical treatment for high myopia, due to its controversial safety and efficacy profile [10]. Additionally, mastering the surgical skills for PSR procedures poses a challenge for ophthalmologists. Furthermore, the scarcity of reinforcing materials hinders the widespread adoption of the PSR surgery.

In recent years, numerous researchers have dedicated their efforts to refining and simplifying the PSR procedure, yielding promising results [11–13]. Human donor sclera, the traditional material used for reinforcement, is difficult to procure and preserve. To address this limitation, we conducted a retrospective study to evaluate the safety and efficacy of using a bovine pericardial patch as a reinforcing material in PSR surgery for managing the progression of progressive high myopia, with a follow-up duration of one year.

Methods

Study design and patients

Thirteen eyes from 11 patients diagnosed with progressive high myopia underwent PSR surgery at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University from April 2021 to March 2022. These patients were retrospectively enrolled in the study. The study received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University and was conducted in compliance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects after a comprehensive discussion of the potential complications and expected outcomes of the surgery. Preoperative and postoperative ocular assessment data for all patients were collected from their clinical medical records.

The inclusion criteria for this study were patients with progressive high myopia, defined as a spherical equivalent (SE) of ≤ − 8.00 diopters (D) in patients aged 8 to 12 years, ≤−10.00 D in patients aged over 12 years, or an annual increase in SE of ≥ − 1.00 D accompanied by an annual AL growth of ≥ 0.45 mm. Patients with other ocular conditions that could compromise visual function, including cataracts, glaucoma, keratoconus, acute or chronic inflammation, or tumors, as well as those with systemic disorders that could impede surgery or who had participated in other clinical trials within the past three months, were excluded from the study.

Outcome measures

All subjects underwent comprehensive preoperative and postoperative assessments at regular follow-up intervals (at 1, 3, 6 months, and 1 year following PSR surgery). These assessments included the measurement of SE, calculated as the sum of the spherical power plus half the cylindrical power, measurement of best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), converted to LogMAR notation using a standard logarithmic visual acuity chart, determination of AL using an IOL Master (Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany), intraocular pressure (IOP) measurements, corneal refractive power assessments, slit-lamp examinations, and dilated fundus examinations. All statistical measurements were repeated five times to obtain an average value.

Surgery procedure

After the successful induction of general anesthesia, a 120° temporal bulbar conjunctival incision was made, maintaining a 2 mm distance from the corneal Limbus. Expose the lateral rectus muscle and the inferior rectus muscle, and separate the fascia, suspensory Ligaments, and the underlying sclera surrounding the muscles. Traction sutures were fixed beneath them to gently draw the eyeball towards the superior-nasal direction. Using a strabismus hook, the inferior oblique muscle was carefully hooked and isolated to fully expose its insertion. The bovine pericardial patch was sequentially inserted beneath the inferior oblique, lateral rectus, and inferior rectus muscle, exercising utmost caution exercised to protect the optic nerve and vortex veins. The reinforcement strips were positioned to ensure a flat placement on the posterior pole of the eyeball, precisely corresponding to the macular area, which is situated between the optic nerve and the insertion of the inferior oblique muscle. The two sections of the reinforcement strips were sequentially anchored onto the sclera at the temporal side of the superior rectus muscle insertion and the nasal side of the inferior rectus muscle insertion. The conjunctival incision was then interruptedly sutured with 8− 0 absorbable sutures. After surgery, 0.5 g of tobramycin eye ointment was applied to the operated eye, which was then patched.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was utilized. The normality of the continuous variable distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The results of the Shapiro-Wilk test demonstrated that AL, SE, and IOP followed a normal distribution (P > 0.05), whereas BCVA did not (P < 0.05). Therefore, AL, SE, and IOP ware reported as mean values ± standard deviations (SD), and were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc tests, and BCVA was reported as median (interquartile range, IQR) and was analyzed using the Friedman test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Notably, no statistically significant changes were observed in the SE and IOP values during the follow-up visits (P = 0.204 and P = 0.053, respectively). Over the course of the 1-year follow-up, the SE improved slightly from − 15.13 ± 3.43 D at baseline to − 14.43 ± 4.07 D at 1 month after the PSR surgery, and subsequently increasing to − 15.25 ± 3.08 D at the end of the follow-up, resulting in a net change of − 0.12 ± 0.90 D (Table 2).

Regarding the BCVA, no statistically significant difference was observed between the preoperative and postoperative data (P = 0.121). At the end of the follow-up, eight eyes (61.5%) demonstrated an improvement in BCVA, three eyes (23.1%) remained stable, and the BCVA decreased in two eyes (15.4%) within 0.10 LogMAR (Table 2).

Intraoperative congestion and edema were observed in all patients. Among them, severe bulbar conjunctival edema occurred in 2 eyes (15.4%), all of which returned to normal within 1 week. Elevated IOP was noted in 4 eyes (30.8%). In one case, the pressure normalized within 5 days following the use of travoprost, while in the remaining cases, it resolved spontaneously within 3 days. One patient developed postoperative diplopia, which resolved 1 month after the surgery. No severe complications such as fundal hemorrhage, retinal detachment, or globe perforation occurred.

Discussion

High myopia, a genetically predisposed blinding ocular condition, is characterized by the progressive elongation of the AL and a gradual increase in refractive error over time. This pathological process, characterized by asymmetric thinning of the posterior sclera and the formation of staphyloma at the posterior pole, can result in various fundus pathologies, such as chorioretinal atrophy, myopic foveoschisis, choroidal neovascularization, and retinal detachment [14]. These conditions are often attributed to abnormalities in scleral collagen metabolism [15, 16]. Currently, the main treatment approaches for high myopia include refractive correction methods, such as wearing glasses or undergoing intraocular lens implantation, as well as therapies targeting fundus complications, including retinal detachment repair and anti-vascular endothelial growth factor therapy. In recent years, multiple studies have confirmed the efficacy of non-invasive techniques, such as low-dose atropine eye drops [17], red-light exposure therapy [18], and peripheral defocus contact lenses [19], in slowing the progression of myopia. These methods offer new options for the prevention and control of high myopia by modulating the ocular growth signaling pathways or enhancing the quality of retinal imaging.

The PSR procedure, targeting the pathological alterations in scleral collagen in high myopia eyes, aims to control excessive AL elongation and reduce radial traction in retinal tissue layers. In our study, we observed myopic foveoschisis in five eyes, four of which achieved a collapse of the foveoschismatic intermediary zone by the end of the follow-up period. The OCT images for a typical patient demonstrated that the potential of the PSR procedure to facilitate the reattachment of the inner and outer layers of the neurosensory retina (Fig. 1). Consistent with our findings, a study conducted by Zhu et al. [20] also observed similar results after treating 24 highly myopic eyes with macular detachment and retinoschisis using genipin cross-linked PSR. After a one-year follow-up, the retina was successfully reattached in 21 eyes (87.5%), further validating the effectiveness of the PSR surgery in addressing the reattachment of the neurosensory retina to the retinal pigment epithelium.

Preoperative and postoperative optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans of the same baseline position in one patient with myopic foveoschisis. a Preoperative OCT scan of the myopic foveoschisis. b Six months after posterior scleral reinforcement(PSR), the foveoschismatic intermediary zone revealed to be reduced slightly. c One year after PSR, there was a nearly complete resolution of the myopic foveoschisis

The notable effectiveness of the PSR procedure in treating high myopia can be attributed to several mechanisms. Firstly, it harnesses the tensile force and immune-inflammatory response of the reinforcement material to mechanically reinforce the posterior sclera, enhancing its structural tenacity [21]. Secondly, it improves microcirculation within the macula, thereby enhancing the metabolism of photoreceptors and ultimately improving vision [22]. Additionally, it reduces the rate of posterior scleral creep, a critical factor in the elongation of the AL in high myopic eyes [23]. In our current study, we employed the modified Snyder-Thompson PSR surgery, which, after decades of experience and refinement, is simpler to perform, requiring less surgical expertise and placing fewer demands on surgical equipment. This approach effectively minimizes damage to ocular tissues.

We utilized bovine pericardial patch as the reinforcement material for the PSR. This material boasts excellent biocompatibility, flexibility, and tensile resistance [24], and has been successfully employed in cardiac surgeries such as valvular replacement and atrial septal defect repair. Remarkably, we observed that the AL growth rate increased by just 0.13 mm in the first year after surgery, a significant reduction compared to the preoperative rate of 0.45 mm/year. Furthermore, there was no significant increase in myopia diopter.

Although the BCVA remained stable during the one-year follow-up, we noted improvement in eight eyes. These findings are in agreement with previous studies [25, 26], further validating the efficacy of the PSR surgery in managing high myopia. This can primarily be attributed to the fact that, following surgery, the formation of local neovascularization and improved blood supply at the posterior pole of the eyeball can enhance the metabolic functions of the retina and choroid. This process has the potential to alleviate the retinal ischemia and hypoxia caused by high myopia, thereby mitigating damage to the retinal pigment epithelial cells and photoreceptor cells and ultimately contributing to an indirect improvement in visual function [22, 25]. Moreover, our observations indicate that patients with improved BCVA exhibited high compliance in postoperative care, including adhering to medication schedules, attending regular follow-up appointments, and avoiding strenuous physical activities. Effective postoperative management is likely to have minimized the occurrence of complications and supported the recovery of visual function.

Regarding the Limitations of this study, it was conducted retrospectively with a Limited sample size and a short follow-up duration. Consequently, the statistical power of this study is limited, and the generalizability of the research findings is significantly restricted. The age range for participants included in this study was aged 9 to 61 years. Given that the development of high myopia and AL elongation are predominantly concentrated before the age of 30, and the progression of myopia is extremely minimal after 30 years old, the increase in myopia in patients approaching the age of 60 years may be associated with nuclear sclerosis of the lens. This broad age range may potentially have an impact on the accuracy of the study results. Given these constraints, further research involving a broader participant pool, incorporates a contralateral-eye control design, and an extended follow-up period is imperative to comprehensively assess the long-term safety and efficacy of this surgical procedure.

In conclusion, our results indicate that the modified Snyder-Thompson PSR surgery utilizing bovine pericardial patch could induce a non-significant reduction of the AL and SE in highly myopic eyes for one month. After that, there was a progressive increase of AL and SE to reach and exceed the baseline values at one year postoperatively. This observation suggests that PSR has the potential to partially retard axial elongation. Additionally, the observed improvements in BCVA occurred in a subset of eyes. These changes were not statistically significant at the group level. This finding may hold promise in addressing the current scarcity of reinforcement materials for the PSR surgery.
