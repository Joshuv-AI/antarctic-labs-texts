A novel interval prediction method with adaptive calibration for ship energy consumption prediction
Authors: Zhihui Hu, Jiale Li, Tianrui Zhou, Ke Zhang, Shenglei Xu, Xin Song, Yunfei Guo, Shiqi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReliable Uncertainty Quantification for Ship Energy Consumption (SEC) is important for abnormality detection and energy efficiency management under complex operating conditions. Existing studies mainly estimate SEC as single deterministic values under different operating conditions, which cannot characterize uncertainty in complex maritime environments. Using operational data from a container ship, this study proposes a novel interval prediction method with adaptive calibration, named Gradient-resolved Adaptive Calibration for Energy consumption (GrACE). The method formulates SEC interval prediction as a multi-objective learning problem involving interval quality and interval width, and further introduces adaptive calibration to adjust the aggregated prediction intervals. The method was validated on five neural networks. Results show that GrACE consistently achieved the best interval quality. Under the best performing Convolutional Neural Network at the 90% confidence level, GrACE achieved a Prediction Interval Coverage Probability of 0.8976, and reduced the Mean Prediction Interval Width (MPIW) by 56.55% compared with classical LUBE. These results suggest that GrACE can provide useful uncertainty information for SEC prediction under complex operating conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56570-x.Keywords: Ship energy consumption prediction, Uncertainty quantification, Interval prediction, Lower upper bound estimation, Adaptive calibrationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Background

More than 80% of cargo is transported by maritime shipping in the global trade system1, which highlights the indispensable role of maritime shipping in maintaining the continuity and stability of global supply chains. At the same time, the shipping industry is under increasing pressure to reduce Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions. Although shipping is widely regarded as a relatively low carbon mode of transport2, the latest GHG assessment released by the International Maritime Organization (IMO) indicates that the shipping industry contributes almost 3% of global GHG emissions. The IMO has set the industry a long-term target to achieve net-zero GHG emissions by 20503. These facts indicate that improving ship energy efficiency is an important task for the long term decarbonisation of maritime transport.

In this context, Ship Energy Consumption (SEC) prediction and related operational optimization are widely regarded as important means of improving energy efficiency management4,5. Reliable SEC prediction models can provide effective support for speed planning, route selection, and onboard energy management, thereby helping reduce both GHG emissions and fuel costs6–8. However, in real maritime environments, SEC is affected by frequent variations in wind, waves, and currents, while operating conditions and ship loading states are also characterized by considerable uncertainty. As a result, SEC exhibits strong fluctuations and pronounced uncertainty9. Traditional SEC prediction models estimate energy consumption from multiple input variables and are essentially Point Prediction (PP) methods. However, SEC is influenced by many factors, including wind, waves, currents, main engine operating conditions, and ship loading conditions. In actual ship operations, these factors are inherently random and variable, which gives rise to uncertainty in SEC. Therefore, Interval Prediction (IP) should be introduced for SEC.

Literature review

SEC point prediction method

SEC point prediction methods estimate a single deterministic value from input variables. Early studies mainly used hydrodynamic formulas or empirical regression equations to estimate energy consumption under specific operating conditions10–12. However, these models rely heavily on ship parameters and often show limited accuracy under complex sea conditions.

In recent years, data driven methods have become the main approach for SEC prediction because they can capture nonlinear relationships directly from historical operational data without relying on complex physical derivations13. Early studies mainly used traditional machine learning models, such as Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)14, Random Forest (RF)15, and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)8. With the development of deep learning, neural network models have been widely used in this field13,16,17, including Backpropagation Neural Network (BPNN)18,19, Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)20, Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) and Deep Neural Network (DNN)21–23. These models can describe nonlinear relationships among ship speed, trim, environmental disturbances, and energy consumption, extract useful features from multi source navigation data, and learn temporal patterns in operational records. Nevertheless, their black box nature may reduce interpretability and user trust in practical applications.

To improve both interpretability and prediction performance, hybrid prediction methods have attracted increasing attention. Some studies calibrate mechanistic models through statistical regression, least squares estimation, or intelligent optimization24,25, while added resistance analysis and noon report based correction can reduce prediction bias under different sea conditions26. Other studies combine mechanistic models with data driven methods, using physical knowledge to constrain the model and data driven components to capture nonlinear effects and residual errors27,28. These hybrid methods often improve accuracy and stability under complex navigation conditions or limited data scenarios29,30, and have been applied to trim optimization and energy consumption optimization31–33.

Although these methods improve SEC prediction accuracy, they still provide deterministic point estimates. In real operations, prediction errors vary with speed, loading condition, wind, waves, and currents. Therefore, IP methods are needed to provide upper and lower bounds for SEC prediction and support uncertainty analysis.

SEC interval prediction methods

IP methods provide upper and lower bounds for SEC prediction and quantify predictive uncertainty. Their goal is to achieve target coverage with narrow intervals. Since SEC is affected by changing environmental conditions, engine operation, and loading states, PP alone is insufficient for reliable monitoring. Therefore, IP can better support the reliability assessment of SEC prediction in real ship operations.

Research on interval prediction for SEC is still limited and can be broadly divided into two types. The first type is based on probabilistic and statistical inference, where Bayesian inference or Monte Carlo sampling is used to propagate parameter uncertainty and random noise to prediction results34. Although these methods are theoretically clear, they often depend on prior settings and approximation assumptions, and coverage is difficult to guarantee when operating conditions change. The second type directly constructs interval bounds. For example, Zhou9 used quantile regression to learn conditional quantiles without explicitly assuming an error distribution. However, because ship operational data are often nonstationary, heteroscedastic, and sparse under extreme sea conditions, current studies still struggle to achieve both reliable coverage and narrow intervals. Therefore, a robust and general method for complex operating conditions is still lacking.

By contrast, IP has been more systematically studied in wind power prediction and other reliability critical engineering tasks, where relatively mature methods and evaluation frameworks have been developed. These methods may provide useful guidance for SEC prediction after adaptation to ship data characteristics. Existing methods can generally be grouped into three categories: Bayesian estimation, Lower Upper Bound Estimation (LUBE), and ensemble methods35,36. Bayesian methods combine prior knowledge with assumptions about error distributions and use posterior inference to construct prediction intervals37,38. However, most were developed for strongly sequential tasks such as wind power prediction, whereas SEC is more strongly influenced by ship speed, loading condition, and wind, waves, and currents. If prior knowledge and error models cannot reflect differences in sea conditions, the resulting intervals may not match real operating conditions well. LUBE methods directly estimate interval bounds by jointly optimizing interval width and coverage through a composite loss function9,39. However, because the coverage term is often non smooth, training may become unstable, so smoothing strategies or modified loss functions are often needed40–42. Ensemble methods combine multiple independent models to improve generalization and reduce the uncertainty of a single model, thereby improving interval reliability37,43. Therefore, they can help reduce prediction risk and support reliability evaluation in practice.

Interval prediction research for SEC remains limited. Existing methods mainly rely on probabilistic inference or simple nonparametric interval construction, and still struggle to balance coverage stability and interval width under complex conditions. Unlike wind power forecasting, SEC is jointly affected by ship speed, loading state, wind, waves, and currents. Therefore, IP methods need task specific adaptation for ship operating conditions.

Knowledge gap and contributions

Existing IP methods remain limited in characterizing SEC uncertainty under complex sea conditions. Distribution based methods may fail to capture condition dependent uncertainty, while Lower Upper Bound Estimation (LUBE) methods can suffer from gradient conflicts and insufficient calibration. Therefore, this study proposes a LUBE based IP method that combines gradient conflict mitigation, interval aggregation, and adaptive calibration to improve interval reliability and stability.

Methodology

Ship energy consumption interval prediction research framework

To improve SEC prediction and uncertainty quantification, this study proposes an interval ensemble method named Gradient-resolved Adaptive Calibration for Energy consumption (GrACE). The method is designed for multi-objective interval learning and adaptive calibration. The overall framework is shown in Fig. 1.

Ship energy consumption interval prediction methodology flowchart.

Step 1: Data collection and processing. Operational data from a large oceangoing vessel are used in this study. Key features, including ship speed, draft, and environmental factors such as wind, waves, and currents, are selected. Data processing and feature selection are then performed to reduce scale differences and build a high quality input dataset.

Step 2: Interval prediction model construction. To compare the performance of the five neural network models under the GrACE framework, DNN, CNN, ResNet (Residual Network), RNN (Recurrent Neural Network), and Transformer are used as prediction models. The model uses multi-objective learning for SEC interval prediction to optimize interval coverage and interval quality. PCGrad is introduced to reduce gradient conflicts between interval coverage and interval quality objectives.

Step 3: Nonparametric aggregation and adaptive calibration. The SNM aggregation method is used to combine prediction intervals and reduce epistemic uncertainty. An AIC method is then applied to recalibrate the aggregated intervals. This helps ensure that the prediction intervals meet the preset Confidence Level (CL) and improves the coverage of traditional neural network methods.

Step 4: Multi-dimensional evaluation and generality verification. Model performance is evaluated from both IP and PP perspectives. Comparative experiments with multiple models are conducted to verify the uncertainty quantification ability of the proposed method under complex sea conditions.

Data preprocessing and feature construction

Because the real operating environment of ships is complex, the raw data inevitably contain noise, outliers, and missing values. Therefore, systematic data processing was carried out before modeling. The overall data processing workflow is shown in Fig. 5.

Data processing flow for SEC.

Feature reconstruction

Because onboard sensors operate at different sampling rates, the raw data were first reconstructed into a unified time series. Since energy consumption was recorded at 15 min intervals, all variables, including GPS, wind, wave, and current data, were synchronized to the same 15 min interval by averaging values within each window. The 15 min energy consumption data were then converted to daily fuel consumption for subsequent analysis.

For feature construction, forward draft and aft draft were converted into mean draft and trim to describe the ship loading condition more directly. In addition, wind, wave, and current directions were transformed from true directions into relative directions with respect to ship heading. This ensured that environmental factors and ship motion states were represented in a consistent reference frame for subsequent modeling. The specific method follows14.

Data cleaning

Before modeling, data cleaning was performed following14 to improve data reliability. Scattered missing values were interpolated to maintain time-series continuity, while samples with continuous missing records in key variables, such as ME fuel consumption, were removed. Noise and outliers were identified according to adjacent observations, temporal continuity, and ship operating principles, and were then corrected or removed to maintain the physical plausibility of the dataset.

Feature selection

After data cleaning, feature selection was conducted to reduce redundancy and improve training stability. Since SEC is closely related to navigation and environmental variables, the Pearson correlation coefficient was used to screen candidate features. The correlation matrix is shown in Fig. 6.

Correlogram of variables.

As shown in Fig. 6, both engine speed and ship speed are strongly correlated with SEC. However, engine speed is highly correlated with ship speed, with a coefficient of 0.93, indicating clear redundancy. Since ship speed is also a primary reference variable in actual navigation decisions, engine speed was removed and ship speed was retained as the representative motion-related feature. Mean draft and trim show moderate correlations with SEC, suggesting that loading condition affects propulsive efficiency and energy use. Environmental variables, including wind speed, wave height, current speed, and their relative directions, show weaker linear correlations. Nevertheless, they were retained because their effects on SEC may be nonlinear and coupled with ship motion under real operating conditions.

Based on the above analysis and the physical mechanism of ship navigation, nine variables were finally selected as model inputs. These variables include ship speed, mean draft, trim, wind speed, relative wind direction, wave height, relative wave direction, current speed, and relative current direction.

Results and discussion

The experiments were conducted on a Windows 11 (64-bit) platform with an AMD Ryzen 5 processor and 16 GB memory. Python 3.9 was used as the software environment. All data processing and experiments were performed under the same configuration to ensure reliability and reproducibility.

The dataset contains 7,493 samples and was randomly divided into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 0.81:0.09:0.10. The input and target variables were standardized before model training.

All five neural network models were trained under the same settings. The Adam optimizer was used with an initial learning rate of 0.001, a mini-batch size of 512, and 800 training epochs. Random seeds were fixed during the experiments to improve reproducibility. Since the neural network models were used as representative models for evaluating the proposed interval prediction framework rather than as the main methodological contribution, architecture-level hyperparameter tuning was not conducted. Comparative analysis of interval prediction algorithms

For each neural network model, including DNN, CNN, RNN, ResNet, and Transformer, all interval prediction methods were implemented under the same experimental settings for comparison. Each method generated lower and upper prediction bounds, while the final point prediction was calculated from the midpoint of the prediction interval. For the classical LUBE baseline, the model was trained by directly minimizing a scalar weighted loss consisting of interval width loss, coverage constraint loss, and center regression loss. As shown in Table 4, the classical LUBE method generally produces PICP values higher than the target CL. This is especially evident under ResNet, where the PICP reaches 0.9965. However, this high coverage is obtained at the cost of excessively wide intervals. Across the five neural networks, LUBE consistently gives larger MPIW and AIS values, indicating that it tends to improve coverage by expanding the prediction intervals. The large standard deviations under CNN and ResNet also suggest that classical LUBE is sensitive to training randomness and may suffer from unstable interval learning.

PCGrad improves the interval quality by mitigating gradient conflicts among multiple learning objectives. Compared with classical LUBE, PCGrad reduces MPIW and AIS in most neural networks, showing that gradient conflict mitigation can alleviate interval over-expansion to some extent. However, PCGrad alone does not include ensemble aggregation or calibration. As a result, its PICP remains relatively high in several cases, such as DNN, RNN, ResNet, and Transformer. This suggests that gradient correction improves training stability, but it is still insufficient for accurate coverage control.

After SNM aggregation is introduced, the PICP values become closer to the target CL, and the standard deviations of interval metrics are generally reduced. This indicates that SNM can reduce the randomness caused by data splitting and parameter initialization. However, when SNM is used alone, the interval width and AIS are still limited by the quality of the base interval model. Therefore, although SNM improves stability, it does not fully solve the problem of interval sharpness.

By contrast, GrACE achieves the best overall interval performance across the five neural networks. It consistently provides the lowest MPIW and AIS while maintaining PICP around the target CL. Compared with classical LUBE, GrACE reduces MPIW by 42.88%, 56.55%, 36.98%, 57.98%, and 43.24% under DNN, CNN, RNN, ResNet, and Transformer, respectively. Meanwhile, AIS is reduced by 25.30%, 41.41%, 24.75%, 44.29%, and 29.37%, respectively. These results show that the combination of multi-objective training and adaptive calibration enables GrACE to maintain coverage close to the target CL while reducing MPIW.

From the perspective of PP accuracy, GrACE also maintains competitive performance. It achieves the best MSE and R2 under DNN, CNN, and ResNet, and obtains similar performance to PCGrad under RNN and Transformer. This suggests that the improvement in interval quality is not obtained at the cost of point prediction accuracy. Instead, the auxiliary point-wise supervision and more stable interval learning help the model better characterize SEC patterns under complex operating conditions.

Interval prediction performance analysis for SEC

To further evaluate the effectiveness and compactness of GrACE in interval prediction estimation, this section compares the four IP methods in terms of coverage, interval width distribution, and average interval score.

PICP analysis

PICP is the primary metric for interval prediction evaluation. Figure 10 compares the PICP results of different IP methods under five neural network models: DNN, CNN, RNN, ResNet, and Transformer. The black dashed line indicates the target CL of 90%.

Comparison of interval coverage probability.

As shown in Fig. 10, SNM also achieves PICP values close to 0.9 under the target CL. This suggests that interval aggregation improves robustness under complex sea conditions. However, this result is partly achieved by producing wider intervals. In other words, SNM smooths variance by combining multiple model outputs, rather than by improving the quality of a single interval. This point will be further discussed in the MPIW analysis.

By contrast, the PICP values of LUBE and PCGrad are much higher than the target CL of 90%, and in some cases are close to 100%. This indicates that their intervals are overly wide. As a result, they are less effective in describing the complex patterns of SEC and provide limited value for refined energy efficiency management.

The GrACE shows the best stability. Under all five neural network models, its PICP remains close to the target CL of 90%. This indicates that the GrACE can characterize uncertainty under complex sea conditions more accurately. It avoids both insufficient coverage and excessive redundancy. Such accurate interval control can provide more reliable support for speed optimization, fuel planning, and intelligent ship operations.

MPIW analysis

When coverage is satisfied, interval width directly determines the practical value of prediction intervals. Figure 11 shows the distribution of MPIW on the test set using Kernel Density Estimation (KDE). The horizontal axis represents interval width, and the vertical axis represents density. Curves that are further to the left and have higher peaks indicate narrower and more concentrated intervals.

Probability density of interval widths.

As shown in Fig. 11, GrACE exhibits a clear left shifted and sharp peaked distribution under all neural network models. Its density is concentrated in the low width region. For example, under CNN and RNN, MPIW is mainly distributed between 8 and 15, with only a short right tail. This indicates that GrACE does not only reduce MPIW on average. It also provides effective coverage with narrow intervals for most samples.

By contrast, LUBE shows a long tail or even a bimodal distribution under models such as ResNet and DNN. In particular, under ResNet Fig. 11d, the MPIW ranges from 20 to above 100. This suggests that LUBE lacks effective control for some complex samples, which limits its practical value for refined fuel management. Although SNM meets the coverage requirement, its MPIW distribution is clearly shifted to the right compared with that of GrACE. For example, under the Transformer model, the center of the SNM distribution lies in a region with larger interval widths. This indicates that its reliability is achieved partly at the cost of wider intervals. PCGrad shows a flatter distribution. This suggests that, although it reduces gradient conflicts, its prediction consistency across samples is still limited.

Overall, the GrACE avoids the common strategy of achieving high coverage by simply widening the intervals. It maintains coverage close to the target CL while keeping MPIW small. As a result, it provides more precise and useful support for SEC management.

AIS analysis

AIS penalizes both overly wide intervals and coverage failure, and therefore reflects the overall quality of prediction intervals. A lower AIS indicates that the model achieves narrow intervals while maintaining high coverage, which means better prediction quality and practical value.

Together with the quantitative results in Table 4, it can be seen that GrACE achieves the lowest AIS under all networks and performs better than the other methods. For example, under the CNN model, the AIS of GrACE is only 14.75, which is about 41.4% lower than that of LUBE and about 13.5% lower than that of PCGrad. These results show that the GrACE can avoid the common weakness of LUBE, which improves coverage by sacrificing MPIW. While keeping PICP near the target CL, GrACE reduces MPIW effectively and achieves a better balance between PICP and MPIW.

A comparison across different models shows a similar pattern. Because LUBE lacks an effective control mechanism, its AIS values are generally high and its standard deviations are also large. This is especially clear under the ResNet architecture, where the AIS reaches 44.20. This indicates that its prediction results are neither accurate nor stable. Although SNM and PCGrad both reduce AIS to some extent, their AIS values are still clearly higher than those of GrACE. This suggests that combining gradient optimization, interval aggregation, and adaptive calibration is more effective for uncertainty quantification under complex sea conditions.

The robustness of the GrACE is also clear. In Fig. 12, the error bars reflect the stability of the models under different data splits and random initializations. LUBE shows very large standard deviations under deep architectures such as ResNet and Transformer, which suggests poor stability. In contrast, the AIS standard deviations of GrACE remain very low under all networks, for example, 1.36 under CNN and 1.49 under DNN. This shows that the GrACE can effectively reduce training randomness and provide stable support for practical applications.

Conclusion

To address UQ in SEC prediction, this study proposed a novel interval prediction method with adaptive calibration for SEC uncertainty quantification. The method is designed to improve training stability and interval quality in neural network based interval prediction. By combining gradient conflict mitigation, statistical interval aggregation, and adaptive calibration, it provides reliable support for energy efficiency management under complex sea conditions.

The main conclusions are as follows.

A Lower Upper Bound Estimation (LUBE) based interval prediction method for SEC was developed. To address the gradient conflict between Mean Prediction Interval Width (MPIW) and Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP) in traditional LUBE methods, Projecting Conflicting Gradients (PCGrad) was introduced to reduce optimization interference among multiple loss terms. As a result, heterogeneous models, including DNN, CNN, RNN, ResNet, and Transformer, were all trained stably. This improves the trainability of LUBE in neural network based SEC interval prediction.

A Lower Upper Bound Estimation (LUBE) based interval prediction method for SEC was developed. To address the gradient conflict between Mean Prediction Interval Width (MPIW) and Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP) in traditional LUBE methods, Projecting Conflicting Gradients (PCGrad) was introduced to reduce optimization interference among multiple loss terms. As a result, heterogeneous models, including DNN, CNN, RNN, ResNet, and Transformer, were all trained stably. This improves the trainability of LUBE in neural network based SEC interval prediction.

High accuracy and high reliability were achieved in IP. By introducing Split Normal Mixture (SNM) aggregation and Adaptive Interval Calibration (AIC) into the Lower Upper Bound Estimation (LUBE), the proposed method reduced the uncertainty caused by data noise and model randomness. At the 90% CL, the GrACE outperformed LUBE under all neural network models. Under CNN, for example, PICP remained close to 0.9, MPIW was reduced by 56.55%, and the lowest AIS was achieved. These results show that the GrACE can generate prediction intervals with reliable PICP and narrow MPIW.

The method showed consistent interval prediction performance across the tested neural network architectures and practical value. A comparison across five types of neural networks showed that the method can be applied across the tested neural network architectures. It produced high quality prediction intervals under both CNN and Transformer, as well as the other models. Although GrACE shows clear advantages, several limitations remain. The ensemble aggregation strategy improves interval stability but inevitably increases training cost, and its training and inference efficiency under different hardware and deployment settings requires further evaluation. The present validation is based on data from one vessel, while SEC patterns may vary across ship types, voyage profiles, and sensor configurations, future work will explore transfer learning to improve adaptation to new ship types with limited data. In addition, long-term operational changes, such as hull fouling and machinery degradation, may cause distribution drift and require online updating of model parameters and calibration factors. Future work will further examine architecture-level ablation, model-specific hyperparameter sensitivity, and the use of prediction intervals in uncertainty-aware speed optimization and fuel planning.
