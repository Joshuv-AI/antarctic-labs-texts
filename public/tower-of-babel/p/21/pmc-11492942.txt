Emerging lessons from experiences at transitions in care among hospitalised patients with cancer with postdischarge frequent emergency department use: a qualitative study using linked clinical and patient-reported interview data from Quebec, Canada
Authors: Siyana Kurteva, Nabil Nassar, Robyn Tamblyn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundWhile teamwork is essential to providing high-quality patient-centred care, challenges in interprofessional collaboration and decision-making in hospital settings are common, especially for patients with cancer. The purpose of this qualitative study was to identify emerging themes and potential challenges related to hospital discharge experiences among patients hospitalised for cancer who became frequent emergency department (ED) users postdischarge.MethodsA cohort of patients with cancer discharged from an academic health centre in Montreal (Canada) between October 2014 and November 2016 was assembled. Using health administrative claims from the provincial universal healthcare programme, frequent ED (FED) users were identified as patients who had a ≥4 ED visits in the year following hospital discharge. Qualitative analysis of transcripts from semistructured telephone interviews conducted with patients 25–30 days’ postdischarge was used for in-depth exploratory analyses to characterise hospital discharge experiences and transition process from the hospital to the community.ResultsOverall, 182 (14.5%) of 1253 patients with cancer who became FED users were included in this study. The mean age was 69.1 (SD=11.5), 59.9% (n=109) were male, and the most frequent cancers were 80 (43.9%) respiratory and 52 (28.6%) upper digestive cancer. Content analyses revealed six emerging themes from the FED patient interviews. Overall, these included (1) incomplete communication of information, (2) hospital discharge planning, (3) coordinating care among team members, (4) follow-up with outpatient providers, (5) monitoring and managing symptoms after discharge and (6) enlisting help of social and community supports.ConclusionsUsing integrated data from clinical, administrative claims and patient interviews, this study provided insights into the challenges related to hospital discharge experiences and transition into community among hospitalised patients with cancer with FED use. Application of our findings could assist in hospital discharge preparation and improvement in healthcare delivery and health outcomes.Trial registration numberNCT01179867.Keywords: Patients, Hospital to Home Transition, Health Services

Introduction

While teamwork is essential to providing high-quality patient-centred care,1 2 challenges in interprofessional collaboration and decision-making in hospital settings are common.3 4 This is especially true among patients with cancer, for whom fragmentation of healthcare services is common.5 There have been initiatives to help establish an optimal model of care to improve interprofessional teamwork and outcomes for hospitalised patients.6 7 Lack of collaborative discharge planning can lead to disruptions in patient discharge preparation, which is associated with an increased risk of emergency department (ED) visits and readmissions.8,10 Failure to arrange community-based follow-up may lead to avoidable returns to the ED for the treatment of worsened existing conditions or the management of newly emerging problems following hospital discharge.11,14

Poor communication of the treatment intent and duration for medications prescribed at discharge to community-based physicians may lead to medication-related problems.15 While some ED visits may be necessary for patients who experience acute symptoms, the occurrence of a significant number of ED visits is medication-related and preventable.16,21 High use of acute healthcare services is a major healthcare challenge, with hospitals being penalised for elevated readmission rates.22 The literature has identified key factors derived from administrative data that have an impact on readmission rates including the receipt of chemotherapy, previous ED use, high number of ambulatory visits, higher comorbidity, multiple healthcare providers and previous opioid use.23,28 However, the patient hospital discharge experiences of frequent ED (FED) use is poorly understood. Qualitative studies have mainly focused on patients with unplanned hospital readmission29 or healthcare provider perspectives.30,32 No study to date has had access to information collected from patient-reported hospital discharge experiences among the high-risk group of hospitalised patients with cancer who become FED users. Thus, to improve care delivery and prioritise opportunities for quality improvement, we sought to elucidate key patient experiences during hospital discharge and the transition from hospital to home that may play a role in the potentially avoidable use of subsequent ED services. The goal of this research was to conduct an in-depth exploratory analysis of patient-reported discharge experiences among hospitalised patients with cancer who went on to become frequent users of ED services.

Methods

Study design and setting

This qualitative study used interview data collected from patients who were part of a cluster-randomised trial of medication reconciliation conducted at the McGill University Health Centre,33 a >1000 bed quaternary care teaching hospital in Montreal (Canada) that operates within the universal healthcare plan of the province of Quebec (Régie de l’Assurance Maladie du Québec, RAMQ). The RAMQ covers all necessary medical care and includes drug insurance for registrants 65 years of age and older, income security recipients and those not insured through their employer (approximately 50% of the 8.5 million Quebec population). The main trial aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of electronic medication reconciliation in reducing medication discrepancies at hospital discharge and adverse drug events in the first 30 days postdischarge. As part of the original intervention, the Australian Adverse Drug Reaction Report was used to collect patient self-reported information. The Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research was used for the study reporting.34 A qualitative descriptive design was chosen for this study because the goal was to understand the experience of patients at hospital discharge and what features might have contributed to the challenges in transitions of care.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or public were not involved in the study design or interpretation of the data. Patients did not contribute to the writing or editing of this document for accuracy.

Participants

The study participants consisted of all hospitalised patients with cancer with non-metastatic disease (stages I–III) admitted to medical and surgical units between October 2014 and November 2016 who subsequently became frequent users of the ED services in the 1-year postdischarge. To be eligible for the original trial, patients had to be 18 years or older at admission, admitted from the community or transferred from another hospital, with at least 1-year continuous provincial healthcare coverage prior to admission.

To be eligible for inclusion in this qualitative study, patients had to be discharged alive, have an active confirmed diagnosis of cancer (ICD-9: 1400–1999; ICD-10: C77–C80, C18–20, C33, C45, C61), in the 12 months prior to or at admission, and be flagged as FED users in the 1-year postdischarge. FED use was defined as having ≥4 ED visits in the year following hospital discharge, ascertained using the provincial medical services claims databases. This definition for FED use was based on the most widely used definition found in the available literature and was deemed to represent a valid threshold.35,38 The population was previously described elsewhere.27

Data sources

Multiple data sources were assembled and linked to address the study objectives. Patient-reported interview data on hospital discharge experiences were collected within 25–30 days following hospital discharge. Additional patient characteristics describing the overall clinical profile, history of medication use and healthcare utilisation as well as in-hospital organisational factors were also extracted to complement the qualitative themes and better contextualise the study findings. In-hospital interventions such as the receipt of surgery were extracted from hospital discharge abstract database. Community-based, ER and hospital-based medical services, and medication use in the year prior to and after the hospitalisation were retrieved from RAMQ’s medical and pharmacy administrative claims data to characterise preadmission healthcare use and identify the frequent users of acute healthcare services in the postdischarge period. Complimentary information related to the prescribing physician and system-level characteristics, such as length of stay, discharge destination, training status of the healthcare provider who signed the discharge prescription, and number of years since licensure, was abstracted from the patient medical chart, medication reconciliation software databases and the hospital data warehouse.

Questionnaire development and administration

A telephone semistructured interview was conducted with consented patients within 25–30 days after discharge by trained research assistants as part of the original medication reconciliation trial called RightRx (NCT01179867). The interview questions were informed by the Australian adverse reaction and drug event report. As part of this questionnaire, a dedicated section containing open-ended questions from the interview focused on gathering specific information about patient experiences and suggestions related to improving hospital discharge as well as the transition to the community. This qualitative study used part of the original questionnaire aimed to solicit patients’ perceptions of the hospital discharge processes that may have played a role in the care transition and healthcare quality. The research questions asked were ‘Do you have any suggestions on how to improve the discharge processes, that is the transition from hospital to home?’ and ‘What was helpful to you during the discharge process?’. A copy of the questionnaire on discharge experiences used in this study could be found in online supplemental material, appendix S1. All individual sessions were audio recorded and transcribed by trained research assistants. The discussions were conducted in both English and French, but for the purpose of synthesising and reporting the data, they were all translated into English.

Qualitative analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the main patient demographic and clinical characteristics and selected in-hospital organisation factors. Interview transcripts were stored, synthesised and organised in NVivo V.12 where they were coded using content analysis techniques,39,41 and emerging themes related to hospital discharge experiences were inductively generated from the data. Through iterative readings of the interview transcripts, a list of codes was inductively developed to characterise sentences or sections of the transcripts. Codes were then compared and contrasted, and similar codes were grouped into categories to delineate emerging themes that corresponded to factors characterising patient’s hospital discharge experiences. Quotations illustrating these themes were extracted from the interview transcripts, and identifying information was masked to protect the confidentiality of the patients. The analyses were performed independently by two of the authors (SK and NN). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion among the research team.

Results

Overall, 182 (14.5%) of the 1253 patients with cancer who became FED users were included in this study (figure 1). The mean age was 69.1 (SD=11.5), 59.9% (n=109) were male, and the most frequent cancers included 80 (43.9%) respiratory and 52 (28.6%) upper digestive cancer (table 1). Mean length of hospital stay was 8.6 days (SD=11.4), with less than half of the patients having received surgery during the hospitalisations (n=76, 41.7%); 91.8% (n=167) of patients were multimorbid with a high Charlson Comorbidity Index of ≥3, a high mean number of 10.2 active prescriptions at admission (SD=11.1) and a high number of ED visits in the year proceeding hospitalisation (mean=13.0, SD=15.5). With respect to in-hospital characteristics, 94.5% (n=172) of patients were discharged to their home, 78.6% (n=143) had their discharge prescription signed by a resident as opposed to the attending physician, and for almost half of the patients (47.3%, n=86), the years of practice of the attending physicians was between 20 and 40. Medication reconciliation was carried out in only 19.8% of the FED users (n=36) (table 1).

Content analyses identified 19 concepts from the FED patients’ interviews that were organised into 6 themes (online supplemental material, table S1). Overall, these included (1) incomplete communication of information, (2) hospital discharge planning, (3) coordinating care among team members, (4) follow-up with outpatient providers, (5) monitoring and managing symptoms after discharge and (6) enlisting the help of social and community supports. A complete list of all patient quotes is provided in online supplemental material, table S1.

Incomplete communication of information

Lack of information on medications prescribed, stopped or modified, or medical information in general, was one of the main challenges identified by patients with cancer. While some patients were given explanations as to the discharge material, others mentioned the lack of communication about modified medications, what needs to be continued and at what dosages, what medications were stopped and which ones were newly prescribed. This became especially important for patients who did not have a family doctor.

One of the old medications was taken off the prescription list given at discharge, but no explanation was given. This caused confusion. It would be helpful if [the] doctor explained why medications [were] prescribed or stopped.

A list of medications in terms of what needs to be continued and the dosage prescribed or modified needs to be communicated especially when I do not have a family doctor.

One of the patients specifically mentioned concerns related to pain medication regimens prescribed at hospital discharge and the need for assistance on how to take the medications and taper the dose in the postdischarge period:

There needs to be assistance with respect to pain medications. The hospital told me to gradually reduce the dosage but this was difficult given the number of pain medications prescribed.

Communication of medications prescribed, having the discharge material provided and explained to patients were all identified by participants as helpful practices during the transition from hospital to home:

Paperwork given at discharge, which was a summary of the hospital stay, information about what happened and my medications. I gave this to my family doctor, and they found it extremely helpful and useful.

The lack of information on what the patient needs to do in the postdischarge period appeared to be an issue with the transition of care for some patients. Communicating information about recovery, what is normal and expected after surgery, or the time it would take for the patient to resume their normal activities was reported as lacking for some at hospital discharge. The process of communicating information to their community doctors was also questioned by one of the participants due to the lack of communication between the hospital discharge team and the patient at the time of discharge:

Would have like to have been kept updated in the process of follow-up appointments. I was unaware of how information was getting to my allergist (and whether information was actually sent).

More information [is needed with respect to] what to expect [post-discharge] and how long [recovery would take]. There was no instruction given.

I was not able to have a direct answer when it came to the duration of my recovery; [the] answers I got varied from 6 weeks to one year. No one mentioned how difficult it would be and the pain I would endure.

In addition to communicating medical information to the patient, the lack of clarity and organisation of the information received by patients also seemed to be an issue. For instance, the lack of computerised prescription, difficulties following changes in the medications on the discharge summary or receiving information that turned out to be erroneous were concerns shared by some patients:

Prescription was hard to read (hand-written by doctor).

Exit papers were ready but they were not correct (there were health problems listed on the papers that I did not have).

Have the generic and the brand name of the medications on the discharge summary so that is more easily recognizable for the caregiver/patient. They might not be familiar with all the names of the medications; it becomes difficult to follow the changes.

Hospital discharge planning

Many patients perceived the hospital discharge to be too early and believed it increased their risk of readmission. Some patients mentioned postdischarge complications and emerging of new health issues shortly after discharge such as pneumonia that could have been avoided if patients had been kept in the hospital for longer:

I was not ready to leave, felt weak; as proof, I went back two hours later and stayed for two more weeks.

Patient’s caregiver would have preferred if the patient stayed in the hospital longer; this way, they think the pneumonia [that developed during the post-discharge period] might have been avoided.

Having uncontrolled pain at hospital discharge, and lack of knowledge on whether the prescribed pain regimen was effective, were also mentioned as concerns shared by a few of the participants:

The hospital discharge was too soon; I was still in a lot of pain and had constipation. Nevertheless, I was still discharged without being ready. I should have stayed until the pain and the constipation have gotten better.

I left the hospital too early and without knowing if the pain medications were working.

Insufficient recovery time following surgery, feeling unstable and not ready health wise to leave the hospital were also reiterated by some patients as reasons as to why they believed they were discharged too early.

The hospital needs to keep hospitalized patients for at least four days after having had surgery as to ensure that our health condition is stable enough to leave the hospital.

All participants also identified inadequate preparation of discharge papers as one of the most common issues at hospital discharge. Delays in the preparation of patient’s chart, finalising the discharge prescription and obtaining follow-up appointments were some of the challenges shared by patients. Keeping patient’s chart up to date throughout the hospitalisations and making sure only the last modifications are entered at the date of discharge were some of the suggestions added by patients to improve efficient discharge processes:

The charts need to be kept up-to-date all along during the hospital stay so that on the day of discharge, only the last modifications will need to be entered.

The discharge should be prepared way ahead of time, so that we don’t have to wait for paperwork and prescriptions to be filled out.

Additional challenges identified by patients were problems with not having been given a supply of the prescribed medication at hospital discharge. One participant shared that they ‘would have liked to have medication supply of the prescribed medications to avoid going to the pharmacy right after the hospital discharge.’

Coordinating care among in-hospital team members and community providers

Patients also shared that the lack of communication between different hospital specialists was a problem for them when transitioning back into the community. Lack of consistency in the care team’s messaging and being told different information from different doctors was a concern among some patients.

There was no coordination between the many different specialists at the hospital. Felt like there was some confusion amongst staff with my files.

I feel as though there is no communication between my doctors. I feel like each of them only knew my situation within their specialty only. They didn't know what was happening with my general health.

More communication between different departments at the hospital is needed; I think the units are pretty isolated and self-sufficient. I am not sure if medical information is communicated amongst the different doctors who are in charge of the case.

One patient also emphasised the lack of communication and coordination between hospital and community pharmacy: ‘Discharge summary and prescription need to be faxed directly (automatically) to the community pharmacy.’ Other patients mentioned the help of a nurse as crucial during inpatient stays in maintaining good communication between doctors.

Follow-up with outpatient providers

The need to schedule follow-up appointments beforehand and provide them to the patient at the time of hospital discharge was identified by patients as a common problem in the transition back into the community. Some patients shared how difficult it was to obtain an appointment with a doctor. As a result of the lack of community care and follow-up with a specialist, these patients were considering going back to the ED because of worsening of existing or new health conditions.

I am having difficulty with my medications and am considering going to the ER to handle the problems, as getting an appointment with the doctor is near impossible.

I have had a worsening in my condition, but I am not sure what is happening it as I was not given a follow-up appointment with a specialist.

Follow-up appointment with doctors needs to be given at hospital discharge. It takes a very long time to schedule them. Because of this, I missed my follow-up appointment with the doctor that I was supposed to see post-discharge.

This was reported as especially important and helpful for patients who have been on pain medications during the hospital stay, affecting their cognitive abilities and making postdischarge planning more difficult.

I found very helpful the follow-ups given to me by the hospital. Everything was organized for and I didn’t have to worry about it after my discharge especially after being on anesthetics for so long & I couldn’t think clearly for a while.

Monitoring and managing symptoms after discharge

Lack of a number to call postdischarge in case of medical questions or advice in case of emergency situations was also a concern for some patients. One of them shared that having an avenue of communication after discharge would have been helpful in enabling them to manage their symptoms postdischarge.

Give a number for patient to be able to call in case of any questions/worries or just to re-assure patient that everything is ok; give them advice on what to do if a problem appears (like a help line).

Enlisting help of social and community supports

Lack of referral to community social and health services were also concerns addressed by some patients when asked about discharge opportunities for improvement. Conversely, others shared that the organisation and referral of patient to community health and social services during the discharge period was one of the most helpful aspects of the discharge process, and in particular, the visit to patient’s home by a nurse that helped with postrecovery processes and answered questions related to recovery.

A social worker should be organized for patient (or some kind of social support) so that [they] can have someone to follow-up with [the patient] after the operation to help with the recovery (so that patient does not stress about/think too much about the operation).

Discussion

In summary, our findings demonstrate a variety of challenges and complexities present at hospital discharge for hospitalised patients who go on to become frequent users of the ED services. These included lack of communication with patients related to medical information, inefficiencies in hospital discharge planning, lack of coordination and communication among the multidisciplinary discharge team, follow-up with outpatient providers, monitoring of symptoms postdischarge and enlisting help from social and community services. These findings highlight the need for policy and practice changes to improve the transition experiences from hospital into the community setting for hospitalised patients with cancer.

Provider-to-patient and provider-to-provider communication practices, and disruptions in care delivery

Poor communication among healthcare providers and with patients was identified as a major challenge contributing to the disruptions in the transition from the hospital to home among patients who ended up using acute healthcare services frequently following their hospitalisation. Communication failures have been shown to be a leading cause of healthcare disruptions and are linked to several adverse events occurring after hospital discharge.31 42 43 One study found that the lack of communication and coordination in the patients care among care team members was shown to be present in more than half of preventable adverse events experienced by patients soon after discharge.8 The findings from a qualitative study exploring hospital-based dynamics in discharge planning for patients experiencing delayed care transitions in Ontario, Canada found a consensus among healthcare providers about the lack of collaboration among different physicians and the importance of clear communication with the patient along with disseminating consistent information between team members and with the patient and their families.44 Moreover, a qualitative study that interviewed patients who had an early unplanned readmission 3 days following the index hospitalisation found that some of the elements of discharge planning, which could have enhanced the discharge experiences, were improved communication between members of the healthcare team, as well as with patients and families, ensuring readiness for discharge and shared decision-making regarding the timing of discharge.29 In another retrospective study, good communication with medical staff and providing discharge instructions when it came to medications and postdischarge care were associated with lower 30-day readmission rates, adjusting for hospital and system-level factors.10 Research also shows that increased face-to-face interaction also leads to improved collaboration between healthcare professionals,43 45 which, in turn, improves patient safety.46 This highlights the need to investigate alternative models or interventions that facilitate multidisciplinary communication between pharmacists, doctors and other healthcare providers in order to reduce the risk of potential medication-related harm in the community.

Communication of medication changes with patient providers in the community and making sure the patient continues therapy as intended is key to improving patient outcomes and reducing medication-related harms.46 47 Insufficient communication between hospitals, patients and primary care clinicians is all linked to an increased risk of medication-related harm following discharge from acute healthcare settings.48 Our findings support the need for ongoing efforts and strategies to improve communication and overall multidisciplinary discharge planning and postdischarge community care in order to reduce the burden associated with medication-related adverse events.49

Inefficiencies in hospital discharge processes and its related risk of subsequent ED visits

In addition to communication gaps, the lack of accuracy and completeness of the discharge material was also raised as a concern by our participants. In our study, most discharge prescriptions were signed by residents. A qualitative study that explored pharmacists’ perspectives on preparing discharge medicine lists found that most pharmacists have expressed concerns over correcting discharge errors on discharge reconciliations done by junior doctors.31 Providing more opportunities for supervised learning through immediate feedback would help junior staff with developing their prescribing knowledge while reducing discharge reconciliation errors and delayed planning.

Hospital discharge planning was identified as one of the main challenges at transitions in care, with patients reporting on being discharged too early, and not being ready to leave the hospital. Reasons for patients’ perceptions of early discharge could be insufficient recovery time for patients undergoing surgery and/or inadequate preparation of postdischarge care. Of note, the majority of patients in our study had a length of hospital stay of longer than 6 days. Other qualitative studies that examined patients’ and providers’ perspectives on discharge planning did not find early discharge as a discharge barrier.18 30 50 51 However, a few patients mentioned leaving the hospital in pain in addition to having the perception of being discharged earlier than necessary. Poorly controlled pain following acute hospital discharge is a well-known risk factor for increased morbidity, delayed recovery time and higher healthcare use.52,54 Thus, uncontrolled pain as a result of insufficient recovery time or a lack of assistance in pain management may have resulted in an increased healthcare utilisation for this cohort of patients with FED use.

Rushed discharge processes, not keeping patients’ files up to date or communicating medical decisions with the patient could all result in erroneous information. Indeed, some patients identified that there were mistakes not only in terms of the medications prescribed but also in terms of the medical conditions listed as part of the discharge summary. Inefficiencies in the discharge processes such as completion and accuracy in the preparation of discharge papers need to be prioritised to improve the discharge preparation and avoid discharge delays.

Policy implications and future directions

Numerous interventions aimed at optimising discharge medication practices have been identified. Some of these interventions include medication reconciliation on admission and discharge, patient education, scheduling a postdischarge follow-up, and communication and coordination among care team members.79 55,57 These interventions have been shown to successfully improve clinical outcomes and facilitate smooth hospital transitions for patients discharged back to the community. Medication reconciliation has been shown to successfully reduce medication discrepancies at hospital discharge.33 56 58 Of interest, the original RightRx trial, in which these patients participated, also showed a significant reduction in medication discrepancies for patients on multiple medications.33 Very few patients with cancer in our study were part of the intervention arm of the original trial and had their discharge prescription finalised with the electronic medication reconciliation software. Despite evidence and the existence of such interventions, discharge barriers from the patient and provider perspective continue to be present. We saw patients reporting the presence of erroneous information on the prescription coupled with the lack of explanation and clarity on modifications made to their medication therapies. The lack of postdischarge support in managing symptoms and medications was also lacking. Previous studies have identified the importance of providing discharge instructions, patient education and patient empowerment on improving medication adherence, patient outcomes and reduced readmission rates. Supportive tools and solutions to improve medication management following hospital discharge and communicating the updated drug therapy to patients have been developed.59,61 Future efforts should target the integration of such interventions into routine patient care to facilitate better follow-up with patients and improved medication management.

The themes identified in this research overlap with the majority of the domains proposed by the Ideal Transition at Care model, which has identified several modifiable risk factors to consider when ensuring safe transitions of care.7 The framework was based on expert guidelines, critical analyses of the literature and clinical experience. It has been proposed as a method to create process measures to monitor quality of care transitions. Our findings show the majority of these modifiable risk factors are still at play during most of the patients’ hospital discharge experiences. While we did not study causation or independent associations with the occurrence of FED use, findings generated from this cohort of patients who went on to become FED provided insights as to potential risk factors related to hospital discharge planning and processes that may be associated with increased burden on the healthcare system. Future studies could use these qualitative findings and further examine these associations and the risk of acute healthcare services. Strategies that focus on opportunities to improve patient-centred care and overcome discharge barriers through multidisciplinary discharge coordination, better collaboration between hospital, community and primary care providers are needed. The future use of electronically enabled tools that facilitate medication display of changed therapies and improve the communication with the patient and the community-based providers could benefit and improve patient care delivery.

Strengths and limitations

An important strength of our study includes the unique linkage of data across the care trajectory including patient-reported data on hospital discharge experiences, clinical data and administrative data. To categorise FED users, we accessed information from a single, universal health insurance coverage provider, which provided information on consumption of all healthcare without regards to type or site. To our knowledge, this study is the first to elucidate challenges at transitions of care using a large sample of patient-reported data linked to administrative data on ED use.

Our study has several limitations. Data used were limited to a single jurisdiction, and factors influencing the transition of care at hospital discharge may be different from the experiences of patients in other jurisdictions. In addition, we investigated the discharge experiences of a single group of patients with a high readmission rate. It is possible that experiences vary by the clinical or demographic profile of patients. Nevertheless, participants in our study represent populations coming from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, where for many of them, English or French is their second or third language. In addition, while we presented the challenges encountered by FED users, we did not study their association with the occurrence of FED use. Moreover, we only included patients who went on to become FED and we cannot comment on what extent the discharge experiences of low FED users differed from high FED users. Lastly, our study did not involve the participation of patients and future studies could possibly be improved by adding them to the research team.

Conclusion

This study sought to identify emerging lessons from hospital discharge experiences among patients who subsequently became FED users. Improving the transition from hospital to the community necessitates multifaceted interventions to target multiple challenges identified in patients discharged from the hospital. Areas of improvement should involve communicating medical information and changes to the patient medication regimen to the patient at hospital discharge, careful discharge planning, avoiding early discharge, coordinating care among in-hospital team members as well as the community prescribers and providing the patient with the help of community social and health services. Future work evaluating the role of each of these challenges in patients’ risk of readmissions may prove beneficial in ensuring safe transitional care.
