CO2‐Responsive Dual‐Charge‐Switchable Membranes for Protein Foulant Cleaning
Authors: Niloofar Zamanian, Yue Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTKnown CO2‐responsive membranes exhibit single‐charge‐switching (neutral–cationic), which limits their ability to regulate electrostatic interactions with oppositely charged foulants in membrane cleaning. Herein, CO2‐responsive poly(ether sulfone) (PES) ultrafiltration membranes with a dual charge‐switchable surface are prepared by adding an amphoteric diblock copolymer additive to PES via the nonsolvent‐induced phase separation (NIPS) method. By tuning the isoelectric point (IEP) of the copolymer additive to around 5, the membrane surface charge reversibly switches between positive and negative states under alternating CO2 bubbling, lowering pH to ∼4 (below IEP), and N2 bubbling, raising pH to ∼7 (above IEP). ATR‐FTIR and water contact angle analyses indicate that increasing the copolymer content in the casting solution increases the amount of amphoteric chains on the membrane surface. Using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a model foulant with a similar IEP, protein‐fouled membranes are effectively cleaned with water when CO2 and N2 are alternately bubbled due to the constantly existing electrostatic repulsion between the membrane and protein. The gas‐triggered cleaning efficiency is comparable to conventional acid/alkaline cleaning and remains stable over repeated fouling‐cleaning cycles, underscoring the potential of this approach for environmentally friendly and chemical‐free membrane cleaning.

Introduction

Inspired by the self‐regulation ability of cell membranes in response to environmental changes, artificial stimuli‐responsive membranes have been engineered in recent years [1, 2]. These membranes can respond to various external stimuli, including temperature, pH, light, electric and magnetic fields, or redox agents, which alter their pore size and surface properties and therefore control their permeability and separation selectivity [3, 4]. As pH is an important and easy‐to‐adjust parameter in membrane separation processes, pH‐responsive membranes have become a popular area of research [5, 6]. However, most conventional pH‐responsive membranes offer only single‐charge switching functionality, either between neutral and positively charged or between neutral and negatively charged states [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Although interesting, such single‐charge‐switchable membranes have limitations and cannot meet the needs of treating complex separation environments or systems, where a membrane capable of switching between positively and negatively charged states in response to pH change, namely a dual‐charge‐switchable membrane, can augment the function and performance. For instance, a microfiltration polypropylene membrane treated with dopamine hydrochloride and grafted with poly(N,N‐dimethylamino‐2‐ethylmethacrylate) (PDMAEMA) could effectively separate oil droplets from water at acidic pH and, in the same time, adsorb anionic surfactants and dyes in the emulsion due to their electrostatic attraction with positively charged membrane (protonated amine groups); afterward, adsorbed surfactants and dyes could be released at pH 9 when the membrane became negatively charged (deprotonated hydroxyl groups of catechol) [14]. Similarly, a porous poly(vinylidene fluoride‐co‐chlorotrifluoroethylene) (PVDF‐CTFE) membrane, treated with diethylenetriamine and itaconic acid to introduce amino and carboxyl groups, was shown to display a clear pH‐dependent charge reversal, enabling improved antifouling performance and efficient flux recovery during alternating acid‐alkali membrane cleaning cycles [15]. In the reported cases, dual charge‐switching polymer membranes are generally fabricated through surface modification, polymer grafting, coating, or interfacial polymerization [14, 15, 16, 17]. While these techniques can successfully introduce charge‐switchable groups, they often rely on multi‐step surface modification, additional chemical treatments, or dedicated equipment.

In a previous work, we demonstrated the use of an amphoteric diblock copolymer additive (CoP) to prepare a dual‐charge‐switchable poly(ether sulfone) (PES) ultrafiltration membrane using the non‐solvent‐induced phase separation (NIPS) method [18]. The CoP additive, polystyrene‐b‐poly(N, N‐dimethylamino‐2‐ethyl methacrylate‐ran‐methacrylic acid) (PS‐b‐PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc), is composed of a hydrophobic polystyrene (PS) block and a hydrophilic random copolymer block consisting of DMAEMA and MAAc units. PES solution with dissolved CoP (a few wt.%) is subjected to phase separation in a coagulation bath of water, while hydrophobic PS chains stay mixed with PES; the hydrophilic chains bearing tertiary amine and carboxylic acid groups migrate to the membrane surface, resulting in a stable dual‐charge switchable PES membrane. We further applied this membrane to whey protein filtration. Whey protein, a byproduct of cheese production, is a mixture of several amino acids and has an isoelectric point (IEP) range of 4.5–5.3. By adjusting the IEP of the CoP additive, we showed that the CoP‐added PES membrane performed better than the PES membrane without CoP due to reduced membrane fouling by proteins and enhanced membrane cleaning using acid and base solutions, which results from a constant electrostatic repulsion between the whey protein and the membrane: during the cross‐flow filtration at pH 6, both are negatively charged; during the acid cleaning of the membrane at pH 2, both are positively charged, and during the base cleaning at pH 10, both return to negatively charged state. The study showed that a dual‐charge‐switchable membrane that can synchronize its surface charge with whey protein or, more generally, switch its charge according to the charge state of the objects of interest in filtration provides better control over adsorption, release, and cleaning processes. Moreover, because NIPS is an established industrial fabrication method, the demonstrated strategy can be implemented without infrastructure changes, is easily scalable, and remains general enough to be applied to different polymer systems [3, 4, 19].

An issue remains, however. Although the CoP‐enabled dual charge switchable PES membrane increases the membrane cleaning efficiency by requiring milder cleaning conditions, lowering chemical usage, and shortening cleaning times, the membrane cleaning still involves the use of acid and alkaline solutions. The repeated use of chemicals can gradually damage the membrane structure, reduce its mechanical strength, and compromise its separation efficiency [20, 21]. Chemical cleaning also generates large volumes of wastewater containing chemical residues, raising concerns about secondary environmental pollution [22]. A possible solution to resolve this problem is to replace the repeated addition of acids and bases for pH changes with repeated input and removal of an acid gas. As a matter of fact, gas is an ideal trigger for designing novel stimuli‐responsive membranes because it is not limited by penetration depth, does not generate chemical residues, and can be introduced or removed easily during operation [23, 24]. Among the reported gas‐responsive membranes, CO2 is the most widely used stimulus source due to its low cost, availability, non‐toxicity, and environmentally friendly nature [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Most CO2‐responsive membranes reported so far are based on tertiary‐amine‐containing polymers such as poly(2‐(diethylamino)ethyl methacrylate) PDEAEMA [26], PDMAEMA [32, 33], and poly(N,N‐diethylacrylamide) PDEAAm [34]. For example, Zhang et al. developed a membrane of polyacrylonitrile (PAN) blended with PAN‐co‐PDEAEMA (random copolymer), for which CO2 bubbled into the solution lowers the solution pH and protonates the tertiary amine groups in PDEAEMA, transforming the positively charged polymer segments into a hydrophilic, extended state, while bubbling N2 removes CO2 and returns PDEAEMA chains to a neutral, hydrophobic, collapsed state [35]. This reversible transition weakens protein adhesion and allows foulants to detach during CO2/N2 bubbling cycles, enabling self‐cleaning without chemical reagents. While this strategy has been explored for tertiary amine‐based single‐charge switchable polymers, a CO2‐controlled dual‐charge switchable polymer membrane containing weak acid and weak base groups has not yet been reported. This has motivated us to investigate the use of two gases—CO2 and N2 – to trigger the dual charge switch of the PES membrane containing the PS‐b‐PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc block copolymer additive self‐assembled on the membrane surface [18] and demonstrate the desired chemical contamination‐free membrane cleaning in protein filtration as well as reversible adsorption and desorption of charged dyes. Our hypothesis is that if the CoP‐added PES membrane possesses an IEP that falls within the pH swing range under alternating CO2 and N2 bubbling (approximately between pH 4 and pH 7), dual charge switching of the membrane could be achieved. Herein, we report our study confirming the hypothesis and underscoring the potential applications of such gas‐switchable ultrafiltration membranes.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Polyethersulfone (PES, 6020 P, Mw = 72 000 g mol−1, BASF) was thoroughly dried before use. Styrene (99%, Fisher Scientific, Canada), tert‐butyl methacrylate (tBuMA, 98%, Fisher Scientific, Canada), and 2‐(dimethylamino) ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA, 98%, Aldrich) were each passed through a basic aluminum oxide column before use. Azobis(isobutyronitrile) (AIBN, Aldrich) was recrystallized from methanol, while trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, 99%, Aldrich) was used without further purification. The chain transfer agent (CTA) for RAFT polymerization, S‐(thiobenzoyl) thioglycolic acid (99%, Aldrich), was used as received. A polypropylene/polyethylene (PP/PE) nonwoven support material (Novatexx 2471, Freudenberg Filtration Technologies, Germany) was used as the membrane substrate. Toluene (Fisher Scientific, Canada) was distilled before use. All other solvents were obtained at the highest purity available and used as received.

Characterization

Attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared (ATR‐FTIR) spectra of the membranes were recorded (Agilent Cary 630 with a diamond ATR crystal) to examine the presence of the PS‐b‐PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc additive on membrane surface. 1H‐NMR spectra were obtained using a 400 MHz spectrometer (Bruker Avance‐III) for structural characterization. The zeta (ζ) potential of the PS‐b‐PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc samples of various compositions in aqueous solution, in the form of micelles, was measured using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) apparatus (Zetasizer Nano ZS ZEN3600) to determine the IEP. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Thermo Scientific Apreo 2) was used to characterize the morphology of the membrane. The surface zeta potential of the membranes was measured using an electrokinetic analyzer (SurPASS 3, Anton Paar, Austria) with an aqueous 1 mM KCl solution as the electrolyte. Since direct zeta potential measurements under alternating CO2 and N2 bubbling conditions were not feasible with the instrument setup, the membrane surface zeta potential was measured at pH values between 4 and 7, corresponding to the pH range generated by alternating CO2 and N2 stimulation, to evaluate the gas‐induced charge‐switching behavior. To evaluate the membrane hydrophilicity and wettability, water contact angle measurements were performed using an instrument equipped with an Appro BV‐7105EN B/W camera. Dye adsorption and desorption tests were conducted using Toluidine Blue O (TBO) and Acid Orange II (AO II) as probe molecules, employing two different test protocols. In the first, a given membrane sample (3 cm2) was immersed in a 1 mM dye solution, with adsorption at pH 10 for TBO and pH 2 for AO II, followed by desorption using a 1:1 (v/v) glacial acetic acid aqueous solution for TBO and water adjusted to pH 10 for AO II. In the second test, CO2‐responsive adsorption and desorption experiments were performed using 0.1 mM dye solutions, where AO II adsorption and TBO desorption were both induced by CO2 bubbling, resulting in pH ≈ 4. In addition, cyclic adsorption–desorption tests for both dyes were performed under alternating CO2 and N2 bubbling to evaluate the reversibility of surface charge switching. Dye concentrations before and after adsorption were determined from the absorption peaks on the UV–vis absorption spectra (Cary UV–vis–NIR spectrometer, Agilent Technologies).

Synthesis of PS‐b‐PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc Diblock Copolymers

The PS‐b‐PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc diblock copolymer additive was synthesized according to the procedure previously reported [18]. A brief recapitulation of the employed two‐step RAFT polymerization followed by the hydrolysis procedure is shown in Scheme S1. As an example, styrene (23 g, 0.220 mol), CTA (0.3 g, 0.00141 mol), and AIBN (0.046 g, 0.00028 mol) were added to a reaction vessel containing toluene (10 mL). The solution was degassed using three freeze‐thaw cycles and then put in a thermostatic oil bath at 100°C for a 24 h reaction. The resulting PS macro‐CTA was precipitated in cold methanol, filtered, and vacuum dried at 60°C. Next, PS macro‐CTA (6 g) was dissolved in toluene (6 mL) along with AIBN (0.0146 g, 0.088 mmol), tBuMA (5.164 g, 0.036 mol), and DMAEMA (4.672 g, 0.030 mol). The mixture was degassed by three freeze‐thaw cycles and placed in a thermostat oil bath (100°C) for a 24 h reaction. The resulting PS‐b‐PDMAEMA‐ran‐PtBMA was precipitated in n‐hexane, collected, and vacuum‐dried at 60°C. Finally, to convert t‐butyl methacrylate to methacrylic acid groups in the PDMAEMA‐ran‐PtBMA block, the diblock copolymer (11 g) was hydrolyzed with excess TFA in DCM (110 mL) and stirred overnight. The product was then precipitated in n‐hexane and vacuum‐dried at 60°C for 24 h to obtain the final PS‐b‐PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc diblock copolymer additive (CoP).

Membrane Preparation

To prepare the membrane casting solution, PES and CoP (with 3%–10% CoP content, Table 1) were dissolved in N‐methyl‐2‐pyrrolidone (NMP) at 55°C for 72 h under mechanical stirring. The solution was then degassed under vacuum to remove air bubbles. The homogeneous casting solution was cast onto the nonwoven support substrate placed on a glass plate using a casting knife with a fixed 200 µm gap. The cast film was immediately immersed in a coagulation bath containing deionized water (pH 11, 12°C; Figure S1). Phase inversion occurred during immersion, leading to the formation of a solid porous membrane. The membranes were then thoroughly rinsed with water and stored in deionized water before further characterization.

Flux Measurement of Membranes

The filtration measurement of the PES‐CoP membranes was carried out on a dead‐end filtration setup with a 10 cm2 active area (Figure S2). Before testing, the membranes were pre‐pressed with DI water at a negative pressure of 1 bar for 20 min to reach a steady water permeance. Then, the volume of filtrate water was collected every 5 min at 1 bar with alternating treatment of CO2/N2 to the feed solution for 20 and 60 min, respectively. The pure water flux (Jw1) of the membrane was calculated according to the following Equation (1): (1) JW1=VA×t×P×100 where V is the permeate volume, A is the membrane active area (m2), P is the transmembrane pressure difference in the test (1 bar), and t is the testing time (h).

Measurement of Flux Recovery After Membrane Cleaning

Pure water filtration was conducted for 20 min to reach a steady state, and the water flux was recorded (Jw1). Then, the ultrafiltration of Bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution (C = 0.2 g L−1) was performed continuously for 30 min, and the membrane flux was measured. After membrane fouling with BSA, cleaning was performed using different methods, including water rinsing, acid/base cleaning (pH = 3 oxalic acid and pH = 10 NaOH solutions), and alternating CO2/N2 aeration. After the cleaning process, the pure water flux was remeasured (Jw2). The cleaning efficiency was evaluated by calculating the flux recovery ratio (FRR) using Equation (2): (2) FRR=JW2JW1×100

Membrane Switching Procedures With CO2/N2 Aeration

BSA‐contaminated membranes were cleaned by immersing them in DI water, while CO2 was purged into the water at room temperature at a flow rate of 100 mL min−1. This was followed by N2 aeration under the same conditions. The CO2/N2 aeration cycle was repeated three times.

Results and Discussion

Design and CO2 Switchability of PS‐b‐PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc Copolymer

In order to design a copolymer additive suitable for the fabrication of a CO2‐responsive membrane with reversible dual charge‐switching behavior, two key design criteria were considered: (i) stable incorporation of the copolymer within the PES matrix during membrane formation, and (ii) effective and reversible surface charge switching in aqueous environments under alternating CO2 and N2 stimuli. First, to ensure structural stability, PS was selected as the anchoring block because of its hydrophobicity and intermolecular interactions with PES, which promote strong compatibility with the PES matrix and minimize additive leaching during operation [9]. To address the second design criterion, the charge‐switching behavior of the amphoteric PDMAEMA and PMAAc random copolymer used in our previous work was tailored to have an IEP matching the pH variations induced by the CO2/N2 gas bubbling. In an aqueous solution of the amphoteric copolymer, CO2 bubbling can decrease the solution pH to approximately 4, while subsequent N2 aeration restores the pH to around 7. Based on this pH window, the IEP of the amphoteric copolymer was deliberately tuned to lie near the midpoint of this range (∼5.5), allowing the polymer to maintain about the same net charge (but with opposite signs) before and after CO2 aeration (Figure 1a). Accordingly, three PS‐b‐PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc diblock copolymers with DMAEMA:MAAc molar ratios of 30:70, 40:60, and 45:55 were synthesized (synthesis and characterization details in ESI, Scheme S1 and Figure S3), and their pH‐dependent zeta potentials were measured (Figure S4). By increasing the proportion of PDMAEMA, which has a pKa of approximately 7.5, the IEP of the copolymer gradually shifted from pH 4.5 for the 30:70 composition to pH 5.7 for the 45:55 composition. The charge switching arises from the protonation of tertiary amines in PDMAEMA and the deprotonation of carboxylic acids in PMAAc, which are dictated by their respective pKa values and relative molar fractions in the copolymer. Consequently, the copolymer is negatively charged at high pH and positively charged at low pH [36, 37]. To examine their CO2/N2‐triggered charge‐switching behavior, the dynamic zeta potential responses of the three diblock copolymers dissolved in water at pH 7 were monitored under alternating CO2 and N2 bubbling (Figure 1b). In all cases, upon CO2 bubbling, the initial negative potential (pH 7> IEP ∼5.7) dropped quickly to zero and switched to positive potential (pH ∼4 with CO2< IEP) until reaching a plateau value (with all amine groups protonated). As N2 was bubbled through the solution to remove CO2, the positive potential switched back to a negative potential due to the recovery of the initial pH 7 (with all acid groups deprotonated). It is noticeable that under N2 bubbling, the positive‐to‐negative zeta potential switch is slower than the opposite charge switching under CO2, which is caused by a slower CO2 removal process. On the other hand, the relative magnitude of positive and negative zeta potential is determined by the molar ratio of tertiary amine to carboxylic acid groups in the amphoteric random copolymer block. Indeed, as the molar fraction of DMAEMA increases while that of MAA decreases, the positive zeta potential that can be achieved upon CO2 bubbling increases at the expense of the negative zeta potential under N2 bubbling. Among these three diblock copolymers, PS‐b‐PDMAEMA0.45‐ran‐PMAAc0.55 exhibits a relatively equal magnitude of positive and negative zeta potentials during the CO2/N2 bubbling cycles. The stable zeta potential switch under repeated cycles of alternating CO2 (5 min) and N2 (10 min) bubbling is shown in Figure 1c. Because of the similar positive and negative zeta potentials, PS‐b‐PDMAEMA0.45‐ran‐PMAAc0.55 was selected as the diblock copolymer additive—CoP—for fabrication and investigation of the gas‐triggered dual charge‐switchable PES membrane.

Characterization of CoP‐Loaded PES Membranes

To identify the optimal CoP additive content for achieving desirable surface properties and CO2‐responsive behavior, a series of PES membranes with 0, 3, 5, 7, and 10 wt.% CoP (with respect to PES) were prepared using the NIPS method (see the formulations in Table 1). In contrast with the previous study [18], ATR‐FTIR spectroscopy was utilized to directly detect the presence of CoP chains on the membrane surface. From the spectra shown in Figure 2a, compared to neat PES, the blend membranes exhibit two additional characteristic absorption bands at 1668 and 1723 cm− 1, corresponding to the C = O stretching vibration of the MAAc and DMAEMA units on the hydrophilic block, respectively. The intensity of both peaks increases with increasing CoP content from 3 to 10 wt.%, with respect to the characteristic absorption peak at ∼1578 cm− 1, assigned to the aromatic C = C stretching of the PES backbone (Figure 2b). This result indicates that by increasing the CoP content in the solution for membrane casting, more hydrophilic PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc chains are present on the resulting hydrophobic PES membrane, thus confirming the effectiveness of the amphiphilic diblock copolymer additive design for surface functionalization of the membranes fabricated using the NIPS method. In addition, a broad absorption band in the 3200–3600 cm− 1 region becomes more pronounced with higher CoP loading. This band is characteristic of the O─H stretching vibration of hydrogen‐bonded water molecules, which implies increased water adsorption by the membrane surface due to enrichment of hydrophilic functional groups on the membrane surface. The increased amount of the hydrophilic PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc chains with increasing CoP content in the membrane was further confirmed by the water contact angle measurements on the membrane surfaces. As can be seen in Figure 2c, the contact angle for the neat PES membrane is relatively high, with a value of 83°, indicating its comparatively lower hydrophilicity. However, the water contact angle of the CoP‐loaded membranes decreases continuously with increasing CoP content, reaching about 65° at the highest CoP content used (10%). This behavior is attributed to the increasing presence of carboxylic acid and tertiary amine groups that render the membrane surface increasingly hydrophilic.

(a,b) ATR‐FTIR spectra of neat PES, CoP, and PES membranes containing 3%, 5%, 7%, and 10% CoP. The characteristic absorption peaks of CoP and PES are marked. (c) Water contact angle of the membrane as a function of the CoP content (water pH at 7).

The cross‐sectional membrane morphology was examined by SEM to evaluate the effect of CoP on membrane structure. As shown in Figure 3, all membranes display a typical asymmetric morphology, consisting of a thin dense skin layer supported by a porous sublayer with finger‐like macrovoids. As the CoP content increased from 3 to 10 wt.%, a clear evolution in the sublayer morphology was observed. At lower CoP contents (3–5 wt.%), the finger‐like macrovoids became more elongated and well developed, indicating an enhanced liquid–liquid demixing process [10]. This effect originates from the hydrophilic PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc segments, which strengthen the interaction between the casting solution and water in the coagulation bath and modify the phase‐separation dynamics. With further increase in CoP content (7–10 wt.%), the finger‐like structures gradually shortened and transitioned into a more sponge‐like porous sublayer with increased pore density. This transition reflects a delayed liquid–liquid demixing process, where the amphiphilic copolymer improves the thermodynamic stability of the casting solution and reduces the mutual diffusion rate between solvent and non‐solvent [38, 39, 40]. Consequently, phase separation proceeds more slowly, favoring the formation of thicker pore walls and a denser sponge‐like structure, which is particularly evident in the PES‐CoP‐10% membrane. Figure S5 shows the surface images of PES‐CoP‐10% and neat PES membranes, both exhibiting uniform and compact surface morphology.

Cross‐section SEM images of the neat PES and PES‐CoP (3–10 wt.%) membranes.

Due to the improved hydrophilicity and pore structure, the membrane permeability increases gradually with increasing CoP content (Figure S6). Except for the PES‐CoP (3%) membrane, the water flux of the membranes with 5–10 wt.% CoP increases steadily. Meanwhile, all membranes exhibit similarly high BSA rejection (97%–99%), mainly governed by size sieving since BSA molecules (14 nm × 3.8 nm × 3.8 nm) are larger than the membrane pores [41].

Gas‐Triggered Dual Charge‐ and Hydrophilicity‐Switching of CoP‐Loaded PES Membranes

Given that the PES‐CoP‐10% membrane has the highest amount of the amphoteric PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc chains on the membrane surface, it was chosen for investigation of the membrane under alternating CO2 and N2 stimulation. For comparison, the neat PES membrane was also subjected to gas stimulation under the same conditions. First, to gain insight into the membrane surface charge variation under CO2/N2 stimulation, streaming zeta potential measurements were performed. Since direct measurements during gas bubbling were not feasible with the equipment, the membrane surface zeta potential was measured over the pH range of 4–7, corresponding to the pH variation induced by alternating CO2 and N2 bubbling. As shown in Figure 4a, while the neat PES membrane remained negatively charged over this pH range, the PES‐CoP‐10% membrane exhibited charge reversal with an IEP of approximately 5.3. This result confirms the amphoteric nature of the membrane surface and its ability to switch surface charge within the pH range generated by gas stimulation. Next, the change in surface hydrophilicity after CO2/N2 aeration and at the isoelectric point (solution pH adjusted to pH ∼ 5.5) was investigated by water contact angle measurements. As shown in Figure 4b, after CO2 aeration, the contact angle of PES‐CoP‐10% decreased from 77° at IEP to membrane showed 56°, indicating increased hydrophilicity. This change is no surprise since the CO2 bubbling lowers the pH below IEP, making the membrane surface positively charged. Following N2 aeration, the water contact angle of the membrane remained low, at about 58°. This observation is consistent with the fact that N2 bubbling removes CO2 and recovers the DI water pH ∼7, making the membrane surface negatively charged as pH is above IEP. In sharp contrast, the neat PES membrane showed negligible changes in water contact angle upon gas stimulation, with the contact angle maintained at around 80°, confirming its inherently hydrophobic nature and the absence of the dual charge‐switching functionality.

(b) Water contact angles after CO2 aeration (positively charged), at IEP (neutral), and after N2 aeration (negatively charged); (c,d) Cyclic adsorption‐desorption behavior of TBO (c) and AO II (d), expressed as the relative amount of dye remaining on the membrane during alternating N2 (60 min) and CO2 (5 min) bubbling cycles.

The CO2/N2‐induced dual charge‐switching behavior of the membrane surface was further examined using dye static adsorption as a functional probe. Positively charged TBO was first adsorbed at a neutral pH ∼7, where the membrane surface was negatively charged. The membrane was then removed from the dye solution and immersed in DI water, followed by desorption induced by CO2 bubbling, switching the membrane surface to a positively charged state (pH ∼ 4). In contrast, adsorption of the negatively charged AO II dye was first induced by CO2 bubbling, where the membrane surface became positively charged, followed by desorption of the dye by immersing the membrane in DI water at neutral pH, with the membrane surface reverting to a negatively charged state. The adsorption densities at equilibrium are shown in Figure S7. Compared to neat PES, the PES‐CoP‐10% membrane exhibited significantly higher adsorption capacities for both dyes, confirming the effective and reversible surface charge switching enabled by the amphoteric copolymer additive under gas‐triggered conditions. Unlike conventional single‐charge‐switchable membranes [42, 43], which can only provide electrostatic repulsion toward foulants of one charge polarity, the dual‐charge‐switchable membrane developed here can reversibly switch between positive and negative charge states under CO2/N2 stimulation. This capability allows the membrane surface charge to be synchronized with the charge state of different foulants, thereby maintaining electrostatic repulsion under a broader range of conditions and enhancing fouling control and cleaning efficiency.

To verify the reversibility and repeatability of the CO2/N2‐triggered charge‐switching behavior, cyclic dye adsorption and desorption experiments were carried out under alternating gas bubbling conditions. As shown in Figure 4c, positively charged TBO was first adsorbed at pH ∼ 7 and desorbed in the same solution by CO2 bubbling (pH ∼ 4); subsequently, the dye was re‐adsorbed by bubbling N2 through the same solution and desorbed again by bubbling CO2. This gas‐controlled adsorption–desorption process was repeated for six cycles, and the adsorption efficiency remained around 90%–95%, while the desorption efficiency exceeded 90% in each cycle. In the case of AO II, as shown in Figure 4d, adsorption occurred under CO2 bubbling with efficiencies of approximately 90%–95%, while the AO II desorption occurred upon N2 bubbling, leading to efficiencies of around 70%. This behavior remained stable over multiple cycles. These results, together with the visual observation of rapid TBO adsorption under N2 bubbling and release under CO2 bubbling (Video S1), confirm that the membrane exhibits fast, reversible, and stable dual charge switching driven solely by gas stimuli. In contrast, the neat PES membrane, which lacks dual charge‐switching functionality, showed significantly lower adsorption and desorption efficiencies, reflecting its limited responsiveness to CO2‐induced pH changes in solution.

CO2‐Responsive Change of Water Flux

The pure water permeance of the PES‐CoP‐10% membrane was investigated under the stimulation of CO2 and N2 (Figure 5a). Before gas stimulation, the membrane's pure water permeation reached a stable state at 1 bar (Pressure difference). Upon CO2 bubbling, the water flux first increased rapidly and then decreased. This apparently intriguing behavior can be explained by the amphoteric nature of the PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc chains of the CoP on the membrane pore surface. As schematically illustrated in Figure 5b, CO2 bubbling decreases the solution pH from approximately 7–4, causing the membrane surface charge to pass through its isoelectric point (IEP ≈ 5.3). Around this neutral charge state, electrostatic repulsion between the amphoteric chains is minimized, leading to dehydration of the chains and their collapse into a transient aggregation state. This chain conformational change opens the membrane pores and results in a temporary increase in water flux. With continued CO2 bubbling, as the pH decreases further below the IEP, the PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc chains become positively charged and expand due to electrostatic repulsion, resulting in a gradual pore closure and decrease in water flux. A similar but slower response was observed during N2 bubbling, which removes dissolved CO2 and restores the pH toward pH 7. Because CO2 desorption and pH recovery occur more gradually, the membrane passes through the IEP more slowly, leading to a smoother flux variation. Importantly, the water permeance remained essentially constant during the whole CO2 and N2 bubbling process, showing a slight decrease. This observation indicates that during the CO2 and N2 bubbling cycles, except for the transient passage through the IEP, the PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc chains are hydrated and have extended chain conformation. This result is important because it confirms that the membrane design using the CoP enables switchable rejection of cations and anions mainly by reversible surface charge alteration (sign reversal), without changing the membrane pore size.

Gas‐Assisted Membrane Cleaning

In the dairy industry, an ultrafiltration PES membrane is used to purify whey proteins through crossflow filtration, in which the protein solution flows parallel to the membrane surface, allowing impurities to go vertically through the membrane to be removed while retaining the proteins in the retentate to be collected. During the process, membrane fouling by deposited large protein molecules is inevitable, which leads to a severe decline in permeation flux and compromises the purification. Conventional chemical cleaning methods, such as acidic or alkaline washing, are effective in foulant removal but suffer from several drawbacks, including the generation of chemical wastewater, secondary environmental pollution, and potential long‐term damage to membrane structure and performance. As mentioned above, these drawbacks can be overcome, at least partially, if strong acid and alkaline solutions can be replaced by water with CO2 and N2 bubbled through, which leaves no chemical contamination behind, regardless of the amount of gases used. Therefore, we carried out experiments to evaluate the potential of using CO2 and N2 to clean a protein‐fouled PES‐CoP‐10% membrane.

The designed test using the dead‐end setup consisted of the following steps: (1) the pure water flux of the membrane was measured; (2) a BSA solution (0.2 g L−1) was filtered by the membrane, which was clogged quickly and the water flux dropped; (3) the fouled membranes were cleaned either by rinsing with pure water or alternating acid/alkaline washing (NaOH solution at pH 10 and oxalic acid solution at pH 3) for 3 h with 30 min for each step, or by water cleaning under alternating CO2 and N2 bubbling (100 mL min− 1) for 3 h, with 30 min CO2 followed by 30 min N2 in each cycle; and (4) the pure water flux of the cleaned membrane was measured. As can be seen from the results reported in Figure 6a, in the initial 30 min, the membrane first reached a stable water flux under DI water filtration, followed by a sharp flux decline after 30 min of BSA filtration due to clogging of the membrane pores by BSA. After the membrane cleaning under different conditions, the water flux was greatly recovered. Using water subjected to alternating CO2 and N2 bubbling, the flux recovery ratio (FRR) through the PES‐CoP‐10% membrane was about 91%, which, interestingly, is similar to the FRR after acid/base washing (92%). In both cases, the water flux recovery degree was much higher than that after cleaning with pure water washing (73%). Because the IEP of the CoP (about 5) is similar to the IEP of BSA (4.7–4.9), during cleaning with acid solution or water under CO2 bubbling, both the membrane and BSA molecules were positively charged. This like‐charge electrostatic repulsion significantly weakened protein‐membrane interactions and promoted the release of adsorbed protein foulants. Likewise, during cleaning with alkaline solution or under N2 bubbling, both the membrane surface and BSA molecules switched to a negatively charged state, keeping the electrostatic repulsion force between them and further facilitating protein detachment. Under the conditions used for the PES‐CoP‐10% membrane, the results in Figure 6a show that cleaning using gases offers similar efficiency with respect to the acid/base cleaning. Even in cases where achieving flux recovery comparable to chemical cleaning requires more CO2/N2 bubbling cycles or a longer time, mainly due to the smaller achievable pH swing (about 4–7) and the longer time required for CO2 removal by N2 bubbling, this gas cleaning approach offers clear advantages. First, no chemical cleaning agents are used, avoiding the generation of chemical wastewater and secondary environmental pollution. Second, the absence of harsh chemicals reduces the risk of damaging the membrane structure and performance. However, it is worth mentioning that after 10 consecutive cleaning cycles using either two gases or acid and base solution (pH 3 and pH 10), SEM images (Figure S8) show that while the overall asymmetric morphology remained largely unchanged using both cleaning methods, slight deformation of the finger‐like channels and more localized irregularities could be observed after acid/base cleaning, whereas the membrane cleaned under CO2/N2 stimulation maintained a more uniform morphology. Compared to the fouled membrane, both cleaning methods resulted in a slightly more open porous structure. Detrimental effects using acid and base solutions are expected to become more significant upon prolonged filtration and after large numbers of repeated cleaning cycles. To investigate the reversibility of the membrane cleaning performance under alternating CO2/N2 bubbling, cyclic BSA filtration, and membrane cleaning tests, as described above, were conducted with cleaning. From the results shown in Figure 6b, the membrane maintained a nearly constant flux recovery ratio over three consecutive cycles, confirming the reversible and stable CO2/N2‐responsive cleaning behavior enabled by surface charge switching. It should be mentioned that the practical applications of this dual‐charge‐switching mechanism, just like those of single‐charge‐switching, may be impacted by the ionic strength of the feed solution. In environments with high salt concentrations, mobile ions screen the surface charges of both the membrane and the BSA molecules [44, 45, 46, 47]. This charge‐shielding effect compresses the electrical double layer and reduces the Debye length, which can significantly weaken the electrostatic repulsion required for efficient membrane cleaning. Under extreme salinity conditions, hydrophobic interactions or van der Waals forces may dominate, potentially reducing the cleaning efficiency.

(a) Water flux recovery of BSA‐fouled PES‐CoP‐10% membrane after different cleaning procedures. (b) Cyclic change in normalized pure water flux (Jw) during repeated BSA fouling and CO2/N2‐assisted cleaning, showing stable and reversible flux recovery over three consecutive cycles.

Conclusion

We have prepared CoP‐added PES ultrafiltration membranes and demonstrated their dual charge‐switching functionality in response to alternating CO2 and N2 bubbling into the aqueous solution. ATR‐FTIR spectroscopic measurements found evidence that using the NIPS method, the amount of the amphoteric PDMAEMA‐ran‐PMAAc chains on the membrane surface increased with increasing the amount of CoP in the PES casting solution, which endows the PES membrane with the gas‐triggered dual charge switching. By adjusting the IEP of the CoP additive to about 5, the membrane surface charge could switch between positive and negative states with comparable potential under CO2 bubbling (solution pH ∼ 4 below IEP) and N2 bubbling (solution pH ∼ 7 above IEP), respectively. Using BSA, whose IEP is similar to the CoP‐added PES membrane, we show that a protein‐fouled membrane can be cleaned using water under alternating CO2 and N2 bubbling by retaining electrostatic repulsion between the like‐charged membrane and protein molecules, with a similar efficiency to the industry‐adopted alternating acid (pH 3) and alkaline (pH 10) cleaning. Moreover, the gas cleaning efficiency remained stable over multiple membrane fouling/cleaning cycles. This result underscores the potential to develop a chemical contamination‐free, environment‐friendly membrane cleaning technology.
