Elevated BMI Is Not Associated With Adverse Outcomes in Open Airway Reconstruction
Authors: Dylan Bertoni, Elizabeth Shuman, Choo Phei Wee, Karla O'Dell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOpen airway reconstruction is indicated for structural airway stenoses but may be associated with significant morbidity. While obesity influences perioperative outcomes in many surgical domains, its role in airway reconstruction remains unclear. This study evaluated associations between body mass index (BMI) and postoperative complications, intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital length of stay, and need for revision procedures following cricotracheal or tracheal resection.MethodsA retrospective review of adults undergoing cricotracheal or tracheal resection between 2014 and 2025 was performed. Demographic, surgical, and postoperative data were analyzed. Associations between BMI (continuous and categorical) and postoperative outcomes—including complication rates, length of stay, reintubation, re‐tracheotomy, restenosis, and death—were assessed using Poisson, Cox, negative binomial, and firth logistic regression.ResultsAmong 93 patients (mean BMI 28.8 ± 6.1 kg/m2), 70% were overweight or obese: 29 (31.2%) overweight (BMI 25–29.9), 24 (25.8%) Class I obesity (BMI 30–34.9), 6 (6.5%) Class II obesity (BMI 35–39.9), and 6 (6.5%) Class III obesity (BMI ≥ 40). Overall complication rate was 38.7%, with no significant difference across BMI categories (p = 0.99). BMI was not associated with ICU stay (IRR 1.01, p = 0.43) or total hospital stay (IRR 0.98, p = 0.12). Rates of specific complications—including hematoma, dehiscence, restenosis, and death—did not associate significantly with BMI. The only variable significantly associated with complications was resection length (p < 0.001).ConclusionsBMI and obesity class were not associated with increased complications or prolonged recovery after tracheal resection. Obesity alone should not be considered a contraindication to open airway reconstruction.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

Stenosis of the subglottic and tracheal airway most commonly results from iatrogenic injury, following prolonged intubation or tracheotomy [1, 2]. Patients may present with dyspnea, stridor, cough, and/or dysphonia. While a variety of endoscopic techniques have been developed for the management of subglottic and tracheal stenosis, structural or recalcitrant stenoses may require open airway resection and anastomosis. Success rates of open airway reconstruction exceed 95% at high‐volume centers [3, 4], with lower rates of additional surgery, longer surgery‐free intervals, and higher decannulation rates compared to endoscopic procedures [2, 5]. However, open reconstruction carries significantly higher perioperative risks, including anastomotic complications, worse voice outcomes, and prolonged recovery periods [4, 5, 6, 7].

Patient‐specific factors can significantly influence surgical outcomes in airway reconstruction. Comorbidities, stenosis length/severity, previous interventions, and anatomic considerations all contribute to postoperative success [6, 8]. Among these factors, obesity has emerged as a particularly complex variable in surgical risk. In general, obesity makes airway management more difficult, creating challenges with both ventilation and intubation [9, 10]. Obesity itself is an independent risk factor for acquiring tracheal stenosis [11]. Adipose tissue around the abdomen and rib cage reduces functional residual capacity and can create dyspnea independent of airway disease [12], can enhance the perception of dyspnea or exacerbate symptoms of existing airway disease [13], or can mask symptoms of airway stenosis [9]. Bariatric surgery has been shown to reduce the number of airway interventions needed for patients with comorbid stenosis [14]. Endoscopic airway procedures can be more challenging in these patients as well, with lower rates of decannulation among obese patients [15]. Increasing body mass index (BMI) is associated with higher end‐tidal CO2 during jet ventilation and a higher need for intermittent intubations during procedures [16].

In open surgical reconstruction for posterior glottic stenosis, BMI over 35 has been identified as an independent risk factor for reduced disease‐free intervention interval and decannulation rate [17]. The impact of obesity on open tracheal reconstruction outcomes has not been comprehensively evaluated. Despite limited empirical evidence, there seems to be a clinical consensus among airway surgeons that significant elevations in BMI are contraindications to surgery, with some surgeons anecdotally quoting BMI cut‐offs for offering open airway surgery. Given the unique challenges of airway surgery—including perioperative airway management, anastomotic healing, and the critical importance of avoiding complications—understanding the relationship between BMI and outcomes is essential for patient counseling and surgical planning. This study aims to examine the association between obesity and perioperative outcomes following open airway reconstruction.

Materials and Methods

A retrospective cohort study was conducted among patients at two partnered institutions. Patients were included if they had open tracheal resection or cricotracheal resection between 2014 and 2025 and were excluded if they had incomplete records. Patient demographics, comorbidities, surgical characteristics, and postoperative outcomes were collected and analyzed. Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, race, BMI, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class, smoking status, and relevant comorbidities (including Type II Diabetes, hypertension, pulmonary disease, radiation history, and neurologic disease). Surgical variables included procedure type, operative time, and perioperative management strategies. Primary outcomes included hospital length of stay, intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay, overall complication rate, and specific complications such as hematoma, anastomotic dehiscence, re‐stenosis, death, re‐intubation, re‐tracheotomy, and repeat surgery (defined as return to the OR during initial hospitalization). Overall complication rate included all post‐surgical complications, including: fever, delirium, stroke, surgical site infection, respiratory failure/ARDS, pneumonia, pneumothorax, vocal fold paresis/paralysis, dysphonia, dysphagia, ileus, etc. Complications were classified using the established Clavien‐Dindo classification system [18]. Early complications were defined as complications occurring within 7 days of surgery, and late complications were those occurring after 7 days.

The distribution of patient and surgical characteristics was summarized using mean and standard deviation for normally distributed continuous variables, median and interquartile range for non‐normally distributed continuous variables (e.g., hospital length of stay and ICU length of stay), and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Categorical variables with small sample sizes in some categories were collapsed to ensure adequate cell counts for meaningful analysis and interpretation. Ethnicity was categorized as Hispanic versus non‐Hispanic, and ASA class was dichotomized as ≤ 2 and > 2.

Negative binomial regression models were used to assess associations between patient and surgical characteristics and hospital length of stay and ICU length of stay. Because the likelihood of observing a first complication increases with longer follow‐up, survival analysis methods were used to appropriately account for varying follow‐up durations among patients who had not experienced a complication by their last observation.

Complication was analyzed as a composite surgical outcome defined as the occurrence of hematoma, anastomotic dehiscence, re‐intubation, re‐tracheotomy, re‐stenosis, death, or repeat surgery. As approximately 40% of patients experienced at least one complication, this outcome was considered relatively common; therefore, odds ratios from logistic regression were expected to overestimate the association. A modified Poisson regression model with robust standard errors was therefore used to assess the associations between patient and surgical characteristics and complication. In addition to the composite outcome, individual complication outcomes were also examined. Because each occurred in fewer than 10% of patients, Firth penalized logistic regression models were used to evaluate associations with these outcomes to reduce small‐sample bias associated with rare events.

Additional univariable analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between BMI and complication severity, stenosis etiology, surgical type, and release maneuvers. Ordinal logistic regression was used to assess the association between BMI and Clavien‐Dindo complication grade. Multinomial logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between BMI and stenosis etiology. Modified Poisson regression models with robust standard errors were used to examine the associations of stenosis etiology, surgical type, and release maneuvers with the composite complication outcome.

Effect measures were selected according to the outcome type and corresponding regression model. Associations with hospital and ICU length of stay are reported as incidence rate ratios (IRR), associations with composite complication as risk ratios (RR), and associations with individual complications and Clavien‐Dindo complication grade as odds ratios (OR), and associations with stenosis etiology as relative risk ratios (RRR). All estimates are presented with 95% confidence intervals and corresponding p‐values.

Separate univariable linear regression models were fitted to examine the association between surgical duration and BMI, modeled both as a continuous variable and as categorical BMI groups. Because the analyses were restricted to univariable models without covariate adjustment, the reported estimates represent crude associations.

Multivariable analyses were not performed due to the limited number of outcome events and sparse data within several covariate categories, which could result in unstable model estimates. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using StataNow/SE 18.5 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Results

Demographics

A total of 93 patients were included. Mean age was 49 years old (range: 19–82 years), and 60 (65%) were male. Twenty‐eight (30%) had diabetes, 44 (47%) had hypertension, 15 (16%) had pulmonary disease, 17 (18%) had neurologic disease, three (3%) had radiation history, and 30 (32%) had a smoking history. Mean BMI was 28.8 (range 16.6–46.1). One patient was underweight (BMI 16.6), and 27 (29%) were normal weight (BMI 19.2–24.9). 70% were overweight or obese: 29 (31.2%) overweight (BMI 25–29.9), 24 (25.8%) Class I obesity (BMI 30–34.9), 6 (6.5%) Class II obesity (BMI 35–39.9), and 6 (6.5%) Class III obesity (BMI ≥ 40).

Hospital Length of Stay

Among 93 patients, the median hospital length of stay was 6 days (IQR 5–7). Increasing age was significantly associated with a longer length of stay (IRR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.001–1.02, p = 0.025), corresponding to an approximate 1% increase in expected length of stay for each additional year of age. Overweight patients had a significantly shorter length of stay compared with normal weight patients (IRR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.47–0.97, p = 0.03), representing an expected decrease of 33%. In addition, patients with Class I obesity (IRR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.39–0.86, p = 0.01) and Class III obesity (IRR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.22–0.82, p = 0.01) had significantly shorter lengths of stay than normal weight patients. Each additional centimeter of segment length was associated with a 22% longer length of stay (IRR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.08–1.38, p = 0.002), and each additional hour of surgical duration was associated with a 30% longer length of stay (IRR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.17–1.45, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

ICU Length of Stay

Among the 93 patients, the median ICU length of stay was 2 days (IQR: 1–5). Patients with Class II obesity had significantly longer ICU stays compared with normal weight patients (IRR = 2.81, 95% CI: 1.25–6.28, p = 0.01). This finding should be interpreted with caution because the small sample size in this group may have resulted in unstable estimates. Longer segment length, resection length, and surgical duration were each significantly associated with increased ICU length of stay (all p < 0.05) (Table 2). Specifically, each additional centimeter of segment length was associated with a 34% increase in ICU length of stay (IRR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.13–1.58, p = 0.001). Similarly, each additional centimeter of resection length was associated with a 30% increase in ICU length of stay (IRR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.09–1.56, p = 0.004). Each additional hour of surgical duration was also associated with a 60% increase in ICU length of stay (IRR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.37–1.87, p < 0.001).

Patients who experienced complications had significantly longer ICU stays than those without complications (IRR = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.48–3.28, p < 0.001). Early complications were also associated with longer ICU length of stay (IRR = 2.76, 95% CI: 1.78–4.27, p < 0.001).

Complication (Composite Surgical Outcome)

Overall, 38% (n = 35) experienced at least one complication. Neither BMI nor obesity category was significantly associated with the risk of complications (Table 3). Longer segment length and longer surgical duration were each significantly associated with an increased risk of complications. Specifically, each additional centimeter of segment length was associated with a higher risk of complications (RR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.14–1.55, p < 0.001). Similarly, each additional hour of surgical duration was associated with a 23% increase in complication risk (RR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.01–1.49, p = 0.04). No patient demographic characteristics were significantly associated with complication risk (Table 3).

Complications were classified using the Clavien‐Dindo system for measuring complication severity. A substantial portion of the complications were Grade 1 (17, 47%). There were two Grade 2 complications (6%), eight Grade 3b (22%), six Grade 4 (17%), and three Grade 5 (8%). Higher BMI was not significantly associated with Clavien‐Dindo grades in ordinal logistic regression analyses (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.93–1.07, p = 0.864). Repeat surgery was defined as return to the OR during the initial hospitalization; however, for Clavien‐Dindo grades, any return to OR within 1 year of surgery was classified as Grade 3b. Twenty (22%) patients required surgery within 1 year, including granulation tissue excisions, dilation procedures, thyroplasties, and stent placements/revisions. Note that this included surgeries for indications unrelated to airway surgery complications (abdominal surgeries, repeat cancer operations, thyroplasty for baseline vocal fold immobility, etc.).

Individual Complications

Neither BMI nor obesity category was associated with the risk of any specific complications. No significant associations were observed for anastomotic dehiscence, restenosis, or death (Tables 4, 5).

Stenosis Etiology and Surgical Technique

The majority (78, 84%) of patients had traumatic (post‐intubation or post‐tracheostomy) related stenoses. Four (4%) had idiopathic subglottic stenosis, 10 (11%) had cancer diagnoses, and one (1%) had an open reconstruction for “other” reasons. On univariate analysis, there was no statistically significant relationship between stenosis etiology and complications. Additionally, in multinomial logistic regression analyses, relative to traumatic stenosis, higher BMI was not significantly associated with the relative likelihood of idiopathic stenosis (RRR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.93–1.26, p = 0.286), cancer‐related stenosis (RRR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.92–1.14, p = 0.616), or other stenosis etiologies (RRR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.68–1.38, p = 0.853). There was no statistically significant relationship between BMI and stenosis etiology.

Sixty‐four (69%) patients had a tracheal resection, 27 (29%) had a cricotracheal resection, and two (2%) had multilevel tracheal resection with open post‐cricoid split for comorbid glottic stenosis. All tracheal resections or cricotracheal resections (excluding those with open post‐cricoid split) were performed as single‐stage, without planned placement of tracheostomy or T‐tube at the time of the initial surgery. A hyoid‐release maneuver was performed in 52 (56%) of cases. Although multiple‐staged resection with post‐cricoid split was significantly associated with complications (RR = 2.78, 95% CI: 2.00–3.87, p < 0.001), this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the subgroup included only two patients, which may limit the stability of the estimate. Surgery type, stenosis etiologies, and release maneuver use were not significantly associated with complications in modified Poisson regression analyses.

Discussion

This study provides comprehensive data on outcomes following open airway reconstruction across diverse patient populations, with particular attention to the impact of obesity on surgical success. The findings contribute to the evolving understanding of risk stratification in complex airway surgery and inform evidence‐based patient counseling. Our findings align with the established literature demonstrating excellent overall outcomes for open airway reconstruction. Previous large series have reported success rates exceeding 95% for laryngotracheal resection [3, 4, 19] The temporal pattern of complications observed in our Kaplan–Meier analysis is consistent with prior reports showing that most complications occur in the early postoperative period, with anastomotic issues (including dehiscence and granuloma formation) typically manifesting within the first few days to weeks after surgery [20, 21].

The impact of obesity on specific complications requires nuanced interpretation. We hypothesized that increasing BMI would lead to higher rates of complications following tracheal resection. In our study, we found no increase in complication rate among patients with elevated BMI. In fact, we found shorter hospital length of stays among those with elevated BMI, which could reflect this protective effect. Of all reported complications (36 complications in 35 patients), many (47% of complications, 18% of patients) were Clavien‐Dindo Grade 1, indicating a slight deviation of the expected post‐operative course with minimal intervention required. Grade 3b indicates a need for repeat surgery (22% of complications, 8% of patients), Grade 4 (17% of complications, 6% of patients) represents a life‐ or organ‐threatening complication requiring ICU management, and Grade 5 represents death (8% of complications, 3% of patients). BMI was not statistically significantly associated with Clavien‐Dindo Grade. Our study demonstrated that segment/resection length and operative time (which likely correlates with resection length) showed stronger associations with complications, which is consistent with the literature. There was no significant association between stenosis etiology, use of release maneuvers, or type of surgery (tracheal resection vs. cricotracheal resection) and complications. The observed association between multi‐stage tracheal resection in combination with post‐cricoid split for glottic stenosis and increased complication risk should be interpreted cautiously, as the estimate was based on only two patients in this sub‐group. In general surgery and head and neck cancer surgery, there is literature to support an “obesity paradox,” where moderate levels of obesity provide a protective effect [22, 23, 24, 25]. However, this protective effect appears to be non‐linear, with both underweight and morbidly obese patients experiencing worse outcomes compared to overweight or mildly obese individuals [23, 26]. The balance of these competing factors likely varies based on the specific surgical procedure, patient comorbidities, and institutional perioperative protocols. Large database analyses across multiple surgical specialties have shown that obesity increases the risk of specific complications—particularly infection, venous thromboembolism, and renal complications—while not uniformly increasing overall morbidity or mortality [27]. This pattern suggests that targeted perioperative interventions addressing obesity‐specific risks may optimize outcomes without necessarily precluding surgery in obese patients. The mechanisms underlying the obesity paradox remain incompletely understood. Proposed explanations include metabolic reserve during physiologic stress, earlier presentation, and diagnosis due to comorbidity screening, and differences in perioperative management protocols for obese patients [26, 28]. In airway surgery, some studies demonstrate poorer long‐term outcomes in patients with elevated BMI [15, 17]. In the context of open airway reconstruction, additional factors such as tissue vascularity, wound healing capacity, and inflammatory responses may contribute to the complex relationship between BMI and outcomes [29, 30]. Studies of free flap reconstruction have demonstrated that obesity increases operative time but does not significantly increase complication rates [12]. Our study found no association between BMI or obesity class and increasing operative time.

The findings of this study have several important clinical implications. Obesity should not be considered an absolute contraindication to open airway reconstruction, as outcomes across BMI categories demonstrate the feasibility and safety of these procedures in diverse patient populations. Patient counseling should address the specific risks and benefits relevant to individual BMI categories, recognizing that the relationship between obesity and outcomes is complex and multifactorial. Perioperative protocols should be tailored to address obesity‐specific risks, including enhanced thromboprophylaxis, optimized nutritional support, and vigilant monitoring for wound complications [31, 32].

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design. The analysis presents unadjusted associations, which may be confounded by unmeasured variables or selection bias. The lack of multivariate analysis limits the ability to determine independent predictors of outcomes. Additionally, the study does not capture long‐term functional outcomes, quality of life measures, or patient‐reported outcomes, which are recognized as important endpoints in airway reconstruction [33, 34]. The heterogeneity of surgical techniques, stenosis etiologies, and patient populations may limit generalizability. Future prospective studies with standardized protocols and comprehensive outcome assessment are needed to validate these findings and establish evidence‐based guidelines for patient selection and perioperative management. Additionally, our analysis included only six patients (6%) with Class III obesity, and a maximum BMI of 46.1. Validation of our results with a larger population of extremely obese patients (BMI ≥ 50) is warranted to further elucidate the relationship between BMI and complications following open airway reconstruction.

Conclusion

This analysis provides insight into factors associated with complications following open airway reconstruction in a clinically complex patient population. BMI and obesity class were not associated with increased complications or prolonged recovery after tracheal resection. The data contribute to the growing literature examining the obesity paradox in head and neck surgery, suggesting obesity alone should not be considered a contraindication to open airway reconstruction.

Open airway reconstruction remains the gold standard for definitive treatment of severe laryngotracheal stenosis, with excellent success rates when performed at experienced centers [1, 2, 3, 4]. The choice between approaches should be individualized based on stenosis characteristics, patient comorbidities, functional priorities, and institutional expertise. Future research should focus on prospective, multicenter studies with standardized outcome measures, including patient‐reported functional outcomes and quality of life assessments.
