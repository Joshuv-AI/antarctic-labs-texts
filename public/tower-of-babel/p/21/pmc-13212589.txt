Model-based nitrogen optimization for bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) production in central Oromia, Ethiopia using CERES-wheat
Authors: Seifu Kibebew, Nigussie Dechassa, Yibekal Alemayehu, Feyera Merga, Girma Megersa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) production in Ethiopia, particularly in Central Oromia, is constrained by the continued use of blanket nitrogen (N) fertilizer recommendations that fail to account for site-specific conditions and increasing climate variability. This study aimed to optimize N management for yield and environmental sustainability and to determine economic optimum nitrogen rates (EONRs) under current and future climate scenarios. The CERES-Wheat model within the DSSAT framework was calibrated and validated to simulate wheat growth under multiple N application rates and split strategies across three representative locations (Degem, Fitche, and Bishoftu) using projected climate scenarios (SSP4.5 and SSP8.5) for the 2050s and 2080s. Model outputs were analyzed for grain yield, aboveground biomass, nitrogen uptake, nitrous oxide emissions, nitrogen leaching, and economic returns, and nitrogen response functions were used to estimate EONRs. Simulations showed that grain yield, biomass, N uptake, and N2O emissions increased very highly significant (P < 0.001) with increasing N rates, with the highest biological performance observed at 115 kg N ha−1 applied in three splits. Economic analysis indicated that 115 kg N ha−1 applied in three splits maximized net returns at Degem and Bishoftu, while two split applications were economically optimal at Fitche. The control treatment resulted in the lowest net benefit and marginal rate of return (0%). Nitrogen leaching was not significantly affected by fertilizer management, suggesting a stronger influence of climatic variability than N rate or splitting strategy. Nitrogen response function analysis projected higher future EONRs, ranging from 158–169 kg N ha−1 at Degem, 182–185 kg N ha−1 at Fitche, and 180–191 kg N ha−1 at Bishoftu. Overall, the results demonstrate that current blanket N fertilizer recommendations are suboptimal under both present and future climates. The study recommends adopting climate-smart, site-specific nitrogen management strategies supported by crop simulation models such as CERES-Wheat to enhance wheat productivity, profitability, and environmental sustainability in Ethiopia.Keywords: Climate change, Economic optimum, Grain yield, Nitrogen management, Nitrogen response functionSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is a strategic food-security crop in Ethiopia, playing a central role in livelihoods and the national grain supply1. Despite Ethiopia being the largest wheat producer in Sub-Saharan Africa, national average yields of about 3.1 t ha−12 remain well below the global average3, particularly in the central highlands where production is constrained by climate variability and suboptimal nitrogen (N) management. Nitrogen is the most yield-limiting nutrient for wheat in these systems, yet its efficient use remains a persistent challenge4.

In Ethiopia, less than half of the applied nitrogen is typically recovered by wheat crops, resulting in low nitrogen use efficiency (NUE), economic losses, and environmental risks5,6. Numerous field-based studies have attempted to optimize nitrogen application rates across agro-ecological zones7–9. However, many of these recommendations are based on short-term trials and do not sufficiently account for strong interannual variability in rainfall and temperature, limiting their reliability across seasons10.

Climate variability further complicates nitrogen management by influencing soil moisture dynamics, crop phenology, and nitrogen uptake patterns11,12. During dry seasons, crops may fail to effectively utilize applied nitrogen, whereas in wetter years, enhanced leaching and denitrification reduce nitrogen availability and increase environmental losses13. Consequently, nitrogen recommendations derived solely from fixed-rate field experiments are increasingly unreliable under changing and uncertain climatic conditions, particularly in rain-fed wheat systems of Central Oromia.

Current wheat nitrogen management in Ethiopia largely relies on blanket fertilizer recommendations that apply uniform nitrogen rates across diverse agro-ecological zones14. This approach overlooks spatial variability in soil fertility and temporal variability in climate, resulting in suboptimal nitrogen use efficiency and economic returns15.

Despite these challenges, there remains a lack of climate-responsive, region-specific analyses that integrate nitrogen management with seasonal climate variability for wheat production in Ethiopia16,17. Crop simulation models provide an effective means to address this gap by evaluating nitrogen responses under a wide range of weather conditions that cannot be practically tested through field experiments alone.

The Decision Support System for Agrotechnology Transfer (DSSAT) integrates crop genetics, soil properties, management practices, and historical and projected climate data to simulate crop growth and nitrogen response18,19. Within DSSAT, the CERES-Wheat model has proven effective for assessing nitrogen management options and supporting adaptive, climate-informed fertilizer recommendations in variable agro-ecological settings20,21.

Therefore, the objective of this study is to use the CERES-Wheat model within the DSSAT framework to (i) optimize N management for yield and environmental sustainability under variable climatic conditions and (ii) determine the economically optimal nitrogen rate for major wheat-producing areas of Central Oromia, Ethiopia. This modeling-based approach aims to support adaptive, climate-informed nitrogen management strategies for sustainable wheat production.

Materials and methods

Description of the study sites

The study was conducted at three stations in Central Oromia, Ethiopia: Bishoftu, Fitche (Wakene), and Degem, to evaluate nitrogen optimization strategies. Geographically, Bishoftu is located at latitude 8.76° N and longitude 39.02° E, at an elevation of 1800 m above sea level. Fitche (Wakene) is located at 9.76° N latitude and 38.74° E longitude, and Degem is located at 9.81° N latitude and 38.63° E longitude, with an elevation of 2700 m above sea level. The areas experience a bimodal rainfall pattern, consisting of the Belg and Kiremt seasons. The Belg season (February to May) is a short rainy period with relatively low rainfall, while the Kiremt season (June to September) is the main rainy season, providing the bulk of annual precipitation.

Model description

The Decision Support System for Agrotechnology Transfer (DSSAT) Version 4.8.5 is a software application program that comprises crop simulation models for over 45 crops19. DSSAT is supported by data base management programs for soil, weather, crop management and experimental data, and by utilities and application programs to make it functional for users18. These crop simulation models simulate growth, development, and yield as a function of the soil–plant-atmosphere dynamics19. DSSAT and its models are widely applied across scales from gene-based modeling and on-farm precision management to regional assessments of climate variability and change impacts22. Among these models, CERES-Wheat remains one of the most widely used crop simulation models globally18.

Experimental treatments and design

Using the DSSAT model in a factorial design, the study simulated 13 treatment combinations with four nitrogen fertilizer rates (46, 69, 92, and 115 kg N ha−1), three application timings (T1: single application at sowing; T2: split application half at sowing and half at tillering; T3: triple application one-fourth at sowing, half at tillering, and one-fourth at flowering), and one wheat cultivar (Dendea), chosen for its higher expected yield response in comparison to the Wane cultivar. Additionally, a control treatment without fertilizer was used. Simulations were conducted over a 35-year period of 2030–2064 (2050s) and 2065–2099 (2080s) under two scenarios (SSP4.5 & SSP8.5) in order to represent variability, and the results were examined using a randomized complete block design framework, where years served as replications for every climate scenario.

Model input types and sources of data

Climate, soil physical and chemical characteristics, cultivar specific parameters, and field crop management data are the minimum data sets required to simulate the model19,23.

Climate data sources

Observed daily gridded rainfall, maximum and minimum temperature data for the baseline period (1991–2022) to simulated current wheat yield, for model calibration (2021/22–2023/24), and for evaluation (2024/25) were obtained from the Ethiopian Meteorological Institute (EMI), while solar radiation data were downscaled from NASA. For future analysis, simulated daily precipitation and temperature datasets were analyzed using five General Circulation Models (GCMs) under two Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP4.5 and SSP8.5) for mid- and late-century periods (2050s and 2080s).

Soil data source

The initial soil samples from the experimental field at Degem station was collected following the soil horizon layers, extending up to a depth of 2 m. The samples were analyzed for their physical and chemical properties at the Fitche Research Center’s soil laboratory (Table 1). For the Fitche and Bishoftu stations, secondary soil data were used, as up-to-date and reliable information was readily available from the respective research centers (Table 1). In contrast, primary soil data were collected at Degem, where such official records were lacking. This data sourcing approach was considered during the model calibration process. However, to enhance accuracy and consistency, future studies will aim to collect primary soil data for all sites. Additionally, soil water balance parameters were estimated based on soil texture using the SBUILD software, integrated within DSSAT v4.8.5 (Table 1).

Experimental data source

Calibration data were obtained from independent datasets covering the 2021/22 and 2022/23 seasons provided by Fitche Agricultural Research Center, along with a field trial conducted at the Fitche (Wakene) station during the 2023/24 cropping season. After calibrating the cultivar coefficients, the accuracy of the model was evaluated using independent experimental data collected in 2024/25 cropping season from Degem and Bishoftu stations.

Cultivars coefficient estimation

Several statistical tools and techniques are commonly used to estimate and refine genetic coefficients, including regression analysis, sensitivity analysis, and optimization methods24. In this study, optimization approaches particularly trial and error adjustments were employed to systematically calibrate the coefficients, aiming to minimize the differences between simulated and observed data.

Reference cultivars like Yecora Rojo help initiate this process in model DSSAT. Yecora Rojo, a semi-dwarf spring wheat from CIMMYT, is ideal due to its well-documented coefficients, consistent performance, and widespread use in calibration studies18, making it a reliable baseline for comparative calibration.

The resulting genetic coefficients were then used for model evaluation. The CSM–CERES-Wheat model incorporates seven cultivar-specific coefficients that define the growth and development characteristics of a wheat cultivar. Four of these coefficients (P1V, P1D, P5, and PHINT) relate to phenological development and life cycle processes, while the remaining three (G1, G2, and G3) represent growth characteristics25 (Table 2). The adjusted genetic coefficient would somewhat simulate for conditions of wheat productivity under the recommended management practice options for Dendea cultivar at Fitche. CERES-wheat model calibration and performance evaluation

Model calibration

Model calibration is essential in crop modeling to estimate genetic coefficients that determine cultivar performance under varying conditions24. In this study, cultivar-specific coefficients were calibrated to align DSSAT-simulated outputs with observed field data. The model was configured using site-specific soil characteristics, daily weather data, and detailed crop management information, with initial simulations run using default coefficients. The GLUE Coefficient Estimator within DSSAT was then applied to iteratively calibrate key genetic coefficients (P1, P2, P5, G1, G2, G3, and PHINT) using a probabilistic approach (Ferreira et al., 2024). Calibration employed independent field data from Fitche for the 2021/22–2023/24 seasons, based on flowering and maturity dates, leaf area index (LAI), top weight, above-ground biomass, and grain yield.

Model evaluation

After calibration, the model was evaluated using independent field data from Bishoftu and Degem during the 2024/25 cropping season. The calibrated genetic coefficients were applied to simulate wheat yield under varying weather conditions. Model performance was assessed by comparing simulated and observed crop development and growth traits using single-point indicators (days to flowering and physiological maturity, LAI at harvest, final biomass, and yield) and time-series data (LAI and biomass dry weight), supported by statistical measures of agreement to evaluate accuracy and sensitivity (10.1002/agj2.70238).

Nitrogen optimization using simulated DSSAT output variables

The study integrates agronomic, environmental, and economic components to evaluate nitrogen management strategies. This integrated approach allows simultaneous assessment of crop yield response, nitrogen losses, and the economic implications of fertilizer application, providing a comprehensive framework for optimizing nitrogen use efficiency. Nitrogen optimization was conducted using DSSAT-simulated outputs to identify management strategies that balance productivity, economic efficiency, and environmental sustainability, following the approach of26. Simulated wheat yields and related output variables were analyzed across different nitrogen rates and application timings, including split applications. These model outputs were further used to evaluate wheat responses under projected future climate conditions and to determine nitrogen management practices that maximize nitrogen use efficiency while maintaining or improving yield.

Environmental sustainability in nitrogen optimization

To maintain environmental sustainability, nitrogen management was optimized to reduce nitrogen losses and enhance crop nitrogen uptake27. Nitrogen losses through denitrification, runoff, leaching, and volatilization reduce plant-available nitrogen and contribute to soil degradation, water pollution, and greenhouse gas emissions, particularly nitrous oxide (N2O)28. In this study, nitrogen management options were evaluated using DSSAT simulation outputs to identify practices that minimize these losses while increasing nitrogen use efficiency (NUE). Techniques such as precision agriculture, split fertilizer applications, and controlled-release fertilizers were considered for their potential to synchronize nitrogen availability with crop demand29. Split application, in particular, was emphasized because applying nitrogen in smaller, well-timed doses during the growing season improves uptake, reduces leaching and volatilization, and enhances overall nitrogen efficiency30.

Nitrogen uptake (kg N ha−1) is the total amount of nitrogen that the wheat crop takes up from the soil; the objective is to maximize uptake to boost crop growth and yield. The data source is the NUCM DSSAT model outputs.

Nitrate leaching (kg N ha−1) refers to the downward flow of nitrate (NO₃⁻) through the soil profile, frequently outside the root zone. This movement can contaminate groundwater and cause surface waters to become eutrophic. The information came from NLCM output-based seasonal DSSAT model runs.

The nitrous oxide emissions (kg N ha−1) are caused by microbial activities, notably nitrification and denitrification in the soil, based on data from seasonal DSSAT model simulations utilizing the N2OEM output. According to the31, N2O is a powerful greenhouse gas that has the potential to cause 298 times more global warming than carbon dioxide over a 100-year period.

Agronomic productivity

The yield response to nitrogen fertilizer in this component assess the response of wheat productivity to nitrogen application across future climatic scenarios (SSP4.5 and SSP8.5 for the 2050s and 2080s).

Aboveground biomass (kg ha−1)

The entire dry weight of all plant matter above the soil surface, including grain, leaves, and stems. It was derived from the DSSAT model outputs of CWAM and shows the cumulative growth of the crop’s aboveground components over the course of the growing season. It is a crucial indication of crop productivity and health in simulation models such as DSSAT.

Grain of yield (kg ha−1)

It refers to the weight of the grain that was harvested. It is derived from the DSSAT model outputs of HWAM, which are used to assess crop response to nitrogen under various conditions. It is a crucial indication of crop performance and economic value and represents the crop’s final productive output.

Partial economic analysis

Using the concepts of partial budget analysis, the economic advantage of each nitrogen rate was examined. If a treatment’s marginal rate of return (MRR) is greater than 100%, it can be considered economically viable32.

Nitrogen response function (NRF) and Economic optimum nitrogen rate (EONR)

Nitrogen response function (NRF)

The Nitrogen Response Function (NRF) is a mathematical model that describes how the rates at which nitrogen fertilizer is applied and wheat output are related. Regression analysis is usually used to derive it, and it measures how yields respond to increasing nitrogen inputs. This method makes it easier to determine the rate of fertilizer that will yield the greatest agronomic efficiency by identifying the point at which more nitrogen produces diminishing returns33. The NRF predicts yield dynamics up to a plateau or peak, after which further nitrogen supply no longer considerably increases productivity34.

The nitrogen response function (NRF) can be represented using several mathematical formulations, including exponential response models and empirical functions such as Mitscherlich, quadratic, quadratic–plateau, and linear–plateau models. Among these alternatives, the Mitscherlich function was selected for this study because it represents diminishing marginal yield response and asymptotic yield behavior consistent with crop physiological responses to nitrogen. Unlike quadratic models, it avoids unrealistic yield decline at high nitrogen rates, and compared with plateau models, it captures gradual saturation rather than abrupt yield thresholds. These characteristics make it well suited for estimating economic optimum nitrogen rates (EONR) and have led to its widespread application in nitrogen optimization studies35–37.

The NRF model was fitted using data derived from DSSAT-simulated yields at different nitrogen rates in various stations, climate conditions, and time periods. The model was selected because of its distinctive response pattern, which shows that yield gradually plateaus as it approaches a maximum yield and increases quickly at low N rates. This nonlinear response model, which is frequently employed in agronomic research, shows that crop output rises asymptotically with nutrient inputs38.

Economic optimum nitrogen rate (EONR)

The Economic Optimum Nitrogen Rate (EONR) is defined as the nitrogen application rate that maximizes economic return by balancing grain revenue and fertilizer cost. It is determined at the point where the marginal value product of nitrogen equals the marginal cost of nitrogen fertilizer, obtained by taking the first derivative of the Nitrogen Response Function (NRF) and solving for the nitrogen rate39. Application of nitrogen beyond the EONR is economically inefficient because additional fertilizer costs exceed the value of yield gains. Thus, EONR integrates agronomic yield response with economic conditions to provide practical nitrogen management recommendations.

The EONR was estimated using a nitrogen response function derived from the fitted Mitscherlich model40. Economic analysis incorporated location-specific market conditions using three-year average prices (2023–2025). Grain and fertilizer prices were 66 and 111Birr kg−1 in Degem, 68 and 105.5Birr kg−1 in Fitche, and 72 and 95Birr kg−1 in Bishoftu, respectively, based on local market data41. These correspond to baseline prices of US$0.46, US$0.47, and US$0.50 kg−1 for wheat grain, and US$0.77, US$0.73, and US$0.66 kg−1 for nitrogen fertilizer at Degem, Fitche, and Bishoftu, respectively.

To assess the robustness of the estimated EONR under changing economic conditions, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by varying grain and fertilizer prices by ± 20%42. Uncertainty in EONR estimates was quantified using Monte Carlo simulation based on the variance–covariance matrix of the fitted Mitscherlich parameters, from which 95% confidence intervals were derived43

Suggested nitrogen fertilizer management

The study generated two sets of nitrogen recommendations to improve wheat nitrogen management. The first set was derived directly from DSSAT-simulated outputs for discrete nitrogen rates, based on yield, nitrogen uptake, leaching, and economic performance. The second set was developed by fitting a Nitrogen Response Function (NRF) to these simulated data across locations, climate scenarios, and time periods. The NRF establishes a continuous relationship between nitrogen rate and yield, extending the DSSAT estimates obtained at specific nitrogen levels (0, 46, 69, 92, and 115 kg N ha−1). This enables more precise estimation of the Economic Optimum Nitrogen Rate (EONR) and improves the accuracy and adaptability of recommendations under varying agro-climatic conditions.

Results and discussions

The DSSAT-simulated output variables used for nitrogen optimization indicate a balanced improvement in environmental sustainability, agronomic productivity and economic viability.

Maintaining environmental sustainability in balance

Simulated nitrogen uptake (N-Uptake)

Simulated N-uptake increased very highly significant (P < 0.001) with higher N rates and split applications across all climate scenarios and periods (Table 3). At Degem, N-uptake ranged from 53 kg N ha−1 (control) to 147 kg N ha−1 under 115 kg N ha−1 applied in three splits in 2050 (SSP4.5). Under SSP8.5, uptake increased from 69 to 159 kg N ha−1 for the same treatments. The highest uptake occurred in 2080, reaching 150 kg N ha−1 (SSP4.5) and 171 kg N ha−1 (SSP8.5) with 115 kg N ha−1 in three splits. Lower N rates and fewer splits resulted in significantly reduced uptake (46–69 kg N ha−1), indicating inadequate N supply (Table 3). These results indicate that split nitrogen application improves synchronization between nitrogen supply and crop demand, and while elevated CO2 enhances crop growth, increased nitrogen uptake occurred mainly at 115 kg N ha−1, where sufficient nitrogen availability met the higher demand induced by enhanced photosynthesis. Therefore, 115 kg N ha−1 applied in three splits is recommended for optimal N-uptake at Degem under future climate scenarios.

At Fitche, N-uptake increased from 64 kg N ha−1 (control) to 161 kg N ha−1 under 115 kg N ha−1 in three splits in 2050 (SSP4.5). Under SSP8.5, uptake was 63 kg N ha−1 (control) and 158 kg N ha−1 (115 kg N ha−1 in three splits). The highest uptake (166 kg N ha−1) was projected in 2080 for both scenarios (Table 3). Uptake under two splits was only slightly lower than three splits, suggesting limited additional benefit from the third split. Hence, 115 kg N ha−1 applied in two or three splits is recommended, with two splits being more feasible for resource-limited farmers.

At Bishoftu, simulated N-uptake was lower than at Degem and Fitche but still increased very highly significant (P < 0.001) with higher N rates and split applications. Under SSP4.5 in 2050, uptake increased from 35 kg N ha−1 (control) to 123 kg N ha−1 (115 kg N ha−1 in three splits). Under SSP8.5, uptake ranged from 37 to 125 kg N ha−1. The maximum uptake declined to 109 kg N ha−1 by 2080. Uptake was similar between 92 kg N ha−1 in three splits (111 kg N ha−1) and 115 kg N ha−1 in two splits, indicating a plateau (Table 3). Therefore, 92 kg N ha−1 in three splits or 115 kg N ha−1 in two splits is recommended for Bishoftu, offering similar efficiency with lower labor or fertilizer input.

Across all stations and scenarios, the highest N uptake was obtained with 115 kg N ha−1 applied in two to three splits, indicating that improved timing and availability of N enhanced crop uptake. This suggests that the current maximum rate (115 kg N ha−1) may not be optimal and higher rates could be required. The slightly greater N uptake under SSP8.5, particularly in 2080, likely reflects increased crop demand under higher CO2 and temperature. However, site-specific differences such as the plateau observed at Bishoftu indicate the need for tailored N management that balances uptake with economic and environmental sustainability.

These findings align with previous studies showing linear increases in N uptake with higher N rates and timely application. For example, Bahiru et al.44 reported maximum uptake of 298.2 kg ha−1 (station) and 193.3 kg ha−1 (on-farm) at 184 kg N ha−1. Fresew et al.45 observed N uptake of 178.5 kg ha−1 at 360 kg N ha−1 and 113.5 kg ha−1 at 253 kg N ha−1. Similarly, Temesgen et al.46 and Nano47 found that N uptake increased with N rate, reaching maximum values at 92 kg N ha−1 and 120 kg N ha−1, respectively.

Simulated nitrate leaching

Simulated nitrate leaching did not differ significantly (P < 0.05) among split applications or N rates within each period and climate scenario at Degem, Fitche, and Bishoftu (Table 4). However, the model suggested a general increase in leaching over time and under more severe climate scenarios. At Degem, leaching was projected to rise from approximately 21–22 kg ha−1 in 2050 (SSP4.5) to 24–25 kg ha−1 in 2080 (SSP4.5), and further to around 35 kg ha−1 under SSP8.5 in 2080 (Table 4). Fitche showed a similar trend, increasing from roughly 27–28 kg ha−1 in 2050 to 29–30 kg ha−1 in 2080 (SSP4.5) and 33–34 kg ha−1 under SSP8.5 (Table 4). Bishoftu exhibited lower simulated leaching (6–7 kg ha−1 in 2050 to ~ 10 kg ha−1 in 2080), but the upward trend remained (Table 4). These results suggest that climate change, particularly under SSP8.5, may contribute to increased nitrate leaching through changes in rainfall and soil processes, although the magnitude of these projections should be considered indicative rather than precise. The increase in simulated nitrate leaching for 2080 is likely may be associated with higher rainfall intensity and greater soil moisture, which may enhance nitrate movement below the root zone.

These results emphasize the need to integrate agronomic response and local economics in nitrogen management69,70.

Net economic benefit (NEB) under price sensitivity analysis

Net economic benefit (NEB) at the economic optimum nitrogen rate was estimated for three Ethiopian sites (Bishoftu, Degem, Fiche) under two climate scenarios (SSP4.5, SSP8.5) for 2050 and 2080 across five price scenarios, with 95% confidence intervals reflecting uncertainty propagation from Mitscherlich function parameters via Monte Carlo simulation (Fig. 4). Baseline NEB ranged from 1577–2099 USD ha−1, demonstrating strong site-specificity and climate influence, with Fiche consistently showing highest values (2028–2099 USD ha−1), followed by Degem (1752–1946 USD ha−1) and Bishoftu (1577–1685 USD ha−1) (Fig. 3). Grain price changes exerted larger effects (± 18–24%) than fertilizer price changes (± 1–2%), as revenue responses outweigh cost adjustments (Fig. 4). Temporal trends varied by location: NEB increased at Degem under both scenarios (+ 2–8%) but declined at Fiche under SSP8.5 (–1.9%) and at Bishoftu under SSP4.5 (–3.1%), suggesting location-specific climate thresholds. SSP8.5 often outperformed SSP4.5 in 2050 but caused declines by 2080 at sensitive sites, indicating optimal temperature ranges may be exceeded under high emissions. Confidence intervals highlight where estimates are less robust, aiding cautious interpretation, while results emphasize that grain price stabilization offers greater economic leverage than fertilizer subsidies, though both influence EONR and environmental outcomes. Site-specific adaptation strategies are essential given divergent responses to climate and price changes.

Limitations of model-based nitrogen optimization

The calibration and validation of the CERES-Wheat model for nitrogen optimization are subject to several limitations due to inherent model assumptions and data constraints. First, the model relies on simplified representations of complex biological and environmental processes, which may not fully capture the effects of pest and disease pressure, extreme weather events, or nutrient deficiencies that influence wheat growth and yield. Second, calibration depends on accurately estimating genetic coefficients for cultivars; however, these coefficients may not fully represent the genetic variability or adaptive capacity of local wheat varieties. Environmental nitrogen loss processes involve complex soil–water–microbial interactions that introduce higher uncertainty compared with crop yield simulations. Therefore, environmental indicators derived from model simulations should be considered exploratory.

Furthermore, the model’s accuracy is highly sensitive to the quality and availability of input data, including weather, soil properties, and management practices. In regions like Central Oromia, limited or inaccurate data can reduce confidence in simulation results. Temporal and spatial constraints also affect model performance because field experiments used for calibration and validation often span only a few years and are conducted at limited locations, which may not reflect broader climatic variability or soil heterogeneity across the region. Finally, the model generally assumes uniform management practices, whereas actual farmer practices may vary widely, potentially leading to discrepancies between simulated and observed yields.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study employed the CERES-Wheat model within the DSSAT framework to optimize N management for yield and environmental sustainability and estimate economic optimum nitrogen rates (EONRs) for bread wheat in Central Oromia under future climate scenarios. Model simulations indicated that increasing nitrogen application rates significantly (P < 0.001) enhanced nitrogen uptake, aboveground biomass, and grain yield across all sites, with the strongest responses generally observed at higher nitrogen levels applied in split doses. Nitrous oxide emissions tended to increase with higher nitrogen rates, suggesting a potential trade-off between productivity gains and environmental impacts.

Nitrogen leaching showed no statistically significant differences among nitrogen treatments or climate scenarios, suggesting that projected climate variability may exert a stronger influence on leaching dynamics than fertilizer management alone in the simulated environments. Partial budget analysis demonstrated that higher nitrogen rates combined with split applications generated greater economic returns than the control treatment, which consistently resulted in the lowest net benefit and a marginal rate of return of zero across all locations.

The nitrogen response function (NRF) analysis further indicated that future EONRs are likely to increase under projected climate conditions, with estimated ranges of 158–169 kg N ha−1 at Degem, 182–185 kg N ha−1 at Fitche, and 180–191 kg N ha−1 at Bishoftu. A 20% increase in grain price produced the highest economic returns, while a 20% decrease reduced profitability. Fertilizer price changes caused smaller variations in net benefit, indicating that wheat price has a stronger effect on economic outcomes than input costs. These findings highlight pronounced spatial and temporal variability in nitrogen requirements under climate change, emphasizing the limitations of uniform fertilizer recommendations.

The results of this study demonstrate the potential value of crop simulation models for exploring site-specific and climate-responsive nitrogen management strategies in wheat-based systems. However, as the findings are derived solely from model simulations, the estimated nitrogen rates and split application strategies should be considered indicative rather than prescriptive. Multi-location and multi-season field experiments are therefore essential to validate the simulated responses before these recommendations can be confidently promoted through extension services or adopted by farmers.

Future research should integrate long-term field trials with modeling approaches to refine EONRs, improve nitrogen use efficiency, and assess trade-offs between yield gains, economic returns, and environmental impacts, particularly greenhouse gas emissions. Policymakers and researchers may use these results as a scientific basis for reviewing Ethiopia’s blanket nitrogen fertilizer recommendations and for designing climate-smart fertilizer strategies, while ensuring that any policy or extension decisions are supported by robust field validation. Capacity building for extension agents and farmers should focus on adaptive nitrogen management principles rather than fixed application rates.
