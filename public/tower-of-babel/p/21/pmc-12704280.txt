Mechanism of NUMBL enhancing axitinib resistance in clear cell renal cell carcinoma by UCHL1-mediated deubiquitination of MMP9
Authors: Ziyi Wang, Lian Chen, Hang Zhang, Zhiliang Yan, Kailong Liu, Jinchun Qi, Baosai Lu, Yuewei Yin, Chenming Zhao, Yalin Niu, Wei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe 5-year survival rate for metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is only 8%. Although antiangiogenic therapy like axitinib is the first-line treatment, tumors often develop resistance rapidly. This study investigates the role of NUMB-like endocytic adaptor protein (NUMBL) in this resistance. We found that NUMBL expression is upregulated in axitinib-resistant ccRCC cells. Knocking down NUMBL in a resistant 769P cell line significantly reduced the axitinib IC50 from 218.3 μM to 113.2 μM, thereby restoring drug sensitivity. Mechanistically, NUMBL upregulates ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCHL1). UCHL1 then interacts with and deubiquitinates matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9), leading to MMP9 protein stabilization and increased expression. This cascade ultimately promotes vasculogenic mimicry (VM), a nonangiogenic vascularization process that contributes to axitinib resistance. Our research elucidates this novel NUMBL/UCHL1/MMP9 axis in driving VM-mediated resistance at molecular, cellular, tissue, and animal levels. By integrating the roles of NUMBL, VM, and deubiquitination, this work establishes a theoretical foundation for understanding axitinib resistance and provides new perspectives for improving ccRCC treatment efficacy.Keywords: NUMBL, deubiquitination, vasculogenic mimicry, antiangiogenesis therapies, clear cell renal cell carcinoma

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the most prevalent form of primary kidney cancer, comprising 80% to 85% of such tumors, with approximately 70% of RCC cases classified as clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the most common histological subtype (1). Despite the advent of immunotherapy and targeted therapies, the incidence of RCC continues to rise, and resistance to these treatments frequently develops within months to years. Consequently, the 5-year survival rate for patients with metastatic ccRCC remains at a mere 8% (2, 3). Thus, a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving ccRCC resistance is essential for the development of novel therapeutic agents and the identification of potential therapeutic targets.

It is well established that the growth of solid tumors is dependent on an adequate blood supply for nourishment. When the diameter of a solid tumor exceeds 2 mm, the absence of new blood vessel formation to ensure sufficient blood supply results in tumor necrosis because of ischemia and hypoxia. In 1999, Maniotis et al. introduced the concept of vasculogenic mimicry (VM) in the context of human melanoma. VM is characterized by the formation of blood-transporting networks by invasive cancer cells, independent of endothelial cells, to supply nutrition and oxygen to solid tumors. This phenomenon contributes to the complexity of the tumor microenvironment and the emergence of therapeutic resistance (4, 5, 6).

Ubiquitination (UB), a vital post-translational protein modification, facilitates protein degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome system (5). Conversely, deubiquitination, executed by deubiquitinating enzymes, involves the removal of ubiquitin moieties from substrate proteins, thereby reversing their ubiquitinated state and impacting various biological processes (7, 8). Genetic variations or malfunctions in this process are intricately linked to the onset and progression of numerous human diseases, including neurological disorders and malignancies across different systems. Ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCHL1) plays a significant role in mediating the AKT signaling pathway, thereby promoting the progression of osteosarcoma and enhancing the invasive potential of breast cancer cells (9, 10). In addition, the inhibition of UCHL1 markedly reduces UCHL1-mediated cell migration in cells expressing endogenous UCHL1.

NUMB-like endocytic adaptor protein (NUMBL), an evolutionarily conserved factor, functions as an endocytic adaptor protein involved in cytokine-mediated signaling pathways and protein metabolism (11). It plays a vital role in cell fate determination during development and significantly influences processes such as targeted UB (12). NUMBL is associated with the progression of various diseases, including diabetes, glioma, osteoclastogenesis, pancreatic cancer, and multiple myeloma (12, 13, 14, 15). However, its potential role in promoting the progression of ccRCC remains unexplored.

In this study, we examined the relationship between the endocytic adaptor protein NUMBL and UCHL1, investigating their roles in VM in ccRCC and their involvement in axitinib resistance in ccRCC. Our objective is to identify novel therapeutic strategies for managing disease progression in ccRCC patients with drug resistance.

Results

Upregulation of NUMBL expression is correlated with poor prognosis in patients diagnosed with ccRCC

A prior study has indicated that increased NUMBL expression facilitates the progression of thyroid cancer. To explore the potential relationship between NUMBL and cancer onset and progression, we analyzed mRNA expression levels of NUMBL across 33 types of human cancers and corresponding normal tissues utilizing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset. Our pan-cancer analysis demonstrated that NUMBL expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues in several cancer types, including cholangiocarcinoma, esophageal carcinoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma, liver hepatocellular carcinoma, lung adenocarcinoma, lung squamous cell carcinoma, pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma, and stomach adenocarcinoma. Conversely, in bladder carcinoma, cervical squamous cell carcinoma, kidney chromophobe, and prostate adenocarcinoma, NUMBL expression was found to be downregulated in tumor tissues (Fig. 1, A and B). We conducted paired differential analysis using 72 patient samples from the TCGA database and corroborated these findings with protein expression data from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) database (Fig. 1, C and D). Furthermore, we investigated the mRNA expression of NUMBL in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets GSE68417 and GSE168845, both of which indicated elevated NUMBL expression levels in tumor tissues (Fig. 1, E and F). Subsequently, we evaluated the prognostic significance of NUMBL across 33 human cancers and found that it was associated with poorer prognosis in ACC, bladder carcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, KIRC, kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma, low-grade glioma, lung adenocarcinoma , ovarian carcinoma, and uveal melanoma (Fig. 1G). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis using the TCGA database further revealed that elevated NUMBL expression in ccRCC patients was linked to a reduced overall survival rate (Fig. 1H). In addition, NUMBL expression levels were positively correlated with clinicopathological factors, such as pathological stage, tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis, whereas no significant correlation was found with age or gender (Table 1). The diagnostic utility of NUMBL for ccRCC was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, which demonstrated its efficacy in differentiating tumor from normal tissues, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.784 (Fig. 1I). A nomogram incorporating multiple clinical variables was developed to further assess the prognostic potential of NUMBL (Fig. 1J), and the calibration curve confirmed that the predicted 1, 3, and 5-year overall survival rates were in agreement with observed outcomes (Fig. 1K). To examine NUMBL expression in ccRCC tissues, clinical specimens were collected and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin staining to distinguish between tumor and normal tissues (Fig. 1M). Immunohistochemical staining was employed to assess the expression of NUMBL in tissue samples. The findings demonstrated a significant upregulation of NUMBL protein expression in ccRCC tissues, as illustrated in Figure 1N. Complementary Western blot analyses corroborated this observation, revealing a pronounced increase in NUMBL protein levels in ccRCC tissues (Fig. 1, L and O).

NUMBL is implicated in promoting the malignant phenotype of ccRCC

To elucidate the role of NUMBL in ccRCC, we initially examined its expression across various RCC cell lines using data from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia. The analysis indicated that OSRC2 and 769P cell lines exhibited relatively high expression levels of NUMBL, whereas ACHN cells displayed comparatively low expression levels (Fig. 2A). Subsequent Western blot analyses confirmed these findings, showing elevated NUMBL protein expression in OSRC2 and 769P cells and reduced expression in ACHN cells (Fig. 2, B and C). To further investigate, we developed cell models with stable transfection of sh-NUMBL lentivirus to achieve NUMBL knockdown in OSRC2 and 769P cells and a cell model with stable transfection of oe-NUMBL lentivirus to overexpress NUMBL in ACHN cells. As shown in Fig. S1, OSRC2 and 769P cells were transfected with three distinct lentiviral sequences. The results indicated that sequence no. 3 efficiently knocked down the target gene in both OSRC2 and 769P cell lines (Fig. S1, A and B).To rescue the NUMBL knockdown phenotype, we performed a rescue experiment by transducing the NUMBL-knockdown cells with an NUMBL-overexpressing lentivirus. The results showed that NUMBL expression was significantly restored following lentiviral transduction (Fig. S1, E and F). Consequently, the stable cell line generated using lentivirus no. 3 was selected for subsequent experiments. Following lentiviral transduction, NUMBL protein expression was markedly reduced in both OSRC2 and 769P cell lines. In contrast, a significant increase in NUMBL expression was observed in ACHN cells (Fig. 2, D–F). Wound healing assays, Transwell migration or invasion assays, and clone formation assays demonstrated that the knockdown of NUMBL significantly diminished the migratory, invasive, and proliferative capacities of ccRCC cells. In contrast, the overexpression of NUMBL markedly enhanced these cellular capabilities. Upon NUMBL knockdown, within the same period, both OSRC2 and 769P cells exhibited a significant reduction in wound-healing area, a decreased number of cells migrating through Transwell chambers, and a marked decline in colony formation capacity compared with the control group (Fig. 2, G, H, J, K, M and N). Then, NUMBL overexpression in ACHN cells resulted in a significant increase in wound-healing area, enhanced cell migration through Transwell chambers, and an elevated number of colonies formed relative to controls under identical experimental conditions (Fig. 2, F, I, L and O).

Differential expression network analysis of NUMBL in ccRCC and gene set enrichment analyses

To further elucidate the biological function of NUMBL in ccRCC, we performed a single-gene differential expression analysis using the TCGA–KIRC dataset (Fig. 3A). This analysis revealed that 8550 genes (represented by blue dots) exhibited a negative correlation with NUMBL, whereas 18,680 genes (represented by red dots) showed a positive correlation (p < 0.05). Gene Ontology (GO) annotation indicated that these genes are associated with processes, such as extracellular structure reorganization, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and the regulation of ECM structural constituents. In addition, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis identified enrichment in pathways related to calcium signaling, protein digestion and absorption, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and aldosterone-regulated sodium reabsorption (Fig. 3, B–D). GO term annotation from gene set enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes associated with NUMBL are predominantly involved in processes, such as ECM proteoglycans, ECM degradation, ECM organization, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs; Fig. 3, E–H). Previous studies have indicated that during the treatment of ccRCC with targeted antiangiogenic drugs, tumor cells may undergo VM as a result of their epithelial-to-endothelial transition capabilities, thereby reducing their sensitivity to these therapeutic agents. Furthermore, ECM remodeling, which underpins the biological basis for VM capability, is intricately linked to the VM formation potential of malignant solid tumors.

Upregulated NUMBL promotes the VM of ccRCC

Subsequently, we confirmed the presence of VM structures formed by tumor cells in ccRCC tissues using paraffin-embedded sections from patients (Fig. 4A, indicated by red arrows). VM formation assays demonstrated that OSRC2 and 769P cells, characterized by high NUMBL expression, exhibit robust VM formation capabilities, whereas ACHN cells, with low NUMBL expression, do not form VM (Fig. 4B). Following NUMBL knockdown in OSRC2 and 769P cells and NUMBL overexpression in ACHN cells, we employed tube formation assays to evaluate VM formation capabilities of these three cell lines following lentiviral transfection. Following NUMBL knockdown, OSRC2 and 769P cells exhibited a significant reduction in tube formation ability compared with the control group within the same period (Fig. 4, C, D, F and G). NUMBL overexpression in NUMBL-knockdown OSRC2 and 769P cells restored tube formation capacity compared with the prerescue state (Fig. S1, H–K). Conversely, NUMBL overexpression markedly enhanced the tube formation capacity of ACHN cells under identical experimental conditions (Fig. 4, E and H).These results demonstrate that NUMBL plays a role in regulating VM in ccRCC cell lines. Considering prior research, we further examined the effects of altered NUMBL expression on the resistance of ccRCC cells to axitinib. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays demonstrated that NUMBL knockdown substantially increased the sensitivity of OSRC2 and 769P cells to axitinib, whereas NUMBL overexpression significantly reduced the sensitivity of ACHN cells to the drug. NUMBL knockdown reduced the IC50 of axitinib from 77.8 μM to 54.3 μM in OSRC2 cells and from 42.6 μM to 21.3 μM in 769P cells compared with their respective controls (Fig. 4, I and J). In contrast, NUMBL overexpression increased the IC50 from 71.9 μM to 110.8 μM in ACHN cells relative to the control group (Fig. 4K).

Upregulated NUMBL enhances the mRNA stability of UCHL1

Transcriptomic (Fig. 5A) and proteomic analyses were conducted on 769P cells transfected with sh-NUMBL. The transcriptome sequencing results suggested that knocking down NUMBL resulted in decreased RNA levels of UCHL1. And the proteomic sequencing analysis indicated that, with a threshold of Log|fold change| >1.5, NUMBL knockdown resulted in the upregulation of 11 genes and the downregulation of 13 genes (Fig. 5B). These observations led us to hypothesize that in ccRCC, elevated NUMBL expression primarily facilitates its function by upregulating gene expression (16). Among the sequencing outcomes, ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase L1 emerged as a molecule of interest. Validation through the TCGA and CPTAC databases demonstrated a positive correlation between UCHL1 expression at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5, G and H). Further cellular validation revealed that NUMBL knockdown in 769P cells resulted in decreased protein and RNA levels of UCHL1 (Fig. 5, C and E), whereas NUMBL overexpression in ACHN cells led to increased protein and RNA levels of UCHL1 (Fig. 5, D and F). To explore whether NUMBL levels influence the mRNA stability of UCHL1, actinomycin D assays were performed using 769P cells stably transfected with sh-NC and sh-NUMBL. Actinomycin D, an extensively researched antibiotic sourced from Streptomyces, functions by intercalating into DNA, thereby inhibiting DNA-dependent RNA synthesis through the suppression of RNA polymerases I, II, and III. Upon the introduction of actinomycin D into the cell culture medium, the transcription of new mRNA is halted, facilitating the assessment of mRNA stability by quantifying mRNA levels at designated time intervals following treatment (17). As illustrated in Figure 5I, the RNA of UCHL1 exhibited a more rapid decline in sh-NUMBL-transfected cells compared with control cells, suggesting that NUMBL contributes to the stabilization of UCHL1 RNA. Subsequently, we performed an RNA immunoprecipitation (IP) assay using an NUMBL-specific antibody. The results showed no significant difference between the IP and immunoglobulin G (IgG) control groups (Fig. S1G), suggesting that NUMBL does not directly bind to UCHL1 RNA. It may instead regulate UCHL1 mRNA stability indirectly through other genes.

UCHL1 stabilizes MMP9 protein expression through deubiquitination

The study conducted by Tang et al. elucidates that UCHL1 is a pivotal deubiquitinating enzyme within its family, responsible for the removal of ubiquitin molecules and the stabilization of target proteins. To investigate the mechanism by which upregulated NUMBL facilitates VM, we examined the correlation between NUMBL and the VM gene set (Fig. 6A). Among the genes analyzed, SNAI2, SNAI1, LOXL2, MMP9, MMP2, TWIST2, TWIST1, TGFB1, TF, and SERPINF1 exhibited a moderate correlation with NUMBL. By intersecting the ECM gene set with the VM gene set, we identified four genes: MMP9, MMP2, SERPINF1, and LOXL2 (Fig. 6B). Analysis using the CPTAC database demonstrated a positive correlation between UCHL1 and MMP9, with the highest correlation coefficient observed (Fig. 6, C–F). These findings lead us to hypothesize that UCHL1 stabilizes and upregulates MMP9 levels in ccRCC through deubiquitination, thereby promoting VM. To test this hypothesis, we initially observed a reduction in MMP9 levels following the knockdown of NUMBL expression in 769P cells (Fig. 6, G and H), suggesting that alterations in NUMBL expression influence MMP9 levels. In the second phase of our study, we exposed 769P cells to 20 μM MG-132, a peptide aldehyde known for its ability to inhibit the proteasomal degradation pathway. This inhibition occurs through the covalent binding of MG-132 to the active site of the β-subunit, thereby effectively obstructing the proteolytic activity of the 26S proteasome complex and preventing the degradation of ubiquitinated protein substrates. Following a 12-h treatment period, cell lysates were collected for subsequent analysis, which demonstrated an upregulation in MMP9 expression levels (Fig. 6, I and J). MG-132 is recognized as a potent, reversible, and specific inhibitor of the 26S proteasome (18). In parallel, we conducted stable transfection of 769P cells with FLAG-oeMMP9 lentivirus and performed coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays to validate the direct interaction between UCHL1 and MMP9 within the cellular environment. The results indicate that UCHL1 directly regulates MMP9, thereby modulating cellular functions (Fig. 6K). We next examined the UB of MMP9 by co-IP. The results confirmed that MMP9 undergoes UB (Fig. 6L). Subsequently, we targeted UCHL1 mRNA expression in 769P cells that were stably transfected with FLAG-oeMMP9 lentivirus. After confirming the efficiency of UCHL1 protein knockdown (Fig. 6, M and P), co-IP assays revealed an increase in MMP9 UB levels following UCHL1 knockdown (Fig. 6N). Immunofluorescence analysis further demonstrated that UCHL1 and MMP9 predominantly colocalize within the cytoplasm of 769P cells (Fig. 6O). Collectively, these findings suggest that UCHL1 directly interacts with MMP9, stabilizing its protein levels by inhibiting ubiquitin–proteasome-mediated degradation through deubiquitination.

To investigate the impact of other deubiquitinating enzymes on MMP9, we knocked down USP7 expression using siRNA in OSRC2 and 769P cells. The results showed a significant reduction in USP7 protein levels upon siRNA transfection (Fig. S1, L–O). Consistent with this, MMP9 expression decreased concomitantly with USP7 knockdown (Fig. S1, P–S). Furthermore, co-IP assays revealed no direct interaction between USP7 and MMP9 (Fig. S1T). Finally, we examined the effect of UCHL1 or USP7 knockdown on tube formation ability. Knocking down UCHL1 significantly impaired the tube formation capacity of 769P cells compared with the control group within the same time frame (Fig. S1, W and X). In contrast, USP7 knockdown had no significant effect on tube formation in 769P cells (Fig. S1, U and V).

To investigate whether NUMBL functions through antagonizing the Notch signaling pathway, 769P cells were treated with N-[N-(3,5-Difluorophenacetyl)-l-alanyl]-S-phenylglycine t-butyl ester (DAPT) at concentrations of 0, 25, 50, and 75 μM. Western blot analysis showed that 25 μM DAPT significantly inhibited Notch-1 expression (Fig. S2, A and B). However, inhibition of the Notch-1 signaling pathway did not significantly alter the tube formation ability of 769P cells compared with the control group within the same period (Fig. S2, C and D).

Finally, we examined the effect of MMP9 on tube formation ability. Knockdown of MMP9 in 769P cells significantly reduced tube formation, whereas overexpression of MMP9 in ACHN cells markedly enhanced this capability compared with the control group within the same time frame (Fig. S2, E–G). Then we examined the effect of MMP9 on axitinib sensitivity. The results indicated that overexpression of MMP9 significantly reduced the drug sensitivity of 769P cells to axitinib (Fig S2H).

The NUMBL–UCHL1–MMP9 pathway is implicated in the pathogenesis of ccRCC in vivo

To elucidate the pathophysiological role of the NUMBL–UCHL1–MMP9 pathway in the development of ccRCC, we developed ccRCC xenograft models by implanting nude mice with 769P cells that were genetically modified to either stably overexpress NUMBL, have NUMBL stably knocked down, or have NUMBL stably knocked down while overexpressing UCHL1. As anticipated, compared with the control group, the tumor volume was significantly increased in nude mice implanted with 769P cells overexpressing NUMBL and significantly decreased in those implanted with 769P cells with NUMBL knockdown (Fig. 7, A and C). Notably, the tumor volume was significantly larger in nude mice implanted with 769P cells with NUMBL knockdown but overexpressing UCHL1 compared with those with NUMBL knockdown alone (Fig. 7B). Immunohistochemical analysis clearly demonstrated that NUMBL overexpression resulted in a significant increase in UCHL1 and MMP9 expression, whereas NUMBL knockdown led to decreased UCHL1 and MMP9 expression. Importantly, UCHL1 overexpression in the context of NUMBL knockdown resulted in a significant rebound in MMP9 expression (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining techniques were employed to assess Ki-67 expression within the xenograft tissues. As illustrated in Figure 7, E and F, the overexpression of NUMBL resulted in a marked increase in Ki-67 expression compared with the control group, whereas NUMBL knockdown led to a significant reduction in Ki-67 expression. Importantly, the overexpression of UCHL1 in the context of NUMBL knockdown induced a notable rebound in Ki-67 expression levels. These findings clearly suggest that the dysregulation of the UCHL1–MMP9 regulatory pathway, mediated by NUMBL upregulation, plays a contributory role in the tumorigenesis of ccRCC in vivo.

Inhibition of NUMBL expression reverses resistance to axitinib in ccRCC cells

Furthermore, the inhibition of NUMBL expression reverses resistance to axitinib in ccRCC cells. To investigate whether the inhibition of NUMBL can reverse the resistance of ccRCC cells to axitinib, we developed an axitinib-resistant cell line, 769PR, derived from the 769P cell line through a stepwise concentration escalation method, as depicted in Figure 8A, The IC50 values of axitinib were determined to be 42 μM in 769P cells and 226.4 μM in 769PR cells. The IC50 was determined for the induced cell line, and the drug resistance index was calculated by dividing the IC50 of the resistant cells by the IC50 of the parental cells. A drug resistance index value greater than five was established as the criterion for the successful development of the resistant model. Western blot analysis confirmed an upregulation of NUMBL expression in the axitinib-resistant cell line (Fig. 8, B and C). Subsequently, NUMBL was knocked down in 769PR cells (Fig. 8, D and E). The results demonstrated that the inhibition of NUMBL expression led to a reduction in the proliferative capacity of 769PR cells (Fig. 8, E and F), impaired their ability to form VM (Fig. 8F), and inhibited their migration and invasion capabilities (Fig. 8, G–J). Notably, the inhibition of NUMBL partially reversed the resistance of 769PR cells to axitinib (Fig. 8K).

Discussion

To date, no research has investigated the association between VM and the subject of this study. In this research, we performed a single-gene differential analysis and an enrichment analysis of differential genes utilizing data from the TCGA database. Our results demonstrate that NUMBL is significantly positively correlated with genes involved in the ECM and its degradation. Notably, ECM remodeling is a fundamental mechanism underpinning VM (19, 20, 21).

VM is a critical factor in cancer biology, often significantly elevated in solid tumors with abundant blood supply, particularly following the development of drug resistance (22). The VM observed in tumors is closely linked to drug resistance and poor prognosis across various cancer types. Consequently, VM has emerged as a promising mechanism for predicting the malignant behavior of solid tumors and for reversing therapeutic resistance (23, 24). Moreover, VM has emerged as an innovative target for tumor diagnosis and treatment, offering novel insights and strategies for cancer therapy.

Within tumor tissues, antiangiogenic therapy induces apoptosis in the tumor's native blood vessels, resulting in tumor necrosis (25, 26). In contrast, VM represents a nonendothelial mechanism of tumor microcirculation, wherein tumor cells independently construct vessel-like structures, facilitating blood perfusion to the tumor tissue. This architecture enables tumor cells to directly acquire nutrients and oxygen from the bloodstream, thereby satisfying their rapid growth and metabolic requirements and diminishing the tumor's susceptibility to antiangiogenic therapies (27, 28). Initial research by Cheng et al. (29) demonstrated that VM substantially enhances the proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance of ccRCC cells (30).

In our study, the knockdown of NUMBL in ccRCC cells significantly decreased proliferation, migration, invasion capabilities, and VM, while moderately increasing sensitivity to axitinib. Conversely, overexpression of NUMBL produced the opposite effects. These findings corroborate that VM significantly facilitates the progression and drug resistance of ccRCC. Based on these two observations, we conducted an in-depth investigation into the mechanism by which NUMBL promotes the malignant behavior of ccRCC.

We explored how NUMBL influences the malignant behavior of ccRCC by examining its role in progression and drug resistance. Using NUMBL single-gene differential expression analysis and in vitro proteomic sequencing, we found a positive correlation between NUMBL and UCHL1, with NUMBL elevating UCHL1 RNA levels. Conversely, NUMBL knockdown reduced UCHL1 protein expression. The UCHL protein family, particularly UCHL1, is known for its deubiquitinating enzyme activity (31, 32), which aids in cancer proliferation, metastasis, and drug resistance (33).

Although this study has established that NUMBL upregulates UCHL1 expression through post-transcriptional regulation by enhancing mRNA stability, the precise molecular bridge connecting these two factors remains to be elucidated. Our RNA IP results demonstrate that NUMBL does not directly bind to UCHL1 mRNA, strongly suggesting the involvement of one or more intermediary factors. Elucidating this unknown mechanism—whether it involves RNA-binding proteins, microRNA networks, or other post-transcriptional regulatory components—will be a key objective for future research.

Previous studies, such as those by Cheng et al. (34), have shown that UCHL1 stabilizes KDM4B, enhancing vascular endothelial growth factor signaling and contributing to bevacizumab resistance in ccRCC. Kgara et al. demonstrated that CpG hypermethylation of the UCHL1 gene promoter is implicated in the pathogenesis and poor prognosis of RCC (35). Furthermore, Seliger et al. (36) observed that UCHL1 overexpression is associated with tumor progression and alterations in von Hippel–Lindau gene expression. Despite these findings, the upstream regulators and downstream mechanisms of UCHL1 remain inadequately characterized. In our study, the knockdown of NUMBL resulted in a reduction of UCHL1 mRNA levels. In addition, when cellular transcription was inhibited using actinomycin D, the degradation rate of UCHL1 mRNA was accelerated in the NUMBL-knockdown group. These experimental findings confirm that UCHL1 expression is diminished following NUMBL knockdown.

To identify the effective target through which NUMBL facilitates VM via UCHL1, we intersected gene sets related to the ECM with those associated with VM, identifying four potential target genes. Correlation analysis of these four molecules with UCHL1, conducted using the CPTAC database, ultimately identified MMP9 as the potential target with the highest correlation coefficient. MMP9, a pivotal enzyme implicated in the degradation of the ECM, is a member of the MMP family (37, 38). Previous research has established that MMP9 can facilitate VM-mediated resistance to antiangiogenic therapies in various solid tumors (39).

To elucidate the influence of UCHL1 on MMP9 expression, we employed an in vitro model involving the stable transfection of FLAG-oeMMP9 into 769P cells. Initial analyses confirmed the presence of UB modifications on MMP9 and identified an interaction between UCHL1 and MMP9. Further investigations revealed that UCHL1 knockdown markedly enhanced the UB of MMP9. These functional insights suggest that NUMBL may affect the progression of ccRCC by modulating MMP9 degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, mediated by UCHL1 (Fig. 9).

NUMBL enhances the mRNA stability of UCHL1 and promotes the deubiquitination of MMP9. MMP9, matrix metalloproteinase 9; NUMBL, NUMB-like endocytic adaptor protein; UCHL1, ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1.

It remains to be formally established that UCHL1's catalytic activity is essential for MMP9 deubiquitination, a key limitation of our present model. Although our data suggest that UCHL1 likely modulates MMP9 through its enzymatic function, definitive proof awaits future experiments employing catalytically inactive point mutants (e.g., UCHL1 C90S) in both cellular and cell-free systems to definitively establish this requirement.

In addition, we examined the clinical relevance of NUMBL in stabilizing MMP9 protein expression through the upregulation of UCHL1 in renal cancer. Our findings demonstrate that both NUMBL and MMP9 are markedly overexpressed in renal cancer tissues compared with adjacent noncancerous tissues, with particularly notable overexpression observed in axitinib-resistant strains of ccRCC. Moreover, the inhibition of NUMBL effectively suppressed the progression of ectopic renal cancer in ccRCC mouse models. These results elucidate the potential mechanisms through which NUMBL regulates UCHL1 and MMP9 in renal cancer and suggest that targeting NUMBL may represent a promising therapeutic strategy, particularly for patients exhibiting concurrent axitinib resistance, thereby potentially improving their prognosis.

Our study presents several limitations that warrant consideration in future research initiatives. The incorporation of various inhibitors into cellular assays to analyze and compare their effects on cellular behavior would significantly enhance the research outcomes, thereby providing further support for the scientific hypothesis that NUMBL upregulation plays a critical role in promoting VM and contributing to axitinib resistance. Furthermore, while we successfully induced the axitinib-resistant cell line 769PR from the parental 769P line and conducted in vitro experiments demonstrating that NUMBL inhibition can reverse drug resistance in ccRCC and increase sensitivity to axitinib, it is essential to validate these findings through in vivo studies. Consequently, we plan to investigate a range of inhibitors to assess cellular drug sensitivity and conduct in vivo experiments to elucidate the role of NUMBL in axitinib resistance in ccRCC cells. This comprehensive approach will enhance our understanding of the underlying mechanisms and reinforce our conclusions regarding the significance of NUMBL in mediating axitinib resistance.

However, NUMBL acts as a tumor suppressor in certain other cancers, such as malignant melanoma and glioma. This apparent contradiction with its tumor-promoting role in ccRCC leads us to hypothesize that the functional outcome of NUMBL-mediated signaling is highly context dependent, influenced by cell type–specific networks and the overall signaling landscape. We speculate that the characteristic von Hippel–Lindau deficiency and hypoxic microenvironment in ccRCC may reprogram NUMBL’s function, ultimately contributing to its opposing role in this malignancy.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that in renal cancer models, NUMBL leads to increased expression of UCHL1, which is critical for enhancing renal cancer cell proliferation and invasiveness. UCHL1 plays a pivotal role in maintaining the stability of the MMP9 protein by removing ubiquitin modifications. Downregulation or inhibition of NUMBL can effectively reduce the level of vascular mimicry in ccRCC, enhance sensitivity to axitinib, and reverse drug resistance, thereby presenting a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of ccRCC.

Experimental procedures

Data acquisition

RNA-sequencing data and clinical data for patients were obtained from the TCGA database. After excluding cases with no clinical information and duplicates, we included count and reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads data for 531 ccRCC patients and 71 normal tissues (accessed at https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/ on November 20, 2023). Protein expression data for ccRCC were sourced from the CPTAC database, accessed via the UALCAN data portal (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/ on November 20, 2023). Expression matrices and clinical information for GSE68417 and GSE168845 were obtained from the GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ on November 23, 2024). The microarray platforms for GSE68417 and GSE168845 were GPL6244 and GPL21185, respectively. The raw data from the GEO database were log2-transformed. Data on NUMBL expression levels in cell lines were sourced from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia database (broadinstitute.org).

Relationship between NUMBL and prognosis of ccRCC and construction of a predictive nomogram.

We employed the survival [3.3.1] and rms [6.3-0] packages to assess the clinical predictive value of NUMBL in ccRCC. The log-rank test was applied to evaluate statistical significance, and Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to illustrate survival outcomes. ROC curves were utilized to assess the differences in NUMBL expression between ccRCC samples and normal tissues. A higher area under the ROC curve value indicates greater predictive accuracy. To provide a visual prediction of ccRCC prognosis, we conducted a proportional hazards assumption test and Cox regression analysis using the survival package. Furthermore, we constructed and visualized a nomogram model based on T stage, N stage, M stage, pathological stage, and histological grade using the rms package.

Differential expression analysis

Single-gene differential analysis of NUMBL was performed based on the TCGA database using DESeq2 [version 1.36.0] and edgeR [3.38.2]. The top 25% (i.e., upper quartile) were considered as high NUMBL expression, while the bottom 25% (i.e., lower quartile) were deemed as low NUMBL expression. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05, and genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were selected. Genes with an absolute fold change ≥ 2 were identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The results were visualized using a volcano plot generated with the R package ggplot2 [3.4.4]. GO (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways, and gene set enrichment analysis were conducted using clusterProfiler [4.4.4].

Human renal tissue specimens

This study enrolled four patients with RCC who underwent radical nephrectomy, including ccRCC tissues and matched normal tissues. The tissues were collected by the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University. All patients underwent radical nephrectomy without any prior treatment, and the postoperative pathology confirmed ccRCC in all cases. Immediately after surgical removal of the affected kidney, two different types of tissues were collected: nontumorous tissue (Normal, N) located more than 2 cm away from the tumor margin, and tumorous tissue (Tumor, T). Each type of tissue was divided into two parts; one part was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and the other part was fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin for subsequent experiments.

Cell culture and compound treatment

The human renal cancer cell lines 769P, OSRC2, and ACHN (obtained from the American Type Culture Collection) were cultured using standard methods. The recommended cell culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum was used. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Axitinib was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide.

Xenograft model

Four-week-old male BALB/c-nu nude mice, specific-pathogen free, were purchased from SiPeiFu Biotechnology Co, Ltd. The mice were housed in a controlled environment with regulated temperature and humidity and provided with free access to food and water, in accordance with the guidelines established by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Hebei Medical University. Four groups (N = 5 per group) of mice were subcutaneously injected with 769P cells (1 × 106) stably transfected with lentiviruses: (1) sh-Negative Control (sh-NC), (2) sh-NUMBL, (3) sh-NUMBL + oe-UCHL1, and (4) oe-NUMBL, into the skin on the lateral side of the right forelimb. Tumor sizes were measured using calipers, and tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: volume = width2 × length/2. The mice were closely monitored throughout the study period; any mouse exhibiting persistent lethargy or a rapid weight loss of 30% was immediately euthanized. At the conclusion of the experiment, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following anesthesia, and the tumors were harvested for further analysis.

Vector construction and cell transfection

The human LV-oeNUMBL, LV-shNUMBL, LV-oeUCHL1, and FLAG-oeMMP9 constructs were generated with the assistance of GeneChem; the detailed sequences are provided in Table S1. These plasmids were introduced into ccRCC cells via lentiviral transduction, and stable cell lines were established through puromycin selection. UCHL1 siRNA was purchased from GenePharma.

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay according to the product specifications (YEASEN; 40203ES80). 769P, OSRC2, and ACHN cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well. After 48 h of treatment with axitinib, 10 μl of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 1 to 4 h. All experiments were performed in triplicate. The absorbance at 450 nm for each well was analyzed using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with cell-free wells serving as blanks.

Clone formation

For the colony formation assay, cells transfected with lentiviral vectors were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 600 cells per well and cultured for 1 week. The cells were then fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (catalog no.: G1064; Solarbio) to visualize the colonies. Photographs were taken, and the number of colonies was counted.

Transwell migration and Matrigel invasion assay

In this experiment, the upper chambers of 8.0 μm pore polycarbonate membrane filters (Corning Incorporated) were coated with or without Matrigel. Transfected and nontransfected cells (2.5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in serum-free medium into the upper chambers, whereas 600 μl of complete medium was added to the lower chambers. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 10 h. Three replicate wells were set up for each experimental group, and the experiment was repeated three times. Fields were randomly selected for counting and photographed simultaneously. The number of migrating and invading cells was measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). After incubation, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. The number of cells that invaded through the Matrigel or across the polycarbonate membrane was counted under a microscope.

Glycogen staining

Paraffin sections were placed in a 65 °C environment for 2 h, followed by dewaxing with xylene. The sections were then dewaxed through a gradient of alcohol to water. Subsequently, the periodic acid-Schiff staining kit (catalog no.: C0142S; Beyotime) was used. Iodic acid solution was applied, and the sections were incubated in the dark for 10 min. After removing the iodic acid solution, an appropriate amount of Schiff's reagent was added for staining. The sections were then stained in the dark at 37 °C for 40 min. The staining solution was removed, and the sections were soaked in distilled water and washed on a shaker for 5 min. Next, 100 μl of hematoxylin staining solution was applied to each sample and stained for 30 s. The staining solution was removed, and the sections were rinsed twice with distilled water until the floating color was washed off. Differentiation was performed using hydrochloric acid–alcohol differentiation solution for 30 s. After removing the differentiation solution, the sections were rinsed with tap water and blued. Following gradient alcohol dehydration and xylene clearing, the sections were mounted with neutral gum. Observations and photographs were taken under a microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin sections of 4 μm thickness were subjected to a 2-h incubation at 65 °C, followed by dewaxing with xylene. The sections were then dewaxed through a series of graded alcohols to water and subsequently underwent high-pressure antigen retrieval. After returning to room temperature, the sections were processed using the ORIGENE PV-9000 kit. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by applying the blocking agent and incubating the sections at room temperature for 30 min. The specimens were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, the primary antibodies were removed, and an appropriate amount of reaction enhancer was added. Following a 30-min incubation at 37 °C, enhanced enzyme-labeled goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG polymer was applied, and the sections were incubated for another 30 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, a freshly prepared 3,3′-diaminobenzidine chromogen solution was added in an appropriate amount, and the sections were observed under a microscope. The color development reaction was terminated at the appropriate time. After counterstaining with hematoxylin, the specimens were dehydrated, cleared with xylene, and mounted with neutral gum for microscopic examination. The following antibodies were utilized: anti-NUMBL (catalog no.: 10111-1-AP; Proteintech, 1:200 dilution), anti-Ki67 (catalog no.: 27309-1-AP; Proteintech, 1:100 dilution), anti-CD31 (catalog no.: AB9498; 0.5 μg/ml, Abcam), and anti-MMP9 (catalog no.: 10375-2-AP; Proteintech, 1:100 dilution).

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin sections were treated at 65 °C for 2 h, followed by dewaxing with xylene and then with a gradient of alcohols to water, prior to high-pressure antigen retrieval. After returning to room temperature, the sections were treated with 0.5% Triton for 15 min and then incubated with goat serum at 37 °C for 30 min. The tissues were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, after removing the primary antibodies, the sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (1:50) staining was applied for 15 min, and then the sections were mounted with an antifade mounting medium for observation. The following antibody was used: anti-Ki67 (catalog no.: 27309-1-AP; Proteintech, 1:100 dilution). For cellular immunofluorescence staining, the antibodies used were anti-UCHL1 (catalog no.: ME2100612S; Abmart, 1:200 dilution) and anti-MMP9 (catalog no.: 10375-2-AP; Proteintech, 1:200 dilution).

Coimmunoprecipitation

Co-IP assays were performed using 769P cells transfected with FLAG-oeMMP9. Co-IP experiments were conducted using 769P cells transfected with FLAG-oeMMP9. After treatment with 20 μM MG132 for 12 h, cell lysates were prepared and subjected to IP using a FLAG antibody, with IgG serving as a negative control. The resulting immunocomplexes were analyzed by Western blotting to detect the presence of MMP9, UCHL1, and ubiquitination. Protein A/G magnetic beads were incubated with the anti-FLAG antibody at room temperature with gentle rotation for 1 h to facilitate binding. Subsequently, the bead–antibody complex was mixed with the cell lysates and incubated with gentle rotation for another 1 h. Western blot analysis was then performed using anti-MMP9 (catalog no.: 10375-2-AP; Proteintech, 1:2000 dilution), anti-UCHL1 (catalog no.: ME2100612S; Abmart, 1:1000 dilution), and antiubiquitination (catalog no.: 10201-2-AP; Proteintech, 1:10,000 dilution) antibodies.

Assessment of RNA stability

RNA stability was determined as described by Wang et al. Briefly, cells were treated with 5 μg/ml actinomycin D (Sigma–Aldrich) to inhibit global mRNA transcription. Samples were collected at specified time points. Total RNA was extracted, reverse transcribed, and the levels of the gene of interest were analyzed by real-time quantitative PCR.

Quantitative RT–PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the SevenFast Total RNA Extraction Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. For human renal cancer tissues, total RNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A. Total RNA Extraction Kit (OMEGA). RNA concentrations were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Reverse transcription was performed using a reverse transcription kit (catalog no.: 11141ES60; YEASEN) to convert RNA into complementary DNA. The quantitative RT–PCR was then conducted using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (No Rox) (catalog no.: 11201ES08; YEASEN), with β-actin serving as the reference gene. The relative expression levels of transcripts were calculated using the 2-△Ct formula.

Western blotting analysis

Western blotting analysis was performed using cells that were lysed and cleaned with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min in a 4 °C centrifuge. Protein expression levels were determined using equal amounts of protein. The immobilized proteins were incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies. The membranes were then incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 90 min, and the expression was captured using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents. We acquire unsaturated original images (in TIFF format) and use ImageJ to measure the integrated optical density of both the target and loading control bands. Following background subtraction, the target band intensity is normalized to the loading control band intensity to determine relative expression levels. Finally, statistical analysis is performed on the normalized data from multiple independent experiments to compare differences between groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 23.0 (International Business Machines Corporation) and GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad) software. Data from at least three independent experiments are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Correlations between variables were assessed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s coefficients. For all statistical calculations, a p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
