Validation of a Dermatoscopy-Based Algorithm for the Diagnosis of Acral Melanoma
Authors: Christoph Müller, Harald Kittler, Philipp Tschandl, Christoph Rinner, Marie-Luise Grausenburger, Athanassios Kyrgidis, Hiroshi Koga, Elvira Moscarella, Zoe Apalla, Alessandro Di Stefani, Ken Kobayashi, Elisabeth Lazaridou, Caterina Longo, Alice Phan, Toshiaki Saida, Elena Sotiriou, Masaru Tanaka, Luc Thomas, Iris Zalaudek, Giuseppe Argenziano, Aimilios Lallas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDiagnosis of acral melanocytic lesions can be challenging. The BRAAFF checklist was introduced as a tool to help differentiate between acral nevi and melanoma but has not been validated.MethodsWe asked raters with varying expertise in dermatoscopy to diagnose dermatoscopic images of 533 acral nevi and 144 melanomas via an online platform with and without use of the BRAAFF checklist. From the ratings, we calculated sensitivity, specificity, and interrater agreement. Additionally, a new simplified version of the checklist was also tested.ResultsWe collected 6,880 ratings from 175 readers. The BRAAFF checklist achieved a sensitivity of 92.5% and a specificity of 65.0%, which was similar to diagnosis from pattern recognition (sensitivity 90.0%, specificity: 72.1%). Interrater agreement for the BRAAFF criteria ranged from fair to moderate, with lowest agreement for parallel ridge and fibrillar pattern (alpha = 0.31) and highest for asymmetry of colors and structures (alpha = 0.46). Agreement and diagnostic accuracy were higher for more experienced readers. A simplified version with only two criteria achieved similar sensitivity (95.0%) and lower specificity (60.0%) as the original BRAAFF checklist.ConclusionThe BRAAFF checklist is a useful tool for the diagnosis of melanocytic acral lesions with acceptable sensitivity and reasonable specificity but is not superior to pattern recognition. A simplified version of the checklist could be easier to use with equal sensitivity while exhibiting a modest reduction in specificity.

Introduction

Melanoma is responsible for most skin cancer deaths. Εarly diagnosis can help reduce mortality, as tumor thickness is directly associated with prognosis. Although early detection has greatly improved with the use of dermatoscopy, melanomas located on specific anatomic sites differ in their dermatoscopic morphology and remain challenging to recognize. Palms and soles represent such an example, as suggested by the fact that acral melanomas are usually thicker at the time of diagnosis as compared to melanomas on other sites [1, 2]. Acral melanoma accounts for a small proportion of melanomas in individuals with fair skin but represents the most common subtype in patients with dark skin [3].

The most important clue to distinguish between nevi and melanoma on acral sites is the distribution of melanin. In acral melanoma, the pigment is more often found on the epidermal ridges (parallel ridge pattern), whereas in acral nevi it is more likely to be found on the furrows (parallel furrow pattern) [4–10]. Although the parallel ridge pattern is relatively specific for acral melanoma, it may be absent in up to 50% of acral melanomas and has low sensitivity [6]. Furthermore, recent data showed that the parallel ridge pattern is less frequent in small acral melanomas [11]. In 2015, the BRAAFF checklist was introduced as an algorithm to improve the diagnosis of acral melanoma [12]. It includes six criteria, four malignant and two benign features. Malignant features are “irregular blotch” (B), “parallel ridge pattern” (R), “asymmetry of structures” (A) and “asymmetry of colors” (A). Benign features are “parallel furrow pattern” (F) and “fibrillar pattern” (F), a variant of the furrow pattern.

One point is added for each malignant feature (except for the parallel ridge pattern, for which three points are added), and one point is subtracted for each benign feature. A total score of one or more points indicates acral melanoma. The reported sensitivity and specificity of the algorithm in the initial test set was 93.1% and 86.7%, respectively. However, sensitivity and specificity were not calculated by applying the algorithm to an independent test set and the evaluations used to calculate these measures were performed by three independent dermatoscopy experts only.

To date, no study has validated the BRAAFF checklist and investigated whether it facilitates the diagnosis of acral lesions compared with pattern recognition and to which extent user expertise affects accuracy. Using retrospectively collected images from multiple centers, we conducted an online reader study, involving significantly more readers and more lesions than in the original study.

Material and Methods

Online Reader Study

Data collection was based on a voluntary participation in an anonymous online reader study on melanocytic acral lesions. Raters were recruited via calls in the social media platforms of the International Dermoscopy Society (IDS). The reader study was conducted via the online platform “Dermachallenge” (https://dermonaut.meduniwien.ac.at/dermachallenge). During the registration process on this platform, the following variables were collected after informed consent was obtained: name, e-mail address, username, age (categorical), country of origin, gender, level of education and expertise. Each registered user completed a series of standardized tests to calculate a score that represented the user’s skill level. The score was calculated by combining the difficulty level for each case, determined by assessing the mean and standard deviation of correct answers for that specific case, with the individual reader’s performance. This individual performance is measured based on the number of correct answers given by the reader in relation to the overall number of correct answers provided by other readers. In essence, the score reflects both the complexity of the case and how well the reader performed compared to their peers. The score ranged in the cohort from 87.5 to 119.5.

Readers with a score up to 100 were classified as “beginners,” those from 100 to 105 as “intermediate” and all above 105 as “experts.” Only dermatology residents and board-certified dermatologists were included for all analysis. Each reader received a short tutorial explaining the BRAAFF checklist (tutorial shown in online suppl. Fig. S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000541591). Each round consisted of a random selection of 10 lesions including 5 acral nevi and 5 acral melanomas, randomly selected from the pool of nevi and melanomas included in the study. To reduce bias resulting from one reader participating in multiple rounds, we calculated the median sensitivity and specificity per reader across all readings in all rounds. For each lesion, the readers were asked to perform two different sequential evaluations, one using the BRAAFF checklist and one using pattern recognition. In the former, readers were asked to assess the presence or absence of each feature of the checklist, while in the pattern recognition approach, readers were asked to classify the lesion as nevus or melanoma. Pattern recognition can be considered as a “blink” approach, i.e., the first diagnosis that comes to mind without a structured analysis of the lesion. The two tasks were presented separately and in alternating sequence. The raters could complete multiple rounds, each one consisting of different lesions, at their discretion.

In addition to the BRAAFF checklist, we also performed all analyses using a new, simplified version of the checklist (see Table 1). In this simplified checklist, a lesion is classified as melanoma if any of the following four features is present: parallel ridge pattern, irregular blotch, asymmetry of structures or colors. The latter three features can further be combined to the single descriptor “chaos.” Leading to the final simplified checklist with only two attributes.

The simplified checklist itself was not subject to direct evaluation in the online reader study. Rather, its efficacy was inferred from the evaluations conducted by the readers.

Cases

The dermatoscopic images that were used in the reader study consisted of consecutive, retrospectively collected and anonymized images from two different datasets (1) the sample of the original publication of the BRAAFF checklist – 603 lesions (472 nevi and 131 acral melanomas) and (2) a new verification dataset with 74 additional lesions (61 nevi and 13 acral melanomas, see examples in Fig. 1). Lesions from the latter dataset were collected retrospectively in Greece and Austria. The study predominantly included patients with a European ethnic background.

Example of a nevus (a) and a melanoma (b) of the new verification dataset. Numbers of correct diagnosis with either the BRAAFF checklist, the simplified checklist or pattern recognition and picks of the BRAAFF variables are given.

All melanomas from both datasets were excised and examined histopathologically. Of the 131 melanomas, 33% (n = 48) were in situ. Nevi with typical morphology that were not excised or biopsied were monitored for at least 1 year to confirm the diagnosis. For each round, the randomization procedure was stratified so that 5 lesions were taken from each subgroup.

Statistics

The primary outcome measures were the true positive rate (sensitivity), the true negative rate (specificity) and the proportion of correct diagnoses (diagnostic accuracy) with and without application of the BRAAFF checklist. For descriptive statistics, categorical variables are given as absolute frequencies and frequencies as percentages and continuous variables are given as median and interquartile range (IQR).

A feature from the BRAAFF checklist was considered present if more than 66% of raters agreed on its existence; otherwise, it was deemed absent. In cases where a reader assessed a lesion multiple times, only the initial evaluation was considered for analysis.

Sensitivity (true positive rate) and specificity (true negative rate) were calculated for each individual user for the BRAAFF checklist and the simplified checklist as well as for pattern recognition. Then the median sensitivity and specificity were calculated among all readers. In a focused subset analysis, we exclusively utilized lesions from the newly established verification set (n = 74). For these lesions, we determined the diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity for each testing approach, including pattern recognition and both scoring methods. Finally, we calculated a “collective” answer, in which the pattern recognition, BRAAFF checklist and simplified checklist diagnosis of the majority (>50%) of the readers was used to calculate the sensitivity and specificity of each method.

We used Krippendorff’s alpha coefficient to measure interrater agreement. For this analysis, only lesions that were evaluated by at least two readers were included. Only the initial evaluation conducted by each reader was included in the analysis. For the interpretation of agreement, we used the classification from Landis and Koch [13]. The values range from 0 to 1, indicating the strength of agreement. A high value would indicate a good agreement.

Fisher’s exact test was employed to compare proportions, while the Wilcoxon-Signed-Rank test was utilized for comparing paired outcome measures (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy). All p values are 2-sided and a p value of <0.05 was regarded to indicate statistical significance. The Bonferroni correction was used to adjust for multiple comparisons. All analyses were performed using R statistical software v4.2.1 [14].

Results

Within 1 year, 175 readers (73% women) rated at least one set of 10 lesions. Among these raters, 60% were dermatology residents (n = 105) and 40% board-certified dermatologists (n = 70). The readers were separated into three skill levels: “beginner” (n = 49), “intermediate” (n = 60), and “expert” (n = 66) as described before. Altogether, the readers performed 6,880 evaluations of acral lesions. The number of lesions rated by each reader ranged from 10 to 560 (median = 20).

Frequency of Criteria by Diagnosis

Due to random sampling, 9 lesions were rated less than twice and were not included in the casewise analysis. The number of readings for the other cases (n = 668) ranged from 2 to 56 per case. If more than 66% of raters concurred that a feature from the BRAAFF checklist was present, it was deemed to exist. The parallel ridge pattern was present in 17.4% of melanomas and in 2.3% of nevi, asymmetry of structure was present in 58.3% of melanomas and in 3.6% of nevi, and asymmetry of color was present in 63.9% of melanomas and in 4.6% of nevi (Table 2).

We also compared the frequencies of criteria of in situ and invasive melanoma. While the presence of the parallel ridge pattern was equally common in invasive and in situ melanomas (16.7% vs. 18.8%, p = 0.82), dermatoscopic attributes associated with chaos (such as asymmetry of color or structure and irregular blotch) were more prevalent in invasive melanomas when compared to in situ melanomas (78.1% vs. 35.4%, p < 0.001 for asymmetry of color; 72.9% vs. 29.2% p < 0.001 for asymmetry of structure and 32.3% vs. 6.3%, p < 0.001 for irregular blotch) online supplementary Table S1.

Accuracy by Reader

In the original publication, the BRAAFF checklist achieved a sensitivity of 93.1% and a specificity of 86.7%. In the current study, the readers achieved a median sensitivity of 90.0% with pattern recognition (IQR = 20.0), 92.5% (IQR = 20.0) with the BRAAFF checklist and 95.0% (IQR = 18.6.), with the simplified checklist. The specificity was 72.1% (IQR = 21.3) with pattern recognition, 65.0% (IQR = 30.0) with the BRAAFF checklist and 60.0% (IQR = 35.0) with the simplified checklist (Fig. 2, 3). The highest diagnostic accuracy was achieved by majority vote with a sensitivity of 93.1% and a specificity of 82.9% for pattern recognition. More experienced readers had better diagnostic accuracy than beginners (Fig. 4). Expert readers achieved a median sensitivity of 94% (IQR = 16) with the BRAAFF checklist at a specificity of 77% (IQR = 26). No group improved in comparison to pattern recognition, neither with the BRAAFF checklist nor with the simplified checklist.

Accuracy by Lesion

In a subset analysis exclusively utilizing lesions from the novel verification dataset (n = 74), diagnostic accuracy was computed on a per-lesion basis. Pattern recognition achieved a median sensitivity of 95.8% (IQR = 28.0), whereas the BRAAFF checklist and the simplified checklist demonstrated sensitivities of 97.7% (IQR = 32.7) and 97.7% (IQR = 30.9), respectively. The specificity was 77.8% (IQR = 31.7) for pattern recognition, 71.4% (IQR = 32.4) for the BRAAFF checklist, and 66.7% (IQR = 33.3) for the simplified checklist, as illustrated in online supplementary Figure S2. The diagnostic accuracy was 79.3% (IQR = 33.6) for pattern analysis, 75.0% (IQR = 34.6 p = 0.053 for the difference from pattern analysis) for the BRAAFF checklist, and 69.6% (IQR = 35.4, p < 0.001 for the difference from the BRAAFF checklist) for the simplified checklist.

Interrater Agreement

Interrater agreement was calculated for 668 lesions with a median of 7 evaluations per case. Interrater agreement for diagnosis was only fair and depended on experience (Table 3 and online suppl. Table S2). Interrater agreement tended to be lower for the BRAAFF checklist than for the simplified checklist and pattern recognition. The interrater agreement for criteria of the BRAAFF checklist ranged from fair to moderate (Table 4). We found the highest levels for asymmetry of colors and asymmetry of structures and the lowest for parallel ridge pattern and fibrillar pattern. Interrater agreement on the features of the BRAAFF checklist improved with experience (online suppl. Table S3).

Discussion

Diagnostic algorithms are developed to support the diagnostic process and should be accurate, easy to use, robust, and reproducible. When algorithms are created, a high accuracy is usually the primary goal, but as algorithms are used in practice, the other attributes become much more relevant. Unfortunately, such algorithms are rarely reevaluated or revised after their initial release. Here, we re-evaluated the BRAAFF checklist for dermatoscopy of acral lesions in an online reader study. Our data confirmed that the BRAAFF checklist achieves reasonable diagnostic accuracy for acral melanoma and is a useful tool. The specificity we found was lower than in the original study, which is not surprising since we also invited novice and less experienced users to apply the checklist. Interestingly, even experts achieved lower specificity in this reader study as compared to the original study, which could be explained by the fact that in the current study the range of expertise in the expert group was broader compared to the designated experts in the original study so that the original study may have overestimated specificity.

We further demonstrated that the original checklist, which included six features, can be simplified to only two attributes without losing sensitivity. To achieve this, we combined three features related to asymmetry (irregular blotch, asymmetry of colors, asymmetry of structures) to the single attribute “chaos,” meaning any type of asymmetry. The other important attribute that bears significance for acral melanoma is the parallel ridge pattern. Benign features can be eliminated because, when chaos or the parallel ridge pattern is present, their significance is limited. In this simplified checklist, melanoma should be considered if either one feature of chaos or the parallel ridge pattern is present. This simplified checklist achieved a sensitivity of 95.0% while maintaining an adequate specificity. An alternative algorithm for acral lesions originally developed by Saida et al. [15] additionally used the criterion of size but is otherwise similar [7].

We also showed that the original BRAAFF checklist and the new simplified checklist achieve a similar diagnostic accuracy to pattern recognition. This raises the question why an algorithm should be used at all, if it does not improve diagnostic accuracy but mainly confirms what one already knows. However, an important secondary function of an algorithm is to challenge the blink diagnosis and refute it if necessary. Another advantage of algorithms is that they can justify and explain one’s diagnosis to others in a comprehensive and repeatable way, which renders them particularly suitable for teaching. While pattern recognition is subjective, hard to articulate, and challenging for others to grasp, an algorithmic approach offers an objective and robust method that should yield consistent results across different users.

However, our study showed that interrater agreement in diagnosis is no higher when the algorithm is used than when it is not. This observation may be attributed to the generally moderate agreement on dermatoscopic criteria, as indicated by previous studies and confirmed by the current one [16]. In fact, the interrater agreement for the individual features of the checklist was only fair to moderate, with the strongest agreement observed for asymmetry of colors and structures. The agreement in general was higher for experts and lower for beginners, which underlines the influence of experience not only on diagnostic accuracy but also on agreement.

Furthermore, for all three evaluation methods (BRAAFF checklist, simplified checklist and pattern recognition), readers with a higher expertise had a better sensitivity and specificity. Beginners and intermediate readers had a slightly improved sensitivity using the simplified checklist compared to the BRAAFF checklist and pattern recognition but also the lowest specificity. Expert readers showed a minimal increase in sensitivity using the BRAAFF checklist as compared to pattern recognition, without losing specificity. As expected, the highest accuracy was achieved by the “crowd wisdom” confirming the importance of diversity of opinions.

Features of chaos were significantly more frequently found in invasive melanomas compared to in situ melanomas while there was no significant difference in the prevalence of the parallel ridge pattern. The latter was found in 16% of invasive and 18% of in situ acral melanomas which is lower compared to other reports. This could be duo to several reasons. First, in our study a feature was only considered present if at least 66% of the readers described it while other studies relied on assessments of one or few experts. Second, the online format of our study may have influenced the reporting of features compared to face-to-face assessments. Take together the study is limited in this context as the frequency of features might be underrepresented. Additionally, our study focused on the BRAAFF algorithm, potentially leading to reduced focus of the readers on identification of single features.

Limitations

In the datasets used for this study, skin type was not documented. Given that the majority patients had a European ethnic background, individuals with darker skin type are underrepresented, which might lead to biased results. The study was performed online without clinical impact and differs from a real life setting in which false decisions might harm patients. Additionally, readers did not have access to metadata such as patient age or their cancer and family history, factors that could potentially influence clinical evaluations. Another limitation is that clinicians, especially those with more dermatoscopy experience, might choose variables of the BRAAFF checklist to subconsciously confirm the diagnosis already made by pattern recognition. However, the latter only reflects how the algorithms are used in practice, namely that they are often used to justify the blink diagnosis rather than to improve it.

Conclusions

Our data confirm that the BRAAFF checklist serves as a valuable tool for diagnosing melanocytic acral lesions, boasting acceptable sensitivity and reasonable specificity. Its performance is comparable to that of pattern recognition. Additionally, a simplified version of the checklist demonstrated similar sensitivity with moderately lower specificity and offers greater practicality in real-world scenarios.

Key Message

The BRAAFF checklist but also a new simplified version shows an acceptable diagnostic accuracy.
