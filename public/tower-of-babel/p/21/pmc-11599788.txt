Eight‐year efficacy and safety of tenofovir alafenamide for treatment of chronic hepatitis B virus infection: Final results from two randomised phase 3 trials
Authors: Maria Buti, Young‐Suk Lim, Henry Lik Yuen Chan, Kosh Agarwal, Patrick Marcellin, Maurizia R. Brunetto, Wan‐Long Chuang, Harry L. A. Janssen, Scott K. Fung, Namiki Izumi, Maciej S. Jablkowski, Dzhamal Abdurakhmanov, Frida Abramov, Hongyuan Wang, Irina Botros, Leland J. Yee, Roberto Mateo, John F. Flaherty, Anu Osinusi, Calvin Q. Pan, X. Shalimar, Wai‐Kay Seto, Edward J. Gane
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundIn two phase 3 studies, tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) showed non‐inferior efficacy versus tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), with more favourable renal and bone safety in patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB).AimsHere, we report the studies' final 8‐year results.MethodsCHB patients (hepatitis B e antigen [HBeAg]‐negative and HBeAg‐positive) were randomised (2:1) to double‐blind TAF 25 mg/day or TDF 300 mg/day for up to 3 years, followed by open‐label (OL) TAF through year 8. Virological, biochemical, serological and fibrosis responses, and safety, including bone and renal parameters, were evaluated. Resistance to TAF was assessed annually by deep sequencing of polymerase/reverse transcriptase and by phenotyping.ResultsAmong 1298 patients randomised to double‐blind TAF (n = 866) or double‐blind TDF (n = 432), 775 in the TAF group and 382 in the TDF group received OL TAF, including 180 and 202 who switched from TDF to TAF at year 2 (TDF2y → TAF6y) or year 3 (TDF3y → TAF5y), respectively. At year 8, among patients in the TAF8y, TDF2y → TAF6y and TDF3y → TAF5y groups, 69%, 66% and 73% (missing‐equals‐failure analysis) and 95%, 94% and 97% (missing‐equals‐excluded) of patients, respectively, achieved HBV DNA <29 IU/mL. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (Cockcroft‐Gault method; eGFRCG) and hip/spine bone mineral density (BMD) remained stable in patients receiving double‐blind/OL TAF, with only small declines at year 8. Decreases in eGFRCG and hip/spine BMD observed during double‐blind TDF improved after switching to OL TAF. No patients developed resistance to TAF.ConclusionLong‐term TAF treatment exhibited favourable safety and tolerability with high rates of viral suppression and no development of resistance. ClinicalTrials.gov numbers NCT01940341 and NCT01940471.

INTRODUCTION

Approximately 316 million people worldwide have chronic hepatitis B (CHB), and in 2019, the World Health Organization estimated that infection with hepatitis B virus (HBV) resulted in over 800,000 deaths, primarily from cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). 1 , 2 Nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) are first‐line treatments for CHB as they have been shown to suppress viral replication effectively, slow or reverse fibrosis/cirrhosis progression, and reduce HCC risk. 3 , 4 , 5 However, hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) seroclearance, an indicator of a functional cure, is rarely seen (<5%) with NAs, and as a consequence, lifelong treatment is often required, highlighting the need for safe and effective long‐term therapies. 6 , 7 , 8

Treatment with tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), an oral prodrug of the HBV polymerase/reverse transcriptase (pol/RT) inhibitor tenofovir (TFV), 9 results in high rates of viral suppression and reductions in fibrosis progression and HCC development. 3 , 4 , 6 , 7 However, renal and bone toxicities can limit its use in some patients. 10 , 11 , 12 Tenofovir alafenamide (TAF), a newer TFV prodrug, was specifically designed to enhance delivery of the active moiety (TFV‐diphosphate) to hepatocytes and lymphocytes (for the treatment of HIV infection), with 90% lower circulating TFV levels relative to TDF—a factor that is considered important in the improved safety of TAF. 13 , 14 In two randomised, double‐blind (DB), phase 3 studies (GS‐US‐320‐0108 ‘Study 108’ [Clinicaltrials.gov: NCT01940341] and GS‐US‐320‐0110 ‘Study 110’ [NCT01940471] in hepatitis B e antigen [HBeAg]‐negative and HBeAg‐positive patients, respectively), TAF demonstrated non‐inferior antiviral efficacy and superior renal and bone safety versus TDF at years 1 (48 weeks) 15 , 16 and 2 (96 weeks). 10 In addition, findings from an interim analysis at year 5 (240 weeks) were recently published. 17

As initially designed, patients were to receive 2 years of DB treatment followed by 1 year of open‐label (OL) TAF in both studies. However, at the request of the US Food and Drug Administration, a protocol amendment was enacted extending DB treatment for an additional year, while the OL TAF phase was extended to year 8 (week 384). Due to the timing of amendment implementation, approximately half of the patients in each study had DB treatment extended to year 3, but while the remainder had already switched to OL TAF at year 2, all remained eligible to continue OL TAF through year 8. Here, we report final results of the efficacy, safety and resistance analyses through year 8.

METHODS

Study design

In Study 108, the first patient was screened on 12 September 2013, and the last visit of the last participant was on 31 August 2022. In Study 110, the first patient was screened on 11 September 2013 and the last participant, last visit was on 13 October 2022. The designs and methods for Studies 108 and 110 were previously described in detail, including randomisation and power analyses. 10 , 15 , 16 Briefly, HBeAg‐negative and HBeAg‐positive adults with CHB with HBV DNA ≥20,000 IU/mL, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) >60 U/L (males) or >38 U/L (females), with and without compensated cirrhosis, and estimated glomerular filtration rate by Cockcroft‐Gault (eGFRCG) ≥50 mL/min were included. Patients were randomised 2:1 to DB TAF 25 mg or DB TDF 300 mg (each with matching placebo) once daily, followed by OL TAF for up to 6 years (Figure S1). Randomisation was stratified by HBV DNA level and oral antiviral treatment status (experienced vs. naïve). Patients coinfected with HIV, hepatitis delta virus or hepatitis C virus were excluded as well as pregnant/lactating female patients.

Protocols were approved by the review board/ethics committee of each institution. Studies were conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent. All authors had access to the study data and reviewed and approved the final manuscript.

Analysis populations

This 8‐year analysis included three groups: (1) TAF8y, patients who received DB TAF then OL TAF; (2) TDF2y→TAF6y, patients who received DB TDF for 2 years then OL TAF for 6 years; and (3) TDF3y→TAF5y, patients who received DB TDF for 3 years then OL TAF for 5 years. The full analysis set (FAS) and safety analysis set (SAS) included all patients who were randomised and received ≥1 dose of DB study drug according to their assigned treatment and actual treatment received, respectively. The OL SAS included all patients who completed DB treatment and received ≥1 dose of OL TAF.

Study assessments and endpoints

Efficacy was evaluated in the pooled population and by individual study. Efficacy endpoints included viral suppression (HBV DNA <29 IU/mL, target detected or not detected [TND]), ALT normalisation (ALT ≤upper limit of normal [ULN] in patients with ALT >ULN at baseline) as determined by central laboratory (Labcorp Drug Development [formerly Covance Laboratories], Indianapolis, IN, USA) and 2018 American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) criteria, 18 serologic responses (HBeAg and HBsAg loss/seroconversion) and fibrosis change by FibroTest (BioPredictive, Paris, France). Shifts from baseline in fibrosis categories using FibroTest scores were assessed as follows: 0.00–0.48 (Metavir F0–F1; no to mild fibrosis), 0.49–0.74 (Metavir F2–F3; moderate to severe fibrosis) and 0.75–1.00 (Metavir F4; cirrhosis). Routine safety assessments and changes in renal and bone parameters were evaluated as previously described. 10 Deep sequencing of HBV pol/RT was performed as previously described among patients with HBV DNA ≥69 IU/mL. 19 Phenotypic analysis was conducted for those with conserved site substitutions, polymorphic site substitutions when observed in ≥2 patients, or virologic breakthrough with any amino acid substitution. Serum alpha‐fetoprotein level was evaluated in all patients during screening to help rule out the presence of HCC before patients entered the study. Screening and diagnosis of HCC adhered to local standards of care; however, following the protocol amendment, hepatic ultrasonography was performed every 6 months from year 2 until the completion of study treatment. The presence of HCC was captured as an adverse event (AE).

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used for within‐group treatment outcomes. Efficacy was evaluated using the FAS; fasting lipids and renal and bone parameters were assessed using the SAS; AEs and laboratory abnormalities were analysed using the SAS and OL SAS for events occurring during the DB and OL phases, respectively. Given that rollover to OL TAF was driven by the protocol amendment timing, between‐group comparisons for efficacy and safety were not performed. While a missing‐equals‐failure (M = F) analysis was conducted as the primary approach for this final analysis, it is important to note that the predominant reasons for early study discontinuation, which occurred in approximately 25% of patients in these long‐term studies, were not attributable to lack of efficacy or AEs. Therefore, a missing‐equals‐excluded (M = E) approach for 8‐year efficacy results are more reflective of the long‐term efficacy and safety of TAF. Of note, one site did not participate in Protocol amendment 3, and all patients from this site discontinued the study on or before year 3 (week 144); therefore, those patients who completed the planned study treatments were excluded from the M = F analysis for all visits after year 3.

RESULTS

Patient disposition and baseline characteristics

A total of 1298 patients—including 425 who were HBeAg‐negative and 873 who were HBeAg‐positive—were randomised to receive DB TAF (n = 866) or TDF (n = 432; Figure 1). Of 866 patients receiving DB TAF, 89% (n = 775) entered the OL phase (at either year 2 or 3), and 75% (n = 647) completed OL treatment. Of 432 patients receiving DB TDF, 88% (n = 382) entered the OL phase, including 180 at year 2 (TDF2y → TAF6y) and 202 at year 3 (TDF3y → TAF5y), and 76% (n = 327) completed OL treatment. Efficacy

Virologic response

The high rates of viral suppression achieved and maintained during the DB phase were also maintained over the OL treatment period through year 8 (Table 2). Similar proportions of patients achieved HBV DNA <29 IU/mL across treatment groups using M = F analysis (TAF8y, 69% [95% CI, 65%–72%]; TDF2y → TAF6y, 66% [95% CI, 59%–73%]; TDF3y → TAF5y, 73% [95% CI, 67%–79%]) (Table 2, Table S1; Figure S2A). Similar rates of viral suppression were observed for HBeAg‐negative and HBeAg‐positive patients; response rates were also comparable by M = E analysis (TAF8y, 95% [95% CI, 93%–97%]; TDF2y → TAF6y, 94% [95% CI, 88%–97%]; TDF3y → TAF5y, 97% [95% CI, 93%–99%]; total TDF → TAF, 96% [95% CI, 93%–98%]), and 57%–64% achieved HBV DNA <29 IU/mL with TND (Figures S3 and S4).

Biochemical response

Rates of ALT normalisation during the DB phase were higher in patients receiving TAF versus TDF with similar results by HBeAg status. Moreover, the proportions achieving ALT normalisation by both criteria increased after TDF‐treated patients were switched to OL TAF (Table 2, Tables S1 and S2; Figures S2B, S2C, S5, and S6). At year 8, ALT normalisation rates were similarly high across treatment groups by M = F analysis (central laboratory criteria, 59%–63%; AASLD criteria, 50%–58%) and M = E analysis (central laboratory criteria, 82%–87%; AASLD criteria, 71%–79%; Table 2).

Serologic response

Among HBeAg‐positive patients, rates of HBeAg loss and seroconversion were similar between TAF‐ and TDF‐treated patients during DB treatment, with progressive increases seen during the OL phase (Table 2; Figures S2D, S2E, and S7). At year 8, rates of HBeAg loss (M = F, 29%–32%; M = E, 44%–46%) and seroconversion (M = F, 18%–23%; M = E, 27%–33%) were similar across the three treatment groups (Table 2). Small but progressive decreases in HBsAg levels occurred across treatment groups; at year 8, mean change from baseline ranged from −0.80 to −0.93 log10 IU/mL (Table S2; Figure S8). By year 8, 2%–3% of patients had achieved HBsAg loss with or without seroconversion (Table 2; Tables S1 and S2). Overall, 12/1298 (0.9%) patients (TAF8y, n = 8 [DB, n = 4; OL, n = 4]; total TDF → TAF, n = 4 [DB, n = 0; OL, n = 4]) had their study treatment discontinued for HBsAg seroconversion (Figure 1).

Fibrosis change

At baseline, approximately 10% (range: 8%–12% across groups) of patients had cirrhosis based on FibroTest score category analysis (Table 1). Mean (SD) FibroTest scores decreased minimally, ranging from −0.05 (0.150) to −0.01 (0.147) at year 8 across groups (Table 2). Of note, the proportion of patients with cirrhosis by FibroTest category was substantially lower at year 8 compared to baseline across all treatment groups (Tables S2 and S3). Specifically, when evaluated by categorical shifts from baseline, roughly 30% of patients overall with cirrhosis at baseline remained in this category at year 8, with the majority shifting to lower categories. Conversely, only small proportions of patients in the TAF8y and total TDF → TAF groups with either no/mild fibrosis or moderate/severe fibrosis (baseline FibroTest scores) showed progression to a higher fibrosis category at year 8 (Table S3). In general, shifts in fibrosis were similar among HBeAg‐negative and HBeAg‐positive patients (Table S4).

HBV drug resistance

As previously reported, TAF resistance was not observed through year 5. 17 Sequencing results are presented in Table S5 for years 6–8. At year 8, 2% (n = 29) of patients met the criteria for pol/RT sequencing: 59% (n = 17) for viral blip, 31% (n = 9) for virologic breakthrough and 10% (n = 3) with persistent viraemia. Among 22 successfully sequenced patients, eight had no changes from baseline, two had conserved site substitutions and 12 had polymorphic site substitutions. Eight patients (28%) qualified for phenotyping at year 8, two with conserved site substitutions and six for polymorphic site substitutions seen in two or more patients. All viruses containing these substitutions at year 8 remained sensitive to TAF in vitro (fold change in half‐maximal effective concentration <2 from baseline).

Safety

AEs and laboratory abnormalities

A safety summary for the DB phase is provided in Table S6. Similar incidences of AEs were reported across groups during OL TAF treatment (Table 3). Most AEs during the OL phase were mild or moderate (Grades 1 or 2); 6%–8% of patients experienced AEs of Grade 3 or greater severity. Serious AEs occurred in 12%–13% of patients across groups, with four serious AEs—all occurring in the TAF group—that were reported by the investigator as related to the study drug. Over 8 years, a total of six deaths occurred (TAF8y, n = 3; total TDF → TAF, n = 3), with one death occurring during the OL phase (pancreatic adenocarcinoma in the TAF8y group).

The most common AEs (≥5%) were headache, upper respiratory tract infection, nasopharyngitis and cough, as reported in the year 5 publication 17 ; in addition, through year 8, hypertension, arthralgia and back pain were commonly reported. Twelve of 1157 (1%) patients (TAF8y, n = 9; total TDF → TAF, n = 3) discontinued the study during the OL phase due to an AE. Throughout the entire study period, 21 cases of HCC were identified (1.8%; TAF8y, n = 12; total TDF → TAF, n = 9; p = 0.33; Figure S9), 10 of which occurred during the OL phase. Approximately 25% of patients experienced a laboratory abnormality of Grade 3 or higher (Table 3). The most common (≥2%) Grade 3 or 4 chemistry abnormalities were increases in serum amylase, fasting total cholesterol (TC), fasting low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and creatine kinase. No Grade 3 or 4 haematological laboratory abnormalities occurred at a frequency of ≥2%. Urine glucose and occult blood (all Grade 3) were the most common abnormalities on urinalysis.

Metabolic changes

Among patients receiving TAF, median (quartile [Q]1, Q3) increases in TC (6 [−17, 25] mg/dL), LDL cholesterol (16 [−5, 35] mg/dL) and triglycerides (9 [−15, 39] mg/dL), along with a decrease in high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (−6 [−14, 1] mg/dL), occurred at year 8 (Figure 2A–D). Among total TDF → TAF patients, decreases in fasting lipids occurred during DB TDF treatment; however, following the switch to TAF, median lipid values were found to increase, attaining levels similar to the TAF8y group, which then stabilised through year 8. Notably, the median change in the TC:HDL ratio, a marker of cardiovascular risk, was minimal (0.5 in all three groups) at year 8 (Figure 2E).

During the OL phase, 6/775 (0.8%) and 2/382 (0.5%) in the total TDF → TAF group had an AE of weight increase, none of which were reported as study drug related. Median increases in body weight of 1.1–2.0 kg were observed at year 8, with the largest increase seen in the TAF8y group (Figure 2F). To explore this further, shifts in body mass index (BMI) categories were assessed during the study period. At baseline, approximately two‐thirds of patients had a BMI categorised as either normal or underweight (Table 1). In the TAF8y group, after 8 years of treatment, 73% of patients who were underweight had shifted to a normal BMI, and none shifted to the overweight or obese categories. Of 333 patients treated solely with TAF who were in the normal BMI range at baseline and remained on study at year 8, 74 (22%) shifted to the overweight category and 2 (<1%) to the obese range, while 254 (76%) retained normal BMI status. Similar proportions (12% and 13%) of TAF‐treated patients with paired data who were overweight at baseline shifted either to an obese or normal BMI at year 8, respectively. Finally, of 44 TAF8y patients in the obese BMI range at baseline with results available, the vast majority (39 [89%]) remained in the same category at year 8 (Table S7). The results at year 8 were generally comparable for patients who switched from TDF to TAF at year 2 or year 3.

Renal parameters

In the TAF8y group, eGFRCG remained relatively stable during the study. At year 8, all treatment groups showed median decreases in eGFRCG, ranging from −4.9 to −5.4 mL/min. In both TDF → TAF groups, eGFRCG values decreased during DB treatment followed by improvement after switching to TAF (Figure 3A). At baseline, most patients with available results had chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage 1 (normal) or 2 (mild impairment) based on the entry requirement of eGFRCG ≥ 50 mL/min, and by year 8, the majority of patients treated solely with TAF remained at their baseline stage and/or had improved renal function (i.e. stage 2 to stage 1 shift). In the groups randomised to TDF that switched to TAF at year 2 or 3, similar trends were generally observed (Table S8).

Median percent increases in the ratios of beta 2‐microglobulin to creatinine (B2M:Cr), and retinol‐binding protein to creatinine (RBP:Cr), two markers of proximal tubulopathy, were observed during TDF treatment, which improved after switching to TAF (Figure 3B; Figure S10). Median percent increases in B2M:Cr among patients in the TAF8y group were small, and levels remained stable over 8 years. Comparable results were observed for RBP:Cr (Figure S10).

Bone mineral density

Small (<2%) mean percent decreases in hip and spine BMD were seen in the TAF8y group at year 8 (Figure 3C,D). Similarly, in the TAF8y group, serum markers of bone turnover, including C‐terminal telopeptide of type 1 collagen (CTX; bone resorption) and N‐terminal propeptide of type 1 procollagen (P1NP; bone formation), exhibited small median decreases of −2.7% and −9.03%, respectively, at year 8 (Figure 3E,F). During DB treatment, compared to the TAF group, larger mean percent decreases in hip and spine BMD and median percent increases in CTX and P1NP were observed with TDF; however, in the OL phase, switching to TAF resulted in increased BMD and diminished increases in CTX and P1NP.

DISCUSSION

Our results, derived from a large dataset over an extended period of time, demonstrate that high virologic and biochemical response rates, along with improvements in liver fibrosis by non‐invasive measures, occurred with TAF treatment of HBeAg‐negative and HBeAg‐positive patients with CHB. Further, no resistance was detected, and no new safety signals were identified. Together, our results derived from a diverse, global population confirm the long‐term favourable benefit/risk profile for TAF in treating CHB.

Treatment with TAF, or TDF followed by TAF, resulted in rapid and maintained viral suppression over 8 years. These findings extend previously reported results at year 5, 17 and compare favourably to findings reported for TDF treatment given for up to 10 years. 6 , 7 Notably, we observed steadily increasing rates of undetectable HBV DNA (<29 IU/mL with TND), with approximately two‐thirds of patients achieving this endpoint at year 8. The clinical importance of achievement of undetectable (vs. less than the lower limit of quantification) HBV DNA for patients with CHB in the absence of HBsAg loss requires additional study.

In both studies, higher biochemical responses were seen with TAF treatment compared with TDF through 2–3 years. 10 , 15 , 16 , 17 Of note, rates of ALT normalisation increased after patients switched from TDF to TAF such that by year 8, biochemical responses were similar across treatment groups when assessed by both central laboratory and AASLD criteria. The underlying mechanism(s) for different biochemical responses for these TFV prodrugs remains unclear. Increased ALT normalisation might be of relevance as it pertains to reduction in HCC risk; however, this requires further validation for TAF relative to other NAs, including TDF. 20 , 21 In a 5‐year interim analysis, we noted a lower incidence of HCC compared to predicted rates for untreated CHB using the REACH‐B tool, 20 and early (year 1) normalisation of ALT was an independent predictor for lack of HCC development. 21 When treatment was extended to 8 years, the low incidence (<2%) of HCC was maintained across the treatment cohort.

Long‐term viral suppression is associated with reduced disease progression and improved outcomes, including slowing and/or regression of fibrosis/cirrhosis. 4 , 5 While liver biopsy is considered the gold standard for assessing fibrosis, serial biopsies were not performed in these studies, thus precluding any histological assessments. Rather, we noted improvement in fibrosis status when assessed centrally by non‐invasive means (FibroTest). In our analysis, approximately 10% of patients had compensated cirrhosis at baseline, and a substantial proportion of these patients shifted to a lower stage of fibrosis based on FibroTest findings, while only a minority in the lower fibrosis categories (no/mild or moderate/severe) had worsened fibrosis.

Across groups, the rates of HBeAg loss and seroconversion (in patients who were HBeAg positive at baseline) increased progressively over the 8‐year study period. Our results, derived from a much larger dataset, are generally comparable to long‐term results previously reported for TDF 6 , 7 and entecavir (ETV), 8 and demonstrate that approximately one‐third of patients achieve HBeAg seroconversion with an extended duration of TFV‐based therapy. Conversely, rates of HBsAg loss and seroconversion were low at 2%–3%, and quantitative HBsAg titres declined minimally over 8 years. Similar low rates of HBsAg loss have been reported with long‐term TDF and ETV, 6 , 7 , 8 which only serves to further confirm the need for novel treatments that enhance rates of HBV functional cure. 22 However, the high viral suppression, favourable safety and lack of resistance development demonstrated by TAF support its use as an ideal NA to consider when designing clinical trials evaluating new approaches towards HBV cure.

Consistent with previous findings, 17 and in a comparable manner to data reported with long‐term TDF treatment, 6 , 7 we observed no resistance to TAF through 8 years, with the proportions that qualified for viral sequencing showing progressive decreases over time, particularly in cases due to persistent viraemia. This finding is in contrast to the rising rates of resistance reported with continued use of second‐line NAs (e.g. lamivudine, adefovir dipivoxil, telbivudine) even when given for shorter periods and with long‐term ETV when used in lamivudine‐refractory patients. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26

In the present analysis, no new safety signals were identified for TAF, and overall, few patients discontinued treatment due to an AE. With TAF treatment, small median changes (±6 mg/dL) were observed in fasting TC and in HDL cholesterol, whereas a slightly greater median increase (16 mg/dL) was observed in LDL cholesterol at year 8. Conversely, with TDF treatment, decreases in all fasting lipid parameters (including HDL) were seen, consistent with its known lipid‐lowering effect. 27 These reductions occurred early and decreased following the switch to TAF. Notably, the ratio of TC to HDL cholesterol, a marker of cardiovascular risk, increased only minimally over 8 years in all groups (0.5). These observations are consistent with our recent work, which evaluated 10‐year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk scores for a subset of patients meeting the American College of Cardiology criteria for risk assessment. 28 The DB phase of the studies found that ASCVD risk scores were similarly low in patients with CHB treated with TAF or TDF. 29 This is further supported by recent data from a real‐world study in patients with CHB, which used propensity score matching to demonstrate that the long‐term cardiovascular risk in TAF‐treated patients with CHB is comparable to that of TDF‐treated patients, despite different changes in lipid profiles. 30 Collectively, these observations support neutral long‐term cardiovascular effects for both TDF and TAF; however, as with any treatment, patients' risks and potential benefits must be assessed.

As previously reported at year 5 (week 240), small median increases in body weight were observed at year 8 in all treatment groups; importantly, further changes in body weight relative to year 5 results were minimal. Data showed that, in comparison with TDF, body weight increases and shifts in BMI over 8 years were greater with TAF. The issue of changes in body weight with TDF and TAF has been evaluated in a more comprehensive manner in both individuals with HIV infection and healthy individuals starting pre‐exposure prophylaxis, and a recent report that evaluated available clinical trial data as well as expert panel input concluded that TAF and TDF have weight neutral and weight loss properties, respectively. 31

In earlier analyses, TAF demonstrated improved renal safety compared to TDF, and our present results confirm that renal function changes remained stable over the course of an 8‐year period of TAF treatment. In the TAF group, the median decline in eGFRCG (~5 mL/min/1.73 m2) that we observed at year 8 is comparable to the rate of decline that would be expected with the normal ageing process (~8 mL/min/1.73 m2 per decade), although there is considerable variability in this figure. 32 Importantly, the larger declines in eGFRCG we observed with TDF treatment were reversible when switching to TAF after either 2 or 3 years, which is also supported by substantial declines in quantitative markers of tubular proteinuria following the change in therapy from TDF to TAF. When renal function was assessed by CKD stage changes, the majority were stage 1 or 2 at baseline, which generally remained stable at year 8 in the TAF group.

Mean percent declines in hip and spine BMD, which were prespecified safety endpoints at years 1 (48 weeks) and 2 (96 weeks), showed TAF to have a superior bone safety profile compared to TDF. 10 , 15 , 16 Importantly, after 8 years, mean percent declines in BMD remained small in patients randomised to TAF—consistent with what might be predicted due to ageing. 33 In our diverse study population, TDF‐associated decreases in BMD appeared to be reversible following the switch to OL TAF, which is also supported by reductions in serum markers of bone turnover.

Although all patients were eligible to receive OL treatment with TAF following the DB phase, an important limitation of our analysis is that the timing of the rollover was not homogeneous, having been impacted by the timing of implementation of an amendment to the protocol extending DB treatment. As such, the ability to perform between‐group statistical comparisons for efficacy responses was compromised, albeit response rates for viral suppression at year 8 were numerically similar. Enrolment of elderly patients and of ethnicities other than Asian and White was minimal; thus, the direct applicability of our results to other race/ethnicity groups, particularly Black people with CHB, is impacted. While most patients benefited from the extension of TAF treatment to 8 years, approximately 25% discontinued early for reasons other than lack of treatment efficacy, development of resistance or safety/tolerability concerns. As in a previous long‐term safety and efficacy study with TDF, 6 , 7 the main reasons for study discontinuation included patient loss to follow‐up and withdrawal of consent. These factors reflect the inherent difficulties in maintaining patient retention in long‐term trials. Additionally, retention was further impacted by the trials' >2‐year overlap with the COVID‐19 pandemic. In conclusion, patients with CHB treated with TAF, or with TDF followed by TAF, demonstrated high rates of efficacy and favourable safety through 8 years, and no resistance development was observed. These findings provide further support for the inclusion of TAF as a first‐line treatment for CHB.
