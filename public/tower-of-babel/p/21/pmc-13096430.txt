High-performance graphene oxide desalination membranes enabled by size-sieving, ion exclusion, and cation recognition mechanisms
Authors: Elahe Bashiri, Mehrdad Manteghian, Alireza Sharif, Arezou Jafari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphene oxide (GO)-based membranes are promising for sustainable water purification due to their sub-nanometer interlayer channels, which provide ultrafast water transport and high salt rejection. However, they generally exhibit limited structural stability in aqueous media, leading to increased interlayer spacing and reduced salt rejection. Here, the polysaccharide chitosan (CH) is introduced into the GO galleries through a one-pot pressure-assisted self-assembly method to stabilize the GO interlayer spacing at a specific distance. Furthermore, CH, as a positively charged polysaccharide, confers the membrane with local positive charges. These, in combination with the inherent negative charges on the GO surface, result in GO-based membranes possessing simultaneous size-sieving, cation recognition, and anion exclusion capability. The ratio of CH: GO was found as a key parameter, controlling the interlayer spacing, nanochannel total charge density, and cation charge density along the channel. The results showed that the membrane containing 0.02 mg/cm2 GO and 0.15 mg/cm2 CH exhibited superior rejection for MgSO4, NaCl, and MgCl2, alongside the highest water permeability due to the optimal interplay of size sieving, ion exclusion, and cation recognition mechanisms. The strong interactions between GO layers and CH chains via hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions significantly improved the structural stability of the membranes in various harsh environments. This work provides a new insight for designing sustainable GO-based membranes with both high ionic separation performance and water permeability through simultaneously utilizing size-sieving, ion exclusion, and cation recognition mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41327-3.

Introduction

Continuous population growth and excessive human activities have led to an increase in the demand for fresh water. Seawater desalination through membrane technology has been hailed in the last few decades as a practical approach to meet this urgent need. Based on the structure of membranes and separation mechanisms, the membrane desalination process is classified as reverse osmosis (RO), forward osmosis (FO), membrane distillation (MD), electrodialysis, and nanofiltration (NF). Despite the advantages of these processes, such as low energy consumption and environmental friendliness, these membrane processes still suffer from fouling, low resistance to chlorine, etc.

In the last decade, graphene-based materials, especially graphene oxide (GO), have attracted much attention for membrane technology, due to their two-dimensional structure with one atom thickness, inherent hydrophilic nature, and desirable physico-chemical properties1–4. These unique features enable GO sheets to create sub-nanometer interlayer channels in the membrane structure, which provide rapid water transport and separation of ionic species through a steric mechanism5. However, the uncontrolled swelling of GO sheets in aqueous environments, which enlarges the interlayer spacing (d-spacing), can compromise the ionic selectivity and long-term stability under pressure-driven operation. This swelling can lead to reduced selectivity and stability, which are crucial factors in the performance of membrane technology.

Numerous approaches have been performed to control the interlayer spacing of GO membranes and increase their swelling resistance in aqueous solutions, including chemical crosslinking6,7, reduction of GO sheets8, intercalation of metal cations or nanoparticles9–11, and chemical modification of GO sheets12. Among these, the intercalation of polymeric chains between GO sheets is widely accepted as an effective strategy for precisely controlling interlayer spacing of GO sheets due to its simplicity, low cost, and high potential for enhancing water permeability13. The interaction of oxygen-containing functional groups of GO with the polymer chains allows for the uniform distribution of the polymer chains between GO layers. Furthermore, using polymers with various functional groups provides the ionic or covalent crosslinking of GO sheets, which can simultaneously enhance stability and adjust the interlayer spacing of GO membranes. Lou et al.14 employed polyethyleneimine (PEI) / poly (acrylic acid) (PAA) intercalation to suppress swelling and enhance dye rejection; Zhu et al.15 crosslinked polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) /PAA to narrow d-spacing and improve flux; and Shen et al.16 introduced calcium alginate to regulate channels via ionic coordination.

Although intercalator agents have improved the structural stability of GO membranes by controlling interlayer spacing, enhancing ionic separation capabilities through this method primarily relies on a size-sieving mechanism.​ Therefore, achieving high ion rejection requires very narrow water transport nanochannels. However, these narrow nanochannels inherently restrict the passage of water molecules, thereby reducing membrane water flux.

It has been reported that the presence of ionic functional groups, such as carboxylic acid, within GO sheets enables ion separation not only through size exclusion but also via ion exclusion mechanisms17. By manipulating both size-sieving and ion exclusion through controlled d-spacing and nanochannel charge density, wider nanochannels with high ion rejection and enhanced water permeability can be created. For example, Guan et al.18 improved salt rejection and enhanced water permeability by increasing nanochannel charge density through grafting anionic oxidized polyphenol onto GO sheets. However, this method can involve complex synthesis and may compromise structural stability due to insufficient crosslinking.

​​Recently, Yang et al.19 demonstrated that incorporating polycations as intercalators in GO membranes introduces positive functional groups along the nanochannels, which can enhance salt rejection and increase water permeation through the cation recognition effect. This effect arises from electrostatic repulsive interactions between the positive functional groups of the intercalator and the cationic species passing through the nanochannels.

Chitosan (CH), as a biocompatible polycation, has emerged as a promising candidate for improving the separation performance of water treatment membranes, owing to its high hydrophilicity, low toxicity, and cost-effectiveness20–22. The presence of amine and hydroxy functional groups in the CH structure allows simultaneous control of the interlayer spacing and increased structural stability of GO membranes by creating ionic cross-linking between GO sheets within the membrane23,24. Liu et al.25 incorporated varying molecular weights and dosages of CH within GO membranes through layer by layer technique to improve rejection of natural organic matter (NOM). Their findings indicated a direct correlation between the molecular weight of CH and the d-spacing of the GO membranes, where higher molecular weights led to increased d-spacing. Conversely, an increased dosage of CH resulted in a decrease in d-spacing, specifically from 1.03 to 1.12 nm to 0.88–0.91 nm as CH loading increased from 0.2 mg to 0.5 mg. This modification significantly enhanced the rejection capabilities of the GO membranes for bovine serum albumin (BSA), sodium alginate (SA), and humic acid (HA), achieving over 95% rejection.​ This improved performance was attributed to the formation of narrower nanochannels between the GO sheets due to the presence of intercalated CH. However, these narrow nanochannels limited the enhancement of the water flux of the membranes. Moreover, Lin et al.26 developed GO-based membranes by grafting CH chains onto GO sheets to enhance the rejection of negatively charged dyes. The intercalation of CH enhanced water permeability (91 L.m− 2.h− 1), stability, and anti-fouling properties of the membranes. However, the corresponding improvement in dye rejection was moderate, remaining below 90%. Moreover, the membranes exhibited low separation performance for oppositely charged dye molecules. This limited enhancement was attributed to the wide nanochannels of the membranes, which had a d-spacing of 1.2–2 nm.​ Although the previous research focused on improving the separation performance of GO membranes through controlling d-spacing by CH intercalation for various water treatment applications, these studies did not specifically address desalination processes.​ Additionally, the improvement of separation performance of the membranes only from the perspective of controlling d-spacing via CH polymer limits the full potential for simultaneously enhancing both ion rejection and water permeability in such critical applications.

Beyond adjusting the d-spacing, intercalated positively charged CH allows for fine tuning of the total charge density of the nanochannels and controls the ionic rejection of GO membranes through the cation recognition effect19. Therefore, the intercalation of cationic CH polymer in GO membranes could control the ionic separation capabilities through a combination of size-sieving, Donnan exclusion (ion exclusion), and cation recognition effects27. This multi-faceted approach holds promise for achieving superior ionic separation and water permeability in GO membranes for desalination processes. ​​Nevertheless, the precise impact of positively charged CH in subnanometer channels on the behavior of ionic species and, consequently, on the ionic separation performance of GO membranes, remains largely unexplored. This highlights an urgent need for a deeper understanding of how internal charges within nanochannels affect water molecules and salt ions, which is crucial for developing highly selective GO-based membranes that prioritize water transport over ionic species.

To this aim, in this study, we developed an advanced GO-based membrane by simply incorporating biodegradable CH polymer into the membrane via a one-pot pressure-assisted self-assembly (PASA) method. We investigated the effect of CH intercalation on ionic separation performance by simultaneously controlling size-sieving, ion exclusion, and cationic recognition effects, a novel approach previously unreported for GO-based membranes.​ We optimized key parameters such as d-spacing, nanochannel total charge density, and cation charge density along the channels to achieve a membrane with both high water permeability and enhanced ionic rejection. This optimization was achieved by carefully adjusting the CH and GO content in the membrane’s skin layer through comprehensive structural, physicochemical, and transport characterizations. The separation mechanisms of the GO-based membrane were elucidated through exploring the correlation between d-spacing/ total charge density/ cation charge density along nanochannels and the behavior of the membranes. Furthermore, we evaluated the long-term structural stability of the membranes under harsh conditions, including strongly acidic and alkaline environments, and after withstanding 3 h of ultrasonic treatment. Finally, to truly validate industrial relevance beyond conventional studies, the membrane’s performance was examined with synthesized salt solutions and crucially through the desalination of real RO feedwater from the Golgohar Mining & Industrial Complex.

Experimental

Materials

Low molecular weight Chitosan (CH, Degree of deacetylation of 85%) and potassium permanganate (KMnO4) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Graphite powder, acetic acid, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 95 wt%), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 37 wt%), magnesium dichloride (MgCl2), magnesium sulfate (MgSO4), sodium chloride (NaCl), pyridine, phenol, 2-naphtol and hydrochloric acid were purchased from Merck. Blue3 (296.3 g/mol) and Yellow ME4G dye (818.13 g/mol), denoted by Alvan Co., Ltd., Iran. Commercial polyether sulfone (PES) microfiltration membrane as substrate membrane with a 30 kDa molecular weight cut-off was offered by Sepanta Polymer Co., Ltd., Iran.

Synthesis of GO

Graphene oxide (GO) was prepared by the modified Hummers’ method28. Briefly, 1 g of graphite powder was dissolved in 50 mL of H2SO4, and 6 g of KMnO4 was gradually added to the solution while the mixture was in an ice bath and stirred for 48 h at room temperature. Next, the mixture was diluted with 150 ml of distilled water (DI), and 50 ml of hydrogen peroxide solution (25 ml of DI + 25 ml of 37 wt% H2O2) was added to the mixture to react with residual KMnO4 in the mixture. Then, the mixture was centrifuged, and the resulting precipitate was washed 3 times with 10 wt% HCl solution and 4 times with DI. The final precipitate from the washing process (graphite oxide) was dissolved in distilled water and sonicated for 30 min. In order to remove non-exfoliated graphite oxide particles from the GO dispersion, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. Finally, the concentration of GO nanoparticles in the solution was determined using a gravimetric method, and the resulting dispersion was stored for membrane preparation.

Preparation of membrane

GO-based skin layer membranes were prepared through a PASA method using dead-end filtration of GO-based dispersions (Fig. 1). To prepare GO/CH membranes, a certain amount of CH solution (0.4 mg/mL) was initially prepared in 2 wt% acetic acid. Then, a certain amount of GO solution (4 mg/mL) was added dropwise to the CH solution under magnetic stirring at room temperature, and the final volume of the mixture was adjusted to 30 mL by adding DI. Subsequently, the final solution was filtered through a PES microfiltration membrane in a dead-end cell under 1 bar for 24 h. Finally, the prepared membrane was air-dried and stored for subsequent characterization. The same method was used to prepare the pristine GO membranes by filtration of the GO solution. The obtained membranes were labeled as GOx/CHy or GOx, where x and y represent the GO and CH content in the skin layer membrane (mg/cm2), respectively.

Characterization

The structure of GO nanoparticles was analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Perkin Elmer spectrum, USA) in the wave number range of 400–4000 cm− 1. Additionally, attenuated total reflectance–Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to investigate the membrane structure. The morphology of GO sheets was observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM 1200EX, Japan). The interlayer spacing of both GO nanoparticles and the GO sheets in the membranes was investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD, X’Pert MPD model, Philips, Holland) equipped with Cu-Kα radiation operated at 40 kV, 30 mA. This calculated d-spacing represents the center-to-center distance between adjacent GO layers, which includes the thickness of a single GO nanosheet and the actual free space (interlayer gap) between them. For further investigation, the actual free interlayer spacing (available space for transport) was calculated by subtracting the estimated thickness of a single GO sheet (0.7 nm)29 from the d-spacing obtained from XRD results.

The morphology of the membrane structure was analyzed using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, TESCAN MIRA3, Czech Republic). Atomic force microscopy (AFM, Veeco Auto Probe CP Research, USA) was used to observe the surface topography of the membranes and evaluate their surface roughness. The surface roughness was quantified using the parameters Rq, Rz, and Ra, representing root mean square roughness, mean roughness depth, and average roughness, respectively. The surface hydrophilicity of the membranes was investigated by determining the water contact angle (WCA, CAG-20 SE, Jikan) of the membrane surface. The Zeta potential of the membranes at natural pH (pH: 7) was measured using an electrokinetic analyzer (Anton Paar SurPASS, Austria).

Results and discussion

Characterization of the GO sheets

Figure S3 shows the FTIR spectra of graphite and GO sheets. The spectrum of graphite has a characteristic peak at 1559 cm-1, which can be attributed to the vibrations of the C = C bond in its structure. Also, the peak appearing in the range of 2900–3400 cm-1 is related to the stretching vibration of the O-H bond from the water molecule adsorbed to graphite24. In the GO spectrum, the peak observed at 1724 cm-1 is related to the C = O stretching vibration of carboxyl groups. In addition, the peaks appearing at 1585 cm-1 and 1374 cm-1 can be attributed to the stretching vibration of C = C and the stretching vibration of C-O-H, respectively. Furthermore, the two peaks observed at 1223 cm-1 and 1000 cm-1 correspond to the C-O stretching vibration of the alkoxy and epoxy groups on the GO surface, respectively6. The results of the FTIR spectrum indicate the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups in the structure of GO sheets. Moreover, the XRD results of GO nanoparticles revealed a prominent reflection peak at 2θ = 8.65◦, with an increased d-spacing of 1.02 nm, compared to the original graphite peak (2θ = 26.6◦, d-spacing = 0.33 nm) (Figure S4). This shift towards a lower angle and larger interlayer spacing confirms the effective exfoliation of the GO sheets. The increased d-spacing is attributed to the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface of GO sheets during the oxidation process. Furthermore, a secondary diffraction peak was observed at 2θ = 17.80◦, corresponding to a spacing of 0.49 nm, which implies the presence of short-range order within the stacked GO layers19. As can be seen from Figure S5, the TEM image shows a monolayer of GO sheets, which indicates a high degree of exfoliation of GO layers.

Physico-chemical properties of the membrane

Figure 2 indicates ATR-FTIR spectra of the PES substrate, crude CH, GO, and GO/CH membranes. The PES substrate exhibited some characteristic peaks at 1578 cm-1 and 1485 cm-1, attributed to the C = C stretching vibration of the benzene ring. Furthermore, the peaks appearing at 848 cm-1, 1110 cm-1, and 1152 cm-1 correspond to the structure of the sulfone group. Moreover, the peak of aromatic ether bands was also observed at 1246 cm-127. All of these characteristic peaks also appeared in the spectra of the membranes. The GO membrane shows a peak at 1616 cm-1, related to the C = C of the aromatic ring6. In the spectrum of the CH/GO membrane, two peaks are observed at 1580 cm-1 and 1655 cm-1, attributed to the stretching vibration of C = O of amid I and amid II, respectively. These peaks shifted to lower wavenumbers compared to the crude CH (1596 cm-1 and 1660 cm-1, which indicates the creation of hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between the amine group of CH and oxygen-containing functional groups of GO sheets. These interactions facilitate intercalation and uniform structure of CH in the GO-based skin layer, which can improve the stability of the membranes during the filtration process.

Figure 3(a) shows the XRD patterns of pristine GO membranes. The GO0.02 membrane exhibits a reflection peak at 10◦ and d-spacings of 0.88 nm, consistent with previously reported values32. With increasing GO content in the membrane (GO0.04 and GO0.05), the peak shifts to 9.5◦ and 9.3◦, respectively, corresponding to interlayer spacing of 0.92 nm and 0.94 nm. This increased d-spacing can be attributed to the intensified decrease in the filtration rate of the high-concentration GO solution over time. This behavior leads to a wider interlayer spacing of the sheets farther from the substrate, while those closer to the substrate have a denser structure. Therefore, the membranes with higher GO content create wider water transport nanochannels. Compared with the pristine GO membranes, the XRD patterns of GO/CH membranes show that in the presence of CH, the reflection peak shifts toward lower angles, indicating the ordered structure of GO/CH membranes with higher interlayer spacing (Fig. 3(b)). The presence of hydroxyl groups and positively charged amino groups in the chitosan structure results in the electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces between CH chains and oxygen-containing functional groups of GO sheets. These interactions facilitate the intercalation of CH chains into the GO laminates, leading to an increase in the d-spacing33. Furthermore, as shown in Figure. 3(c), as the weight ratio of CH to GO in the membrane increases from 2.5 to 10, the d-spacing value increases from 0.94 nm to 1.07 nm, corresponding to actual free interlayer spacing of 0.24 nm and 0.37 nm, respectively. This behavior indicates that more CH chains intercalate with increasing CH concentration in the membrane, leading to a larger d-spacing of GO sheets. The swelling resistance of the membranes in aqueous solutions was evaluated by comparing the interlayer spacing of membranes at dry and wet states. All pristine GO membranes exhibit an increased d-spacing at wet conditions (Fig. 3(a)). This behavior is attributed to the high hydrophilicity of GO sheets and their significant hydration when exposed to aqueous solutions. Furthermore, as the GO loading within the membrane increases, the observed expansion of the interlayer spacing in the wet state tends to decrease. This phenomenon may result from a less ordered alignment of GO sheets at higher loadings. Such a reduction in alignment could lead to fewer accessible oxygen-containing functional groups on the GO sheets for interaction with water molecules, consequently reducing the overall hydration and expansion in wet conditions. Compared to the pristine GO membranes, GO/CH membranes show insignificant changes in d-spacing at wet states. Although the high hydrophilicity of cationic CH would suggest an increase in interlayer spacing, this is not observed. This stability is attributed to the formation of an ionic crosslinking between GO sheets through electrostatic interactions between the positively charged amino groups of CH and the negatively charged carboxylic acid groups on the GO sheets.

Morphology of the membrane

The morphology of the skin layer membranes was investigated using surface and cross-sectional FESEM images of the membranes. Figure 4 and Figure S6 show a dense and wrinkled skin layer formed by GO sheets on the substrate (GO0.15). During the PASA process, the interaction between water molecules and oxygen-containing functional groups on the GO sheets and water passage through nanochannels induces drag forces on the flexible sheets. This phenomenon leads to the creation of wrinkles along nanochannels29. After the intercalation of CH chains between sheets, the surface of the membrane turns into a soft and uniform surface. Moreover, the cross-sectional image of GO/CH membranes indicates an ordered structure with an increased thickness from 0.32 μm for GO0.02 to 0.77 μm and 0.95 μm for GO0.02/CH0.1 and GO0.02/CH0.17, respectively (Fig. 5). These observations reveal the uniform intercalation of the CH chains between all GO sheets in the skin layer membrane, due to the electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces between CH chains and functional groups of GO sheets29. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 5, compared to the GO/CH membranes with different GO loading, changing the CH content in the membrane leads to more significant changes in the membrane thickness. This behavior indicates that the thickness of the skin layer is mainly controlled by the amount of CH.

Figure 6 indicates AFM images of the GO and GO/CH membranes containing different GO and CH content. Surface topography of the GO membranes shows that wrinkles of the sheets in the skin layer increase with increasing GO loading. Moreover, as detailed in Table 1, the surface roughness value follows the order of GO0.02 < GO0.04 < GO0.05. This increased roughness of the membrane with GO loading can be attributed to the random orientation of GO sheets far away from the substrate due to the reduction of the filtration rate as more GO sheets precipitate on the substrate30. These observations are consistent with the XRD results obtained from the membranes. Compared to the GO membranes, the AFM results of the GO/CH membranes show that in the presence of CH in the skin layer, the wrinkles of the sheets disappear due to the coverage of the GO sheets by the CH polymer. Furthermore, CH chains can act as an electrostatic crosslinking agent between GO sheets, which prevents further flexibility of GO sheets. Therefore, the formation of wrinkles during the PASA process is limited. The comparison of the AFM results of GO0.01/CH0.1 and GO0.02/CH0.1 reveals that the surface roughness increases with increasing GO loading at a constant CH content. In addition, at a fixed GO loading, increasing the CH content results in a smoother surface (Table 1). This behavior can be attributed to the different degrees of CH intercalation with a changing CH/GO ratio. At a low CH/GO ratio, the CH chains are discontinuously placed between the sheets, leading to higher surface roughness. However, as the ratio increases, a continuous intercalation of CH is formed, leading to a decrease in the overall surface roughness.

Hydrophilicity and zeta potential of the membrane surface

The hydrophilicity of the membranes was analyzed by measuring the static water contact angle (WCA) on the membrane surface. The PES substrate exhibited a contact angle of 88◦ (Fig. 7(a)). Following the self-assembly of 0.02 mg/cm2 GO sheets on the PES, the water contact angle decreased to 70°. This reduction is attributed to the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups on the GO sheets, enhancing the hydrophilicity of the membrane. However, increasing the GO sheet loading in the skin layer to 0.05 mg/cm2 resulted in an increased WCA of 82◦. This increase may be due to the random orientation of GO sheets at higher concentrations, which reduces the availability of their functional groups for interaction with water molecules34. Compared to the GO membranes, the incorporation of CH into the skin layer containing a certain amount of GO led to a decrease in the WCA of the membrane. It could be related to the presence of hydroxyl and amine functional groups in the structure of CH chains, causing more surface hydrophilicity of the membrane. Moreover, for hydrophilic surfaces, increasing surface roughness leads to a greater contact area for interaction with water, which results in the decreasing WCA of the membrane34. Therefore, the increased surface roughness of the membrane in the presence of CH can also intensify the hydrophilicity of the membranes (Table 1). Furthermore, for membranes with a fixed CH content (0.1 mg/cm2), the WCA exhibits a minimum trend with increasing GO loading. Based on the surface roughness results (Table 1), increasing GO content from 0.01 mg/cm2 to 0.02 mg/cm2 leads to higher roughness, thereby enhancing membrane hydrophilicity. However, at higher GO loadings, the random orientation of GO sheets reduces the availability of oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface. Additionally, the more hydrophilic CH polymer intercalates between the GO sheets, limiting their contact with water on the surface. Consequently, GO/CH membranes with higher GO loading show a decreased surface hydrophilicity.

In addition, Fig. 7(a) indicates that at a certain GO loading, increasing CH content results in a maximum WCA. It seems that decreasing surface roughness with increasing CH content from 0.1 mg/cm2 to 0.17 mg/cm2 leads to an increase in WCA of the membrane. However, for higher CH content, the GO membrane is uniformly coated with hydrophilic CH, resulting in a decreased WCA. The results of WCA indicate that the amount of GO and CH in the skin layer controls the surface hydrophilicity of the membranes through both manipulating surface roughness and the order degree of the GO sheets in the membrane.

The surface zeta potential of the membranes as an effective parameter in the ionic rejection performance of the membranes was evaluated at a neutral pH value. Figure 7(b) shows that all the membranes possess a negative value of zeta potential. PES substrate shows a zeta potential of -10.78 mV, due to the presence of sulfone and ether groups in its structure35. With the loading of 0.02 mg/cm2 of GO on the PES, the absolute value of zeta potential increased to 16.02 mV. Moreover, it indicates an increase in the absolute value of zeta potential with increasing GO loading in the membrane. This can be related to the presence of carboxylic acid groups with a negative charge in the structure of GO sheets. Furthermore, the addition of CH polymer to the GO skin layer membranes causes a decrease in the absolute value of surface zeta potential. The decreased absolute value of zeta potential can be attributed to the positively amine functional groups in CH chains, which electrostatically interact with the negative carboxylic acid groups of GO sheets. This phenomenon results in the reduction of the surface zeta potential of the membranes. However, the absolute value of the zeta potential of GO/CH membranes, at fixed CH content (0.1 mg/cm2), increases from 12.1 mV to 17.23 mV with increasing GO loading from 0.01 mg/cm2 to 0.05 mg/cm2. This could be ascribed to the increasing negative charge functional groups of GO sheets with increasing GO loading in the membrane. Moreover, at a certain amount of GO, increasing CH content in the GO/CH membrane reduces the absolute value of the zeta potential, which can be due to increasing positive charge amine functional groups of CH polymer in the membrane.

Membrane performance

Pure water flux

PWF was investigated as a critical parameter affecting the performance of the membranes. As shown in Fig. 8(a), for the pristine GO membranes, PWF decreases with increasing GO loading. According to the XRD results, increasing GO in the membrane leads to increasing d-spacing and wider nanochannels for the transport of water molecules through the membrane. Thus, it is expected that as the GO increases, the water flux will also increase. The observed inconsistency between the d-spacing results and PWF can be due to the reduction in the surface hydrophilicity of the membranes (Fig. 7(a)), which can decrease the water flux despite the structural enhancements provided by the increased d-spacing. For further analysis, the tortuosity (τ) of the water transport pathway was investigated as an effective parameter in determining water permeability. According to the Nielsen model, the tortuosity for the GO-based skin layer membranes depends on the d-spacing and can be calculated as follows36:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:=1+\frac{L}{2\:(d+W)}$$\end{document}

where L is the lateral dimension of the GO sheets, W is the thickness of the GO sheets, and d is the d-spacing of the GO sheets. As can be seen from Fig. 8(a), with increasing GO content, τ of the pristine GO membranes decreases. This decrease in τ is attributed to the increase in interlayer spacing caused by the increase in GO content in the membrane (Fig. 3(a)). It is accepted that the less tortuous water transport path may increase the PWF of the membrane. Accordingly, the analysis of the effects of d-spacing, tortuosity, and surface hydrophilicity on PWF reveals that for pristine GO membranes, surface hydrophilicity has a greater contribution to controlling membrane water flux. Compared to pristine GO membranes, GO/CH membranes show improved PWF with the addition of CH in the skin layer of the membrane. This could be due to the creation of wider water transport nanochannels in the presence of CH chains between GO sheets. In addition, the intercalation of CH chains, due to the presence of hydroxyl and amine functional groups in their structure, increases the hydrophilicity of nanochannels, which can improve the mobility of water molecules through nanochannels. Furthermore, the CH intercalation increases the surface porosity of the membrane through increasing d-spacing, which leads to easier permeation of water molecules37. As shown in Fig. 8(a), increasing GO loading in GO/CH membranes consisting of a certain CH content leads to a decrease in the PWF from 25.4 to 11.8 L.m[-2.h[-1. Based on the XRD results of GO/CH membranes, this decrease in PWF can be attributed to the decrease in d-spacing and increase in tortuosity of GO/CH membranes with GO loading. Further analysis indicates that compared to GO0.01/CH0.1 and GO0.02/CH0.1, GO/CH membranes containing high GO loading (0.04 mg/cm2 and 0.05 mg/cm2) exhibit a significant reduction in PWF. This decreasing PWF may be attributed to the reduced surface hydrophilicity of the GO/CH membranes with GO content, which is aligned with high τ and low d-spacing, leading to a drastic reduction in PWF. Figure 8(b) shows a maximum behavior in PWF with increasing CH content in the GO/CH membranes containing 0.02 mg/cm2 of GO. Furthermore, increasing CH content decreases the τ of the membrane due to the increase in the d-spacing of GO sheets (Fig. 3(c)). The initial increase in PWF with CH content could be related to the creation of wider nanochannels and reduction of the pathway tortuosity. However, a further increase in CH in the skin layer increases the mass transfer resistance of the membrane and consequently decreases the PWF of GO/CH membranes from 27.2 to 17.6 L·m-2.h-1(Fig. 5). This mass transfer resistance (R, s/cm) for GO-based membranes can be defined as follows:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:R=\frac{T}{{D}_{w}}$$\end{document}

where Dw is the diffusion coefficient of water molecules through the GO skin layer membrane, and T is the thickness of the membrane (Fig. 5). Dw depends on the interlayer spacing between GO nanosheets. So, an increase in interlayer spacing enhances the diffusion of water molecules between GO sheets. By increasing the CH content from 0.1 mg/cm2 to 0.15 mg/cm2, the diffusion of water molecules improves and consequently the mass transfer resistance decreases. However, further increase in CH content leads to a higher thickness of the membrane. On the other hand, Dw decreases with more intercalation of CH, due to the formation of a dense CH structure between GO sheets. This phenomenon significantly increases mass transfer resistance, thereby reducing the PWF.

Salt and dye separation performance

The performance of the membranes in water desalination was investigated by the filtration of monovalent ions (NaCl) and divalent salts (MgCl2, MgSO4). As shown in the figures. 9(a-c), for all membranes, the salt rejection follows the order MgSO4 > NaCl > MgCl2. The hydrated radius of Mg2+, Na+, SO42-, and Cl- are reported to be approximately 0.428 nm, 0.178 nm, 0.3 nm, and 0.195 nm, respectively38. Based on the size-sieving mechanism, NaCl rejection is predicted to be lower than the others. This discrepancy could be due to ion-exclusion effects of charged groups on the wall of nanochannels on the co-ions. Given the negative surface zeta potential of all membranes (Fig. 7(b)), the charged groups of channels effectively reject negatively charged ions via electrostatic repulsion interactions (Fig. 10(a)). To quantitatively investigate the impact of channel charged groups on ion separation, the effective channel total charge density (Xd, mEq/L) was calculated using zeta potential results as39:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{d} = A + B\xi \quad {\mathrm{where}}\;A = - 0.17e^{{0.16C_{0} }} \;{\mathrm{and}}\;B = 2.51 + 0.17C_{0}$$\end{document}

where C0 and ξ are the salt concentration in the feed (g/L) and zeta potential (mV), respectively. As depicted in Fig. 10(b), the charge density of nanochannels for both GO and GO/CH0.1 membranes increases with increasing GO loading. This behavior can be attributed to the rise in carboxylic acid functional groups, as negatively charged sites within the channels, with GO loading. Based on the Donnan exclusion theory, the rejection of co-ionic species by the charged groups in nanochannels results in the rejection of counter-ionic species to maintain electroneutrality across the membrane40. This theory suggests that salts with a high valence ratio (Z-/Z+) of co-ions (SO42-, Cl-) to counter-ions (Mg2+, Na+) exhibit a higher rejection rate. The calculated Z-/Z+ values for MgCl2, NaCl, and MgSO4 are 0.5, 1, and 1, respectively41. This would theoretically indicate a rejection order of MgSO4 ≈ NaCl > MgCl2. However, this theoretical order also shows a slight inconsistency with the experimental results. This discrepancy is likely related to the smaller radius of the hydrated anions and cations of NaCl compared to MgSO4, which results in lower rejection of NaCl than MgSO438. As observed from the figures. 9(a-c), pristine GO membranes exhibit improved salt rejection for all types of salts as the GO loading increases. This behavior appears contrary to the expected ionic separation through the size-sieving mechanism, especially considering the high d-spacing of membranes with high GO loading (Fig. 10(b)). This behavior is attributed to the more negative charge density of the membranes with increasing GO content (Fig. 10(b)). A higher channel total charge density intensifies the electrostatic repulsive forces against co-ions in the feed solution, which enhances salt rejection. Additionally, GO/CH0.1 membranes indicate improved salt rejection as the GO content in the membrane increases. This enhancement is likely due to a reduction in d-spacing and an increase in the channel-charge density with GO loading. Moreover, the increase in GO loading leads to a thicker membrane (Fig. 5) and higher tortuosity (Fig. 8), which can enhance salt rejection. This behavior shows that with increasing GO loading, both size-sieving and ion exclusion mechanisms govern the separation efficiency of GO/CH membranes. A comparison of the pristine GO and GO/CH membranes containing the same amount of GO reveals that in the presence of 0.1 mg/cm2 of CH in the structure of the membranes (GO/CH), salt rejection decreases. This reduction in the rejection can be attributed to two main reasons. First, the addition of CH polymer to the GO skin layer increases the interlayer spacing between GO sheets, creating wider nanochannels that facilitate the diffusion of ionic species. Second, the presence of CH chains in the structure of the membrane causes the decreasing charge density of nanochannels and consequently reduces the electrostatic repulsive forces on the ionic species (Fig. 10(b)). Thus, adding a small amount of CH to the membrane reduces salt rejection. However, for GO/CH membranes containing 0.02 mg/cm2 of GO, increasing the CH content from 0.1 mg/cm2 to 0.2 mg/cm2 leads to the improvement of salt rejection. Specifically, GO0.02/CH0.2 shows the highest rejection of 90.3%, 95.2%, and 94.5% for MgCl2, MgSO4, and NaCl, respectively.

It is interesting that with increasing intercalation of cationic CH polymers into GO membranes, d-spacing increases and charge density of nanochannels decreases. This behavior is unfavorable for ionic separation through size sieving and ion exclusion mechanisms. The presence of cationic polymers within the interlayer spacing of GO membranes can enhance salt rejection through a cation-recognition effect19. This effect arises from electrostatic repulsion interactions between positive ionic species and the cationic polymer chains located between the GO sheets. This repulsion creates an energy barrier for the permeation of cation ions through the channels, while simultaneously increasing the permeation of water molecules (Fig. 10(c)).

Fig. 10(a) Illustration of ionic repulsion interactions on negative charge ions, (b) channel total charge density and d-spacing at wet state for the membranes, and (c) Illustration of cation-recognition effect on the ion separation of the membrane.

(a) Illustration of ionic repulsion interactions on negative charge ions, (b) channel total charge density and d-spacing at wet state for the membranes, and (c) Illustration of cation-recognition effect on the ion separation of the membrane.

As the concentration of intercalated cationic CH polymer in GO membranes increases from 0.1 mg/cm² to 0.15 mg/cm², the density of cationic sites within the nanochannels also increases. This rise in cationic sites enhances the contribution of cation recognition to ionic separation, leading to improved performance. This also results in increased water permeation due to larger d-spacing and enhanced hydrophilicity of the nanochannels in the presence of the CH polymer. However, excessive addition of CH leads to a sharp increase in thickness and the formation of a dense structure between GO sheets, which prevents the diffusion of ionic species and water molecules through the nanochannels. Consequently, while high CH content may still achieve high salt rejection, the water flux tends to decrease. With respect to the above discussions, the intercalation of cationic CH into GO membranes allows for a synergistic combination of size-sieving, ion-exclusion, and cation-recognition effects, leading to both high salt rejection and improved water flux.

To further analyze the separation performance of the membranes, the Yellow ME4G dye rejection was evaluated. Figure 11 shows that the dye rejection of all membranes exhibits a behavior similar to salt rejection. Compared to the salt rejection, the dye rejection indicates higher values, which can be attributed to its anionic nature and large molecular size. These features enhance dye separation through ion-exclusion and size-sieving mechanisms. According to the results of membrane performance, GO/CH membranes showed higher performance compared to the pristine GO membranes. Moreover, with increasing CH loading to 0.15 mg/cm2, permeation flux reached a maximum value, and the rejection improved; this increase in the rejection was insignificant for CH loading higher than 0.15 mg/cm2. Based on the ion and dye separation performance of the membranes, GO0.02/CH0.15 was determined as the optimal membrane.

The effect of operating pressure on the separation performance of GO0.02/CH0.15 and GO0.02 membranes was also investigated. As shown in Fig. 12(a), the permeation flux of both membranes increases continuously with increasing operating pressure from 1 to 5 bar and exhibits a nonlinear growth at higher pressures. These findings are consistent with previously reported results6,42. The nonlinear increase in permeation flux is attributed to the structural compactness of the membranes at high pressures6. Furthermore, the NaCl rejection improves with increasing pressure. Since ion transport through the ion exclusion and cation-recognition effects is independent of the pressure gradient19,43, the observed increase in rejection can be attributed to the greater contribution of the size exclusion mechanism in the separation process. Therefore, ion exclusion and cation-recognition effects are mainly the dominant mechanisms in the separation process at low pressure, while size exclusion controls the separation at higher pressure. Compared to the results of GO0.02/CH0.15, the permeation flux of GO0.02 indicates less increase with pressure and exhibits a more nonlinear behavior. Additionally, the NaCl rejection of GO0.02 increases at a higher rate compared to that of GO0.02/CH0.15. This behavior implies that the structure of GO0.02 becomes more compact under high pressure, while the intercalation of CH chains in GO0.02/CH0.15 prevents excessive compaction of the GO layers. Accordingly, it can be concluded that the size exclusion mechanism has a greater impact on the separation performance of pristine GO membranes compared to GO/CH membranes, due to their greater structural sensitivity to pressure changes. It should be noted that the obtained d-spacing from XRD results does not reflect the precise interlayer spacing of the nanochannels during the NF process. Therefore, the MWCO of GO0.02 and the optimized membrane (GO0.02/CH0.15) were determined by filtration of aqueous solutions of pyridine, phenol, 2-naphtol, and blue3 with various molecular weights and molecular sizes (Table S1). As shown in Fig. 12(b-c), for both GO0.02 and GO0.02/CH0.15, with increasing operating pressure, the rejection of solute increased, which consist with the results of NaCl separation at various pressures (Fig. 12(a)). Furthermore, the rejection of solute increases with increasing molecular weight, indicating the passage limitation for larger molecules. For the GO0.02 membrane, at different pressure all solutes show rejection values lower than % 90. This indicates that the MWCO of the GO0.02 membrane is out of the molecular weight range of the used solutes. However, the intensity changes of solute rejections with pressure show the potential of controlling the MWCO of the pristine GO membranes through tuning operating pressure. Figure 12(c) shows that the GO0.02/CH0.15 membrane possesses a different MWCO with pressure. At 1 bar operating pressure, pyridine, phenol, 2-naphtol, and blue3 exhibit rejection of 73%, 80%, 85% and 90%, respectively, indicating MWCO more than 300 g/mol (blue3). With increasing pressure to 3 bar, MWCO of GO0.02/CH0.15 decreased to 144 g/mol according to the 90% rejection of 2-napthol. It is interesting that with increasing the pressure to 5 bar, pyridine also shows rejection value more than 90%, which indicates a MWCO value lower than 79 g/mol. Comparison of the results for GO0.02 and GO0.02/CH0.15 membranes demonstrates that the intercalation of CH between GO sheets allows for control over the MWCO of the membranes across a broad spectrum of solutes.

To understand the mutual effects of salts on membrane performance under practical conditions, the salt rejection of GO0.02/CH0.15 was investigated by filtration of binary salt mixtures. As shown in Fig. 13 (a), for the MgCl2/MgSO4 binary salt mixture, the Cl- rejection increases exponentially with increasing Cl- concentration in the solution. In contrast, SO42- rejection shows insignificant changes with the Cl-/SO42- ratio. This behavior can be explained by the Donnan exclusion effect. Given that Mg2+ is a high-valent ion, its high rejection, driven by cation-recognition effect, induced the membrane system to retain additional Cl- in the feed solution to preserve electroneutrality. Consequently, an increase in Cl- rejection is observed as its concentration rises.

Fig. 13(a) Cl− and SO42− rejection of MgCl2/MgSO4 mixture and (b) Na+ and Mg2+ rejection of NaCl/MgCl2 mixture.

(a) Cl− and SO42− rejection of MgCl2/MgSO4 mixture and (b) Na+ and Mg2+ rejection of NaCl/MgCl2 mixture.

Additionally, at higher Cl– concentrations, the gradient of chemical potential across the membrane intensifies, further enhancing chloride rejection. However, for SO42–, a larger charge and size, along with its stronger hydration energy (− 1080 kJ/mol for SO42– compared to − 340 kJ/mol for Cl–), create a significant electrostatic and steric barrier that maintains its high rejection over concentration ranges40. As shown in Fig. 13 (b), the rejection behavior of mono (Na+) and divalent cations (Mg2+) in NaCl/MgCl2 solution exhibits similar trends to those observed for anionic co-ions. Additionally, at high Na+ concentrations, Mg2+ has lower rejection than Na+, which can be attributed to the lower valence ratio of MgCl2 relative to NaCl. However, the changes of Mg2+ rejection in the range of concentration are insignificant, due to the repulsion interactions on Mg2+ through the cation-recognition effect.

Further analysis of the ionic separation mechanisms and their interplay was performed by examining the effect of d-spacing, channel total charge density, and cation charge density along nanochannels on salt rejection (Fig. 14). As shown in Fig. 14 (a-c), the effect of d-spacing, cation charge density along nanochannels, and the total charge density of the channels on the salt rejection is insignificant when controlling the GO content​. Furthermore, salts with a high valence ratio (MgSO4) demonstrate greater rejection compared to other salts across the varying range of these three parameters. In addition, with decreasing GO content, the cation charge density increases, leading to the reduction of salt rejection. Actually, in a certain amount of CH, a decrease in GO loading leads to a higher concentration of intercalated CH. However, this reduction in GO causes to the channel total charge density and an increase in d-spacing, which results in more permeation of ion species through channels. These findings suggest that by manipulating GO loading, the ion exclusion (Donnan effect) mechanism contributes more significantly to the membrane’s separation performance than the cation recognition effect​. However, the slight changes in the rejection and their low values indicate that the ion exclusion mechanism is primarily more efficient on the membrane surface compared to within the nanochannels. Figure 14(d-f) shows the effect of d-spacing, total charge density of channels, and cation charge density along channels, all regulated by CH loading, on salt rejection. ​As CH loading increases, both d-spacing and cation charge density rise, while the total charge density of channels decreases. It can be observed that salt rejection varies significantly with these three parameters, reaching its highest values under certain conditions. Additionally, the difference in salt rejection between salts with high and low valence ratios decreases as d-spacing and cation charge density increase. ​Conversely, this difference in rejection grows as the channel total charge density increases. ​​In comparison to controlling GO loading (Fig. 14(a-c)), ​when the CH content is controlled, the total charge density within the channel exhibits changes in lower ranges, whereas the cation charge density undergoes alterations in higher ranges.​ This behavior leads to a more pronounced cation recognition effect and a weakened ion exclusion effect. Therefore, by adjusting the CH content, the ionic separation performance is mainly controlled by the cation recognition effect. According to the above discussions, tuning the separation performance by adjusting the GO loading intensifies the ion exclusion effect while weakening the cation recognition effect, leading to only a slight improvement in salt rejection (Fig. 14(g)). Whereas, manipulating the CH loading enhances the cation recognition effect in the nanochannels, leading to a significant increase in salt rejection. This suggests that utilizing the cation recognition effect, rather than relying solely on ion exclusion, enables the achievement of the highest salt rejection​.

Stability of the membranes

To evaluate the structural durability of the membranes during the water desalination process, the membranes were exposed under operational conditions (5 bar at 25 ◦C) for 7 days. As can be seen from Fig. 15, the skin layer of the pristine GO membranes detached from the PES substrate after the desalination process. In contrast, the GO/CH membranes exhibited strong structural stability. Further investigation compared the stability of the optimal membrane (GO0.02/CH0.15) with GO0.02 in an ultrasonic bath for 3 h. Figure S7 confirms the high stability of GO0.02/CH0.15, whereas the GO0.02 membrane showed detachment of the skin layer from the PES substrate during the ultrasonic bath. Additionally, the long-term structural stability of GO0.02/CH0.15 and GO0.02 was evaluated under harsh conditions, including strongly acidic and alkaline environments, over 90 days (Figure S8). The pristine GO membrane disintegrated in acidic, alkaline, and neutral solutions within 90 days. In contrast, the GO0.02/CH0.15 membrane maintained its high integrity over this long-term period. These observations clearly show that the presence of the CH polymer in the skin layer and its electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces with GO sheets significantly enhance the durability of membranes under operational conditions.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel strategy was developed to enhance both ionic separation performance and water permeability of GO-based membranes by integrating size-sieving, ion-exclusion, and cation-recognition mechanisms. This was achieved by precisely manipulating key parameters such as d-spacing, channel total charge density, and cation charge density along sub-nanometer channels through the intercalation of positively charged CH into GO membranes with varying GO/CH content via a one-pot pressure self-assembly method. The addition of CH polymer allowed for the precise control of interlayer spacing in both dry and wet states through ionic crosslinking of GO sheets. As the intercalated cationic CH polymer increased, the channel total charge density decreased, while the cation charge density along the nanochannels increased. The GO0.02/CH0.17 membrane, which featured optimized d-spacing, channel total charge density, and cation charge density along channels, demonstrated superior ionic rejection rates of 88% for MgCl2, 90% for NaCl, and 93% for MgSO4, along with excellent water permeation rates of 18–24 L.m-2.h-1. This enhanced performance was attributed to the synergistic effect of the manipulated parameters on separation. Furthermore, the uniform intercalation of the cationic CH polymer between GO sheets improved the long-term structural stability of the membrane under harsh conditions, driven by electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces. This research offers a promising and effective approach for designing GO-based membranes with enhanced water permeability and improved salt rejection, thereby addressing critical needs in water purification applications.
