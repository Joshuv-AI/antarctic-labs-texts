Outcomes and Predictors of Stroke After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in the Cerebral Protection Device Era
Authors: Shashank Shekhar, Toshiaki Isogai, Ankit Agrawal, Roop Kaw, Gauranga Mahalwar, Amar Krishnaswamy, Rishi Puri, Grant Reed, Amgad Mentias, Samir Kapadia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudies have shown inconclusive results on the effectiveness of cerebral protection devices (CPDs) with transcatheter aortic valve replacement. We aimed to analyze the national statistics on stroke and other outcomes with CPD use.Methods and ResultsThe Nationwide Readmissions Database (2017–2020) was queried to obtain data on patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement. Outcomes were compared between patients with a CPD and patients without a CPD. Of 271 804 patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement, CPD was used in 7.3% of patients. In a multivariable logistic regression analysis, CPD use was not associated with lower overall stroke rates (1.6% versus 1.9% without CPD; odds ratio, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.84–1.07]; P=0.364), but it was significantly associated with lower major stroke rates (1.2% versus 1.5% without CPD; odds ratio, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.74–0.98]; P=0.02). Patients with a CPD also had a shorter length of stay, higher routine discharges to home/self‐care (74.9% versus 70.6%), and lower mortality rates (0.7% versus 1.3%). The 30‐day (9.6% versus 11.7%) and 180‐day (24.6% versus 28.2%) readmission rates were significantly lower in the CPD cohort. Among patients who developed stroke, patients with a CPD had more frequent routine discharges. Prior valve surgery was associated with the highest risk of overall and major stroke.ConclusionsCPD use during transcatheter aortic valve replacement was not independently associated with a lower risk of overall stroke but was associated with a lower risk of major stroke in a multivariable model. Data from future randomized trials that may offset any potential confounders in our study are required to help identify patients who would benefit from the use of these devices.Keywords: cerebral embolic protection, sentinel device, stroke, TAVRSubject Categories: Cerebrovascular Disease/Stroke, Aortic Valve Replacement/Transcather Aortic Valve Implantation

What Are the Clinical Implications?

The present data reinforce the notion of a protective benefit of cerebral protection device use during transcatheter aortic valve replacement upon major/disabling stroke, but not necessarily overall stroke, and cerebral protection device use may still be considered beneficial in causing less harm to patients if stroke does occur.

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has gained popularity in the past decade owing to its minimally invasive approach and promising data in patients with severe aortic stenosis at all surgical risk levels. 1 , 2 Despite technical advancements, including new valve types, and operator experience, stroke remains a feared complication affecting nearly 2% of patients undergoing TAVR. 3 , 4 Besides patient‐related factors such as prior history of stroke, atrial fibrillation, and severe calcification, procedural factors such as embolization during valve implantation, prolonged procedure time, rapid pacing, and extensive manipulation of the aorta have all been described as factors leading to stroke following TAVR. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 To mitigate the risks of stroke and stroke‐related morbidity and death, cerebral protection devices (CPDs) have been developed. 10

The SENTINEL CPD (Boston Scientific Corp., Boston, MA) is designed to capture and remove debris elaborated during TAVR. The pivotal trial of the device randomized 363 high‐risk patients with aortic stenosis 1:1:1 into a safety arm, device arm, and control arm. The trial demonstrated its safety and led to approval by the US Food and Drug Administration despite the lack of a significant difference in stroke as assessed by magnetic resonance imaging. 11 , 12 Recently, the results of the PROTECTED TAVR (Stroke Protection With Sentinel During Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement) trial, which randomized 3000 patients undergoing TAVR from >50 global sites and all surgical risk levels to either the SENTINEL CPD group (n=1501) or no‐CPD group (n=1499) were published. 13 The investigators reported no significant difference in the primary end point of clinical stroke within 72 hours of TAVR or before discharge between the groups (2.3% with CPD versus 2.9% with no CPD; P=0.30). However, the secondary end point of disabling stroke was less among the protected group (0.5% versus 1.3%; P=0.02). This latter finding falls in line with a number of single‐center and registry‐based analyses that suggest a 60% to 80% relative risk reduction in stroke with SENTINEL. 14 Despite the aforementioned data, use of the device is still quite limited with the majority of TAVR centers citing a lack of clear‐cut randomized trial evidence for its benefit, coupled with a challenging economic climate for a currently nonreimbursed device.

To better understand the larger effects of CPD use during TAVR, we sought to analyze the hospital outcomes, readmission rates, and predictors of stroke from a nationally representative all‐comers database.

Methods

Data Source

The data supporting the present study's findings are publicly available and can be accessed at www.hcup‐us.ahrq.gov/nrdoverview.jsp. For this retrospective cohort study, we used the Nationwide Readmissions Database (NRD) maintained by the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project of the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality in the United States. 15 Over 17 million unweighted and 32 million weighted discharges are annually recorded from hospitals across different states, representing ≈52% of all US hospitalizations. Patients are tracked using verified linkage numbers within a state during a calendar year. Thus, the maximum follow‐up duration in the database is from January to the end of December of each year. Deidentified data are presented using the International Classification of Diseases Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) and Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) and Procedure Coding System (ICD‐10‐PR) codes. Since the Sentinel CPD was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2017, our patient cohort was extracted using ICD‐10‐CM and Procedure Coding System codes codes from the NRD 2017 to 2020 (4 years). As the publicly available database contains deidentified data, approval from the Institutional Review Board was not required for this study. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiologychecklist was used when writing the present report. 16

Study Cohort

Table S1 lists the ICD‐10‐CM and Procedure Coding System codes used in the present study. From the NRD, data on all patients undergoing transfemoral TAVR (TF‐TAVR) were extracted using relevant, previously verified ICD‐10‐PR codes (02RF37Z, 02RF38Z, 02RF3JZ, and 02RF3KZ). 17 , 18 Patient records were excluded if their age was <18 years or if patients underwent concomitant surgery (surgical aortic valve replacement, coronary artery bypass grafting, or thoracic aorta surgery) during the same hospitalization. Missing data on in‐hospital death were also excluded. Eligible patients were categorized into those who underwent TF‐TAVR with the use of a SENTINEL CPD (ICD‐10‐PR code X2A5312) and those who underwent TF‐TAVR without the use of a CPD.

Patient Characteristics

Demographics and baseline characteristics were identified using patient‐level variables provided by the NRD. The following variables were identified using ICD‐10‐CM codes as part of patient characteristics (Table S1): age, sex, bicuspid aortic valve, prior valve surgery, hypertension, diabetes with and without complications, chronic pulmonary disease, atrial fibrillation, carotid artery disease, end‐stage renal disease on dialysis, prior coronary artery bypass grafting, prior percutaneous coronary intervention, prior pacemaker insertion, and prior cerebrovascular accidents. 19 Hospitals were categorized into metropolitan teaching hospitals versus others.

Definitions and Outcomes

In the present study, major stroke was defined as stroke resulting in death or discharge to another hospital/nursing facility (Data S1). Other outcomes that were evaluated between the CPD and no‐CPD cohorts were length of stay, in‐hospital death, 30‐day readmissions, and 180‐day readmissions. For examining 30‐day readmissions, only patients discharged in the first 11 months of each year were included to allow for 30‐day follow‐up. Similarly, for 180‐day readmission analysis, only patients discharged within the first 6 months of each year were included. If patients had multiple readmissions during a calendar year, only the first readmission was considered for readmissions analysis.

Centers that performed ≤99 TAVR procedures in each year were categorized as low‐volume, while those that performed ≥100 TAVR procedures in each year were categorized as high‐volume centers. Similarly, depending on the number of CPDs used in each year, centers were categorized as low‐volume CPD centers (≤19 CPDs each year) or high‐volume CPD centers (≥20 CPDs each year).

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics and outcomes were compared between the CPD and no‐CPD cohorts. Fisher's exact text or χ2 test was used to analyze categorical variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test or Student t test was used for analysis of continuous variables.

We analyzed the overall in‐hospital stroke rate and major stroke rate among patients undergoing TF‐TAVR between the CPD and no‐CPD cohorts. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the predictors of overall stroke and that of major stroke in patients undergoing TF‐TAVR. Another multivariable logistic regression analysis was also conducted to examine the predictors of major stroke among patients with a CPD undergoing TAVR. Based on clinical judgment and prior literature, 19 variables that showed statistical significance in a univariate model for overall stroke were included in the multivariable logistic regression models. Since operator experience can play a role in successful device deployment and outcomes, we included procedural volumes in the multivariable logistic regression models. All 16 patient and hospital characteristics, hospital procedural volume, calendar time, and the usage of CPDs were included in the multivariable logistic regression models. A 2‐sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The weight variable provided by the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project was used to obtain national estimates. 20 Data extraction and statistical analyses were performed using SAS (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM SPSS, Armonk, NY).

Results

Study Cohort

The Figure shows the patient selection for the present study. A total of 272 886 patients undergoing TF‐TAVR were identified from the NRD 2017 to 2020. After excluding patients who underwent concomitant surgery, 271 804 patients met the study criteria and were included in the present analysis. Among them, CPD was used in 19 821 (7.3%) of patients, while 92.7% underwent TAVR without the use of a CPD. The number of TAVR procedures increased during the study period from 53 853 in 2017 to 81 474 in 2020. The use of CPD also increased from 0.8% in 2017 to 11.3% in 2020.

Outcomes

Table 2 shows the outcomes of the study population. The median (interquartile range) length of stay for patients undergoing TAVR was 2.0 (1–4) days, with 70.9% of patients being routinely discharged. The overall stroke rate was 1.8% among patients undergoing TAVR, and major stroke rate was 1.5%. The 30‐ and 180‐day readmission rates were 11.6% and 27.9%, respectively. Patients with a CPD in comparison with no‐CPD had a shorter length of stay (2.0 [1–3] days versus 2.0 [1–4] days; P<0.001), higher routine discharges (74.9% versus 70.6%), lower mortality rates (0.7% versus 1.3%), lower stroke rates (1.6% versus 1.9%; P=0.023), and lower major stroke rates (1.2% versus 1.5%; P<0.01). The 30‐day (9.6% versus 11.7%) and 180‐day (24.6% versus 28.2%) readmission rates were also significantly lower in the CPD cohort than in the no‐CPD cohort.

Predictors of Overall Stroke

Table 4 shows the results of the multivariable logistic regression model analyzing the predictors of overall in‐hospital stroke among patients undergoing TAVR. Factors associated with increased incidence of overall stroke were increasing age, female sex, bicuspid aortic valve, atrial fibrillation, carotid artery disease, and end‐stage renal disease on dialysis. TAVR at high‐volume centers in comparison with low‐volume centers was associated with lower major stroke but not overall stroke (Tables S3 and S4). Prior valve surgery had the highest association with the occurrence of stroke (odds ratio, 4.94 (95% CI, 4.55–5.36]; P<0.001), followed by carotid artery disease (2.01 [1.84–2.19]; P<0.001). Usage of CPDs was not independently associated with lower stroke rates (0.95 [0.84–1.07]; P=0.364).

Predictors of Major Stroke

Table 5 shows the results of the multivariable logistic regression model analyzing the predictors of major stroke among all patients undergoing TAVR and patients with CPD undergoing TAVR. Factors associated with increased incidence of major stroke were increasing age, female sex, atrial fibrillation, carotid artery disease, and end‐stage renal disease on dialysis. Prior valve surgery had the highest association with the occurrence of major stroke (4.59 [4.18–5.04]; P<0.001). CPD usage was independently associated with lower incidence of major stroke (0.85 [0.74–0.98]; P=0.024). Among patients who received a CPD, increased major stroke risk was associated with increasing age, female sex, prior valve surgery, diabetes with chronic complications, and carotid artery disease.

Discussion

In the present study from a large, real‐world, nationally representative database, we found that CPD use during TAVR was not independently associated with a lower risk of overall stroke, but was independently associated with a lower risk of major stroke. Additionally, patients undergoing TAVR in whom a CPD was used had shorter length of stay, lower in‐hospital mortality rate, and more frequent routine discharges to home/self‐care. Rates of 30‐ and 180‐day readmissions were also lower among patients who underwent TAVR with a CPD. Among patients who suffered a stroke, CPD use was associated with shorter length of stay and higher routine discharges. Prior valve surgery was associated with the highest risk of overall and major stroke. These findings are generally consistent with PROTECTED TAVR outlining a clinical benefit to patients undergoing TAVR using a CPD.

There have been conflicting data on the benefit of CPDs in reducing stroke after TAVR procedures. A recent meta‐analysis by Al‐Abdouh and colleagues of 7 randomized control trials (RCTs) (n=3835 patients; CPD, 2031 versus 1804 without CPD) reported no significant difference in stroke, disabling stroke, or nondisabling stroke between the CPD and no‐CPD groups, 21 with similar results reported in other pooled studies. 22 , 23 , 24 On the other hand, analysis of RCTs has revealed a reduction in imaging markers of brain injury after TAVR with the use of a CPD. 25 , 26 , 27 Interestingly, when analyses were restricted to the SENTINEL device only, pooled studies have shown a significant benefit on outcomes. For instance, Shrestha and colleagues compared different CPDs and demonstrated a reduction in death and stroke among patients in whom the SENTINEL CPD was used but not with other CPDs. 28 Another meta‐analysis by Reddy and colleagues of 8 RCTs (n=4043 patients) highlighted a 60% reduction in disabling stroke with the SENTINEL CPD. 29 In the latest meta‐analysis of 7 CPD RCTs, the authors concluded that CPD use was associated with a high probability of any benefit (94.4%) but the probability of a clinically relevant effect was 0% to 0.1% highlighting an unlikely association of CPD use with clinically relevant benefits. 30

In our study, while patients with a CPD had lower overall stroke rates, this association was lost in the multivariable logistic regression model. These findings are consistent with a recent study from the Vinzient database, which captures data from 400 US academic centers that also reported no significant reduction in post‐TAVR stroke rate with CPD use. 31 However, a reduction was noted in major stroke, which is also consistent with findings from the large, randomized PROTECTED TAVR trial. 13

While defined as a subgroup of all stroke, disabling stroke is a particularly important complication with strong association to morbidity and death among TAVR recipients. A study from the STS/ACC TVT (Society of Thoracic Surgeons–American College of Cardiology Transcatheter Valve Therapy) registry demonstrated an association of early postprocedural stroke with higher risk of 30‐day death (16.7% versus 3.7% in those without stroke) and a lower likelihood of routine discharge (36.1% versus 78.9% in those without stroke). 32 Similarly, an earlier publication from our group analyzing the NRD database from 2018 to 2019 reported lower stroke‐related deaths after TF‐TAVR with CPD than without CPD (odds ratio, 0.539 [0.291–0.996]; P=0.049). 19 In the present study, major stroke defined as stroke leading to death or nonroutine discharge, occurred less frequently in the CPD as compared with the no‐CPD cohort. The association of CPD use with lowering the risk for major stroke following TAVR remained significant in the multivariable logistic regression model. Taken together with the aforementioned studies, the present data reinforce the notion of a protective benefit of CPD use during TAVR upon major/disabling stroke but not necessarily with overall stroke risk. Perhaps the reason is that SENTINEL only protects 80% of the cerebral circulation, with the L side posterior circulation remaining vulnerable. “Bigger” strokes (ie, major/disabling) may be more likely to occur in the territory covered by the SENTINEL CPD, further shedding light on the findings of PROTECTED TAVR and other analyses, including ours. 10 This also suggests the need for the design of future CPDs that can protect the entire brain. 10

We found in the current analysis that CPD use was associated with lower in‐hospital death rates and more frequent routine discharges after TAVR. Seeger and colleagues reported similar findings from their single‐center study, 33 as did a German national database study demonstrating lower mortality rates after TAVR with CPD use. 34 In another propensity score–matched analysis from the 2017 National Inpatient Sample, investigators demonstrated a lower mortality rate (0% versus 1%; P<0.01) and discharge to a nursing facility (12.4% versus 14.8%) for patients with CPD use. 35 As the SENTINEL device had just been approved in May 2017, the study only included one half of a single year. Our present study that includes patients over 4 years of SENTINEL use therefore adds a more robust analysis of the latest available data.

Given the current lack of RCT evidence demonstrating a reduction in overall stroke with CPD for all‐comers, there is interest in understanding who are the “high‐stroke‐risk patients” for whom CPD may be applied on an individual basis. Similar to prior findings, in our analysis, female sex, atrial fibrillation, and carotid artery disease were associated with both overall stroke and major stroke after TAVR. 35 , 36 The PARTNER (Placement of Aortic Transcatheter Valves) trial showed higher stroke rates among patients with small aortic annuli and smaller aortic valve area, which are more common in women. 37 This could be due to increased mechanical interaction between the native and prosthetic aortic valves, as evidenced by Doppler studies. 38 , 39 In this regard, we also found that prior valve surgery had the highest association with the occurrence of both overall and major stroke. In a recent study, Zhang et al reported that CPD use was associated with lower stroke risk in patients with a bicuspid aortic valve. 17 Among CPD patients in our cohort, although old age, female sex, prior valve surgery, and carotid artery disease were independently associated with major stroke risk, we did not find this association patients with a bicuspid aortic valve. While it is currently difficult to ascertain the clinical profile of patients that may benefit from the SENTINEL device, an updated meta‐analysis suggested that patients at high and intermediate surgical risk scores were more likely to benefit from its usage. 40 However, the consequences of a disabling stroke, even though less common, can be very devastating, particularly in low‐surgical‐risk patients. While the present data demonstrate a rather modest reduction in overall and major stroke with an absolute risk reduction of 0.23% (number needed to treat ≈430) and 0.3% (number needed to treat ≈333), respectively, data from future randomized trials that may help to identify patients who would benefit from CPD use would be another step in the right direction for the revolutionary TAVR procedure.

There have been limited studies regarding the readmission outcomes of patients undergoing TAVR with and without CPD. A study from the 2017 to 2018 NRD reported fewer 30‐day readmissions among patients with a CPD than with patients with no CPD (hazard ratio, 0.839 [95% CI, 0.773–0.911]; P<0.01). 41 In the present study, we found that the 30‐ and 180‐day readmission rates were significantly lower among patients with a CPD.

It is important to acknowledge the safety of SENTINEL use that has been demonstrated in all analyses of the device thus far. Further, the almost‐universal applicability and ease of use of the device should be recognized. Our single‐center study of all consecutive patients presenting for TF‐TAVR, we demonstrated the feasibility of SENTINEL use among 1680 patients with a device placement success rate of 97.2% and no device‐related vascular complications. 42 The major reasons for deployment failure were inability to gain radial access, excessive tortuosity at the subclavian/innominate artery, and tortuosity in the forearm.

Study Limitations

First, the results of the present study are retrospective, observational, and derived from an administrative claims database, which are prone to coding errors and under‐ or overcoding. Individual learning curves for the procedures cannot be obtained from such studies. Second, stroke is reported as a site‐specific outcome in the database, and neurologist assessment or a stroke scale could not be obtained for patients. However, it has been suggested in a study from a large‐volume TAVR center that CPD use may be associated with an attenuated severity of stroke evidenced by a comprehensive neurological assessment. 34 , 41 , 43 Therefore, even though the present study is limited by the lack of data on neurological assessment, CPD use may still be considered beneficial in causing less harm to patients if stroke does occur. Third, the outcomes from this study focused on the SENTINEL CPD and, as such, cannot be generalized to other CPDs. Newer‐generation devices with more comprehensive protection may be even more beneficial compared with the SENTINEL device. Fourth, procedural details such as valve type, pre‐ and postdilation, or procedural success (both for TAVR and CPD) are not available from the database. Fifth, while a multivariable logistic model was employed to adjust for patient comorbidities between the CPD and no‐CPD cohorts, it is possible that outcomes are still driven by other unadjusted or adjusted confounders.

Conclusions

CPD use was associated with lower major strokes but not all strokes following TAVR in a multivariable model. Data from future randomized trials that may offset any potential confounders in our study are required to help identify patients who would benefit from the use of these devices.
