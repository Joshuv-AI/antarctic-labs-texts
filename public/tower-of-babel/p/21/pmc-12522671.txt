Distal pancreatectomy with splenectomy for a large pancreatic lymphoepithelial cyst: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Hongjie Qiu, Youwen Zhu, Guanting Dong, Changsheng Fan, Qin Yang, Huachun Luo, Mingzhen Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic lymphoepithelial cyst is a rare benign pancreatic lesion with challenging preoperative diagnosis due to its radiological similarity to other cystic pancreatic diseases. Definitive diagnosis typically relies on histopathological examination after surgical excision. We present a case of pancreatic lymphoepithelial cyst and review 21 patients who underwent surgical treatment for pancreatic lymphoepithelial cyst from 2016 to 2024, aiming to enhance the diagnostic and therapeutic management of this disease.Case presentationA 66-year-old Asian male from China was admitted to the hospital owing to left upper abdominal pain and bloating for 3 months. Imaging revealed a 7.5-cm cystic mass in the pancreatic tail, closely associated with the splenic vessels. The patient underwent a distal pancreatectomy with splenectomy. Postoperative pathology confirmed pancreatic lymphoepithelial cyst. The patient recovered uneventfully and remained symptom-free during a 1-month follow-up.ConclusionAlthough rare and of uncertain origin, pancreatic lymphoepithelial cyst should be considered in the differential diagnosis of pancreatic cystic lesions. Imaging combined with endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration may aid in diagnosis, and management strategies should be tailored to individual cases.

Introduction

Pancreatic lymphoepithelial cyst (PLEC) is a rare form of true pancreatic cyst, accounting for approximately 0.5% of all pancreatic cystic lesions. They are typically solitary and may occur anywhere in the pancreas, with a predilection for the body and tail regions [2]. More than half of the reported lesions are smaller than 2 cm in diameter, while fewer than 5% exceed 5 cm. PLEC predominantly affects adult males, with an estimated male-to-female ratio of 4:1 [3]. The first case of PLEC was described by Lachtrath et al. in 1985, who noted the characteristic histopathological features: the cyst is lined with mature keratinized stratified squamous epithelium and surrounded by lymphoid tissue containing follicles, typically filled with keratinous debris [2]. Radiologically, PLEC demonstrates variable imaging characteristics and may mimic other pancreatic cystic lesions, such as mucinous cystic neoplasms, serous cystic neoplasms, or cystic components of adenosquamous carcinoma [4]. As such, preoperative differentiation can be challenging. Given the difficulty in excluding malignancy preoperatively, extensive pancreatic resection is often undertaken in clinical practice. Here, we present a case of a large PLEC successfully managed with surgical resection, along with a comprehensive literature review focusing on its histological origin, differential diagnosis, and current diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [1–21].

Case presentation

A 66-year-old Asian male (body mass index, BMI 25.95 kg/m2) from China was incidentally found to have a mass in the pancreatic tail during a routine abdominal ultrasound more than 20 years ago. Owing to its small size and the absence of clinical symptoms at the time, no further evaluation or intervention was performed. However, 3 months prior to admission, he developed intermittent left upper abdominal pain accompanied by bloating. The patient had a 20-year history of smoking and reported occasional alcohol consumption. He had no history of abdominal surgery, pancreatitis, pancreatic insufficiency, or trauma. Laboratory tests showed a serum amylase level of 83 U/L (reference range 0–135 U/L) and an elevated lipase level of 103 U/L (reference range 10–60 U/L), while other biochemical parameters were within normal limits. Serum tumor markers were elevated, with carbohydrate antigen (CA)19-9 at 67.52 U/mL (reference < 36 U/mL), CA50 at 42.34 U/mL (reference < 25 U/mL), and CA24-2 at 22.72 U/mL (reference < 20 U/mL). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a well-circumscribed, hypodense mass in the tail of the pancreas, measuring approximately 7.5 × 6.5 cm2, with a CT attenuation of 25 Hounsfield units (HU) (Fig. 1A). Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a spherical cystic lesion in the pancreatic tail, approximately 7.7 × 6.8 cm2 in size, with indistinct margins and adhesion to the spleen and left kidney (Fig. 1B–E). On T1-weighted imaging, the mass appeared isointense (Fig. 1F), while it showed high signal intensity on T2-weighted imaging (Fig. 1G). Small internal septations were seen in the lower portion of the lesion, and delayed enhancement of the cyst wall and septa was observed following contrast administration (Fig. 1H). The mass exhibited no communication with the pancreatic duct, which was clearly visualized with a normal caliber and natural course. On the basis of the imaging and laboratory findings, a preoperative diagnosis of a pancreatic space-occupying lesion was made. The patient subsequently underwent a laparoscopic distal pancreatectomy with splenectomy. During surgery, the gastrocolic ligament was dissected to access the lesser sac, allowing full exposure of the pancreas and spleen. A cystic mass, measuring approximately 7.5 × 6.5 × 6.0 cm3, was identified in the pancreatic tail. The lesion was densely adherent to the spleen and encased the splenic artery and vein, making separation impossible. Additional adhesions were noted between the lesion and the left kidney and adrenal gland. No abnormalities were observed in the liver or regional lymph nodes during exploration. The pancreatic body and tail, along with the splenic vessels, were carefully mobilized. After ligation and transection of the splenic vasculature, the pancreas was transected approximately 2 cm proximal to the tumor using a linear stapler. The spleen was then carefully mobilized and removed en bloc with the tumor. The surgical specimen, including both the mass and spleen, was sent for histopathological examination (Fig. 1I). The operation lasted 2 h and 40 min, with an estimated blood loss of 100 mL.

On postoperative day 5, histopathological examination of the resected specimen revealed a cystic mass adjacent to the spleen, containing gray–brown fluid. The cyst wall was slightly rough, with a thickness of 0.2–0.3 cm. The splenic parenchyma appeared soft and gray–red upon sectioning, with no distinct areas of firmness. Microscopic examination showed that the cyst was lined with mature stratified squamous epithelial cells, surrounded by numerous lymphocytes (Fig. 2A, B). Additionally, lymphoplasmacytic infiltration was observed in the adjacent pancreatic tissue, accompanied by lymphoid follicle formation and numerous immunoglobulin G (IgG)4-positive plasma cells (> 100/high-power field (HPF)). Immunohistochemical staining (EliVision method) confirmed strong IgG4 positivity (Fig. 2C), as well as positive expression of IgG, cluster of differentiation (CD)38 (plasma cells), Kappa, Lambda, CD3 (T cells), CD20 (B cells), and CD21 (follicular dendritic cells). The patient had an uneventful postoperative course and was discharged on day 10. At the 1-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic and reported no complaints.

Discussion

Pancreatic lymphoepithelial cyst (PLEC) is a rare benign pancreatic cyst characterized by well-defined borders and clear demarcation from the surrounding pancreatic parenchyma. Macroscopically, the cyst typically contains caseous or curd-like material, which is indicative of keratinous debris. Histologically, it is lined by stratified squamous epithelium and surrounded by dense lymphoid tissue, occasionally containing lymphoid follicles [8].

The exact pathogenesis of PLEC remains uncertain. One hypothesis suggests that it originates from displaced branchial arch remnants owing to their shared histological features [2]. Another theory proposes that chronic pancreatic duct obstruction leads to squamous metaplasia, which may subsequently extend into adjacent peripancreatic lymph nodes [6]. Additionally, some studies have suggested that PLECs may arise from ectopic pancreatic tissue within regional lymph nodes. Capitanich et al. reported the presence of benign ectopic pancreatic tissue in peripancreatic lymph nodes under normal conditions [10].

In most cases, PLEC presents with nonspecific symptoms. Patients commonly experience vague abdominal discomfort, occasionally accompanied by gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, or loss of appetite. Laboratory tests may show elevated levels of tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen (CA)19-9 [7, 13]. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are the primary imaging modalities used to evaluate pancreatic cystic lesions. These techniques provide detailed information on lesion localization, morphology (unilocular versus multilocular), and cyst content characteristics (solid versus fluid-filled). On CT, PLEC typically appears as a well-circumscribed, slightly hypodense or isodense lesion, occasionally exhibiting internal septations or punctate calcifications. Mild enhancement of the cyst wall and septations may be observed after contrast administration, with no significant dilation of the pancreatic duct, suggesting an exophytic growth pattern [3]. On MRI, PLEC usually appears as a homogeneous low-signal lesion on T1-weighted images and demonstrates high signal intensity on T2-weighted sequences [14, 18].

Although cross-sectional imaging modalities such as CT and MRI significantly enhance the diagnostic accuracy of pancreatic cystic lesions, they have limitations in precisely characterizing the internal cystic contents. In this context, endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) has emerged as a valuable diagnostic tool for PLECs. On endoscopic ultrasound, PLECs typically appear as cystic lesions with homogeneous internal contents and high echogenicity. Cytological evaluation of EUS-FNA specimens often reveals benign keratinizing squamous epithelium, anucleate squamous cells, keratinous debris, and scattered lymphocytes under light microscopy [10]. Cyst fluid analysis may demonstrate elevated levels of amylase and tumor markers, including CEA, CA19-9, and CA12-5. However, EUS-FNA is not without risk. Potential complications include bleeding, pancreatic fistula, and cyst rupture with subsequent peritoneal dissemination. Notably, a case report from Japan described peritoneal seeding following EUS-FNA of a presumed benign pancreatic lesion [14].

Owing to the extremely low incidence of PLEC and the absence of specific radiological features, distinguishing it from other benign and malignant pancreatic tumors remains challenging. The differential diagnosis primarily includes other squamous epithelial lesions of the pancreas, such as epidermoid cysts arising from an intrapancreatic accessory spleen, dermoid cysts, adenosquamous carcinoma, and metastatic squamous cell carcinoma. Epidermoid cysts of an intrapancreatic accessory spleen most commonly occur in the pancreatic tail. These are true cysts lined by squamous epithelium, similar to PLECs, but are histologically characterized by the presence of normal splenic tissue within the cyst wall. Superparamagnetic iron oxide-enhanced MRI (SPIO-MRI) has proven useful in diagnosing these lesions, as the solid components of the cyst exhibit SPIO uptake patterns and T2 hypointensity similar to splenic tissue [7]. Dermoid cysts typically occur in younger individuals with no sex predilection. Histologically, they are lined by squamous epithelium and contain skin appendages as well as tissues derived from the gastrointestinal tract, respiratory tract, and mesoderm. These components can often be identified by FNA [18]. Adenosquamous carcinoma and metastatic squamous cell carcinoma often exhibit malignant cytological features, including nuclear atypia, abnormal mitotic figures, and necrotic background, making them generally distinguishable from PLEC on pathological examination. Other pancreatic cystic lesions—such as pseudocysts, cystadenomas, and cystic pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors—may demonstrate imaging characteristics that resemble those of PLEC. Histologically, they may also contain squamous debris or keratinous material originating from adjacent structures such as the digestive or respiratory tract. Therefore, careful pathological differentiation is essential [17, 18].

PLEC is a benign lesion, and to date, no cases of malignant transformation have been reported in the literature [18]. Clinically, surgical intervention may be considered in patients with suspected PLEC who present with significant abdominal symptoms, when pancreatic malignancy cannot be definitively excluded after comprehensive evaluation, or when the patient has a strong preference for surgery (the diagnostic and treatment process for PLEC is shown in Fig. 3). The choice of surgical approach depends on several factors, including the size of the lesion, its anatomical relationship with adjacent organs, the presence of concurrent pancreatitis, and whether there is compression of the pancreatic or biliary ducts. Common surgical procedures include local excision of the lesion, pancreaticoduodenectomy, and distal pancreatectomy with or without splenectomy [2, 4, 5]. Furthermore, as reported by Pereira et al., some asymptomatic patients may undergo EUS-FNA to exclude malignancy and, if confirmed benign, may be managed conservatively with routine follow-up [15]. In the present case, the cystic lesion was located in the body and tail of the pancreas, with a diameter exceeding 5 cm. The patient exhibited significant symptoms at admission, and imaging studies showed close adherence of the lesion to the splenic vessels. Conclusions

Pancreatic lymphoepithelial cyst (PLEC) is a rare benign lesion, and its pathogenesis remains poorly understood. Owing to the absence of specific clinical manifestations and its potential to mimic other pancreatic cystic lesions, preoperative diagnosis is often challenging. Comprehensive imaging evaluations, combined with endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration (EUS-FNA), are essential tools for accurate diagnosis. On the basis of the diagnostic findings, patients may be managed conservatively or undergo surgical resection.
