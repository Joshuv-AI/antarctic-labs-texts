Contradictions in value systems, social norms, and the disconnect between expectations and reality of sexuality and contraceptive use among youth in Karamoja, Uganda
Authors: Lillian Ojanduru, Godfrey Siu, Nazarius. M. Tumwesigye, Justine N. Bukenya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial norms are known to have a powerful influence on health-related behaviors including sexuality and uptake of contraceptives among different population categories. Globally and in sub–Saharan Africa, millions of young people experience challenges related to sexual and reproductive health (SRH) such as unintended pregnancies, HIV/ Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs), and limited access to services, which are exacerbated by negative social norms. Using Social Norms Theory, this study explored how social norms affect young people’s attitudes, perceptions, and behavior regarding sexuality and contraceptive use in the Karamoja region, Uganda.MethodsThis qualitative study involved 12 focus group discussions (FGDs), eight in-depth interviews (IDIs), and 8 key informant interviews (KIIs). Participants were purposively selected, and data were coded using Dedoose and analyzed thematically. The FGDs comprised 6–12 participants each (N = 80; 42 females and 38 males), with eight conducted among young people aged 15–24 years and four with community members aged 25 years and older. The IDIs included youth (4 males and 4 females, aged 15–24 years), while the KIIs engaged parents (n = 4) and community leaders (n = 4). Social norms that were examined were related to sexuality and contraceptive use.ResultsContradictory and shifting social norms shaped youth sexuality and contraceptive use in Karamoja. Premarital sex was discouraged and sanctioned, yet engagement sex (rape) is legitimized as a pathway to marriage. Gendered expectations restricted girls more severely, linking their value to virginity and bride price, while early and forced marriages were common, driven by paternal authority and cattle wealth. Norms varied by context: rural and less‑educated youth faced tighter regulation, while urban, educated peers reported greater freedom and covert contraceptive use. Fertility was celebrated as a measure of women’s respect and marital value, tied to bride price, with strong pressure to bear children immediately after marriage. Contraceptive use was stigmatized as “addictive” and incompatible with marital expectations as a girl who starts using contraceptives would continue with that habit even when married.ConclusionContradictory and gendered social norms, combined with misconceptions about modern contraceptives, constrain youth agency and reinforce gendered control over reproduction. This limits women’s autonomy to use contraceptives in Karamoja, underscoring the need for culturally sensitive, gender‑responsive interventions.

Introduction

Despite global progress in sexual and reproductive health (SRH), indicators in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) remain alarming. Among youth aged 15–24, Intimate Partner Violence (IPV) prevalence ranges from 4 to 15% in countries such as Zambia, Liberia, and Senegal. In Uganda, however, rates are considerably higher, averaging 15–25% depending on age group and setting [1–3].

The HIV burden is similarly concerning. In 2024, youth accounted for 370,000 new infections—28% of global cases [4, 5]. In Uganda, prevalence stood at 0.9% among young men and 2.2% among young women, with approximately 150,000 young adults living with HIV [6].

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) further underscore the challenge. Across 27 Sub-Saharan African countries, self-reported prevalence averages 6.9%, with higher rates among women aged 20–24. In Uganda, prevalence among young women is strikingly higher at 26%, far above the regional mean [7, 8].

Teenage pregnancy rates in SSA stand at approximately 25%, compared to 13% globally [9, 10],

Adolescent birth rates in the region continue to exceed 100 per 1,000 girls aged 15–19, among the highest worldwide [11]. In 2021, an estimated 6.1 million births occurred among young people aged 15–19, alongside 332,000 births among younger adolescents aged 10–14 [12]. More recent global estimates show that 21 million pregnancies occur annually among girls aged 15–19 in low- and middle-income countries, with 12 million resulting in births [10].

Uganda’s demographic profile underscores the urgency of these challenges: 75% of the population is under 30, and adolescents constitute 22% of the population [13]. The teenage pregnancy rate in Uganda remains high at 25%, while 30% of adolescents aged 15–19 report an unmet need for family planning [14]. Child marriage continues to affect about 34–35% of girls, despite national strategies aimed at ending the practice [15]. HIV prevalence declined slightly to 5.1% in 2023, yet young people remain disproportionately affected, accounting for over one‑third of new infections [16]. Access to SRH information, services, and supplies in Uganda remains limited for women and girls, while the needs of young men are comparatively under‑researched. Recent studies highlight barriers such as stigma, restrictive gender norms, and inadequate youth‑friendly services, which continue to hinder equitable access to SRH care [17, 18].

Karamoja, one of Uganda’s most remote regions, illustrates these disparities. The modern contraceptive prevalence rate is only 10%, and the total fertility rate (TFR) is the highest nationally, with women aged 15–49 bearing an average of 6.7 children compared to the national average of 5 [14]. Child mortality is also elevated at 55.7 per 1,000 live births, exceeding the national average of 52 per 1,000 [14].

Recent studies have highlighted the role of social norms—informal, often unwritten rules that define acceptable and obligatory behaviors within a community [19]—in shaping contraceptive use [20]. While development efforts have traditionally emphasized the availability of trained providers and quality SRH services [21], the influence of social norms on service uptake has been underestimated [22]. Evidence increasingly points to the need for context-specific SRH programs that account for variations in attitudes and norms affecting contraceptive access [23]. However, studies that examine the mechanisms through which social norms influence SRH uptake—particularly those incorporating the perspectives of male youth—remain limited, thereby constraining efforts to shape contraceptive use and the uptake of SRH services in Uganda [17].

This study was guided by Social Norms Theory, which distinguishes between descriptive norms (what people commonly do) and injunctive norms (what people believe they ought to do) [19]. This framework provided a lens to examine how prevailing norms around sexuality shape young people’s attitudes, perceptions, and behaviors related to contraceptive use in Karamoja.

Methods

Study context

The Karamoja region of Uganda, is a unique semi-arid agro-pastoral area with one rainy season in a year. The main source of livelihoods in Karamoja is livestock keeping, mainly cattle. Cows are valued and one person can own up to a thousand cows or more. The cows are mainly used for marriage (paying bride price/dowry/bride wealth) and cultural prestige. The pastoral community moves with their cattle in search of water and pasture during dry season. There is a low literacy level among the people and the region is the least economically developed [13]. The region is renowned for its strong cultural and traditional norms that are protected by a social structure comprised of the council of elders locally termed “Akiriket”. Most of the population is young with the average age being 15 years [13]. Out of the total estimated population of 1.2 million people in the region, half are female [13].

Study design, data source and collection

Study design

We conducted a cross‑sectional qualitative study using Focus Group Discussions (FGDs), In‑Depth Interviews (IDIs) with youth aged 15–24 years, and Key Informant Interviews (KIIs) with community leaders and parents. FGD, IDI, and KII guides were developed to explore existing social norms and how they affect attitudes, perceptions, and behaviors relating to sexuality and contraceptive use among youth.

FGDs were used to capture collective views, shared cultural norms, and group dynamics around sexuality and contraceptive use. They provided insight into how opinions are shaped, reinforced, or contested within peer groups and communities. IDIs complemented this by creating space for more personal, sensitive, or nuanced accounts that participants might hesitate to share in a group setting. KIIs further enriched the data by drawing on the perspectives of parents and community leaders who are knowledgeable about local social norms and sanctions. Together, these methods offered a comprehensive understanding of the interplay between sexuality, cultural expectations, and contraceptive use in Karamoja.

Data collection

Discussion and interview guides were developed and pretested prior to the commencement of data collection. Data were collected in three districts—Moroto, Napak, and Nabilatuk. These districts were selected because they share similar cultural values and economic activities, particularly agro‑pastoralism, compared to other districts that practice more pastoralism. Data were collected by the lead author (LO), a female PhD student at the time, together with trained Research Assistants (RAs). The RAs, all university graduates fluent in the local language and with prior experience in qualitative research, received training on data collection tools and research ethics, while the lead author, an experienced qualitative researcher, provided oversight throughout the process. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved. Across the 12 FGDs and 16 interviews, recurring themes emerged, and no new insights were identified.

Focus group discussions (FGDs)

Twelve FGDs were conducted, each comprising 6–12 participants (total 80 participants: 42 females and 38 males). Of these, eight FGDs were held with youth (aged 15–24 years), and four with community members (aged 25 + years). All FGDs were conducted separately for males and females. Participants were identified with the assistance of local leaders. Eligibility criteria required participants to be residents of the selected study area for at least twelve months, able to provide written informed consent, and willing to participate voluntarily. Once identified and mobilized, RAs approached them to obtain consent prior to the discussions. FGDs were conducted in central village locations considered safe and appropriate, typically under large trees used for community meetings, with nearby homes available as shelter in case of rain. Each FGD lasted approximately one and a half hours.

In‑depth interviews (IDIs) and key informant interviews (KIIs)

Eight IDIs were conducted with youth (4 males and 4 females, aged 15–24 years), purposively selected based on their knowledge and active participation in cultural events. In addition, eight KIIs were conducted with parents (n = 4) and community leaders (n = 4), all aged 25 + years. These informants were chosen based on their knowledge of local social norms and their residence in the area for at least twelve months. Each interview lasted approximately one and a half hours.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. It formed part of a PhD project approved by the Makerere University School of Public Health Research Ethics Committee (MUSPH‑REC), approval number SPH‑2022‑294. Additional approval was obtained from the Uganda National Council of Science and Technology (UNCST), the national regulatory body, under approval number HS2547ES. District authorities in the study sites also provided letters of permission.

All participants gave verbal informed consent prior to the interviews, and were assured of confidentiality, safety, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. For participants younger than 18 years, parental or guardian consent was obtained in line with Ugandan law, and the minors themselves provided assent. Participants were informed of the purpose of the research, which was clearly outlined in the consent form.

Data management and analysis

All audio recordings were transcribed and translated from the local language (Nga’karimojong) into English by trained RAs. The lead author (LO) reviewed the transcripts thoroughly, crosschecking against the recordings to correct any inconsistencies. Data were coded and analyzed using Dedoose qualitative analysis software. Following the principles of thematic analysis [24], LO first read a sample of transcripts to identify recurring words, terms, and ideas related to social norms and contraceptive use, which were then assigned code names. A coding tree was developed in consultation with co‑authors to organize key themes and subthemes. Themes were named either by summarizing their content or by adopting a respondent’s phrase judged to capture the meaning appropriately. Several subthemes were subsequently grouped under two overarching themes, as presented in Table 1. The preliminary findings were validated with participants.

Initiation and learning of sexual norms in Karamoja: Influence of “Edonga”, family, and peers

This study explored the acquisition of social norms regarding sexuality and contraceptive use among young people in Karamoja, highlighting three central practices and value systems.

First, through the Edonga cultural festival. The Edonga is a central ritual among the Karamojong, during which social norms around sexuality and courtship are formally transmitted to youth. This highly anticipated annual festival, organized by elders and families, attracts diverse participants, especially young people from within and beyond the village. During the event, youth learn social norms from peers, family members, and community elders. Characterized by cultural performances, dancing, and celebration, the festival also serves as an official dating and engagement platform where boys and girls can interact freely. While dating and sexual exploration are permitted, boys are prohibited from forcing girls into sex. Mothers prepare their daughters with new clothes and beads to enhance their chances of attracting potential husbands, while parents take precautionary measures to prevent premarital sex after the festival—for example, restricting visits to the boy’s family and moving the girl to sleep in the main house with her parents. Despite its cultural significance, some stakeholders criticized the “Edonga”, arguing that it encourages risky behavior and sexual immorality, particularly given the widespread use of alcohol during the festivities.

Second, through siblings and peers. Older siblings and peers were identified as important channels through which young people learn local norms around sexuality. While parents also transmit these norms, their influence appears weaker compared to siblings and peers. Youth reported that peers and siblings are more influential because they spend significant time together while grazing animals, fetching water, or socializing in the evenings. Unlike parents, who tend to be conservative, peers and siblings openly discuss a wide range of topics, including relationships, challenges, sexual experiences, contraceptive use, and other personal matters. This peer-to-peer exchange was described as a critical avenue for socialization and learning about sexuality.

Third, through elders and religious leaders. Elders and religious figures play a central role in reinforcing community expectations and moral codes around sexuality. Elders transmit traditional values, emphasizing abstinence before marriage, respect for cultural rituals, and adherence to gender roles. Religious leaders complement this by promoting abstinence and condemning contraceptive use, framing it as immoral or contrary to cultural and spiritual teachings. These voices serve as authoritative sources of guidance, shaping youth perceptions of sexuality and contraceptive use, and often discouraging behaviors deemed inappropriate or illegitimate.

Social norms, youth identity, and contraceptive use in Karamoja

This study also explored how social norms and youth identity influence young people’s attitudes, perceptions, and behaviors regarding sexuality and contraceptive use. Among Karamojong, several norms were identified that applied across age groups, while others were specific to youth. Norms directed at young people governed the construction of youth identity and influenced their engagement with sexuality and contraceptive uptake. Older men and women emphasized that these norms were not static, often contrasting current practices with “the olden days” to highlight shifts over time. Participants distinguished between active, dormant, and dead norms, reflecting varying levels of relevance and enforcement. Many norms around sexuality were contradictory: while discouraging sexual activity among youth, they simultaneously encouraged early marriage. Below, we describe the most dominant norms influencing contraceptive uptake among Karamojong youth and the extent to which they were endorsed by different stakeholders. For the most part, these norms were controversial and inconsistently applied.

No sex before marriage: contradictions and inconsistent application to different youth categories

One of the most debated social norms regarding sexuality among the youths in Karamoja was the expectation that they do not practice sexual activity before marriage. Older men and women overwhelmingly emphasized that among the Karamojong, it was not acceptable for young people to have sex before marriage. Pre-marital sex was prohibited traditionally, and those found engaging in it faced harsh and severe punishment.“If anyone is caught having sex before marriage, such a person is punished heavily by beating him up in front of the community by fellow youth during “Ametho”., and they pay many cows (50+) to the girl’s family. The girls are always forced to marry whoever has slept with them, KII, an elder in Lorengechora.

“If anyone is caught having sex before marriage, such a person is punished heavily by beating him up in front of the community by fellow youth during “Ametho”., and they pay many cows (50+) to the girl’s family. The girls are always forced to marry whoever has slept with them, KII, an elder in Lorengechora.

Furthermore, premarital sex was more restricted among girls and only allowed after bride price was paid. While boys were materially fined or physically punished for engaging in premarital sex, girls were not subjected to direct corporal punishment. Instead, the sanction for girls operated through social expectation: once sexual relations occurred, girls were expected—and often compelled—to marry the boy involved. In this sense, girls were positioned as passive actors in the disciplinary process, with their agency curtailed by the obligation to transition immediately into marriage. Thus, although boys bore the immediate punishments, girls carried the longer-term consequence of being forced into marriage, reflecting a gendered asymmetry in how sexual transgressions were managed.“The family of the boy pay fines to the family of the girl for the son using their girl without paying her bride price, a cow is slaughtered for elders for cleansing” KII older woman, Nanduget.“You cannot have sex with someone’s daughter before marrying her. It’s punishable by beating you when are undressed. In addition, you pay cows to the girl’s family.” FGD, young men 15–24 years Lorengechora.

Similarly, young women emphasized the value associated with avoiding sex before marriage, further indicating the gender disparity in this expectation. They stated that girls should avoid sex before marriage to avoid losing value and getting ridiculed.

However, young people, elders and parents acknowledged that the norms restricting premarital sex among youth, over time had changed. For instance, young men and women (15–24 years) reported that this norm is more relevant and applied in rural areas than among the urban-based Karamojong, and that it applied more among the less educated than the educated. Rural youth and those with little or no schooling are still closely regulated by parents and community members, whereas urban educated youth (secondary level and above) are less bound by these restrictions. The latter often have access to mobile phones, which they use to communicate with their partners secretly, making parental monitoring more difficult. In rural settings, however, young people expressed that using contraception would expose them to community censure, since it would signal premarital sexual activity. As one younger man (15–24 years, Nadunget) explained:“It is always those who are putting on trousers [urban exposed] that are free to use [contraceptives], but those from the village would not feel free… if they ever hear that there are contraceptives being used… like use of a condom on someone’s daughter, it will become a very big problem. Here at the sub county [more urban area], it’s a different scenario—the girls and boys at the sub county are very comfortable.” FGD younger men (15024) years, Nadunget subcounty.

Pre-marital rape: endorsed as a legitimate strategy to get a marriage partner

Although sex before marriage was generally considered unacceptable, premarital rape was reported as an accepted practice when committed by a young man with the intention of securing a wife. Findings revealed a culture of organized rape among male youth aged 18 years and older, in which groups of young men systematically waylaid their female target and assaulted her. Within the community, this was regarded as a legitimate dating and engagement strategy, endorsed by both men and women.

Such incidents were typically planned for occasions when girls were vulnerable, such as Sundays after church, during trips to fetch water, or on market days. If a girl resisted a boy’s advances, he would often organize peers to isolate and rape her. Contraceptives, particularly condoms, were not used in these scenarios, as this would undermine the purpose of the act and could even result in punishment by elders. As one young man explained:“You cannot dare use something that can distract conception like a condom. Using condoms at this point of engagement sex is punishable.” — FGD younger men, 15–24 years, Lorengedwat.

“You cannot dare use something that can distract conception like a condom. Using condoms at this point of engagement sex is punishable.” — FGD younger men, 15–24 years, Lorengedwat.

After such an incident, the boy, his peers, and his family would inform the girl’s family, and elders would endorse the act as a form of marriage engagement. The girl was then handed over to the boy, since a girl who had already had sex was not permitted to remain in her parents’ home. As one older man stated:“You cannot allow a girl whom you know has already had sex to stay home with you. You must marry her off to the boy who slept with her.” — FGD older men, 25 + years, Lorengechora.

“You cannot allow a girl whom you know has already had sex to stay home with you. You must marry her off to the boy who slept with her.” — FGD older men, 25 + years, Lorengechora.

Parents and community leaders emphasized that the intention was not to dishonor the girl but to initiate her into marriage. For many young women, this practice marked the beginning of married life.

Early and forced marriages: parental conflicts and power dynamics

According to participants, youth aged 15 years and older were free to participate in courtship and marriage. Early marriage was widely valued, with decisions by boys or girls in their teens often receiving parental endorsement. Virginity was particularly prized among young women, as parents noted that virgins attracted higher dowries, a view confirmed by youth and community leaders. This norm promoted marrying girls off while still young and likely virgins.

Fathers were reported to be stronger supporters of early marriage than mothers, largely because cattle are a symbol of masculinity in Karamoja and dowry payments increase herd size. Mothers, on the other hand, often felt girls should wait until their late teens to mature before marriage. However, women had little authority once a father decided to marry off his daughter. As one participant explained:“If the mother of the girl refuses, the father takes to aggression saying he is the father of the family and the wife must comply.” — FGD older women, 25 + years, Lorengechora.

“If the mother of the girl refuses, the father takes to aggression saying he is the father of the family and the wife must comply.” — FGD older women, 25 + years, Lorengechora.

Young girls described how their fathers’ approval was decisive, leaving them with little control over whom to marry. Fathers often prioritized dowry size, marrying daughters to the highest bidder regardless of age. One young woman noted:“Whichever husband your parents give you, you go by him whether old or young. It’s even worse when they see an old man with a lot of cattle, they will accept him for you.” — FGD younger women, 15–24 years, Nadunget.

“Whichever husband your parents give you, you go by him whether old or young. It’s even worse when they see an old man with a lot of cattle, they will accept him for you.” — FGD younger women, 15–24 years, Nadunget.

In some cases, girls were removed from existing marriages if another suitor later offered more cattle. As one older woman explained:“Girls are a source of wealth… Sometimes even if the girl is married, and another man comes with more cows, the girl will be forced to marry that one. The mother and the girls are not involved because such decisions are not for women.” — FGD older women, 25 + years, Lorengechora.

“Girls are a source of wealth… Sometimes even if the girl is married, and another man comes with more cows, the girl will be forced to marry that one. The mother and the girls are not involved because such decisions are not for women.” — FGD older women, 25 + years, Lorengechora.

Cultural norms and traditional methods of family planning

In Karamoja, family planning is largely practiced through traditional methods that are celebrated as safe, culturally compatible, and free of side effects. Abstinence after childbirth was common, with men leaving for the kraal (an enclosure where herds of cattle are kept, usually far from home in grazing areas where herders can find access to water and pasture for the animals for extended periods and it serves as a safe place for their management and protection). As one older woman explained,“Our men would go to the kraal and spend two years. That’s our family planning method. It has no sickness [side effects], and is appreciated by our culture” (IDI, older woman, 25 + years, Nadunget).

“Our men would go to the kraal and spend two years. That’s our family planning method. It has no sickness [side effects], and is appreciated by our culture” (IDI, older woman, 25 + years, Nadunget).

Younger men even likened this to modern injectables, noting,“This is the same as giving injection for a specific period” (FGD, younger men, 15–24 years, Nadunget).

“This is the same as giving injection for a specific period” (FGD, younger men, 15–24 years, Nadunget).

Women also delayed sexual activity until children reached certain milestones, such as lifting a heavy jerrican of water:“If your child can lift a three to five litre jerrican of water…that means you can now start sexual activity” (FGD, older women, 25 + years, Lorengedwat).

“If your child can lift a three to five litre jerrican of water…that means you can now start sexual activity” (FGD, older women, 25 + years, Lorengedwat).

Men sometimes spaced births by marrying additional wives, with one participant remarking,“Another way to do family planning is to get another wife…allow the child to reach at least 2 years of age” (FGD, younger men, 15–24 years, Nadunget).

“Another way to do family planning is to get another wife…allow the child to reach at least 2 years of age” (FGD, younger men, 15–24 years, Nadunget).

Yet some young men acknowledged the fragility of these practices. With kraal traditions declining and insecurity limiting men’s mobility, abstinence was increasingly difficult:“Not everyone is comfortable with abstaining…Going to the kraal does not happen now since cows are few and there is insecurity” (FGD, younger men, 15–24 years, Lorengechora).

Stigma and misconceptions surrounding modern contraceptive use in Karamoja

In Karamoja, modern contraceptive use is heavily stigmatized, with collective narratives framing it as inappropriate or even dangerous. Young people often described contraceptives as “addictive,” likening them to alcohol, and fear that once a girl begins using them, she will continue into marriage, creating conflict with her husband. As one young man explained,“When a girl starts family planning, she cannot stop; it becomes a habit like drinking alcohol” (FGD, young men 15–24, Lerengedwat).

“When a girl starts family planning, she cannot stop; it becomes a habit like drinking alcohol” (FGD, young men 15–24, Lerengedwat).

Similarly, young women expressed concern that“I fear starting family planning because once you begin, you will not stop—it becomes addictive. Even after marriage, you will continue using it, and to avoid problems or conflict with your husband, it is better not to start at all” (FGD, young women 15–24, Lerengedwat).

“I fear starting family planning because once you begin, you will not stop—it becomes addictive. Even after marriage, you will continue using it, and to avoid problems or conflict with your husband, it is better not to start at all” (FGD, young women 15–24, Lerengedwat).

These perceptions reinforce a broader social norm that positions family planning as incompatible with marital expectations, leaving women caught between unsustainable traditional practices and strong stigma against modern contraception.

Communal sleeping and gendered control

In Karamoja, boys and girls traditionally sleep in separate houses (round huts known as manyatas) rather than under the same roof as their parents. Boys are often encouraged to construct their own huts next to their parents’ homes, where they sleep alone. Girls, however, commonly practice communal sleeping, with 5–8 adolescents from different households but within the same village sharing one hut.

Communal sleeping was considered a protective tradition, helping safeguard girls against sexual transgressions and minimizing risks of bringing in boyfriends or being attacked by male predators. Parents closely monitored this arrangement, and if they suspected a girl was engaging with boyfriends, she was moved back to the main house to sleep with her parents:“When you suspect or hear rumors that she has started seeing boys, it’s time to bring her back to the main house to sleep with you to avoid chances of her escaping to go see her boyfriend at night, exposing her to premarital sex” (KII, female parent, Nadunget).

“When you suspect or hear rumors that she has started seeing boys, it’s time to bring her back to the main house to sleep with you to avoid chances of her escaping to go see her boyfriend at night, exposing her to premarital sex” (KII, female parent, Nadunget).

Boys, however, did not receive the same level of control or protection. Even when parents suspected premarital sexual activity, boys continued sleeping in their separate huts, highlighting a gendered difference in parental oversight“Boys are free to continue sleeping in the communal house even if you suspect them. They have their plans with peers—what if they are planning engagement sex (rape) to get a wife? My boy should continue sleeping there; it’s good they learn from fellow boys.” (KII, male parent, Lorengedwat).

“Boys are free to continue sleeping in the communal house even if you suspect them. They have their plans with peers—what if they are planning engagement sex (rape) to get a wife? My boy should continue sleeping there; it’s good they learn from fellow boys.” (KII, male parent, Lorengedwat).

Fertility as a measure of respect, honor, and social value

In Karamoja, fertility is central to women’s social status and marital respect. Bearing many children is celebrated as a source of pride, with women reporting that it enhances their standing in marriage and the wider community. In polygamous households, wives compete to produce more children, as the one with the largest number is perceived to earn greater respect. As one participant explained,“Children are the pride of a woman and the more children you have the more love you gain from the man” (FGD, older women 25+, Lorengedwat).

“Children are the pride of a woman and the more children you have the more love you gain from the man” (FGD, older women 25+, Lorengedwat).

Childbearing is also closely tied to the cultural practice of bride price, where producing children is seen as a way of “rewarding” the husband for marriage payments. As one woman noted,“Having children is a reward for your husband for paying bride price” (FGD, older women 25+, Lorengedwat).

“Having children is a reward for your husband for paying bride price” (FGD, older women 25+, Lorengedwat).

Community expectations further reinforce these norms. Young married women face strong pressure from peers, parents, and in-laws to bear children immediately after marriage, with family planning only considered acceptable after a woman has already had two to three children. As one younger woman explained,“Young people cannot use family planning unless one already has 2–3 children” (FGD, younger women 15–24, Lorengechora).

“Young people cannot use family planning unless one already has 2–3 children” (FGD, younger women 15–24, Lorengechora).

These norms highlight how fertility is socially constructed as a measure of respect, honor, and family value, while limiting women’s autonomy over reproductive choices.

Cultural norms and sanctions for sexual misconduct and contraceptive use

Ametho: sanctions for sexual misconduct in Karamoja

Participants identified the Ametho—the main traditional mechanism for disciplining community members who violate societal laws and norms—as a central means of correcting unacceptable youth behavior, particularly around sexual misconduct. The Ametho, usually organized by a council of elders, involves a public punishment event where offenders are caned, often by fellow energetic youths and men in the community. Although any individual who fails to conform to acceptable community behaviors may be punished through the Ametho, the primary culprits are typically youth and women.

A wide range of violations are punishable under the Ametho. In relation to sexuality and contraceptive use, it is applied to behaviors such as engaging in premarital sex, cohabiting without paying dowry, and impregnating a girl before marriage. In cases of pregnancy before marriage, punishments can be particularly severe, involving public beatings by peers and heavy fines of no less than 50 cows—cattle that are not considered part of the bride price to be paid later. As one young man explained:“If you make a girl pregnant before marrying her, or you are caught having premarital sex, you will be forced to pay a high bride price, almost double (many animals/cows, 50+), then pay school fees compensation even though the girl has not gone to school.” (FGD younger men, 15–24 years, Lorengechora).

“If you make a girl pregnant before marrying her, or you are caught having premarital sex, you will be forced to pay a high bride price, almost double (many animals/cows, 50+), then pay school fees compensation even though the girl has not gone to school.” (FGD younger men, 15–24 years, Lorengechora).

Condom and contraceptive use as socially penalized

In Karamoja, condom and contraceptive use is strongly regulated by cultural norms that equate fertility with masculinity and marital legitimacy. Elders and youth described sperm as proof of male virility, with condom use condemned as “wasting” or “murdering” potential children. As one young man explained,“I can’t use a condom, it’s like a murder case because I will be wasting what would result into a child” (FGD younger men, 15–24 years, Lorengechora).

“I can’t use a condom, it’s like a murder case because I will be wasting what would result into a child” (FGD younger men, 15–24 years, Lorengechora).

Within marriage, condom use was considered inappropriate and a sign of mistrust, with participants noting,“There is no need to use a condom. I am going to my wife—why would I use a condom? Using a condom is like stealing” (FGD younger men, 15–24 years, Lorengechora).

“There is no need to use a condom. I am going to my wife—why would I use a condom? Using a condom is like stealing” (FGD younger men, 15–24 years, Lorengechora).

These beliefs were reinforced through sanctions and penalties. Condom use during engagement sex was vilified and punished with physical beatings, payments to the girl’s family, or ritual cleansing ceremonies involving the slaughter of a cow. Newly married men discovered to have used condoms faced cleansing rituals for “wasting sperm”. Women who used contraceptives were labeled as prostitutes, and condoms were described as “ugly” and associated with prostitution. As one older man explained,“Condom is something which is ugly to use; it is something the community takes as prostitution” (FGD older men, 25+, Loregendwat).

“Condom is something which is ugly to use; it is something the community takes as prostitution” (FGD older men, 25+, Loregendwat).

Religious leaders reinforced abstinence as the expected norm for unmarried youth, further discouraging condom use.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the existing social norms around sexuality and contraceptive use among young people in Karamoja, a remote and poor region in northeastern Uganda. We used qualitative methods to generate data from young people, community leaders, and parents.

We found remarkable contradictions and inconsistencies between the social expectations around youth sexuality and the reality of their sexual lives and contraceptive use. It was ironic that while the society generally articulates narratives that prohibit sex before marriage, particularly among the girls, it was considered culturally normal to marry off girls at an early age. The legal age of marriage in Uganda is 18 years, but the law is rarely enforced and the inclination to cultural relativism and identity are an important ideal in many Ugandan settings. It is estimated that 34% of Ugandan girls get married before the age of 18 years, with Karamoja having the highest prevalence of child marriages, accounting for 50% [25]. Among the Karamojong people, girls have no control over this practice and any resistance from them is seen by the family and the community as disrespectful and such a girl is termed a “rebel” and can be disowned by her parents and community. However, we noted that mothers held views that conflicted with fathers regarding child marriage, with mothers, especially the younger generation, being against it.

We found that pre-marital rape was widely endorsed as a legitimate strategy for getting a marriage partner in Karamoja by both males and females, young people and adults. Engagement sex (sex that defines the start of a marriage) has been described as a common practice in this region [26], and other parts of SSA [27]. These studies have reported that this practice involves bride capture (forcefully carrying away bride when she is out of home) for the purpose of sex and marriage. Our study adds more insight into this, suggesting that rape may precede bride capture but once it has occurred, the girl in question is presumed to have consented to marriage and boy reports the incident to his parents to commence legitimate marriage negotiations with the parents of the victim.

Stigma and misconceptions surrounding modern contraceptive use in Karamoja were strong, often linked to fears of side effects. These findings mirror evidence across SSA, where myths, rumors, and cultural narratives consistently undermine uptake among youth [28], Comparative studies in South Africa, and Kenya, report similar patterns of fear, misinformation, and moral framing that discourage contraceptive use, reinforcing gendered expectations and limiting young people’s autonomy [29, 30]. The belief that contraceptives can be addictive is an interesting finding. In Karamoja, contraceptive use was frequently described as “addictive,” with young men and women likening it to alcohol consumption. This framing positioned contraceptives not as a health resource but as a dangerous habit, reinforcing fear and discouraging uptake. Similar misconceptions have been documented elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa. In South Africa, Jonas et al.2022 reported that adolescent girls and young women avoided contraceptives due to rumors and myths linking them to infertility and immorality, showing how misinformation becomes embedded in collective discourse and shapes behavior [28]. In Kenya, Mwaisaka et al.2020 found that young people perceived modern contraceptives as harmful, with fears of infertility and health complications discouraging use [29]. A systematic review by Bain et al. 2021. further confirmed that across the region, stigma and misconceptions—often sustained by cultural narratives and moral framing—remain consistent barriers to uptake [30]. This discourages potential users, since contraceptive use is stigmatized in almost every society [31–33].

Another unanticipated finding from our study was that among young people in Karamojong, condom use was widely condemned, and in some instances, known users or possessors were heavily penalized by the community and family. Condom use to prevent HIV and STIs, and as a form of contraceptive to prevent unintended pregnancies, has been the subject of vast research in many settings, and common barriers to their use include negative attitudes of the users, concerns about pleasure and size, inaccessibility, ignorance on use, and fear of risk of breaking [30, 34–36]. However, to our knowledge, no study has so far documented the existence of sanctions and penalties on condom users, even amongst some religious groups that are traditionally known to hold strong negative views against condom use. Among the Karamojong people, condoms use is presumed to conflict with the norms of trust, love for a partner, and expectations to prove one’s fertility. In particular, their use is considered to be a waste of sperms which would have fathered a human being, and therefore its users deserve heavy societal penalties and sanctions. Societal sanctions against condom use by young people make them particularly vulnerable to risky sexual behavior and reproductive health choices [37]. In our opinion, there is need for further studies on the phenomenon of condom criminalization in this community to establish why the wider community would have such vested interest in young people’s condom use.

Many studies in SSA [38–40] and Uganda [41–43] tend to depict men as reluctant users or supporters of their partners’ contraceptive use, and that generally they feel entitled to their wives’ sexual and reproductive capabilities. However, the narratives of the Karamojong young men seem to construct a contrasting and positive narrative about the role of men in preventing unplanned pregnancies. Our findings suggest that the Karamojong men value child spacing and believe that it was the man’s responsibility to ensure their wives did not conceive another child if the other child was still considered young. As such, these men were willing to avoid sexual contact with their breast-feeding wives as much as possible to allow their wives to breastfeed and raise the newborn child. This perspective provides a great opportunity to shift the conversation around social norms and reproduction among young men, by articulating a progressive and non-stereotypical role of men in family planning. We also found that men who did not respect this norm were easily blamed by their peers and family for not being a ‘serious man’, suggesting that there might be an existing norm among men that upholds child spacing as an important ideal and measure of manliness.

The finding that mothers of young children did everything possible to avoid sexual encounters with their husbands to prevent unintended pregnancy if their current children were still young, including separating houses, suggests that women in Karamoja are not passive victims of uncontrollable male sexual appetite and crave for children. We heard many practices in the narratives that illustrated women’s active role in determining when to have the next pregnancy, for instance by separating houses with their husbands or by returning to her parents’ home for as long as she felt she was not ready for the next child. Men often avoided to pressure their lactating wives into sex and instead waited until the woman herself looked for her husband to conceive another child. Similar practices of postpartum sexual abstinence have been documented across SSA. For instance, in Eswatini, cultural norms prescribe prolonged abstinence after childbirth, with women often delaying sexual relations until they feel ready for another pregnancy [44]. Likewise, comparative studies across 27 African countries show that ethnic heritage strongly influences the duration of postpartum abstinence, which serves to extend birth intervals and reflects women’s active role in reproductive decision-making [45]. These parallels highlight that while abstinence may sometimes be culturally enforced, in Karamoja it also represents a deliberate strategy by women to assert control over the timing of subsequent pregnancies.

Our findings suggest that the Edonga and Ametho – the two main cultural practices traditionally used by the Karamojong people to educate young people about sexuality send potentially mixed messages to the young people about social expectations. On the one hand, the Edonga festival promotes risky sexual behavior, under the official watchful eyes of the elders and parents who participate in sanctioning and organizing these events. On the other hand, the Ametho public punishment of wrong doers serves as stern criticism and disapproval of those who engage in sexual habits perceived to be unacceptable in the society. While the pro Ametho argument is not necessarily difficult to understand, the Edonga festival comes across as inconsistent and hypocritical, because there seems to be an attempt to combine restrictions on youth sexuality with opportunities for sexual exploration and pleasure. Given the diversity in the norms and value expectations of these two cultural practices, it can be a complex situation for young people to carefully navigate both value systems. Comparable cultural sanctions on youth sexuality have been documented elsewhere in Africa. In Eswatini, postpartum sexual abstinence is culturally enforced, and women who transgress face stigma and disapproval from their communities [44]. Ethnographic studies further highlight how religion, culture, and law converge across SSA to regulate sexual behavior through sanctions and moral codes [46].

Strength and limitations

This study provides unique insights into how social norms shape sexuality and contraceptive use among youth aged 15–24 years in Karamoja, a pastoral community spanning rural and peri‑urban areas. By including both male and female youth and drawing perspectives from younger and older men and women across different districts, it captured authentic voices and revealed how norms vary by age, gender, and context. The use of FGDs and IDIs provided rich, nuanced data, while conducting interviews in the local language with community‑based research assistants enhanced trust and encouraged open discussion of sensitive issues. At the same time, limitations include the potential for social desirability bias, and the cross‑sectional design, which restricts understanding of how norms evolve over time. Future research could strengthen validity by using longitudinal and mixed‑methods approaches, expanding to other regions for comparison, and employing bilingual transcription and community feedback sessions to minimize translation bias.

Conclusion

Young people in Karamoja face contradictory norms and value systems that restrict healthy sexuality and discourage contraceptive use, while simultaneously exposing them to risk through practices like Edonga and Ametho. Heavy sanctions, stigma, and misconceptions further undermine uptake of contraceptives. Addressing these contradictions requires co‑created interventions with elders, kraal leaders, opinion leaders, parents, women, and youth themselves to harmonize messages, dismantle harmful norms, and promote informed, healthier choices.
