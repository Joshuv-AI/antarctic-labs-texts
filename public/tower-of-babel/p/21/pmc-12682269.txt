Identification of photoperiod as a regulator of dopamine-mediated behavior in female mice
Authors: Beimnet B Kassaye, Alexis N Jameson, Katherine Moore, Douglas G McMahon, Brad A Grueter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotoperiod is the primary environmental cue that regulates changes in behavior across seasons. Previously, we have shown that photoperiod has sex-specific effects on synaptic dopamine dynamics in the nucleus accumbens (NAc). Further, evidence suggests that the dopamine transporter (DAT) is a potential locus of action for the sex-specific effects of photoperiod on NAc dopamine. The NAc is a critical node within the reward circuit that brings motivation to action, and changes to NAc dopamine dynamics at the synapse can result in robust changes in behaviors. Cocaine is a psychostimulant that targets monoamine transporters, including DAT, and generates robust behavioral effects. Thus, using cocaine-mediated behavior, we can determine whether photoperiod impacts DAT function and dopamine physiology. Here, using male and female mice we examined the effect of seasonally relevant photoperiods on DAT function in the NAc and dopamine-dependent behavior. We found that females raised in Short, winter-like photoperiod have blunted cocaine-induced hyperlocomotion. Conversely, females raised in Long, summer-like photoperiod exhibit greater DA release and cocaine-mediated DAT inhibition while we observe decreased sensitivity to cocaine-associated learning. The combined work presented here provides evidence that photoperiod has differential, female-specific effects on NAc DAT function and DAT-mediated behaviors.Keywords: Photoperiod, Dopamine, Dopamine transporter (DAT), Nucleus accumbens, Cocaine

Introduction

Organisms entrain their internal clocks to seasonal changes in the length of daylight, or photoperiod, to increase their adaptive fitness and maximize their survival (Wood and Loudon, 2014). Photoperiod is the primary environmental cue that governs changes in behavior across seasons (Bronson, 1988; Rosen et al., 1990; Magnusson, 2003; Melrose, 2015; Devore et al., 2018). The time of year in temperate zones is encoded by photoperiod, where days are longer in summer months and shorter in winter months (Khavrus and Shelevytsky, 2012). Evidence suggests that photoperiod influences components of the mesolimbic dopamine pathway, an important component of the reward system responsible for motivated behaviors (Eisenberg et al., 2010; Goda et al., 2015; Siemann et al., 2020; Jameson et al., 2023). However, it has yet to be shown that changes to the dopamine system by photoperiod manifest in observable differences in behavior. Understanding how photoperiod-driven differences in the mesolimbic dopamine system impact behavior is a critical step in elucidating the chronobiological components of neuropsychiatric disorders including seasonal affective disorder, major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder and substance use disorder (Siemann et al., 2020; Hersey et al., 2023; Dollish et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024; Stewart and Albrecht, 2025).

The mesolimbic dopamine pathway consists of dopaminergic projections from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) to the nucleus accumbens (NAc) (Nestler and Carlezon, 2006; Turner et al., 2018). After release, dopamine is rapidly taken back up into the axon terminal by the dopamine transporter (DAT) and repackaged for future release (Nolan et al., 2020). DAT function, therefore, is the primary driver of the temporal dynamics of synaptic dopamine release (Jones et al., 1998). Changes in DAT function can powerfully influence behavior—psychostimulants such as cocaine alter DAT function and cause a robust hyperlocomotor activity and conditioned place preference (Ritz et al., 1966; WL et al., 1989; Giros et al., 1996; Rocha, 2003; Medvedev et al., 2005; Hall et al., 2009; Cagniard et al., 2014; Calipari et al., 2017; Drake and Scott, 2018). Photoperiod has also been shown to influence DAT in both rodent (Sorg et al., 2011; Jameson et al., 2023, 2024) and human studies (Neumeister et al., 2001). However, photoperiod's influence on neural circuit mechanisms and their resulting behavioral response remains underexplored.

Here, we provide evidence for sex-specific effects of photoperiod on the sensitivity of the dopamine system, which is important for reward-based learning involving salient and novel stimuli. Using cocaine's properties as a strong psychostimulant, we report the effect of photoperiod on physiological and behavioral response to a stimulus. We found that females raised in Short, winter-like photoperiod had a blunted locomotor response to cocaine, and that females raised in Long, summer-like photoperiod were more sensitive to DAT inhibition by cocaine in the NAc. To assess photoperiod influence on saliency and associated learning, we utilized a cocaine dose response curve measuring conditioned place preference (CPP) to test sensitivity of the dopaminergic system. Interestingly, Long photoperiod female mice showed greater locomotor response relative to Short photoperiod but lacked cocaine-induced CPP. Together, our findings establish that photoperiod has sex-specific effects on dopamine physiology including DAT function and cocaine-dependent behaviors and further implicate NAc DAT as an integral component of the neural basis of behavioral photoperiodism.

Methods

Animals

Male and female C3Hf+/+ mice between postnatal days 50 (P50) and P90 were used. While most typical lab strains of mice do not produce physiological levels of melatonin, a hormone that is necessary for aligning the central biological clock to environmental photoperiod (Goto et al., 1989; Tosini and Menaker, 1998; Liu et al., 2016; Tordjman et al., 2017). C3Hf+/+ mice synthesize melatonin and are genetically modified to lack the retinal degeneration alleles of the parent C3H strain. Mice were bred, raised, and group-housed in light tight boxes set to either Short, winter-like (8 h light:16 h dark), Equinox (12 h light:12 h dark), or Long, summer-like (16 h light:8 h dark) photoperiod. To avoid time-of-day effects across groups, experiments were performed during the mid-light phase, or middle 2 h of the light phase, for each group. With zeitgeber time (ZT) 12 denoting lights off, experiments were conducted at ZT8 for Short, ZT6 for Equinox, and ZT4 for Long photoperiod mice. Experiments were performed in accordance with the Vanderbilt University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and National Institutes of Health guidelines.

Fast-Scan Cyclic Voltammetry (FSCV)

As described in (Yorgason et al., 2011), ex vivo FSCV was used to assess dopamine release and uptake dynamics in the NAc core of Short, Equinox, and Long photoperiod mice (n = 4–7 mice per sex and photoperiod group, using 1 brain slice per mouse). Mice were killed at the middle of the light cycle for each photoperiod as described above, and the brain was rapidly removed. Using a Leica Vibratome, 250 μm-thick sagittal sections containing the NAc core were collected from whole brain tissue in oxygenated (95 % O2; 5 % CO2) artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing (in mM): 126 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 2.4 CaCl2, 1.2 MgCl2, 25 NaHCO3, 11 glucose, 0.4 L-ascorbic acid, and pH adjusted to 7.4. Slices were transferred to a chamber containing oxygenated aCSF. All experiments were performed using a Scientifica SliceScope Pro System in 32 °C aCSF with a flow rate of 2 ml/min. The carbon fiber microelectrode (100−200 μM length, 7 μM radius) and bipolar stimulating electrode were placed in close proximity within the NAc core. A single electrical pulse (750 μA, 4ms, monophasic) was applied to the tissue every 4 min to evoke dopamine release. We also evoked dopamine release using a train of 5 pulses at 5 Hz, 10 Hz, or 20 Hz (750 μA, 4ms, monophasic). Extracellular dopamine was recorded by applying a triangular waveform (−0.4 to +1.2 to −0.4 V vs Ag/AgCl, 400 V/s).

For pharmacological experiments using cocaine HCl (Tocris Bioscience/Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, MN), peak evoked dopamine release was collected in aCSF until a stable baseline was established (3 collections with <10 % variability) using a single electrical pulse every 4 min. Cocaine was bath applied to the slice at 300 nM, 1 μM, and 3 μM concentrations. We collected peak evoked dopamine release at each concentration until stable responding was achieved (3 collections with <10 % variability) before moving onto the next highest concentration.

FSCV data analysis

Demon voltammetry and analysis software was used to analyze all FSCV data (Yorgason et al., 2011). Data collected using single electrical pulse were modeled using Michaelis-Menten kinetics using the kinetic analysis tool. By using Michaelis-Menten kinetic analysis, we derived parameters such as the peak dopamine concentration released following stimulation (amplitude of signal) and maximal rate of dopamine uptake by DAT (Vmax). As described in (Calipari et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2001; Yorgason et al., 2011), Km from the Michaelis-Menten equation represents the affinity for dopamine binding to DAT. According to standard FSCV analysis methods (Yorgason et al., 2011) and based on previous research on the affinity of dopamine for the DAT (Wu et al., 2001), we set the Km parameter to 160 nM for each recording and allowed Vmax to vary to determine baseline Vmax. For experiments using cocaine, we determined a baseline Vmax for each recording once stable baseline responding was achieved in aCSF as described above. For subsequent drug concentrations, we held the baseline Vmax constant and allowed Km to vary to determine apparent Km. Apparent Km is the Michaelis-Menten constant as observed under conditions, such as competitive inhibition, that would impede determining its true value. Measuring apparent Km allows determination of the sensitivity of DAT to pharmacological competitive inhibition by cocaine. Raw data was calibrated in each data file prior to Michaelis-Menten modeling. Recording electrodes were calibrated by recording the peak current (nA) to a known concentration of dopamine (3 μM) via flow-injection system, and we used these values to convert current to dopamine concentration.

Behavioral testing

Open field & thigmotaxis

At a time corresponding to the middle of the light period for each photoperiod condition, mice were placed into a novel open field chamber for 20 min and distance traveled was measured using Ethovision software, as described in (Joffe et al., 2017, 2018; Kashima and Grueter, 2017; Turner et al., 2021). To assess thigmotaxis, the open field chamber was split into two zones, center and surround, which were equal in area. Time spent in each of these zones was also measured using Ethovision software.

Cocaine-induced hyperlocomotion

The following day, mice were placed into the same open field chamber. After 20 min, mice were briefly removed to administer cocaine (15 mg/kg cocaine HCl, intraperitoneal injection) then placed back into the open field chamber for another 60 min. Distance traveled was measured throughout the experiment using Ethovision software.

Cocaine place conditioning

Cocaine-place conditioning was conducted in open field activity chambers (ENV-510, Med Associates) equipped with two chamber place preference inserts (ENV-512, Med Associates) and infrared beams that allowed tracking the position of the mice. The two chambers had different grid patterns for tactile stimulus, and the walls were fitted with different patterns to include visual cues. Conditioning for saline/cocaine and pre-/post-tests were all performed at the mid-day time point for each photoperiod condition.

Day 1 of the place conditioning assay was the pretest. Mice were placed in the 2-chamber apparatus with the barrier removed allowing the mice to freely roam the chambers for 20 min. Time spent on each side was calculated and mice that showed high preference for either chamber (70 % of the 20-min duration) in the pretest were excluded from the study. On conditioning days mice were injected with ascending doses of cocaine (day 2; 2.5 mg/kg, day 5; 7.5 mg/kg and day 8; 15 mg/kg) or saline (days 3, 6, and 9) via intraperitoneal injection as reported in (Lim et al., 2012). For cocaine/saline pairing, the barrier between chambers was closed and mice were placed in the chamber immediately following saline/cocaine administration and allowed to roam that chamber for 20 min. Distance traveled was measured during the 20-min exposure. On test day, mice were allowed to freely roam both chambers for 20 min and their position was recorded. In total, there were four testing days: a pretest and a testing day per conditioning cycle (cocaine dose). See Fig. 4A for CPP experimental design.

For comparison across photoperiods, the change in preference for the cocaine-paired side after conditioning was calculated as the difference between the time spent during the pretest and the time spent during the respective test days. Cumulative distance traveled by the mice each day during the conditioning days was also collected.

Data analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9. A two-way ANOVA was used to analyze all voltammetry data. A one-way ANOVA was also used to analyze data from the open field paradigm including distance traveled and thigmotaxis. A repeated measures two-way ANOVA was used to analyze overall cocaine-induced locomotor activity. A one-way ANOVA was used to compare total distance traveled in the first 20 min after cocaine injection. Repeated measures two-way ANOVA was used to analyze the raw duration in the cocaine place conditioning while a two-way ANOVA was used to analyze the difference in duration from pretest. Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used for all post-hoc data analysis.

Results

Female mice raised in Long, summer-like photoperiod show higher basal dopamine levels and increased sensitivity to cocaine in the NAc core relative to Short and Equinox mice

We assessed electrically evoked dopamine release in the NAc core of male and female mice raised in Short, Equinox, and Long photoperiods by taking acute brain slices in the middle of the light phase and running fast-scan cyclic voltammetry (Fig. 1A and B). In the females, we found a significant main effect of photoperiod (F(2, 44) = 6.664, P = 0.003) but did not detect a significant effect of cocaine dose (F(3, 44) = 0.5609, P = 0.6437) on dopamine release. Additionally, Tukey's multiple comparisons post-hoc test revealed a significant difference between Short and Long photoperiod in evoked dopamine release (P = 0.0299) where evoked release was enhanced in the Long photoperiod females (Fig. 1C and D). We also found a significant main effect of photoperiod (F(2, 44) = 5.579, P = 0.0069) and cocaine dose (F (3, 44) = 10.84, P < 0.0001) on the magnitude of DAT inhibition by cocaine, as measured by Km, in the females using a two-way ANOVA. Tukey's multiple comparisons post-hoc test revealed a significant difference between photoperiod groups at the 10 μM cocaine condition as follows: Short vs Long (P = 0.0005), Equinox vs Long (P = 0.0149) (Fig. 1E). No significant effect of photoperiod (F(2, 40) = 0.9251, P = 0.4048) or dose (F(3, 40) = 1.071, P = 0.3723) on cocaine action on dopamine release was found in males (Fig. 1F and G). We also did not find a significant effect of photoperiod (F(2, 40) = 1.318, P = 0.2791) but did find a significant effect of dose (F(3, 40) = 25.82, P < 0.0001) on DAT inhibition by cocaine in males (Fig. 1F and H). These data indicate that photoperiod modulation of DAT sensitivity to cocaine is female-specific, where Long photoperiod females have enhanced sensitivity to DAT inhibition by cocaine.

Female mice raised in Short, winter-like photoperiod exhibit reduced locomotor activity and center time upon initial exposure to a novel environment

The open field test revealed sex-specific effects of photoperiod on locomotor activity and thigmotaxis. We found that photoperiod had a significant effect on general locomotor activity in female mice (F(2, 23) = 12.22, P = 0.0002) using a one-way ANOVA. Tukey's post-hoc multiple comparisons test revealed there was a significant difference between females raised in Short and Long photoperiod (P = 0.0048) as well as Short and Equinox photoperiod (P = 0.0002) where locomotor activity was decreased in Short photoperiod females (Fig. 2A and B). We found no significant effect of photoperiod on distance traveled in the open field test (F(2, 22) = 0.4249, P = 0.6591) in male mice (Fig. 2A and C). We also found a significant effect of photoperiod on thigmotaxis (F(2, 23) = 3.983, P = 0.327), where Tukey's multiple comparisons test revealed that females raised in Short photoperiod had decreased time spent in the center of the chamber compared to Equinox photoperiod (P = 0.026) (Fig. 2D). In the males, there was no effect of photoperiod on thigmotaxis (F(2, 22) = 0.7167, P = 0.4994) (Fig. 2E). Taken together, photoperiod shows a female-specific anxiety-like behavior, where Short photoperiod females show less locomotor activity and center time in a novel environment.

Short, winter-like photoperiod blunts locomotor response to cocaine in female mice

We next tested locomotor response to a single exposure to cocaine. We measured total distance traveled in male and female Short, Equinox, and Long mice before and after 15 mg/kg IP injection of cocaine. In females, using a two-way repeated measures ANOVA, we found a significant effect of photoperiod (F(2, 30) = 4.351, P = 0.0219) and cocaine (F(2.27, 68.10) = 10.80, P < 0.0001) on distance traveled. Tukey's multiple comparison test revealed significant differences between Short and Long photoperiods (P = 0.0076) (Fig. 3A). We also quantified total distance traveled for the first 20 min after cocaine injection. Using a one-way ANOVA we found a significant effect of photoperiod on cocaine-induced locomotor activity within this time frame in female mice (F (2, 30) = 4.081, P = 0.0271). Tukey's multiple comparisons post-hoc test revealed a significant difference between Short and Long mice (P = 0.0205) (Fig. 3B). In males, using a two-way repeated measures ANOVA, while cocaine had a significant effect on locomotor activity (F(2.940, 64.67) = 11.80, P < 0.0001), there was no significant main effect of photoperiod (F(2, 22) = 0.1036, P = 0.902) (Fig. 3E). Additionally, one-way ANOVA revealed no significant effect of photoperiod (F(2, 22) = 0.3765, P = 0.69) on cocaine-induced locomotor activity in the first 20 min after cocaine injection (Fig. 3F). Thus, while the male mice did have increased locomotor activity in response to cocaine, photoperiod did not modulate this behavior.

Considering the differences in locomotor activity (Fig. 2B), we normalized results to baseline locomotor activity of each photoperiod group to address whether photoperiodic differences in psychostimulant-induced hyperlocomotion were due to differences in sensitivity to the drug or due to basal differences in locomotion. We calculated the average distance traveled in a single 5-min bin over the first 20 min of the behavioral paradigm to use as our baseline and measured the percent increase in locomotor activity for each 5-min bin after cocaine injection. In the female mice, using a two-way repeated measures ANOVA on the normalized locomotor activity, we found a significant effect of photoperiod (F(2, 30) = 4.293, P = 0.0229) and cocaine (F(2.503, 75.09) = 9.637, P < 0.0001) as well as a significant interaction effect of cocaine and photoperiod (F(30, 450) = 1.786, P = 0.0073) (Fig. 3C). Additionally, when comparing the average percent increase in locomotor activity in the first 20 min after cocaine injection, using a one-way ANOVA we found a significant effect of photoperiod in the females (F(2, 30) = 4.135, P = 0.0259) (Fig. 3D). In the male mice, using a two-way repeated measures ANOVA we found that, as expected, cocaine had a significant effect (F(2.331, 51.287) = 7.734, P = 0.0007) while there was no significant effect of photoperiod (F(2, 22) = 0.2698, P = 0.766) (Fig. 3G). We also did not find a significant effect of photoperiod when comparing the average percent increase in locomotor activity over the first 20 min after cocaine injection (F(2,25) = 0.4721, P = 0.6292) (Fig. 3H).

Taken together, these results suggest that photoperiod has female-specific effects on basal locomotor activity in a novel environment and on cocaine induced hyperlocomotion, where female mice raised in Short photoperiod show less basal locomotor activity and a blunted locomotor response to cocaine.

Long, summer-like photoperiod attenuates cocaine saliency in female mice

Using ascending doses of cocaine in our place-conditioning protocol, we measured photoperiod-driven sensitivity to cocaine-associated cues. Male and female mice from Short, Equinox or Long photoperiods were conditioned with single IP injections of 2.5, 7.5 and 15 mg/kg cocaine and tested for learned association (Fig. 4A). In female mice, using repeated measures two-way ANOVA, we observed a significant main effect of cocaine dose (F(2.903,116.1) = 27.88, P < 0.0001) on time spent in the cocaine-paired side. Post-hoc Tukey analysis showed significant differences as follows: in the Short photoperiod mice, Pretest vs 7.5 mg/kg (P = 0.0038) and Pretest vs 15 mg/kg (P < 0.0001); and in the Equinox mice Pretest vs 7.5 mg/kg (P = 0.0258) and Pretest vs 15 mg/kg (P = 0.0034). However, the post-hoc analysis did not show any significant differences between Pretest and all three doses of cocaine (all P > 0.06) in Long photoperiod female mice (Fig. 4B). When we compared the female mice across the three photoperiods by looking at the change in preference from the pretest, we saw that both photoperiod (F(2,120) = 3.924, P = 0.0223) and cocaine dose (F(2,120) = 11.53, P < 0.0001) had significant main effects. Post-hoc Tukey analysis showed a significant difference between the Short and Long photoperiods at 15 mg/kg (P = 0.0136) (Fig. 4D). In the male mice, all three photoperiods expressed robust cocaine-induced CPP with cocaine dose having a main effect (F(2.458,63.92) = 24.54, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4C). And our comparison of change in preference from pretest did not find a significant main effect of photoperiod (F(2,78) = 0.7947, P = 0.4553) on cocaine-induced CPP in male mice (Fig. 4E).

In addition to the preference data in the CPP paradigm, we were also able to extract locomotor data. This allowed us to measure the dose-response function for cocaine-induced hyperlocomotion. Complementing prior locomotor results (Fig. 2, Fig. 3), photoperiod had a significant main effect on cocaine-induced hyperlocomotion in female mice (F(2,40) = 14.69, P < 0.0001). We observed decreased basal locomotor activity (Tukey's post-hoc test: Short vs Long, P = 0.0209) and reduced cocaine-induced hyperlocomotion in the Short photoperiod females at 7.5 mg/kg (Tukey's post-hoc test: Short vs Long, P = 0.003 and Short vs Equinox, P = 0.0426) and 15 mg/kg cocaine (Tukey's post-hoc test: Short vs Long, P = 0.0003 and Short vs Equinox, P = 0.0048) (Supplemental Figure A). In the males, photoperiod did not affect cocaine-induced locomotor behavior (F(2,26) = 2.213, P = 0.1296) but we observed a significant main effect of cocaine dose (F(1.472,38.27) = 54.35, P < 0.0001) (Supplemental Figure B).

Taken together, these results suggest that photoperiod exerts female-specific effects on both cocaine-induced hyperlocomotion and the formation of associations with positive stimuli. Specifically, female mice raised under a Short photoperiod exhibit a reduced hyperlocomotor response to cocaine, while those raised under a Long photoperiod display diminished sensitivity to cocaine-associated cues.

Discussion

Here, we show that photoperiod has a sex-specific effect on the mesolimbic dopamine system and dopamine-dependent behavior, where female mice raised under a Short, winter-like photoperiod show blunted hyperlocomotor response to cocaine, and it should be noted that these animals showed less general locomotor activity compared to females in other photoperiods, However, when locomotor response to cocaine was adjusted to baseline locomotor activity, it was clear that the Short photoperiod females still had a blunted locomotor response. These results could be indicative of a shift in the sensitivity of Short photoperiod females to cocaine. We have previously demonstrated that there is a difference in the sensitivity of DAT inhibition in the NAc core between Short and Long photoperiod female mice (Jameson et al., 2023, 2024). Long photoperiod females are more sensitive to DAT inhibition by the selective DAT inhibitor GBR 12783. In this current study, we report that photoperiod modulated the sensitivity to the synaptic effects of cocaine in the NAc in a sex-specific manner. Short photoperiod females had decreased sensitivity to DAT inhibition by cocaine in the NAc, relative to Long and Equinox females, suggesting that the blunted locomotor response in these females is a result of photoperiod-driven differences in NAc DAT function.

We also found that photoperiod influenced associative learning to cocaine cues in a sex-specific manner, where female mice raised under a Long, summer-like photoperiod fail to show cocaine-induced CPP. We used a modified place conditioning protocol with testing after a single exposure to increasing doses of cocaine (2.5 mg/kg, 7.5 mg/kg and 15 mg/kg) to determine the dose-response function of cocaine as a rewarding stimulus paired with a specific environment. The ascending doses of cocaine offered greater precision in detecting changes in cocaine sensitivity driven by photoperiod (Lim et al., 2012). The Short and Equinox photoperiod female mice showed cocaine-induced CPP indicating the photoperiod-dependent response to saliency (Kutlu et al., 2021). We observed that Long photoperiod female mice lacked sensitivity to a salient stimulus.

Since Long photoperiod females show enhanced dopamine release and sensitivity to cocaine in the NAc core, one would also expect enhanced NAc dopamine-mediated learning in these animals. However, associative learning is a function that involves additional brain regions to the NAc. For example, the hippocampus is involved in the formation and retrieval of memory, which is important to associate the rewarding experience of cocaine with the paired environment. Indeed, place cells in the hippocampus are coupled in activity with NAc medium-spiny neurons during cocaine conditioning in a specific environment (Ito et al., 2008, Sjulson et al., 2018). In addition to the hippocampus, the prefrontal cortex (PFC) is another brain region involved in learning and memory retrieval. Previous lesion studies in the medial PFC (mPFC) have obtained contradictory results in the role of mPFC in cocaine-associated learning. Some studies showed that the subregions of the mPFC are necessary for cocaine CPP acquisition and lesions lead to disruptions in learning (Isaac et al., 1989; Tzschentke and Schmidt, 1999) while others found no effects in cocaine CPP acquisition (Zavalla et al., 2003). Although these results remain inconclusive, previous work in rats has also shown that cocaine self-administration directly into the mPFC is reinforcing (Goeders and Smith, 1993), which indicates that the mPFC plays a role in cocaine-related learning. Taking all this into consideration, in the studies of female mice presented here, it is possible that photoperiod modulates cocaine associative learning by inducing different changes in brain regions other than the NAc core.

Another alternative explanation for our results could be the various mechanisms of action of cocaine. Cocaine has other targets, including the inhibition of the serotonin transporter (SERT), the norepinephrine transporter (NET) as well as activation of the sigma-1 receptor (Hall et al., 2009; Drake and Scott, 2018; Manz et al., 2021, 2022). Thus, our behavioral data may reflect photoperiod effects on psychostimulant action at these other sites. Photoperiod has been consistently shown to modulate the serotonin system, including SERT expression and the excitability of serotonergic neurons in the dorsal raphe nucleus (Praschak-Rieder et al., 2008; Willeit et al., 2008; Praschak-Rieder and Willeit, 2012; Green et al., 2015; Matheson et al., 2015; McMahon et al., 2016; Tyrer et al., 2016; Nørgaard et al., 2017). In fact, we have previously demonstrated a sex-specific increase in NAc serotonin content in Short photoperiod females (Jameson et al., 2024). NAc serotonin dampens the locomotor response to cocaine (Pradhan et al., 1978; Molina et al., 2001; Taracha, 2021). Therefore, it is possible that increased NAc serotonin level in the Short photoperiod females contributes to the blunted locomotor response to cocaine reported here.

Although we did not track for estrous cycling and sex hormone levels in our voltammetry and behavior experiments, we expect an interaction between photoperiod and estrous cycling to modulate DAT function and reward-based learning (Calipari et al., 2017). The effect of photoperiod on dopamine release and reuptake is age dependent (Jameson et al., 2024). Female mice raised in a Long, summer-like photoperiod have enhanced dopamine release and reuptake during young adulthood (postnatal day 50), but not pre-puberty at P30 (Jameson et al., 2024). This age-dependent effect of photoperiod implicates the role of sex hormones and estrous cycling in dopaminergic signaling. During the estrous cycle, fluctuations in estradiol levels modulate the activity of VTA dopamine neurons and downstream NAc DAT function though an ERK-dependent phosphorylation pathway leading to enhanced DAT sensitivity to inhibition and DAT-mediated behavior like augmented cocaine CPP (Calipari et al., 2017). Moreover, in human studies, women report higher reward to cocaine use during the follicular phase than the luteal phase (Evans et al., 2002, Evans and Foltin, 2010). Considering these hormonal effects in the dopamine system, future studies of the contribution of specific hormonal signaling across the estrous cycle is warranted.

Consistent with the data presented here and our previous findings of photoperiodic modulation of NAc dopamine dynamics (Jameson et al., 2023, 2024), circadian neurobiology modulates the dopamine system at various levels. The gene for the rate-limiting enzyme in the production of DA, tyrosine hydroxylase, shows a diurnal rhythm in its expression in the VTA with a peak in the middle of the light phase (ZT6) and a nadir just before the dark phase (ZT12) (Webb et al., 2009). Phasic dopamine release and reuptake in the NAc is also regulated by time of day with the highest release and reuptake reported during the light phase (ZT6 – ZT12) followed by a nadir throughout the night (Ferris et al., 2014). Future studies are needed to determine if photoperiod shifts the phase or alters the magnitude of dopamine signaling in the NAc. In addition to the direct VTA to NAc dopamine projections, rapid dopamine signaling that is important for associative learning is also diurnally modulated by cholinergic interneurons in the NAc (Stowe et al., 2022). Mechanisms underlying altered dopamine signaling across photoperiod have yet to be determined.

Due to these levels of circadian modulation, the dopaminergic system is responsive to photic inputs in ways that are relevant for neuropsychiatric conditions including seasonal affective disorder, bipolar disorder, addiction and associative learning (Itzhacki et al., 2018; Kwiatkowski et al., 2020; Stowe et al., 2022). Changes in photoperiod have been shown to drive physiological and behavioral phenotypes that model these disorders, and a reversal in photoperiod rescues these phenotypes (Dulcis et al., 2013; Itzhacki et al., 2018).

Perinatal photoperiod leaves long-lasting physiological and behavioral marks that persist even after an animal adapts to a different photoperiod later in adulthood (Ciarleglio et al., 2011; van Dorp and Deboer, 2025). Perinatal photoperiodic programming imprints clock neurons in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) and resulting circadian activity by the animal. Long perinatal photoperiod shortens the waveform and the rhythmic period of SCN clock neurons, and this shortening is also observed in the behavioral period as measured by free wheel-running (Ciarleglio et al., 2011). Considering the model presented here is the same as Ciarleglio et al. we would expect no difference in photoperiodic entrainment between male and female mice.

We conducted all our experiments in the middle of the light phase for all three photoperiods. With zeitgeber time (ZT) 12 denoting lights off, voltammetry recordings and behavior testing were conducted at ZT8 for Short, ZT6 for Equinox, and ZT4 for Long photoperiod mice. This limits our study to one circadian time point while dopamine-dependent behavior is influenced by circadian rhythms (Page, 2015; Parekh et al., 2015; Depoy et al., 2017). Future studies will explore the effect of photoperiod on dopamine-mediated behavior across the circadian period.

In summary, our results provide evidence that photoperiod has sex-specific effects on cocaine action at the level of NAc DAT function and NAc-mediated behavior. This work lays the foundation for future studies examining the relationship between photoperiod, sex hormones, and the mesolimbic dopamine system and how this interaction contributes to seasonal behavioral variations in health and disease.
