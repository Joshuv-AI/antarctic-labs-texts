Association between socioeconomic position and transitions to multimorbidity involving major chronic diseases in Northwest Italy
Authors: Francesca Mori, Luca Manfredi, Roberto Gnavi, Elisa Ferracin, Roberta Picariello, Lorenzo Milani, Federica Buscema, Maria Teresa Giraudo, Fulvio Ricceri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multimorbidity, the co-occurrence of multiple chronic conditions in individuals, complicates clinical management, increases healthcare use, and reduces life quality. This study investigates the association between socioeconomic position and multimorbidity, restricted to co-occurrence of two or more conditions among type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, selected cancers as a single category, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and depression, in a population-based cohort in Piedmont, Italy. A multimorbidity registry was built from 2013 to 2019, and diseases were identified through healthcare databases. Multistate models estimated the association between multimorbidity and socioeconomic position, stratifying by sex. Among 1,534,975 individuals, multimorbidity incidence was 1.31 (95% CI: 1.29–1.33) per 100,000 person-days. Lower SEP was associated with higher risk in almost all transitions to T2D and to CVD. In women, borderline significant associations were found from cancer to depression and from COPD to depression, comparing the lowest to the highest SEP. In men, significant associations were found for transitions to COPD as well as from COPD to cancer and from depression to cancer. Lower SEP was consistently associated with higher risks of single diseases and multimorbidity, with some sex-specific patterns. These findings highlight cumulative disadvantage and the need for policies targeting health inequalities in aging populations.

Introduction

In recent years, demographic and epidemiological changes have reshaped global health landscapes1. In particular, population aging represents a major demographic shift that poses a significant burden on patients, clinicians, and healthcare systems1,2. As individuals age, they often live with one or more diseases for an extensive period of their lifespan.

Multimorbidity, i.e., the coexistence of multiple chronic conditions, is related to other health aspects such as frailty and polypharmacy2. For these reasons, individuals with multimorbidity often experience reduced quality of life, complex treatment regimens, and higher healthcare costs3,4. Despite the upward global trend in multimorbidity and the multifaceted, holistic approach required to address the medical, psychological, and social dimensions of care, a significant gap in research and practice endures5,6. Clinical guidelines still focus on disease-specific treatments and on a linear approach that simply sums up the different conditions and related pharmacological treatments, which quickly become unbearable for patients with multiple diseases7,8. Moreover, multimorbidity is not a uniform condition but varies according to the diseases involved, with distinct patterns showing different prognoses, treatment challenges, and care needs9. Alongside most traditional definitions of multimorbidity that focus on cardiometabolic and cancer diseases, due to their high prevalence and the impact on public health, other non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are increasingly recognized in the literature on multimorbidity, due to shared risk factors and pathophysiological mechanisms, and are now often included in modern syndemic approaches10. In particular, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is among the most common NCDs in people with multimorbidity. It often coexists with cardiovascular diseases (CVD), lung cancer, and type 2 diabetes (T2D), co-evolving according to shared risk factors and biological and environmental mechanisms11,12. Overall, cancer, CVD, T2D, and chronic respiratory diseases are among the most prevalent NCDs and are responsible for most NCD deaths globally13. Furthermore, depression, sometimes described as a modern epidemic14, demands greater attention within multimorbidity frameworks and integrated care models. In particular, depression is two to three times more likely in individuals with multimorbidity compared to those without, can be exacerbated by clusters of physical illness, and can itself increase the risk of developing multiple NCDs15,16. The inclusion of a larger spectrum of diseases in the multimorbidity definitions, including mental health conditions, is needed for a more holistic, integrated approach to multimorbidity. Our operational definition of multimorbidity was hence based on the co-occurrence of at least two among the previously mentioned NCDs, along with depression.

Multimorbidity is a complex phenomenon that is also related to underlying biological mechanisms and determinants of health, such as socioeconomic deprivation3,5. In particular, socioeconomic position (SEP) is strongly and consistently associated with the risk and patterns of multimorbidity3. Individuals with lower SEP, often measured by education level, income, or area deprivation, exhibit a significantly higher likelihood of developing multiple chronic conditions compared to those with higher SEP17. Furthermore, socioeconomic deprivation is linked not only to higher prevalence but also to earlier onset of multimorbidity and more complex disease combinations, including mental comorbidities. These disparities contribute to increased healthcare utilization and poorer health outcomes among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups18,19.

Among the different possible indicators of SEP, educational level was chosen in the present article. Educational level offers several advantages as an indicator of SEP. It is typically attained early in life and remains relatively stable beyond the age of 30, thereby minimizing the risk of reverse causality, unlike income, which can vary considerably over time. Moreover, education reflects an individual’s family and cultural background and serves as a proxy for health awareness, self-care practices, and the ability to navigate the healthcare system. It is also strongly correlated with income in adulthood20,21.

The study explores how educational level, as a proxy for SEP, can influence multimorbidity patterns, assessing the impact of social inequalities across diverse multimorbidity trajectories involving five common diseases, namely CVD, T2D, cancer, COPD, and depression, within an Italian regional population-based administrative cohort.

Regarding educational attainment, the three levels are distributed as follows: 23.90% with a low educational level, 66.27% with a medium educational level, and 9.83% with a high educational level. In women, both medium and low SEP were associated with increased risks for all transitions from the disease-free state to a single NCD (and to death), except for the transition to cancer, for which both levels showed a reduced risk compared to the high SEP category. For transitions to multimorbidity, the comparison of medium vs high SEP among women highlighted an increased risk for the transitions from cancer to T2D (HR: 1.51; 95% CI: 1.11–2.06) and CVD (HR: 1.86; 95% CI: 1.14–3.05), and from depression to T2D (HR: 1.34; 95% CI: 1.10–1.63), CVD (HR: 1.38; 95% CI: 1.11–1.72) and COPD (HR: 1.40; 95% CI: 1.13–1.74) (Table 3). A stronger increase was present for the comparison between low and high SEP: among women who developed cancer as the first disease, low SEP level was linked to a higher risk for the transitions to T2D (HR: 2.11; 95% CI: 1.52–2.91), CVD (HR: 3.31; 95% CI: 2.01–5.44) and depression (HR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.01–1.41), although this association was no longer statistically significant after FDR correction, compared to the high SEP level. For the transitions from the CVD state, being in the low SEP level implied a higher risk for the transitions to T2D (HR: 1.48; 95% CI: 1.00–2.18), although this association became non-significant after FDR correction. For those with COPD as the first disease, low SEP was associated with an increased risk of transitioning to T2D (HR: 2.39; 95% CI: 1.53–3.73), CVD (HR: 1.45; 95% CI: 1.02-2.05) and depression (HR: 1.30; 95% CI: 1.03–1.63), although the last two associations lost statistical significance after FDR correction. In men, a similar pattern was observed, although some associations and effect sizes differed. In particular, both medium and low SEP were risk factors for developing cancer from a disease-free state. In the transitions to multimorbidity, the comparison between medium and high SEP revealed a higher risk in the transitions from T2D to CVD (HR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.04–1.52), from CVD to T2D (HR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.01–1.42) (non-significant after FDR correction), cancer (HR: 1.51; 95% CI: 1.06–2.14) and COPD (HR: 1.56; 95% CI: 1.20–2.02), from cancer to COPD (HR: 1.65; 95% CI: 1.08–2.52), from COPD to CVD (HR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.07–1.73) and from depression to CVD (HR: 1.30; 95% CI: 1.10–1.54) and COPD (HR: 1.55; 95% CI: 1.20–2.00). Moreover, those in the medium SEP category had a lower risk of transitioning from T2D to depression (HR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.64–0.93) compared to those in the high SEP category (Table 3).

As for the low vs high SEP comparison, in men transitioning from a T2D disease state, low SEP was associated with an increased risk of transitioning to CVD (HR: 1.51; 95% CI: 1.25–1.84) and COPD (HR: 2.18; 95% CI: 1.46–3.27). For the transitions from the CVD state, being in the low SEP level implied a higher risk for the transitions to T2D (HR: 1.37; 95% CI: 1.14–1.64), cancer (HR: 1.79; 95% CI: 1.25–2.57), and COPD (HR: 2.83; 95% CI: 2.18–3.67). For the transitions from a cancer disease state, being in the low SEP level implied a higher risk for the transitions to T2D (HR: 1.48; 95% CI: 1.05–2.10), CVD (HR: 1.64; 95% CI: 1.09–2.45), and COPD (HR: 2.38; 95% CI: 1.54–3.67). From a COPD disease state, low SEP increased the risks of transitions to T2D (HR: 1.74; 95% CI: 1.22–2.49), CVD (HR: 1.84; 95% CI: 1.44–2.35), and cancer (HR: 1.78; 95% CI: 1.21–2.61). For those with depression as the initial disease, low SEP was associated with higher risks of transitions to T2D (HR: 1.38; 95% CI: 1.12–1.70), CVD (HR: 1.69; 95% CI: 1.42–2.02), cancer (HR: 1.50; 95% CI: 1.13–2.00) and COPD (HR: 2.32; 95% CI: 1.78–3.03) (Table 4). Results of the grouping analysis when the sample was split into two age groups showed similar patterns, as presented in Tables S3 and S4 of the Supplementary Information. When SEP was operationalized using the multidimensional DI, associations were generally weaker than those observed for education, although the direction of effects was largely consistent. The strongest associations were observed for transitions from baseline to first pathology, whereas estimates for subsequent multimorbidity transitions were attenuated. The corresponding detailed results are shown in the Supplementary Information (Tables S5 and S8).

Discussion

In this large population-based cohort, we found consistent evidence that SEP shapes multimorbidity trajectories, with individuals in lower SEP groups experiencing higher risks of transitioning between chronic disease states compared to those with higher SEP. Moreover, SEP appears to influence multimorbidity trajectories differently in women and men, with no association observed across transitions to cancer and COPD, except from depression to COPD, among women, and no association observed across transitions to depression among men, while in women, the transition from cancer to depression remained borderline significant even after FDR correction.

We found that SEP was linked to the risk of transitioning to T2D across all transitions, and to CVD except from T2D and COPD in women. In cancer survivors, CVD risk is increased due to treatment-related cardiotoxicity and premature coronary disease, and because the two diseases share common risk factors, particularly lifestyle-related factors such as smoking and diet. These risks are further amplified in disadvantaged populations, who are disproportionately affected by limited resources, health literacy, and psychosocial stressors22–24. Biological mechanisms in cell signaling and epigenetic effects could further link SEP to cancer- and treatment-related CVD complications24. Evidence on SEP and T2D risk among cancer patients is scarcer, but higher post-cancer T2D incidence25, socioeconomic gradients in T2D26, disparities in diabetes care and metabolic burden27, and shared risk factors3, suggest that inequalities extend into survivorship. Moreover, CVD is known to be the most common comorbidity for COPD, driven by smoking and air pollution, along with possible cardiac side effects of COPD therapies28. In COPD, social disadvantage is associated with poorer survival, including higher cardio-cerebrovascular mortality, suggesting that SEP may amplify CVD risk within COPD populations29. Similar mechanisms link socioeconomic disparities in COPD to T2D transition, through inflammation, oxidative stress, reduced physical activity, altered body composition, and dysregulated lipid metabolism30. Psychological factors further shape these transitions. Depression partly mediates the SEP-CVD association, implying that depressed adults in disadvantaged settings carry higher incident CVD risk31,32. Moreover, chronic depression may contribute to diabetogenesis via stress-related allostatic load, although evidence is scarce33. Finally, socioeconomic disparities are well documented in T2D complications, particularly CVD34,35. Our finding of a significant SEP role in the CVD to T2D transition in men aligns with prior work and perhaps reflects the results for the transition from disease-free status to T2D3. By contrast, the absence of SEP disparities for the transition from CVD to T2D, and from T2D to any other disease, and CVD in particular, in women may reflect sex-specific distributions of lifestyle factors such as smoking and physical activity in our cohort3.

Transitions to cancer from CVD, depression, and COPD found in men are likely influenced by shared biological pathways (inflammation, oxidative stress), lifestyle factors, and socioeconomic determinants. In particular, cardiovascular risk factors were found to strongly mediate the association between SEP and comorbid CVD and cancer36. Therefore, incident comorbid CVD and cancer may be at least partly explained by cardiovascular health. Patients with depression are more likely to engage in harmful behaviors, and conditions such as obesity are more prevalent, increasing the risk of cancer occurrence37,38. Pathways such as sleep disorders and stress-response dysregulation may further contribute to both depression and carcinogenesis. Epidemiological evidence supports a positive association between depression and overall cancer occurrence, highlighting the potential mediating role of unhealthy lifestyle factors39. COPD is associated with tobacco-related cancers, particularly lung and gastrointestinal cancers, due to the shared exposure to smoking. Given the strong social patterning of smoking, with higher prevalence in lower socioeconomic groups, a socioeconomic gradient in the COPD-cancer transition is expected40.

Low SEP likely amplifies health risk across these transitions by increasing exposure to behavioral and environmental stressors, thereby compounding their biological impact.

As for the risk reduction associated to low SEP in the transition from depression to cancer in women, depression diagnosis can be biased when it relies on drug registry data or prescription records. In addition, after FDR correction the association appeared to be only borderline significant. Nonetheless, although mostly non-significant, the associations between lower SEP levels and transitions to cancer in women show an opposite gradient compared to the other transitions. This pattern might partly reflect differential detection. Women with lower SEP, particularly those with depression, might still face barriers to screening adherence or experience delayed diagnoses41. However, evidence on socioeconomic inequalities in breast cancer screening is mixed and depends on the type of screening considered. While disadvantaged groups often show lower overall screening uptake42, organized population-based programmes can attenuate or even reverse socioeconomic differences in participation43. Conversely, the inverse gradient has been extensively reported in the literature3,42,44, and may be explained by known reproductive and sex-related factors. In particular, the use of hormone therapy, the tendency of having fewer children at a later stage in life, and a higher prevalence of alcohol consumption are more common among women with higher SEP, and these factors are established risk factors for post-menopausal breast cancer, the most common cancer among women in our cohort45,46. Furthermore, our analysis was restricted to four cancer types, which may limit the generalizability of these findings, as socioeconomic gradients can vary across cancer sites. In particular, the high prevalence of breast cancer among women might outweigh other possible associations.

The differences in the role of SEP on these transitions by sex may stem from lifestyle and occupational exposures more common in men, while hormonal and genetic factors may be more important in women.

The association of SEP with transitions from CVD and T2D to COPD was only significant among men in our cohort, likely reflecting sex differences in lifestyle behaviors, occupational exposures, and the distribution of SEP across sexes. COPD frequently co-occurs with CVD, with bidirectional effects: as some COPD medications can have adverse effects on the risk of CVD, the reverse can also be true, with some CVD medications increasing COPD risk28. The same is true for cancer, for which inequalities could be further exacerbated after cancer diagnosis by treatment-related lung injury47,48. In our cohort, SEP influenced the cancer to COPD transition only in men, consistent with historical sex differences in COPD prevalence and possible sex-specific pathophysiologic mechanisms49. Moreover, T2D may further worsen COPD progression, as combined exposure to cigarette smoke and metabolic dysregulation can impair lung function. Also, obesity has been proposed as a factor linking diabetes and COPD50. Depression increases COPD risk, with disadvantaged groups experiencing higher prevalence of both conditions51. SEP could amplify this effect, as social disadvantage interacts with depressive symptoms and other maladaptive behaviors to increase COPD incidence33,51,52.

Overall, low SEP likely magnifies the risk of transitioning to COPD by increasing exposure to behavioral, environmental, and clinical risk factors across multiple upstream conditions.

In our findings, SEP was associated with the transition to depression from cancer in women. In cancer patients, depression is common, though its magnitude varies according to sex, age, educational attainment, and social support. Structural barriers such as treatment costs, difficult healthcare access, stigma, and untreated symptoms disproportionately affect disadvantaged groups37. Cancer treatments themselves may increase depression risk, and lack of coordinated care or poor communication can exacerbate this. Anxiety and depression also cluster more frequently in lower socioeconomic groups37,53,54.

We found that SEP influenced transitions from CVD, COPD, and depression to cancer only in men, while it affected the transition from cancer to depression only in women. In addition, SEP was associated with transitions from T2D to CVD and COPD in men but not in women. These sex-specific patterns may reflect differences in lifestyle distributions, such as higher prevalence of smoking and alcohol use among men, while in women, biological, genetic, or endocrine factors unrelated to SEP may play a stronger role. For women, a potential role of SEP may be more evident in psychosocial pathways (transitions to depression), whereas factors that are less tied to SEP may dominate for somatic disease transitions. SEP consistently affected all transitions from depression to other diseases in both sexes, except to cancer in women, where the association showed an inverse gradient, which was only borderline significant after FDR correction, indicating that the socioeconomic gradient is reflected in multimorbidity once depression is established. This highlights depression as a critical vulnerability where social disadvantage amplifies the accumulation of chronic conditions55.

Finally, when conducting a grouping analysis by splitting the sample into two age categories (45–60 versus 61–80), results were consistent with the main analysis. In particular, the association between SEP and the risk of transitions was similar across groups, with a slightly stronger link for the 45–60 group, though with wider confidence intervals. The discrepancies among the findings may reflect fewer events and greater heterogeneity in risks in the younger group, as well as higher baseline risk, a potential survivorship bias, and higher mortality in the older group.

Among the strengths of the present study, it is worth highlighting that it is based on individual-level data from a large administrative database including a population-based cohort. The use of different healthcare data sources allowed for precise and accurate ascertainment of various chronic conditions. Moreover, the longitudinal design reduced the risk of reverse causality by preserving the principle of temporality. In addition, the multistate framework enabled us to account for transitions to multimorbidity across specific pairs of conditions, rather than simply summing up individual diseases into a common definition of multimorbidity.

However, several limitations should also be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the database did not include information on participants’ lifestyle factors. As a result, it was not possible to assess the distribution of lifestyle-related risk factors in the population or to explore sex differences in these factors. It is possible that the mediating effect of lifestyle was stronger in men in this cohort, which is suggested by the fact that some transitions were significant only among men. Nonetheless, the main aim of the present research was to examine the total effect of SEP on the risk of transitions to different chronic conditions. Furthermore, the observed sex differences in transitions may also partly reflect factors unrelated to lifestyle or SEP. Second, the operational definition of multimorbidity was restricted to five conditions (T2D, CVD, four cancer types, depression, and COPD). Therefore, our findings pertain specifically to patterns of co-occurrence and trajectories within this selected set of diseases, rather than to the full spectrum of multimorbidity. However, these conditions account for a substantial share of the global disease burden, in particular regarding NCDs13,15, and are frequently studied in combination. Our focus allowed for a more detailed examination of clinically relevant disease trajectories, though the findings should be interpreted as pertaining to this specific subset rather than to multimorbidity as a whole.

Related to this aspect, the definition of multimorbidity did not include other prevalent chronic diseases such as musculoskeletal disorders, chronic kidney disease, dementia, or arthritis. This restriction may lead to an underestimation of the overall prevalence and incidence of multimorbidity, particularly in older individuals, among whom excluded conditions are common56. If these excluded conditions are socially patterned, their omission may also bias the estimated association between socioeconomic position and multimorbidity, potentially attenuating or altering observed inequalities. However, the selected conditions represent major contributors to disease burden and healthcare use, and thus capture clinically relevant multimorbidity trajectories. Third, disease identification relied primarily on administrative healthcare databases. While this approach enables large-scale population analyses, such data may be affected by underdiagnosis and coding inaccuracies, potentially leading to misclassification and some degree of measurement bias that could vary across socioeconomic groups. This issue is particularly relevant for conditions such as depression, which are often under-recognized in routine data. Unlike other conditions, depression has a less clear-cut diagnosis and was mainly captured through information on medication use. This could have introduced an underreporting bias, especially among higher socioeconomic strata less reliant on the public healthcare system for prescriptions. In addition, psychosocial factors likely play a role involving relevant sex differences, such as stigma or a reluctance to seek help within the healthcare system, among individuals at risk or with latent or related conditions57. Fourth, the use of education as the sole proxy for SEP represents a limitation. Although education is an effective measure that captures several dimensions of SEP and correlates with other indicators such as income and family background, and has many advantages which have been highlighted before, these socioeconomic measures do not fully overlap3. Finally, despite careful consideration of the relationships between available variables and the specification of the models, residual confounding is likely to be present.

Our results confirm that a lower SEP is consistently associated with a higher risk of developing both single chronic diseases and multimorbidity, although patterns vary across specific transitions and by sex. These findings highlight the cumulative disadvantage experienced by individuals in lower socioeconomic groups and underline the importance of implementing targeted policies to reduce health inequalities in the context of an aging population.

Methods

Data and setting

Data were obtained from the Piedmont Longitudinal Study (PLS), an administrative cohort built from anonymized, individual-level linkages across multiple social, health, and administrative sources, including Hospital Discharge Registries, Drug Prescriptions Registry, Regional Diabetes Registry, and Cause of Death Registry. The demographic information was retrieved from the regional archive of assisted residents, linked to the 2011 national census to obtain information on the educational level. This studied cohort included residents in Piedmont, a north-western region of Italy (4,252,000 inhabitants, 7% of the Italian population), on 1 January 2013, aged 45–80 years, who had no recorded diagnosis of any of the five chronic conditions under investigation at the beginning of the follow-up time. The choice to include subjects aged 45 or older is due to the low prevalence of multimorbidity among younger individuals. Moreover, selecting participants older than 80 years could attenuate socioeconomic differences in health outcomes and introduce potential survivorship bias, given that age dominates risk and that most individuals in this age group already have one or more diseases.

Variables of interest

The primary outcome of interest was multimorbidity, defined as the co-occurrence of two chronic diseases among the examined conditions. A comprehensive data integration enabled the reconstruction of individual health trajectories over time, allowing for the identification of diagnoses of T2D, COPD, cancer (lung, breast, pancreas, and colorectal), CVD, i.e., heart failure and ischemic heart disease, and depression. We identified the first occurrence of a chronic disease, which was considered the incident case, with the date of diagnosis defined as the earliest date recorded across the sources. Any subsequent chronic disease, among those under study, was considered a second disease, defining the participant as multimorbid. Case identification was based on algorithms previously defined and extensively used in epidemiological studies in Italy and described in the Supplementary Information8.

The exposure of interest was SEP, defined on the basis of educational level and categorized as follows: low education, encompassing illiterate individuals, those with basic literacy but no formal education, and those with only primary school completion; medium education, encompassing individuals with lower secondary education, or upper secondary education diplomas; high education, encompassing individuals holding university diplomas, bachelor’s or master’s degrees.

All prevalent cases before the beginning of the study were excluded using health data from 2007. Moreover, subjects for whom no linkage to the 2011 regional census was possible were also excluded. Follow-up continued until 31 December 2019.

Analyses were conducted in R58 and SAS59.

Statistical analysis

To account for the different transitions toward multimorbidity, a multistate modeling approach was adopted. Multistate models generalize competing risk models by describing transitions between intermediate states and are particularly useful for characterizing a stochastic process in which each individual occupies one of a finite number of discrete health states60. In this context, events are defined as transitions between such states. In particular, longitudinal data were analyzed using multistate models based on Cox regression61. If multiple types of events are possible, these models allow for the estimation of transition-specific hazard functions to describe the progression to each outcome. To examine the transitions between the baseline (disease-free) state and the diagnosis of the first NCD, participants were followed from the date of enrollment (1/1/2013) to one of the following events: date of first diagnosis, date of death, end of follow-up, or censoring date. Similarly, to investigate transitions to multimorbidity, follow-up time was calculated from the date of first diagnosis to one of the following: date of a diagnosis of the second NCD (i.e., progression to multimorbidity), death, end of follow-up, or censoring date. All possible transitions between the defined health states and their associations with SEP were examined: from the disease-free state to each of the individual diseases, and from each of the first diagnoses to all possible multimorbidity combinations of two diseases. To account for multiple testing, p-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) approach. Moreover, death was modeled as a separate outcome. The analyses were conducted separately by sex, using age group as a stratification variable in the model. In addition, secondary models were estimated, stratifying the sample by age class (45–60 years and 61–80 years). The main analysis was also repeated using a deprivation index as the exposure measure, based on the Italian deprivation index (DI) developed at the census section level. The index is derived from five census-based indicators: low educational attainment, unemployment, living in rented accommodation, household overcrowding, and living in a single-parent family, and is categorized into quintiles, with higher quintiles indicating greater deprivation62.

All the analyses for this research were performed according to the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki. The study, included in the National Statistical Plan, does not need approval or permits from the ethics committee. For privacy purposes, the data used for analysis were anonymized.
