Correlation of Positron Emission Tomography and Low‐Field Standing Magnetic Resonance Imaging in Horses With Forelimb Lameness Localized to the Foot
Authors: Maureen E. Kelleher, Stacie Aarsvold, Sergey I. Anishchenko, Yevgeniya Gokun, David Beylin, Xiaoping Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLow‐field magnetic resonance imaging (LF‐MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET) imaging can be obtained in the standing horse. There is potential for increased diagnostic capacity combining PET with MRI, given that PET assesses the metabolic activity of injured tissues, whereas MRI provides structural and functional details on both bone and soft tissue anatomy. This study aimed to describe 18‐fluorine‐fluorodeoxyglucose (18F‐FDG)–PET and 18‐sodium fluoride (18F‐NaF)–PET imaging of horses with forelimb lameness localized to the foot and compare PET with LF‐MRI. Fourteen horses with lameness localized to the foot underwent standing MRI and PET with a dual‐tracer protocol. MRI abnormalities and PET isotope uptake were subjectively graded. Weighted kappa statistics were used to compare the agreement between the ordinal grading of MRI and PET images. Maximum, minimum, and mean standard uptake values (SUV) were calculated at increased radiopharmaceutical uptake (IRU) regions. Metabolic volume (MV) within a cuboidal area of the IRU was calculated. No clinically significant kappa results were obtained comparing MRI to 18F‐FDG–PET or 18F‐NaF–PET. The mean SUV of background radiation was significantly lower than the mean SUVmax for subjectively mild and moderate‐severe 18F‐NaF–PET (p < 0.001). When comparing the SUV ratio for subjectively mild and moderate‐severe groups, there was no significant difference between the two (p = 0.546). The major limitation of the study was the small population size. Low‐field MRI may underrepresent bone remodeling and potentially misrepresent the source of lameness. We report various standardized uptake values and MVs for regions within the foot with IRU for 18F‐FDG and 18F‐NaF.

Introduction

The horse's forefoot is the most common region to cause lameness, resulting in days of lost use in athletic endeavors. Standing low‐field magnetic resonance imaging (LF‐MRI) has been validated as a significant diagnostic tool for identifying bone and soft tissue injuries in horses’ distal limbs [1, 2, 3].

Positron emission tomography (PET) has recently become available for equine diagnostic imaging. PET is a nuclear imaging modality with the advantage over bone scintigraphy as a cross‐sectional imaging modality with increased specificity in lesion localization [4, 5]. Like 99m‐technetium‐methylenediphosphonate (99mTc‐MDP), the radiotracer used in bone scintigraphy, 18‐sodium fluoride (18F‐NaF) is used as a marker of bone turnover, binding to exposed hydroxyapatite crystals in remodeling bone [6]. Studies using 18F‐NaF–PET in horses have shown excellent results for detecting and monitoring bone injury in the metacarpal bone, tarsal bones, phalanges, and tarsus [7, 8, 9, 10].

The radiotracer 18‐fluorine‐fluorodeoxyglucose (18F‐FDG) is a glucose analog taken up in metabolically active cells. Recent research has shown usefulness in the identification of soft tissue injuries in human [11], canine [12], and equine patients [13, 14]. A recent study explored a dual‐tracer protocol for administering 18F‐FDG and 18F‐NaF in horses under anesthesia [15], concluding the optimal protocol would be an injection of 18F‐NaF before anesthesia induction and acquiring 18F‐NaF images approximately 1 h later. 18F‐FDG was then administered 2 h after the 18F‐NaF for the acquisition of 18F‐FDG images.

In human medicine, metabolic tumor volume (MTV) measures metabolic activity in a selected area on PET imaging and is used prognostically to determine response to intervention. Compared with standard uptake values (SUV)max, which represents a single voxel in the selected region and has been commonly reported in equine PET [16], metabolic volume (MV) calculations allow for the subtraction of background (bg) radiation and specification of threshold ranges to remove clinically insignificant outliers, resulting in a better estimation of the metabolic burden of the entire tumor [17]. MTV values obtained from 18F‐FDG PET–CT imaging are predominantly used in oncologic medicine, estimating the high glucose metabolism of the tumor based on the uptake of 18F‐FDG. Here, MTV, alongside other clinical measures, is used prognostically in many cancer types, with higher MTV carrying a poorer prognosis [18]. Similarly, 18F‐NaF, being a bone biomarker, has been used for fluoride tumor volume (FTV) to determine skeletal tumor burden [19]. However, the use of the FTV is to monitor for metastasis, especially in cancers such as prostate cancer [20]. MV, using either isotope, has not been previously reported with equine PET imaging. Use in equine imaging may be to better represent “injury activity” or, as used with cancer patients, to monitor response to interventions with serial imaging.

This study describes a same‐day dual‐tracer protocol to acquire PET images of the forefeet in lame horses. Given that both low‐field MRI and PET imaging of the feet can be obtained in the standing horse, there is the potential for increased diagnostic capacity with a standing PET and MRI combination. Given the frequency with which the forefoot is injured and the previously reported sensitivity of PET, the goal of this study was to describe the findings of 18F‐FDG–PET and 18F‐NaF–PET imaging of the front feet in a population of lame horses and compare the PET imaging with LF‐MRI. On the basis of previous imaging by the authors (unpublished) and the high sensitivity of 18F‐NaF to identify areas of bone remodeling, we hypothesized that 18F‐NaF–PET would be more predictive than LF‐MRI for identifying bone remodeling. Conversely, 18F‐FDG PET, as a glucose analog, has higher radiopharmaceutical uptake in metabolically active tissues, such as tumors, the brain, and the heart. Given the relatively lower vascularity in tendon and ligament, we hypothesize that 18F‐FDG PET would be less predictive than LF‐MRI for identifying active soft tissue injury. SUVmax and MV were calculated for each region of increased radiopharmaceutical uptake (IRU) for 18F‐FDG and 18F‐NaF.

Methods

Inclusion

Fourteen client‐owned horses were recruited for this study. Inclusion criteria included horses of any breed, aged over 2 years, with no upper age limit if the horse was athletically active. Horses had lameness of the forelimb localized to the foot with either palmar digital nerve anesthesia or intra‐articular anesthesia of the distal interphalangeal joint (DIPJ), with no hind limb lameness present. The primary author or a referring veterinarian performed diagnostic anesthesia before the study was conducted. The study was approved and conducted following the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocols (IACUC #: 19–128). Horse owners signed a consent form before admission to the hospital and were allowed to withdraw at any time.

Imaging

After a physical examination, one jugular vein was sterilely prepared, and a 14‐gauge, 5.25″ polypropylene intravenous catheter (Angiocath, Beckton‐Dickinson and Co., Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was placed in the patient. The catheter remained indwelling until all imaging was completed. When horses were not under sedative medications, they were allowed access to feed and water throughout the hospital stay.

On Day 1, horses were pre‐medicated with 0.03 mg/kg acepromazine maleate intramuscularly 15 to 20 min before imaging. Approximately 5 min before entering the MRI suite, horses were administered an intravenous (IV) dose of 0.01 mg/kg detomidine hydrochloride. A moderate plane of sedation was maintained throughout MRI imaging by administering additional IV doses of 0.002–0.006 mg/kg detomidine hydrochloride or 0.006–0.01 mg/kg butorphanol tartrate. Standing, low‐field 0.27T (Hallmarq Veterinary Imaging Ltd., Guildford, UK) MRI was obtained using a hoof coil and a standard foot imaging protocol. The protocol (Table 1) included sagittal, transverse, and frontal plane images in various imaging sequences of both front feet. After MR imaging, images were transferred and stored on a designated picture archiving and communication system (PACS) (Keystone Omni, Asteris, Monument, CO, USA), which was later transferred to a cloud‐based software for processing medical images (Alienbyte, Rockville, MD, USA).

Horses were prepared for PET imaging by having a light horse blanket and standing wraps on all four distal limbs placed the night before PET imaging. On Day 2, horses were walked to a designated nuclear medicine (NM) suite. Each horse wore hoof boots on both front feet (SoftRide, Bacliff, TX, USA). The horse was injected with 30 mCi (1100 MBq) 18F‐FDG via the jugular vein catheter and returned to the stall. After a 1‐h isotope distribution period, the horse was walked to the NM suite, sedated lightly with an alpha‐2 agonist intravenously alone (0.006–0.01 mg/kg detomidine hydrochloride or 0.3–0.5 mg/kg xylazine hydrochloride) or in combination with 0.006–0.01 mg/kg butorphanol tartrate, depending on temperament.

For PET imaging, the left foot, still wearing the hoof boot, was placed weight‐bearing within the center of a 22 cm diameter field of view (FOV), ground‐placed, prototype PET (15,210 LYSO crystal) ring (LONGMILE, Rockville, MD, USA) with a 2 mm isotropic spatial resolution. The horse's head was placed on a sturdy stand with a handler nearby. Once the horse was resting quietly on the head stand, with one foot placed in the PET ring and the other placed outside of but adjacent to the PET, the handler stayed at least an arm's length from the horse (Figure 1). Scan duration for each limb was a 12‐min acquisition period, with an energy window of 430–650 keV, a timing window of 4 ns, and a listmode acquisition model. After the left foot was imaged, the same protocol was repeated for the right front foot. The horse was returned to the stall for a 3‐h wash‐out period.

Study horse with left front limb placed within the center of a prototype positron emission tomography scanner. Note the horse is wearing boots, standing wraps, and a light blanket to keep the periphery of the animal warm.

After the wash‐out period, the horse returned to the NM suite for injection of 20 mCi (740 MBq) 18F‐NaF via the jugular vein catheter. The horse was returned to its stall for the 30‐min 18F‐NaF distribution period. Subsequently, returning to the NM suite, each foot was imaged for a 12‐min acquisition period with the hoof placed in the center of the ground‐placed PET unit, as described for 18F‐FDG imaging. After all PET imaging, the intravenous catheter was removed, and the horse was returned to the stall.

Horses were released from radiation after 10 half‐lives had passed from the injection of the 18F‐NaF isotope and discharged from the hospital the day following PET imaging.

Image Analysis

PET images were reconstructed on a 300 × 300 × 288 grid using 25 iterations of an iterative maximum likelihood expectation maximization (MLEM) algorithm with a 0.8 mm3 isotropic voxel. Matrix dimensions were 240 mm × 240 mm × 230.4 mm, with 230.4 being the axial image dimension. One hundred six pixels were populated axially (8.5 mm axial FOV). PET images were used as a reference for co‐registration, and the MRI was resliced to match the PET. No attenuation correction was applied.

Once co‐registered to the MRI, the primary author, experienced with PET in previous (unpublished) work with co‐authors and others, subjectively graded PET uptake using a previously established subjective grading scale [4, 5, 7, 8, 14]. 18F‐FDG (Figure 2) and 18F‐NaF (Figure 3) uptake was graded at eight regions of the foot and pastern. Each region was further categorized by specific anatomy into subregions (Table 2). A 0.10–0.22 cm3 region, centered within each scored subregion with an IRU, was selected as the region of interest (ROI) to report minimum (min), mean, and maximum (max) SUV and MV for both 18F‐FDG and 18F‐NaF (Figure 4).

The MRI images were graded by a board‐certified radiologist (DACVR, DACVR‐EDI) who could not access the PET images. Using the same regions and subregions, the MRI was graded as normal, mild disease (unlikely to be the cause of lameness), or moderate‐severe disease (likely to be the main cause of lameness).

Statistical Analysis

Both MRI and PET were graded on a 3‐point ordinal scale (0 = normal, 1 = mild disease, or 2 = abnormal finding responsible for lameness). Each foot's measurement was treated as a separate observation due to the unit of scientific interest being the foot, not the horse as an aggregate, and because measurements were taken at the foot level with meaningful biological and functional differences between feet. Descriptive statistics, such as ranges for continuous variables and frequency counts and percentages for categorical variables, were used to report the subjective scoring of MRI and PET imaging and the SUV and MV data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots; assumptions were met, allowing the use of parametric paired and two‐sample t‐tests.

Paired t‐tests were used to compare the distribution between SUVbg and SUVmax within mild and or moderate‐severe disease measurements. Moreover, we used a two‐sample t‐test to compare mean differences between subjective grade mild and moderate‐severe measurements between SUVmax and SUVbg. Weighted kappa statistics and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to compare the agreement between the ordinal grading of MRI and PET images [21]. All these analyses were done using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute; Cary, NC, USA), and a two‐sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Inclusion

Fourteen horses were included in this study, consisting of three mares and 11 geldings. Age ranged from 6 to 21 years, with a mean of 12 years. Breed distribution included five thoroughbreds, three warmbloods, two draft crosses, and one each of Quarter Horse, Appaloosa, Irish Sport Horse, and Pinto. Disciplines included four actively ridden pleasure horses, three dressage horses, two show hunters, one fox hunter, an event horse, a show jumper, a ranch working horse, and an English‐discipline school horse. Eight horses were lame on both front limbs and six on one forelimb only (three left and three right). For the bilaterally lame horses, four were predominantly lame on the left front and four on the right. Thirteen horses were blocked with a palmar digital nerve block. One horse blocked approximately half with an intra‐articular DIPJ, and the remainder of the lameness was resolved with a palmar digital nerve block. Six horses had less than 1 year of forelimb lameness, with the remainder having a history of chronic (greater than 1 year) forelimb lameness.

Imaging

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Of the 28 limbs, 25 limbs (89%) had at least one region with an MRI lesion, mild or moderate‐severe; 13 of 14 primarily affected limbs, 6 of 8 secondarily affected (contralateral) limbs, and 6 of 6 unilaterally lame contralateral (UCLL) limbs. Table 3 describes the breakdown of regions and subregions of MRI lesions. Sixteen of the 74 (22%) grade mild to moderate‐severe MRI lesions had 18F‐FDG uptake at the site of the lesion, 14 with mild isotope uptake (Figure 5), and 2 with moderate‐severe isotope uptake. Seventeen of the 74 (23%) grade mild and moderate‐severe MRI lesions had 18F‐NaF isotope uptake at the site of the lesion, 1 with mild uptake, and 16 with moderate‐severe isotope uptake.

Discussion

We hypothesized that 18F‐NaF–PET would be more predictive than LF‐MRI for identifying bone remodeling. We accept this hypothesis because only 23% of the mild and moderate‐severe MRI lesions had 18F‐NaF IRU. In limbs with moderate‐severe 18F‐NaF IRU, only 55% had MRI abnormalities, with the average MRI grade being low (1.3 on a 3‐point scale). Whether the regions of 18F‐NaF IRU that did not have corresponding identifiable lesions on LF‐MRI are the source of the lameness, indicating a significant lack of sensitivity of LF‐MRI, or physiologic, non‐painful remodeling, remains challenging to determine. Previous PET studies show that common areas of bone injury have significant IRU, which can be attributed to lameness [8, 13]. The region P3 had the most measures of IRU, confirming that this bone is often undergoing remodeling. Whether P3 remodeling is the source of pain causing lameness is difficult to determine. In this study, many of the non‐lame (UCLL) limbs had multiple regions of IRU, including moderate‐severe uptake.

In a rat model of early osteoarthritis, operated femorotibial joints showed 18F‐NaF IRU in limited regions by Week 2 postoperatively and all study regions 8 weeks postoperatively with the regions of IRU significantly correlated with histopathologic changes [22]. Although the rat study had no MRI or other imaging comparison to the PET imaging, a 2015 paper by Kobayashi et al. investigated the usefulness of 18F‐NaF for the detection of early hip osteoarthritis in human patients. In this study, the rate of findings indicative of osteoarthritis (MRI) or bone remodeling at the joint (PET) in the hip was higher when using PET compared to MRI. Additionally, in patients in the early‐stage osteoarthritis groups, as indicated by the Kellgren and Lawrence (radiographic) score, the PET uptake (SUVmax > 6.5) rate was greater than the MRI positivity rate [23]. It is likely give the information in this small population of horses, and studies in equines and other species that 18F‐NaF IRU may often precede MRI findings in the early stages of bone disease, especially with the limitations of low‐field MRI.

One surprising 18F‐NaF IRU region was the palmar cortex of P2, with 9 of 28 limbs having isotope uptake, including two with moderate‐severe IRU. The uptake in this region may reflect the effect of the sesamoidean or axial/abaxial palmar ligament entheses on proximal P1 and P2. However, there are no reported MRI findings in the palmar cortex of P2 in reports of straight and oblique sesamoidean desmitis, although bone pathology was not a focus of the reports [24, 25]. A single report of palmar/plantar ligament desmitis of the PIPJ diagnosed by MRI indicated 3 of 29 horses had enthesopathy of the axial ligament (2 cases) or abaxial (1 case), and this study noted the difficulty with identification of these ligaments with LF‐MRI [26]. None of the horses in our study with palmar cortical 18F‐NaF IRU had evidence of sesamoidean desmitis on the MRI imaging. A retrospective study of outcomes of horses diagnosed with straight or oblique sesamoidean ligament injury reported only 55% returned to soundness, and on 31% returning to intended use [24]. Bone pain at the insertion of these ligaments, with or without desmitis, could be a plausible source of pain in some horses.

We also hypothesized that 18F‐FDG PET would be less predictive than LF‐MRI for identifying active soft tissue injury. We accept this hypothesis given that the study's primary soft tissue regions and subregions, DDFT, hoof subregion lamina, and DIPJ subregions medial and lateral collateral ligaments, had no IRU in any limbs. However, in this study, 18F‐FDG PET did identify 15 limbs with an increased 18F‐FDG PET uptake in some areas within the navicular bursa, and 13 of these limbs had MRI abnormalities associated with the navicular bursa. Our results indicate that 18F‐FDG PET may be useful for detecting activity in the navicular bursa, but it was underwhelming for other soft tissue structures in the foot.

At the time of this study, protocols for dual‐tracer studies were just being developed. Spriet et al. [15] found that 18F‐FDG could be identified as early as 10 min after injection of the isotope in tendinous lesions. This study, 15 investigating the optimal approach for a dual‐tracer PET protocol, concluded that 18F‐NaF should be injected first. After approximately 2 h (one half‐life 18F‐NaF), 18F‐FDG is injected, with imaging beginning as early as 10 min later. Spriet et al. [15] noted poorer uptake of 18F‐NaF in bone when injected, whereas the horse was under general anesthesia versus when the isotope was injected prior to anesthesia. Multiple studies site decreased radioisotope uptake in the distal limb, specifically the foot [27, 28, 29] implicating reduced perfusion due to shunting of blood away from the distal limb or differences in pressures between laminar tissues and capillaries of the hoof [28, 29], whereas the same group noted good distribution and retention of radiolabeled mesenchymal stem cell to the hoof when administered to the standing horse via intra‐arterial injection [30]. Although decreased perfusion may be a scenario for inability to detect 18F‐FDG uptake in hoof soft tissue injuries, another possibility for the current study's lack 18F‐FDG uptake in soft tissues is the ability or affinity of the isotope itself to bind at sites of tendinous or ligamentous injury. In human medicine, 18F‐FDG is commonly used to identify inflammatory and infectious conditions such as fever of unknown origin, bacteremia or septicemia, spondylodiscitis, and osteomyelitis [31]. Patients afflicted with these conditions are critically ill, and there is likely significant glucose activity of inflammatory or infectious cells with increased expression of GLUT transporters. Given the reported relative lack of vascularity in the suprasesamoidean DDFT [32], despite an increase in blood vessels with injury [33], it is plausible that even with a severe DDFT injury, there could be compromised 18F‐FDG uptake, due to a relative lack of upregulation of GLUT transporters in the injured region to result in significant uptake. In this study, 15 of 28 limbs were recorded as having DDF tendinopathy by our DACVR, DACVR‐EDI, with nine of the 15 DDFT lesions being scored as the cause of lameness. However, despite the frequency of DDFT lesions in this study and in other studies [27], no increased uptake of 18F‐FDG was noted in any lesion (Figure 10), which is in contrast to seven of 16 lesions detected using 18F‐FDG reported by Wilson et al. [14]. In both the current and in Spriet et al. [15], horses received the same systemic dose of 18F‐FDG, and imaging was done approximately 1‐h post isotope injection, with the major differences being the anesthesia and the order of isotope administration. In our protocol, 18F‐FDG was the first isotope injected in contrast to Spriet et al. [15], which concluded, as mentioned, that the ideal order of administration would be injection of 18F‐NaF first; however, our study preceded Spriet et al. [15], thus, did not have this information available for planning our protocol.

This study aimed to report 18F‐FDG and 18F‐NaF SUVmax for specific regions in the foot with IRU. SUV are a quantitative measurement of the concentration of a radiotracer in a defined region. Most reported are SUVmax, which is the single voxel in the selected region with the highest radiopharmaceutical uptake. Other measures include SUVmean, the single voxel in the selected region with the average RU, and SUVmin, the voxel with the lowest RU. SUVmax may misrepresent the extent of the uptake, and therefore the extent of the injury, tumor, and so forth, as it represents only a single voxel and the voxel with the maximal uptake. To improve the performance of SUV, SUVratio is the ratio of SUVmax of a clinically affected region to the SUVmax of a universal target (clinically unaffected) region, allowing for normalization of the SUVmax in the affected area. The SUVmax of a universal target is background uptake and referred to as SUVbackground (SUVbg). In the current study, SUVbg was determined at the distal aspect of the second phalanx, as this region was a uniform area without significant bone remodeling. Using the BRU software, for each region and subregion, SUVmin, SUVmean, SUVmax, and SUVratio were reported for both 18F‐FDG and 18F‐NaF. The utility of SUVratio may come into play when comparing recheck or serial PET scans of the same horse, as an objective measure of change between scans, especially given that in the current study, the means of the subjectively graded 18F‐NaF studies were not significantly different from each other, indicating subjective grading is unlikely to be beneficial for comparison between studies.

MV has not been previously reported in the horse. MV differs from SUVmax as it accounts for background isotope uptake and includes thresholds to remove clinically insignificant outliers within a selected cuboidal region of voxels rather than the uptake at the single highest voxel. More studies with MV measurements need to be performed to determine the best use in equine musculoskeletal imaging.

The study's primary limitation was the small population size (n = 14 horses/28 limbs). The low number of horses significantly impacted the statistical analysis and significance of the data. Additionally, both limbs of each horse were included in the study, including the non‐lame limbs of horses with unilateral lameness. Despite these horses presenting for and having lameness localizing only to a single limb, these “unaffected” limbs all had abnormalities on MRI imaging, as described by the radiologist, and regions of IRU on PET imaging. Interpretation of MRI and PET findings in these nonclinical limbs must be performed with caution. Lastly, as with much of the PET literature published, there is no histologic correlation of any PET or MRI findings in this study, as none of the horses in the current study were euthanized. Another concern is the calculation of SUVratio for the 18F‐FDG portion of our study, which was the use of bone tissue for SUVbg, that is, the distal aspect of the trabecular bone of P2, rather than soft tissue. SUVratio may have been more significant for soft tissues, such as the DDFT, if it had been calculated using a background of a soft tissue structure, rather than bone. Selecting a sesamoidean ligament or a more proximal portion of the DDFT could have been considered.

Our kappa results reflect the relative severity concordance rather than direct equivalence between the grading of MRI and PET images. MRI and PET scoring systems are modality‐specific and not directly interchangeable. Our intent, however, was not to equate categories across modalities, but rather to evaluate whether increasing degrees of abnormality on MRI correspond to increasing degrees of metabolic activity on PET at an ordinal level. Because both scales define ordered categories (normal, mild, abnormal on both MRI and PET) reflecting severity, we used weighted kappa to assess ordinal agreement rather than exact categorical concordance. This approach has been widely used to compare ordered clinical grading systems that differ in underlying measurement characteristics.

Lastly, as mentioned, there was no attenuation correction used in the processing of the PET images, which may have resulted in an artifactually low or incorrect RU.

This study describes the findings of 18F‐FDG–PET and 18F‐NaF–PET imaging of the front feet in a population of lame horses and compares the PET imaging with LF‐MRI. On the basis of our results, low‐field MRI may underrepresent bone remodeling occurring and potentially misrepresent the source of lameness. We report various standardized uptake values for regions within the foot with IRU for 18F‐FDG and 18F‐NaF. Future research should aim at larger populations of horses undergoing dual‐tracer 18F protocols paired with low‐ and high‐field MRI.
