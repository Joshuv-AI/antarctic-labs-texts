An integrated framework for evaluating sustainable outcomes of vocational college graduates under ethnic heterogeneity and unequal college level resource allocation
Authors: Jun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In ethnically diverse regions, sustainable employment and entrepreneurship outcomes for vocational college graduates are constrained by two structural challenges: (i) pronounced heterogeneity across ethnic groups in cultural background, social capital, and development pathways, and (ii) unequal allocation of college-level resources that widens disparities in capability development and opportunity access. Their interaction amplifies outcome heterogeneity and increases the complexity of sustainability-oriented evaluation. To address this, we propose an integrated assessment framework combining Dual-Dimension Enhanced Pattern Rule Recognition (D-DEPRR) and a Factor Contribution Measurement (FCM) model. The framework identifies weakly expressed yet high-impact factors and quantifies their influence under heterogeneous ethnic and institutional conditions. Using survey data from 1,979 graduating students at a vocational college in an ethnic minority region of China, we benchmark the proposed approach against LSTM, BPNN, and SVM. Results show consistent improvements in ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, MCC, and Brier score, together with enhanced interpretability for key sustainability drivers. Findings indicate that intrinsic motivation and skill acquisition are core dimensions supporting sustainable outcomes. In addition, low-frequency support-related factors, including ethnically targeted guidance services and unequal access to institutional resources, exert disproportionately large impacts on minority student subgroups.

Introduction

Research background

Xiangxi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, located in Hunan Province, China, is a remote ethnic minority region characterized by a pronounced imbalance in the hierarchical distribution of higher education resources. According to official statistics1, the prefecture has only one undergraduate institution, whereas vocational institutions number 28. Under this structural configuration, vocational education assumes the principal role in cultivating skilled human capital, thereby directly influencing regional human capital accumulation and endogenous development capacity.

At the national level, higher vocational graduates face structural pressures in the labor market. The expansion of vocational education does not necessarily resolve skill mismatches or guarantee stable employment outcomes2. Educational credentials function as screening signals in recruitment processes. Under China’s higher education expansion, academic degrees serve as salient market signals that shape employers’ hiring expectations and generate differentiated returns across educational levels3,4, consistent with signaling theory in labor markets. Meanwhile, technological transformation and labor market restructuring continue to raise skill thresholds and intensify competition5,6,7, and youth unemployment and underemployment remain persistent8. Accordingly, the central concern extends beyond employment attainment to the long-term stability, quality, and developmental sustainability of graduates’ career trajectories. This issue is closely related to structural opportunity inequality, as differential returns to education may systematically disadvantage certain educational groups.

In ethnic minority regions, these structural pressures may be further amplified by contextual constraints. Perceived returns to higher education differ across ethnic groups, implying that identical educational attainment may translate into unequal employment opportunities across social settings9. In addition, vocational students in remote minority regions are predominantly drawn from local communities and often exhibit strong place attachment and relatively lower mobility intentions, reinforcing preferences for local employment10. When local labor demand is limited, such retention tendencies may heighten the risk of regional human capital imbalance and constrain endogenous development. In this context, employment and entrepreneurship sustainability becomes not merely an individual labor market outcome, but a structural determinant of regional resilience and inclusive growth.

Consequently, enhancing the quality and effectiveness of vocational education in minority regions and promoting sustainable employment and entrepreneurship among local graduates are essential for strengthening regional human capital retention and supporting long-term socioeconomic resilience. Achieving this objective requires evaluation methods capable of accurately evaluating sustainability outcomes and identifying the key determinants shaping these trajectories under structurally heterogeneous conditions.

Existing methods and deficiencies

Existing evaluation methods for employment and entrepreneurship outcomes in vocational education are primarily structured around two dimensions: feature selection and model algorithm design.

From the perspective of feature selection, recent studies conducted both domestically and internationally have explored the employment and entrepreneurial capacities of vocational graduates from various angles, including employment determinants, vocational competence assessment, and industry-education integration, yielding partial theoretical and practical insights. For instance, reference11 constructs multidimensional employability indices that integrate personal attributes, competency development, and external environmental factors; reference12 analyzes employability expectations from the employer perspective to identify key capability requirements and better align training outcomes with labor market needs. Still others highlight the roles of innovation capability, social adaptability, and self-efficacy in enhancing undergraduates’ employability, emphasizing the impact of intrinsic motivation and behavioral tendencies on career trajectories13 While these studies provide valuable insights from different dimensions, they tend to adopt single-perspective or component-based analytical approaches and lack a systematic construction of the overall indicator framework.

In terms of weight assignment and evaluation algorithm design, traditional approaches such as the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) and entropy-based methods remain widely used14. These techniques derive weights from expert judgments or global sample statistics, providing structural clarity but implicitly assuming balanced data distributions and stable indicator behaviors15. Their effectiveness tends to decline in heterogeneous environments where group composition varies and indicator dynamics deviate from linear additivity16. Specifically, AHP relies on subjective pairwise comparisons that may inadequately capture systematic subgroup differences17, while entropy-based methods often overweight frequent variables and underrepresent pivotal but infrequent indicators due to sensitivity to data composition and preprocessing choices18. To address nonlinear interactions and complex feature relationships, machine learning and deep learning approaches have increasingly been introduced into evaluation tasks19. SVM-based models enhance classification robustness in heterogeneous data settings20, LSTM-based models capture temporal dependencies and nonlinear employment risks21, and BPNN frameworks model complex educational evaluation processes22. Although these methods improve predictive performance, they often prioritize overall accuracy over interpretability, thereby limiting their ability to explicitly identify which determinants drive sustainability outcomes across structurally diverse contexts.

Methodological content and contribution

In response to the challenges identified above, this study proposes an integrated analytical framework termed D-DEPRR-FCM, which combines big data mining techniques with system-level contribution analysis to evaluate sustainable employment and entrepreneurship outcomes in ethnic minority vocational colleges.

The first component of the framework builds upon Pattern Rule Recognition (PRR), a data-driven mining approach capable of extracting interpretable association rules from large-scale multivariable datasets without requiring predefined model structures23,24,25. By identifying feature combinations that link premise conditions to target outcomes, PRR enables multidimensional interaction analysis and overcomes the single-perspective limitation inherent in traditional feature selection strategies. In this study, four major feature dimensions are integrated within the PRR-based structure, including static individual attributes, behavioral drivers, competence development indicators, and external environmental conditions, thereby facilitating a comprehensive representation of sustainability determinants.

However, conventional PRR approaches typically rely on global frequency-based metrics such as support and confidence to assess rule stability and significance26,27. While effective in relatively balanced datasets, such frequency-driven mechanisms may introduce identification bias in structurally heterogeneous contexts. Feature combinations that are weakly expressed at the aggregate level but substantively influential within specific minority subgroups may fail to satisfy global thresholds and thus be undervalued.

To address this limitation, this study develops a Dual-Dimension Enhanced Pattern Rule Recognition (D-DEPRR) model. D-DEPRR introduces a progressive dual-layer mining strategy. The first stage extracts globally frequent and stable rules using unified thresholds, while the second stage focuses on rare yet potentially high-value patterns omitted in the initial screening. By segmenting the dataset according to ethnic groups and incorporating two structural mechanisms—Ethnicity-Guided Adaptive Threshold Mechanism (EG-ATM) and Discipline-Guided Structural Scoring Mechanism (DG-SSM)—the model recalibrates rule evaluation criteria to reduce bias induced by uneven ethnic distribution and school-level resource disparities.

Notably, the feature variables identified through D-DEPRR often correspond to small-sample or low-frequency combinations that exert substantial influence on sustainability outcomes. Direct application of frequency-based static weighting may severely underestimate their importance, whereas predictive machine learning methods may produce unstable estimates due to limited sample size.

To overcome this challenge, the framework further incorporates a Factor Contribution Measurement (FCM) component. The evaluation of employment and entrepreneurship sustainability is conceptualized as an integrated system composed of multiple feature-variable components that exert synergistic or constraining effects. By examining the magnitude of change in the system’s overall output when each feature occurs or does not occur, FCM quantitatively measures the relative contribution of each determinant to sustainability outcomes.

By integrating D-DEPRR and FCM, the proposed D-DEPRR-FCM framework achieves both structural bias correction and contribution quantification. Owing to the inherent interpretability of rule-based mining and system-level contribution analysis, the framework enables transparent identification of feature combinations associated with sustainability outcomes as well as their relative importance. This provides actionable evidence for institutional adjustment, including refinement of training structures, targeted enhancement of high-leverage factors, and optimization of support mechanisms in ethnic minority vocational colleges.

Models and methods

This chapter introduces the three core components of the integrated model developed in this study. First, a multidimensional feature database is constructed to support the sustainability evaluation of employment and entrepreneurship among vocational college students in ethnic minority regions, comprehensively integrating key dimensions such as Personal characteristic, intrinsic motivation, skill acquisition, and support environment. Second, the D-DEPRR model is designed to identify high-relevance rule combinations associated with sustainability objectives under conditions of uneven ethnic distribution and unequal allocation of school-level resources. Third, the FCM model is developed to quantify the contribution levels of different feature variables to the system’s target outcomes, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to capture the significance of weakly expressed but highly influential variables.

Construction of the input feature database

To scientifically evaluate the sustainability of employment and entrepreneurship among students in ethnic minority vocational regions, it is essential to establish a high-quality data foundation that incorporates both structural awareness and indicator completeness. This study builds a sustainability feature database based on the 2024 graduate survey data from Xiangxi Vocational and Technical College for Nationalities, focusing on key influencing factors throughout the employment and entrepreneurship processes. The database is systematically designed to capture a comprehensive profile of students by integrating multiple dimensions, including static individual attributes, behavioral drivers, competence development, and external environmental conditions. In this study, sustainability is defined as the capacity of graduates to maintain stable and development-oriented employment or entrepreneurial trajectories within a specified observation period. This definition follows multi-dimensional decent work and job quality frameworks that emphasize employment stability, earnings adequacy, working time conditions, and access to social protection. Accordingly, we construct a binary outcome variable to indicate whether a graduate achieves sustainable employment or sustainable entrepreneurship status.

Sustainable employment is coded as 1 if the graduate (i) remains employed for at least six consecutive months after graduation, (ii) possesses at least one verifiable element of employment security, such as a formal contract or participation in statutory social insurance, and (iii) meets minimum job quality standards in terms of income and reasonable working hours. These criteria reflect internationally recognized dimensions of employment quality and labor market security28,29. Sustainable entrepreneurship is coded as 1 if the graduate completes formal registration and maintains active business operation for at least six months, consistent with the widely adopted practice of using venture survival as a baseline indicator of entrepreneurial sustainability rather than registration alone30. Graduates who fail to meet these conditions are coded as 0.

Selection of Pattern Rule Recognition (PRR) Indicators

The Pattern Rule Recognition (PRR) model is a pattern recognition technique designed for multivariable feature databases. Its purpose is to extract stable combinations of variables from large and complex sets of input features, thereby revealing which feature combinations exert a significant guiding influence on employment and entrepreneurship outcomes. The core task of this process is to identify important rules that capture the connection between premise conditions and target results.

Conventional PRR methods generally apply a unified scoring system and fixed thresholds across the entire sample, executing a frequency-oriented global filtering strategy. This approach is well-suited for datasets with relatively balanced feature distributions, enabling the effective extraction of high-frequency, stable, and generalizable rules. However, in the practical context of higher vocational education in ethnic minority regions, students often exhibit significantly biased distributions in employment and entrepreneurship outcomes due to differences in ethnic affiliation and disparities in internship opportunities, recognition levels, and other resource allocations associated with their school-level disciplines. These biases make it difficult for globally oriented models to effectively capture weakly expressed yet High-Contribution rule patterns, leading to the risks of important information being obscured and interpretive imbalance.

To address these issues, this study develops a Dual-Dimension Enhanced Pattern Rule Recognition (D-DEPRR) model that accounts for biases in both ethnic affiliation and school-level resource allocation, and proposes a progressive “global pre-filtering – local fine-filtering” identification framework. In the first stage, a unified identification criterion is applied to the entire sample to rapidly extract initial high-contribution variable sets whose support and confidence values meet the threshold requirements. In the second stage, for variables omitted during the initial filtering, the sample is partitioned into subsets according to ethnic attributes, and two perception mechanisms are introduced for local re-identification:

Conventional PRR methods perform rule identification directly based on statistics from the entire sample and by applying uniform indicator thresholds. To enhance the adaptability and precision of the model in addressing evaluation problems with biased feature distributions, and to strengthen its capacity for mining weakly expressed yet high-contribution rules within small-sample subgroups, this study introduces an Ethnicity-Guided Adaptive Threshold Mechanism (EG-ATM) based on Bayesian smoothing. Starting from the baseline thresholds established for the entire sample, EG-ATM integrates ethnic group proportions, sample sizes, and structural disturbance variables, and applies a dynamically constructed adjustment function to heterogenously modify the minimum filtering thresholds for the five rule evaluation metrics. Design of the Factor Contribution Measurement (FCM) model

19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{M}\mathrm{s}}_{{F}_{m(i,j)}}^{\mathrm{B}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{b}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{m}}=\frac{\vartheta\:\left[{\mathcal{I}}^{\xi\:}\left({M}_{f}\right)\right]}{\vartheta\:\left[{\mathcal{I}}^{\xi\:}\left({F}_{m(i,j)}\right)\right]}$$\end{document}

Traditional weight allocation methods typically assign static, uniform evaluation weights to feature variables based on their frequency proportions or statistical dispersion measures in the full sample. While such approaches offer advantages in computational simplicity and global interpretability, they are essentially built on the assumption that the importance of a feature variable can be measured by its occurrence frequency in the overall dataset and that this importance remains consistent across subgroups or application contexts. However, among the feature combinations extracted through the D-DEPRR model proposed in this study, many rule patterns, although occurring infrequently in the overall sample, exhibit strong target associations and pronounced group-specific characteristics. Continuing to adopt traditional static weighting strategies would systematically underestimate the importance of these “weakly expressed but high-contribution” rules, thereby introducing substantial bias in the evaluation results.

To address this problem, this study proposes the FCM model. Drawing on the concept of system-component contribution analysis31, the employment and entrepreneurship sustainability evaluation task is conceptualized as an integrated system composed of multiple feature-variable components that exert either synergistic or constraining effects. By analyzing the magnitude of change in the system’s overall output trend when each feature variable occurs or does not occur, the model quantifies its contribution. The contribution value reflects the relative importance ranking of each component in achieving the system’s objective. Therefore, by adopting the contribution value as the benchmark for feature weighting, the model avoids the systematic underestimation of weakly expressed data inherent in traditional static methods. Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The research team accessed the dataset with institutional authorization and used it solely for academic research. Empirical case study

Input data analysis

This study is based on survey data from the 2024 graduating cohort of Xiangxi Vocational and Technical College for Nationalities, covering all eight secondary schools of the institution. As the only higher vocational institution in Xiangxi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, the college serves as the primary provider of advanced vocational education and skilled talent cultivation in the region. The data were collected via a structured questionnaire focusing on four primary dimensions of employment and entrepreneurship, with 26 indicators established in total. To ensure rigorous out-of-sample validation and avoid information leakage, the dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%) and an independent test set (30%) prior to model development. All subsequent rule mining, threshold calibration, structural scoring, and contribution measurement procedures were conducted exclusively using the training data. The test set was strictly held out for final performance evaluation. As the graduate survey was conducted in questionnaire form, the extracted heterogeneous data include continuous variables. Since the proposed model requires discrete inputs, a discretization process was applied to the continuous variables using the training data only, and the derived discretization rules were then applied to the test set. The specific input feature variables are presented in Table 1.

Their domain organization was informed by prior studies on graduate employability, graduate capital, career adaptability, and entrepreneurial intention, which collectively support the inclusion of individual background conditions, motivational orientation, competency accumulation, and external support factors in the analysis of graduate employment and entrepreneurship outcomes33–35. Accordingly, the questionnaire was not treated as an arbitrary collection of survey items, but as a structured multidimensional instrument aligned with the analytical objectives of this study. Each retained item was explicitly mapped to its corresponding empirical variable, variable type, and coding rule, thereby ensuring consistency between questionnaire design and subsequent analytical implementation. On this basis, the validation of the questionnaire in the present study was primarily reflected in the literature-informed domain structure, the explicit item-to-variable mapping, and the coherence between survey design and model input construction.

In implementing the proposed model workflow, the D-DEPRR model is first applied to the rule pattern recognition stage of the survey dataset to identify and retain key variables that exert significant influence on the evaluation results but appear infrequently in the sample, thereby ensuring the comprehensiveness and accuracy of the evaluation. Performance analysis evaluation

To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed D-DEPRR-FCM model, a 10-fold cross-validation strategy was adopted. Four evaluation metrics were considered: the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC)36 curve, the Precision–Recall (PR)37 curve, the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and the Brier Score38. ROC and PR curves assess overall classification ability under varying thresholds, with PR being particularly sensitive to the detection of minority classes under class imbalance. MCC captures the joint contribution of all confusion-matrix components (TP, TN, FP, FN), providing a balanced performance measure across thresholds, while the Brier Score evaluates the calibration of probabilistic predictions, where lower values indicate superior reliability. The general structure of the confusion matrix used for metric derivation is presented in Table 3.

Confidence interval and robustness analysis

To rigorously evaluate the robustness of the proposed model, this section applies a confidence interval–based performance analysis using the four evaluation metrics introduced in Sect. 3.2: ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, MCC, and Brier Score. The purpose is to assess whether D-DEPRR-FCM outperforms conventional approaches and whether the observed differences are statistically meaningful across data partitions. A 10-fold cross-validation procedure is adopted to reduce sampling bias and ensure the reliability of the results. For each fold, the performance of all candidate models (D-DEPRR-FCM, LSTM, BPNN, and SVM) is recorded, and the aggregated mean values and corresponding 95% confidence intervals are reported. In addition, significance testing against random baselines is performed to confirm that the observed improvements are not due to chance. Empirical interpretation analysis

This section turns to empirical interpretation. Explanatory variables continue to follow the four dimensions in Table 1, with ethnicity and school-level resource allocation included as structural controls. Rather than using the full set of raw features, this section focuses on the high-contribution factors identified by the D-DEPRR-FCM model, including both widely expressed variables and weakly expressed but high-impact factors. These variables were selected because the rule-recognition and contribution analysis of the D-DEPRR-FCM model have identified them as key determinants of sustainable academic employment and entrepreneurship.

Empirical testing was thus designed to quantify their explanatory value. At the univariate level, χ² tests were applied to binary factors and Spearman or point-biserial correlations to ordinal factors, while Fisher’s exact test was used for weakly expressed variables to ensure robustness. At the multivariate level, logistic regressions incorporated all high-contribution variables, with ethnicity and school fixed effects, and were summarized using odds ratios (OR) and average marginal effects (AMEs). In addition, the Factor Contribution Measurement (FCM) method was directly integrated into the regression framework, with contribution scores reported alongside ORs and AMEs. This combined strategy ensures that both statistical significance and systemic importance are captured simultaneously.

Conclusion

To address the challenges of evaluating employment and entrepreneurship sustainability among vocational college students in ethnic minority regions, this study proposes the D-DEPRR-FCM model, which integrates D-DEPRR with FCM. The main findings can be summarized as follows:

By constructing a structurally annotated dataset that integrates ethnic attributes and school-level resource allocation information, the proposed model achieves the identification of rules that are “weakly expressed yet high in contribution”—a type of rule often overlooked in conventional evaluation methods. Specifically, the D-DEPRR component is designed with two key mechanisms: an ethnicity-guided adaptive threshold and a discipline-guided scoring mechanism. These mechanisms work in synergy to enhance the recognition of valuable latent patterns, especially under the challenge of data imbalance.The FCM component evaluates the contribution of both initial and weakly expressed but high-contribution variables by measuring their perturbation effects on the overall system, rather than relying on frequency counts. For policymakers and educators, this implies strengthening general training, planning, and support systems, while also investing in selective but transformative opportunities that disproportionately benefit students. Future work will extend the framework to multi-institutional and longitudinal datasets and incorporate interaction-aware or causal modeling techniques to further validate robustness and applicability.

For policymakers and educators, this implies strengthening general training, planning, and support systems, while also investing in selective but transformative opportunities that disproportionately benefit students. Thus, the D-DEPRR-FCM framework contributes both methodological innovation and practical guidance for promoting inclusive and sustainable employment and entrepreneurship development in ethnic minority vocational education.

Limitations and future work. This study is based on a single-institution graduate questionnaire survey and self-reported measures, which may limit external generalizability and introduce reporting bias. Moreover, the cross-sectional design supports associative rather than strictly causal interpretation. In addition, the modeling process implicitly assumes relative independence among factors, while real-world contexts may involve causal coupling and interaction effects. Future work will extend the framework to multi-institutional and longitudinal datasets and incorporate interaction-aware or causal modeling techniques to further validate robustness and applicability.
