An intervention mapping approach to closing the gap in maternal OUD and infant NOWS care: a study protocol
Authors: Sara L. Kornfield, Emma Farber, Erica M. Reisinger, Nia Bhadra-Heintz, Navid Roder, Rebecca F. Hamm, Henry Kranzler, David S. Mandell, Emily Becker-Haimes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing prevalence of pregnant and postpartum individuals affected by opioid use disorder (OUD) in the US is highlighted by the growing number of babies referred to the NICU for neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome after birth. Despite advances in integrating mental health and substance use treatment into adult healthcare settings, no treatment models exist to integrate maternal OUD treatment and referrals into the pediatric care setting. Integrating maternal OUD treatment into the NICU could help mothers access care and improve maternal functioning, while preserving the dyadic bond and maintaining stability for the infant.MethodThis study will use implementation mapping to develop and refine strategies to support NICU setting buprenorphine induction for mothers of babies hospitalized with NOWS to ensure treatment meets the needs of both providers and patients. Aim 1 will identify the primary barriers and facilitators to implementing bedside NICU OUD treatment and referrals to ongoing outpatient care for postpartum women through in-depth interviews with local NICU providers. Aim 2 will use the insights gained in Aim 1 to guide a process of implementation mapping to generate a suite of implementation strategies to support co-located buprenorphine induction and referrals for treating pregnant women with OUD in the NICU. Finally, in Aim 3 we will refine and examine the feasibility and acceptability of the implementation strategies by surveying stakeholders (n = 32); and conduct a pilot case series (n = 20 mothers). This will allow us to determine the best composition of the treatment team, where care should be delivered, and how providers, patients, and stakeholders respond.DiscussionResults will establish the acceptability and feasibility of integrating OUD care into the NICU setting and identify the most promising implementation strategies for supporting this model in future confirmatory trials testing the effectiveness of NICU bedside buprenorphine induction and referrals to treatment on maternal and pediatric outcomes.Trial registrationThis study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov on 08/28/2024 (NCT06576323) https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06576323?term=R21DA058815&rank=1.

Introduction

The prevalence of opioid use disorder (OUD) among women giving birth in US hospitals has increased annually since 2001, with accidental drug overdoses accounting for the majority of all pregnancy-associated (pregnancy and the one year period after delivery) morbidity and mortality in many states [1–4]. OUD affects 9.6% of all pregnancies, and is associated with increased maternal death, overdose, HIV acquisition, mental health diagnoses, exposure to violence, and inadequate prenatal and postpartum care [5–10]. These risks are elevated in those who are younger, of color, single, unhoused, with lower completed education levels, and for those not on medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) [11].

Despite ACOG recommendations for universal screening, the diagnosis of OUD during pregnancy is often inadequate [12, 13]. Even for those who are diagnosed with OUD in the prenatal or postpartum periods, connection to treatment is only successful for 50% of women [14]. Additionally, for those who are in OUD treatment prenatally, substantial rates of postpartum treatment cessation are reported [15]. The risk of overdose among peripartum individuals with OUD peaks between 7 and 12 months postpartum, highlighting the critical role of connection to treatment during the early postpartum period [16]. For individuals with OUD, barriers for treatment include fear of criminalization, past experiences of healthcare discrimination, and insufficient provider knowledge about MOUD during pregnancy and the postpartum [17].

Neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS) is a withdrawal syndrome in infants following in utero exposure to substances, characterized by irritability, poor feeding, respiratory distress, tremors, and seizures [18]. NAS increases the length of hospital stays for affected infants by 3.5 times, up to an average of ~ 16 days [19]. While NAS broadly refers to the exposure of any licit or illicit substance, Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (NOWS) is specific to opioids. The terms NAS and NOWS are often used interchangeably, however NOWS refers specifically to infants with opioid exposures, while NAS can be used to describe polysubstance exposure. As the opioid epidemic has spread across the United States, the incidence of NAS has increased exponentially [20]; as of 2014, one infant was born with NAS every 15 min in the US, totaling 32,000 infants annually [21]. Increases in maternal opioid use directly correspond to an 82% increase in NAS from 2010-2017 [22, 23]. From 2000 to 2016, NOWS occurrences increased from 1.2 to 8.8 per 1000 hospital births [24].

Rates of postpartum follow-up for those with active opioid use and not using MOUD are lower than for those using MOUD [25, 26]. Those in active use and not connected to treatment or MOUD represent an especially vulnerable population and are at highest risk of non-engagement in outpatient general postpartum care and overdose. As many mothers are at the beside in the NICU during their infants’ prolonged hospitalizations, the NICU stay therefore represents a critical period in which to diagnose maternal OUD in those at highest risk of morbidity and mortality, initiate treatment or reconnect with care, and refer for ongoing care in a setting that prioritizes and preserves maternal-infant dyadic bonding.

The current study will focus on women with OUD whose infants are diagnosed with and remain hospitalized for treatment of NOWS. As above, infants with NOWS have longer hospital stays and are more likely than non-opioid exposed babies to be transferred to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) [27, 28], while their mothers generally are discharged from inpatient postpartum services at the traditional time.

While obstetric providers generally report feeling confident about screening pregnant and postpartum women for OUD, few feel prepared to prescribe MOUD to postpartum women [29]. Similarly, large-scale studies investigating screening for perinatal mental health conditions in pediatric settings have found high rates of screening that result in no benefit given the absence of systems organized to initiate treatment [30, 31]. And even in cases where postpartum individuals are screened and referred, many addiction treatment centers may not feel comfortable treating pregnant patients, or are not up to date on treatment practices in pregnancy and the postpartum, leaving folks without care or with inadequate care in the peripartum period [32]. The gold standard for OUD treatment during pregnancy and postpartum is MOUD, which differs from recommendations for the non-peripartum population, where medically managed withdrawal may be an option [33, 34]. This vulnerable population, at very high risk for return to use and overdose, thus “falls through the cracks” during the postpartum period.

Having a child hospitalized in the NICU offers a critical opportunity to identify mothers and to start treatment while preserving the mother-child bond. The stresses of being newly postpartum and caring for a sick and hospitalized infant can make pursuing OUD treatment feel insurmountable. Our formative work supports this; clinicians report that when referred off-site for MOUD treatment, mothers cite time, transportation, and effort as barriers to initiating or engaging in treatment [35]. Offering on-site buprenorphine initiation and follow-up care during the average 16-day infant stay could revolutionize the way we care for postpartum mothers with sick children [19]. Additionally, postpartum mothers with OUD caring for their children in the NICUs may be more motivated and receptive to starting treatment if offered in a collocated setting.

Leading methods from implementation science offer potential solutions for overcoming barriers to integrated MOUD treatment in the NICU. Specifically, implementation mapping is a useful tool to design theory-guided implementation strategies to support successful delivery of interventions in new contexts [36, 37]. It provides a step-by-step process to systematically develop and refine implementation strategies to maximize intervention fit and successful uptake of the intervention in novel practice settings via five iterative tasks [36, 38]. By systematically identifying performance objectives, determinants, and change objectives in collaboration with stakeholders, we can map theoretically derived methods to influence identified determinants to facilitate implementation success [36].

Objective

We outline a study protocol for the “SUPPORT” study- Strategies for Unifying Postpartum OUD Referrals and Treatment. Following the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [39], we will gather data on organizational, workflow, and psychological factors that impede successful OUD treatment and referral for postpartum mothers. We will share this information with a community advisory board to identify implementation barriers. We then will use implementation mapping to generate suggestions to overcome them, guided by leading models of behavior change [40]. This process has been used to address similar implementation challenges in other settings [41]. In collaboration with the advisory board, we will use this information to design implementation strategies and refine them via a pilot case series to make buprenorphine induction and referral more feasible and acceptable in NICUs. Our goal is to identify the needs of new mothers with OUD, adapt existing evidence-based models of co-located care to fit the NICU, and pilot test an implementation plan for NICU buprenorphine induction and referral to ongoing care in preparation for large-scale testing. Specifically, we will:

Aim 1: systematically identify the primary barriers and facilitators to implementing bedside OUD treatment in the NICU and referrals for MOUD

We will conduct an in-depth examination of local NICU parents and clinicians. We will interview mothers to determine how to make co-located or off-site OUD treatment more attractive and engagement more likely. We will interview NICU clinicians to determine which factors affect the likelihood of engaging in a discussion about the mother’s immediate postpartum health, making referrals for MOUD, and the potential acceptability of co-located MOUD in their setting. We will interview other clinicians who provide MOUD to determine their willingness and ability to do so in the NICU. This will include providers from specialties including but not limited to obstetrics and gynecology, family medicine, addiction medicine, and psychiatry.

Aim 2: use steps from implementation mapping to systematically develop implementation strategies for buprenorphine induction and referral for maternal OUD in the NICU

We will collaborate with a community advisory board to determine how best to deliver MOUD to postpartum mothers in the NICU. The result will be a flexible blueprint for initiating MOUD and transitioning patients to community treatment that is consistent with the resources and capabilities of each NICU.

Aim 3: examine the acceptability and feasibility of the implementation plan and refine the model for use in larger trials

We will pilot the resultant implementation strategies in two local partner NICUs. We will evaluate.

the acceptability of the model to patients, providers, and clinical and administrative leaders that were interviewed in Aim 1 using qualitative data from provider and parent feedback surveys and make additional refinements needed before larger scale confirmatory testing. We also will gather treatment initiation and retention metrics as preliminary outcomes data.

Methods

Conceptual framework

The SUPPORT study is guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) for the systematic assessment of potential barriers and facilitators to MOUD treatment in the NICU [39]. The CFIR is characterized by five major domains: (1) the intervention characteristics or features (i.e., on-site buprenorphine initiation); (2) the individuals involved in implementing the new program; (3) inner setting (i.e., the organizational characteristics of the hospital system and the NICU itself); (4) the outer setting (i.e., the social, economic, and political context surrounding the hospital system); and (5) the implementation process. For example, neonatology clinicians’ training in pediatric care and the belief that maternal care falls within the realm of OB care may conflict with the OB providers’ belief that their job is done when the patient is discharged from the postpartum unit, and both provider types may lack comfort and expertise in initiating MOUD. Similarly, inner setting barriers may include an unrecognized need for co-located care for postpartum mothers with OUD. In the outer-setting, administrative, financial, and policy barriers may prevent care provided to adults in a pediatric setting. To date, there has not been a systematic investigation of the factors that facilitate or impede the adoption of co-located NICU MOUD initiation and follow-up for postpartum mothers. As the CFIR is agnostic to causal pathways by which determinants of implementation interact to drive change, we will integrate CFIR determinants with leading theories of behavior change to guide implementation mapping.

Study design

The study uses a hybrid Type II design model to equally weight the importance of implementation and effectiveness of our intervention. First, we will conduct a needs assessment via in-depth qualitative interviews. The target intervention performance objective is induction and provision of buprenorphine to adult patients located in a pediatric setting (NICU). Consistent with previous studies of implementation mapping, the final implementation strategies are not yet known and will be guided by stakeholder and advisory board member input considering the barriers, facilitators, and patient preferences identified in Aim 1 [42]. Then, in partnership with our community advisory board, we will create a suite of implementation strategies resulting in a flexible protocol for implementing buprenorphine induction at the NICU bedside. We will then pilot the protocol in two partner NICUs and evaluate the acceptability of the model to patients, providers, and clinical, and administrative leaders. See Fig. 1.

Mixed methods (qualitative and quantitative, and their integration) will be used to achieve the aims and objectives of the study. Qualitative methods include the use of qualitative interviews, advisory board feedback, and surveys and workflow observations. Quantitative methods include the use of descriptive, frequency, and more advanced statistical analyses of the data collected from participant survey responses, and medical chart data collection, and review.

Advisory board

The PI and Co-investigators will be equal partners in the advisory board and will direct the implementation mapping process. Other stakeholders including clinical and administrative leaders from both of the partner NICUs, as well as those in Addiction services, Obstetrics and Gynecology, and Family Medicine; NICU nurses, social workers, Addiction Psychiatry and Medicine care managers, and parents will serve as expert members of the research team, offering advice based on their understanding of contextual factors in perinatal care, the NICU, addiction services, and their own experiences combined with the feedback from our qualitative data collected in Aim 1. Mothers asked to sit on the advisory board will be patients in recovery who are known to the clinicians on our team.

Selection of participants

Aim 1

Inclusion criteria for providers

The provider sample will include healthcare providers working within the health system who generally treat perinatal women or any individuals with OUD. Eligible provider participants must engage in the direct care to NICU patients and/or their mothers. For the purposes of this study, provider refers to a Medical Doctor (MD) or Doctor of Osteopathy (DO), Advanced Practice Provider, Nurse, Social Worker, or Administrator. Clinician/provider participants will be recruited by colleague referral and targeted emails.

Inclusion criteria for mothers

The parent sample will include biological mothers of children who have been hospitalized for NOWS within the last 2 years. Eligible mothers must be 18 years or older and have a diagnosis of an opioid use disorder. Birthing-parent participants will be recruited from clinics within an urban academic medical center that treat perinatal patients with substance use disorders, and the Intensive Care Nurseries and Well Baby Nurseries at two affiliated urban hospitals.

Aim 3

Inclusion criteria for clinicians

The same criteria used for providers in Aim 1 will be applied to clinicians in Aim 3. The criteria will be used to recruit new participants for Aim 3 if the same stakeholder interviewees are unavailable at the time of Aim 3.

Inclusion criteria for mothers

The mother sample in Aim 3 will include biological mothers of children actively hospitalized in either of two local NICUs for NOWS, be no more than 4 weeks postpartum, and have an OUD than is currently untreated with MOUD. Mothers who are under the age of 18, those who are experiencing active suicidal ideation, or other severe psychopathology that must be addressed before effective MOUD can be prescribed will be excluded.

Procedures and measures

Aim 1

Qualitative interviews

We anticipate conducting no fewer than 32 interviews (i.e., at least 10 clinicians, 6 social workers, 4 administrators, 12 patients); however, we will continue to recruit and conduct interviews until thematic saturation has been reached (i.e., when additional interviews are no longer yielding new themes). We recognize that patient diversity may present multiple themes that make thematic saturation challenging; including parents on our advisory board will assist the members in understanding important aspects of patient interview responses.

Interview questions will focus on barriers and facilitators at the clinician, intervention, inner setting, and outer setting barriers, guided by the CFIR and behavior change theory. We will query providers about their role in facilitating MOUD referral and treatment in the NICU, and about ways in which they think this can be achieved and sustained. “What do you perceive your role to be in connecting postpartum mothers to OUD treatment?” is an example of a question that addresses the clinician level. “What about the NICU setting may make co-located buprenorphine induction easier or harder” is an example of a question addressing the inner setting.

Interview questions for parent participants will focus on their opinions on receiving bedside buprenorphine induction for OUD care or being referred elsewhere, the likelihood of attending outpatient treatment in a setting outside of the NICU, and their past experiences with OUD care and what they would want in an ideal world. These may be phrased as “What would you like or not like about being offered treatment for OUD in the NICU?” or “Are there any issues that would make you less likely to engage in care for yourself, at your baby’s bedside?” and “Tell me about your good and bad experiences of OUD care before now.” To optimize our ability to translate qualitative findings into action, interviews will specifically query parents and clinicians about how they make decisions about seeking/referring to care, as well as factors that may facilitate or impede doing so (e.g., self-efficacy, competing demands, motivation, etc.) [43]. We will conduct in depth literature reviews to present information on various models of existing collocated OUD programs to seek feedback on the acceptability on each of these models to the interviewees. Interviews will take ~ 45 min and participants will receive $100.

Qualitative analysis

Aim 1 includes qualitative data collection from in-depth interviews with NICU providers, social workers, administrators, and mothers. This work is preparatory for Aim 2 in which we will use this data to develop a suite of implementation strategies for delivery of evidence-based models of MOUD to adult patients in a pediatric care setting. For this preparatory qualitative data collection, we will use sampling methods described by Guest, Bunce, and Johnson (2006) [44] to estimate the number of individuals in each group necessary to achieve saturation of themes from the interviews. Recruitment of approximately 6 members per group is expected to be sufficient to reach thematic saturation and identify issues that will be useful in Aim 2. However, should saturation not be attained with this sample size, we will continue to recruit until saturation is reached (anticipated maximum sample size = 10 per group, 12 for mothers to account for the diversity of themes given diversity of experiences among mothers with OUD). We anticipate enrolling 10 clinicians, 6 social workers, 4 administrators, and 12 mothers. Rather than a formal power analysis, recruitment numbers are based on reaching saturation in the data obtained [45–48].

Qualitative analysis of semi-structured interviews will allow us to better understand: (1) the acceptability and feasibility of bedside NICU MOUD induction and treatment, and (2) contextual implementation barriers and facilitators that must be addressed to achieve implementation success. Interviews will be transcribed with analyses supported by use of a qualitative software database. Qualitative analysis will be guided by an integrated approach [49] which uses an inductive process of iterative coding to identify recurrent themes, categories, and relationships. A structured codebook will be developed. We will code for expected themes based on pilot data using modified grounded theory [50], which provides a systematic and rigorous approach to identifying codes and themes. Using the qualitative data analysis software program, two members of the research team will separately code a sample of 3 transcripts and compare their application of the coding scheme to assess the reliability and robustness of the coding scheme. Disagreements in coding will be resolved through discussion and the codebook refined and applied to all transcripts. Coders will be expected to reach and maintain reliability at κ ≥ 0.85. After coding, we will read through all codes to examine themes and produce memos of examples and commentary. Qualitative interview data will be integrated with observational data [51] by triangulating or iteratively using them as a guide to ensure comprehensive assessment; and to guide the generation of a matrix of change targets for implementation to facilitate the development of a logic model of change, in preparation for Aim 2. Mixed methods will analyze themes as a function of clinician and organizational characteristics to identify patterns of responding, following NIH guidelines for best practices in mixed methods [52].

At the end of Aim 1, we anticipate having guidance from the themes identified from the interview data. Themes that we anticipate identifying include (1) parent and provider attitudes about the proposed concept of integrating maternal OUD care into the NICU setting, (2) feasibility, (3) desire, (4) specific suggestions for how to implement this protocol, (5) particular barriers including how privacy, space, and provider and/or parent time may play a role in our success, among others. We plan to use this information to lead the development of a standardized protocol that will be created in consultation with the advisory board.

Aim 2

The advisory board will meet monthly for 9 months for 1 h at a time and members will be compensated $50 per session for their attendance. Based on our pilot work, we hypothesize there are a discrete number of potential barriers and facilitators of providing buprenorphine in the NICU; Aim 1 will clarify which of our hypothesized barriers are most salient and critical to address in the design of implementation strategies in the implementation mapping process.

We will use our collective experience, expertise, and data from Aim 1 to generate strategies for delivering the evidence-based intervention with the goal of considering feasibility in the NICU setting, provider availability, and patient needs and desires. We will revisit the descriptions presented during the Aim 1 interview of the existing evidence-based models of providing buprenorphine in co-located settings such as primary care clinics [53–55], HIV specialty clinics [56, 57], inpatient units [58] and prenatal care clinics [59], and present the most promising models e.g. [60], based on stakeholder interview data so that we ensure our implementation plan includes strategies that will directly address change targets (i.e., provider attitudes, workflow issues, etc.) identified in the interviews. Leaders will come to each meeting with a drafted outline of a protocol which will be presented for feedback. The document will be revised in real time based on suggestions from the board. Advisory board members will be encouraged to suggest barriers to successful implementation of each component part based on their experiences, other themes identified in Aim 1, and the existing literature. We will identify possible strategies to support implementation of these core components, considering all relevant contextual domains (e.g., intervention, individual, inner, and outer settings), so that we can enhance the applicability to the NICU setting. We will make final decisions via consensus of a 70% majority, consistent with the literature [61]. Participatory design will allow flexibility to determine who provides the intervention and how, based on board member input. This also allows the generation of possible variations if iterative pilot testing reveals weaknesses in the initial implementation plan. Next, we will refine the program structure and organization, develop an intervention protocol, and conduct brief pilot tests of our protocol as a pretest. Relevant activities include but are not limited to identifying alternative workflows for clinicians, spaces where buprenorphine induction can occur for mothers, and administrative barriers.

Aim 3

In Aim 3 we will test and refine our adapted intervention model. We conceptualize this as a case-series, which refers to a non-randomized, observational design that follows participants subjected to the same intervention. A case-series is an efficient design to test novel strategies and question theoretical assumptions in preparation for more rigorous evaluation [62, 63]. Case series procedures will follow established recommendations [62, 64]. Barriers faced by individual participants during the case series will inform adaptations to address identified challenges throughout the case series phase. Pilot data from this case series will also be presented to the Advisory Board to allow for bidirectional exchange regarding changes needed to the novel adapted intervention model, to proactively identify additional barriers and facilitators to its implementation and to refine the manual outlining procedures. This will allow for rapid adaptations and refinements to be made to the intervention model in preparation for a more rigorous implementation trial in subsequent confirmatory trials.

Pilot case series (evaluating the implementation plan)

In partnership with the infant NICU medical team, a clinician comfortable with providing MOUD will be added to the team to care for the mother’s addiction treatment needs. The exact provider type (Ob/Gyn, Addiction Medicine, Addiction Psychiatry, Family Medicine, etc.), workflow strategies, and other details of the implementation of buprenorphine induction in the NICU will be identified in Aim 2. Ten mothers from each NICU site will be enrolled in the case series for a total of 20 mothers. Qualitative and quantitative data will be collected at enrollment (Time 1; T1) and at the end of the intervention phase, i.e., after a patient participant and clinician have met in the NICU (Time 2; T2). Observational workflow and medical chart data will be collected at T1 and T2 as well as at baby’s hospital discharge (Time 3; T3), and 4–8 weeks post-discharge (Time 4; T4). See Table 1.

Other data collection

Observational fidelity measures to determine whether the intervention was delivered in the way in which it was designed, will be gathered by on-site observation by study staff and medical chart review. We will collect quantitative data from medical chart review on the number and percentage of women who agreed to initiate MOUD in the NICU, the length of time it took to engage them in care, and the length of time they remained in treatment without relapse. On-site observations in our two partnering NICUs will be focused on de-identified provider behaviors (not that of study participants); therefore, we do not anticipate privacy concerns. Observations including, but not limited to, the following will be made: how long each provider type spends with the patient, where the intervention takes place (i.e., bedside, or another private room, etc.), whether there are interruptions by other members of the care team during the mothers’ treatment, etc.). These observations coupled with survey data from clinicians and patients will allow us to see what aspects of the adapted intervention succeed and fail. The PI will train the study research coordinator to conduct these observations which will take place when the prescribing clinician is meeting with the patient, with the consent of the patient. During the Aim 3 time period, the research coordinator will meet regularly with the PI to review the qualitative field notes.

Sample size justification

A power analysis was not conducted for Aim 3, as this is conceptualized as a case series, for which the recommended minimum is 4 participants [65]. Given the diversity of patient experiences, enrolling 4 participants does not seem sufficient for identifying major issues and refining the model. However, there is no data on which to base a formal sample size calculation. For studies with an expected medium standardized effect size, a case number of n = 15 per group has been recommended for pilot studies preceding a possible main study with a power of 90% [66]. We therefore plan to recruit 10 individuals from each NICU site for a total of N = 20 in order to identify major issues and refine the adapted intervention model.

Discussion

This study will develop a set of implementation strategies that have been systematically refined to maximize acceptability and feasibility of an intervention model that may substantially improve the lives of mothers with OUD and will inform future proposals to evaluate the effectiveness of NICU bedside buprenorphine induction and referral to treatment as a means of co-locating maternal and infant care in the inpatient pediatric setting.

This study contains several key innovations. Most importantly, we focus on the gap between pediatric care and maternal care; efforts to integrate behavioral health care into medical settings have largely been conducted in settings where the behavioral healthcare is targeted to the individual who is the identified patient, not a family member caring for a hospitalized loved one. We will test strategies to integrate care by merging maternal health care with pediatric inpatient care in a single location, potentially expanding the impact of behavioral health care and MOUD access significantly. Further, we will address novel implementation barriers unlike those seen in other contexts, where the individuals targeted for the intervention are not patients in the location where they will be served, thus contributing important information about overcoming such barriers. This innovation extends to the consideration of the mother and child as a dyad, with the benefit to each member of the dyad seen as necessary to the functioning and well-being of the other. This is a novel way to address early parenting and bonding for the dyad, both of whom are affected by OUD.

Goals and outcomes

Study deliverables include an MOUD implementation plan designed to be optimally feasible and acceptable to the NICU setting and to its users. Co-development of the implementation plan in tandem with our advisory board will increase likelihood that the resultant protocol will be acceptable and feasible for use. While the details will ultimately be determined via study activities, we anticipate this will result in a protocol for NICU bedside buprenorphine induction that (1) identifies the provider-type that is best equipped to provide the treatment, and (2) establishes guidelines for a clinician to provide care to an adult patient in a pediatric medical setting that adequately addresses identified implementation barriers (i.e., alternative workflows, identification of spaces, contracts with insurance providers), and (3) addresses patient wants and needs.

Limitations and design considerations

We may encounter difficulties with our advisory board coming to consensus. While we will aim for a 70% majority, consensus does not necessarily mean that all will agree, but that the weight of the opinion falls in one direction. We considered focusing on bedside buprenorphine induction for inpatient mothers in the postpartum unit. However, we chose to focus on NICU mothers because the average NICU stay for a baby with NOWS can be as many as ~ 16 days [19], while a postpartum mother is usually discharged from the hospital 24–48 h after delivery. The immediate postpartum stay does not allow for enough time for a mother to be evaluated for OUD, seen by a certified provider, and induced on buprenorphine while developing a trusting relationship with the care team to support sustained follow-up care. While we hope that many mothers will be enrolled in OUD treatment programs via postpartum unit buprenorphine induction, we know a large subset of women may be missed. Targeting the NICU sets a precedent for co-locating maternal OUD and infant NOWS care to preserve the maternal-child dyad and enable mothers to bring their babies home with an established recovery plan.

We may not recruit enough participants or providers interested in or able to prescribe buprenorphine in the NICU setting. However, based on interviews through our pilot work, we feel confident that many providers are open to delivering these services. Philadelphia is considered “ground zero” for the opioid addiction epidemic in the US [67]. This, combined with the numbers of pregnant mothers who make contact with our outpatient clinical program yet fail to enroll, plus the number of NICU admissions for NOWS across our two partner NICUs assure us that we will encounter a fair number of women who will be appropriate for the intervention and implementation plan. Unexpected administrative issues may emerge, although strong leadership buy-in will mitigate this concern. If we encounter such issues our advisory board will examine the barriers and generate solutions.

Potential impact

In 2018, the last year that data were available, over 58% of pregnancy associated deaths in Philadelphia occurred in women with a documented history of substance abuse, excluding tobacco use [68]. As substance abuse history is often underreported, these numbers are likely an underestimate of the true extent of the effect of opioid use in postpartum women. Our study stands to have a major impact on the way in which MOUD is delivered to postpartum women, capitalizing on the fact that we can literally “meet them where they are.” This effort recognizes the substantial barriers to engagement in OUD care in the challenging postpartum days when risk for return to use and accidental overdose are highest, and strives to make care convenient and feasible while preserving the dyadic bond by not separating mother and baby in order to treat both.
