Hepatic function is associated with cognitive function in patients with stable schizophrenia
Authors: Jian Han, Xiu Li, Yanping Feng, Yan Guo, Binyou Wang, Yilin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe liver-brain axis, mediated by inflammatory cytokines, metabolic byproducts, and oxidative stress, shares pathological pathways with schizophrenia neurobiology. Despite this overlap, direct clinical evidence linking hepatic function indicators to psychiatric symptoms or cognitive function in schizophrenia remains limited. Therefore, this study investigated associations between clinical hepatic function indicators and both psychiatric symptoms and cognitive performance in individuals with stable schizophrenia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 170 inpatients with clinically stable schizophrenia. Liver function indicators were obtained from fasting venous blood samples. Psychiatric symptoms were assessed using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), and cognitive function was assessed with the Montreal Cognitive Assessment–Chinese version (MoCA-C). Multiple linear regression models, adjusted for covariates, were used to examine associations between hepatic indicators and outcomes (PANSS total/subscale scores; MoCA-C total score).ResultsAfter covariate adjustment, total bilirubin (TBil) (β = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.08–0.51; P = 0.008) and indirect bilirubin (IDBil) (β = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.17–0.80; P = 0.003) were significantly positively associated with MoCA-C scores. No significant associations were observed between any hepatic indicators and PANSS total/subscale scores.ConclusionsIn this cross-sectional study of clinically stable schizophrenia, higher TBil and IDBil levels were associated with better cognitive performance. These findings suggest a potential role of bilirubin metabolism in cognitive function in schizophrenia, highlighting the need for further investigation into its relevance for cognitive impairment management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07448-1.Keywords: Hepatic function, Stable schizophrenia, Psychiatric symptoms, Cognitive function

Introduction

The liver-brain axis represents bidirectional communication between hepatic and central nervous systems [1, 2], mediated by immune-inflammatory pathways, neuroendocrine signaling, and metabolic regulation [3–5]. On one hand, the brain regulates hepatic metabolism via neuroendocrine pathways and autonomic (particularly vagal) inputs [6, 7]. On the other hand, hepatic dysfunction—given the liver’s central role in systemic homeostasis—can drive neurological impairment through toxin accumulation (e.g., ammonia), altered neurotransmitter dynamics, and blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption [1, 8, 9]. This axis is classically represented by hepatic encephalopathy (HE), where liver failure-induced hyperammonemia and neuroinflammation cause astrocyte swelling, neurotransmission deficits, and cognitive decline [10, 11]. In addition to acute syndromes, impaired hepatic clearance of neurotoxic proteins (e.g., α-synuclein in Parkinson’s disease, and Aβ in Alzheimer’s disease) and systemic metabolic dysregulation further increase neurodegenerative progression [12, 13]. These pathophysiological mechanisms underscore the liver–brain axis as a critical contributor to neuropsychiatric disorders.

Within this framework, the liver–brain axis likely contributes to schizophrenia pathophysiology. Mechanistically, chronic antipsychotic regimens induce metabolic stress via hepatic enzyme dysregulation, hepatosteatosis, and mitochondrial impairment [14–16], increasing vulnerability to metabolic syndrome and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [17]. This risk is increased by behavioral factors common in schizophrenia, such as physical inactivity, substance misuse, and poor dietary patterns, which further predispose to NAFLD and other hepatopathies [17, 18]. Progressive hepatic dysfunction may in turn compromise BBB integrity, facilitating entry of neurotoxins into the central nervous system and stimulating astrocytic and microglial release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [19]. Peripherally elevated cytokines (e.g., IL-6) can cross the disrupted BBB, amplifying neuroinflammation [10, 20], a process exacerbated by schizophrenia-associated neurovascular abnormalities [21]. Clinical evidence further substantiates the role of the liver–brain axis in schizophrenia, with several hepatic indicators demonstrating significant associations with psychopathological features. For instance, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and albumin (ALB) levels have been correlated with the severity of negative symptoms [22]. Conversely, elevated bilirubin has been linked to worsened psychotic manifestations [23, 24].

Moreover, these hepatic associations extend beyond psychiatric symptoms to cognitive impairment—a core feature of schizophrenia that underlies deficits in social, occupational, and daily functioning [25–27]. Liver-related indicators—including ALP, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), bilirubin, and albumin—are well established as correlates of cognitive decline in aging and neurodegenerative disorders [28–31]; however, evidence within schizophrenia remains limited. Only a few studies have described bilirubin–cognition associations in this population [32, 33], potentially reflecting bilirubin’s neuroprotective properties [34].

Despite these preliminary insights, the liver–brain axis as a mechanistic link between hepatic dysfunction and cognitive/psychiatric deficits in schizophrenia, particularly in clinically stable patients, where findings are less confounded by acute disease fluctuations, remains to be fully elucidated. Therefore, this study aimed to preliminarily investigate the associations between clinical hepatic function indicators and both psychiatric symptoms and cognitive performance in patients with stable schizophrenia. It was hypothesized that specific hepatic dysfunction indicators correlate with poorer cognition and more severe psychopathology. These findings may inform individualized management strategies targeting liver-brain axis dysregulation in schizophrenia.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study analyzed Wave 2 (2024) data from the schizophrenia-specific cohort (IRB No. 2023024) of an ongoing longitudinal project initiated in 2023 by the Psychiatry Department of Zigong Mental Health Center, which evaluates multiple clinical domains. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles, with the Institutional Review Board granting an exemption for this secondary analysis based on three criteria: (1) the original informed consent covered future use of anonymized data; (2) no additional procedures were performed; and (3) all personally identifiable information had been irreversibly anonymized. All participants provided written informed consent. Schizophrenia diagnoses were confirmed by board-certified psychiatrists according to the criteria of the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD-10) [35], with stable disease defined as sustained symptom control, significant clinical remission, and restored social/occupational function.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a psychiatrist-confirmed diagnosis of schizophrenia; and (3) provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) lack of confirmation of stable schizophrenia; (2) presence of significant hepatic or renal dysfunction; (3) history of autoimmune disorders or cancer treatment; and (4) missing hepatic function test results. Of 216 individuals initially screened during Wave 2 (2024), 170 participants were included in the final analysis.

Measurement of hepatic function

Hepatic function indicators were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical records and included total bilirubin (TBil), direct bilirubin (DBil), indirect bilirubin (IDBil), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), creatine kinase (CK), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). All values were derived from laboratory analyses of fasting venous blood samples collected from each participant.

Psychiatric and cognitive function evaluation

Psychiatric symptoms were assessed using the 30-item Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), a validated measure of symptom severity in schizophrenia [36]. The PANSS comprises 7 positive symptom items, 7 negative symptom items, and 16 general psychopathology items [37]. Each item is scored on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = absent to 7 = extreme), with higher total scores indicating higher symptom severity.

Cognitive function in schizophrenia is typically assessed using the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB) [38] and Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS) [39], both of which demonstrate strong reliability and validity in clinical trials for this population [40–43]. However, in this study, implementation of these batteries was limited by staff expertise and logistical limitations (hardware, software, and time requirements for comprehensive assessment). Therefore, the Chinese version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-C) [44] was adopted as a pragmatic alternative. This decision was supported by evidence of concurrent validity between MoCA and MCCB scores in schizophrenia populations [45–47], confirming its utility as a brief screening tool. The MoCA-C requires approximately 15 min to administer, yields a total score ranging from 0 to 30 (lower scores indicate greater impairment), and was administered by trained psychiatrists.

Covariates

Covariates were systematically collected from self-reported questionnaires and electronic medical records. Demographic and clinical characteristics included age, sex, marital status, body mass index (BMI), and educational level (illiterate, high school and below, university and above). Clinical data encompassed disease duration (years), hospitalization duration (months), age at onset (years), family psychiatric history, first episode status, substance use history (smoking and alcohol consumption), treatment modality (monotherapy or antipsychotic medication combined with electroconvulsive therapy [ECT]), chlorpromazine-equivalent dose (mg/day), and the presence of chronic diseases. Other variables included vision or hearing impairment, history of hospital-acquired bacterial pneumonia, COVID-19 infection history, falls history, relapse history of psychiatric symptoms, self-reported sleep quality over the past month, anxiety and depression status. Anxiety and depression were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale [48], and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [49], respectively. These are well-validated and widely used measures in schizophrenia research [50, 51]. Considering the specific features of the study population, licensed clinicians administered all items using accessible wording to maximize comprehension. A score of 5 or higher on either scale indicated clinically significant symptoms [52].

Data collection followed a standardized sequence: extraction of relevant clinical variables from medical records, immediate anthropometric measurements, and administration of symptom and cognitive scales. All assessments, including demographic, anthropometric, psychiatric, cognitive, and clinical data, were completed within 48–72 h of routine blood testing. Trained research staff ensured accurate measurements and consistent administration of assessments. No missing data were present, eliminating the need for missing data imputation.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Two-tailed P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were presented as frequency counts and percentages, while continuous variables that were not normally distributed were presented as medians (25th percentile [P25] and 75th percentile [P75])

Relationships between covariates and hepatic indicators were further explored using Spearman’s correlation, Mann–Whitney U tests (two groups), or Kruskal–Wallis tests (three groups). Associations between hepatic function indicators, covariates, and psychiatric/cognitive measures were assessed using univariate linear regression. Scatter plots visualizing the relationships between independent and dependent variables were generated using SPSS 25.0. Hepatic indicators showing significant univariate associations (P < 0.05) with PANSS, PANSS subscale or MoCA-C scores were retained for covariate-adjusted multivariable linear regression models. Covariate selection was based on predefined criteria: (i) a significant association (P < 0.05) with the outcome variable, (ii) a significant association (P < 0.05) with the hepatic indicator, and (iii) absence of multicollinearity. Potential multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with VIF ≥ 5 indicating significant collinearity [53]. Forest plots for visualizing multivariate regression results were generated using the Zstats online platform (https://www.medsta.cn/software).

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate the associations between clinical hepatic function indicators and both psychiatric severity and cognitive performance in patients with stable schizophrenia. The results demonstrated that higher TBil and IDBil levels were associated with better cognitive performance, while no associations were observed with psychiatric symptoms. This dissociation from psychiatric symptoms suggests that hepatic metabolism may exert a domain-specific effect on cognition, independent of psychotic symptom severity. These findings highlight systemic contributors to cognitive impairment beyond central nervous system pathology, underscoring the potential clinical relevance of liver function monitoring in schizophrenia management.

Previous research has identified associations between abnormal bilirubin levels and cognitive decline in schizophrenia. For instance, Yin et al. [32] reported a positive correlation between serum TBil levels and immediate memory scores on the RBANS in 34 patients with schizophrenia. Similarly, Becklén et al. [33] observed a positive association between plasma TBil and working memory in a cohort of 39 first-episode schizophrenia patients. The findings of this study align with these of previous studies, collectively supporting the hypothesis that lower bilirubin levels may be associated with cognitive impairment in individuals with schizophrenia. Mechanistically, bilirubin is the end product of heme catabolism in erythrocytes. Mild elevations have been shown to reduce oxidative stress and neuronal injury, suggesting a neuroprotective role [54–56]. Bilirubin depletion may reduce these protective effects, therefore exacerbating neurodegeneration and cognitive decline.

Although HE—a complication of severe liver dysfunction—can cause delirium and acute cognitive impairment [57, 58], it is an unlikely explanation for our findings. This condition typically manifests in decompensated cirrhosis or acute liver failure, both of which were exclusion criteria in our study. Given that our cohort exhibited preserved liver function (median TBil: 9.58 µmol/L) and no clinical signs of severe hepatopathy (e.g., hyperammonemia or ascites), the observed association is more plausibly attributed to the intrinsic neuroprotective properties of bilirubin rather than to overt hepatic dysfunction with subsequent delirium.

Recent studies suggest potential domain-specific associations between hepatic biomarkers and schizophrenia symptoms. For instance, ALP and ALB have been associated with PANSS negative symptoms in stable patients [22], and CK levels have shown positive correlations with aggressive behavior in large patient cohorts [59]. Other studies have identified relationships between TBil and PANSS positive symptoms during early antipsychotic treatment [23], as well as between elevated TBil/IDBil and total Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale scores in drug-naïve, acute-phase patients [24]. Compared to these studies, this study found no significant associations between hepatic function indicators and psychopathological symptoms in clinically stable, hospitalized, chronically treated schizophrenia patients. Discrepancies may be due to variations in (1) demographic, regional, and environmental factors, as well as comorbidities and (2) clinical features, including disease stage, treatment duration, and cumulative systemic and neuroendocrine adaptations over time.

Cognitive impairment — a core determinant of adverse outcomes in schizophrenia including relapse [60], physical frailty [61], disability [62], and mortality [63] — may be partially shaped by bilirubin metabolism, as suggested by our preliminary findings of positive TBil/IDBil-cognition associations. Given the study’s limitations, these observations primarily generate hypotheses for future research. Two strategic directions emerge: first, longitudinal studies should validate whether bilirubin monitoring supports cognitive risk stratification, using its neuroprotective properties [34] and clinical accessibility. Second, prospective studies incorporating comparison groups could test bilirubin’s potential in refining prediction models for cognition-related outcomes, building on existing hepatic indicator–based models for frailty [64], disability [65], and mortality [66, 67].

Additionally, adjusted models further identified unmarried status, higher education, and better sleep quality as independent predictors of better cognitive performance, consistent with previous reports [68–70]. Education showed disproportionately high β coefficients, which may reflect skewed educational distribution in our sample: only 10.6% were illiterate, and 3.5% had university degrees. Such limited subgroup representation may inflate effect estimates due to increased variability within small strata.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design and the lack of healthy controls or comparisons with other psychiatric disorders preclude causal inferences regarding temporal relationships between hepatic indicators and cognitive function, and hinder conclusions about disease specificity. Second, the inclusion of only inpatients with a median age of 51 years from a single center may limit generalizability to outpatients, younger or older populations, and those at different illness stages. Third, antipsychotic exposure was quantified using chlorpromazine equivalents rather than plasma levels, which may not capture individual pharmacokinetic differences; moreover, the relatively small sample and use of over 10 different agents prevented medication-specific subgroup analyses. Fourth, unmeasured confounders—such as nutritional status, gut microbiome composition, and detailed pharmacokinetic parameters—may have influenced the outcomes. Fifth, the assessment instruments employed may not fully capture domain-specific deficits: global cognition was evaluated with the MoCA-C instead of more comprehensive tools like the MCCB or RBANS, and depression was assessed with the PHQ-9 rather than schizophrenia-specific scales such as the Calgary Depression Scale. Finally, while markedly elevated bilirubin has been linked to neurotoxicity [71, 72], this study did not examine potential biphasic (neuroprotective versus neurotoxic) concentration-dependent effects. Future longitudinal and multicenter studies incorporating control groups are needed to distinguish disease-specific from medication-related effects, clarify the role of bilirubin concentration gradients, and better establish the liver–brain axis in schizophrenia.

Conclusions

In summary, this study indicates that elevated bilirubin levels (TBil/IDBil) are associated with better cognitive performance in patients with stable schizophrenia. Although these findings are preliminary and constrained by the study’s limitations, they underscore the potential role of the liver–brain axis in cognitive impairment associated with schizophrenia. Further longitudinal investigations are necessary to validate these associations and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms.
