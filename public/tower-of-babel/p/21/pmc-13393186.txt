Neural Network–Based Prediction and Sensitivity Analysis of CO2 Solubility in Ionic Liquids
Authors: Avanish Kumar, Mashita Emmanuel, Oladele Roqeebat, Christabel S. Solomon, Swati Dubey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, the prediction of the dissolution of CO2 in the ionic liquid BMIM was achieved using Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) together with a feedforward multilayer perceptron design constructed in MATLAB. The models were trained with two recognized learning algorithms: Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) and Scaled Conjugate Gradient (SCG). A proper data set made up of experimental results and measurements was used for the training and validation aspect. These experimental results cover temperatures ranging from 228 to 322 K and pressures up to 90 bar. The ANN trained with the SCG algorithm was less effective than the ANN trained with the LM algorithm as this algorithm visibly outperformed the SCG model by getting a regression coefficient of R = 0.9999 and low error (MSE ∼10–1). The results showed that these models did not just memorize the already given data; instead, they were trained smoothly and were able to make accurate predictions on new sets of data given without any signs of overfitting. Thermodynamics principles have established that the solubility of gases depend heavily on temperature and pressure, which in this case, is the solubility of CO2 in [BMIM]­[BF4]. Sensitivity analysis also confirms this principle, and well-trained ANNs are in alignment with this theory, therefore they are reliable for the prediction of complex and nonlinear chemical processes.

Introduction

The increasing rise in atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) is recognized globally as one of the most disturbing environmental problems of the twenty-first century. Many human activities including deforestation, burning fossil fuels, and agricultural practices have driven the amount of atmospheric CO2 from 280 ppm to over 420 ppm since the Industrial Revolution began. , These continued human activities that have caused the increase in carbon dioxide in the atmosphere have created a much stronger greenhouse effect, thereby making oceans to become more acidic and increasing global warming. It has also led to the altering of climate abruptly in different ways. To measure how fast these changes are occurring, careful analysis was performed, and it was discovered that CO2 levels increased gradually in the 1960s, less than 1 ppm annually. It may interest you to know that this rate has become more than twice of what it once was, reaching over 2.5 ppm annually ­(Figure ). Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has stated that the average global temperatures could exceed 1.5–2.0 °C unless immediate action is taken to reduce the emission of greenhouse gases. Many scientists and researchers have been investigating methods for capturing, storing, and utilizing CO2 in large power plants and factories where CO2 emissions are particularly high. Chemical absorptions with amines being the most widely used still has its own drawbacks and limitations. These include the excess amount of energy required for the process leading to very high costs, solvent degradation over time due to presence of oxygen from flue gases, and the potential for equipment corrosion. The continuous use of these capture technologies by large-scale industries can exacerbate these drawbacks, making them increasingly difficult to manage. Continuous equipment corrosion significantly increases maintenance costs and amine degradation, resulting in increasing replacement costs. This has led to active research on ionic liquids (ILs) as an alternative solvent for amine solvents. Ionic liquids have been found to have strong CO2 affinity and structural tunability and requires lesser energy for separation. Recent studies reveal that they are very promising. For example, Hydrophobic Protic ILs such as [BEHA]­[Tf2N] can absorb significant amount of CO2 at moderate pressures and temperatures, and they can also be reused efficiently. As pressure increases, dissolution of CO2 increases, whereas as temperature increases, the dissolution of CO2 decreases. This principle is same for any ILs such as imidazolium, ammonium, and phosphonium. These findings have also been confirmed with mathematical modeling pointing out pressure as the main factor responsible for the amount of CO2 dissolved in ionic liquids. Even more exciting, hybrid machine learning and thermodynamic models are now being used to predict the performance of different ILs, helping scientists design tailor-made solvents for maximum efficiency (Qin et al., 2024). Ongoing attempts to increase absorption capacity and enable commercial deployment are highlighted by recent thorough reviews that describe structure–property correlations, including the impacts of cation–anion selection, viscosity, and functional group insertion on CO2 capture efficiency.

One of the biggest challenges with ionic liquids (ILs) is their sheer variety. Because scientists can mix and match countless cations and anions, the number of possible ILs is practically endless. However, it also makes testing them a huge task. Measuring how much CO2 each IL can absorb under different temperatures and pressures takes enormous amount of time, money, and effort. To overcome this, researchers are using data-driven methods to study ILs rather than conducting experiments for each IL. This brings about the use of machine learning tools that can handle very large data sets. ANNs are able to analyze large amounts of experimental data. They can capture the complex and nonlinear thermodynamic interactions responsible for the CO2 solubility with good accuracy. This way, fewer experiments are needed, saving resources while still providing reliable predictions. In the study, ANN models are developed to predict CO2 solubility in selected ionic liquids over a varying range of thermodynamic conditions. Connecting the increasing global CO2 growth trend to the development of predictive computational models, this research addresses the need for efficient solvent screening and design, thereby contributing to a broader effort to mitigate climate change through innovative CO2 capture technologies.

The reason behind the uniqueness of this study is that new ANN models have been developed based on the use of Levenberg–Marquardt and scaled conjugate gradient methods and then evaluated in relation to their performance in CO2 solubility prediction in the ionic liquid [BMIM]­[BF4]. Besides internal validation, the optimized ANN model has also been externally tested with an independent set of data. Sensitivity analysis was also conducted in order to estimate the contribution of thermodynamic variables to the behavior of CO2 solubility.

Computational Tools

MATLAB Environment

All computations involved were generated on MATLAB R2023b along with its Neural Network Toolbox. MATLAB is very easy to navigate and understand, hence one of the reasons why it is widely used. Training ANNs on MATLAB is preferred because of its extensive library of functions and tools curated specifically for ANNs. Its toolbox provides a comprehensive set of functions for building various types of networks, including feedforward and convolutional networks. Additionally, MATLAB has so many advanced algorithms including the Levenberg–Marquardt backpropagation method that have the ability to develop handle large data sets.

Artificial Neural Network (ANN) Architecture

Model Structure

An artificial neural network (ANN) that uses a feedforward multilayer perceptron that consists of multiple layers of neurons was created for the purpose of this study. As illustrated in Figure , the network consists of three sequential layers. Each layer has its purpose in processing the data, which is significantly different from the purpose of other layers, giving it the opportunity to capture nonlinear interactions in order to match the predicted output to the given input.

General architecture of the ANN developed for CO2 solubility prediction in ionic liquids.

Input Layer

This layer was created with two neurons, where one neuron represents temperature (K), while the other represents pressure (bar). These are the operating variables.

Dissolution of CO2 in ionic liquids is an exothermic process; therefore, temperature is of great importance in the procedure. The solubility levels drop as the temperature increases. The model learns the relationship between temperature and the system, recognizing that CO2 solubility decreases with increasing temperature, thereby ensuring consistency with thermodynamic principles.

Pressure influences the system in an opposite way. An increase in pressure creates more room for CO2 to dissolve. At moderate pressures, the amount of CO2 that dissolves in the liquid is directly proportional to pressure, which supports Henry’s law. But as the pressure continues to rise, Henry’s law no longer applies and the system becomes nonlinear. This nonlinearity births the need for an ANN where linearization can happen.

Hidden Layer

The hidden layer is the second layer after the input layer that is situated between the input and the output layer and is therefore hidden. The hidden layer has the ability to learn and model complex nonlinear relationships in data received from the input layer between the input variables and CO2 solubility. The number of neurons was optimized through repeated testing to obtain high accuracy without adding an unnecessary extra computational load. Each neuron used a sigmoid activation function, which gave the network the ability to model subtle nonlinear interactions between temperature and pressure.

Output Layer

The output layer contained only one neuron and produced the network’s prediction on the solubility of CO2 in the ionic liquid [BMIM]­[BF4] after all the computations. The output layer uses a linear function that allows it to take numerical values rather than being confined to a fixed range. The Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) was simple and easy to train while being accurately predictive. With one hidden layer and 1–10 neurons, the ANN could capture nonlinear interactions between pressure, temperature, and solubility without getting too complex or risking the chances of overfitting.

To ensure that the model was trained and tested thoroughly, the data set was divided into three sets: the training set, the validation set, and the testing set randomly.

The training set covered 70% of the entire data. It was used for the network’s learning process, adjusting the network’s internal weights, allowing it to learn from the input-output relationships provided, minimizing the error between predicted and experimental solubility values. This is the stage where the ANN learned the mapping between temperature, pressure, and CO2 solubility.

The remaining 30% of the data set was split into two equal parts to represent the validation set (15%) and the testing set (15%). The validation set of data was used to monitor the training and prevent overfitting. While the model was being trained, its performance was tested continuously on the validation set and stopped early whenever an error began to arise. The remaining 15% of the data set was used solely for evaluation. This was done to ensure that the model gives accurate predictions even when new data is fed to it, proving that the predictions are not being given as a result of memorizing information that was fed to it. to test the accuracy of the prediction with metrics like R2 (regression coefficient) and MSE (mean square error) showing the predictive capability of the ANN.

Training Algorithm

In this study, our artificial neural network (ANN) was trained using two well-established optimization methods: Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) and scaled conjugate gradient (SCG). Choosing the right training algorithm is a critical step in ANN modeling because it controls how the network’s internal weights and biases are adjusted during learning. These updates happen iteratively, gradually reducing prediction errors and helping the model converge toward accurate results.

Efficient training algorithms are especially important in regression problems such as this one. They not only improve the precision of predictions but also ensure that the process remains stable and computationally manageable. In short, the choice of LM and SCG gave the ANN both accuracy and reliability, making it well-suited for modeling CO2 solubility.

Normalization

Before training, the input and output variables were normalized between [0,1]. This process was done so as to reduce numerical instability, improve convergence speed, and avoid domination of variables with larger magnitudes.

Randomization

In order to ensure an unbiased representation across validation, training, and testing subsets, the data were randomized.

Performance Evaluation

Three statistical measures that are highly acknowledged were used to evaluate the performance of the model, which are the regression coefficient (R 2), mean square error (MSE), and residual analysis, as shown by Hayyan et al. If the R 2 value approaches unity, it shows a strong agreement between experimental and predicted data, whereas a lower MSE value shows higher predictive accuracy. Furthermore, in order to ensure that there was no systematic bias in the model, residual plots were analyzed to make sure that prediction errors were randomly distributed around zero. All these evaluation criteria show a reliable and comprehensive assessment of the ANN performance and are well used in solubility prediction studies and modeling in chemical engineering.

Regression Coefficient (R 2)

In order to determine the strength of correlation between experimental and predicted values, R2 was used. If the R2 value is close to 1, it shows a near-perfect prediction.

Mean Square Error (MSE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)

In order to determine the magnitude of error, these metrics were employed, and low values show high prediction accuracy.

Sensitivity Analysis of ANN

In order to examine the relative influence of the input variables on the predicted solubility of CO2 in the ionic liquid [BMIM]­[BF4], the sensitivity analysis was performed to increase the interpretability of our developed artificial neural network (ANN) model. The analysis provides quantitative insights into how variations in temperature and pressure affect the predicted solubility, thereby allowing verification that the ANN behavior remains consistent with established thermodynamic expectations.

The method that was used was a perturbation-based sensitivity where each input variable was varied independently over its experimental range while the remaining variables were held constant at their mean value. Furthermore, changes in the predicted CO2 mole fraction were recorded. Sensitivity indices were calculated on the basis of the normalized absolute change in the model output with respect to the variation in each input parameter. This approach is particularly suitable for nonlinear regression models and has been widely employed in ANN-based thermodynamic and solubility studies.

Accordingly, the sensitivity of the ANN output with respect to the ith input variable was determined using the following expression: 1 Si=1N∑k‐1N|y(xi,k+Δxi)‐y(xi,k)Δxi|

where S i shows the sensitivity index of the input variable x i, y represents the ANN-predicted CO2 mole fraction solubility, x i, k shows the value of the ith input variable at the kth data point, Δx i represents a small perturbation applied to the input variable (typically 1–5% of its range), and N is the total number of data points.

Effect of Layer Size on Prediction Accuracy

A feedforward backpropagation network was generated with two inputs (temperature, pressure, and one output (CO2 solubility)). In this study, different training functions, including Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) and scaled conjugate gradient (SCG), were applied.

Figure illustrates the variation of the overall regression coefficient (R value) with hidden layer size (number of neurons) for two ANN training algorithms: Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) and Scaled Conjugate Gradient (SCG). The R value represents the strength of correlation between predicted and experimental CO2 solubility values, with values closer to 1 indicating better prediction accuracy.

Effect of hidden layer size on the overall regression coefficient (R) for ANN models trained using Levenberg–Marquardt and Scaled Conjugate Gradient algorithms.

Using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm, the R value increased sharply as the layer size increased from one to three neurons, showing a rapid improvement in the model accuracy. Beyond the four neurons, the R value reaches unity and remains consistently high (R ≈ 0.99–1.00) across larger layer sizes, illustrating stable convergence and strong generalization. This behavior indicates that the LM algorithm efficiently recognizes the nonlinear relationship between pressure, temperature, and CO2 solubility even with a moderate number of neurons, , whereas the Scaled Conjugate Gradient algorithm shows a more fluctuating trend. Although the R value generally improves with increasing layer size, noticeable variability is observed at intermediate neuron counts, particularly around 4 neurons where a temporary decrease in performance occurs. At higher layer sizes (≥6 neurons), the SCG algorithm achieves reasonably high R values; however, these remain slightly lower and less stable than those obtained using the LM algorithm.

Conclusions

This study used a machine learning mechanism called artificial neural network (ANN) models to predict the dissolution of CO2 in the ionic liquid [BMIM]­[BF4] across varying temperatures and pressures. Through using data from experimental reports, we were able to make feedforward multilayer perceptron networks in MATLAB and trained them using two well-established algorithms: Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) and scaled conjugate gradient (SCG). From these two, we were able to do a comparison on the intricate, nonlinear thermodynamic behavior that is associated with CO2 absorption in the ionic liquids using ANNs.

The ANN, which was trained, achieved, and an almost unity regression coefficient (R ≈ 0.9999), a mean-squared error (∼10–1) that was very low, and a smooth convergence. The regression analyses, performance graphs, and error histograms consistently illustrated perfect alignment between experimental solubility and predicted data, showing minimum bias and strong generalization across training, test, and validation data sets. Whereas the network that was trained using the SCG algorithm converged more quickly but was less robust, which resulted in the error levels being higher, broader error distributions, and reduced prediction accuracy.

Through the conduction of sensitivity analysis, additional insight was offered on the impact of temperature and pressure on CO2 solubility. From the results it was clearly seen that depending on the training algorithm used either pressure or temperature was identified at as the major influencing factor. These results were consistent with the well-known thermodynamic principles of gas–liquid equilibrium in ionic liquids, thereby proving the validity of the ANN method.
