A human iPSC-derived 3D spinal cord organoid model to study radiation-induced neural injury
Authors: Yifei Jiang, Xubo Li, Jingguang Zeng, Shiju Chen, Runtao Zhou, Yixun Wu, Ganggang Kong, Fawei Liao, Bo Wang, Fangze Qi, Cheng Gu, Shiyi Yin, Qiguang Pang, Weidong Mu, Qingtang Zhu, Canbin Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Background: Radiation therapy remains a cornerstone in the treatment of primary and metastatic tumors; however, its efficacy is limited by the spinal cord’s high sensitivity to radiation-induced injury. To better understand the mechanisms underlying spinal cord radiosensitivity, we developed a three-dimensional human spinal cord organoid model derived from human-induced pluripotent stem cells using a neuroectodermal differentiation protocol that closely mimics embryonic spinal cord development. Methods: Mature spinal cord organoids were exposed to a clinically relevant 2 Gy dose of ionizing radiation, and subsequent assessments included evaluation of DNA damage, astrocytic response, and neuronal functionality. Results: The organoids successfully recapitulated key features of spinal cord development, including neural differentiation, spontaneous electrophysiological activity, and functional maturation. Radiation exposure led to pronounced DNA double-strand breaks, particularly in SOX2-expressing progenitor cells. Astrocyte hyperplasia was evident through increased GFAP expression, indicating a reactive gliosis response. Electrophysiological analysis revealed a marked reduction in spike frequency and burst activity, indicative of impaired neuronal function. Although partial recovery was observed over time, functional deficits persisted, suggesting sustained damage. Conclusion: This human spinal cord organoid model offers a physiologically relevant platform for studying radiation-induced spinal cord injury and provides valuable insights into the cellular and functional consequences of radiation exposure. It holds significant potential for advancing neuroprotective strategies and therapeutic interventions targeting radiation-induced damage in the central nervous system.Keywords: human pluripotent stem cells, spinal cord organoids, radiation-induced damage, DNA-double strand break, DNA damage response

This study introduces a human spinal cord organoid model that closely mimics early spinal cord development and function. By exposing this model to radiation, we reveal how the spinal cord responds to injury at the cellular and functional levels. The findings highlight lasting damage to nerve cells and support cells, offering new insight into why the spinal cord is so sensitive to radiation. This model provides a powerful tool for studying spinal cord injury and may help guide the development of safer, more effective treatments for patients undergoing radiation therapy.

Introduction

Radiation therapy is a crucial treatment modality for both primary and metastatic tumors. The spinal cord is considered one of the most critical dose-limiting organs, also referred to as organs-at-risk (OARs). Due to its anatomical proximity, the spinal cord is not only an OAR in the radiation treatment of spine, spinal cord and paraspinal tumors but also head and neck and lung neoplasms.1 Even low volumes of radiation exposure to the spinal cord can result in DNA damage, compromising normal spinal cord function and potentially leading to irreversible myelopathy.2,3 Radiation is known to induce cytotoxic, cytogenetic, and inheritable effects, which contribute to unrepairable DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) in cells. DNA damage and impaired repair mechanisms severely affect cellular function, promoting cell senescence and carcinogenesis.4 Currently, no standardized therapies exist to prevents radiation-induced spinal cord damage, and existing personalized models have been insufficient in providing a comprehensive understanding the underlying mechanisms needed for therapeutic development.

While previous studies have explored radiation-induced neural injury using animal models and 2D cultures, there remains a lack of human-relevant, three-dimensional models that accurately recapitulate the structural and functional features of the spinal cord. Traditional in vitro models lack the complexity of in vivo spinal cord tissue, and animal models have species-specific differences that limit direct translation to human physiology.5,6 In recent decades, organoids derived from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) have been extensively studied and utilized as in vitro models across various research applications. Organoids, which are three-dimensional structures, closely mimic the architecture and function of specific human organs, offering a more accurate model of tissue response.4,7,8 This is particularly important for tissues sensitive to radiation, such as the brain, spinal cord, and liver, which are challenging to study in vivo. Studying organoids in the context of radiation-induced damage is essential for advancing our understanding of how radiation affects human tissues at a cellular and molecular level. By using organoids to study radiation-induced damage, researchers can observe the direct cellular effects of radiation, including DNA damage, cell death, and repair mechanisms, within a controlled and human-relevant environment. This approach not only enhances our understanding of radiation’s impact on normal tissue development but also provides critical insights into the mechanisms underlying radiation toxicity. Ultimately, these insights can drive the development of protective strategies and personalized therapeutic interventions to mitigate radiation-induced damage, benefiting patients undergoing radiotherapy and informing safer radiation practices.9–12

In this study, we modified an established protocol to generate human three-dimensional (3D) spinal cord organoids (hSCOs) from hPSCs. To validate the functionality of hSCOs, we differentiated them to produce motor neurons and established neuromuscular junctions (NMJs) through co-culture with human skeletal muscle organoids (hSMOs). Furthermore, these hSCOs serve as a valuable model for investigating the effects of clinically relevant radiation doses (2 Gy) on healthy spinal cord tissue. We have developed and characterized a 3D hSCOs model that mimics key aspects of human spinal cord development and function, and to use this model to assess the cellular and molecular responses to radiation exposure. This will provide a platform for studying radiation-induced damage and potential therapeutic interventions in a human-relevant system.

Materials and methods

Cell culture of human pluripotent stem cells

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) were gifted by the Center for Stem Cell Biology and Tissue Engineering, Key Laboratory for Stem Cells and Tissue Engineering, Ministry of Education, Sun Yat-Sen University, which we previously established.13,14 The hiPSCs used in this study were originally obtained from an established and ethically approved source. Documentation from the original provider confirms that the collection of human biological materials was performed with appropriate ethical approval and that informed consent was obtained from all donors prior to participation. The cell line was tested for mycoplasma contamination and was maintained mycoplasma free. The hiPSCs were cultured in mTeSR medium (STEMCELL Technologies) on Matrigel (Corning)-coated 6-well plates. Cells were passaged using ReLeSR passaging reagent (STEMCELL Technologies) when they reached 80-90% confluence.

In vitro generation of 2D motor neurons from hiPSCs

The differentiation of hiPSCs into 2D motor neurons was performed following previously established protocols with modifications.15 In brief, hiPSCs were dissociated with Accutase (STEMCELL Technologies) and seeded on Matrigel-coated plates at a density of 1.1 × 104 cells/cm2. The cells were cultured in a neural differentiation medium consisting of a 1:1 mixture of DMEM/F12 (GIBCO) supplemented with 1× N2 (GIBCO) and Neurobasal Medium (GIBCO) supplemented with 1× B27 (GIBCO), along with 1× l-glutamine (GIBCO), 40 ng/mL BSA (Sigma), and 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (GIBCO). The medium also contained 10 µM ROCK inhibitor (Selleck), 10 µM SB431542, 5 µM Dorsomorphin (dual SMAD inhibitors), and 10 ng/mL FGF-2. After 48 hours, the ROCK inhibitor and dual SMAD inhibitors were removed, and 0.1 µM retinoic acid (RA, Selleck) along with 0.1 µM SAG (Millipore) were added to the medium. The medium was replaced daily.

In vitro generation of 3D hSCOs from hiPSCs

3D hSCOs were generated using a modified version of Andersen’s protocol,16 with specific adaptations to optimize ventral patterning and minimize exogenous signaling influences during later stages of differentiation. Briefly, mTeSR-E6 medium was supplemented with 10 µM SB431542 and 5 μM Dorsomorphin and applied until Day 5. The neural medium consisted of Neurobasal-A medium (GIBCO), supplemented with 1× B27 supplement, 1× N2 supplement, 1× l-glutamine, and 1× NEAA (GIBCO). From days 6 to 19, the neural medium was further supplemented with 0.1 μM RA (Selleck), 20 ng/mL EGF, 10 ng/mL FGF-2 (Peprotech), and 0.1 μM SAG. Additionally, 3 µM CHIR99021 (Selleck) was added from Day 4 through Day 18. Between Days 19 and 42, the organoids were transferred to neural medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL BDNF (Peprotech) and 0.1 μM RA. From Day 43 onward, the neural medium was refreshed every four days. This reduction in exogenous signaling factors was intended to minimize external influences and enable a more accurate assessment of intrinsic cellular responses, particularly in the context of radiation exposure experiments.

Generation of 3D hSMOs from hiPSCs

3D hSMOs were generated using our previous established protocol.17 Briefly, hiPSCs were cultured in N2B27 medium supplemented with 3 µM CHIR99021, 40 ng/mL FGF-2, for 48 h to induce into neuromeodermal progenitors (NMPs). On Day 0 of organoid formation, NMPs (9000 cells per well) were seeded into an ultra-low attachment 96-well plate (Corning) in LIFHC medium, which consisted of DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1× ITS-G (GIBCO), 1× l-glutamine, 1× NEAA, 10% Knockout SR (GIBCO), 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 0.5 µM LDN193189 (STEMCELL Technologies), 20 ng/mL IGF, 20 ng/mL FGF-2, 20 ng/mL HGF (Peprotech), and 3 µM CHIR99021. From Days 3 to 5, LDN193189, CHIR99021, and ROCK inhibitor were removed from the medium, followed by FGF-2 removal from Days 5-10. From Day 11, organoids were maintained in the same basal medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL IGF, and 20 ng/mL HGF, with media changes every 4 to 6 days on an orbital shaker (80 rpm).

Axon outgrowth from hSCOs

The hSCOs were transferred on Matrigel-coated plates and cultured in neural medium. The organoids and axon growth was monitored by live-cell imaging system (Leica DMi8TB).

Generation of hSCOs-hSMOs assembloids

To generate spinal cord-skeletal muscle (hSCOs-hSMOs) assembloids, hSCOs and hSMOs were initially cultured separately and then combined by placing them in close proximity within 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes for 3-4 days, cultured in the neural medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL HGF and 20 ng/mL IGF. Following assembly, the assembloids were transferred to a 6-well ultra-low attachment plate, with the medium refreshed every 4-6 days. The assembly process was conducted between Days 15-20 for both hSCOs and hSMOs.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Samples were fixed in 4% PFA for 30 to 90 minutes, then dehydrated overnight in a 30% sucrose solution. The organoids were embedded in OCT (Sakura Finetek) and stored at -80°C. For OCT embedded sample, the sections (10 um) were washed in PBS at room temperature for 10 minutes, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma Aldrich) for 20 minutes, and then blocked with 10% normal goat serum (Fdbio Science) for 2 hours at room temperature. Primary antibodies (Table S1) were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with PBS, the slides were incubated with secondary antibodies (Table S2) for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the slides were washed three more times with PBS for 15 min each and mounted using Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Sparkjade Biotechnology). Images were captured using a high-resolution live cell imaging system (Leica, DMi8TB) and inverted fluorescence phase contrast motorized microscope (Zeiss, Axio Observer7).

Microelectrode array (MEA) recording and analysis

MEA recordings and analyses were conducted using a neuron spike detection approach, following previously established methods.18,19 The MEA plate (Multi Channel Systems MCS GmbH) contains 59 recording electrodes and 1 internal reference electrode, arranged in an 8x8 grid with 30 µm diameter electrodes spaced 200 µm apart. Prior to use, the MEA plate was pre-coated with poly-lysine. D60 spinal cord organoids were placed in a pre-coated MEA well, immobilized with Matrigel, and incubated for 15 minutes. Fresh NB medium was then added. To stimulate organoid activity, 50 µM glutamate was introduced into the NB medium. Neuron spike recordings were performed for 1 min using the MEA system’s Multiwell Experimenter software, and the data were analyzed with Multiwell Analyzer software.

H&E and TUNEL studies

Organoids (n = 3) were harvested on day 30, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Specimens were sectioned at a thickness of 10 µm using a Leica EG 1160 microtome. The sections were then deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). Images of each section were acquired using a 40× objective on a Labophot 2 microscope (Nikon). For the TUNEL assay, the organoid frozen sections were stained with the TUNEL agents according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime) and imaged by a high-resolution live cell imaging system.

X-ray irradiation

Organoids were exposed to ionizing radiation using a Biological X-ray Irradiator (Rad Source Technologies). A 2 Gy radiation dose was chosen as it corresponds to the standard fraction used in clinical radiotherapy and has been previously demonstrated to induce DNA damage and functional impairment in neural tissues, including organoid models.5,20 The X-ray source operated at a voltage of 225.0 kV, determining the energy of the X-rays, and a dose rate of 112 cGy/min, indicating the rate of exposure. The irradiated groups were collected at 30 min, 18 h, and 72 h post-irradiation. Non-irradiated organoids served as controls.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from ten hSCOs per condition at Day 50, with three biological replicates collected for each experimental group (control, 30 minutes, 18 hours, and 72 hours post-irradiation), all derived from the same differentiation batch. RNA isolation and purification were performed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermofisher), and RNA integrity was evaluated with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). High-throughput sequencing was conducted using 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd) following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

Cell counting and statistical analysis

For each organoid, three immunostained sections were analyzed. From each section, three non-overlapping microscopic fields were randomly selected for imaging. All marker-positive cells within each field were quantified. Multiple organoids derived from independent differentiation batches were analyzed for each experimental group. The percentage of marker-positive cells was calculated relative to the total cell count. The generated stacks were analyzed using ImageJ’s Particle Analyzer (Image J, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Data are presented as mean values ± s.e.m. for three different organoids per experimental group. Statistical comparisons were conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc Tukey’s tests or paired two-tailed Student’s t-tests (GraphPad Prism 9). Exact p values are reported in the results section with significance levels defined as: *P ≤ .05, **P ≤ .01, ***P ≤ .001, ****P ≤ .0001. All data were pooled together for analysis, and outlier tests were not performed.

Results

Generation of 3D hSCOs from hiPSCs

To recapitulate the key features of early human spinal cord development, we adapted Andersen’s method16 to generated hSCOs from hiPSCs (Figure 1A). To begin, we first identified the critical factors involved in spinal cord development using a 2D cell culture system. Based on prior studies, retinoic acid (RA) and sonic hedgehog (SHH) were recognized as essential signaling molecules governing the development of the dorso-ventral axis of the spinal cord system,21,22 we hypothesized that RA and the SHH agonist (SAG) would influence motor neuron development. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed co-expression of TUJ (a general neuronal marker) and ISL-1 (a motor neuron marker, Figure 1B and C), confirming that RA and SAG facilitated motor neuron differentiation. Additionally, we observed the expression of SOX2 (a neural progenitors marker), and HOXC6 (a homeodomain transcription factor associated with spinal cord identity) (Figure 1C).

To characterize the early stages of motor neuron differentiation, we examined the expression patterns of PAX6 and OLIG2, markers of neural progenitors and motor neuron progenitors, respectively. Co-expression was observed in 2D-differentiated neuronal progenitors between Days 6 and 14 (Figure 1D). The temporal expression profile of these markers aligns with the expected developmental timeline, wherein PAX6 expression precedes that of OLIG2 during motor neuron specification. As motor neurons mature, PAX6 expression gradually decreases, while OLIG2 expression increases, reflecting the progressive commitment to the motor neuron lineage. Based on these findings, the combined exposure to RA and SAG enhanced the differentiation of motor neurons and promoted ventral neural identity. Consequently, we incorporated both RA and SAG into the differentiation medium to better mimic the complex in vivo environment and developmental processes of the human spinal cord. The resulting aggregates expanded to over 2.0 mm by Day 40, exhibiting a round, uniform, and relatively homogeneous morphology (Figure 1E and F).

Characterization of cellular composition in 3D hSCOs from hiPSCs

Our 3D culture system successfully recapitulated key aspects of neural tube development, providing a platform for modeling human spinal cord formation. To assess the fidelity of our system in mimicking spinal cord development, we evaluated several hallmark features of neural tube formation at Day 10 using specific developmental markers. First, the presence of tight junction protein ZO-1, an apical polarity marker, along with SOX2, a neuroectodermal marker, confirmed the establishment of a neuroepithelial layer, indicative of neural tube-like organization (Figure 2A). Additionally, the co-expression of SOX2 and NESTIN further validated the formation of apical-basal polarity within the organoids (Figure 2A). We also identified the co-expression of SOX2, HOXC6, and SOX1, which reinforced the neural identity of the developing organoids (Figure 2A). These results demonstrate that by Day 10, the organoids exhibited key features of early neural development, including the establishment of apical-basal polarity, neural specification, and the initiation of neural tube formation, crucial steps in spinal cord development.

At Day 20, the neuroectodermal markers SOX2 and PAX6 were still strongly expressed (Figure 2B), signifying the initiation of neural tube folding and the formation of neural groove-like structures. Notably, we observed co-expression of TUJ (a pan-neuronal marker) and NESTIN at the periphery of these tube-like structures, indicating the differentiation of neural stem cells into immature neurons (Figure 2B). This finding suggests that our culture system initiates early neuronal differentiation, with the neuronal precursors primarily located along the outer regions of the tube. Furthermore, the emergence of radial cell alignment and the formation of primary cilia within the apical cavity highlighted the developing organization of the neuroepithelial layer, further supporting the neural tube-like architecture (Figure 2C). These results collectively demonstrate that our culture conditions efficiently promote the specification of hPSCs toward a neural fate.

To further evaluate the developmental potential of the hSCOs, we performed a detailed analysis of several key markers at different time points during the culture period. At Day 50, we observed the expression of glial markers S100β (astrocyte marker) and GFAP, as well as MBP (myelinating oligodendrocyte marker), confirming the presence of both glial cells and myelination within the organoids (Figure 2D). This finding mirrors the typical progression of spinal cord development, with neurons and glial cells becoming closely associated, a hallmark of mature spinal cord formation. Additionally, we observed the expression of ISL-1 and its co-expression with SMI32, indicating the development of motor neurons and a strong ventral patterning within the organoid (Figure 2D). The expression of spinal cord interneuron markers, PAX2 (for V0 and V1 subtypes) and CHX10 (for V2a subtype), further confirmed the formation of complex neural networks, characteristic of the ventral spinal cord (Figure 2D). Continued expression of CHAT, a cholinergic neuron marker, alongside ISL-1, validated the sustained presence of motor neurons, reinforcing the resemblance to ventral spinal cord development in vivo (Figure 2D). Together, these findings demonstrate that the spinal cord organoids generated in our culture system exhibit the key features of spinal cord development, closely mirroring the processes observed in the human spinal cord, particularly in the ventral region.

Assessment of neuronal activity and function in 3D hSCOs

To thoroughly assess the neuronal function of hSCOs, we employed MEA analysis to evaluate the electrical activity. MEA recordings successfully detected spontaneous electrical activity, which demonstrated the active and spontaneous neuronal function within the hSCOs (Figure 3A-E). This initial observation highlighted the capability of the organoids to generate intrinsic neural activity, reflecting their functional potential. Following this, we aimed to further investigate neuronal activation by subjecting the hSCOs to glutamate treatment, a known excitatory neurotransmitter (Figure 3A-E). The results revealed a significant increase in both the frequency of neuronal spikes and the mean spike count following glutamate exposure, indicating a marked elevation in neuronal activation. Additionally, we observed a notable reduction in the mean interburst interval after glutamate treatment, suggesting a shortened refractory period between bursts (Figure 3A-E). This reduction in the interburst interval is indicative of an enhanced responsiveness and excitability of the neural network within the organoid, signifying functional maturation of the neurons (Figure 3A-E). These findings provide evidence of an active and responsive neuronal network in hSCOs, further confirming their functional capacity.

Remarkably, we also observed substantial axonal growth within hSCOs, which is a key indicator of neuronal network development. The robust axonal outgrowth underscores the potential for intricate neural networking and connectivity within these organoids. This development is crucial for the application of hSCOs in therapeutic contexts, where effective integration and communication between neurons are vital for successful transplantation and functional recovery. Additionally, the expression of specific neuronal markers-TUJ (a general neuronal marker), CHAT (a cholinergic marker), and SMI-32 (a marker of mature neurons)—further substantiated the presence of functional neurons within the organoids, particularly cholinergic neurons (Figure 3F and G). The expression of these markers indicates the formation of a diverse neuronal population, reinforcing the model’s capacity for generating mature, functionally active neurons.

To further explore the potential of hSCOs in neural-muscle interactions and to evaluate the functional connectivity of neurons derived from hSCOs with muscle tissue, we co-cultured the hSCOs with hSMOs to form assembloids model (Figure 3H). Co-culture revealed that TUJ-expressing neurons from the hSCOs successfully innervated the FAST MHC-labeled skeletal muscle region, forming functional connections. The skeletal muscle markers MYOD and DESMIN were specifically localized to the muscle region, confirming the identity of the skeletal muscle cells within the culture (Figure 3I). Moreover, we observed rhythmic contractions within the skeletal muscle region, further supporting the functionality and integration of the hSCOs neurons with the muscle tissue (Video S1).

Simulation of radiation-induced spinal cord damage in vitro using hSCOs

Clinically, single-fraction spinal cord irradiation typically ranges from 1 to 4 Gy, with higher per-fraction doses (4-7 Gy) used in multifraction regimens.23–25 To determine the optimal irradiation dose for hSCOs, Day 50 organoids were exposed to a single dose ranging from 0.5 to 30 Gy and analyzed at 30 minutes, 72 hours, and 7 days post-irradiation, using NEUN+ neuron percentage as the primary indicator of integrity (Figure 4A and B). At 30 minutes, all irradiated groups exhibited a reduction in NEUN+ cell fractions relative to non-irradiated controls, with the decline being slightly less pronounced at 0.5 Gy. By 72 hours, partial recovery of NEUN+ neurons was evident in the 0.5-2 Gy groups, whereas exposure to 10-30 Gy led to further reductions, indicating sustained neurotoxicity. At 7 days, the decline in NEUN+ neurons persisted in the 10-30 Gy groups, and NEUN+ cells were nearly undetectable at 20-30 Gy, suggesting irreversible neuronal loss and organoid necrosis at these high doses. In contrast, the 0.5-5 Gy groups displayed varying degrees of recovery: NEUN+ neurons in the 0.5 Gy group returned to near-baseline levels, whereas the 5 Gy group recovered to only approximately half of the control value. Notably, the 2 Gy group showed no substantial improvement compared with its 72-hour measurement, indicating persistent injury without full recovery. Thus, these findings indicate that 2 Gy represents an appropriate dose for modeling sustained radiation-induced neuronal injury while preserving sufficient tissue viability to assess intrinsic recovery mechanisms. This dose aligns with clinical recommended single-fraction parameters and was therefore applied in subsequent experiments to characterize the radiation response of spinal cord tissue. At Day 0, the irradiated organoids received a 2 Gy radiation dose, while control groups underwent identical culture and fixation protocols without irradiation. The DNA damage response was evaluated at three different time points following irradiation: early (30 minutes and 18 hours) and later (72 hours). At 30 minutes, the total area of 53BP1 foci (in μm2) in SOX2+ cells was significantly increased, reflecting the initial accumulation of DNA damage. The foci area peaked at 30 minutes post-irradiation and gradually decreased over time. By 72 hours, most of the smaller foci had resolved, suggesting recovery in some cells, while larger and more complex foci persisted, indicating that more severe DNA damage required a longer recovery period (Figure 4D). Additionally, notable increase in SOX2 expression at 30 minutes post-irradiation, suggesting a robust early DNA damage response and activation of neural progenitor cells. By 72 hours, the percentage of SOX2+ cells returned to baseline, indicating that the progenitor population either differentiated or underwent cell cycle arrest as part of the repair process (Figure 4E).

The progenitor cells in the hSCOs at Day 50 are primarily differentiated into GFAP+ astrocytes and NeuN+ neurons, together with SOX2 as the marker of progenitor cells. Further analysis using immunofluorescent staining for NEUN (a marker for mature neurons) and GFAP (a marker for astrocytes) revealed significant damage to mature neurons at 30 minutes and 18 hours post-irradiation (Figure 5A and B). Over time, the number of NEUN+/TUNEL+ neurons decreases, suggesting neuronal loss stabilizes after an initial phase of damage (Figure 5C). Despite some recovery observed in the later time points, the mature neurons did not fully recover by 72 hours, indicating persistent radiation-induced damage (Figure 5C). GFAP+ astrocytes decreased due to apoptosis at the early damage phase (30 minutes), but it gradually increased over the intermediate (18 hours) and late (72 hours) time points, suggesting the initiation of reactive astrogliosis in response to radiation-induced injury (Figure 5B). The persistent increase in GFAP expression at 72 hours indicated that reactive astrocyte proliferation continued long after the initial insult (Figure 5B). This distinction is crucial for understanding how radiation differentially affects glial and neuronal populations within the organoid model.

In parallel, MEA analysis of neuronal activity demonstrated significant functional impairment following radiation exposure. At 30 minutes post-irradiation, the number of spikes, mean spike count, and spikes within bursts were significantly reduced. Additionally, the mean interburst interval and burst duration were prolonged, indicating impaired neuronal activity. Interestingly, these parameters did not show significant differences between the 30-minute and 18-hour time points, suggesting that spinal cord function was continuously affected during the early and intermediate phases of radiation exposure. By 72 hours, MEA analysis revealed partial recovery in spinal cord function, with an increase in the number of spikes, mean spike count, and a normalization of burst duration and interburst intervals, although the recovery was incomplete (Figure 6A-E). To further assess neuronal activity post-irradiation, glutamate was administered at various time points. Glutamate stimulation at 30 minutes and 18 hours post-irradiation revealed a marked reduction in neuronal excitability, as evidenced by a significant decrease in spike frequency, burst activity, mean spike count, and burst duration compared to control conditions. The prolonged interburst interval following glutamate stimulation further highlighted impaired synaptic transmission and network functionality during these phases. By 72 hours, glutamate stimulation induced a partial recovery of neuronal activity, with increased spike frequency and burst activity; however, these responses remained below pre-irradiation levels (Figure 6A-E). These findings suggest that while some functional recovery occurs, synaptic efficacy and network dynamics remain impaired even after 72 hours.

Transcriptomic profiling reveals time-dependent molecular responses to radiation in hSCOs

To gain deeper insights into the molecular responses of Day 50 hSCOs to radiation-induced injury, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across four groups: control, 30 minutes, 18 hours, and 72 hours post-irradiation. KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were subsequently applied to characterize the functional categories and signaling pathways associated with these DEGs. The comparative transcriptomic profiling revealed distinct temporal expression patterns and progressive pathway perturbations following radiation exposure.

Principal component analysis and a sample-sample distance heatmap demonstrated clear segregation among the groups (Figure 7A and B). Samples within each time point exhibited strong intra-group similarity, indicating high reproducibility. The moderate separation between the 30-minute and control groups reflects early transcriptional alterations of limited magnitude. In contrast, the 18-hour group showed substantially greater divergence from both control and 30-minute samples, suggesting extensive secondary transcriptional reprogramming. By 72 hours, samples exhibited the greatest global distances relative to all other groups, consistent with widespread transcriptomic remodeling during the late response phase. Volcano plot analysis revealed 170, 548, and 1404 uniquely upregulated mRNAs in the 30-minute, 18-hour, and 72-hour groups, respectively (Figure 7C-E), and 416 shared upregulated transcripts between the 18-hour and 72-hour groups (Figure 7F).

GO enrichment analysis revealed a clear time-dependent progression of biological processes following irradiation. At 30 minutes, hSCOs exhibited an acute stress response, with enrichment of pathways related to arachidonic acid metabolism, inflammatory signaling, and DNA repair, indicating rapid activation of pro-inflammatory and damage-response mechanisms (Figure 7G). By 18 hours, apoptotic pathways became predominant, including TRAIL-mediated and extrinsic apoptotic signaling, alongside sustained p53-dependent DNA damage responses, cell-cycle arrest, and inhibition of DNA biosynthesis. These changes collectively promote the elimination of severely damaged cells and maintain an inflammatory microenvironment (Figure 7I). At 72 hours, DNA damage and apoptosis-related pathways remained active, while processes associated with astrocyte activation and neuronal protection were increasingly enriched, suggesting a transition toward tissue stabilization. Concurrent enrichment of neurotransmitter and calcium transport pathways indicates that surviving neurons begin to restore functional activity and synaptic communication (Figure 7I). KEGG enrichment analysis comparing the 18-hour and 72-hour groups revealed enhanced calcium signaling and neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions at 72 hours. Enrichment of the cholinergic synapse pathway suggests functional remodeling or partial restoration of specific neural circuits, while the synaptic vesicle cycle pathway provides a direct indicator of synaptic maturation and activity.

Together, these transcriptomic findings support the notion of progressive neuronal recovery by 72 hours, consistent with the electrophysiological and histological improvements observed in earlier experiments.

Discussion

Our study builds upon and modifies previous methodologies to generate hSCOs model, enhancing previous protocols to better capture motor neuron development within a 3D culture system. Specifically, we investigated key differentiation factors for motor neurons, identifying that simultaneous supplementation of SAG and RA more effectively promotes motor neuron formation compared to earlier methods.28–31 This finding guided our decision to incorporate both SAG and RA at the early stages of spinal cord organoid development, thereby better supporting neural tube formation and enabling the organoids to self-organize with structural and functional properties akin to the human spinal cord. In our culture system, we leverage the intrinsic self-organization of hPSCs and a 3D suspension culture environment. This approach enabled not only foundational developmental processes but also the modeling of complex spinal cord characteristics, including apical-basal polarity, neuroepithelial alignment, and cellular diversity. Consistent with prior research,32,33 our culture protocol promotes a progressive differentiation cascade, driving hPSCs toward a neuroectodermal fate, as evidenced by strong expression of markers such as SOX2, PAX6, and NESTIN. These markers are reflective of early neural tube development, indicating a successful induction of neuroectoderm identity within the organoids.

Through precise modulation of signaling pathways, our hSCOs exhibited dorsoventral (DV) patterning within the neural tube, forming five distinct ventral domains (P0-P2, pMN, and P3) that each corresponded to specific molecular markers:34,35 P0-P2 domains were characterized by PAX6, the P3 domain by NKX2.2, and the pMN domain by NKX6.1 and OLIG2. These ventral domains subsequently gave rise to various neuronal subtypes, including V0 and V1 (PAX2+), V2a (CHX10+), and motor neurons (ISL1+). This carefully orchestrated differentiation trajectory in hSCOs underscores the robustness of our protocol in recapitulating the diversity of neuronal subtypes present in the spinal cord, further validated by immunofluorescence and MEA analysis showing spontaneous neural activity and functional maturation. To test functional relevance, hSCOs were co-cultured with hSMOs to model NMJ formation. Within this assembloid model, motor neurons extended axons outward from the hSCOs, similar to the development of motor nerve fascicles in vivo.36 Notably, we observed rhythmic contractions in hSMOs, which required neuron-matured muscle fibers, a process consistent with physiological muscle innervation and previous studies.16,37 The ability of hSCOs to innervate and influence the skeletal muscle tissue highlights their potential for generating functional neuromuscular networks, which is of critical importance for their application in regenerative medicine and therapeutic settings.

In our radiation injury model, we exposed mature hSCOs to a clinically relevant dose of 2 Gy to simulate spinal cord damage. After radiation, we observed elevated 53BP1 expression, marking active DNA damage response. This expression diminished over time, indicating that the initial response to DNA damage was transient. However, radiation primarily affected postmitotic and immature cells, particularly SOX2+ progenitor cells, echoing findings from prior CNS damage studies conducted with both animal models and cerebral organoids.38,39 Subsequent assessments showed a notable reduction in immature and mature neurons (SOX2+ and NEUN+ cells) at early and intermediate stages, suggesting ongoing DNA damage repair and neuronal loss. At later stages, although a partial increase in the number of NeuN+ cells was observed, levels remained below those of the control group. This finding is more likely to reflect the recovery of neuronal structural integrity and the normalization of NeuN protein expression in surviving neurons, rather than de novo neurogenesis. Notably, NeuN expression can be dynamically downregulated in response to acute stress or injury; therefore, its subsequent reappearance may indicate a partial restoration of neuronal physiological function rather than true regeneration of the neuronal population.40 Especially, the transient increase in SOX2-positive cells at early time points may represent an attempt by neural progenitors to initiate DNA repair mechanisms. However, the subsequent decrease in SOX2-positive cells suggests a loss of progenitor function or differentiation, which could contribute to impaired regenerative capacity post-radiation. These findings align with previous studies showing that radiotherapy exerts its strongest effects on dividing cells, often halting proliferation-a critical factor in CNS vulnerability to radiation.5,41 Astrocyte response, as indicated by GFAP expression, further illustrated the organoid’s response to radiation. Early post-radiation, astrocytes appeared damaged, but subsequently, we observed increased GFAP expression, indicating reactive astrocytosis and sustained astrocyte hyperplasia.38 This reaction persisted even at later time points, aligning with known patterns in radiation-induced neuroinflammation, wherein reactive astrocytes play a role in neuronal injury and disruption of neurogenesis. MEA analyses underscored the functional damage, showing reduced spike frequency and burst activity, indicative of persistent functional impairment and potential links to sustained astrocyte hyperplasia and neuronal injury.

While our hSCO model represents a significant advancement in modeling spinal cord development and radiation-induced injury, there are limitations. First, while our Day 50 hSCOs successfully recapitulate key structural and functional features of the spinal cord, they primarily reflect an early fetal developmental stage rather than a fully mature adult spinal cord. This developmental discrepancy represents an inherent limitation of current organoid systems and should be considered when extrapolating these findings to radiation-induced spinal cord injury in adult patients. In addition, organoids lack the full vascularization and immune components, which are crucial for accurately modeling radiation responses, particularly the immune and inflammatory aspects. In future work, incorporating vascularization or co-culturing with microglia could provide a more physiologically relevant model. Further, single-cell RNA sequencing or other high-throughput analyses would offer a more granular characterization of cellular heterogeneity and radiation-specific transcriptional changes. Future systematic investigations across a broader dose spectrum will evaluate potential sublethal effects at lower doses and establish irreversible injury thresholds at higher doses.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we established a 3D hPSC-derived spinal cord organoid model that recapitulates key features of spinal cord development and function, including neural tube-like structures, functional neural networks, and spontaneous neuronal activity responsive to glutamate. Functional innervation was confirmed through co-culture with human skeletal muscle organoids, as evidenced by rhythmic contractions. Following radiation exposure, hSCOs exhibited early DNA damage, sustained astrocyte reactivity, and impaired neuronal function, with partial recovery at later stages. This model provides a robust platform for investigating radiation-induced spinal cord injury and developing therapeutic strategies for neuroprotection and repair.
