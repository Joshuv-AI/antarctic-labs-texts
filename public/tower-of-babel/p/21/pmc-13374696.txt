Negative-pressure pulmonary edema
Authors: Malcolm Lemyze, Maxime Granier, Dominic C Marshall
Published: 2026

Negative-pressure pulmonary edema (NPPE) is an underrecognized cause of acute noncardiogenic pulmonary edema

It should be suspected in patients who rapidly develop acute hypoxemic respiratory distress after forceful inspiratory efforts against an occluded upper airway. Incidence is estimated between 0.1% and 12%, but true rates are likely underestimated and cases underdiagnosed.1–3 Patients typically present with acute signs of respiratory distress (including dyspnea and oxygen desaturation) shortly after an upper airway obstructive event, which progresses to anxiety, stridor, accessory muscle use, cyanosis, and pink, frothy sputum. The pathophysiology centres on hydrostatic stress and alveolar–capillary injury resulting from the deep negative intrathoracic pressure caused by forceful diaphragmatic contractions against occluded airways.1,2,4

Various causes of upper airway obstruction can precipitate NPPE

Potential causes include postintubation laryngeal edema, postictal obstruction, strangulation, obstructing tumour or infection, foreign body, biting an endotracheal tube, and rarely, severe obstructive sleep apnea.1,4

Immediate management involves relief of upper airway obstruction, maintenance of airway patency, and oxygenation

The first priority is reopening the airways (e.g., using jaw thrust, suction, and an oropharyngeal airway as appropriate) to relieve negative pressure. Simultaneously, supplemental oxygen should be applied immediately, and rapid-sequence intubation is appropriate in the most severe cases. In cooperative, awake patients with a patent airway, positive pressure noninvasive ventilation (NIV) is a treatment option. If NIV fails, invasive ventilation is considered.1,2,4

After stabilization, clinicians should confirm NPPE and exclude mimics

A rapid investigation should include electrocardiography, cardiac biomarkers (e.g., brain natriuretic peptide, troponin), chest radiography, and arterial blood gases to exclude cardiogenic edema and explore other causes of acute respiratory failure. Echocardiography or invasive cardiac output monitoring may be helpful.1,3

When airway patency is rapidly restored, prognosis is generally excellent

With appropriate support, improvement typically occurs within 12 to 48 hours owing to brisk alveolar fluid clearance and the predominantly hydrostatic mechanism.1,3
