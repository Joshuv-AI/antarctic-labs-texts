Brucellosis. A Rare Cause of Biliary Dysplasia and Ampullary Adenocarcinoma?
Authors: Zachary Vinton, Kevin Wolfe, Sean MacBride, Thomas Heffron, Vimal Jhaveri, Andrea K. Sotelo, Mark Rosenblatt, Clark Kulig, Matthew Stotts
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBrucellosis, a zoonotic bacterial infection often characterized by nonspecific symptoms including fever, night sweats, and weight loss, has a prolonged and recurrent course, often leading to chronic symptoms if inadequately treated. Hepatic involvement is well-documented with manifestations ranging from mild hepatitis to hepatic abscesses and granulomas. However, the association of brucellosis with biliary dysplasia or ampullary adenocarcinoma has not been previously reported in medical literature. We present a 68-year-old man with hepatic granulomatosis due to chronic brucellosis complicated by numerous episodes of ascending cholangitis secondary to choledocholithiasis and common bile duct strictures, with high-grade dysplasia and development of ampullary adenocarcinoma.

INTRODUCTION

Brucellosis is a zoonosis transmitted through direct contact with livestock or contaminated animal products.1 It affects multiple organ systems with an incubation period ranging from 5 days to 6 months, often presenting within 2 to 4 weeks.2 It can present with cyclic fever, night sweats, arthralgia, myalgias, anorexia, and fatigue lasting more than a year, complicating diagnosis.3 Although uncommon in the United States with 100–200 cases reported annually, it remains a major public health concern internationally with an estimated global incidence of 1.6–2.1 million new cases each year.4 Owing to its predilection for the reticuloendothelial system, hepatic involvement is consistent, although the extent and clinical manifestations vary, often following a benign course with appropriate antibiotic therapy.5 Conversely, biliary complications such as cholecystitis and vanishing bile duct syndrome are seldom reported.6–8 Although cholangitis is a known risk factor for the development of cholangiocarcinoma,9 there are no published cases of dysplasia or malignancy associated with chronic brucellosis in the literature. We present a case of hepatic granulomatosis secondary to chronic brucellosis with the development of high-grade biliary dysplasia and ampullary adenocarcinoma (AAC).

CASE REPORT

A 68-year-old man with a history of tobacco use presented with 18 months of night sweats, headaches, intermittent fever and chills, anorexia, and a 45-pound weight loss after a cholecystectomy. Gallbladder pathology demonstrated no dysplasia or malignancy. He continued to have vague symptoms with aspartate aminotransferase: 55 IU/L, alanine transaminase: 73 IU/L, alkaline phosphatase: 339 U/L, and total bilirubin of 4.5 mg/dL, prompting multiple endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) revealing choledocholithiasis, sludge, and recurrent strictures requiring repeat stenting. Brushings taken at the distal common bile duct (CBD) stricture showed no malignancy, but a biopsy demonstrated high-grade dysplasia. An occluded major papilla stent was present.

Owing to this dysplasia, a pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure) was attempted but aborted after numerous nodular hepatic lesions with abnormal surrounding lymph nodes were observed. Liver and lymph node biopsies revealed caseating granulomas, chronic inflammation, and no evidence of malignancy (Figure 1). Multiple stains were performed and negative including Acid Fast, Grocott Methenamine, Periodic acid-Schiff, IgG4, and Warthin-Starry stains. CD3 staining showed reactive T-cell infiltrates, and CD20 staining showed small B-cell aggregates. Universal polymerase chain reaction was negative for bacterial, mycobacterium, or fungal deoxyribonucleic acid. Further infectious workup included bacterial and fungal blood cultures and testing for syphilis, tuberculosis, histoplasmosis, Coccidioides, Coxiella, and Bartonella antibodies; all of which were negative. However, he had positive serum IgG and negative IgM for Brucella on enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay polymerase chain reaction. Further serology was positive for smooth muscle antibodies, but negative for IgG4, antineutrophil cytoplasmic and antimitochondrial antibodies.

He was treated with doxycycline and rifampin but developed an intolerable allergic reaction, resulting in completion of 5 weeks of the 12-week course. Symptoms partially improved, and he declined further treatment. He agreed to strict return precautions with plans for further laboratory results and imaging if symptoms persisted or worsened.

He returned 15 months later with a recurrence of constitutional symptoms and new-onset pruritus and jaundice, concerning for cholangitis with aspartate aminotransferase: 140 IU/L, alanine transaminase: 220 IU/L, alkaline phosphatase: 520 U/L, and total bilirubin of 4.4 mg/dL. ERCP showed an edematous major papilla with a gaping orifice and moderate dilation of the entire CBD. Biopsy of the ampulla of Vater (AoV) and the distal CBD revealed extensive high-grade dysplasia without evidence of malignancy. He was discharged on antibiotics with planned follow-up. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography 2 months later demonstrated a new CBD dilation and abrupt caliber change at the ampulla suspicious for neoplasm with an enlarged peripancreatic lymph node. Abdominal computed tomography showed no pancreatic lesion. Repeat ERCP with endoscopic ultrasound showed a lobulated 12 × 10 mm mass with sonographic evidence suggesting invasion into the bile duct. Transduodenal fine needle aspiration biopsies of an enlarged lymph node in the porta hepatis region and the ampullary mass confirmed a focal invasive AAC with duodenal-type differentiation. Similar cytologic features indicative of malignancy were also identified within the CBD.

Whipple procedure was successful with moderately differentiated AAC on pathology. There was pancreatic tissue invasion with negative margins. Multiple periportal and mesenteric lymph nodes resected were positive for tumor with a final diagnosis consistent with pT3, pN1, and MX AoV adenocarcinoma.

The patient started adjuvant leucovorin, fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin therapy. An agglutination study was positive for Brucella IgG, and doxycycline was resumed throughout chemotherapy. Repeat liver enzymes showed normalization. Repeat abdominal computed tomography was without focal intrahepatic lesions, pancreatic dilation, or biliary ductal dilation and demonstrated intrahepatic pneumobilia consistent with prior sphincterotomy.

DISCUSSION

Brucellosis rarely causes biliary complications and has no established link to dysplasia or malignancy. However, several case reports implicate brucellosis' involvement in cholecystitis, cholangitis, and vanishing bile duct syndrome.7,8 In our case, chronic hepatobiliary infection—evidenced by positive Brucella IgG and hepatic nodules with caseating granulomas—may have contributed to chronic inflammation of the CBD leading to strictures, recurrent cholangitis, and dysplasia, although there is no definitive link. Nevertheless, it remains a possibility as chronic inflammation is a well-known driver of abnormal cellular proliferation with DNA damage, mutation, and oncogenesis.10 This is exemplified in Helicobacter pylori infection, wherein persistent gastritis leads to intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and carcinoma.11

AAC is rare, accounting for 0.2% of gastrointestinal cancers, and arises sporadically or from ampullary adenomas, particularly in the setting of familial adenomatous polyposis.12 Although there is no known association between AAC and brucellosis or dysplasia, it is plausible through similar inflammatory mechanisms that chronic inflammation and subsequent dysplasia at the distal CBD could contribute to neoplastic transformation at the AoV due to its proximity. Alternatively, AAC may have developed independently from dysplasia and brucellosis as there is no definitive association. Other potential etiologies, such as autoimmune hepatobiliary disease, were also considered in this case. Although smooth muscle antibody positivity raised suspicion for autoimmune hepatitis or PSC, neither imaging nor histopathology revealed characteristic features to support diagnosis. This case highlights the importance of considering brucellosis in unexplained, recurrent cholangitis and the potential for dysplasia and malignancy from chronic infection.
