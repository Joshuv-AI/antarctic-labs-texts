Potassium enrichment mechanism and controlling factors in Cambrian black shale from eastern Guizhou, China
Authors: Junwei Fu, Lingling Tu, Shuang Zhao, Tianrui Jiang, Wen Pan, Wei Cheng, Chunhua Shi, Ruidong Yang, Yonggang Li, Junbo Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPotassium is a critical mineral resource for global agricultural security. Besides soluble potash salts, insoluble potassium resources hosted in black shale series represent an important alternative potash mineral resource. Extensively developed Cambrian potassium-rich black shales occur in the Wanshan District of Tongren, eastern Guizhou Province, southwestern China. They have K₂O contents ranging from 8% to 11% and prospective reserves exceeding 5 billion tons. However, the potassium enrichment mechanism and its controlling factors in the potassium-bearing shales of this region remain unclear currently. Therefore, this study presents petrographic and elemental geochemical analyses of two representative sections in Wanshan, Tongren, Guizhou. The results indicate that potassium-rich felsic terrigenous detritus in the northwestern study area was transported to a passive continental margin setting, and deposited under restricted, suboxic to anoxic-dominated water conditions. The sedimentation-diagenesis stage underwent potassium metasomatism — a process that promoted the transformation of aluminous clay minerals into illite, eventually leading to the formation of potassium-bearing shales with K-feldspar and other minerals as the major potassium-bearing phases. Introduction

Potash resources are critical for potassium fertilizer production, directly related to food security and supply stability, and possess significant economic value and strategic importance1–3. Global potash resources are highly unevenly distributed4, 5. According to statistics, as of 2021, the total global reserves were 3.5 billion metric tons (expressed as K₂O), with 64.29% highly concentrated in countries such as Canada, Russia, and Belarus6. Moreover, the global supply of potash resources is dominated by soluble potash salts. Potash resources are highly concentrated in a few countries, making the global supply chain vulnerable to geopolitical tensions and trade disruptions. In China, for instance, agricultural potassium fertilizer production primarily relies on resources from the Qarhan Playa in Qinghai, the Lop Nur Salt Lake in Xinjiang, and the northern Tibet salt lake regions7. Due to substantial domestic demand, China’s external dependence on potassium fertilizer remains as high as approximately 50%1, 8. Therefore, the exploration and discovery of new types of potash resources is of significant importance for enhancing global agricultural production and ensuring food security.

In contrast to soluble potash resources, insoluble potash resources are mainly hosted in potassium-bearing shales, with the significant advantages of large reserves and wide distribution. Black shales were extensively developed throughout geological history. Statistical analysis of K₂O contents in typical black shales from various periods worldwide reveals two major episodes of potassium-rich black shale deposition during the Archean and Phanerozoic eons, with average K₂O contents reaching 8.01% and 8.13% (data representative of K-rich shale units, not the global average for the era), respectively (Fig. 1). The Tongren area in eastern Guizhou Province, China, is a typical region enriched with black shale-type potash resources, with prospective reserves estimated to exceed 5 billion metric tons9. These deposits are characterized by wide distribution, high grade, great thickness, large scale, and stable occurrence10, making them a crucial natural laboratory for investigating the enrichment mechanisms of potassium in sedimentary systems.

The potassium-bearing shales in the Tongren area are primarily hosted in the Cambrian Aoxi Formation. Although their K₂O content (8–11%)29 is significantly higher than that of other black shales worldwide, their genesis and enrichment mechanisms remain poorly constrained. Therefore, to address the above knowledge gaps and leverage its global relevance, this study focuses on the potassium-rich shales of the Cambrian Aoxi Formation in Tongren, aiming to elucidate the mechanisms, geological processes, and key controlling factors of potassium enrichment, and to reveal its occurrence states. The findings of this study will have multiple implications. First, they will provide an important basis for deepening the theoretical understanding of potassium enrichment and mineralization in black shale-type potassium deposits, while offering practical insights for the comprehensive development and utilization of insoluble potassium resources. Second, the interpretation of the genesis of potassium-rich black shales in this region can serve as a valuable analog for assessing the potential of insoluble potassium resources in other widely distributed but less explored black shale sequences worldwide. Furthermore, given that typical black shales are often enriched with various critical metals such as Ni, Co30, 31, clarifying the sedimentary environment of these potassium-rich black shales can also provide valuable insights into the enrichment mechanisms of these metals.

Geological setting

Against the background of Rodinia supercontinent breakup, the late Ediacaran Period experienced global warming, which led to the melting of glaciers and thus triggered extensive marine transgression. During this period, Guizhou exhibited regionally differential deposition, with its western part dominated by carbonate and clastic rocks, whereas the eastern part was primarily characterized by deep-water siliceous rocks and carbonaceous mudstone formations32. During the Cambrian Taijiang-Niuchehe Age, the paleotopography of eastern Guizhou, including the Tongren area, generally featured higher elevations in the northwest and lower areas in the southeast. Marine transgression, however, proceeded in the opposite direction, from the southeast to the northwest. As shown in Fig. 2a, a series of shoals developed along the NE–NNE-trending platform margin facies zone in the study area and its southern extension. These shoals are spaced at intervals and spatially form a paleogeographic pattern resembling an island chain. In fact, the paleogeography during the deposition of the Cambrian Aoxi Formation in the Tongren area largely inherited the framework from the earlier Duyun Age. Influenced by the platform-margin shoal facies belt during this period, the spatial distribution of potassium-rich shales exhibits lateral discontinuity; nevertheless, their overall occurrence remains confined to the platform margin facies belt.

The regional stratigraphy primarily consists of the Qingshuijiang Formation (Qingbaikouan System); the Tiesi’ao, Datangpo, and Nantuo Formations (Nanhuan System); the Doushantuo and Liuchapo Formations (Ediacaran System); the Muchang, Wuxun, Aoxi, Huaqiao, Chefu, Bitiao, and Zhuitun Formations (Cambrian System); and Quaternary deposits (Fig. 2b). The sedimentary sequence comprises a suite of carbonate rocks and terrigenous clastics, with significant lateral variations in lithology and thickness. In contrast, the strata exposed within the study area are relatively limited, consisting solely of the Cambrian Wuxun and Aoxi Formations. The Wuxun Formation is characterized by gray silty shale and interbedded gray to dark gray thin- to medium-bedded limestone with thin beds of dolomite. The Aoxi Formation lithology consists of gray to light gray dolomite, limestone, and black shale. In the Tongren area, potassium-rich shales are primarily concentrated in the Cambrian Aoxi Formation. Meanwhile, the study region is characterized by relatively well-developed fold and fault structures, with a predominant NNE strike.

Sedimentary characteristics of the section

The potassium-bearing shales in Tongren are primarily hosted in the second member of the Middle Cambrian Aoxi Formation and occur in a stratiform manner. The first member of the Aoxi Formation consists of grey to dark grey, medium-bedded, fine-crystalline dolomite intercalated with grey fine-crystalline limestone; the second member is predominantly composed of potassium-bearing shale. The sedimentary characteristics of the two key sections (BT1 and BT2) focused on in this study are described in detail as follows:

Section BT1 (Fig. 3a): Member 1 of the Aoxi Formation is predominantly composed of grey to light grey, medium-bedded dolomite with a thickness exceeding 10 m, exhibiting parallel bedding and minor siliceous lenses. The lower part of Member 2 consists of dark grey to grey-black shale displaying horizontal laminations (Fig. 3d). The middle-upper part is dominated by grey to light grey, thin-bedded mudstone (shale). The potassium-bearing shale exhibits a thickness of approximately 27.8 m.

Section BT2 (Fig. 3b): Member 1 of the Aoxi Formation features interbedded light grey, thin-bedded limestone and brown to greyish-black shale (Fig. 3f). The lower part of Member 2 consists mainly of black to greyish-black shale, occasionally intercalated with limestone layers approximately 2 cm thick. The middle part is dominated by grey, light grey, and dark grey thin-bedded mudstone (shale); shale near the contact with the lower part contains calcareous limestone interbeds about 20 cm thick. The upper part is characterized by dark grey to greyish-black shale. The potassium-bearing shale has a thickness of approximately 15.3 m.

Sampling and analytical method

Samples were collected from natural outcrops following standard fieldwork practices for basic geological research. We selected two sections, BT1 and BT2, as primary study sites, situated in Wanshan District, Tongren City, Guizhou Province. Exposed strata at these localities consist predominantly of the first and second members (M1 and M2) of the Middle Cambrian Aoxi Formation. To ensure sample continuity and integrity while accounting for lithological variations and sampling intervals, a total of 20 samples were collected from the base to the top, with 9 from section BT1 and 11 from section BT2. (Fig. 3a, b).

Major elements

Major element analysis was performed at ALS Laboratory in Guangzhou. During the experimental process, samples were initially crushed and split, with 300 g selected and ground to 75 μm (200 mesh). Subsequently, the ground material was mixed with a lithium borate–lithium nitrate flux containing lithium nitrate and thoroughly homogenized before being fused at high temperature. The melt was then poured into a platinum mold and cooled to form a flat glass bead, which was analyzed using a PANalytical PW2424 X-ray fluorescence spectrometer (XRF) from the Netherlands. A separate portion of the sample was heated in a muffle furnace at 1000 °C for a defined duration, cooled, and weighed. The mass difference before and after heating was recorded as the loss on ignition (LOI). This method complies with Chinese national standards, and the RSD for substance determination is less than 5%.

Trace and rare earth elements (REE)

Trace and rare earth element analyses were performed at the State Key Laboratory of Ore Deposit Geochemistry, Institute of Geochemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The sample preparation procedure included grinding the samples to below 200 mesh and ensuring thorough homogenization. Then, 50 mg of the powdered sample was placed in a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) crucible, followed by the addition of an HF–HNO₃ acid mixture for digestion under heating. After digestion, the sample underwent drying, volumetric dilution, and redissolution steps, ultimately being prepared as a solution suitable for analysis. The concentrations of trace elements were determined using a German-made Plasma Quant-MS Elite inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS). The accuracy for each element analyzed in national reference materials using the respective methods is better than 5%.

Mineralogy

The X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) experiments were conducted at the Science Guide Laboratory using a Rigaku D/MAX-2600 X-ray diffractometer. The experimental procedure involved grinding the samples to 200–300 mesh, evenly spreading and compacting them onto the sample holder (ensuring alignment with the groove edges), and placing it into the instrument. The test parameters were set as follows: Cu target radiation, voltage 40 kV, current 40 mA, scanning range 5°–90°, step size 0.02°, and scanning time 5 min. Mineral components were ultimately identified by comparing the results with standard cards from the International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD) powder diffraction database.

The Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) experiments were carried out at the Lunar and Planetary Science Research Center, Institute of Geochemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences. A dual-beam scanning electron microscope (DualBeam SEM) integrated with focused ion beam (FIB) processing and deposition capabilities was used, combining scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM), energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) techniques to comprehensively characterize the chemical composition, crystal structure, and microscopic morphology of the samples at the nanoscale.

Characteristics of potassium-bearing minerals

SEM and EDS analyses indicate that potassium-bearing minerals in the shale include K-feldspar, illite and illite-smectite mixed -layer minerals (Fig. 4a–f).

(Py: Pyrite; Qtz: Quartz; Cal: Calcite; Kfs: K-feldspar; Ill: Illite; I/S: Illite-Smectite mixed-layer mineral; Ap: Apatite; Chl: Chlorite).

K-feldspar is widely distributed within the shale, primarily occurring as subangular to subrounded particles (grain size: 5–57 μm), with occasional elongated forms (grain size: 7–43 μm) (Fig. 4a–b). The average grain size predominantly ranges around 10 μm, exhibiting poor sorting and low roundness. Microscopic morphology reveals the development of dissolution micropores on particle surfaces. It commonly occurs as irregular grains intermixed with quartz, illite, and chlorite particles. The EDS results indicate that the K content in K-feldspar from the study area is relatively high (BT1: 10.0%–28.8%; BT2: 10.0%–16.6%) compared to the theoretical K content of standard K-feldspar (14.02%).

Illite morphology is primarily categorized into two types (Fig. 4c–d): one occurs as flakes, detrital plates, or scales dispersed within the shale, with generally incompletely developed crystal forms; the other appears as elongated columns or needles, exhibiting relatively well-developed crystals. Some flaky illite particles show bending or fracturing. EDS results indicate that the K content in illite exceeds the typical theoretical range for illite (4.98%–7.47%).

The illite-smectite mixed-layer mineral primarily exhibits rhombic or elongated shapes, with grain sizes ranging from 6 to 53 μm (average 22 μm), and is mainly distributed between mineral grains such as feldspar, smectite, and illite (Fig. 4e–f).

Discussion

Provenance

Geochemical indices such as Al₂O₃/TiO₂, TiO₂/Zr, and Th/Sc serve as effective indicators for distinguishing different types of source rocks34, 35, 36, 37. Generally, the Al₂O₃/TiO₂ ratio is typically less than 8 in mafic source rocks, ranges from 8 to 21 in intermediate source rocks, and falls between 21 and 70 in felsic source rocks35; The TiO₂/Zr ratio generally exceeds 200 in mafic source rocks, ranges between 55 and 200 in intermediate source rocks, and is typically less than 55 in felsic source rocks35; The Th/Sc ratio is generally < 0.6 in mafic source rocks, ranges from 0.6 to 1 in intermediate source rocks, and is typically > 1 in felsic source rocks. A value approaching 0.6 suggests a significant contribution from mafic components37. The shale samples from section BT1 exhibit Al₂O₃/TiO₂ ratios ranging from 24.12 to 30.9 (avg. 26.54), TiO₂/Zr ratios from 45.17 to 62.00 (avg. 55), and Th/Sc ratios from 1.03 to 1.19 (avg. 1.10). For section BT2, the Al₂O₃/TiO₂ ratios range from 24.01 to 31.93 (avg. 26.49), TiO₂/Zr ratios from 48.95 to 59.05 (avg. 52), and Th/Sc ratios from 0.91 to 1.23 (avg. 1.13). Based on the Al₂O₃/TiO₂ ratios, all samples fall within the range of 21–70, indicating a felsic provenance. The TiO₂/Zr ratios are mostly below 55, although a small number of samples slightly exceed this value, suggesting a mixed provenance of felsic and intermediate igneous rocks, yet dominated by felsic sources. Regarding the Th/Sc ratios, all samples except BT2-5 (0.91) are greater than 1, implying a felsic provenance with some input from intermediate igneous rocks. Considering the characteristics revealed by these three geochemical indices, it can be concluded that the provenance of the potassium-bearing shales is a mixture dominated by felsic materials with intermediate igneous components.

Th and La are both large-ion lithophile elements (LILE) with similar geochemical behavior. However, Th is less mobile and more stable than La33. The La/Th ratio varies during magmatic differentiation. For instance, highly fractionated felsic magmas typically exhibit low and stable La/Th ratios (generally around 3.5)38. In the crust, Hf is primarily hosted in zircon. Zircon is extremely stable and resistant to weathering. In sediments derived from young volcanic arcs, where the source rocks have not undergone extensive weathering and erosion, labile minerals remain abundant, while the content of stable zircon is relatively low, resulting in generally low Hf concentrations (~ 3–7 ppm)38. Therefore, the La/Th–Hf diagram provides valuable insights for provenance tracing38. In the La/Th–Hf diagram (Fig. 6a), the BT1 and BT2 shales are mainly distributed within the “felsic provenance and felsic/basic mixed provenance” fields. Considering that most data points fall within the felsic provenance field, with some plotting near the boundary between the felsic and felsic/basic mixed provenance fields, and that the La/Th ratios cluster around 2.5, we interpret the provenance as a mixture of felsic to basic sources, dominated by felsic components.

(a) Covariation diagram for discriminating the provenance types of potassium-bearing shales (after38); (b) MoEF–UEF relationship diagram (after39).

In summary, integrating the results from the Al₂O₃/TiO₂, TiO₂/Zr, and Th/Sc ratios, as well as the La/Th–Hf diagram, we conclude that the provenance of the potassium-bearing shales in the study area is predominantly felsic, with admixtures of intermediate and even basic components. The characteristics of the exposed basement rocks in the northwestern part of the study area provide favorable support for the inference. These basement rocks belong to the Neoproterozoic Qingbaikouan Fanjingshan Group, which is extensively distributed in the Fanjingshan area. The group represents a typical back-arc basin ophiolite-arc volcanic-sedimentary assemblage, whose lithologies include the spilite-quartz keratophyre suite, layered mafic-ultramafic intrusions, and Wulingian (approximately 835 Ma) muscovite-bearing S-type granites32.

Depositional environment

The Ceanom value, Th/U ratio, and MoEF–UEF discriminatory diagrams can be used to trace the depositional environment of sedimentary rocks39–42. Wright et al40. proposed the Ceanom value (Ceanom=lg[3CePAAS/(2×LaPAAS+NdPAAS)]) for analyzing the depositional environment of rocks, where Ceanom > − 0.1 indicates an suboxic-anoxic environment, and Ceanom < − 0.1 suggests an oxic environment. The calculated Ceₐₙₒₘ values of the potassium-rich shales from sections BT1 and BT2, which mostly range from − 0.1 to 0 (BT1: −0.05 to −0.01, avg. −0.03; BT2: −0.11 to 0.00, avg. −0.05), indicate that the sedimentary water body was generally in a suboxic to anoxic environment. Notably, the Ceanom values of two samples from the BT2 section (BT2-4: −0.10; BT2-6: −0.11) are close to the oxic/suboxic-anoxic boundary (Ceanom = −0.1), suggesting that temporary fluctuations (e.g. short-lived oxidation events) may have occurred in the redox conditions.

Under strongly reducing conditions, uranium (U) is immobile and tends to become enriched in sediments (or sedimentary rocks), while thorium (Th) is not influenced by aqueous redox conditions. Therefore, the Th/U ratio serves as a useful proxy for discriminating sedimentary redox conditions. When the Th/U is < 2, it suggests an anoxic environment; when it falls between 2 and 7, it indicates a suboxic environment; and when it exceeds 7, it implies an oxic environment41. The Th/U ratios of the potassium-bearing shale samples mostly range from 2 to 7 (BT1: 1.10–5.73, avg. 3.37; BT2: 1.06–5.11, avg. 3.06), indicating a suboxic environment. Five samples (BT1-3, BT1-5, BT2-1, BT2-3, BT2-5) have Th/U < 2, pointing to intermittent anoxic episodes. This is consistent with the suboxic to anoxic depositional environment indicated by Ceanom.

The UEF–MoEF covariation diagram (Fig. 6b) can also be used to discriminate the redox conditions of the depositional water body. Furthermore, the differences in their enrichment levels in sediments allow for assessing the degree of confinement and restriction of the depositional environment39. In Fig. 6b, most shale samples from the Aoxi Formation sections BT1 and BT2 plot within or near the suboxic zone and the suboxic-anoxic transition zone, with a minority falling within the particulate shuttle zone. The MoEF/UEF ratios range from 0.3 to 3 times the seawater value (×SW). This distribution suggests deposition occurred in a restricted, suboxic to anoxic environment.

Different indicators may vary in their sensitivity. For example, in the BT2 section, certain individual samples show Ceanom values approaching the oxic threshold (−0.1), while their Th/U ratios do not simultaneously indicate oxic conditions. This subtle discrepancy may stem from the differing response times of various elements to changes in redox conditions. Ce is more sensitive to redox variations and better captures short-term, transient fluctuations43, 44, whereas the Th/U ratio is more influenced by the behavior of U near the sediment-water interface. Its enrichment requires a longer period of reducing diagenetic conditions, likely reflecting an average or sustained state. Consequently, brief oxidative events may be recorded by the Ceanom but are insufficient to significantly alter the Th/U ratio.

The average values and ranges of the indicators from the BT1 and BT2 sections are highly similar, indicating that their depositional environments were generally consistent. However, the Ceanom values from the BT2 section exhibit slightly greater fluctuations (with a minimum as low as −0.11), and there is a slightly higher number of samples with Th/U ratios below 2. This may suggest that the redox interface at the location of the BT2 section experienced more frequent fluctuations.

We argue that the evidence collectively provided by Ceanom values, Th/U ratios, and MoEF-UEF indicates that the potassium-bearing shales of the Aoxi Formation were deposited in a restricted, suboxic to anoxic-dominated environment. The observed data fluctuations most likely reflect dynamic variations in redox conditions, rather than a stable and homogeneous anoxic environment.

Paleoweathering

The Chemical Index of Alteration (CIA = [Al₂O₃/(Al₂O₃ + CaO* + Na₂O + K₂O)] × 100) can be used to constrain paleoweathering processes45. Here CaO* represents CaO in silicate minerals (CaO* = CaO - (P₂O₅ × 10/3)). If CaO* < Na₂O, then CaO* = CaO and if CaO* > Na₂O, then CaO* = Na₂O46. This index classifies weathering intensity into a three-tier classification: 50–60 (weak), 60–80 (moderate), and 80–100 (strong). The CIA values of potassium-bearing shales in Tongren range from 58.33 to 65.46 (avg. 62.78) for section BT1 and from 64.60 to 70.75 (avg. 67.88) for section BT2, collectively indicating moderate chemical weathering intensity. It should be specifically noted that potassium metasomatism during diagenesis may lead to anomalously high K content, resulting in systematically reduced CIA values.

The A-CN-K triangular diagram is widely used to evaluate rock weathering45 and potassium metasomatism47. In the A-CN-K triangular diagram, the weathering trend during the initial stage runs approximately parallel to the A–CN join, indicating the absence of potassium metasomatism. During the intermediate stage, the trend becomes nearly parallel to the A–K join, which is indicative of potassium metasomatism. In the final stage, the weathered residues are predominantly clustered near the A apex. Figure 7 shows that the potassium-bearing shale samples experienced moderate weathering intensity. Their weathering trend is subparallel to the A–K join, indicating the occurrence of potassium metasomatism. In such cases, the application of the CIAcorr (Corrected Chemical Index of Alteration) enables more accurate evaluation of weathering intensity by correcting for the effects of potassium metasomatism45. Occurrence state

Analysis of the correlation between K₂O and Al₂O₃ as well as SiO₂ reveals a significant positive relationship (Fig. 8), indicating that potassium exists in the form of aluminosilicate minerals (e.g., K-feldspar and illite). Further SEM observations confirmed notable potassium enrichment within K-feldspar (Fig. 9). Combined with the previously published leaching experiments on soluble potassium from similar shales in the same study area, conducted by Li et al.48, these results indicate that the deposit belongs to the insoluble potash resource type, with potassium likely being structurally bound in mineral lattices. It is noteworthy that although X-ray powder diffraction results indicate that the content of illite is higher than that of K-feldspar in both sections, the potassium content in K-feldspar is significantly higher than that in illite. Therefore, we suggest that potassium in the Aoxi Formation shales in Tongren primarily occurs in K-feldspar, followed by illite and minor illite-smectite mixed-layer minerals.

Comparison between sections BT1 and BT2

Geochemically, the BT1 section exhibits significantly higher average contents of SiO₂ (55.51%), Al₂O₃ (15.33%), and K₂O (8.63%) compared to the BT2 section (averages: 52.56%, 14.34%, and 6.20%, respectively). Mineralogically, the contents of K‑feldspar (6.5%–19.9%) and quartz (29.9%–46.9%) in the three representative samples from the BT1 section are all higher than those in the corresponding samples from BT2 (K‑feldspar: 3.5%–9.6%; quartz: 16.1%–38.1%). In addition, pyrite is consistently present in all three representative samples of BT1, whereas it is absent in BT2 (Table 1).

K-feldspar is the primary potassium-bearing mineral in the study area, and its abundance controls the whole-rock K₂O level. The higher K-feldspar content in BT1 is the direct mineralogical reason for its elevated K₂O compared to BT2. Further comparison reveals that BT2 has higher CIA and CIAcorr values, and its data points plot closer to the A apex in the A-CN-K diagram (Fig. 7), indicating that its source materials underwent stronger chemical weathering. This intense weathering may have led to a relative reduction in the supply of detrital K-feldspar. The presence of sedimentary pyrite typically indicates an anoxic environment49. The stable occurrence of pyrite in BT1, combined with subtle differences in the MoEF-UEF diagram (Fig. 6b), Ceanom values and Th/U ratio between BT1 and BT2, collectively suggests that BT1 was deposited in a more restricted and anoxic water environment. Therefore, we suggest that relatively weaker chemical weathering provided BT1 with a richer source of K-feldspar, while a more reducing and restricted depositional environment favored the preservation and further enrichment of potassium-bearing minerals. These two factors are the main reasons for the higher potassium (K₂O) enrichment in the BT1 profile compared to BT2.

Potassium enrichment process

Based on previous regional geological studies32, 50, stratigraphic evidence indicates that the basal conglomerate of the overlying Banxi Group (e.g., the Furong Formation) contains granite and diabase gravels derived from the underlying Fanjingshan Group. Meanwhile, the regionally extensive unconformity between the Qingbaikou System and the Nanhua System suggests that the “Xuefeng Movement” caused the area northwest of the Yuping–Tianzhu–Sandu line in Guizhou to uplift into land and undergo erosion. This tectonic event led to the widespread exposure of felsic-rich basement rock series (e.g., the Fanjingshan Group) in the northwestern part of the study area, thereby providing abundant potassium-rich felsic provenance for the subsequent formation of potassium-bearing shales. These potassium-enriched felsic detrital materials were transported to a passive continental margin setting and deposited in a restricted, suboxic to anoxic-dominated environment. During this process, the potassium-rich detritus underwent short-distance transportation and moderate weathering. Under such weathering conditions, mafic and ultramafic components were more susceptible to weathering and dissolution, whereas the potassium-rich felsic components remained relatively stable and were preserved.

Meanwhile, the restricted, suboxic to anoxic-dominated water conditions imply a lack of free oxygen below the sediment-water interface and in the bottom water, strongly inhibiting the activity of aerobic microorganisms. The degradation rate of organic matter slows down, preventing the generation of large amounts of acidic pore fluids due to microbial activity during early diagenesis. In oxygen-rich environments, rapid decomposition of organic matter produces CO₂ and organic acids, leading to acidification of pore water51, 52. Such acidic conditions would aggressively dissolve and destroy potassium-bearing minerals (e.g., K-feldspar) and immature clay minerals, causing potassium ions (K⁺) to be mobilized and lost with migrating pore water53, 54. In contrast, under suboxic to anoxic-dominated water conditions, pore water can maintain a relatively neutral to weakly alkaline state, providing the necessary chemical environment for the stable preservation of potassium-bearing minerals54. The restricted water body exacerbates oxygen deficiency in the bottom water and prevents dissolved substances (including K⁺ derived from terrestrial inputs) in the pore water from being diluted and removed, thereby creating a relatively “closed” microenvironment with elevated potassium concentration within the sediment. Potassium ions released from mineral transformations (such as plagioclase dissolution) are not lost within the closed system. Instead, they alter aluminosilicate minerals through potassium metasomatism, forming authigenic illite.

The A-CN-K diagram indicates that potassium metasomatism occurred during the diagenesis of the shale (Fig. 7). Fedo et al.47 pointed out that potassium metasomatism in sedimentary rocks and paleosols is mainly manifested in two modes: ① transformation of plagioclase into K-feldspar in sandstones (weathering trend shifts from parallel to the A–CN join toward the K apex, with little change in CIA values); ② conversion of aluminous clay minerals (e.g., kaolinite) into illite in sandy/muddy/shale sequences (weathering trend subparallel to the A–K join, indicating input of external potassium sources). In the A-CN-K diagram, samples of the potassium-rich shales from Tongren exhibit a distribution subparallel to the A–K join and are relatively close to the K apex (Fig. 7), which is similar to the second mode proposed by Fedo et al.47. Combined with the depositional environment of these shales, we suggest that potassium metasomatism occurred primarily within a relatively confined diagenetic system. This process relied on pre-existing minerals in the sediment, such as plagioclase and kaolinite. During burial diagenesis, dehydration, dissolution, and mineral transformation (e.g., dissolution of plagioclase and kaolinite → illite conversion) released and redistributed potassium, thereby activating and reallocating potassium. This further facilitated the transformation of clay minerals into potassium-rich illite, ultimately leading to effective potassium enrichment.

In summary, the potassium enrichment mechanism in the potassium-bearing shales of the Aoxi Formation in the Tongren area is not driven by a single factor, but results from the synergistic effects of three key processes: proximal sourcing from acidic igneous rocks, deposition in a restricted suboxic to anoxic water environment, and Potassium metasomatism during diagenesis.

Conclusions

Based on integrated studies of the geological setting, mineralogy, and geochemistry of the Cambrian potassium-bearing shales in Tongren, the following key conclusions are drawn:

The ore-forming materials of the Cambrian Aoxi Formation potassium-bearing shales in Tongren were likely derived primarily from felsic igneous rocks. The main potassium-host minerals are K-feldspar, followed by illite and illite-smectite mixed-layer minerals.

In summary, this study preliminarily proposes a potassium enrichment model that emphasizes the critical synergistic relationship among source materials, sedimentary environments, and diagenetic metasomatism. The analysis of potassium enrichment mechanisms and controlling factors, with the potassium-bearing shales of the Aoxi Formation as a typical case, could provide certain references and case insights for understanding the formation processes of analogous insoluble potash resources in black shale systems worldwide.
