“Pinch-and-Tuck” Arthroscopic Technique for Capsular Laxity in Posterior Shoulder Instability
Authors: Chirag D Sheth, Larry D Field
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractShoulder instability is a common orthopaedic condition that often requires surgical intervention. Anterior instability occurs more commonly, but posterior instability is increasingly recognized and managed as well. Trends toward earlier surgical intervention for instability patients have been recently promoted, and surgical management is continually evolving as more data and new techniques are published. The objective of this article is to describe and show a unique plication technique to address capsular laxity in patients with posterior shoulder instability.

Shoulder instability occurs in approximately 2% of the general population. Of those cases, posterior shoulder instability is less common, accounting for 2% to 10% of all shoulder instability cases.1,2 Posterior shoulder instability is commonly the result of repeated microtrauma, which can lead to attenuation and tearing of the posterior capsulolabral complex.3 When present, it is very important for surgeons to adequately address pathologic capsular laxity during surgical intervention.4

The treatment algorithm for patients with posterior instability is evolving. Nonoperative management is often effective and includes physical therapy, which strengthens the dynamic glenohumeral stabilizers, and activity modifications.5 Operative intervention includes both open and arthroscopic procedures, ranging from soft tissue repair to bony augmentation, depending on a patient’s degree of instability and pathologic changes. In patients with posterior labral pathology, it is important to recognize that capsular laxity is a very common contributing factor and that incorporation of the posterior capsule into a stabilization procedure should be accomplished.4,6,7 The objective of this article is to present an alternative technique for capsular plication that effectively and safely addresses capsular laxity in patients with posterior shoulder instability.

Surgical Technique

The surgical “pinch-and-tuck” technique, described and shown (Video 1), is indicated for patients with posterior instability due to combined labral pathology and pathologic posterior capsular laxity. This procedure can be carried out in either the beach-chair or lateral decubitus positions, but we recommend and perform all arthroscopic posterior instability procedures in the lateral decubitus position due to ease of both visualization and access to posterior joint pathology. It is important to perform an examination under anesthesia before the surgical procedure. Next, the operative extremity is prepped and draped in standard fashion and anatomic landmarks referenced. A standard posterior portal is established, and a 30° arthroscope is introduced into the glenohumeral joint. Anterior and anterosuperior portals are then created using an outside-in technique through the rotator interval. A diagnostic arthroscopy is performed, and any intra-articular pathology is identified. Once the posterior labral pathology is recognized, it is debrided and thoroughly mobilized. The bony glenoid neck is prepared using an arthroscopic shaver and rasp to create an adequate bed for healing of the reapproximated labrum. When capsular plication is to be carried out, we use a rasp to roughen the capsular surface to stimulate a postoperative healing response. Once the site has been prepared, anchors are inserted using standard techniques. We place anchors into the glenoid rim most inferiorly first and then work more superiorly. This is typically done half a centimeter from the most inferior extent of the labral tear, then adding anchors superiorly while making sure to spread the anchors approximately 1 cm apart from one another. Once a suture anchor (SutureTak 3.0 mm anchor; Arthrex) is in place, a suture grasper or suture shuttle can be used to capture the appropriate tissue (Fig 1). We use a disposable, retrograde retriever (IDEAL Suture Grasper; DePuy Mitek). Importantly, we use the retrograde retriever to penetrate the capsular tissue at a location that is inferior to the anchor position on the glenoid to allow for an inferior-to-superior capsular shift when sutures are tied (Fig 2). Typically, this is just inferior to the anchor, as this will allow a capsular shift. Care must be taken not to take too big a bite, as it may result in overtightening. Once a separate and complete pass through the labral tissue has been completed (Fig 3), this shift can be evaluated by moving the retrograde passer to the anchor to approximate the extent of a plication generated. These anchors and retrievers are almost always inserted by us using an accessory posterior instability portal that is established more lateral and inferior to the standard posterior portal. This accessory posterior portal is better positioned and oriented to access the inferior labral and capsular tissue and provides an improved angle for anchor insertion. As shown in Video 1, a dedicated “pinch” of the posterior inferior capsule is performed initially. This is adjacent to the labral tissue identified for repair. After this pinch of the posterior capsule is captured, one should be able to see the tip of the retrograde passer to signify that a complete bite was taken. The retriever is then further advanced to incorporate the posterior inferior labrum separately. Again, the tip of your passer should be visible, signifying 2 complete and separate bites were obtained, thus achieving a “pinch-and-tuck” plication. Once the suture is retrieved and shuttled through, it should be visibly seen passing through both the labrum and capsular tissue (Fig 4). This reduction and repair technique can be done multiple times across the labrum to create an effective repair (Fig 5).

Discussion

This article describes our preferred technique for addressing capsular laxity in patients with posterior instability. Nonoperative treatment should always be part of one’s treatment algorithm when addressing posterior instability pathology, especially in low-demand patients, but recent studies have shown increased indications for surgical intervention8,9 and that younger and more active patients often have unfavorable results after conservative management.10 Many recent clinical studies evaluate the success of arthroscopic posterior stabilization, reporting success rates ranging from 85% to 97%.4,5,10,11

Posterior instability procedures include suture anchor repair, anchorless suture repairs, and capsular plication procedures. Isolated capsular plication has been compared with suture anchor stabilization procedures in numerous studies. Provencher et al.12 showed that capsular plication has a higher failure rate than repair with suture anchors. Capsulolabral reconstruction, which commonly refers to the combination of labral repair and capsular plication, has been studied by Bradley et al.4 and has been shown to be very effective. Bradley et al.4 reported on 200 patients who underwent posterior labral repair with 94% satisfaction, 90% return to sport, and 7% persistent instability. Bradley et al.4 emphasized that 70% of patients also had pathologic posterior capsular laxity and that capsular plication for these patients was very important.

We believe that there are some advantages to our capsular plication technique. (Table 2) A retrograde retriever obviates the need for suture shuttling, and the angled tip allows for great control of the amount or capsule incorporated and the depth of penetration of soft tissue included. This device also allows the surgeon the opportunity to perform a “trial reduction” of capsule by penetrating the capsule and then leveraging the device to translate that capsular tissue superiorly to establish whether the amount and tension of that capsule that is to potentially be advanced is appropriate. Also, use of this curved retrograde passer allows the surgeon to individualize the plication in other ways as well. For example, depending on the degree of capsular laxity and the amount to be plicated, this retrograde device allows the surgeon to pinch the capsule at several points by making multiple passes for a single retrieved suture, as is seen when a thrown rock skims across the surface of water. In addition, because the surgeon continuously visualizes the curved tip of the retrograde passer and its depth of soft tissue is reliably known, they have great confidence that the axillary nerve is protected. Finally, this pinch-and-tuck technique is just as easily and reliably applied to cases in which inferior and anterior capsular plication procedures are to be carried out.

Capsular plication for patients with posterior instability is often an important and valuable supplemental procedure, when indicated.13 No specific capsular plication technique has been shown to be superior, but our goal is to present an easy, reproducible, and effective way to perform an individualized capsular plication that incorporates both the patulous capsule and detached labrum.
