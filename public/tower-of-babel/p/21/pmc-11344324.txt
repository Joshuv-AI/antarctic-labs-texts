Prevalence and associations of chronic kidney disease among antiretroviral therapy-naïve persons living with HIV in Lagos, Nigeria
Authors: Oluwatosin Olaseni Odubela, Nkiruka Nnonyelum Odunukwe, Nasheeta Peer, Adesola Zaidat Musa, Babatunde Lawal Salako, Andre Pascal Kengne
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) affects almost 10% of the global populace including people living with HIV (PLWH). PLWH acquire CKD from both traditional and HIV-specific CKD risk factors. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of CKD and associated factors among antiretroviral therapy (ART) naïve PLWH in Lagos, Nigeria. MethodsThis is a secondary data analysis among adult (≥ 18 years) ART-naïve PLWH enrolled at a large ART clinic in Lagos over 6 years. CKD was defined as estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFR) below 60ml/min/1.73m2 over 3 months. Three estimators [Body surface area corrected Cockcroft Gault (BSA-CG), Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD), Chronic kidney disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI)] were used to determine the burden of CKD with no race correction factor. Age- and sex-standardised prevalence rates were determined. Cohen Kappa and Spearman correlations were used to compare the estimators. Logistic regressions were applied to identify variables associated with prevalent CKD.ResultsAmong 2 772 PLWH, the mean age was 38 years with males older than females (p < 0.001). The majority of participants were females (62.1%), married (54.8%), employed (85.7%), had underweight or normal body mass index (BMI) (62.2%), and were diagnosed with World Health Organization (WHO) clinical stages 1 and 2 (55.5%). The age- and sex-standardised prevalence of CKD ranged from 10.0 – 17.6% with the highest Spearman’s correlation (0.928) observed with MDRD and CKD-EPI equations. Increasing age [AOR (95% CI), equation] was significantly associated with CKD across all equations [1.09 (1.06 – 1.13), BSA-CG; 1.07 (1.05 – 1.10), MDRD; 1.09 (1.06 -1.12), CKD-EPI]. Other variables associated with CKD [AOR (95% CI), equation] were anaemia [2.50 (1.34 – 4.68), BSA-CG; 1.73 (1.04 - 2.86), MDRD], BMI <25 kg/m2 [3.35 (1.55 – 7.26), BSA-CG; 2.02 (1.18 – 3.46), CKD-EPI], and CD4 counts ≤ 200 cells/µL [2.02 (1.06 – 3.87), BSA-CG].ConclusionThere was a high prevalence of CKD among ART-naïve PLWH at enrollment, which highlights the need to evaluate this population for CKD. Aside increasing age and low CD4 counts, none of the traditional or HIV-specific risk factors were related to CKD diagnosis.

Background

The successful rollout of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV infection from a death sentence to a controllable chronic infection [1, 2]. As people living with HIV (PLWH) live longer, they are exposed to increased risks of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) including chronic kidney disease (CKD) [3, 4]. Global prevalence of CKD among PLWH has been estimated to be 2 – 38% [5] with differences noted across the World Health Organization (WHO) regions [6]. CKD prevalence varies according to the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) estimators used to approximate kidney function, as well as the ethnic characteristics of the population being evaluated [7, 8].

Similar to PLWH, CKD is also of concern in general populations where it is estimated that 10% of the global populace (approximately 850 million persons) have the disease [9]. The aetiology of CKD is varied and includes aging, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, infections, vascular diseases, kidney stones, and drugs [10, 11]. Among PLWH, the contributions of HIV-specific factors to the burden of CKD may include HIV infection per se, ART, and co-infection with hepatitis B or C viruses. Considering the varying influence of these factors, more studies are required to delineate the contributions of these risk factors to the burden of CKD in PLWH.

The clinical picture of kidney diseases among PLWH has evolved from HIV-associated nephropathy (HIVAN) to more diverse forms of kidney disease or injury and has contributed to the increasing profile of CKD in this population [5, 6, 10]. The spectrum of kidney disease among PLWH can be broadly classified into four dominant classes; glomerular nephropathies, tubulointerstitial nephropathies, vascular nephropathies, and other nephropathies such as diabetic nephropathy and age-related nephrosclerosis [12, 13]. These diverse forms of kidney disease seen among PLWH could be attributed to the early diagnosis of HIV infection, early commencement of ARVs, ART-related effects, and co-morbidities [12].

With sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) being the epicentre of the HIV epidemic and Nigeria having the second highest number of PLWH, it is critical to characterize the burden of CKD among PLWH in Nigeria [14–17]. In a global review of CKD burden among PLWH, Africa WHO region had the highest prevalence of CKD with the West African region recording the highest prevalence in Africa irrespective of the kidney function estimator used [6]. Wide-ranging prevalence of CKD (12.0 – 53.3%) has been reported in Nigeria using different criteria for defining CKD as well as associated factors noted [18–22]. Some of the notable risk factors include increasing age, low CD4 counts, anaemia, body mass index (BMI) and female gender [18, 20, 23, 24]. Although ART, especially Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate (TDF) contributes to the burden of CKD in PLWH, CKD occurs even before the commencement of ART [25–27]. Thus, it is relevant to explore the burden of CKD and its associated factors among ART-naïve PLWH to establish the inherent burden of CKD in this population.

This study aimed to determine the prevalence and associations of CKD among ART-naïve PLWH enrolled at an HIV clinic in Lagos, Nigeria. Our specific objectives were to determine the crude as well as age- and sex-standardised prevalence of CKD using the three common GFR estimators. Other specific objectives were to determine the concordance among these GFR estimators and determine the associated factors for prevalent CKD among ART-naïve PLWH in Lagos, Nigeria.

Methods

Study setting

The Clinical Sciences Department at the Nigerian Institute of Medical Research (NIMR) has been operating an ART clinic since 2002. The clinic is supported by the US President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) programme and a primary objective was to evaluate the effectiveness of generic ART in the country.

Clinical, treatment, and laboratory parameters are entered into a longitudinal database and each patient enrolled in the clinic is assigned a unique identification number. This is used to collate all information pertaining to patients’ care in the clinic. The clinic operates ambulatory care while PLWH requiring in-patient care are referred to facilities of their choice or closest to their residence.

Study design

This is a secondary data analysis among ART-naïve PLWH enrolled in the clinic over the study period. PLWH enrolled between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2019, were eligible for study inclusion. Those who were younger than 18 years of age, pregnant, accessing care for post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP), or already on ART were excluded from the analysis.

Our study period was limited to 2014 - 2019 to ensure comparability of laboratory parameters. There was a change in the laboratory equipment used in assaying serum creatinine in May 2012. This transition period was to allow the laboratory personnel to learn and adapt to the use of the new equipment to ensure the validity of results.

At the initial visit to the clinic, PLWH received education on the clinic operations and the basics of HIV and medication adherence. Anthropometric and vital signs (blood pressure, weight, height) are measured by the nurses. Study participants in light clothing are directed to stand erect on the weighing scale and stadiometer to measure their weights and heights respectively. Blood pressure measurement is taken with study participants remaining in a seated position (for at least five minutes before the procedure) using a digital blood pressure monitor. Three blood pressure readings are taken and the average is taken as the study participant’s blood pressure for the clinic consultation. Baseline laboratory investigations (full blood count, cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) counts, viral load, serum creatinine, hepatitis B and C screening, and tuberculosis screening (chest x-ray and sputum tests) are performed prior to ART initiation.

Data used for this study was extracted from the clinic database. These include participants’ sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, educational level, occupation, alcohol history, and marital status), weight, height, concurrent co-morbidities at enrollment (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hepatitis B and C, and tuberculosis), and HIV-specific factors (WHO clinical stage, viral load, and CD4 counts). Hypertension was defined as systolic and/or diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP) greater than or equal to 140 mmHg and 90 mmHg, respectively, a prior history of hypertension diagnosis or the use of anti-hypertensive medication [28]. A diagnosis of co-morbid diabetes mellitus was based on self-report or relevant treatment (from clinical history or medical records). Tuberculosis was diagnosed using both clinical history and physical examination [29, 30]. A detailed clinical history of cough, night sweats, weight loss, fever, and recent contact with persons diagnosed with tuberculosis in addition to a physical examination (particularly chest examination) is done. Furthermore, chest X-ray findings, Gene X-pert results along with clinical suspicion are used to arrive at tuberculosis diagnosis for study participants. The HIV-specific factors among study participants were determined from baseline laboratory investigations (viral load and CD4 counts) and clinical evaluation (WHO clinical stage) at enrollment in the clinic.

Serum creatinine was measured using the Roche Cobas C311 Chemistry auto-analyzer. It uses the Jaffe-based creatinine-picrate forming in an alkaline medium. The equipment is maintained per protocol with calibration and quality control conducted once weekly under the supervision of the Quality Control Officer in the laboratory. The coefficient of variation used in estimating creatinine was 3.1% (CV repeatability) and 2.8% (CV intermediate precision). The laboratory at NIMR was issued the South African Accreditation System (SANAS) accreditation following its compliance with industry-wide standards in 2017. This has been reviewed as appropriate with the latest version of SANAS accreditation issued to the laboratory in December 2022.

Definitions

The primary study outcome was CKD prevalence among ART-naïve PLWH at enrollment, defined as a reduction in kidney function (estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) less than 60ml/min/1.73m2) over a 3-month period with associated health implications [31, 32]. Three equations; Body surface area corrected-Cockcroft Gault (BSA-CG), Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD), and Chronic Kidney Disease-Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equations were used to assess renal function. Body surface area (BSA) was calculated using the Dubois equation [33]. The BSA-CG equation was used in the study as it has been shown to correlate better with inulin clearance [34]. The formulae for the three eGFR equations are stated below with serum creatinine (Scr) in mg/dL. eGFRCG = (140 – Age) x Weight ÷ (72 x Scr), multiplied by 0.85 if female [35]. BSA = Weight0.425 x Height0.725 x 0.007184 [33]. eGFRBSA-CG = (eGFRCG x 1.73) x BSA [34]. eGFRMDRD = 175 x (Scr)-1.154 x (Age (years))-0.203 x (0.742 if female) [36]. eGFRCKD-EPI = 141 × min(Scr/κ, 1)α × max(Scr/κ, 1)-1.209 × 0.993Age × 1.018 [if female] [37]. The MDRD and CKD-EPI equations were calculated without the race correction factor [38–40]. The educational level attained by study participants was classified as none, primary (1-6 years), secondary (7-12 years), and tertiary. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared (kg/m2). BMI was classified as underweight (< 18.5), normal (18.5 – 24.9), overweight (25.0 -29.9), and obese (≥ 30.0) [41]. Viral load was classified into two categories (≤ 1000 and ≥ 1001 copies per millimeter) while CD4 count was classified per CDC Staging [42–44].

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as counts and percentages for categorical variables, while mean and standard deviations or median and 25th - 75th percentiles were used for continuous variables. The crude prevalence of CKD was determined from the eGFR using the three equations in the study while age and sex standardised prevalence was computed using the Nigeria HIV/AIDS Indicator and Impact Survey (NAIIS) 2018 report [45]. The NAIIS 2018 report is the latest version of the household survey of HIV infection in the country and a direct standardization method was employed to determine the age and sex standardised prevalence. Cohen kappa and Spearman correlation were used to assess the agreement between eGFR estimates from the three equations, while logistic regression models were used to investigate the attributes associated with prevalent CKD [46]. Potential predictors were tested in univariable models as well as in multivariable models adjusted for age, gender, BMI and year of enrollment in the clinic. Age, gender and BMI are well established CKD risk factors and correlates of most attributes tested in the study, while we assume that both the attribute tested and CKD prevalence in the current study could vary across years of enrolment. General characteristics tested were age, gender, alcohol intake, education, occupation, year of enrollment, BMI, co-morbidities (hypertension, diabetes, tuberculosis, hepatitis B and C), anaemia; while HIV predictive characteristics tested were WHO stage, viral load and CD4 count. Unadjusted and adjusted (AOR) odds ratios and 95% confidence interval (95%CI) are provided as measures of the associations. Statistical significance was set at a p-value of <0.05. Complete cases analyses were conducted. Data were analysed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 (IBM SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL).

Ethical approval

Study participants at enrolment into the ART clinic provided informed consent to document their preference concerning the use of their blood samples and data for research during the course of care offered in the clinic. Their responses however did not have any inclination towards the content and quality of care and treatment received in the clinic. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Nigerian Institute of Medical Research (NIMR) [IRB-21-066]. Additionally, ethical approval was obtained from the Health Research Committee of the University of Cape Town, South Africa [HREC 176/2022].

The median eGFR (ml/min) by estimators in the overall sample was 98.4 (BSA-CG), 85.5 (MDRD), and 73.4 (CKD-EPI), with values higher in women than in men except for the CKD-EPI equation (p < 0.001) (Table 2). The correlation coefficients (from Spearman correlation) between eGFR from different estimators were 0.766 (BSA-CG vs. MDRD), 0.777 (BSA-CG vs. CKD-EPI), and 0.928 (MDRD vs. CKD-EPI) and all p-values were significant (p < 0.001).

There was a relatively high prevalence of CKD in the overall study population with significant differences by gender. The crude prevalence of CKD (95% confidence interval) in the overall sample using the BSA-CG, MDRD, and CKD-EPI equations were 12.0% (10.6 – 13.4), 16.7% (15.3 – 18.1) and 16.5% (15.1 – 17.9), respectively. Equivalent prevalence by gender were 9.3% (7.8 – 10.9), 14.9% (13.2 – 16.6), and 15.7% (13.9 – 17.5) in women, respectively, and 16.3% (13.8 – 18.8), 19.7% (17.2 – 22.2) and 17.8% (15.4 -20.2) in men (p < 0.001 for all gender comparisons). The age and sex-standardised prevalence of CKD was 10.0% (8.6 – 11.4), 17.1% (15.3 – 18.9), and 17.6% (15.7 – 19.5) using the BSA-CG, MDRD, and CKD-EPI equations, respectively. Discussion

The use of different eGFR equations to determine CKD enabled a comparison of the prevalence and associated factors across formulae among ART-naïve PLWH in this study. The prevalence of CKD using the three estimators was found to range from 10.0 – 17.6% indicating a relatively high burden of the disease in this cohort. This is higher than the global burden of CKD in the general population (10%) and supports the epidemiological transition of diseases in Nigeria beyond communicable diseases. The highest age- and sex-standardised (overall) prevalence of CKD was recorded with the CKD-EPI equation while the lowest was found with the BSA-CG equation. Increasing age, BMI (underweight and normal), anaemia, and low CD4 counts (≤ 200) were factors associated with the burden of CKD in our study.

Our study findings (age- and sex- standardised prevalence of CKD) align with recent studies done among ART-naïve PLWH and the general population in Nigeria which had similar CKD prevalence (12.0 – 16.1%) [19, 47, 48]. While these studies utilized the CKD-EPI equation to determine CKD prevalence, a higher CKD prevalence (23.8 – 53.3%) was recorded among PLWH in other Nigerian studies using other CKD criteria (Albuminuria and Serum Creatinine > 132 µmol/L, CG, and MDRD) [18, 21, 24]. Notably, these studies with relatively higher prevalence of CKD were conducted in the early years of HIV in the country when access to ART was limited [18, 21, 24].

The relatively high prevalence of CKD among ART-naïve PLWH potentiates the importance of screening for co-morbidities before the commencement of ART. At our ART clinic, PLWH with creatinine clearance below 50ml/min are deemed to have renal compromise (not CKD) and are offered renal-sparing ART regimens in accordance with the National treatment guidelines [49]. PLWH with creatinine clearance below 50ml/min are also referred to the nephrologist (not within the clinic) for evaluation of the nature and extent of kidney injury. In this manner, PLWH with renal compromise can be managed proactively to delay progression to CKD or end-stage renal disease.

There is a decline in the size, function, and number of nephrons as humans age, resulting in decreased GFR with or without the presence of injury or disease in the kidney [50–52]. The presence of HIV has a cumulative effect on reducing GFR, thus potentiating the burden of CKD among PLWH. Although a significant proportion of those with CKD in our study were aged 24 – 50 years of age, accelerated aging has been postulated to occur among PLWH, attributed to HIV-induced chronic inflammation and persistent immune activation [50, 53, 54]. Increasing age was found to be associated with CKD in our study as demonstrated in other studies among PLWH [18, 20, 55–60]. Our study also showed a statistically significant difference in the ages of those with and without comorbid CKD, thus aligning with increasing age profile and CKD diagnosis.

HIV infection and CKD may independently contribute to the development of anaemia; thus, a combination of these disease conditions could exacerbate the burden of anaemia among PLWH with CKD. Anaemia was found to be associated with CKD amongst ART-naïve PLWH in our study, in concordance with other studies [20, 55, 58, 61]. PLWH with low CD4 counts, high viral loads, and advanced clinical stage of the HIV infection were noted to have increased odds of anaemia in the literature [62].

Underweight or normal BMI in this study was associated with CKD and this finding was similar to another study among ART-naive PLWH in Nigeria [23]. However, these findings contrast with another Nigerian study (general population) that has reported increased CKD prevalence with increasing BMI categories (overweight/obese) [48]. In the general population, patients with CKD tend to correlate with higher BMI categories due to the effects of adiposity on the renin-angiotensin system [63]. Recent systematic reviews in the general population have buttressed the association between obesity and CKD [64, 65]. Furthermore, several studies worldwide have demonstrated no association between BMI and CKD among PLWH irrespective of their ART status [18, 19, 21, 22, 57, 61, 66, 67]. This discordant picture requires further evaluation to elucidate the influence of adiposity on CKD, particularly in ART-naive PLWH. The preponderance of BMI ≤ 25kg/m2 (underweight or normal) among our study population is not a misnomer as this is the usual picture of ART-naïve PLWH at presentation to the ART clinic [68].

Hypertension and diabetes mellitus, known traditional risk factors for CKD were not associated with prevalent CKD in our study. This is in contrast with previous studies conducted among PLWH [69–72]. Although both co-morbidities were correlated with CKD in univariable analysis, this was not sustained in the multivariable analysis, similar to studies done in China among PLWH [61, 66]. In our study, the burden of study participants with diabetes mellitus could not be established due to the inability to conduct a fasting blood sugar profile attributed to the timing of their presentation at the laboratory for blood work. Further evaluation of HIV-specific factors that may modulate the influence of these traditional risk factors in the prevalence of CKD in the cohort is required.

CD4 count was the only HIV-specific factor associated with increased odds of CKD in ART-naïve PLWH. However, this association was only present with the BSA-CG equation. Other studies conducted (in China, USA, and Nigeria) amongst PLWH have also shown that low CD4 counts were associated with increased CKD burden [19, 21, 22, 59, 66, 67]. The majority of studies used the MDRD equation with the exception being Onodugo et al. (CG) and Ekrikpo, Akpan, et al (CKD-EPI), both studies conducted among ART-naïve PLWH in Nigeria. A study conducted among ART-naïve PLWH in Bayelsa State, Nigeria using the CKD-EPI equation did not show any association between HIV-specific factors and CKD prevalence at enrollment into the ART clinic [20].

The inherent additional costs of managing CKD together with HIV infection can be prevented if concerted efforts are channelled towards early detection or screening for this co-morbidity at enrolment. This is evidenced by the associated improvement in quality of life in the study evaluating the cost-effectiveness of screening for CKD using risk scores to determine the most at-risk population [73]. This was a simulation model study using data collected among the general populace in the United States of America.

Furthermore, the risk of cardiovascular disease is higher in the presence of CKD, thus increasing the likelihood of morbidity and mortality among PLWH with this co-morbidity [74]. It is therefore imperative to actively screen for co-morbidities at enrolment of PLWH in clinic settings.

Strengths and limitations

This is one of the largest cross-sectional studies to determine the prevalence of CKD among ART-naïve PLWH in Nigeria and West Africa. This study also provides age- and sex-standardised prevalence of CKD among PLWH in the country to enable ease of comparison across settings. Our study utilized three common eGFR equations to provide a balanced review of the CKD burden in the cohort. Although the study is a single-centre cross-sectional study, our findings are likely representative of the study population in the country as the study site is one of the foremost ART clinics in Nigeria. Our study is limited by the absence of baseline weights and heights in some study participants at enrollment to the clinic. This was attributed to their advanced clinical stage of HIV disease at presentation as they could not maintain an erect posture for such measurements to be taken. In addition, the blood sugar and lipid profile (in their fasting states) of study participants were not included. As a result, the true picture of the burden of hyperglycaemia and dyslipidaemia could not be captured. Furthermore, tobacco consumption was not routinely collected in the case record forms when enrolling study participants into the ART clinic, thereby limiting the exploration of these key associations with CKD. Finally, due to the cross-sectional study design, causality between CKD and PLWH could not be established. Furthermore, the absence of repeat serum creatinine assessment 3 months after enrollment to rule out acute kidney injury was not done due to the study design. However, it is hoped that a cohort study may provide a better view of the associations.

Conclusion

There is a relatively high burden of CKD among ART-naïve PLWH in our study with increasing age, BMI (< 25 kg/m2), low CD4 counts, and anaemia being associated with CKD diagnosis in this population (outcomes differed with estimators). None of the other traditional risk factors (gender, hypertension, diabetes mellitus) were found to be associated with CKD prevalence. Screening for CKD co-morbidity should be incorporated into baseline testing for ART-naïve PLWH when enrolling for care.
