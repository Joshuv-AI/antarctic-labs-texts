Process development for efficient xylitol recovery with ultrasound-assisted hybrid crystallization from fermented rice straw hydrolysate
Authors: Saumya Singh, Shailendra Kumar Arya, Meena Krishania
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Xylitol is a five-carbon sugar alcohol which finds use in the pharmaceutical, food, cosmetic and odontological industries. Xylitol is a valuable platform chemical and also a perfect phase change material for latent heat storage systems, which can integrate wasted heat or renewable energy sources while lowering CO2 emissions [1]. The xylitol market is expected to reach a valuation of $1 billion USD at a compound annual growth rate of 5.8% between 2021 and 2026. With an annual global market size of 2 lakh tonnes, xylitol is one of the top 12 value-added compounds produced from lignocellulose biomass, according to the US Department of Energy [2]. Commercial xylitol production is an energy and cost intensive process that requires catalytic hydrogenation of pure D-xylose at high pressure and temperature. The Raney-nickel catalyst used in this process is carcinogenic and has been recognized as hazardous material [3]. Therefore, the biotechnological method not only makes it possible to manufacture xylitol at a lower cost, but also allows for the sustainable utilization of extra rice straw (RS). Presently, surplus RS is either burnt or disposed of because there is no sustainable way to use it. Alternatively, xylitol can be produced by the bioconversion of xylose present in the rice straw hydrolysate (RSH) with the help of microbes, particularly yeasts such as C. tropicalis, M. guilliermondii, P. stipites etc. [4], [5]. However, post fermentation, isolation, and purification of xylitol from the fermented RSH (FRSH) is a major challenge and the process has not yet been fully developed. This is because, in addition to xylitol, fermented broth may contain inorganic minerals along with leftover pentoses (arabinose, residual xylose) and polyols (arabitol, glycerol) as a result of parallel metabolic pathways and incomplete conversion. These structurally related molecules' similar physicochemical characteristics make downstream purification more difficult [6], [7]. Since existing methods only recover a limited yield (<74%), the downstream costs are higher as compared to production, underscoring the need for more effective purification and crystallization processes. However, to maximize output, purity, and the overall feasibility of bioprocessing of xylitol, effective crystallization techniques are crucial [8]. Other factors that affect crystallization are macromolecule (xylitol) concentration, supersaturation, the oxidative and reductive conditions, the crystallization solvent, ionic strength, pH, and non-macromolecular impurities like salt and metal ions, among others [9]. The processes of nucleation, crystal growth, and agglomeration are the three main steps involved in xylitol crystallization [10]. The solubility and supersaturation behavior of xylitol are the main factors that control its crystallization. The relationship between xylitol concentration, supersaturation, nucleation, and crystal development under various crystallization circumstances can be understood with the use of a conceptual phase diagram (Supplementary Fig. S1) [11], [12]. The metastable zone width is essential for controlling crystal growth and enhancing control over the crystallization process, while supersaturation is the primary force behind crystallization since it encourages nucleation and crystal growth [13]. In recent times, a mild and dependable ultrasound-assisted crystallization method or sonocrystallization has been extensively studied as a possible replacement for conventional crystallization techniques [14]. Sonocrystallization can yield superior quality crystals, much more quickly than traditional crystallization, with a narrower CSD, improved crystal purity and process consistency. For instance, high crystallinity improves xylitol's stability, solubility, and purity, benefiting food, pharmaceuticals, and biopolymer applications by improving sensory attributes, reducing downstream purification, and ensuring process scalability. From an industrial perspective, sonocrystallization offers several benefits such as reduced crystallization induction time, improved yield, controlled nucleation, minimal impurities and improved physical attributes of the final product, leading to cost-effective downstream processing [15], [16]. High crystallinity improves xylitol's stability, solubility, and purity, benefiting food, pharmaceuticals, and biopolymer applications by improving sensory attributes, reducing downstream purification, and ensuring process scalability. Currently, the viability of ultrasonic crystallization technology in industry has been established due to the widespread availability of ultrasonic equipment [17]. The cavitation bubbles created in the medium, function as seeds in the suspension. Thereby, promoting encouraging the formation of crystals by increased local mixing, greater mass and energy transfer, and increased diffusion brought on by quickly shifting concentration gradients in the medium [18], [19]. Ultrasound enhances nucleation through acoustic cavitation, where the creation and collapse of microbubbles produce localized high temperature (5000 °C) and pressure (2000 atm) inside bubble, while rapid cooling of the adjacent liquid occurs, causing nucleation and shortening of induction time [20]. Furthermore, ultrasonication enhances crystal formation by promoting micro mixing and mass transfer, which makes it easier for solute molecules to reach the crystal surface and lower the concentration boundary layers surrounding developing crystals [12]. Several operational factors, such as ultrasound frequency, power, amplitude, temperature, pressure, and process time, have an impact on sonocrystallization and other ultrasound-induced processes [21]. The product's morphology, polymorphic forms, purity, crystallinity, and particle size distribution can all be changed via process optimization [22]. Power ultrasound may be applied to a broad range of industrial processes (including emulsification, microbial fermentation, cell disintegration, and homogenizing), due to availability of large-scale equipment. It has also gained popularity as a crystallization aid in the pharmaceutical and fine chemicals industries [16], [23]. Ultrasound assisted hybrid crystallization presents potential scalability advantages for large-scale xylitol production. While ultrasound can shorten the crystallization period and enhance crystal properties, too much acoustic energy might raise operating expenses and encourage crystal attrition. However, some studies on ultrasound in industrial crystallization have reported up to 30% energy savings and faster processing times [24]. For an efficient process, acoustic power, sonication mode (probe-based, flow-through ultrasonic reactors and bath systems) and residence time are important parameters [25]. In the present study, ultrasound assisted crystallization improved xylitol morphology, crystal quality and yield under mild operating conditions, indicating that this approach could be used in integration with conventional crystallization method as a process intensification strategy. While a lot of research has been done to improve the production of xylitol through the microbial conversion of lignocellulosic hydrolysates, relatively little has been done to recover xylitol downstream, especially from chemically complex fermentation broths where residual sugars, polyols, salts, and other impurities can have a significant impact on the crystallization behavior and product quality. Additionally, there is still little data on xylitol crystallization from actual fermentation media, while the majority of prior crystallization investigations were carried out using purified xylitol solutions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the use of ultrasound-assisted crystallization for the recovery of the sugar alcohol (xylitol) from fermentation derived supernatant. Moreover, the application of sonocrystallization to chemically complex fermented broth, including RSH fermented by Meyerozyma caribbica CP02 and commercial xylose-based fermentation media, having impurities and co-metabolites offer unique crystallization opportunities. The study also analyses crucial crystallization parameters that control particle size distribution, crystallinity, and crystal morphology of xylitol. This work adds to the development of economically feasible biorefinery-based xylitol biomanufacturing techniques by combining ultrasound-assisted crystallization with lignocellulosic bioprocessing, offering fresh insights into process intensification for xylitol purification.

Material and methods

RS pretreatment and media preparation

RS was purchased from nearby farms in Mohali area of Punjab, India. As mentioned in our previous study [26], [27], RS was hydrolysed with dilute acid catalysed steam pretreatment using H2SO4 (1.5% v/v) for 30 min, in accordance with the process optimized previously. The insoluble salt BaSO4 was separated by centrifuging the acidic RSH (pH 0.89) for 25 min after it had been brought to pH 3.5 with Ba(OH)2. The RSH was treated with activated carbon (AC) (2.0% w/v) at 60 °C for 1 h, followed by vacuum filtration through a 0.45 µm cellulose acetate membrane filter. The filtrate was concentrated in a rotary evaporator till a final xylose concentration of approximately 60 gL−1 (Rotavapor RV.10, IKA Staufen, Germany). The treated RSH was supplemented with (gL−1): yeast extract (7.5), MgSO4·7H2O (1.0), CaCl2 (0.5), KH2PO4 (0.5) and sterilized at 110 °C for 15 min and 0.1% (v/v) polypropylene glycol was used as an anti-foaming agent. As mentioned in our previous study, for inoculum preparation, Meyerozyma caribbica CP02 cells were grown in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks filled with medium (50 mL) constituting of (gL−1): xylose (30), yeast extract (7.5), MgSO4·2H2O (1.0), CaCl2·7H2O (0.5), KH2PO4 (0.5) and incubated for 18 h under agitation of 200 rpm at 32 °C.

Microorganism and culture condition

To facilitate crystallization study, a 3 L benchtop bioreactor (Bio-Spin series, Bio-Age Equipment and services, Mohali, India) with a 2.5 L working capacity was used to produce xylitol in a batch process. The fermentation of synthetic media containing 60 gL−1 of commercial xylose was also conducted under the conditions outlined in the preceding section. A temperature sensor, dissolved oxygen detecting probe, and pH probe (Broadley James, USA) were all installed in the fermenter. The airflow was maintained at 1.2 Lmin−1 with 200 rpm agitation for the first 24 h, and then decreased to 0.6 Lmin−1 and 150 rpm for the remaining duration [26].

Post fermentation treatment of RSH

The fermented broth was centrifuged (Eppendorf centrifuge 5810R, Hamburg, Germany) at 4000 rpm for 15 min to remove the cell biomass. The fermented broths were treated with AC (3% w/v) for 60 min at 40 °C and then passed through Whatman filter paper (Grade 41) and analyzed, for determination of the concentrations of xylitol produced and xylose consumed [28]. The purified solution was submitted to vacuum concentration to increase the xylitol content until supersaturation. The concentration was carried out in a rotary evaporator at 55 ± 5 °C and 0.5 atm pressure maintained by a vacuum pump.

Crystallization

Initial crystallization tests were carried out with xylitol rich liquor obtained after detoxification and purification of fermented supernatant of commercial xylose-based media (CFXM). For xylitol crystallization, the method proposed by Zhang et al. [29] was adopted with some modifications [29]. Shortly, after setting the concentration of the purified CFXM to a desired supersaturation level of ∼500 gL−1 xylitol, the syrup was transferred to a flat bottom glass crucible followed by the addition of seed crystals. Unless otherwise stated, all experiments were performed using finely ground commercial xylitol crystals (Sigma Aldrich) at a fixed seed loading of 0.1% w/v [30]. Crystallization, was initiated at 4 °C and after attainment of stable xylitol concentration in the crystalizing syrup, the process was terminated and formed crystals were extracted and placed on Whatman filter paper at room temperature. Whenever necessary, the crystals were washed with hexane to smoothen the seed surface and remove excess syrup stuck to the crystals.

Process optimization using CFXM

The effect of different operational parameters governing crystallization was determined by studying single factor at a time approach. The crystallization parameters studied were xylitol supersaturation concentration (500, 650, and 800 gL−1), temperature (−20, 0, and 20 °C), seed size (0.5, 0.8, and 1 mm) and seed quantity (0.05, 0.2, 0.4, and 0.8% w/v). Supersaturation, temperature, seed size, and seed quantity are important crystallization parameters that control nucleation and crystal development. For preparing seeds, the commercial xylitol crystals were milled and passed through the sieves of Kinematica™ Stainless Steel Sieve for Polymix™ MFC 90 D Mill, to screen different sizes of xylitol crystals. Crystallization was initially performed at 4 °C for 3–4 days under the conditions described above.

Strategy implemented for efficient xylitol crystallization

Effect of sonication on xylitol recovery

For ultrasound-assisted hybrid crystallization study, 250 mL (6.7 cm diameter and 4 cm height) flat bottom glass crucibles were filled with 5 mL purified and concentrated CFXM syrup having final xylitol concentration of 650 ± 13.18 gL−1. After pouring, the syrup was allowed to cool down to room temperature and the glass crucibles were half submerged in water in the ultrasonic bath type sonicator (Model: 08895-15, Cole-Parmer, Illinois, USA; 9 L capacity, 40 kHz frequency, 200 W ultrasonic power and 300 W heating power). Ultrasound was introduced intermittently (soft pulses spaced at 5 s) at 30 °C. The effect of ultrasound duration was studied for 10–25 min at an interval of 5 min. Subsequently, xylitol crystals (0.2% w/v) suspended in butanol, and sieved through a 0.8 mm mesh were added to the supersaturated syrup, to ensure uniform dispersion and homogeneous nucleation [31], [32]. The effects of sonication on crystal size distribution, crystal morphology, and yield were systematically analysed [33]. The glass crucible containing non-sonicated CFXM was taken as control. To differentiate ultrasound specific effects from those resulting solely from improved mixing, an additional silent control experiment was conducted under identical crystallization conditions using low speed mechanical stirring in the absence of ultrasound.

Effect of CFXM-FRSH mixing

The crystallization parameters were initially optimized using, CFXM. To comparatively analyze the crystallization behavior of CFXM and fermented RSH media (FRSH), the optimized conditions of crystallization were subsequently applied to FRSH. Yeast cells were removed from the FRSH by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The cell free supernatant (xylitol rich) thus obtained was detoxified using AC treatment (3% w/v) for 60 min at 40 °C followed by filtering it through Whatman filter paper grade 1 to remove the AC precipitate. The decolorized FRSH was turned into a syrup by vacuum concentration for xylitol supersaturation. Further, to improve the crystallization yield and crystal quality, the xylitol rich FRSH was mixed with CFXM liquor (initial xylitol concentration adjusted with distilled water). The mixing of CFXM to FRSH was carried out in different ratios of 10:90, 30:70 and 50:50. The FRSH syrup devoid of CFXM addition was taken as control. All the fractions were concentrated to final xylitol concentration of ∼650 gL−1 and crystallization was then carried out at optimized conditions. Comparative analysis of crystallization parameters of CFXM and FRSH syrup was also done.

5 L batch fermentation and mass balance of xylitol production

Fermentation of 3.5 L detoxified RSH (xylose concentration adjusted to 60 gL−1), was conducted in a 5 L fermenter (IKA India Private Limited, Karnataka, India). The fermenter was equipped with a pH detecting probe and a sterilizable oxygen sensor. Temperature control (32 °C) was achieved by glass double jacket through circulator and chiller (IKA HRC 2). Agitation (250/200 rpm) was accomplished by two Rushton 6-blade impellers. After attainment of optimal incubation temperature, 18 h old inoculum culture (3% v/v) was added with the help of a peristaltic pump. The rate of agitation was initially set to 250 rpm and that of aeration to 0.8 vvm and then changed to 200 rpm and 0.4 vvm after 34 h, for remaining duration (72 h). pH was measured but not regulated during fermentation. Sample aliquots were collected every 24 h throughout the process for cell biomass quantification, xylose utilization, and xylitol production. The process simulation flowchart was prepared by SuperPro Designer v14 software (Evaluation Ed.) (Supplementary File, Fig. S2).

Analytical methods

The quantification of extracellular metabolites (xylose, glucose, arabinose, and xylitol) was done by High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) (Agilent, HiPlex, Santa Clara, California, USA) supplied with a Hi-Plex H column (300 × 7.7 mm, Agilent) at 60 °C, coupled to a RID detector at 55 °C. At 0.6 mL/min, H2SO4 (5 mM) was employed as the mobile phase. Various analytical techniques were used for the qualitative analysis of xylitol crystals [1], [34].

For Scanning electron microscope (JEOL-6000, Tokyo, Japan) or SEM analysis, xylitol crystals were placed on metal stubs using double-adhesive carbon tape to examine their surface shape and microstructure. Crystals were sputtered with gold under vacuum at 5–10 mA and 1.0 kV in an argon environment before being seen.

Powder X-Ray Diffraction (XPRD) (Rigaku, Smart Lab SE, Japan) was used to determine the phase composition (crystallinity), purity, and structural examination of the produced crystals. The xylitol crystals were crushed into a fine powder, placed in a sample container, and scanned within the 2θ (5° to 70°) diffraction angle range. The diffractograms were conducted using 40 kV of voltage and 40 mA of current, and a Cu-Kα filter generated radiation with a wavelength of 1.5406 Å.

Differential Scanning Colorimetry (DSC) (PerkinElmer model STA 8000, United States) was used to perform thermal analysis in order to determine the melting point and melting enthalpy of the xylitol crystal samples. An aluminium pan containing five milligrams of crystals was sealed. The sample was heated from 40 °C to 180 °C in a nitrogen environment at a scanning rate of 10 °Cmin−1 in order to produce sample thermograms. In order to stop oxidation, it was then cooled to room temperature at the same pace in an environment high in nitrogen. To make sure the instrument was stable, a baseline run with an empty pan was conducted.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (PerkinElmer model STA 8000, United States) was used to assess the crystals' thermal stability and degradation behavior. For this, 5 mg of the crystal samples were heated to between 30 and 800 °C in a crucible. The loss in sample weight was measured simultaneously by the instrument during the heating process.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy or FTIR (IRSpirit-X, Japan) was used to assess the existence of functional groups in the sample was determined by analyzing the acquired spectra in the form of bands and peaks. A wavenumber range of 500–4000 cm−1 was used to assess the xylitol crystal samples.

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) or NMR was used to determine the purity of xylitol crystals. As a reference, commercial grade xylitol (98% purity) was used for the analytical testing. To verify the uniformity of xylitol crystals and assess the purity of xylitol, further 13C and 1H NMR was performed.

Decolorization (color difference) was determined using a spectrocolorimeter (Lovibond® Tintometer) by keeping untreated RSH as the standard. Results and discussion

Downstream processing of fermented broth for xylitol recovery

Detoxification of xylitol rich fermented RSH

The fermentation of synthetic media containing commercial xylose in the 3 L bioreactor, led to 39.70 ± 0.51 gL−1 xylitol production, with a productivity and xylitol yield of 0.55 gL−1h−1 and 0.69 gg−1 respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3). However, in FRSH, a slightly lower titre of 36.10 ± 0.40 gL−1, was obtained with a productivity of 0.50 gL−1h−1 and xylitol yield of 0.62 gg−1. ΔE (decolorization) value of 43.90 was observed after detoxification of FRSH with 3% w/v AC (light yellow), as compared to untreated FRSH (dark brown) (as shown in Supplementary Table T1). Moreover, the final xylitol concentration in AC-treated FRSH was 34.49 gL−1. Decolorizing xylitol broth removes pigments and byproducts that hinder nucleation, distort crystal morphology, and reduce purity. Yellowing and a dirty crystal appearance can result from trapped pigments in FRSH liquid during crystallization [29].

Optimization of xylitol crystallization

Effect of xylitol supersaturation level

The xylitol rich FRSH syrup was concentrated to yield a final xylitol concentration of about 500, 650 and 800 gL−1 having supersaturation values (dimensionless) of 1.00, 1.34 and 1.60 respectively. Based on prior experimental observations, concentrations lower than 500 g L−1 did not produce sufficient supersaturation for effective crystal formation which also led to dissolution of added seed crystals, indicating undersaturation. On the other hand, crystallization at 0 °C with syrup of concentration ∼650 gL−1 slightly exceeded the equilibrium solubility limit, which provided favorable conditions for nucleation and crystal growth [30]. Excessive supersaturation probably accelerated nucleation at higher concentrations (∼800 gL−1), resulting in smaller crystals and lower crystal quality. According to NCBI report, 2026 [37] xylitol is soluble in water at ∼642 gL−1 at 25 °C. Moreover, pure xylitol has a solubility of about 329 g per 100 g of water at 40 °C which decreases to about 65 wt% when the temperature drops to 25 °C [38]. As shown in Table 1, at 650 ± 13.18 gL−1, xylitol crystallization yield (YC) of 81% with transparent, big crystals (500–1000 µm), weighing 4.73 ± 0.5 g was achieved at a crystallization temperature of 4 °C. On the other hand, the minimum xylitol crystallization yield (50.2%) was attained at 500 ± 10.04 gL−1. Effect of temperature

At 0 °C, the highest crystallization yield (Yc) of 73.84% and crystal purity (XP) of 96% was observed. As shown in Table 2, the crystals formed at –20 °C were brittle and opaque in appearance with a lower crystallization yield (52.61%) as compared to those at 0 °C (73.84%). At lower crystallization temperatures, Txyt (0 °C and –20 °C), crystal production initiated in a shorter time with instance of first crystal sighting after 12 h of nucleation. On the contrary, at 20 °C, delayed initiation of crystallization up to five days was observed with formation of small dark brown crystals and lower yield. This is because sudden cooling at very low temperatures can lead to the rapid crystallization of sugar molecules without sufficient time to build a transparent, well-ordered crystal lattice. As a result, small and disordered crystals are produced that scatter light and appear opaque [43]. Furthermore, the appearance of dark brown crystals at high temperature is due to the absorption of colored substances and impurities from fermented broth due to intensification of molecular vibration [44]. A study by Sampaió et al. [10] showed that on lowering the crystallization temperature, an increased crystallization yield was observed, while completely opposite behaviour was seen for degree of crystal purity. They used a temperature of −20 °C for initiation of crystallization followed by 8 °C for crystal growth and observed 43.97% crystallization yield. Misra et al. [30] employed four crystallization cycles to produce xylitol crystallization yields of 76.20% and 68.06% in 50 ml and 5000 ml of synthetic xylose fermentation broth, respectively, using an adapted strain of C. tropicalis. Effect of seed size

As depicted in Table 3, the maximum crystallization yield of 74% was observed in seed crystals (of 800 µm size, whereas the lowest crystallization yield of 40.76% was observed in seed crystals of 1500 µm size, but with the appearance of the bigger crystals (5000–8000 µm). This is attributed to the fact that, larger seed crystals reduce the likelihood of new nucleation events occurring in the crystallising syrup. Due to fewer nucleation sites, the dissolved molecules preferentially attach to the existing large seed crystals, allowing them to grow big rather than forming many smaller crystals [46], [13]. Furthermore, maximum crystal weight, Wc (4.81 g) was observed in 800 μm seed crystals as opposed to 2.65 g obtained in 1500 μm seed crystal. This is because, smaller seed crystals provide more nucleation sites for the dissolved molecules to attach and grow. This leads to the formation of more crystals, resulting in a higher yield of smaller crystals. Smaller seed crystals are also less likely to be contaminated with impurities [46]. Chung et al. Crystals formed in the unseeded control implies that the system might have partially crossed the metastable zone barrier in the conditions under study. In contrast to the unseeded control, which produced fewer but significantly larger crystals, the inclusion of seeds enhanced control over nucleation and crystal development. This is because at optimum seed quantity, balanced nucleation and regulated growth is permitted, resulting in more uniform crystals with higher yield and weight. On the other hand, excessive seeding cause uncontrolled nucleation, which forms many tiny crystals which rapidly depletes the supersaturation. Effect of ultrasonication on xylitol crystallization

The application of ultrasound had a positive impact on xylitol crystallization. The xylitol crystals formed in sonicated sample (Fig. 1b) were having uniform size and well-formed crystal appearance as compared to non-sonicated sample (Fig. 1a). Moreover, in sonicated sample, the xylitol crystals observed were larger (1600–1800 µm) with narrower crystal size distribution (CSD) as compared to the control (non-sonicated) with broad CSD in the range of 800–1300 µm (Fig. 1c). It was also observed that the crystals formed in control were in the form of aggregates. The visual overview and microscope image of change in crystal shape and size has been provided in Fig. S4, Supplementary File. Sonication led to 9.38% increase in the crystal weight with subsequent hike in crystallization yield to 81.08% as compared to 74.13% in control (Table 5). D10, D50, D90, coefficient of variation (CV), and span values showed that ultrasound-assisted crystallization resulted in larger and more homogeneous crystals. In particular, the span dropped from 0.615 to 0.431, the CV dropped from 18.3% to 13.8%, and the D50 increased from around 1198 µm to 1410 µm, in relation to non-sonicated sample. These findings suggest that ultrasonication enhanced crystal development and produced narrower CSD. Sonication creates mechanical disturbances in a liquid via both acoustic streaming and cavitation. Moreover, these influences modify the dynamics of fluids by reducing diffusion boundary layers around growing crystals which enhances solute bulk-phase mass transfer and promotes crystal growth [49]. Similar results were reported by, Ruecroft et al. [16] in D-glucose monohydrate crystallization at 70 °C by sononucleation, where well defined and uniform crystals were obtained. It is also reported that ultrasound mechanically disrupts crystals or loosely bound agglomerates that have already been formed [50]. An additional experiment was also performed by keeping mechanically stirred sample as silent control (Supplementary File, Table T2). Stirring resulted in better crystallization performance, due to increased mixing on crystal development as compared to non-sonicated sample. Effect of sonication time

The sonication of xylitol rich CFXM feed syrup was done at time intervals of 10, 15, 20 and 25 min. The non-sonicated sample at the same saturation level was taken as control. It was observed that on increasing the ultrasonic irradiation time from 10 min to 15 min, overall range of the size of the produced xylitol crystals also increased from 800-900 µm to 1600–1800 µm. This was followed by a rise in crystallization yield (YC) and weight from 68.22% and 3.54 ± 0.43 g to 83.96% and 5.63 ± 0.50 g respectively (Table 6). Similar observation was reported for the cooling crystallization of acetylsalicylic acid, where at low ultrasound power, an increase in the particle size was seen as a function of irradiation time [51]. However, the size decreased in sample sonicated for 20 min, while the smallest crystals were formed in crucible sonicated for 25 min. No effect on crystal purity was observed, which remained constant for all the test samples (95%). This is because at short periods of sonication, the ultrasonic waves fail to blend the solution, so little crystal formation can be seen. In contrast, prolonged sonication reduced crystal size due to excessive secondary nucleation and fragmentation [52]. Furthermore, the estimated dissipated acoustic power for 15 min ultrasonication treatment per 5 mL sample was measured (see Supplementary File, section S1) to be approximately 0.017 W (3.4 WL−1) which falls within the lower end of this range, consistent with the use of a bath-type sonicator and a small sample volume with limited acoustic coupling [21]. Ultrasonic irradiation also promotes crystal fragmentation by producing shear stresses that break larger crystals into smaller nuclei, thereby increasing the number of nucleation sites and shortening the induction time [15]. In a similar study, Guo et al. [22] reported a reduction in the induction time upon the use of sonication in roxithromycin crystallization. The xylitol crystal size obtained in study was bigger than cooling crystallization of synthetic xylitol syrup by using ethanol as an anti-solvent, as compared to other study, where 200–700 µm crystal size was reported [3]. Moreover, the crystallization yield was comparable to a study reported by Ahuja et al. [34], using corncob hydrolysate fermented by P. caribbica MTCC 5703. In a similar study, Canilha et al. [53], reported that vacuum concentration of fermented wheat straw, produced dark viscous syrup which inhibited crystallization. So, on supplementing the concentrated medium with commercial xylitol solution in the ratio of 70:30, a higher crystallization yield of 43.5% was achieved, along with the formation of well-formed crystals. Similarly, in the present study, on increasing the relative proportion of CFX, the crystal purity increased to 97% as compared to 93% purity of pure RS crystal. This is due to the increase in CFXM portion in crystallizing syrup which dilutes the concentration of unconsumed monosaccharides, salts, and remnants of lignin derivatives [55]. Furthermore, after concentration, the fermented lignocellulosic media turns viscous and dark again, which hinders xylitol crystallization [45]. So, supplementing it with CFXM enhances the relative xylitol purity in the crystallising feed syrup [56]. Low molecular weight oligosaccharides attach to some crystal faces with considerable affinity, blocking some of them and allowing other faces to grow and form needle-shaped crystals. For example, when raffinose is present during sucrose crystallisation, needle-like geometries are produced, but rhombohedral shapes are produced when ketoses are present [57]. In contrast to low purity sugar samples, which form smaller rock sugar crystals, Lim et al. [58] demonstrated that purer sugars (>84%) produce larger crystals (2–5 cm).

Characterization of xylitol crystals

Inverted microscopy

When studied under microscope, the xylitol crystals first emerged as spherulite or star-shaped aggregates made of radially oriented needle-like structures (Supplementary Fig. S6a). These clustered structures eventually split into separate faceted crystals as crystallization went on (Fig. S6b), which later evolved into different individual crystals with smoother and more regular crystal faces (Fig. S6c). During the early phases of crystallization, fast multidirectional nucleation and radial crystal development most likely produced the observed spherulitic shape. The crystal aggregates became more distinct and developed into independent mature crystals as local supersaturation decreased and continued to expand [59].

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectra of crystals formed from processing of FRSH, CFXM compared to CX has been shown in Fig. 7b.

Xylitol (C5H12O5) is a sugar alcohol with five carbons attached to OH groups. The bands associated with OH stretching vibrations are those between 3500 cm−1 and 3000 cm−1, and 1470 cm−1 and 750 cm−1, the bands associated with CH2 are those between 920 cm−1 and 884 cm−1, and the bands associated with CO are at 1005, 1063 and 1110 cm−1 [61], [63]. A sharp peak around 2915 cm−1 reflects CH stretching, corresponding to the aliphatic C-H bonds in xylitol. Similar peaks across all samples is indicative of the hydrocarbon backbone. For commercial xylitol, a broad band around 3153 cm−1 depicted stretching vibrations due to water absorption related to –OH groups, while the bands at 1418 cm−1 and 745 cm−1 corresponded to blending vibrations of OH. The RX and CFX crystals showed similar visual characteristics to CX. Similar FTIR peaks pattern was observed by Yang et al. [64] with pure xylitol.

Fermentation of RSH in a 5 L bioreactor

The composition of RSH obtained after pretreatment of RS with 1.5% v/v H2SO4 was (gL−1): 14.89 ± 0.53 xylose, 0.162 ± 0.018 furans, 1.04 ± 0.24 total phenols (TP) and 1.79 ± 0.10 total organic acids (TOA). Following 2% w/v AC detoxification, the composition of detoxified RSH was (gL−1): 12.70 ± 0.40 xylose, 0.20 ± 0.04 TP, and 0.60 ± 0.05 TOA, which was then vacuum concentrated to ∼ 59 gL−1 initial xylose. The fermentation of detoxified RSH, produced a satisfactory xylitol titre (36.7 ± 0.23 gL−1), with a xylitol yield of 0.63 gg−1, productivity of 0.50 gL-1h−1, and conversion efficiency of 69% at 72 h (Supplementary File, Fig. S3). This is much higher than conversion (62%) reported by Kumar et al. [66] of corncob hydrolysate using C. tropicalis MTCC 6192 at pH 4.5 in a 14 L bioreactor, that was run for 60 h. The cell dry weight in this study's 14 L bioreactor run was 20.90 ± 0.64 gL−1, and the cell biomass concentration peaked after 48 h.

Conclusion

Using both conventional and ultrasound-assisted crystallization techniques, this work effectively optimized the crystallization process for xylitol made from RSH, yielding encouraging findings. Significant crystallization yields were obtained under optimal conditions, which included 650 ± 13.18 gL−1 xylitol supersaturation, 0 °C crystallization temperature, 800 µm xylitol seed addition and 0.2% w/v seed quantity. Notably, ultrasound-assisted crystallization encouraged nucleation and crystal development and decreased particle agglomeration. Ultrasound-assisted methods demonstrated significant benefits in crystallization efficiency and crystal shape, with 9.38% increase in crystal weight as compared to non-sonicated samples. Analytical tests validated the high purity (95.15%) and quality of xylitol crystals. Moreover, the purity of xylitol crystals can be further enhanced by recrystallization. All things considered, this strategy makes RS-based xylitol production more feasible while highlighting its promise for sustainable biomanufacturing and providing a high-yield, scalable pathway for bio-based xylitol recovery.
