Characteristic computed tomography features of mild-to-moderate Legionella pneumonia: evaluation of a known pattern and identification of a novel indicator
Authors: Shouichi Okamoto, Mariko Shinomiya, Akio Niimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to re-evaluate the accuracy of known computed tomography (CT) findings and identify potentially novel radiological features to facilitate the accurate diagnosis of Legionella pneumonia.MethodsA total of 18 cases of genetically identical Legionella pneumophila serogroup 1 pneumonia from a single outbreak were retrospectively analyzed. The chest CT scans were assessed for known and novel imaging features. Associations between CT findings and clinical scores, indicated by A-DROP and Legionella scores, were evaluated. Validation was performed using historical Legionella pneumonia cases (n = 4) and cohorts of Streptococcus pneumoniae (n = 22) and Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (n = 30).ResultsA CT pattern described as sharply demarcated peribronchovascular consolidation with ground-glass opacity was identified in nine patients (50.0%).Plate-to-fan-shaped consolidation extending from the peribronchovascular area to the pleura, a newly recognized feature, was observed in six patients (33.3%). At least one of these findings was observed in each of the 11 cases (61.1%), particularly in those with A-DROP scores of ≤ 2. These features were significantly more frequent in patients with Legionella pneumonia than in those with pneumonia caused by S. pneumoniae or M. pneumoniae. The interobserver agreement level was substantial to almost perfect.ConclusionCharacteristic CT patterns were frequently observed in mild to moderate Legionella pneumonia and may assist in early radiologic diagnoses, especially in cases where clinical suspicion is low.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04210-5.

Introduction

Legionella pneumonia (LP) accounts for approximately 4.6% of all cases of community-acquired pneumonia [1]. LP is typically characterized by a rapidly progressive disease course with severe clinical symptoms. The overall mortality rate of LP is approximately 8% [2, 3], and that among patients requiring admission to an intensive care unit is 26%–30% [4, 5]. Legionella is an intracellular pathogen that resides within alveolar macrophages, therefore antibiotics with high intracellular penetration, such as quinolones or macrolides, are required for effective treatment. Early detection is critical for optimizing infection management.

Urinary antigen tests (UATs) are a convenient tool for the rapid diagnosis of LP. However, although the most common UAT, targeting L. pneumophila serogroup 1, is highly specific, it exhibits only moderate sensitivity [6]. A new UAT designed to detect all serogroups of L. pneumophila has demonstrated similar sensitivity [7]. Several clinical scoring models have been proposed for LP detection; however, they have reported sensitivities of only 78%–93% and specificities of only 65%–78.8% [8–10]; therefore, they are suboptimal, particularly in terms of specificity.

The diagnostic role of imaging in LP remains underexplored. Chest computed tomography (CT) scans often reveal multilobar or multisegmental consolidation combined with ground-glass opacities (GGO) and can also display lymphadenopathy and small amounts of pleural effusion [11–15].

Previously, Sakai et al. described a CT pattern that distinguished LP from Streptococcus pneumoniae pneumonia (SP) [13]. However, the specific clinical context in which this pattern appears has not been validated, and no other novel CT features have been definitively associated with LP. To address this knowledge gap, we retrospectively reviewed cases of LP caused by the genetically identical L. pneumophila serogroup 1 to re-evaluate established CT findings and identify potentially novel radiological features that could aid in the diagnosis of LP.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Board of Osaka Saiseikai Ibaraki Hospital (approval no. 2024–13) on December 9, 2024. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the use of anonymized retrospective data.

Patients

We retrospectively analyzed data from 18 patients diagnosed with LP who were consecutively admitted for treatment between September and October 2023. All cases were confirmed as L. pneumophila serogroup 1 infections using UAT (IMMUNOCATCH; Eiken Chemical Co. Ltd.). Molecular epidemiological analysis conducted by a local public health center identified a private cooling tower as the source of the infection. The same L. pneumophila serogroup 1 genotype was detected in patient sputum samples and water samples collected from the cooling tower.

To validate the observed CT findings, we analyzed previous cases of LP (L. pneumophila serogroup 1) from our hospital, as well as cases of SP and Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MP) from 2024. A positive UAT or S. pneumoniae isolation from sputum or blood cultures indicated SP. MP was diagnosed based on antigen detection or polymerase chain reaction analysis of pharyngeal swab specimens or by a four-fold or greater increase in serum antibody titers. Cases of co-infection, indicated by sputum culture yielding bacterial pathogens potentially responsible for bacterial pneumonia, were excluded.

Data collection

The following clinical data were extracted at admission: age, sex, smoking status, comorbidities, clinical signs and symptoms, laboratory data (white blood cell count and C-reactive protein [CRP], sodium, and lactate dehydrogenase [LDH] levels), days from symptom onset to treatment initiation, antibiotic use, and clinical outcome. The severity of pneumonia was assessed using the A-DROP score, as recommended by the Japanese Respiratory Society [16]. The A-DROP score is a modified version of the CURB-65 score used in Japanese clinical settings and is based on the following parameters: age, male ≥ 70 years or female ≥ 75 years; dehydration, blood urea nitrogen ≥ 21 mg/dL (7.5 mmol/L) or dehydration; respiration, SpO2 ≤ 90% (PaO2 ≤ 60 Torr); orientation, presence of confusion; and blood pressure, systolic blood pressure ≤ 90 mmHg. Disease severity was scored as follows: 0 = mild, 1–2 = moderate, 3 = severe, and 4–5, or any score with “shock” at admission = extremely severe [16]. The CURB-65 score is based on five criteria: confusion, blood urea nitrogen > 7 mmol/L, respiratory rate ≥ 30/min, low blood pressure, and age ≥ 65 years. [17]

The Legionella score was calculated using a predictive model for LP that included male sex, absence of a cough, presence of dyspnea, CRP ≥ 1.8 mg/L, LDH ≥ 260 U/L, and sodium levels < 134 mmol/L [10]. Applying this six-point scoring model with a cutoff value of 3 points, Legionella pneumonia was identified with a sensitivity of 93% and a specificity of 75%. [9]

Patients were positioned in the supine position during complete aspiration for chest CT scans, and a slice thickness of < 2 mm was used. Lung window settings were standardized (level − 600 HU; width 1500 HU). For historical cases of LP, SP, and MP, patients with evidence of interstitial changes or emphysema were excluded from the analysis.

CT image analysis

The CT findings of the 18 LP cases were assessed by a pulmonologist (S.O.) and categorized as consolidation, GGO, nodules, cavitary lesions, or pleural effusion. CT findings were defined and described using standardized thoracic imaging terminology according to the Fleischner Society glossary [18]. The distribution patterns of consolidation and GGO were also documented. We defined sharply demarcated peribronchovascular (PBV) foci of consolidation intermingled with GGO (hereafter referred to as demarcated PBV foci with GGO) in accordance with the description provided by Sakai et al. [13]

Furthermore, we evaluated the presence of novel CT features. The presence of such features was independently assessed by two board-certified pulmonologists (S.O. and M.S.), each with over 8 years of experience in respiratory medicine and routine chest CT interpretation, who were unblinded to the clinical data. The final decisions were reached by consensus.

Statistical analysis

McNemar’s test was used to compare paired proportions, and Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical data, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The interobserver agreement for CT findings was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ). When κ could not be computed because of zero variance or when agreement might be underestimated (e.g., low prevalence), Gwet’s agreement coefficient (AC1) was calculated to provide a more robust estimate [19]. All agreement analyses were conducted using weighted coefficients. All agreement measures were unweighted. Agreement levels were classified as slight (0.01–0.20), fair (0.21–0.40), moderate (0.41–0.60), substantial (0.61–0.80), or almost perfect (> 0.80) [20]. All statistical analyses were conducted using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [21].

Twelve patients (66.7%) scored ≥ 3 on the six-point Legionella scoring method, meeting the diagnostic threshold for suspected LP. The mean duration from symptom onset to CT was 4.1 days. Chest CT findings

Chest CT revealed unilateral consolidation and GGO in > 90% of the cases (Table 2). The lesions were predominantly located in the lower lobes. A lobar pneumonia pattern was observed in 12 cases (66.7%). Mild pleural effusion was noted in 11 patients (61.1%); however, no nodules or cavitary lesions were observed. The demarcated PBV foci with GGO were identified in nine patients (50.0%) (Fig. 1). Six patients (33.3%) showed contiguous plate-like or fan-shaped consolidation extending from the PBV bundles to the pleura on sequential thin-section CT images using lung window settings (Fig. 2). The frequencies of the demarcated PBV foci with GGO and contiguous PBV–pleural consolidation in the LP, SP, and MP cases were compared (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Both imaging findings were significantly more frequent in patients with LP than in those with SP or MP.

The relationship between the prevalence of both imaging findings and disease severity was assessed (Supplementary Table 3). Demarcated PBV foci with GGO was observed in 71.4% and 16.7% of LP and MP cases, respectively, with an A-DROP score of 0 (P = 0.009). Contiguous PBV–pleural consolidation was identified in 42.9% and 3.3% of LP and MP cases, respectively, with an A-DROP score of 0, and in 44.4% of LP cases and 20% of SP cases with an A-DROP score of 2 (P = 0.016 and 0.58, respectively). Both imaging patterns were considerably more frequent in patients with LP than with MP when their A-DROP score was 0.

Discussion

This study showed that patients with mild-to-moderate LP (A-DROP score: 0–2) exhibited demarcated PBV foci with GGO more frequently than those with higher A-DROP scores or other types of pneumonia. A novel imaging feature of LP, referred to as contiguous PBV–pleural consolidation, was also identified.

Legionnaires’ disease can be diagnosed using various methods, including culture, UAT, nucleic acid amplification, direct fluorescent antibody staining, and serological testing [22]. Generally, UAT targeting L. pneumophila serogroup 1 is the primary diagnostic method [22]. However, UAT sensitivity is often low during the early stages of infection, leading to false-negative results [23], particularly in cases of mild LP [24]. Legionella testing is recommended for patients who are unresponsive to empirical antibiotics, those requiring intensive care, immunocompromised individuals, and travelers, as well as during outbreaks [23]. In mild early-stage LP without risk factors, UAT may be omitted, potentially delaying diagnosis. Therefore, imaging remains a critical tool for clinically suspected LP, facilitating early treatment.

LP-specific CT features have not been widely defined. CT typically reveals bilateral and multi-lobar or multi-segmental consolidation with GGO [11, 12, 14, 15]. Although Sakai et al. described a distinct CT pattern associated with LP [13], they reported no association between this pattern and disease severity, and provided no details on severity grading. In our study, the frequency of demarcated PBV foci with GGO (50%) was slightly lower than previously reported. The mean number of days from symptom onset to CT in this study was 4.1, whereas Sakai et al. reported a mean of 5.6 days (range, 0–26 days) [13]. The earlier timing of CT imaging in our cohort may have contributed to the reductions in frequency and A-DROP scores. However, the high proportion of patients with A-DROP scores ≤ 2 (83.3%) suggests that this pattern may be more common in milder cases. No association was observed between the Legionella score and demarcated PBV foci with GGO, indicating that LP severity, rather than LP-specific features, influences the occurrence of this CT pattern.

Demarcated PBV foci with GGO were identifiable from a single CT slice, whereas contiguous PBV–pleural consolidation became apparent when consecutive slices were examined. We speculate that a relatively linear, contiguous extension of consolidation along the PBV bundles toward the pleura appears plate-like on thin-section CT. When this PBV-oriented consolidation is accompanied by broader peripheral spread, the configuration may appear fan-shaped. The PBV region extends from the hilum to the centrilobular area of the secondary pulmonary lobule. It comprises pulmonary arteries, bronchi and bronchioles, collagen bundles, bronchial arteries, nerves, lymphatics, adipose tissue, and interstitial space [25]. Abnormalities in this region, such as PBV GGO or consolidation, are associated with infections, malignancies, and inflammatory diseases [25]. The contiguous PBV–pleural consolidation observed in this study originated near the perivenous space adjacent to specific bronchi and pulmonary veins and extended to the pleura, which has not been described previously in any pulmonary infection. Among the six cases in the outbreak cohort with contiguous PBV–pleural consolidation, the images in two cases (33.3%) did not show demarcated PBV foci with GGO. Of the four cases in which both findings were present, two (50%) showed no continuity between their patterns. Therefore, contiguous PBV–pleural consolidation should be evaluated as a distinct imaging finding, separate from demarcated PBV foci with GGO.

A histopathological review of 74 LP cases revealed that acute alveolitis and bronchiolitis are key microscopic features [26]. Within the affected areas, highly cellular regions coexist with fibrin-rich, yet relatively acellular, alveoli. Central inflammatory infiltrates contain both neutrophils and macrophages, whereas macrophages are predominant in the peripheral responses [26]. These features may explain the heterogeneous appearance of CT images, such as the combination of consolidation and GGO reported by Sakai et al. [13]. However, no previous studies have examined pathological PBV involvement in LP, highlighting the need for further investigation. LP commonly shows coalescing foci that extend toward the pleural surface or fissures [26]. Evaluations of continuous thin-slice CT are required to identify these patterns.

This study had some limitations. First, the sample size was small (18 patients), which limited the statistical power and generalizability. The comparison of the CT findings according to A-DROP score was limited by the small number of cases in each severity category, as well as the uniformity of mild cases among all patients with MP. Second, only cases of L. pneumophila serogroup 1 infection were included; thus, our results do not indicate whether the observed imaging features extend to other serogroups or species. UAT-negative LP tends to show lobar consolidation and fewer bronchial walls or centrilobular abnormalities [27]. Moreover, the genetic uniformity of our cases might have contributed to the consistency of the results, as observed in prior outbreak reports [11, 15]. Third, we limited analysis to CT scans with a slice thickness of ≤ 2 mm, which reduced the number of eligible retrospective LP cases. Fourth, a lack of blinding may have influenced image interpretation, because the evaluators were aware of the clinical information. However, κ and Gwet’s AC1 statistics consistently indicated substantial to almost perfect agreement, and thus, robust interobserver reliability. This implies that reasonably high agreement may have also been achieved under blinded conditions. Future research should involve larger, blinded cohorts and utilize continuous multi-slice imaging to validate the diagnostic utility and prevalence of contiguous PBV–pleural consolidation and other LP-specific patterns. Finally, while radiologist-based assessment is common in imaging studies, CT findings in our cohort were evaluated by experienced pulmonologists. Given the independent dual-reader design and consensus process, we believe the assessments were robust. However external validation with radiologist involvement is warranted.

Conclusions

This study revealed distinct features in the CT images of patients with mild-to-moderate LP, as determined by the A-DROP score, which included the CT pattern previously identified by Sakai et al. (demarcated PBV foci with GGO) and the novel contiguous PBV–pleural consolidation feature. These imaging patterns may aid in the early recognition of LP, particularly in cases where clinical suspicion is low or conventional diagnostics yield inconclusive results.
