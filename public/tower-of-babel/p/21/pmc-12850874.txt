Efficacy and safety of over-the-scope endoscopic suturing device for closure of defects after endoscopic submucosal dissection: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Tahne Vongsavath, Kyaw Min Tun, Syed Mohsin Saghir, Jay Bapaye, Renuka Verma, Chun-Han Lo, Ahmad Gill, Banreet Singh Dhindsa, Saurabh Chandan, Douglas G Adler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsEndoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is used for closure of GI lesions after endoscopic resection techniques such as EMR. Generally, a large defect is left that can increase the risk of postprocedural bleeding or perforation, and it has been managed by techniques such as endoscopic placement of clips to reapproximate the defect edges. An over-the-scope suturing system (OSS), OverStitch created by Apollo Endosurgery, has been designed for similar utility in ESD closure. The goal of its design is to achieve full-thickness closure and exchange of suture without endoscopic removal. The goal of this study was to evaluate the technical success and efficacy of using OSS in the closure of ESD.MethodsA comprehensive literature review across multiple databases was performed. Studies including OverStitch and ESD information were evaluated, with 7 articles meeting predetermined eligibility of quality for inclusion. Statistical analysis was completed by using CMA version 3.0 software (Biostat, Englewood, NJ, USA). The primary evaluated end point was technical success and efficacy of instrument use in patients undergoing ESD closure with OSS. The secondary evaluated end point was the overall rate of adverse events related to the use of OSS.ResultsOverall, the pooled rates for instrumental efficacy and technical success were 95.8% (95% CI [confidence interval], 80.6%-99.2%; P = .04) and 99.2% (95% CI, 79.8%-100.0%; P = 1.00), respectively. The pooled rate of adverse events was .9% (95% CI, 0%-24.8%; P = .99) with pain reported as the most common adverse event.ConclusionsOSS seems to be an effective and safe tool in the closure of defects after ESD. However, its design also makes it large and difficult to maneuver. Although it allows for replacement of the suture kit without endoscopic removal, there have been multiple reported episodes of device failure. Further randomized controlled trials are warranted as OSS becomes more widely used.

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is used for resection of GI mucosal lesions that are too large to remove with traditional endoscopic resection techniques or via EMR.1 By injecting the submucosal layer with a fluid or gel, the lesion is lifted, creating a relatively safe submucosal space for endoscopic dissection to occur, therefore sharply decreasing the risk of perforation. The use of ESD is becoming a more common alternative to EMR due to its higher curative resection rates and lower lesion recurrence rates in patients with esophageal, gastric, and colonic neoplasms.2

ESD generally leaves a large mucosal defect that can increase the risk of postprocedural adverse events such as perforation or bleeding.3 Historically, these defects have been managed by endoscopic clipping. A complete closure of a resection site with clipping after a gastric ESD had a reported success rate that ranges between 68.2% and 75.0% depending on the location.4 Thus, it is recommended that endoscopists consider other closure methods or forgo closure altogether in some cases based on the size and location of the specimen. Due to the potential for adverse effects associated with delayed or incomplete closure, studies suggest that early closure of defects may lead to improved patient outcomes.5

An over-the-scope suturing system (OSS) for flexible endoscopy, OverStitch (Apollo Endosurgery, Austin, Tex, USA), was initially used in patients with perforations, leaks, and fistulas because of its ability to achieve full-thickness suturing. The device allows for the placement of separate stitches and a continuous suturing line with internal knot tying. This system also allows the user to reload the suturing device an unlimited number of times without having to remove the endoscope from the patient.5 The utility of this device was initially limited by the first generation’s need for a double-channel endoscope and its second generation’s sole compatibility with double-channel endoscopes; however, a more recent model (the OverStitch Sx) has been developed and is compatible with multiple endoscopes from multiple manufacturers.6 Although successful closure of defects with OSS after ESD has been reported by multiple studies, its outcome can be affected by size and location of the defect and the suturing pattern used.7 We have performed the first systematic review and meta-analysis for the efficacy and safety of OSS in closing mucosal defects after ESD.

Methods

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed across major databases (PubMed/Medline, Embase) using variations of the key words “endoscopic submucosal dissection,” “OverStitch,” and “over the scope suturing system” and related terms to identify original studies published from inception through May 2023. Results were limited to human studies published in English. A total of 42 studies were identified that underwent subsequent formal review.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who underwent ESD; and (2) patients who received intervention with the OverStitch device. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) case reports; (2) published letters to the editor and commentaries that do not require detailed patient data or an extensive review process; (3) studies without patient data; (4) non-English studies; and (5) animal studies.

Quality assessment

The methodologic quality of the included studies was evaluated by using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. The risk of bias was completed through the examination of 9 categories, each scored using a star system. Studies were categorized at low, moderate, or high quality based on a score of 0 to 3 stars, 4 to 6 stars, and 7 to 9 stars, respectively.8 The revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool was used to assess the methodologic quality of randomized controlled trials. The quality of a randomized controlled trial was rated as high, low, or some concern based on evaluation of the individual elements in the 5 included domains: randomization process, derivations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result.9

Quality appraisal was performed by at least 2 of the following authors (K.M.T, R.V., C.-H.L., T.V., or A.G.). If there was any disagreement, a different author (S.M.S., B.S.D., or D.G.A.) evaluated the article and achieved consensus through discussion. Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 (available online at www.igiejournal.org) present quality assessment scores for each study.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes were technical success and efficacy of instrument use for patients undergoing procedures with the OverStitch system. Technical success was defined as the ability to use the device without malfunction. Efficacy of instrument use was defined as achieving closure of the defect after ESD.7 The secondary outcome was the rate of adverse events associated with the use of the OverStitch system.

Study selection and data extraction

A total of 42 studies were retrieved on the initial search after titles and abstracts were independently reviewed by the authors. Full texts were then reviewed by at least 2 of the authors (K.M.T., C.-H.L., R.V., A.G., or T.V.), after which 7 remaining studies (4 prospective and 3 retrospective cohort studies) fulfilled our complete eligibility criteria. In cases of disagreement, a senior reviewer (S.M.S., B.S.D., S.C., or D.G.A.) arbitrated the final decision for inclusion. Study selection process by use of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement is detailed in Supplementary Figure 1 (available online at www.igiejournal.org). Institutional review board and individual patient consent were not required because all data were extracted from published literature and no patient intervention was directly performed.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) for the study selection process.

Statistical analysis

A random effects model with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to calculate pooled estimates of the outcomes. The statistical analysis was conducted by using CMA version 3.0 software (Biostat, Englewood, NJ, USA).

A total of 134 patients from 7 studies conducted from 2014 to 2023 were included in the final analysis.5,10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Table 1 presents a summary of the included studies. Patients’ mean age was 66 years. Four studies reported the sex for 67 patients, of whom 55% (n = 37) were male. Six studies were from the United States and 1 study was from Italy. ESD was performed most frequently in the stomach (40.48% [n = 51]), followed by the rectum (29.1% [n = 39]) and the colon (16.41% [n = 22]).5,10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Other reported, less frequent, sites of OSS use included the cecum (0.75% [n = 1]), the gastroesophageal junction (1.59% [n = 2]), and the esophagus (6.0% [n = 8]).10 Han et al12 and Kantsevoy et al13 reported using double-channel OSS, while Keihanian et al14 used an OSS that was compatible with a single-channel endoscope. Other studies did not report whether a single- or double-channel endoscope was used.

Per the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy lexicon, none of the included studies reported fatal or severe adverse events.16 The adverse events related to pain and dysphagia resolved spontaneously and did not require intervention or delayed procedure and were determined to be mild. The adverse events related to the formation of rectal stricture and colonic stenosis required dilation and temporary placement of a covered metal stent, respectively, and were graded as moderate.

For the instrumental efficacy outcome, a leave-1-out analysis was performed by excluding 1 study at a time to evaluate its effect on the pooled estimate to determine whether any one study had a dominant effect on the meta-analysis. Grande et al,11 Han et al,12 and Kantsevoy et al13 demonstrated a dominant effect on the overall rate of instrumental efficacy. Sensitivity analysis was not completed for technical success as all studies reported a 100% success rate. Similarly, because only 2 studies (Grande et al11 and Keihanian et al14) reported adverse events, a sensitivity analysis was not performed.

Discussion

Post-ESD adverse events primarily include bleeding and perforation, often due to the complexity of the closure process after lesion resection.17 As the indications for ESD continue to expand to larger intramucosal and submucosal tumors, investigations into new methods of closure are warranted. Our meta-analysis showed that OSS is an effective and safe method for closure of defects after ESD. To our knowledge, this meta-analysis is the first to evaluate the efficacy of OSS closure for ESD-related mucosal defects.

Although the included studies reported instrumental efficacy and technical success, it is important to note that because of the pre-insertion mount of the OSS, this suture device is larger than a standard endoscope, can interfere with user dexterity, requires multiple steps for set-up, and may increase technical difficulty in sites such as the fundus due to poor angulation and the duodenum due to narrow space.18 As found in a recent study by Frost et al,19 the suturing process involves many steps per individual suture placement, each of which was a potential area of equipment failure.

Overall, no perforations were reported. Pain after the OSS procedure was the most common adverse event and was reported more frequently in patients with rectal lesions. This finding was possibly due to the involvement of the dentate line and somatic sensory nerves.20, 21, 22 Nevertheless, it is worth noting that the grasping power of OSS is stronger than that of the commonly used endoclips, and its use may thus lead to perforations in areas of thinner tissue such as the duodenum or cecum, especially when handled by a novice operator.20 Ulceration may also occur and can lead to the formation of strictures and dysphagia.23,24

We acknowledge several limitations in this meta-analysis. First, many of the included studies were limited by lack of extended follow-up. Second, we were not able to perform site-specific analysis due to the limited data. The studies also lacked control groups in which neither OSS nor ESD was deployed. Third, the studies may be prone to publication bias in successful cases and selection bias for those needing full-thickness closure or large defect closure. Publication bias was not assessed as the number of studies was <10.25 The dominance of studies from the United States in this meta-analysis may reflect differences in the practice of closing post-ESD defects between various countries and regions. Fourth, moderate heterogeneity was noted in the measure of instrumental efficacy. Large randomized controlled trials with increased sample size and appropriate duration of follow-up are warranted to establish the effectiveness of OSS at the different areas of the GI tract. Lastly, although Ali et al10 reported a single advanced endoscopist, and Kantsevoy et al13 and Keihanian et al14 used a single endoscopist, others did not report skill level, experience, or the number of operators.

Conclusions

Since its development in 2009, the OverStitch system has undergone modifications allowing for use in multiple practice areas, from ESD closure to bariatric sleeve gastroplasty.7,26 Use of OSS seems to be an overall effective and safe method for full-thickness closure of defects after ESD with an overall trend toward shorter intervention time as users become more familiar with the device.7 However, the downsides to use of OSS include limited maneuverability, cumbersome operation, and possible equipment malfunction while in use. As this device becomes more widely used, further randomized controlled trials as well as user education on the device and its intricacies will be needed.
