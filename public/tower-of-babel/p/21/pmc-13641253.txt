Cardiomyocyte GC1 Mediates Estrogenic Angiogenesis in Right Heart Remodeling
Authors: Nobuaki Fukuma, Christos Tzimas, Ilaria Russo, Wen Dun, Michelle L. Lance, Yi Zhang, Jared S. Kushner, Charles W. Emala, Andreas Friebe, Nathan R. Tucker, Emily J. Tsai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Right ventricular (RV) dysfunction increases mortality in heart failure and pulmonary hypertension. However, women demonstrate better RV function and survival than men. This difference is attributed to estrogen, though mechanistic details remain unclear. Given estrogen’s stimulation of NO production, we investigated whether and how cardiomyocyte NO-sensitive GC1 (soluble guanylyl cyclase) mediates female-specific, adaptive RV pressure-overload remodeling.METHODS:Adult male and female mice with cardiomyocyte-specific GC1 deficiency (cardiomyocyte-specific knockout) and littermate controls underwent pulmonary artery banding (PAB) or thoracotomy (Sham). At 6-week postsurgery, RV function was assessed via echocardiography, pressure-volume loops, and treadmill testing. RV function, histopathology, and transcript profiles were compared across sex, genotype, and surgical group. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing of RV tissue was performed to identify putative cardiomyocyte GC1-mediated cell-cell communication in adaptive RV pressure-overload remodeling. Endothelial coculture assays with controls versus cardiomyocyte-specific knockout cardiomyocytes evaluated estrogen and cardiomyocyte GC1-dependence of the identified intercellular signaling.RESULTS:Female control PAB mice adapted RV contractility to overcome RV pressure-overload, thereby preserving RV-PA coupling. In contrast, female cardiomyocyte-specific knockout, ovariectomized female controls, and male PAB developed severe RV dysfunction with RV-PA uncoupling. These groups with maladapted RVs had marked cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and capillary rarefaction; female control PAB had minimal changes. Among histological features, the capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratio showed the strongest correlation with RV function. Ratios were similar between female control PAB and Sham, but abnormally low in all other PAB. Single-nucleus RNA sequence and coculture analyses revealed that cardiomyocyte GC1 is central to Vegf (vascular endothelial growth factor)-Vegfr (Vegf receptor) proangiogenic signaling from cardiomyocytes to endothelial cells in the adaptively remodeled, pressure-overloaded RV.CONCLUSIONS:We identified a novel estrogen- and cardiomyocyte GC1-dependent pathway that mitigates capillary rarefaction, maintaining normal capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratio and preserving RV-PA coupling under RV pressure-overload. This proangiogenic, estrogen- and cardiomyocyte GC1-dependent mechanism contributes to sex-specific differences in RV remodeling and may inform the development of targeted therapies for RV dysfunction.

Given estrogen-driven eNOS (endothelial nitric oxide synthase) activation and higher GC1 expression in the RV, we hypothesized that cardiomyocyte GC1 protects against maladaptive RV pressure-overload remodeling in a female-specific manner. We tested this using functional, histological, physiological, and molecular analyses in a murine PAB model with cardiomyocyte-specific GC1-deficient and littermate control mice of both sexes. Our findings revealed a strong positive correlation between RV function and capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratio—stronger than the inverse correlations between RV function and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy or interstitial fibrosis. Ovary-intact female control PAB mice maintained normal capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratios and RV-PA coupling, distinguishing their adaptively remodeled RV from the maladaptive remodeling observed in cardiomyocyte-specific knockout and male PAB mice. This estrogen-dependent preservation of cardiac microvasculature identifies a novel role for cardiomyocyte GC1 in blunting capillary rarefaction in RV pressure-overload remodeling. These findings underscore how different approaches to enhancing GC1-derived cGMP can influence sex-specific therapeutic efficacy. Direct GC1 stimulators, which bypass estrogen-eNOS signaling, may offer sex-independent protection against RV dysfunction. In contrast, therapies that rely on estrogen-eNOS activity may show greater efficacy in females. Understanding sex hormone regulation of these and other cardioprotective pathways will be key to developing effective, sex-independent treatments. Additionally, targeting capillary rarefaction may represent a promising, sex-neutral therapeutic strategy for RV dysfunction and warrants further investigation.

Right ventricular (RV) dysfunction is an independent predictor of adverse clinical outcomes, including increased mortality, in patients with pulmonary hypertension (PH)—regardless of cardiopulmonary disease cause or left ventricular (LV) function.1–3 Notably, men and women exhibit different RV responses to chronic pressure overload. Women consistently develop less severe RV dysfunction and have higher survival rates, even at comparable pulmonary pressures4–6 This female-specific advantage in RV remodeling has been observed across various conditions, including heart failure, interstitial lung disease, chronic thromboembolic PH, and arrhythmogenic RV cardiomyopathy.6,7 Consequently, animal studies have focused on the protective role of estrogen in mediating this sex difference. Early research emphasized estrogen’s effects on pulmonary vascular remodeling, treating RV adaptation as secondary. More recently, attention has shifted toward estrogen’s direct impact on the RV myocardium.

In animal models of PH due to pulmonary vascular disease (PVD), female RVs demonstrate enhanced adaptation to increased afterload through improved contractility. In contrast, male models show maladaptive remodeling, more severe RV dysfunction, and higher mortality.7 Rodent studies suggest that estrogen enhances RV function by boosting contractility,8–10 preserving mitochondrial content and oxidative capacity,11 and reducing cardiac fibrosis.12–14 However, the precise mechanisms underlying estrogen-mediated protection against maladaptive RV remodeling remain incompletely understood.

Studies comparing ovariectomized and ovary-intact or E2 (17β-estradiol)-replete female animal models have shown that estradiol confers protective effects against LV pressure-overload remodeling.15,16 Using the transverse aortic constriction mouse model, we previously demonstrated that non-nuclear ERα (estradiol/estrogen receptor alpha) signaling activates the NO/GC1 (soluble guanylyl cyclase)/cGMP pathway. GC1-derived cGMP subsequently activates PKG (cGMP-dependent protein kinase), mitigating pathological LV remodeling in female mice.15,17,18

However, the mechanistic details downstream of E2/ERα/NO/GC1/cGMP cardioprotection have yet to be defined in either the LV or across specific cardiac cell types. Although earlier studies did not explicitly investigate sex differences in LV remodeling, our findings in female transverse aortic constriction mice, together with our previous evidence of reduced NO-sensitivity of GC1 in male transverse aortic constriction mice,19 suggest that myocardial GC1 signaling may be sexually dimorphic.

The role of cardiomyocyte NO/GC1/cGMP signaling in protecting the RV from pressure overload, such as in PH, remains unclear—particularly in the context of sex-specific effects. Fundamental differences in embryological origin, morphology, physiology, and cellular composition between the RV and LV influence their responses to stress. Although GC1 is broadly expressed across cardiovascular cell types, its distribution in the human heart is regionally heterogeneous: highly expressed in the right atrium, RV, and subepicardial LV; and low to absent in the left atrium and subendocardial LV.20 This pattern suggests that cardiomyocyte GC1 may play a more prominent role in RV remodeling under pressure overload than in the LV. However, the functional relevance, underlying mechanisms, and potential sex-specific nature of GC1-mediated protection in the RV remain to be elucidated.

In this study, we hypothesized that cardiomyocyte GC1 regulates RV remodeling in response to chronic pressure overload in a female-specific manner. Using transgenic mice with conditional, cardiomyocyte-specific GC1 knockout (cKO) and their littermates with intact GC1 (control), we investigated the role of cardiomyocyte GC1 in distinguishing adaptive from maladaptive RV remodeling in both sexes. Through integrated in vivo, in vitro, and single-nucleus RNA sequence (snRNA-seq) analyses of murine RV tissue and isolated cardiomyocytes, we found that cardiomyocyte GC1 is central for adaptive RV remodeling in females. Specifically, our data indicate that cardiomyocyte GC1 is necessary for estrogen-dependent, proangiogenic VEGF-VEGFRsignaling between cardiomyocytes and cardiac endothelial cells. This pathway counters capillary rarefaction in the pressure-overloaded RV, preserving microvascular support for the hypertrophied RV and promoting adaptive RV remodeling.

Animals

Animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Columbia University and conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animal by the US National Institutes of Health. Transgenic mice carrying Gucy1b1 with floxed exon 10 (Gucy1b1fl/fl)21 were crossed with a tamoxifen-inducible cardiomyocyte-specific Cre mouse line (αMyHC-CreERT2+/−). To generate cKO and controls, Gucy1b1fl/fl/αMyHC-CreERT2+/− mice and their Gucy1b1fl/fl/αMyHC-CreERT2−/− littermates were fed tamoxifen (100 mg/kg per day) in soft rodent chow (Transgenic Dough Diet, Bio-Serv) daily for a cumulative intake of 500 mg/kg. Both male and female, control and cKO mice were used for experiments. Mice were housed in cages in a temperature-controlled room (23 °C and 40% to 60% humidity) with a 12-hour light-dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water.

Experimental Design

Two weeks after completing the tamoxifen regimen, mice (age, 10–12 weeks) were subjected to pulmonary artery banding (PAB) or Sham surgery, as previously described.22 A sub-cohort of females underwent ovariectomy at age 8 to 10 weeks, hence before PAB or Sham.

Analyses

At 6-week postsurgery, all animals underwent 2 or more of the following end-point procedures—echocardiography, treadmill testing, RV pressure-volume loop recording, morphometric analysis, tissue collection, perfusion-fixation, or cardiomyocyte isolation. Other ex vivo and in vitro experiments examined: large vessel function and effective NO-bioavailability (wire myography of aortic rings), transcript and protein expression (RT quantitative polymerase chain reaction, western blots), protein oxidation (redox westerns), ventricular and cardiomyocyte remodeling (histology, immunohistochemistry), GC1 NO sensitivity (cGMP ELISA), oxidative stress and NO-bioavailability (SOD [superoxide dismutase] activity, nitrate/nitrite), serum E2 (EIA), proangiogenic activity (endothelial cell migration), cellular secretion of VEGF-A (ELISA), and intercellular signaling (single-nucleus RNA sequencing analysis).

Results

Reduced Cardiomyocyte GC1 Expression and Attenuated NO-Inducible cGMP Production Confirmed Successful Cardiomyocyte-Specific GC1 Knockdown in Transgenic Mice

cKO and littermate controls were generated by crossing hemizygous αMyHC-CreERT2+/− with exon 10-floxed Gucy1bfl/fl mice21 and administering tamoxifen to the progeny at 7 to 8 weeks of age (Figure 1A). Cardiomyocytes isolated from cKO exhibited a 30% to 40% reduction in expression of the GC1 β1 subunit, which contains the cyclase domain, compared with control cardiomyocytes (Figure 1B and 1C). To confirm cell type-specificity, GC1 β1expression in lung tissue was assessed (Figure 1D). No statistically significant difference was found between cKO and control lungs (Figure 1E). Furthermore, NO-stimulated GC1 activity was significantly reduced in cKO cardiomyocytes (Figure 1F). No sex differences were observed in GC1 expression or NO-inducible cyclase activity in cardiomyocytes.

Cardiomyocyte GC1 Signaling Adapts the RV Response to Chronic Pressure-Overload in a Sex-Specific Manner, Promoting Adaptive Remodeling in Females But Not Males

Adult female and male, cKO and control mice underwent PAB or Sham. At 6-week postsurgery, sexual dimorphism in RV functional remodeling was evident in control-PAB: females exhibited adaptive remodeling with preserved RV function, whereas males developed maladaptive RV remodeling, with reduced RV function as indicated by decreased tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), RV fractional area change (FAC), and RV systolic excursion velocity (S′) on echocardiography. These sex differences were absent in cKO-PAB. Like male control-PAB, cKO-PAB of both sexes developed severe RV dysfunction (Figure 2A through 2C; Figure S1A). Hepatic congestion, reflected by increased liver weight-to-tibia length ratio, further supported the presence of RV failure in PAB groups with maladaptively remodeled RV (Figure 2D and 2E). LV systolic function remained normal across all groups (Figure 2B and 2C).

Cardiomyocyte GC1 deficiency also abolished sex differences in RV structural remodeling. Male control-PAB exhibited more pronounced RV hypertrophy, evidenced by elevated Fulton index and RV/tibia length ratio compared with female control PAB. In contrast, both male and female cKO-PAB developed equally severe pathological RV structural remodeling, like male control-PAB.

These RV remodeling patterns were further distinguished by reactivation of the fetal gene program, a transcriptomic signature of changes in metabolism, contractility, and electrical stability in the maladaptive, failing adult heart. In male control-PAB and both sexes of cKO-PAB, the RV showed increased Nppb expression, elevated Myh7/Myh6 ratio, and reduced Serca2 expression compared with Sham. Fetal gene reactivation was absent in female control-PAB, consistent with adaptive RV remodeling (Figure S2).

In Female Mice, Cardiomyocyte GC1 Deficiency Impaired the Ability of the Pressure-Overloaded RV to generate Sufficient Contractility, Resulting in RV-PA Uncoupling

To assess RV mechanics, we measured pressure-volume relationships under conditions of varying preload and measured cardiac output via an ultrasonic probe. We determined the maximal RV pressure changes during isovolumic contraction (dP/dtmax normalized to instantaneous pressure), load-independent contractility (end-systolic elastance [Ees]), pulmonary arterial elastance (Ea), RV-pulmonary artery (PA) coupling ratio (Ees/Ea), and RV ejection fraction (EF) at consistent heart rates (500–600 bpm). No statistically significant differences in RV contractile function were detected among Sham mice (Figure 3A through 3D). Compared with Sham, PAB mice had higher pulmonary Ea and maximal RV pressure (Pmax). The severity of RV pressure overload did not differ across PAB groups (Figure S1B and S1C).

Although PAB impaired RV contractility in all mice, the extent varied by sex and cardiomyocyte GC1 status (Figure 3E and 3F). Male PAB, regardless of cardiomyocyte GC1 status, showed diminished RV contractility, RV-PA uncoupling, and reduced RVEF compared with male Sham. In contrast, female control-PAB maintained Ees/Ea within normal range (Ees/Ea, 0.7–0.8).23 Female control-PAB had preserved RV-PA coupling, maintaining higher RV contractility than female cKO-PAB (Ees/Ea: 0.734±0.07 versus 0.498±0.04; P=3.9×10–3). Female cKO-PAB, however, showed RV-PA uncoupling and reduced contractile performance—paralleling the hemodynamic decompensation seen in male PAB (Figure 3E and 3F). The low Ees/Ea values for male PAB and female cKO-PAB fell within the range reported for severe RV dysfunction in other murine RV pressure-overload models (Ees/Ea, 0.35–0.53).23,24

Cardiomyocyte GC1 Deficiency Decreased Exercise Tolerance and Reduced Survival in Female, But Not Male, PAB Mice

The differences in RV contractility across all groups were also reflected by their exercise tolerance. On treadmill testing, PAB running distances were reduced compared with Sham, regardless of sex (Figure 3G). However, among PAB mice, a sex-specific effect of cardiomyocyte GC1 deficiency emerged: female cKO-PAB ran significantly shorter distances than female control-PAB, whereas male PAB showed poor performance regardless of cardiomyocyte GC1 status (Figure 3H). Survival at 6-week post-PAB mirrored this pattern. Female control-PAB had the highest survival, whereas female cKO-PAB and male PAB exhibited poor survival (Figure 3I). In male PAB, cardiomyocyte GC1 deficiency did not further impact exercise capacity or survival, both of which were already compromised.

Cardiomyocyte GC1-Mediated Adaptive RV Remodeling in Response to Pressure-Overload Is Estrogen-Dependent

To assess the role of estrogen, female control and cKO mice underwent ovariectomy at 6 to 8 weeks of age, followed by PAB 2 weeks later. Ovariectomized and ovary-intact females were compared at 6 weeks post-PAB. Ovariectomy induced marked uterine atrophy in both control and cKO (Figure 4A and 4B). Circulating E2 levels did not differ between ovary-intact control and cKO females but dropped precipitously in ovariectomized females (Figure 4B). RV Pmax and pulmonary artery Ea were normal in Sham and elevated in PAB females (Figure 4C and 4D). No statistically significant difference in RV Pmax was detected between ovariectomized and ovary-intact PAB (Figure 4E). Despite comparable RV overload, ovary-intact control-PAB showed adaptive RV remodeling, while ovariectomized control-PAB demonstrated maladaptive RV remodeling and hepatic congestion (Figure S3). RV dysfunction in ovariectomized control-PAB was indistinguishable from that in cKO-PAB females, indicating that estrogen is necessary for cardiomyocyte GC1-mediated cardioprotection (Figure 4E). Ovariectomy had no additional effect on the already impaired RV function of female cKO-PAB (Figure 4E). Exercise treadmill testing further supported these findings: ovariectomized control-PAB females had reduced running capacity compared with both Sham counterparts and ovary-intact control-PAB (Figure S3), reinforcing that cardiomyocyte GC1-dependent cardioprotection against RV pressure-overload is estrogen-regulated.

RV Pressure-Overload Induced Oxidative Stress Is Independent of Sex or Cardiomyocyte GC1 Status

Since oxidative stress and diminished NO-bioavailability blunt GC1 activity, we assessed whether sex- and cardiomyocyte GC1-dependent differences existed in these regulatory factors. Oxidative stress was evaluated by redox western blots measuring the oxidized/reduced forms of mitochondrial proteins Trx2 (thioredoxin-2) and CypD (cyclophilin D) in RV and liver tissues (Figures S9 and S10). RV oxidation ratios increased significantly in male PAB relative to Sham and did not differ between control and cKO. In females, cardiomyocyte GC1 had no significant effect on cardiac or systemic oxidative stress response to RV pressure-overload. A nonsignificant trend towards a potential cardiomyocyte GC1-mediated antioxidant action was detected in the pressure-overloaded RV (oxidation ratio: Trx2 PcKO, 0.09; CypD PcKO, 0.06). SOD activity in RV tissue supported these findings (Figure S7A). In contrast, liver oxidation ratios were increased in PAB groups, independent of sex or cardiomyocyte GC1 status.

Cardiomyocyte GC1 Is Necessary for Female-Specific Preservation of Cardiac NO-Bioavailability in Response to RV Pressure-Overload

To evaluate cardiac NO-bioavailability, we measured nitrate/nitrite in RV tissue by ELISA. Baseline RV nitrate/nitrite levels of Sham mice had no statistically significant differences, by either sex or cardiomyocyte GC1 status. However, nitrate/nitrite levels declined in all PAB except female control-PAB (Figure S7B), supporting a female-specific, cardiomyocyte GC1-dependent preservation of NO-bioavailability despite RV pressure overload.

Systemic NO-bioavailability was assessed via wire myography of thoracic aortic rings (Figure S8A through S8D). Acetylcholine (ACh)-induced vasorelaxation and its inhibition by NG-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME) showed no statistically significant differences across control versus cKO or Sham versus PAB (Figure S8B and S8C). However, females consistently exhibited greater ACh-induced vasorelaxation than males (Figure S8D), consistent with higher systemic NO-bioavailability in women.25

In Female PAB Mice, Cardiomyocyte GC1 Deficiency Resulted in More Severe Cardiomyocyte Hypertrophy, More Extensive Interstitial Fibrosis, and Worse Capillary Rarefaction

Cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, and capillary rarefaction are histological hallmarks of the dysfunctional, pressure-overloaded RV.26 We evaluated sex differences in these responses and the impact of cardiomyocyte GC1 deficiency (Figure 5D through 5F; Figure S4). Male control-, male cKO-, female cKO-, and ovariectomized female control-PAB all showed marked cardiomyocyte hypertrophy (increased cross-sectional area), extensive fibrosis, reduced capillary density, and abnormally low capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratios. In contrast, female control-PAB exhibited mild remodeling. Like Sham, they maintained capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratios >1 (Figure 5F; Figure S4B). RV capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratios were below 1 in all the failing, maladaptively remodeled RVs—those of male control-, male cKO-, female cKO-, and ovariectomized female control-PAB. These findings were consistent with data from healthy humans and other species, where normal ratios range from 1.0 to 2.0.27,28 Abnormally low capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratios of maladaptively remodeled RVs indicated that the microvasculature was insufficient to support the oxygen demands of hypertrophied cardiomyocytes. These data indicate that preservation of cardiac microvascular homeostasis in the pressure-overloaded RV depends on estrogen-regulated cardiomyocyte GC1 signaling (Figure S6).

Cardiomyocyte GC1 Is Vital to Proangiogenic, More So Than to Antihypertrophic or Antifibrotic, Cell-Cell Communication in the Pressure-Overloaded RV

Although GC1 stimulation has shown anti-hypertrophic and antifibrotic effects in animal models of LV dysfunction and in vitro studies,29–31 the specific contribution of cardiomyocyte GC1 to these protective mechanisms has not been directly examined. To investigate how cardiomyocyte GC1 influences RV histopathologic remodeling, we analyzed cell-cell communication involved in hypertrophy, fibrosis, and angiogenesis using snRNA-seq on RV tissue from a pilot cohort of control and cKO, Sham and PAB, female mice. After quality control filtering, unsupervised clustering revealed 8 distinct cell types with similar distributions across genotypes (Figure 6A and 6B). Using CellChat, we identified cardiac cell type expression of ligand-receptor pairs that drive hypertrophic, fibrotic, and angiogenic signaling. From this data, we inferred putative cell-cell paracrine signaling pathways in the RV of the different experimental groups. Cardiomyocyte GC1 deficiency did not alter canonical intercellular signaling pathways related to hypertrophy or fibrosis. Instead, the most pronounced effect was on angiogenic signaling—specifically, interactions of Vegf and Vegfr (ie, Flt1, Kdr, Flt4) between cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells (Figure 6C). Low-level Vegf-Vegfr signaling between cardiomyocytes and other nonendothelial, noncardiomyocyte cardiac cells was also detected in control PAB RVs. These low-level Vegf-Vegfr intercellular signaling were subthreshold in cKO-PAB RVs.

Capillary-to-Cardiomyocyte Ratio Is Strongly and Positively Correlated to RV Function

To corroborate the role of proangiogenic signaling in preserving the function of the pressure-overloaded RV, we assessed correlations between RV dysfunction and the key histopathologic features of maladaptive RV remodeling: cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and capillary rarefaction. We performed Pearson correlation analysis using paired histological and echocardiographic data from all study groups, across both sexes and surgical conditions. Echocardiographic measurements of RV function were compared with cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area, extent of fibrosis (collagen content as a percentage of RV cross-sectional area), capillary density, and capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratio (Figure S5). Among these, the capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratio showed the strongest association with RV function (R2, 0.87 [95% CI, 0.84–0.97]). These findings support the conclusion that in females, cardiomyocyte GC1 signaling preserves RV function by blunting capillary rarefaction and ensuring sufficient energy delivery to hypertrophied cardiomyocytes.

Cardiomyocyte GC1 Deficiency Altered Expression of Angiogenesis-Related Genes in Female, But Not Male, PAB Mice

Because snRNA-seq analysis captures only nuclear RNA, we also assessed whole-tissue transcriptional responses to RV pressure-overload, focusing on genes associated with angiogenesis and fibrosis across all experimental groups to identify sex- and cardiomyocyte GC1-dependent effects (Figure 7A and 7B). Differential RV expression of Vegfa (vascular endothelial growth factor A), Ppargc1a (PPARG coactivator 1 alpha), Ctgf (connective tissue growth factor), Tgfb1 (transforming growth factor β1), and Il6 (interleukin 6) aligned with our histological findings on capillary density and interstitial fibrosis (Figure 5). Female control PAB mice and Sham displayed RV angiogenic and fibrotic gene profiles consistent with preserved microvasculature and minimal fibrosis (Figure 7A). In contrast, female cKO-PAB showed impaired angiogenic and increased profibrotic gene expression, resembling the maladaptive profile observed in male PAB mice. Male PAB mice, regardless of cardiomyocyte GC1 status, exhibited downregulated Vegfa and upregulated Il6, Ctgf, and Tgfb1, indicating impaired angiogenesis and increased fibrosis (Figure 7B). These data further support a female-specific GC1-dependent mechanism underlying RV adaptation to pressure overload.

Cardiomyocyte GC1 Mediates Estrogen-Induced VEGF-A Production and Endothelial Cell Migration

Our in vivo and snRNA-seq data suggested that both estrogen and cardiomyocyte GC1 are necessary for promicroangiogenic/anti-capillary rarefaction signaling between cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells. To test this, we cocultured human umbilical vein endothelial cells with cardiomyocytes isolated from female control or cKO mice and assessed endothelial cell migration in response to estradiol stimulation. In co-cultures of endothelial cells and control cardiomyocytes, estradiol increased cardiomyocyte Vegfa expression and increased VEGF-A secretion, promoting endothelial cells' migration towards the cardiomyocytes (Figure 8A and 8B). These effects of estradiol, however, were absent in co-cultures with cKO cardiomyocytes.

To determine whether cardiomyocytes require endothelial cell interaction for estradiol-induced VEGF-A secretion, we cultured isolated control versus cKO cardiomyocytes, with and without E2 stimulation. In monoculture, neither group showed changes in cardiomyocyte Vegfa expression nor media VEGF-A content in response to estradiol (Figure 8C and 8D). These results from cardiomyocyte mono-cultures and cardiomyocyte-endothelial cell co-cultures indicate that cardiomyocyte GC1 is essential for E2-stimulated, VEGF-A-mediated signaling between cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells, highlighting its importance to pro-angiogenic/anti-rarefaction signaling in the pressure-overloaded RV.

Discussion

This study identifies cardiomyocyte GC1-mediated protection against capillary rarefaction as a novel mechanism of estrogen-driven cardioprotection during RV pressure-overload. Specifically, estrogen and cardiomyocyte GC1 preserved the cardiac microvasculature necessary to support the hypertrophied RV, maintaining a normal capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratio and preserving RV-PA coupling. Only ovary-intact, female control-PAB maintained both parameters under pressure-overload. In contrast, ovariectomized females, cardiomyocyte GC1-deficient females, and males—regardless of their cardiomyocyte GC1 function—exhibited low capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratios and RV-PA uncoupling at 6 wks after PAB.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a mechanism by which estrogen protects against severe capillary rarefaction in the pressure-overloaded RV, promoting adaptive RV pressure-overload remodeling in females. Although marked reduction of capillary density is a well-established histopathologic hallmark of RV failure, its mechanisms and sex-specific aspects are poorly understood and underexplored compared with hypertrophy and fibrosis. Our findings help provide a mechanistic basis for sex differences in RV capillary rarefaction and its link to RV failure severity. They also offer new insights into (1) sex-specific cardiovascular pathophysiology, (2) the role of cardiomyocyte GC1 signaling, and (3) the relative contribution of hallmark cardiac histopathologic features to RV dysfunction.

Our discovery of estrogen-dependent, cardiomyocyte GC1-mediated proangiogenic signaling between cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells offers a mechanistic framework for sex differences in cardiovascular disease, particularly in organs with high GC1 expression, such as the lungs, kidneys, and vasculature. Myography studies revealed greater systemic NO-bioavailability and enhanced vasorelaxation in females compared with males, aligning with the lower incidence and severity of hypertension in women compared with men.32 These findings suggest that estrogen-regulated signaling between endothelial cells and vascular smooth muscle cells contributes to sex-specific vascular function. Although this broader intercellular signaling landscape warrants further investigation, it lies beyond the scope of the current study.

Our findings also challenge the traditional view that cardiomyocyte GC1 functions solely through intracellular mechanisms. Historically, GC1/cGMP signaling in cardiomyocytes has been associated with antihypertrophic effects, attenuation of adrenergic contractility, and infarct size limitation during ischemic postcondition.33–36 These conclusions have largely been based on in vitro studies or indirect pharmacological manipulation. Beyond infarct protection,34 little is known about in vivo cardiomyocyte GC1-mediated cardioprotection.

To explore potential contributing factors to the female-specific findings of cardiomyocyte GC1 cardioprotection, we also examined oxidative stress and NO bioavailability in the RV. Redox western analysis showed increased oxidative stress in PAB males, regardless of cardiomyocyte GC1 status, and in female cKO-PAB but not female control-PAB. This could suggest estrogen-dependent cardiomyocyte GC1-mediated antioxidant action. No statistically significant differences were detected in the oxidation ratios of Trx2 and CypD between control- and cKO-PAB females, nor between male and female control-PAB. In contrast, female control-PAB clearly demonstrated cardiomyocyte GC1-dependent preservation of RV NO-bioavailability, with nitrate/nitrite levels like Sham and higher than female cKO-PAB and male PAB. Furthermore, systemic NO bioavailability was consistently higher for females than for males. These results are consistent with the paradigm that higher NO-bioavailability is a factor that underlies the generally improved cardiovascular outcomes of women compared with men.

The novelty of our study lies in its discovery that cardiomyocyte GC1 plays a role in microvascular remodeling in the pressure-overloaded RV. Although capillary rarefaction and reactive microangiogenesis are widely assumed to regulate cardiac adaptation to chronic pressure-overload,37 direct experimental support—especially in RV dysfunction—is limited. We assessed 3 classic histopathologic features of RV remodeling: cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and capillary rarefaction, and correlated these with RV function across all. Among them, the capillary-to-cardiomyocyte ratio showed the strongest correlation with RV performance, underscoring the critical role of cardiac microvascular homeostasis in preserving RV function under pressure-overload.

To date, studies of RV pressure-overload remodeling have primarily focused on myocardial fibrosis. We and others have identified abnormal extracellular matrix remodeling as a key feature of maladaptive RV pressure-overload remodeling.14,38 Using a rat PAB model, Jurida et al reported a transcriptomic signature of RV failure, in which gene expression levels—primarily extracellular matrix-related—correlated with RV dysfunction severity. This suggests that dysfunctional extracellular matrix remodeling is a major driver of RV failure. Their study end point (22–26 weeks post-PAB) captured a late disease stage, when maladaptive RV remodeling is well established in both sexes. In contrast, our study was designed to investigate sex differences during earlier remodeling stages, when females still exhibit adaptive RV remodeling. We previously identified extracellular matrix instability as a mechanism of maladaptive RV remodeling in male PAB mice. However, that study did not assess capillary rarefaction. Thus, our current findings are not in conflict with either Jurida et al or our own earlier study.

The limited number of animal studies addressing capillary rarefaction in RV remodeling may stem from the constraints of common PH models. In these models, PVD is induced via chronic hypoxia, VEGF inhibition, or pulmonary vascular injury—interventions that inherently promote cardiac inflammation, myocardial injury, or impaired angiogenesis.39,40 The confounders complicate the interpretation of microvascular remodeling in the RV. By using the PAB model, we avoided these limitations and isolated the effects of pressure-overload on RV structure and function.

Our study differs significantly from prior rodent PAB studies investigating sex-specific RV remodeling.12,41,42 Unlike studies using wild-type or constitutive knockout models, we used an inducible, cardiomyocyte-specific GC1 knockout, allowing precise interrogation of a single signaling pathway with both cell-type and temporal specificity. Our 6-week PAB duration exceeds that of most rodent PAB studies, which typically assess remodeling after 1 to 3 weeks of RV pressure-overload. In addition to comparing male and female mice, we included ovariectomized females to directly assess the role of estrogen in cardiomyocyte GC1-mediated cardioprotection against maladaptive RV remodeling. Our evaluation extended beyond traditional histopathologic markers—cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis—to include capillary rarefaction, a critical but often overlooked aspect of RV pathology. Moreover, by analyzing RV pressure-volume relationships rather than pressure alone, we quantified RV-PA coupling via the Ees/Ea ratio. Importantly, Ees/Ea distinguishes adaptive from maladaptive remodeling. In mice, normal RV function corresponds to an Ees/Ea range of 0.7 to 0.8, whereas values of 0.35 to 0.53 indicate severe RV dysfunction with RV-PA uncoupling and failure.23,24 Thus, our inclusion of pressure-volume loop analysis provided a more physiologically relevant assessment of RV adaptation to pressure-overload.

Recent studies using global knockouts and transgenic models have begun to elucidate estradiol-stimulated pathways that protect females from maladaptive RV pressure-overload remodeling. Cheng et al showed that female—but not male—rats lacking ERα lost cardioprotection against RV fibrosis, diastolic dysfunction, and RV-PA uncoupling following PAB.42 Frump et al further identified an estradiol/ERα/BMPR2 (bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 2)/apelin signaling axis conferring RV protection in various PVD-PH models.9 Apelin has demonstrated acute procontractile, proangiogenic, antiapoptotic, and vasoactive properties in other systems.43,44 However, whether apelin protects the RV primarily through myocardial or vascular effects remains unclear. Nevertheless, our findings align with and extend this estradiol/ERα/BMPR2/apelin framework, offering new insight into estrogen-mediated protection against RV pressure overload.

Study Limitations

The use of a single animal model may be viewed as a limitation; however, we selected the PAB-induced RV pressure-overload model specifically to avoid confounding factors inherent to PVD-PH models and to focus on myocardial signaling independent of pulmonary vascular remodeling. Most transgenic or knockout PVD-PH models do not develop significant, let alone severe, RV dysfunction.39 Total nitrate/nitrite levels, though commonly used, are an indirect measurement of NO-bioavailability and do not reflect real-time levels of the short-lived and reactive NO molecule. This methodological limitation should be considered when interpreting RV NO-bioavailability. snRNA-seq analysis is inherently limited to nascent nuclear transcripts, as opposed to mature cytoplasmic mRNA. Due to the large size of cardiomyocytes, microfluidics-based approaches require single-nucleus rather than single-cell RNA sequencing. Additionally, nuclear isolation from frozen tissue is well-suited for processing small RV samples. The small size of our snRNA-seq cohort limited traditional differential expression and abundance analyses. We also acknowledge the complexity of VEGF-VEGFR signaling—including diverse receptor subtype interactions, varying ligand affinities, and dimer-specific function, which limits the granularity of ligand-receptor interpretation. However, such complexities would similarly challenge other omics approaches.

Significance in Heart Failure Clinical Trials

The cardioprotective role of cGMP signaling has led to the development of heart failure therapies that enhance cGMP, either directly or indirectly, via GC1 or NPR (natriuretic peptide receptors). However, clinical trials have shown mixed results. The PDE5 (phosphodiesterase 5) inhibitor sildenafil failed to improve outcomes in patients with heart failure with reduced LV ejection fraction,45 whereas the GC1 stimulator vericiguat demonstrated clinical benefit.46 Our findings suggest that these differences may stem from sex-specific effects linked to their mechanisms of action. Sildenafil enhances cGMP by inhibiting its breakdown and thus depends on intact estradiol/ERα/eNOS (endothelial nitric oxide synthase)/NO/GC1 signaling—predominantly active in females—potentially limiting its efficacy in males. In contrast, vericiguat directly stimulates GC1, bypassing this estrogen-dependent pathway and offering sex-independent efficacy. Indeed, post hoc analysis of the vericiguat clinical trials confirmed that its benefit in heart failure with reduced LV ejection fraction was consistent across sexes.47 No such analysis exists for sildenafil, but preclinical studies have shown that cardioprotective effects are estrogen-dependent.48

Summary

Our study provides new insights into estrogen-mediated cardioprotection against maladaptive RV pressure-overload remodeling, the role of cardiomyocyte GC1, and sex-specific differences in capillary rarefaction. Although the precise mechanisms remain to be defined, particularly the PKG phosphorylation targets mediating VEGF-VEGFR signaling between cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells, our findings highlight a novel, estrogen-dependent, proangiogenic signaling axis downstream of cardiomyocyte GC1-derived cGMP. Importantly, these results suggest that estrogen levels may modulate the efficacy of therapies that indirectly enhance GC1/cGMP signaling. Accordingly, patient sex, menopausal status, and estrogen regulation of the therapeutic pathway should be considered in the development, evaluation, and clinical application of emerging treatments for RV dysfunction.
