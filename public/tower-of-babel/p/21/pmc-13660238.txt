Rhizosheath is the key structural element for enhancing the drought resistance in alpine desertified grasslands of Kengyilia hirsuta
Authors: Yutao Yuan, Xiaoxing Wei, Li Wu, Qingping Zhou, Jian Sun, Hui Wang, Chen Chen, Youjun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsTo cope with extreme drought of sandy grassland ecosystems, sand-adapted plants of Kengyilia hirsuta have evolved specialized functional traits for rhizosheath. The majority of studies ignore the natural process of drought-rewatering simulation and lack systematic research on regulatory mechanisms during rhizosheath formation, restricting understanding of the optimal adaptation strategy for sand-adapted plants.MethodsThe research was set up with three drought–rewatering cycles and three sustained drought levels. Rhizosheath weight, root morphology, targeted metabolites, and microbial community structure were measured and analyzed, and the relationships among these variables were examined using multivariate statistics.ResultsUnder continuous drought conditions, maintaining soil moisture at 25% of field capacity (W5) synergistically increased root volume, root tip number, and root hair length, and these changes were significantly positively correlated with the increase in rhizosheath weight. This treatment was also accompanied by the accumulation of stearic acid (C18:0) and the specific enrichment of key microbial taxa, including Myxococcota and Ascomycota, which collectively exhibited a synergistic “morphology–metabolite–microbe” homeostatic optimization association. Under the drought–rewatering cycle regime, rewatering to 25%–40% of field capacity (W2, W3) significantly induced the accumulation of palmitic acid (C16:0) and palmitoleic acid (C16:1N7), and these changes were closely associated with the rapid reassembly of the bacterial community. Proteobacteria were positively correlated with root expansion, Ascomycota were associated with rhizosheath development, and the key fatty acids showed consistency with the directional succession of the microbial community.ConclusionThis study reveals that K. hirsuta adapts to continuous drought and drying–rewetting cycles through two mechanisms: “steady-state resistance” and “plastic adjustments”, which collectively constitute a regulatory network for plant water adaptation, providing a theoretical basis for screening stress‑tolerant forage germplasm and research on drought tolerance mechanisms of forage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09475-w.Keywords: Kengyilia hirsuta, Drought-rewatering cycles, Rhizosheath, Rhizosheath-associated fatty acid profile, Microbial Diversity

Introduction

The Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, recognized as the world’s highest and largest plateau, serves as a critical ecological barrier in Asia [1]. However, global climate change is exacerbating regional water fluctuations, with an increasing trend in both the frequency and intensity of alternating cycles between drought and moisture, which, coupled with human activities such as overgrazing, has led to biodiversity loss, grassland productivity decline, and a reduction in ecosystem multifunctionality in this region [2–4]. These changes have drawn widespread attention, driving research to increasingly focus on the stress-resistance adaptation mechanisms of plateau-adapted plants and the identification of plant resources with high stress tolerance, a key approach to mitigating the aforementioned ecological challenges [5, 6].

In the specific habitat of desertified grasslands on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, water availability is the primary factor limiting plant growth. During the seedling stage, sand-adapted plants often face persistent drought stress, accompanied by sporadic but frequent rainfall events. In this context, pioneer-dominated plants have evolved two distinct adaptive mechanisms: resistance to prolonged drought and buffering responses to precipitation events. Under prolonged drought stress, plants enhance water uptake by promoting root elongation, increasing root hair density, and strengthening root structures [7, 8]. Concurrently, the composition of root exudates undergoes significant changes, manifesting as decreased secretion rates of total carbon, sugars, and amino acids, and an increased proportion of organic acids [9]. This subsequently affects the carbon supply for microorganisms [9, 10] and triggers adjustments in the rhizosphere soil microbial community structure: bacterial diversity decreases, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria declines, while that of Actinobacteria increases [11–13]. In contrast, plants exhibit stronger phenotypic plasticity and resilience under alternating drought and rewatering cycles than under continuous drought. Following rewatering, root growth rapidly recovers, and both root hair density and root activity significantly increase, facilitating the rapid uptake of water and nutrients [14]. Simultaneously, the root secretion pattern shifts, with enhanced release of sugars and amino acids that provide carbon and nitrogen sources for microbial colonization [9, 15]. This temporal adjustment of root exudates specifically recruits beneficial microbial taxa and promotes their rapid colonization in the rhizosphere [16]. Notably, Pseudomonas has been identified as a key genus promoting rhizosheath formation [17]. In contrast, fungal community structure exhibits less change, with relatively high abundances of Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, indicating stronger drought stability [18]. The preliminary consensus regarding the interactions among plant root morphology, secretions, and microorganisms is that root morphology shapes the structure of the rhizosphere microbial community by regulating the release of exudate [8, 19]. Root exudates involved in nutrient uptake and transport are a key driver shaping the structure and function of the rhizosphere microbial community [20]. They act as signalling molecules and nutrient sources and drive microbial involvement in soil nutrient mobilisation [21] and indirectly influence plant drought resistance and soil quality [22]. However, current research in this field exhibits notable limitations. First, most studies have focused exclusively on single stress scenarios, such as continuous drought or a single rewatering event, resulting in a lack of direct evidence regarding the effects of dynamic drought–rewatering alternations on rhizosheath development and functional regulation. Second, investigations into the rhizosheath are often confined to isolated analyses of morphological traits, root exudate components, or microbial communities, with a pronounced absence of an integrated evidence chain elucidating the synergistic interactions among root architecture, exudates, and microorganisms. Third, although the academic community has recognized that plants exhibit persistent adaptive responses to prior drought and other stresses, most related research remains largely at the conceptual level. There is a critical lack of multi-perspective, quantifiable indicator systems and analytical frameworks that integrate metabolic profiles, key microbial taxa, and root structural traits to explore the underlying mechanistic pathways.

The rhizosheath is an integral adaptation developed by plant species in sandy or desertified grasslands to cope with arid environments, playing a critical role in plant drought resistance by optimizing water uptake, enriching beneficial microbes, and enhancing soil aggregation [17, 23]. Its formation is significantly regulated by water conditions [24, 25]. For instance, in soil at 20–30% field capacity, the rhizosheath biomass of K. hirsuta peaks [26]. Under these conditions, ABA-mediated increases in root and root hair length serve as reliable indicators of rhizosheath size [27–29]. Similarly, viscous polysaccharides and long-chain fatty acids secreted by roots and released via root hairs enhance soil particle cohesion, promoting rhizosheath [27, 30]. Long-chain fatty acids not only participate in the biochemical cementation process of the rhizosheath structure but also act as key signaling molecules that regulate the colonization and interactions of rhizosphere microorganisms [31]. Their composition and concentration dynamics directly govern the assembly efficiency of the rhizosheath micro-ecosystem. Consequently, elucidating the functional roles of rhizosheath-associated fatty acid profile through metabolomics has thus emerged as a critical approach to unraveling the underlying mechanisms. The core mechanism of the rhizosheath micro-ecology stems from the interaction between rhizosheath-associated fatty acid profile and microbes [32]. Under drought stress, plants actively recruit beneficial microorganisms (e.g., Bacillus, Aeromonas, and Tsukamurella) by adjusting rhizosheath-associated fatty acid profile, forming mutualistic relationships to enhance stress resistance [20] – a process consistent with the ‘cry for help’ hypothesis [33]. Studies have also found that genera such as Citrobacter and Acinetobacter become locally dominant in the rhizosheaths of crops such as sorghum and barley, thereby improving water and phosphorus uptake efficiency through synergistic optimisation of the rhizosheath structure [33]. Plants adapt to their habitats through phenotypic plasticity, an adaptive process that is underpinned by an integrated system involving root architecture, exudates, and soil microorganisms, and is further shaped by continuous interactions with the environment to form a type of adaptive “stress legacy” [34]. Furthermore, compared to sustained drought, “drought-rewatering” cycles are more effective in strengthening this legacy effect [35–37]. This finding has important implications for understanding the survival mechanisms of pioneer species in the face of climatic fluctuations. While existing studies have revealed various aspects of rhizosheath formation and function, systematic, integrated research on its multidimensional regulatory mechanisms remains lacking.

K. hirsuta is a pioneer forage grass in alpine desertified habitats of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau [38] and is characterized by its ability to form a rhizosheath, a structure comprising rhizosheath-associated fatty acid profile cemented with soil particles into a sheath-like complex [39]. As a specific micro-domain within the rhizosphere, the rhizosheath is considered a potential key trait for achieving sustainable agriculture in the future [23]. Existing research primarily focuses on static responses under continuous drought, paying insufficient attention to the dynamic patterns of metabolism and microbial communities during drought–rewatering cycles. There is also a lack of in-depth analysis of the specific microbial communities within the rhizosheath and their functions. Therefore, our research used K. hirsuta as the experimental material and, through simulated continuous drought and drought‑rewatering treatments, systematically elucidated the coordinated mechanisms by which this species adapts, from coping with short-term fluctuations to enduring long-term drought. By combining GC-MS targeted metabolomics with 16 S/ITS rRNA amplicon sequencing, we monitored changes in long-chain fatty acids (C:14–C:22) and microbial communities in the rhizosheath soil. We constructed metabolite–microbe interaction networks to identify core microbial groups and metabolic targets involved in rhizosheath formation, systematically revealing the causal mechanisms linking ‘root morphology -rhizosheath-associated fatty acid profile – rhizosheath microbes – ecological function’.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Mature wild seeds of K. hirsuta were collected in September 2022 from WaQie Town, Hongyuan County (33.18° N, 102.62° E; altitude 3490 m), Aba Prefecture, Sichuan Province, China, with prior permission from the Forestry and Grassland Bureau of Hongyuan County (Fig. 1A). The plant species was authoritatively identified by Professor Junliang Yang from the College of Life Sciences, Sichuan Agricultural University. A voucher specimen (voucher number: SAUT 201402232) has been deposited in the Herbarium of the Triticeae Research Institute, Sichuan Agricultural University (SAUT) (Fig. B). The growth substrate consisted of sandy soil with the following physicochemical properties: pH 7.69, organic carbon 6.3 g/kg, total phosphorus 0.35 g/kg, nitrate nitrogen 6.68 mg/kg, ammonium nitrogen 6.69 mg/kg, and a maximum field capacity of 23%. Before use, the soil was air-dried naturally and sieved through a 4 mm mesh.

Overview of experimental materials. (A) Natural habitat, (B) Whole‑plant morphology, (C) Rhizosheath phenotypes of K. hirsuta under contrasting water regimes

Experimental design

Mature, plump seeds of K. hirsuta with uniform morphology were selected. To break dormancy, seeds were stratified at 4 °C for 48 h, then surface-sterilized with 75% (v/v) ethanol for 30 s and rinsed three times with sterile water. The sterilised seeds were placed on moist filter paper in Petri dishes and germinated in a controlled climate chamber (photoperiod 16 h/8 h, day/night temperature 24 °C/20°C, light intensity 5000 lx, relative humidity 50%–60%). When the radicles reached approximately 3 cm in length, uniformly grown seedlings were selected and transplanted into pots (10 cm × 7 cm × 8.5 cm). Each pot, filled with 0.5 kg of prepared sandy soil, contained five seedlings, for a total of 120 pots. Six water treatments were established, with 20 pots initially assigned to each treatment; all pots were independent experimental units. Three key destructive sampling periods (T1, T2, T3) were set according to the experimental timeline, corresponding to day 7, day 14, and day 21, respectively. At each of these time points, sampling was conducted after a 24‑hour rewatering period (for the drought–rewatering treatments) or at the corresponding time for the continuous drought treatments. At T1 and T2, four independent pots per treatment were randomly selected for the determination of root morphology and rhizosheath biomass. At T3, an additional four independent pots per treatment were randomly chosen for the final measurement of root morphology and other indicators, while another six independent pots (one seedling per pot) were simultaneously selected specifically for the metabolomic and microbial sequencing analyses of rhizosheath soil. The remaining pots within each treatment were kept as backup materials for the continuous observation of non‑destructive indicators such as plant height and survival rate, ensuring the completeness and reliability of the experimental data. All sampling was destructive and mutually independent; samples collected at different time points and for different analytical endpoints originated from distinct pots, thereby completely avoiding pseudoreplication.

Water treatments commenced at the three-leaf stage. The following six treatments were established: Periodic drought-rewatering group (7-day cycle: within each 7-day cycle, plants were subjected to drought for the first 6 days, rewatered to the target water content on the 7th day and maintained for 24 h, after which they entered the next drought cycle): W1 (rewatered to 10% of FC), W2 (rewatered to 25% FC), W3 (rewatered to 40% of FC). Continuous Drought Group (maintained at constant daily moisture): W4 (maintained at 10% of FC), W5 (maintained at 25% FC), W6 (maintained at 40% of FC). Soil water content was maintained within ± 2% of the target value using the daily weighing method. The entire experiment lasted 21 days (equivalent to three complete drought-rewatering cycles for the periodic drought-rewatering group), with four biological replicates per treatment. The 21-day treatment period used in this study was determined based on preliminary experiments and relevant literature [40]. This duration covers the critical developmental window of rhizosheath formation to structural stabilization in K. hirsuta at the seedling stage, and also represents the period when plant roots are most sensitive to water stress. Thus, it enables effective dissection of the core regulatory mechanisms underlying rhizosheath construction.

Measurements and analytical methods

Rhizosheath biomass quantification

Rhizosheath biomass was determined using a combination of mechanical vibration and ultrasonic separation. Entire plants were carefully excavated, and the root systems were gently shaken to remove loosely attached soil, preserving the intact rhizosheath structure. The roots were then excised, and the fresh weight of the root-sheath complex (W₁) was recorded. Subsequently, the roots were immersed in deionised water and subjected to ultrasonic treatment (40 kHz, 30 min) to completely dislodge the rhizosheath. After blotting the roots dry with absorbent paper, weigh them again to obtain the root fresh weight (W₂). Rhizosheath biomass was calculated as: rhizosheath weight (mg·cm⁻¹) = (W₁ – W₂) / Total root length. Total root length was quantified using a root scanner (EPSON Expression 12000XL, 600 dpi) coupled with WinRHIZO Pro 2017a software.

Root metabolite extraction and GC-MS analysis

At the final sampling period (T3), six plants per treatment (obtained from six independent pots, one plant per pot) were randomly selected for the collection of rhizosheath soil. Intact plants of K. hirsuta were gently shaken until no large soil aggregates detached from the root surface. The soil tightly adhering to the roots was then carefully brushed off, collected into sterile 5 mL EP tubes, and immediately stored at -80 °C for subsequent targeted metabolomic analysis of medium- and long-chain fatty acids. Metabolite extraction was performed at 4 °C. An appropriate amount of sample was homogenised with 5 mL of dichloromethane-methanol solution (2:1, v/v), vortexed, and then washed with 2 mL of ultrapure water. The lower organic layer was collected and dried under a stream of nitrogen gas. The residue was redissolved in 2 mL of n-hexane containing an internal standard and subjected to methylation for 0.5 h. After adding 2 mL of ultrapure water, 2000 µL of the upper supernatant was collected, dried under nitrogen, reconstituted in n-hexane, and transferred to a vial for GC-MS analysis. The injection volume was 1 µL with a split ratio of 10:1. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a capillary column (Agilent 19091 S–433UI: HP-5ms, 30 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm). The temperature programme was as follows: initial temperature 80 °C, increased to 180 °C at 20 °C/min, held for 8 min, then raised to 280 °C at 5 °C/min and held for 3 min. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Quality control (QC) samples were inserted at regular intervals throughout the sample sequence to assess system stability and reproducibility. Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using a 5977B MSD mass spectrometer (Agilent). The parameters were set as follows: inlet temperature 280 °C, ion source temperature 230 °C, transfer line temperature 250 °C; electron impact (EI) ionisation source with electron energy 70 eV, operating in SCAN/SIM mode [41].

A combined univariate and multivariate analysis strategy was employed. First, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) combined with FDR multiple testing correction was used to preliminarily screen for metabolites with significant differences among the different water treatments (FDR < 0.05). Subsequently, the Random Forest algorithm was introduced to rank the importance of each metabolite in distinguishing the six water treatments, thereby identifying key metabolic markers. Finally, integrating the results of the univariate statistics and the Random Forest analysis, the core responsive metabolites were determined.

Microbial diversity sequencing

Rhizosheath soil samples for microbial diversity sequencing were also collected at T3 to ensure alignment with the metabolite data. Six uniformly sized K. hirsuta plants were randomly selected, each from an independent pot (one plant per pot), ensuring consistency with the source of the metabolomic samples. Root systems with adhering soil were carefully excavated, and loosely attached bulk soil was gently shaken off. The samples were then placed on sterile white paper on a clean bench. Using sterilized brushes, the tightly adhering rhizosheath soil was brushed off and thoroughly homogenized. Six independent biological replicate samples were collected per treatment and placed into sterile, labeled 5 mL EP tubes. All collected samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently used for microbial diversity sequencing. Total genomic DNA was extracted from samples using the MagPure Soil DNA LQ Kit (Magan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. Qualified samples were stored at − 20°C for subsequent use. Using the extracted DNA as a template, the V3–V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene and the fungal ITS1 region were amplified with barcoded, specific primers and the Takara Ex Taq high-fidelity enzyme. The primers for bacterial amplification were 343F (5’-TACGGRAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 798R (5’-AGGGTATCTAATCCT-3’). The primers for fungal amplification were ITS1F (5’-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3’) and ITS2 (5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3’) [42]. PCR amplicons were checked by agarose gel electrophoresis, purified using AMPure XP beads, and used as templates for a second round of PCR amplification. After further purification with beads, the final products were quantified using Qubit, normalised, and pooled for sequencing. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform using a 600-cycle reagent kit, generating 250-bp paired-end reads. The average sequencing depth per sample was controlled at ≥ 50,000 clean reads for bacteria and ≥ 40,000 clean reads for fungi. Raw reads were quality-filtered and denoised using the DADA2 pipeline in QIIME2 (version 2022.11), with standard quality filtering thresholds (Q ≥ 20, removal of chimeric sequences, and truncation of low-quality terminal ends). Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated at 100% sequence similarity. For taxonomic annotation, bacterial ASVs were aligned against the SILVA 16 S rRNA gene database (version 138.1), and fungal ASVs were compared against the UNITE database (version 8.3). The core microbiome was defined as ASVs present in ≥ 80% of all samples, balancing conservatism and detection bias, and was used for all subsequent community and association analyses. All sequencing procedures were conducted by Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Data statistical analysis

MSD ChemStation software was used to extract chromatographic peak areas and retention times, and the long-chain fatty acid content in the samples was calculated using standard curves. All statistical analyses were based on biological replicates from independent pots. Prior to performing one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA), the normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homoscedasticity (Levene’s test) of each group were examined, Post‑hoc multiple comparisons were performed using the LSD method and FDR‑corrected pairwise comparisons. Microbial diversity analysis was performed on the QIIME 2 platform: α-diversity was assessed using the Chao1 index [43] and Shannon index [44]; β-diversity was evaluated via Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) based on an unweighted UniFrac distance matrix. Mantel tests were employed to analyze the correlations between microbial communities and root morphological parameters. To investigate the influence of key metabolites on microbial community composition, Detrended Correspondence Analysis (DCA) was first conducted based on the microbial ASV abundance table to determine the length of the first ordination axis (gradient length). The results showed that the axis lengths for both bacterial and fungal communities were less than 3, indicating that a linear model was more appropriate. Therefore, Redundancy Analysis (RDA) was subsequently used to assess the relationship between key metabolites and microbial community composition. Prior to RDA, the community composition data were Hellinger‑transformed to correct for compositional bias, and the variance inflation factor (VIF) of each metabolite variable was calculated; variables with VIF > 10 were removed to eliminate multicollinearity. Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was used to identify taxa with significant differences in abundance between groups. LEfSe is based on non‑parametric tests and directly uses relative abundance data normalized to total abundance. The Random Forest model evaluated classification performance using the out‑of‑bag (OOB) error and ranked metabolite importance to control the risk of overfitting. All statistical analyses were performed in the R language environment, including one-way ANOVA followed by Least Significant Difference (LSD) post-hoc multiple comparisons. It should be noted that all sampling was destructive, and samples from different time points were independent of each other.

Results

25% FC increased the rhizosheath weight of K. hirsuta.

Water treatments influenced the root system architectural parameters of K. hirsuta at different growth stages, including total root length, root surface area, root volume, number of root tips, and number of root forks (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1). The effect of water availability on total root length was significant (P < 0.05). The W6 treatment performed best across the other two stages and was significantly higher than the other treatments (P < 0.05), except for W5 at the T3 stage. Root surface area increased initially, then decreased, at both the T1 and T3 stages, peaking significantly under the W5 treatment. Root volume and number of root tips both exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease, peaking under the W5 treatment (P < 0.05; Table 1). The number of root forks increased continuously with increasing water availability (Supplementary Table 1).

As shown in Table 1, regarding root hair length, values across all growth stages followed T3 > T2 > T1 (P < 0.05). The W5 treatment resulted in the greatest root hair length, measuring 650.29 μm, 712.10 μm, and 871.03 μm at the T1, T2, and T3 stages, respectively (P < 0.05). For root hair density (Supplementary Table 1), the W5 treatment yielded the highest density during the T2 and T3 stages.

Rhizosheath weight is presented in Table 1; Fig. 1C. Overall, during the T1 and T2 periods, rhizosheath weights exhibited a pattern of initial increase followed by a decrease with increasing moisture gradient, with the W5 treatment consistently yielding the highest values across all sampling periods (P < 0.05). Temporally, rhizosheath weights under the W1 treatment showed a continuous and significant increase over the growth stages (P < 0.05). In contrast, the W2, W3, W4, and W5 treatments displayed a trend of initial increase followed by a decline, with all except W4 reaching their peak at the T2 period (P < 0.05). These results indicate that maintaining soil moisture at 25% FC (W5) was most conducive to rhizosheath biomass accumulation, rewatering to 10% field capacity (W1) promoted rhizosheath formation at later stages, and maintaining 40% of FC (W6) exerted its primary influence during the early phase. Notably, under rewatering conditions (W1–W3), all root morphological parameters increased linearly with the water supply level. Under sustained drought stress (W4–W6), however, the treatment maintained at 25% FC significantly enhanced root volume, the number of root tips, root hair length, and rhizosheath weight (P < 0.05), underscoring the critical role of the rhizosheath in root morphological development and drought adaptation.

To further evaluate the effects of time, moisture level, processing mode, and their interactions on root and rhizosheath traits, a three‑way ANOVA was performed (Table 2). The results showed that all three main effects (time, moisture level, and processing mode) were highly significant (P < 0.001) for every trait examined. Specifically, time had the strongest influence on total root length and root hair length, indicating substantial developmental progression. Moisture level exerted moderate effects, with root hair length showing the highest sensitivity. Processing mode (drought‑rewatering (W1-W3) vs. continuous drought (W4-W6)) consistently produced very large F‑values (e.g., F = 1191.93 for root hair length, F = 655.16 for total root length), highlighting that whether water was supplied as sustained deficit or as repeated cycles fundamentally altered root and rhizosheath development.

Several two‑way interactions were also significant. The moisture level × time interaction was significant for all traits except root hair density, suggesting that the effect of water availability on these traits changed over growth stages. The processing mode × time interaction was significant for most traits (not root hair length), indicating that the temporal dynamics differed between continuous drought and rewatering regimes. Notably, the processing mode × moisture level interaction was significant for root volume, number of root tips, rhizosheath weight, root hair length, and root hair density (P < 0.001), demonstrating that the response to moisture levels depended strongly on whether the plants experienced constant drought or rewatering cycles. Finally, the three‑way interaction (processing mode × moisture level × time) reached significance for total root length, root volume, rhizosheath weight, and root hair length, implying that the combined effects of regime, level, and time were not simply additive. These statistical results suggest that continuous 25% FC (W5) and the rewatering treatments were associated with distinct patterns of adaptive changes, rather than reflecting mere differences in overall magnitude.

Water regulation of rhizosheath-associated fatty acid profile and key response markers

A total of 20 major fatty acids were identified in the rhizosheath-associated fatty acid profile, and their relative abundance profiles exhibited significant overall differentiation across the different water treatments (Fig. 2A). Stacked plot analysis revealed that palmitic acid (C16:0) and stearic acid (C18:0) constituted the highest proportions of the total and displayed differential accumulation patterns in response to water regimes. Specifically, the abundances of C16:0 and lignoceric acid (C24:0) were highest following rewatering to 25%–40% FC (W2, W3), whereas C18:0 accumulated most prominently under continuous 25% FC (W5). This indicates that soil water deficit substantially reshapes the overall composition of the root lipidome. Correspondingly, an analysis of metabolic diversity based on the Shannon index demonstrated that water regimes significantly influenced the overall complexity of rhizosheath-associated fatty acid profile (Fig. 2B). Metabolic diversity reached its lowest level in the W5 group (continuous 25% FC), while the W2 group maintained relatively high diversity, suggesting that moderate water conditions support greater activity and complexity in root lipid metabolism.

To further identify the key fatty acids driving the root metabolic response to different water regimes, univariate statistical analyses were conducted based on the relative abundances of the 20 identified rhizosheath-associated fatty acid profile. Following correction for multiple testing using the FDR method, 15 metabolites were identified as significantly different (FDR < 0.05) (Table 3). Among these, palmitoleic acid (C16:1N7) exhibited the highest F-statistic value (96.25) and the lowest FDR value (9.13 × 10⁻⁸), followed by myristic acid (C14:0), palmitic acid (C16:0), lauric acid (C12:0), and pentadecenoic acid (C15:1N5), all of which showed highly significant differences (FDR < 0.0001). Boxplots visually demonstrated that the abundances of these five differential metabolites were extremely significantly elevated in the W2 and W3 treatments but remained relatively low in the other treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 1, P < 0.0001). This suggests that moderate rewatering following drought strongly induces their accumulation in roots. A Random Forest algorithm was employed to rank the overall importance of each metabolite in distinguishing among the six water treatment groups (Fig. 2C). The model results identified C18:3N3 (α-linolenic acid) as the most influential metabolite for differentiating water regimes, based on its highest mean decrease in accuracy (approximately 0.02). Although C18:3N3 showed only marginal significance in the univariate analysis (FDR = 0.057), boxplot analysis revealed that its abundance was significantly higher in the W3 and W4 treatments compared to other groups (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating its role as a key metabolic marker mediating differential root responses under drought and rewatering conditions. Two major saturated fatty acids, arachidic acid (C20:0) and palmitic acid (C16:0), were also ranked highly by the Random Forest analysis and were confirmed as significantly differential metabolites by univariate tests, further consolidating their central regulatory roles in root adaptation to water stress.

Water conditions significantly shape the rhizosheath microbial community and identify treatment-enriched taxa

Diversity and richness of the rhizosheath microbial community under different water treatments

Rhizosheath soil samples from K. hirsuta were subjected to Illumina MiSeq high-throughput sequencing. After quality control, 51,121–69,627 high-quality bacterial sequences and 70,357–76,560 high-quality fungal sequences were obtained, which were clustered into 621–1,721 bacterial and 48–142 fungal amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), respectively (Fig. 3A, B). A total of 124 bacterial and 14 fungal ASVs were shared across all samples, constituting the core microbiome. The drought–rewatering treatment group (W1–W3) and the sustained drought treatment group (W4–W6) contained 1,359 and 1,495 bacterial ASVs, and 94 and 114 fungal ASVs, respectively. Bacterial ASV richness peaked under the W5 treatment (25% FC), while fungal ASV richness was highest under the W4 and W5 treatments (10%–25% FC). These results demonstrate that soil water availability significantly influences the richness of the rhizosheath microbial community.

Alpha diversity analysis (Table 4) revealed that water treatments significantly influenced the structure of the rhizosheath microbial communities. For bacterial communities, species evenness and diversity (Shannon and Simpson indices) were generally higher under sustained drought conditions than under drought–rewatering treatments. Specifically, the W5 and W6 treatments showed significantly higher values than W1 and W2 (P < 0.05), with all diversity indices reaching their peak under the W5 treatment (P < 0.05). Trend analysis indicated that under rewatering conditions, bacterial richness (Chao1 index), Shannon index, and Simpson index increased with rising moisture levels, peaking in the W3 treatment. Under sustained drought, however, these indices exhibited an initial increase followed by a decline, with the highest values observed in the W5 treatment. In contrast, the response pattern of fungal communities was more complex. Fungal Simpson index was significantly higher under drought–rewatering treatments than under sustained drought (W5 and W6, P < 0.05), whereas fungal richness (Chao1 index) reached its maximum under the W6 treatment, significantly exceeding that in W1 and W2 (P < 0.05). Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on ASVs revealed clear separation of bacterial communities among different water treatments (stress value = 0.044; Fig. 3C). Samples from rewatering treatments (W1–W3) clustered in the negative region of NMDS1, displaying a gradient distribution along this axis from left to right, which indicates a continuous shift in bacterial community structure with increasing moisture under rewatering conditions. In contrast, samples from sustained drought treatments (W4–W6) were concentrated in the positive regions of both NMDS1 and NMDS2, showing no overlap with the rewatering group. This reflects a strong restructuring effect of drought stress on bacterial communities. Within the sustained drought group, samples were more widely dispersed along the ordination axes, suggesting that moisture gradients drove more pronounced differentiation in bacterial community structure than under rewatering conditions. Fungal communities exhibited weaker separation among treatments (stress value = 0.049; Fig. 3D). Although partial overlap was observed between rewatering and sustained drought groups, certain pairs (e.g., W1 vs. W4, W2 vs. W6) remained clearly distinct. This indicates that fungal community structure also differed between water regimes, but the degree of differentiation was significantly lower than in bacteria, pointing to a more moderate fungal response to water treatments. Within-group patterns were less distinct: fungal samples under the rewatering (W1–W3) were relatively scattered, while those under sustained drought (W4–W6) did not follow a clear gradient. These results suggest that the influence of moisture gradients on fungal community structure is more complex, with weaker intra-group differentiation compared to bacterial communities. PERMANOVA (Adonis) further confirmed that the water treatment had a highly significant effect on both bacterial (R² = 0.5174, P = 0.001) and fungal (R² = 0.5943, P = 0.001) community structures, explaining 51.74% and 59.43% of the community variation, respectively (Supplementary Table 2). This clearly identifies water regime as a key factor driving the assembly of the rhizosheath microbial communities.

Composition and treatment-enriched taxa of the rhizosheath microbial community

At the phylum level (Fig. 4A), the bacterial community was dominated by Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Gemmatimonadetes, and Myxococcota, which collectively accounted for over 85% of the total relative abundance. Bacteroidetes reached its peak abundance in the W3 treatment, while Myxococcota was most enriched under the W5 treatment. Proteobacteria and Gemmatimonadetes both peaked in the W6 treatment. These patterns suggest that low-moisture conditions promoted the growth of Actinobacteria but suppressed Gemmatimonadetes and Myxococcota, whereas high-moisture conditions showed the opposite trend. This indicates that low moisture promoted the enrichment of Nocardioides and Pseudarthrobacter, while high moisture favoured the growth of Gemmatimonas.

LEfSe analysis identified key microbial taxa within the rhizosheath soil under the different water treatments (Fig. 4E, F). To focus on taxa with substantial and biologically meaningful discriminative power, LDA thresholds were set at 4.0 for bacteria and 2.0 for fungi. The bacterial threshold of 4.0, exceeding the conventional default of 2.0, was chosen to retain only the most robust biomarkers and minimize false positives, given the larger number of significantly differentiated bacterial genera; the fungal threshold was kept at 2.0 to capture moderately discriminating taxa while maintaining interpretability, consistent with standard LEfSe practice. The order Diversisporales, the order Chaetothyriales, the family Trichocomaceae, and the genus Knufia were identified as key differential fungal taxa.

Drivers of microbial community changes

Mantel tests were employed to examine the associations between microbial community composition and root morphological traits, based on the distance matrices of microbial communities and root morphological parameters (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table 3). The results revealed widespread positive correlations among the various root morphological indicators: total root length (TRL), root surface area (RSA), root volume (RV), number of root tips (NRT), number of forks (NF), and root hair length (RHL) all of which exhibited highly significant positive correlations with each other (P < 0.01). Rhizosheath Weight (RW) was significantly correlated with RHL and root hair density (RHD) (P < 0.05). Regarding microbe-root interactions, at the microbial phylum level, the bacterial community matrix, dominated by Proteobacteria and other major phyla (including Actinobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Gemmatimonadota, Myxococcota, Firmicutes, Acidobacteriota, Verrucomicrobiota, and Fibrobacterota) exhibited highly significant correlations (P < 0.01) with most root architecture traits, including TRL, RSA, RV, NRT, NF, and RHD. In contrast, the fungal community dominated by Ascomycota (including Ascomycota, Chytridiomycota, Basidiomycota, and Rozellomycota) showed significant correlations only with RHL and RW (P < 0.05). These results suggest that the Proteobacteria‑dominated bacterial community is widely implicated in the establishment and development of overall root architecture, whereas the Ascomycota‑dominated fungal community appears to function more specifically in regulating particular aspects such as root hair morphogenesis and rhizosheath material accumulation. Their complementary roles collectively constitute a key strategy through which plants enhance drought resistance via root-microbe interactions.

Discussion

Continuous maintenance at 25% Field Capacity (FC) was associated with the strongest rhizosheath development and coordinated shifts in root traits and microbial communities

Under the controlled conditions of this study, root volume, root tip number, root hair length, and rhizosheath weight were all significantly greater in the continuous 25% FC treatment (W5), which also showed the strongest coordination between root architecture and rhizosheath development. The elongation of root hairs was associated with increased entanglement and adhesion efficiency of soil particles, potentially providing a structural basis for rhizosheath densification, consistent with the consensus that root hairs are the most critical trait for rhizosheath formation [27, 45, 46]. Treatments deviating from this moisture level exhibited weaker coordination between root traits and rhizosheath biomass. This finding corroborates earlier work showing that rhizosheath weight initially increases and then decreases within the 10%–40% field capacity range [8]. Excessively strong water stress inhibits root hair development, whereas excessive moisture may dilute mucilage and impair microbial activity [47]; both factors could disrupt such synergistic processes, but direct measurements are required to confirm these effects. Notably, the optimal moisture level for rhizosheath formation differed between water regimes, reflecting distinct adaptive strategies. Under continuous drought, most root parameters peaked at W5, suggesting that plants maintain adaptive homeostasis by optimizing root architecture and rhizosheath development [48]. Under the drought–rewatering cycle, rewatering to 40% FC (W3) promoted rapid early rhizosheath accumulation, whereas rewatering to only 10% FC after extreme drought (W1) led to a sustained increase in rhizosheath weight during later growth stages, implying developmental plasticity driven by prior water experience [49, 50]. The consistent trends among rhizosheath weight, root volume, root tip number, and root hair length indicate a coordinated adaptation between root morphological plasticity and rhizosheath formation [17, 27].

Metabolomic analysis showed that palmitic acid (C16:0) accumulated markedly under drought–rewatering treatments (W2, W3), whereas stearic acid (C18:0) reached its highest abundance under continuous moderate drought (W5). The specific accumulation of C16:0 may reflect a persistent influence of prior water experience on metabolic status, but whether this represents a genuine “memory” effect at the transcriptional or metabolic level requires dedicated experimental verification [51, 52]. Furthermore, these key metabolites were closely linked to the structure of the rhizosheath microbial community: the accumulation of C18:0 coincided with the peak of bacterial alpha diversity, while C16:0 and C16:1N7 were among the fatty acids most strongly associated with differentiation of bacterial and fungal communities, respectively. These fatty acids may influence microbial communities by serving as nutrients, modulating membrane fluidity, or acting as signaling molecules [53], a hypothesis consistent with studies showing that root exudates reshape the rhizosphere microbiome under drought [54]. However, it must be emphasized that this targeted approach captures only a limited fraction of the root metabolic profile. Under drought stress, changes in root exudate composition also include sugars, amino acids, and phenolic compounds, among others. Sugars can act as readily available carbon sources that drive rapid proliferation of rhizosphere microorganisms [55]; amino acids function not only as osmolytes but also in modulating immune homeostasis within the rhizosphere microbiome [56]; and activation of flavonoid biosynthesis pathways, together with enhanced starch and sucrose metabolism, promotes the secretion of defense-related metabolites [57]. As these metabolites were not targeted in the present study, the observed “fatty acid–microbe” associations cannot be interpreted as the sole chemical mechanism, and future non-targeted metabolomics approaches are warranted for comprehensive validation.

The rhizosheath microbial community structure responded significantly to water availability, with soil moisture identified as a key factor driving community differentiation; bacterial communities were more sensitive to moisture changes than fungal communities [28, 33]. Notably, moderate drought (W5) was associated with a significant enrichment of Myxococcota and Ascomycota, and LEfSe analysis revealed that very few bacterial genera were differentially enriched at this moisture level, with only Myxococcus and Polyangium identified. This pattern does not indicate a lack of microbial community response; rather, it suggests a “stabilizing selection” process under optimal water conditions, wherein the enrichment of a limited number of taxa under W5 may reflect selective filtering caused by moderate continuous drought, possibly in relation to fatty acid profiles associated with the rhizosheath [58]. Based on their reported ecological traits, Myxococcus and Polyangium are plausible candidates for contributing to rhizosheath structural stability, but their specific roles require direct experimental confirmation [59]. Collectively, soil moisture may drive compositional changes in the rhizosheath microbiome by modulating soil physicochemical properties and root metabolic activity, as statistically evidenced by the enrichment of specific taxa under different moisture regimes; such association patterns likely vary among plant cultivars and ecotypes [60, 61].

Interactive mechanisms among root architecture, metabolites, and microbial communities

Root morphological plasticity can alter the rhizosheath microenvironment, potentially leading to changes in fatty acid profiles and microbial communities [62]. Our results indicate that the association patterns between roots and microorganisms differ markedly between water regimes, together forming a multi-level response network for plant adaptation to water fluctuations. Under the continuous drought regime (exemplified by W5), an optimized root architecture, characterized mainly by increased root volume, root tip number, and root hair length, was not only associated with an enhanced physical foundation for rhizosheath formation but also accompanied by elevated relative abundances of specific metabolites such as stearic acid (C18:0). These metabolites, as potential niche signals, were correlated with the enrichment patterns of microbial groups including Myxococcota and Ascomycota [19, 63]. Moreover, bacterial community composition was closely correlated with overall root architectural parameters, whereas fungal community composition was more tightly linked to indicators of root hair development and rhizosheath formation, a pattern consistent with the functional differentiation hypothesis that “bacteria predominantly mediate nutrient acquisition while fungi regulate structural formation” [64]. In contrast, under the drought–rewatering regime (exemplified by W2 and W3), the accumulation of unsaturated and medium-chain fatty acids (including C16:1N7, C14:0, and C16:0) was markedly enhanced after rewatering. Redundancy analysis (RDA) showed that fluctuations in these metabolites were highly correlated with fungal community composition and also significantly associated with bacterial community composition, suggesting that metabolite changes related to drying–rewetting cycles may be involved in microbial community reassembly [65]. Notably, the observed associations suggest possible reciprocal interactions between roots and microbial communities. The enriched potentially beneficial microorganisms (e.g., Sphingomonas) are reported in the literature to have the capacity to promote nutrient cycling and secrete extracellular polymeric substances [66]; It is plausible that this process helps consolidate the adaptive advantages conferred by root morphological adjustments, forming a positive feedback loop. Taken together, the correlative evidence obtained in this study outlines a hypothetical framework of synergistic “root–metabolite–microbe” interactions: under continuous drought, this network manifests as a coordinated response triggered by root architectural optimization, whereas under alternating dry–wet conditions, it manifests as dynamic reorganization and persistent adjustment driven by moisture pulses. These findings provide correlative evidence linking drought–rewatering cycles to specific rhizosheath states, fatty acid profiles, and microbial community configurations.

Rhizosheath formation and regulation as a ntegrative trait for drought resistance in K. hirsuta

Rhizosheath formation and its dynamic regulation represent a key integrative trait by which K. hirsuta coordinates multi-level responses to achieve drought adaptation [27, 60]. In response to drought, this species exhibits coordinated morphological and physiological adjustments: at the morphological level, changes in root architecture provide the physical foundation for rhizosheath development, a process potentially associated with phenotypic plasticity regulated by drought-responsive QTLs [54, 67]; at the physiological level, adjustments in the root metabolic profile are closely linked to the selective enrichment of rhizosheath microbial communities [60, 61]. These associations connect drought signals to a cascade of responses ranging from morphological adjustment to microbial community reassembly, ultimately leading to the formation of a structurally dense and functionally optimized rhizosheath [17, 29]. Common psammophytic pioneer plants on the alpine desertified grasslands of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, such as Stipa glareosa, Stipa breviflora, and Agropyron desertorum, rely primarily on extensive root networks for sand fixation but generally lack or rarely develop rhizosheath structures [68, 69]. In contrast, K. hirsuta exhibits a distinct adaptive strategy that places greater emphasis on rhizosheath construction. By establishing a synergistic “root–metabolite–microbe” network, it forms a structurally dense and functionally active rhizosheath. These results preliminarily suggest an integrated strategy of “sand fixation, soil improvement, and growth promotion” in this plant. However, the current evidence is mainly derived from correlational analyses, which do not directly support causal inference. Therefore, based on the findings of this study, it is highly necessary to conduct further in‑depth validation experiments to directly test the causal mechanisms underlying rhizosheath formation and regulation, thereby providing a more solid theoretical basis for the ecological restoration of alpine desertified grasslands.

The rhizosheath provides dual protective functions: it reduces water loss by enhancing soil–root adhesion and protects root hairs from desiccation, while simultaneously improving rhizosphere nutrient availability and plant stress tolerance through the enrichment of beneficial microorganisms [70, 71]. Thus, the rhizosheath is not merely a by-product of drought stress, but rather a micro-ecological protective unit actively constructed by the plant [33]. Although the causal regulatory network among roots, metabolites, and microorganisms remains to be elucidated, the coordinated changes observed in this study suggest the following potential applications. First, rhizosheath weight and root hair length could serve as candidate selection indices for the early screening of drought- and desertification-tolerant forage cultivars in alpine regions, complementing traditional evaluation systems based solely on aboveground biomass. Second, given the characteristic frequent precipitation fluctuations on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, moderate drought regulation techniques could be integrated into the establishment of artificial grasslands, using drought–rewatering cycles to stimulate rhizosheath development as a potential approach to enhance drought resistance and self-sustaining stability.

Conclusion

Under both continuous drought and drought–rewatering regimes, K. hirsute exhibited distinct rhizosheath response patterns that were associated with coordinated changes in root traits, metabolites, and microbial communities, collectively constituting adaptive features to water stress. Under continuous drought, maintaining soil moisture at 25% field capacity was closely associated with coordinated increases in root volume, root tip number, and root hair length, as well as with enhanced rhizosheath biomass. This condition was also accompanied by the accumulation of stearic acid (C18:0) and the enrichment of specific microbial taxa such as Myxococcota and Ascomycota, showing a coordinated “morphology–metabolite–microbe” association that likely contributes to the maintenance of rhizosheath structure and function. Under the drought–rewatering regime, rewatering to 25%–40% field capacity (W2, W3) was associated with the accumulation of metabolites such as palmitic acid (C16:0) and palmitoleic acid (C16:1N7), and was linked to rapid reassembly of microbial communities. Moreover, rewatering to only 10% field capacity after extreme drought (W1) resulted in a sustained increase in rhizosheath weight during the later growth stage, which may reflect a compensatory response induced by early water deficit. These two water regimes suggest the possible existence of two distinct adaptive strategies “steady-state resistance” and “plastic recovery”, that jointly form a rhizosheath-mediated association network for plant water adaptation. This network provides correlative evidence for understanding plant water adaptation strategies in alternating dry–wet habitats, thus laying a critical groundwork for future investigations into the stress resistance mechanisms of sand‑adapted plants.
