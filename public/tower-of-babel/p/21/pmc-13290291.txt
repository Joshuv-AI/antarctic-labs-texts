Unlocking 3D baby face photogrammetry: Multi-view BabyMorph reconstruction from uncalibrated photographs
Authors: Antonia Alomar, Gemma Piella, Esperanza Mantilla-Rivas, Austin Tapp, Antonio R Porras, Ricardo Rubio, Silvia Maya-Enero, Federico M Sukno, Marius George Linguraru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCraniofacial anomalies are important diagnostic markers in early life. Recent studies emphasize the value of 3D imaging for extracting robust facial features that are potential indicators of disease. However, widespread availability of 3D scanning devices in hospitals remains a challenge and this type of technology may not be available in limited-resource settings. For this reason, we present a new approach to generate precise baby 3D face reconstructions from multiple uncalibrated 2D photographs acquired with a smartphone camera.The novel multi-view transformer network presented takes as input three uncalibrated photographs of the baby in frontal, left, and right pose. It then maps these images to a previously learned latent space that captures the baby’s 3D facial morphology, using a 2D vision transformer encoder. Subsequently, the estimated 3D geometry is recovered by decoding the latent vector using a 3D graph convolutional network decoder. Our network demonstrates a normalized mean error of 3.29% and a root mean square error of 2.62 mm between reconstructed and true 3D faces in the baby test dataset. These outcomes are comparable with those reported by both single-view and multi-view 2D-3D reconstruction state-of-the-art errors in adult models.To conclude, the presented Multi-view BabyMorph generates highly accurate 3D baby facial reconstructions from uncalibrated photographs, simplifying the process of 3D photogrammetry. This innovation expands access to advanced baby diagnosis tools, particularly in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Baby 3D face, Multi-view reconstruction, Transformers, 3D photogrammetry

Introduction

Craniofacial anomalies (CA) are diagnostic markers in early life, encompassing a wide range of heterogeneous conditions associated with genetic and developmental disorders (Dowsett et al., 2019; Kruszka et al., 2017a,b, 2018). It is estimated that up to 40% of genetic syndromes produce alterations in the normal morphology of the face and the head. While these associations have been established mainly using data from adult populations, their early recognition and assessment of are often crucial for the early and effective treatment of the associated functional and developmental conditions (Collett et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2016; Junaid et al., 2022; Okkerse et al., 2005). For this reason, it is important to provide an early diagnosis in the peri-natal period.

The recognition of a genetic syndrome from facial phenotype shares similarities with classic facial recognition (Wang & Deng, 2021). However, in practice, developing a system for syndrome recognition is challenging for several reasons, including limited data, subtle facial patterns, and ethnic differences (Cox et al., 2013; Hart & Hart, 2009; Trainor & Richtsmeier, 2015). Recent advances in automatic facial analysis achieved similar performances to expert clinicians in syndrome identification, making it possible to transfer this knowledge to other specialties such as pediatrics or neonatology. This reduces the dependence on the clinician’s experience (Ferry et al., 2014; Rai et al., 2015). Diagnosis can be made from different modalities of data, i.e. from two-dimensional (2D) images (Guo et al., 2019; Pantel et al., 2018; Porras et al., 2021a; Singh & Kisku, 2018; Zhao et al., 2014), from three-dimensional (3D) reconstructions (Goodwin et al., 2014; Hallgrimsson et al., 2020; Hammond, 2007; Hammond & Suttie, 2012; Tu et al., 2018) or from multi-modal data (Porras et al., 2021b).

Until the last decade, the standard approach for facial analysis was using 2D images, as in the DeepGestalt approach (Gurovich et al., 2019). However, with the increasing accessibility to 3D surface scans, there is growing interest in conducting syndrome evaluations using 3D morphology, as a substantial amount of information is lost during the 2D projection process (Atuhaire et al., 2023; Hallgrimsson et al., 2020). Several studies have compared 2D and 3D facial representation for facial morphology analysis, concluding that 3D imaging is a superior method for extracting more robust facial features (Bannister et al., 2023; Qiang et al., 2022).

However, 3D scanning devices (i.e 3dMD, 3DTechMed or Shining 3D) are not widely available in hospitals and clinics, and present several challenges when dealing with babies, who are not prone to sitting upright and can move or cry during scanning. In addition, capturing them with a neutral facial expression adequate for morphological analysis is challenging. In craniofacial analysis or neurosurgery 3D photogrammetry has gain popularity to generate anatomical models, train clinicians and analyze patterns (Duncan et al., 2022; Quispe-Enriquez et al., 2023; Trandzhiev et al., 2023). The photogrammetry term has a wide definition and widespread use to refer to the technique to reconstruct the 3D geometry of an object from multiple overlapping photographs (Nebel et al., 2020). Therefore, an approach to obtain the details offered by 3D photogrammetry using solely multiple 2D uncalibrated images would serve as a valuable tool for CA detection in babies, being less expensive and easy to incorporate in a clinical setup.

The reconstruction of the 3D human facial morphology from 2D images is a challenging task due to its inherent ill-pose nature. To successfully retrieve details such as shape, head orientation, and texture from either a single or multiple images, it is essential to integrate prior information in the solution (Sharma & Kumar, 2022), such as a 3D morphable models (3DMM). Deep learning (DL) is commonly used to extract meaningful features from the 2D images and subsequently establish the mapping that links these features to the 3D shape. Typically, feature extraction from 2D images has been accomplished using convolutional neural networks (CNNs). However, vision transformers (ViTs) can improve CNN performance in some computer vision tasks (Han et al., 2023). In most prior 3D face reconstruction methods, principal component analysis (PCA) is used to define a linear facial shape space, as in well-known 3DMM such as the Basel Face Model (BFM) (Paysan et al., 2009) or FLAME (Li et al., 2017), which are trained on adult datasets.

Recent works have also highlighted the importance of using baby-specific data for modelling infant faces. Evangelos et al. (2022) investigated to which extent adult face 3DMM could be applied to infants. They compared the performance of five state-of-the-art adult 3DMM fitting methods and observed that reconstructing infant faces using 3DMMs designed for adults may be inaccurate, which consistently align with our previous research (Morales et al., 2023, 2025). This limitation is particularly relevant for applications involving the identification of craniofacial dysmorphic patterns. However, constructing a model with good performance and generalization is challenging, given the limited data availability of 3D and 2D pairs of facial data.

In this paper, we present a novel approach to generate high accurate 3D face reconstructions from uncalibrated 2D photographs, simplifying the process of 3D photogrammetry: the Multi-view BabyMorph. In contrast to most previous approaches based on single 2D images, we exploit information from triplets of images taken from multiple views, i.e. frontal, left, and right pose. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to propose a multi-view 3D face reconstruction system designed specifically for babies. The proposed architecture introduces a transformer-based multi-view 2D-to-3D reconstruction network that is capable of learning effectively from a small dataset. Crucially, rather than regressing the parameters of a fixed PCA-based 3DMM (e.g., BFM Paysan et al., 2009 or FLAME Li et al., 2017), our method adopts a geometric deep learning approach that learns a nonlinear facial shape space directly from data. This enables our system to capture complex, high-frequency facial geometry beyond the capabilities of linear PCA models, which are known to struggle with fine details and population-specific variations–particularly in infants. This approach aims to enhance the accessibility of genetic screening based on facial features by providing a cost-effective, simple, and accurate screening method adequate for limited-resource settings.

State of the art

3D morphable models

Originally introduced by Blanz and Vetter (1999), a 3DMM is a generative model used to represent and manipulate 3D facial appearance and shape. It was designed to ensure dense point-to-point correspondence between faces through a registration process. Numerous variations of this model can be found in the literature (Cao et al., 2014; Dai et al., 2020; Li et al., 2017; Paysan et al., 2009). These models leverage low-dimensional representations to capture facial expressions, texture, and identity information. Classical 3DMMs are usually constructed by registering a template mesh to the scanned face, through iterative closest point (ICP) and PCA techniques.

The transformation into a low-dimensional latent space is typically determined by linear or multi-linear operations. However, DL allows constructing 3DMMs by incorporating non-linearities. There has been notable success in employing DL approaches such as autoencoders (AEs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), and hybrid architectures.

The most common approach using an encoder-decoder architecture projects the 3D face geometry to a 2D image, and then applies convolutions in the image domain, while the decoder is fixed to a classical tensor-based face model (Abrevaya et al., 2018; Bagautdinov et al., 2018). An alternative line of work learns GANs for 3D face modeling to obtain models with fine details, especially in the facial texture generation. As facial texture is well represented by non-linear spaces, GANs map a 3D face texture to a 2D image domain to build a facial texture model. This is then combined with a standard PCA-based 3DMM for facial geometry (Gecer et al., 2021). Several methods were proposed for generating 3D facial geometry. Abrevaya et al. (2019) introduced a GAN-based approach that decouples factors of variation such as identity and expression. Shamai et al. (2019) proposed a GAN architecture to generate both facial geometry and texture, with a focus on highly detailed texture information by mapping the face to a unit rectangle.

The aforementioned works used traditional DL operations, which are limited to data with an underlying Euclidean structure, such as speech, images, or videos. However, recent advances in the emerging field of geometric DL, have successfully handled non-Euclidean data, such as 3D facial meshes. These approaches eliminate the need for intermediate Euclidean representations such as PCA latent spaces or images, while preserving the topological structure of the data. In this non-Euclidean framework, graph convolutional networks (GCNs) (Bronstein et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2021), offer advantages over the widely employed CNNs, such as smaller number of parameters, which make them easier to train.

Researchers have highlighted the advantages of GCNs in the context of 3D facial analysis (Cheng et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2020a; Ranjan et al., 2018). These approaches involved training a mesh-to-mesh autoencoder (AE) to learn a non-linear, low-dimensional latent space that captures facial shape deformations. For instance, Ranjan et al. (2018) defined the convolution operator of their graph convolutional AE using spectral convolutional operators and applied kernels based on recursive Chebyshev polynomials to filter the meshes. The CoMA model allows compact representations of the facial geometry. In contrast, Bouritsas et al. (2019) introduced a spiral convolution method that is inherently anisotropic by using a pre-ordering of mesh vertices along spiral trajectories instead of neighboring vertices. This approach enabled them to establish a one-to-one mapping between vertex neighbors and local filter parameters.

In this work, a 3D GCN encoder-decoder is trained to obtain a baby face 3DMM. GCNs allow direct processing of 3D meshes, facilitating the extraction of intrinsic features from non-Euclidean data. Additionally, a non-linear mapping to a low dimensional space is obtained, which can be advantageous for achieving a more complex latent space for the shape of the baby’s face, to which we also map the 2D image information.

2D-3D face reconstruction

A substantial challenge in 3D-from-2D face reconstruction is the lack of pairs of 2D photographs and corresponding 3D ground-truth (GT) scans (photograms). To overcome this limitation, researchers proposed generative approaches to create realistic representative training data. Three main strategies to build synthetic training sets have been proposed: 1) Fit&Render, which consists in fitting a 3DMM to real images and then rendering synthetic images using the estimated 3D faces; 2) Generate&Render, which generates 3D faces by randomly sampling from a 3DMM and then renders synthetic images (Guo et al., 2019; Sela et al., 2017; Tran et al., 2018); and 3) Self-supervised training, which avoids the need for paired 2D-3D data and thus the need to build synthetic datasets. It self-provides supervision by adding a rendering layer that project the 3D estimate in the 2D image domain and compares the result to the original 2D input (Chen et al., 2020; Tran et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2019).

Each of the above approaches has limitations. The main drawback of the Fit&Render approach is that the accuracy of the trained DL method is determined by the 3DMM-fitting algorithm used to reconstruct the GT 3D faces, making it the least used strategy. The most used method is self-supervision. However, even if self-supervision avoids the need for paired 2D-3D data, it makes the reconstruction process much more complex, since it incorporates texture into the 3D face reconstruction problem. Our objective in this paper is to obtain an accurate reconstruction guided by the real 3D shape. Hence, we use the Generate&Render approach and train our network with synthetically generated data. This strategy avoids using an auxiliary 3DMM-fitting algorithm. Thus, the network’s learning capability is not limited to the reconstruction algorithm. However, there are some limitations to this approach as well. The generated 2D images may lack realism due to the fully synthetic rendering process. Moreover, this approach does not address the limitation of learning from linearly modeled data, since a 3DMM is still used to generate the ground-truth 3D faces.

Single-view reconstruction

Statistical model fitting is the classical method used to reconstruct the 3D shape from 2D images. This methodology adds a 3DMM as the prior knowledge of the facial shape. The fitting process consists of estimating the model parameters that generate a 3D face that closely matches the one in the images, using optimization techniques (Cao et al., 2018; Li et al., 2018; Rotger et al., 2019) or training a CNN to learn the mapping (Tian et al., 2019; Zeng et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2022a). The most popular approach is self-supervised learning, where the difference between the original and the synthetic image is minimized (Booth et al., 2018; Gecer et al., 2019; Sariyanidi et al., 2020). The minimization can be driven by the image/landmark error (Bas et al., 2017; Dai et al., 2020; Vlasic et al., 2005).

Although self-supervision avoids the need for paired 2D-3D data, it must accurately recover the 3D face texture, which is not trivial. Furthermore, the training requires careful regularization using additional constraints to guarantee an accurate reconstruction of the 3D face geometry. To constrain the reconstruction to plausible shapes, the solution space is usually described by a PCA 3DMM, which suffers from the same limitations as generating synthetic data from 3DMMs. This inability to reconstruct fine details remains a challenge in 2D-3D reconstruction. Some research efforts aimed to recover fine details using multiple images (Dou & Kakadiaris, 2018; Zhao et al., 2022b) or performing iterative refinements of the resulting face (Dou et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2018). Findings suggest that fine details often need to be estimated separately from overall shape, motivating approaches that divide the task across multiple networks, each responsible for estimating a specific facial attribute (identity, expression, texture, albedo, pose, light etc) (Khan et al., 2021). Among state-of-the-art methods, DECA (Feng et al., 2021), extends the FLAME model by regressing shape, expression, texture, and fine-level surface details using a learned displacement map, enabling high-fidelity reconstruction under diverse conditions.

A different approach was presented by Gecer et al. (2019, 2021), who combined the power of GANs to generate facial texture with deep CNN to reconstruct facial texture and shape from single images. They reconstructed photo-realistic facial textures with high-frequency details. However, their method also relies on estimating facial shape from a PCA model, which limits the level of detail in the shape reconstruction. Nonetheless, Zhou et al. (2019) were among the first ones to use a GCN as a 3DMM for face reconstruction from 2D images, by introducing non-linear 3DMMs as decoders instead of classical PCA models. Following this trend, several works have incorporated non-linear decoders based on GCNs and/or GANs to improve the level of detail in shape and texture reconstruction, demonstrating promising results (Lee & Lee, 2020; Lin et al., 2020b; Tang et al., 2022).

Multiple-view reconstruction

Surprisingly, multi-view 3D face reconstruction using DL is less common than single image reconstruction. Dou and Kakadiaris (2018) proposed using a recurrent neural network to combine identity-related features extracted from a deep CNN, aiming to generate more discriminative reconstructions. To add robustness to variations in image quality and facial expression, they incorporated an identity contextual information constraint by disentangling the facial identity from the facial expression for each single image. Another approach by Ramon et al. (2019) proposed to simultaneously learn 3D shape and camera pose by using a single loss based on the projection error. They used a Siamese network architecture that reconstructs the 3D shape from one or multiple views. A particularly notable approach is MVF-Net (Wu et al., 2019), which performs accurate 3D face regression from multiple simultaneous views using a fully differentiable and self-supervised learning framework. MVF-Net regresses 3DMM and pose parameters from multi-view CNN features and introduces a novel view-alignment loss based on dense optical flow to improve cross-view consistency, achieving significantly improved reconstruction accuracy compared to single-view methods. Building on the FLAME parametric face model (Li et al., 2017), Zheng et al. (2023) proposed the MFNet (multi-view Flame network). The training was achieved through self-supervised learning using differential rendering and optical flow constraints. More recently, DF-MVR (Zhao et al., 2025) proposes a weakly supervised multi-view 3D face reconstruction method using a Tri-UNet architecture with attention and involution for deep feature fusion. A face-parsing mask guides fusion and loss weighting, enabling accurate reconstruction without 3D supervision.

A relevant and recent DL approach is the neural radiance fields (NeRF) (Mildenhall et al., 2020) that optimize a continuous volumetric space function using a sparse set of input views. The optimization is based on a deep fully-connected neuronal network without any convolutional layer. The resulting network regresses a continuous scene represented as a 5D vector (3D location (x,y,z) and 2D view direction (θ,ϕ)) to a single volume density σ and an emitted color c=(r,g,b). In the particular case of faces, we found different works based on NeRF (Gafni et al., 2021; Gu et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2021). StyleNeRF (Gu et al., 2021) is a 3D-aware generative model for photo-realistic high-resolution image synthesis with strong multi-view consistency, which is achieved by integrating the NeRF into a style-based generator. NeRF combined with 3DMMs shows a huge potential for achieving accurate and realistic 3D reconstruction. Gafni et al. (2021) presented a dynamic NeRF for modeling the appearance and dynamics of a human face. They introduced an implicit representation of the head based on NeRF combined with a low-dimensional 3DMM, which controls pose and expressions. However, NeRF requires training for each scene, which makes it computationally expensive and time consuming.

As 2D-3D reconstruction from a single image lacks fine details, and multi-view adds additional information that helps to recover finer details lost in the 2D image projection, we propose a multi-view algorithm to reconstruct the baby faces.

Infant 3D face reconstruction

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of using infant-specific data for modeling infant faces. Evangelos et al. (2022) explored the applicability of adult 3D Morphable Models (3DMMs) to infant data by evaluating five state-of-the-art fitting methods. Their results showed that adult-trained models often fail to accurately reconstruct infant facial geometry, underscoring the need for infant-specific models.

To address this gap, Morales et al. (2023, 2025) introduced BabyFM, the first 3DMM designed specifically for infants. BabyFM significantly improved the morphological representation of infant faces and effectively reduced the mismatch between adult and child face models. It tackled the unique challenges of infant face modeling–such as occlusions, extreme and uncontrollable expressions, and data scarcity–through a non-template-dependent learning framework that jointly estimates both a infant-specific facial template and dense point-to-point correspondences, enhanced by a spectral decomposition-based correspondence method.

Since its release, BabyFM has become the foundation for several downstream works on 3D infant face reconstruction and analysis. Classical approaches, such as that of Alomar et al. (2022), have used it in non-linear optimization settings to fit both 3D ultrasound and 2D image data. More recent studies have explored non-linear representations of infant facial geometry through deep geometric learning (Morales et al., 2023; Zappe et al., 2026), demonstrating their superiority over PCA-based models.

Building on BabyFM, Morales et al. (2023) proposed BabyNet, a method for reconstructing 3D infant faces from single 2D images using ArcFace as a feature extractor. More recently, Schnabel et al. (2024) introduced INFACE, a large-scale 3D infant face model constructed from diverse and incomplete scans via ICP fitting to the BabyFM triangulation. INFACE disentangles identity and expression, enabling robust shape modeling and supporting applications such as facial neutralization, mesh completion, and 3D reconstruction from in-the-wild imagery.

Transformers

Transformers are a new type of neural network initially introduced in natural language processing that quickly became the gold standard architecture in the field. What characterizes transformers is the self-attention mechanisms to extract intrinsic features from data, contributing to their remarkable success (Vaswani et al., 2017).

Researchers have recently extended the application of transformers to computer vision tasks. While CNNs have traditionally been considered the foundational component in vision applications, transformers are emerging as a promising alternative. Dosovitskiy et al. (2021) were the first ones to apply a pure ViT by proposing the division of image into patches to perform full-image classification.

In comparison to CNNs, transformers often struggle to achieve good level of performance on small and medium-sized datasets. Transformers are well-known for being data-hungry, which is primarily attributed to their lack of “inductive bias” (Morrison et al., 2021). As a result, the standard approach for self-supervised learning with transformers involves pre-training the model on a large-scale dataset such as ImageNet. The substantial computational and data requirements to train ViTs can limit their adoption, especially for researchers with limited computational and data resources. However, recently Atito et al. (2022) explored the feasibility of training transformers from scratch with limited data. They introduced the group masked model learning (GMML) approach, where both pre-training and fine-tuning are carried out exclusively on the target dataset. GMML achieves this by manipulating randomly selected groups of interconnected tokens. This results in the effective coverage of meaningful portion of a semantic concept, and subsequent recovery of the hidden semantic information from the visible segment of the concept. Consequently, GMML introduced a novel data augmentation process that makes the ViT data-efficient.

Two studies used transformers for 2D-3D face reconstruction from a single image. The first one is by Basak et al. (2022), who proposed a novel pre-trained hierarchical Swin-transformer model in combination with a feature pyramid aggregation structure; to extract the 3D face parameters from a single 2D image using a semi-supervised training approach. The network underwent supervised training to learn the 3DMM parameters and self-supervised training with a differential rendering to learn facial landmarks and features. The second study is by Chen et al. (2022), who applied transformers on the 3D meshes. Specifically, they proposed an end-to-end algorithm that reconstructs the 3D face geometry from a single image using a conditional GAN that translates the real face images to the rendered style and then feeds them to the mesh transformer for face reconstruction.

Observing the significant improvements and advancements achieved using pre-trained ViTs, we propose a network that uses ViTs as the backbone for the feature extraction process from the 2D input images. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of ViTs for multi-view 3D reconstruction, to extract and map the 2D images features to a 3D shape latent representation.

Clinical data

To train and test the algorithm proposed in this paper we used the three clinical datasets presented below.

3D photograms from Children’s National Hospital

The first dataset comprises 3D photometric scans of babies with healthy facial appearance, obtained at Children’s National Hospital in Washington D.C under ethical approval. The dataset was acquired using the 3dMD Head System (3dMD, Atlanta, GA) in a controlled studio environment with uniform lighting. Images were captured from multiple cameras arranged to cover a full 360° horizontal range, and 3D reconstructions were performed using the proprietary software of the scanner, which follows a multi-view stereo pipeline with optimized parameters. The dataset consists of 151 3D scans, with ages ranging from 1 to 34 months (mean age of 11.25 ± 8.98 months). The data is moderately gender-balanced (64% male) and includes individuals from various races and ethnicities: White (48%), Hispanic (21%), Black and African American (18%), Asian (8%), and 5% other.

Out of the 151 scans, 101 were used to construct the BabyFM (Morales et al., 2025), from which the synthetic train and validation sets are sampled.

The remaining unseen 50 scans are used as the test set to evaluate the performance of our model on real-world data. While this dataset includes 3D textured scans of babies, the photographs captured at the time of scanning do not reliably correspond to frontal, left, or right viewpoints. To address this, we corrected the orientation of these scans by re-rendering them at consistent target angles (see Section 4.1). Since ground-truth 3D geometry is available for these cases, we are able to perform quantitative evaluation of the reconstruction accuracy.

2D photographs from Children’s National Hospital

The second dataset consists of 24.000 2D facial photographs of babies, children and adolescents in different profiles (frontal, left and right), with ages ranging from 0 to 16 years (mean age of 14.10 ± 38.10 months), obtained at Children’s National Hospital between 2013 and 2020. The data is roughly gender-balanced (51% male) and encompasses multiple races and ethnicities: White (47%), Hispanic (6%), Black and African American (30%), Asian (12%), and 5% other. The images were obtained with informed consent. For data collection, a smartphone camera was used with a custom application (app).

The dataset comprises photographs of control subjects who do not have genetic syndromes and of children diagnosed with genetic conditions confirmed through either molecular or clinical diagnosis by their respective institutions. These control subjects were carefully selected to match the age, sex, and ethnicity of the children with genetic conditions and free from illumination or motion artifacts.

2D photographs from hospital del Mar

The third dataset consists of 11 cases of white newborns with healthy facial morphology. For each case, we collected 2D uncalibrated photographs in frontal, left, and right profile views, as well as a face biometry conducted by the neonatologist at 15 ± 5 days and 30 ± 5 days after birth at Hospital del Mar in Barcelona according to its Ethical Research Committee and the current legislation (Organic Law 15/1999). The data is roughly gender-balanced (60% female 40% male). Methods

We propose a novel deep neural network architecture to accurately reconstruct the 3D face of babies from multiple 2D uncalibrated photographs as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Overview of the Multi-view BabyMorph reconstruction system. We presented a network capable of reconstructing the 3D face of babies from triplets of uncalibrated photographs. The approach relies on a Multi-view ViT feature extraction network (illustrated at the bottom) that maps the 2D photograph information to 3D information thanks to a 3D autoencoder. More specifically, the Multi-view ViT learns to map the features extracted from 2D images to the same latent space that encodes the 3D facial morphology of babies, corresponding to the latent space of the graph convolutional (GC) autoencoder (illustrated at the top). The white band present in the baby’s eyes was added for privacy protection.

Inspired by Zhou et al. (2019), our 2D-3D reconstruction network maps 2D image features to a previously learned latent space, which is then decoded using a 3D GC decoder, to recover finer details. A novelty of our work is the replacement of the typical CNN used for image features extraction with a pre-trained ViT. Moreover, our network combines information from multiple views to obtain accurate reconstructions. To achieve this, we use three ViTs, one for each profile picture (frontal, left, right) and a multi-view reconstruction layer to integrate and map the information extracted from different 2D images to the 3D latent space. Finally, the estimated latent space is fed to the 3D GC decoder to reconstruct the 3D mesh. A detailed explanation of the implementation and training details is presented in the following sections.

Here, N corresponds to the number of vertices and M to the number of principal components that are retained.

We obtained 20,000 synthetic 3D shapes sampled from the BabyFM to train the 3D AE, using a shape variation of 95%, retaining 46 modes of variation.

To train the 2D-3D reconstruction network, we use 10,000 synthetic data pairs of 3D shapes and 2D pictures (frontal, left and right profile) sampled from a texture-shape model constructed with the textured 3D scans of the BabyFM (Morales et al., 2025). A total of 101 3D scans from the Children’s National Hospital Dataset are used to create the texture-shape model. In this texture-shape model, x is the concatenation of shape and texture coordinates. To balance the contribution of both texture and shape, the texture values are rescaled by their standard deviation (σ) so that their total variance matches that of the shape coordinates. When sampling, we retain 90% of the texture-shape model variance. Then, the textured 3D faces are rendered to obtain the corresponding frontal, left, and right 2D pictures, using a weak perspective projection. A validation set of 2000 data samples is created following the same procedure as for the training set.

For rendering parameters, our pipeline employs a custom z-buffer rasterization method using an affine (weak perspective) projection model. The camera intrinsics are initialized as an identity matrix, without physical focal lengths or explicit camera field of view (FOV). This setup avoids perspective distortion and is commonly used in mesh rasterization workflows. The mesh is scaled and centered to fit within the rendering canvas, ensuring view-consistent image generation. Additionally, synthetic images are rendered under neutral lighting conditions with uniform ambient light, and a plain white background is applied across all views to prevent appearance bias during training. The ideal capture angles for the input images correspond to 0° for the frontal view, −75° for the right profile, and +75° for the left profile, relative to the frontal axis. The synthetic training and validation data incorporates random pose perturbations within ±15 degrees from the frontal, left and right views. This angular range was chosen based on empirical observations of the most common pose variations in our real-world newborn image data.

3D BabyMorph autoencoder

The first step of our approach is to train the 3D BabyMorph AE with the goal of learning a lower-dimensional latent space that represents the 3D facial morphology of babies. This representation serves as a bridge to map 2D image data to 3D information. Furthermore, the 3D GC decoder aids the reconstruction of 3D facial structures.

To ensure the model robustness in the presence of corrupted and noisy data, we choose a denoising autoencoder (DAE) approach. DAE are widely used for image restoration and unsupervised feature learning. Unlike standard AE, a DAE reconstructs clean data from noisy inputs. To effectively handle facial surfaces using 3D meshes, we design a 3D DAE using GCNs for both the encoder and the decoder. In the graph formulation, a 3D facial mesh is represented as 𝒢={𝒱,A}. Here, 𝒱∈RN×3 represents the set of N vertices of the facial mesh in 3D Euclidean space, and A∈{0,1}N×N corresponds to the adjacency matrix. The adjacency matrix A is sparse and indicates edge connections, with A(i,j)=1 denoting an edge connecting vertices i and j, and A(i,j)=0 otherwise.

The 3D graphical convolution (GC) Encoder consists in 7 blocks, comprising 4 down-sampling GC blocks followed by 3 1D convolutions, to achieve a lower dimensional latent space (z). The decoder consists of 3 1D transpose convolutions followed by 4 up-sampling GC blocks and 1 GC layer, to reconstruct the 3D facial shape vertices.

2D ViT Pre-training

After obtaining a latent space (z) that represents the 3D facial geometry of babies, the next step is to extract meaningful features from 2D baby images and map them to the learned latent space. To do so, we first perform a pre-training of a ViT using the GMML framework (Atito et al., 2022).

The ViT takes an input image ℐ∈RH×W×C and tokenizes it by dividing ℐ into n flattened 2D patches of size p×p×C pixels. Here, H,W, and C represent the height, width, and number of channels of the input image, and n is the total number of patches. Each patch is then projected into d hidden dimensions using a linear layer. To preserve the relative spatial relationships between the patches, a learnable position embedding is incorporated into the patch embedding, serving as an input to the transformer encoder. The transformer encoder (E2D) consists of L consecutive blocks of multi-head self-attention and multi-layer perceptron (MLP) operations (see Fig. 4).

Vision transformer block.

Following the approach outlined in Atito et al. (2022), we design an AE network based on a ViT as encoder and a light decoder. This design allows us to pre-train the ViT to learn features from baby faces, which is particularly valuable given our limited dataset for training the transformer. The ViT is pre-trained with the objective of recovering the original image ℐ from the corrupted GMML image. The corrupted images are obtained by adding noise, cropping, rotating and/or adding alien agents (Atito et al., 2022). Once the ViT is pre-trained, we perform transfer learning and fine-tuning for the task of 2D-3D face reconstruction.

The lightweight decoder is composed by two MLPs with ReLU nonlinearity and a transposed convolution layer to restore the output to image space. Since the GMML architecture incorporates transformer blocks and MLP layers in an isotropic manner, specific transformer blocks can function as a decoder, introducing skip connections from several intermediate transformer blocks to the decoder (see Fig. 5).

For this purpose, we use a fully connected layer that takes as input the vector resulting from concatenating the features extracted from the three 2D ViT encoders (see Fig. 7).

While multi-view fusion and ViTs have become increasingly common in computer vision tasks, our approach introduces a novel structure tailored specifically for view-aware 3D reconstruction. Rather than relying on a standard feature-level fusion strategy (e.g., simple averaging or early concatenation), our model explicitly preserves view-specific information by assigning a dedicated 2D encoder to each input perspective (frontal, left, and right). Each encoder learns geometry-aware representations that are adapted to its respective view-point, capturing both observed and occluded facial structures.

The subsequent multi-view reconstruction layer performs joint reasoning over these complementary latent features to synthesize a complete 3D face, enabling consistent reconstruction even under self-occlusion or asymmetric viewpoints. This design moves beyond conventional pipelines that apply fusion prior to 3DMM fitting or use ViTs solely for global attention, by instead performing structured, cross-view integration of geometric features. To the best of our knowledge, this targeted and modular fusion strategy is novel within the context of infant 3D face reconstruction and contributes to the robustness of our method.

Training strategy

The training of the multi-view network is conducted in three phases due to the architectural complexity and modular design of our framework. Although a fully end-to-end training using synthetic data is technically feasible, we adopt a staged approach to improve training stability, simplify model evaluation, and allow for greater flexibility in future extensions.

This strategy enables more stable convergence by isolating core components such as the shape decoder, 2D encoder, and multi-view reconstruction module. It also facilitates transparent benchmarking across different configurations (e.g., single-view vs. multi-view input, CNN vs. ViT encoders), and promotes modularity, making components like the shape decoder reusable without full re-training.

The common training details are the model selection, the optimizer, the number of epochs (nep=400), the batch size (B=64) and the momentum value (ψ=0.9). A modified version of the stochastic gradient descent is used as training optimizer. In each training phase, the final model selection is based on the checkpoint weights that yield the best performance on the validation set among the 400 epochs.

In Phase 1, the 3D BabyMorph AE is trained with weight decay of 0.0005, and learning rate of 0.008. The learning rate is adjusted after each epoch with a rate decay of 0.98.

In Phase 2, the 3D encoder is replaced with the 2D ViT. Three different ViTs are trained in parallel, one for each profile (frontal, left, right) while the 3D decoder weights are frozen. The ViT weights are initialized with the weights obtained from the pre-training. The learning rate is set to 0.01. No weight decay is used, nor learning rate decay.

The Phase 3, the Multi-view BabyMorph network is trained with the same specifications as in Phase 2. The only frozen weights are those of the 3D decoder.

Experiments and evaluation

A key hypothesis of our work is that performing multi-view reconstruction improves the accuracy of the resulting 3D shape compared to using a single-view. To evaluate this hypothesis, we compare the quantitative and qualitative results of reconstructing the babies 3D facial morphologies from the frontal single-view (see Fig. 6) and from multiple images (see Fig. 7).

To systematically assess the contribution of each architectural component, we structured our experiments as a set of ablation studies covering three main aspects: (1) comparing the original BabyNet AE to our modified DAE with enhanced convolutional layers; (2) evaluating the impact of different 2D encoders by comparing a CNN-based architecture with a ViT; and (3) analyzing the benefits of single-view versus multi-view reconstruction, including the effect of multi-feature fusion and region-weighted loss masking.

Evaluation on real data

To assess the performance of the proposed 3D DAE and the BabyMorph networks (both single- and multi-view variants), we evaluated them on two independent real-world datasets.

Children’s National Hospital (CNH) dataset (Washington DC): From the total of 151 available 3D textured scans, 50 previously unseen cases were reserved as a real test set. These scans include full 3D geometry and were not used during training. Although photographs were captured alongside the 3D scans, they do not consistently align with the desired frontal, left, and right viewpoints. To address this, we rendered synthetic 2D images from the textured 3D scans at the appropriate angles. Since accurate ground truth geometry is available, this dataset enables direct quantitative evaluation of the reconstructed 3D shapes.

Hospital del Mar dataset (Barcelona): this dataset contains real RGB photographs of infants, captured under clinical conditions. However, full 3D ground truth scans are not available. Instead, we rely on a set of biometric measurements for indirect evaluation (see Fig. 1). As such, this dataset is used to assess the generalization of our models to in-the-wild clinical data, where only image-based appearance cues and indirect ground truth are available.

3D graph convolutional autoencoder architecture

Two different AEs are compared, the 3D BabyNet AE (Morales et al., 2023) and the proposed 3D BabyMorph AE. To improve the robustness and accuracy of the latent space representation of the 3D facial morphologies, we modify the layers and latent size of the 3D BabyNet AE (Morales et al., 2023). Instead of using a fully connected layer to obtain a reduced latent space of dimension d=64, we apply multiple 1D convolutions to reduce the channel space and represent the faces in a latent space of dimension d=976 (see Fig. 3).

ViTs vs CNNs

We compare the performance of multiple ViTs and CNNs for extracting meaningful features from 2D images in a medium/small dataset for baby face reconstruction. The one that achieves the best performance is then choosen as the 2D encoder for the 2D-3D reconstruction task.

In our experiments, we implement self-supervised training using the ViT, and ArcFace, which is a CNN, architecture. We employed the Tiny (ViT-T) (Wu et al., 2022), Small (ViT-S) and Base (ViT-B) (Touvron et al., 2022) variants with 224×224 input image size, 16×16 patch size, 12 consecutive multi-head self-attention and MLP blocks. ViT-T, ViT-S and ViT-B have 192, 384 and 768 hidden dimensions and 3, 6, and 12 heads on each multi-head self-attention layer, respectively. For the ArcFace architectures presented by Deng et al. (2019), we use iResNet50 and iResNet34. These two variants are referred to as ArcFace50 and ArcFace34 throughout the paper, corresponding to their respective ResNet-based backbones.

The different networks were trained using the GMML self-supervised training, using the ℓ1 loss on image-to-image error between the original image and the reconstructed (see Eq. (11)).

Single-view vs multi-view reconstruction

To better understand the contribution of each component of our proposed BabyMorph framework, we conducted a series of ablation experiments. These experiments were designed to evaluate both the effect of using multiple views (frontal and lateral) compared to a single view, and the impact of specific architectural components. In particular, we analyzed the role of multi-level feature fusion from the ViT backbone and the introduction of a region-weighted curvature mask in the loss function. The goal is to isolate and quantify how each design choice contributes to overall 3D face reconstruction quality. Tables and quantitative results in the corresponding results (see Section 6.3) section highlight the performance differences across these variations.

Multi-level features fusion

We explore the impact of incorporating features from the lower blocks of the ViT into the 3D reconstruction. The motivation of fusing features from multiple blocks is to provide richer and improved features. Lower blocks are likely to encompass finer color and texture details, whereas higher blocks are expected to contain more contextual information.

In our approach, we input the feature maps from block 12 of the ViT into the 3D GC decoder, and explore the combination of feature maps from various blocks of the transformers. We explore feature fusion of blocks 6, 8, 10 and 12 based on the best results reported by Atito et al. (2022).

Region-weighted mask

In this experiment, we evaluate the impact of adding a curvature region-weighted mask in the training loss. As a major challenge for 2D-3D reconstruction is the loss of finer details, the curvature mask is designed to mitigate this problem by forcing the network to pay more attention to regions with high curvature. The underlying rationale is that it guides the network to better preserve geometric details by amplifying the learning signal in high-curvature (i.e., high-informative) regions, thereby improving the reconstruction of facial features and expression-related details.

The curvature mask is controlled by two parameters: the curvature scaling weight β and the suppression threshold Othreshold. To identify appropriate values for these parameters, we perform a sensitivity analysis over a predefined range. The evaluation of Othreshold is conducted based on qualitative inspection of the resulting curvature masks, using threshold values from 0 to 0.20 with a step size of 0.05. In contrast, the curvature weighting factor β is evaluated quantitatively using the set {0, 1, 40, 80, 100}. All parameter configurations are evaluated under identical training settings on the single-view BabyMorph to isolate the effect of the curvature-weighted loss parameters.

Comparison with state-of-the-art adult face models

To demonstrate the importance of using a baby-specific model for accurate 3D face reconstruction in infants, we conducted both qualitative and quantitative comparisons against two established methods (one single-view and one multi-view) on the same test set from the CNH dataset.

For single-view reconstruction, we used DECA (Feng et al., 2021), which is based on the FLAME 3DMM. Since FLAME is not natively compatible with our BabyFM GT meshes, we aligned the DECA outputs to the BabyFM scans using common facial landmarks. After alignment, we computed the point-to-surface distance error between the reconstructed mesh and the GT scan to quantify accuracy.

For multi-view reconstruction, we applied MVF-Net (Wu et al., 2019), which takes frontal, left, and right profile views as input and uses the BFM topology for 3D face reconstruction. Similar to the DECA evaluation, we used landmark-based alignment and computed the point-to-surface error for each reconstructed mesh.

These evaluations allow us to directly compare how general-purpose models (trained on adult faces using PCA-based 3DMMs) perform on infant data, and they highlight the limitations these approaches face in this specific demographic.

Robustness to view-point variations

To evaluate the robustness of the proposed multi-view model to variations in the view-point of the input 2D images, we conducted a controlled experiment by simulating non-ideal capture conditions. Specifically, we perturbed the canonical camera angles using random sampling from a Gaussian distribution, where the standard deviation (σ) of the angle was varied to introduce increasing levels of view-point noise.

For each triplet of views in the CNH dataset, we applied random angular offsets drawn from a Gaussian distribution with σ values ranging from 0° to 50°, in increments of 10°. This simulates uncontrolled environments in which input views are no longer perfectly orthogonal or aligned. The resulting perturbed views were then used to render new input images, which were passed through our reconstruction pipeline.

We quantitatively measured the reconstruction accuracy at each noise level using the point-to-point distance metric between the predicted mesh and the GT scan. This analysis allows us to assess how sensitive or robust our model is to deviations from ideal camera placement during multi-view capture.

Region-weighted mask

We report the results of the sensitivity analysis for the curvature-weighted loss parameters Othreshold and β.

Fig. 14 presents qualitative visualizations of the curvature masks obtained using different values of Othreshold ranging from 0 to 0.20. When no threshold is applied, the curvature maps exhibit noisy responses across relatively flat regions. As the threshold increases, low-magnitude curvature responses are progressively suppressed, resulting in cleaner masks that emphasize anatomically meaningful regions such as the mouth, chin, and nasal area. For higher threshold values, some finer curvature details are lost, indicating excessive suppression. Overall, the qualitative behavior of the mask remains stable for moderate threshold values. Based on this analysis, we set Othreshold=0.10 for all experiments, which balances noise reduction with preservation of clinically relevant curvature features.

Qualitative sensitivity analysis of effect of the Othreshold on the curvature masks..

Table 6 reports the quantitative reconstruction performance for different values of the curvature weighting factor β. When β=0, corresponding to no curvature weighting, performance reflects the baseline configuration. Increasing β leads to a consistent reduction in MEE, with improvements up to β=80. Beyond this value, further increases result in marginal changes in accuracy and a slight increase in error variability. These results demonstrate that the curvature-weighted loss improves reconstruction accuracy when appropriately parameterized, while remaining stable across a reasonable range of parameter values. Based on these observations, we select β=80, which achieves consistent performance gains without sacrificing accuracy in other regions or increasing the error variance. Overall, this ablation confirms that the method is robust to reasonable variations of β and that the chosen value lies in a plateau region of performance.

Discussion

We developed and evaluated a 3D face reconstruction algorithm that creates 3D representations of the morphology of the baby’s face from 2D uncalibrated photographs acquired with smartphone cameras.

The algorithm uses a 3D DAE to accurately represent the shape of the baby face in a low-dimensional space. This was achieved by using the 3D BabyMorph AE that robustly encoded corrupted and noisy data, as well as fine morphological details. Our experiments suggests that there is a trade-off between reconstruction accuracy and reduction in the size of the latent space. The larger the latent space, the higher is the accuracy of the network to reconstruct fine details. This was also noted in the testing of the 3D BabyNet AE (Morales et al., 2023), where the reduction of the latent space size using fully connected layers led to the lost of finer details and overfitting on the training data (see Fig. 11). Unlike our approach, the 3D BabyNet AE also struggles to represent new data from scans not seen during training. Given our aim of using the obtained reconstructions for clinical diagnosis, we chose to use a larger latent space to achieve higher reconstruction accuracy.

To extract 2D features from baby faces from multi-view photographs (i.e., frontal, left and right views), we used a baby-targeted feature extractor. We pre-trained a 2D encoder specialized on extracting meaningful features from baby faces in different views. We compared CNNs and ViT networks to determine the most suitable architecture for our problem. The results showed that ViT-S outperformed the ArcFace (CNNs) in extraction meaningful features considering the limited available data. The reason why ArcFace performs well in adults but not in babies could be attributed to its training on millions of images. When working with limited baby data, ViTs proved to be more effective in capturing finer details compared to CNNs. This was noticeable in the reconstructed images where ViTs showed less smoothing.

Balancing the trade-off between the performance in reconstruction and the number of parameters should is essential to prevent the network from becoming overly computationally expensive. ViT-S has 22 million parameters compared to the 80 million parameters of the ViT-B. Given the similar performance of ViT-S and ViT-B, we opted for ViT-S as the backbone structure for feature extraction to optimize computational requirements.

Our goal is to achieve a 3D reconstruction from multi-view photographs that captures the identity represented by the 3D GT scan of the baby. This may not necessarily be reflected by a low MEE, and/or NME. These metrics serve well for assessing global shape precision but not for analyzing perceptual changes on surfaces, such as identity or facial expressions. For these reasons, we included a well-known metric that better captures perceptual changes in our evaluation, namely the angle between the surface normals θnomals (Piotraschke & Blanz, 2016). Therefore, the final network architectute was choosen based on a combination of MEE, NME and θnomals.

Our hypothesis that generating 3D reconstructions from multiple viewpoints yields more accurate results compared to using only the frontal 2D image was quantitatively and qualitatively confirmed. The observed improvement, quantified by the three evaluated metrics, was statistically significant. The incorporation of the lateral views to reconstruct the 3D facial morphology improved the overall reconstruction and significantly reduces reconstruction error in clinically critical regions such as the ears, nose, mouth, and chin (see Fig. 12). This can be attributed to the loss of depth perception and the appearance of occlusions in certain facial parts when projecting the 3D face geometry to the frontal view. By combining information from the three views (frontal, right and left profiles), the network recovers the missing information in the frontal view.

The incorporation of the high- and low-level feature fusion from different layers of the ViT improved the mapping into the latent space of the 3D baby face morphology, helping the algorithm to recover finer morphological details. We showed that features fusion significantly improved the mouth and nose reconstruction when performing the region-base analysis on the direction of surface normal vectors. Moreover, the use of a region-based mask in the training loss improved recovering the facial expression, which is better measured by region analysis of the surface normal vectors. This approach significantly improved the reconstruction of the mouth, chin, and forehead regions.

The Multi-view BabyMorph network was extensively compared with BabyNet (Morales et al., 2023), which, to our knowledge is the only other approach specifically designed for 3D baby face reconstruction. We evaluated both networks on a test set consisting of 50 scans from Children’s National Hospital, which neither network had seen during training or validation (see Table 3). Our network significantly outperformed the BabyNet for both single-view and multi-view approach. This can be attributed to the development of a population-specific image feature extractor tailored for babies. In contrast, the pre-trained ArcFace (Deng et al., 2019) used in the BabyNet was trained mainly on adults (age range between 18–80 years). These findings are aligned with the premise that population-specific models are needed to better represent the target population. To demonstrate the generalizability of our model, we also evaluated it on an independent data set from Hospital del Mar, where it achieved results comparable to those obtained on the Children’s National Hospital data. Using separate encoders per view increases the parameter count from 23M to 72M and raises feature extraction time from 1.55 s to 2.31 s, but this overhead is moderate relative to the benefits: reconstruction error decreases from 3.45±1.03 mm to 2.98±0.91 mm. In contexts where precision is critical, such as in clinical applications, higher reconstruction accuracy is prioritized over minimal computational cost. Our approach substantially reduces errors, particularly in clinically important regions such as the chin and mouth, making the trade-off acceptable for the intended application.

Our comparative analysis with state-of-the-art models trained on adult faces (e.g., DECA and MVF-Net) clearly demonstrates that these approaches are not well-suited for reconstructing infant faces. Both qualitative and quantitative results reveal that adult-based models fail to capture the specific anatomical and morphological characteristics of infant facial geometry, often leading to distorted or overly smoothed reconstructions. In contrast, the proposed Multi-view BabyMorph model, designed specifically for infants, achieves significantly higher accuracy and visual fidelity. These findings underscore the need for age-specific modeling in 3D face reconstruction and validate our hypothesis that adult models cannot be effectively generalized to pediatric populations. Furthermore, our results may not be directly comparable to the performance of adult models, as they were not intended to work with nor tested on the same type of data (target population). However, our quantitative results indicate similar performance in a population-specific database of babies. There is another distinction between our work and popular models reported in the literature. We reconstruct the region of the ears, which is important to evaluate in newborns and babies, as the ear morphology can indicate a genetic conditions. For a fair comparison with other models, we excluded the ears region, as most state-of-the-art adult models do not include ears. Without considering the ears, our average NME using the single- and multi-view BabyMorph was 3.78±0.74% and 3.29±0.66%. In comparison, the top performance of the state-of-the-art single-view adult model, i.e., SADRNet (Ruan et al., 2021) reported a NME of 3.25% on the AFLW2000-3D dataset (Yin et al., 2017).

For multi-view adult 3D face reconstruction, the usual reported error is the root mean squared error (RMSE) of the face on the FaceScape database (Yang et al., 2020), also without considering the ears. The top performing state-of-the-art multi-view adult networks are HRN (Lei et al., 2023), MVFNet (Wu et al., 2019) and DFNRMVS, which reported a RMSE of 1.51 ± 1.79 mm, 2.12 ± 1.66 mm, 2.41 ± 2.61 mm, respectively. In comparison, the multi-view BabyMorph network obtained a RMSE of 2.62 ± 0.58 mm. This comparative analysis shows that our models achieved a similar performance in reconstructing faces of babies with potentially higher mean error and lower standard deviation. In the discussion of these competitive results, it is important to highlight that the images used to evaluate the network performance are babies under no controlled environment, making it a challenging dataset.

The results obtained are competitive in both single-view and multi-view 2D-3D reconstruction when compared to the state-of-the-art reconstruction algorithms in adults databases, with errors are in the same range. The results on view-point variations confirm that the proposed BabyMorph model is robust to moderate view-point deviations, maintaining reliable reconstruction performance even under non-ideal input conditions. This demonstrates the model’s practical applicability in real-world, unconstrained image capture scenarios. As an extension of this work, the multi-view BabyMorph architecture could be applied to target the 2D-3D reconstructions from triplets of photographs of other 3D objects, not restricted to anatomical shapes. To do so, the pipeline would need to be re-trained for the specific 3D shape targeted.

Our method has several limitations. First, our dataset is small in comparison to the datasets used to train adult models. This is not surprising, considering the limited amount of related research focused on newborns and children. Moreover, craniofacial photographic and photogrammetric data for children has been collected in conjunction with rare diseases, another major contributing factor to the inequity of data availability. Thus, developing solutions tailored for infant and young patients with limited data, as proposed in this paper, is crucial. Another limitation is the occurrence of larger errors in regions such as the mouth and chin, possibly linked to the large variability in movements and facial expressions within these specific regions across the evaluated datasets. Despite our improvements in the finer detail reconstruction, Fig. 13 shows that extreme expressions, such as the opening of the mouth, were challenging to reconstruct. In future work, we plan to develop a model that disentangles identity and expression, allowing the network to recover better expressions from limited datasets (Alomar et al., 2022).

Other possible directions to reduce errors include performing texture reconstruction and adding the differential render error in the training loss, and by adding more variability in the paired 2D-3D synthetic data used to train the network. This could help the network in establishing a more robust relationship between the 2D images and the 3D representation and addressing the underrepresented facial expressions or face asymmetries. Ultimately, we intend to use the 3D baby face model to support the detection of genetic dysmorphia with simple, low-cost and uncalibrated smartphone technology when 3D photogrammetry scanning is not available, or it is prohibitively expensive and cumbersome to use.

Despite being trained on synthetic data, the model demonstrates the ability to generalize and reconstruct real images. This is shown in its performance on the Hospital del Mar dataset, where it achieves results comparable to those obtained with the synthetic data. Nonetheless, performing a correction or domain adaptation of the real image texture in the test sets could reduce the reconstruction errors of the network. For this reason, as future work it could be interesting to improve the texture domain to a more realistic texture, such as creating a non-linear texture model or performing style transfer to the input images to match with the synthetic generated texture distribution.

Conclusion

We present the first method for reconstructing the 3D face of babies from multiple uncalibrated photographs acquired with smartphone cameras. The Multi-view BabyMorph method was tailored for the specific appearance of baby face and their robust reconstruction from small training datasets. The approach relies on a ViT feature extraction network that maps the image information to a latent space that encodes the 3D facial morphology of babies. By using frontal and profile photographs, we significantly improved the reconstruction accuracy compared to using only a frontal photograph. The model shows robustness to moderate pose variations, supporting its practical use in unconstrained real-world scenarios. Hence, our approach can reconstruct 3D faces with accuracy similar to 3D photogrammetry and could be adequate for medial diagnosis. This approach has the potential to enhance the accessibility to craniofacial diagnosis and genetic screening by providing a cost-effective, simple, and accessible screening tool suitable for limited-resource settings. Moreover, the novel proposed transformer multi-view architecture has the potential to be applied to other 3D reconstruction tasks from triplets of photographs.
