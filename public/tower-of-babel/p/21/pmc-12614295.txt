The Energetic Collapse of the Alzheimer's Brain: Metabolic Inflexibility Across Cells and Networks
Authors: Nicholas J Constantino, Clair C Ashley, Shannon L Macauley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlzheimer's disease (AD) is more than just amyloid and tau. While often described as a disease of metabolic dysfunction, AD can more accurately be described as a disorder of metabolic inflexibility that leads to bioenergetic failure. In the healthy brain, neurons, glia, and vascular cells dynamically share and switch between different fuel sources (e.g., glucose, lactate, ketones, and fatty acids) to match functional demand. In AD, this adaptability is progressively lost because cellular metabolism is actively reprogrammed to support neuroinflammatory and disease‐associated processes at the cost of neuronal function. Microglia, in particular, upregulate glycolytic metabolism, alter lipid handling, and prioritize immune functions, which actively depletes the brain's energy supply. These adaptations are initially compensatory but ultimately trap the brain in a rigid metabolic program that deprioritizes neuronal support. This metabolic shift unfolds along a biphasic trajectory: early, glia‐driven hypermetabolism aligned with inflammation, followed by late‐stage brain hypometabolism and energy collapse that leads to neuronal dysfunction. System‐level consequences include altered excitability, decreased network connectivity, sleep disruption, and cognitive decline. Critically, these changes feed forward to accelerate AD pathogenesis: glycolytically biased microglia and stressed neurons promote amyloid‐β production, tau release, and protein aggregation, adding to metabolic rigidity. Evidence from human neuroimaging studies, brain/cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) multi‐omic studies, and preclinical studies demonstrate that shifts in glycolytic flux, tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) intermediates, and lipid metabolism parallel amyloid and tau pathology and cognition decline. We hypothesize that these metabolic programs, while initially protective, are chronically maladaptive yet reversible. We propose that restoring metabolic flexibility can mitigate amyloid and tau pathology, neuronal loss, and functional decline. Ongoing preclinical studies and clinical trials are actively exploring metabolism as a therapeutic target in AD. Collectively, these findings define AD as a disorder of metabolic inflexibility, where adaptive shifts in cellular metabolism become pathologically rigid and drive disease progression, while offering a promising target for therapeutic intervention in AD. Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, glia, metabolism

Introduction

Metabolic flexibility was first defined in the context of systemic physiology and skeletal muscle in the early 2000s (De and Lj 2000). It refers to an organism's ability to sense, access, and utilize different energy substrates to maintain homeostasis across nutritional (fed vs. fasted) or functional (at rest vs. exercise) states (Goodpaster and Sparks 2017; Palmer and Clegg 2022; Kelley et al. 1999; Smith et al. 2018; Galgani and Fernández‐Verdejo 2021). Efficient substrate switching is now recognized as a defining feature of metabolic health. Despite its relevance, the concept is rarely applied to the brain, even though such flexibility is essential for sustaining the brain's high energetic demand. Although the brain accounts for only ~2% of body weight, it consumes nearly 20% of total energy (Wang et al. 2011; Cunnane et al. 2020; Padamsey and Rochefort 2023; Mintun et al. 2001; Raichle et al. 2001), requiring a continuous and adaptable fuel supply to support sensing, movement, cognition, and basic physiological functions (Mintun et al. 2001; Raichle et al. 2001; Rink and Khanna 2011; Hoge et al. 1999). This adaptability depends on coordinated metabolic flux among neurons, glia, and the vasculature, as well as metabolic integration with peripheral organs. While glucose is the brain's primary fuel, cerebral metabolism is far more dynamic—capable of shifting to lactate, ketones, or other substrates depending on availability, behavioral state, and circadian timing. Importantly, metabolic inflexibility is distinct from general bioenergetic failure. While bioenergetic decline reflects a reduced capacity for ATP generation, metabolic inflexibility describes the loss of metabolic dynamics and adaptability—where cells remain metabolically active but locked into rigid fuel programs that prioritize specific inflammatory or stress pathways at the expense of neuronal support. In Alzheimer's disease (AD), this metabolic flexibility is lost early—cell autonomous metabolic reprogramming emerges early in glia, undermining intercellular cooperation with neurons, and driving an eventual network‐level bioenergetic collapse in the brain.

In order to meet its energy demands, the brain can utilize a range of fuel sources, including carbohydrates, fats, ketone bodies, lactate, and other metabolites (Cunnane et al. 2020; García‐Rodríguez and Giménez‐Cassina 2021; Tracey et al. 2018; Jensen et al. 2020; Magistretti et al. 2018). The choice of fuel depends on physiological state and activity level‐ whether the organism is resting, quietly awake, or performing complex tasks (Raichle et al. 2001; Hoge et al. 1999; Aalling et al. 2018; Caporale et al. 2021; Kay et al. 2016; Paulson et al. 2010; Mangia et al. 2009; Madsen et al. 1991; Constantino et al. 2025; Carroll et al. 2022; Naylor et al. 2012; Braga et al. 2024). This flexibility occurs at multiple levels: the whole organism, within neural networks, across different cell types, and within individual cells. Within the brain, neurons, glia, and vascular cells each have unique metabolic demands and signatures, driven by both cell autonomous and cell non‐autonomous goals (Magistretti et al. 2018; Pellerin et al. 2012; Tsacopoulos 1996; Monsorno et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2025; Nagy et al. 2018; Gustafsson et al. 2024; Chausse et al. 2024). Importantly, the brain largely does not produce its own fuel; it relies on peripheral metabolism to supply metabolites on demand (Padamsey and Rochefort 2023; Magistretti et al. 2018; Pellerin et al. 2012; Kadry et al. 2020; Carroll and Macauley 2019). Thus, systemic metabolic states have a profound impact on brain function. This complexity makes the brain uniquely vulnerable to metabolic stress or injury.

Metabolic inflexibility arises when there are impairments in any component of this system: fuel access, sensing, transport, utilization, switching, or sharing. For example, fasting or hypoglycemia restricts available fuels, while hyperglycemia and type‐2 diabetes cause fuel excess‐ yet both can damage the brain (Carroll et al. 2022, 2024; Takeda et al. 2010; Xue et al. 2019; Lamy Christophe et al. 2014; Cryer 2007; Suh et al. 2007). Disruptions in sensing mechanisms—such as dysfunction of ATP‐sensitive potassium (K ATP) channels, insulin signaling, or hypothalamic circuits—limit the brain's ability to regulate energy use (Constantino et al. 2025; Braga et al. 2024; Carroll and Macauley 2019; Lamy Christophe et al. 2014; Varin et al. 2015; Ashrafi et al. 2017; Chen, Huang, et al. 2023). Transport deficits, such as vascular insulin resistance or impaired glucose and monocarboxylate transport, further compromise energy delivery even when peripheral supply is sufficient (Braga et al. 2024; Chen, Joo, et al. 2023; Reno et al. 2016; Zhao et al. 2009). Fuel switching is a fundamental process of adaptability where the brain shifts fuel preference across the 24‐h day, favoring carbohydrates during active periods and fatty acids/ketones during sleep when dietary input and ATP demand are reduced (Aalling et al. 2018; Caporale et al. 2021; Madsen et al. 1991; Constantino et al. 2025; DiNuzzo and Nedergaard 2017; Belanger et al. 2011; Zhang et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2022; Dennison et al. 2025). Loss of this capacity promotes sleep loss, neuronal dysfunction, and neurodegeneration (Constantino et al. 2025; Carroll et al. 2022; Chausse et al. 2024; Hirata et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2020; Johnson and Macauley 2024; Minhas et al. 2024; Nôga et al. 2024). At the cellular level, brain homeostasis depends on cooperation and resource sharing between different cell types, with distinct metabolic processes compartmentalized among them. A prime example of this is the astrocyte‐neuron lactate shuttle (ANLS) where astrocytes process glucose glycolytically and shuttle lactate to neurons, where neurons use lactate to fuel oxidative phosphorylation or mitochondrial respiration (Magistretti et al. 2018; Pellerin et al. 2012; Tsacopoulos 1996; Belanger et al. 2011). However, when cells are injured or diseased, they can become locked into maladaptive metabolic programs, that divert energy away from homeostatic demands and towards disease‐response pathways. Because brain metabolism is compartmentalized across cell types and dynamically coordinated, loss of flexibility in one cell type or region can deprive neighboring cells, typically neurons, of essential metabolites and create system‐wide rigidity or deficits (Chausse et al. 2024; Johnson and Macauley 2024; Minhas et al. 2024; Xiang et al. 2021; Baik et al. 2019). This metabolic inflexibility compromises metabolic health and drives bioenergetic decline.

Regardless of which aspect of metabolic flexibility is compromised, metabolic inflexibility can stimulate damage via mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, vascular dysfunction, and protein misfolding and aggregation, which feeds forward to cause deficits, like sleep loss, seizures, or cognitive impairment (Baik et al. 2019; Gorelick et al. 2011; Sankar et al. 2020). Maintaining metabolic flexibility is therefore essential for brain survival and function. Any disruption that promotes metabolic inflexibility—whether through energy deficiency, energy excess, or shifts in utilization—ultimately drives disease, injury, and cell death.

In this review, we will describe how AD is a disorder of metabolic inflexibility where the brain fails to utilize, switch, or share fuels across different cell types or neuronal networks which drives AD pathogenesis prior to bioenergetic decline (Figure 1). This shift occurs in response to amyloid pathology and is necessary to support inflammatory, disease‐responsive processes. We first define metabolic flexibility and map the normal fuel handling of metabolites, such as glucose, lactate, ketones, fatty acids, across neurons, and glia relative to highly metabolic functional brain networks. Then, we show how AD impacts metabolic flexibility with a biphasic trajectory: early glia‐driven hypermetabolism followed by late‐stage neuronal hypometabolism. We integrate lipid dysregulation with glucose changes to explain how oxidative stress, lipid droplet accumulation, and impaired flux compound the metabolic crisis that leads to energy failure. We link these mechanisms to functional phenotypes, such as sleep loss, hyperexcitability, neuroinflammation, and cognitive decline. Then we will close by highlighting the therapeutic landscape for metabolic interventions in AD and outline any outstanding questions in the field including cell‐specific contributions, timing of metabolic changes, and reversibility of phenotypes. Since the majority of fuel use in the brain is insulin‐independent, this review will not explore the concept of brain insulin resistance as this topic is elegantly reviewed elsewhere (Rhea et al. 2024; Arnold et al. 2018; Biswas et al. 2025). Rather, this review seeks to understand how Alzheimer's‐related pathology independently causes metabolic inflexibility and suggest this is an important target for treating AD early in the disease process.

In the presymptomatic phase (~15–20 years before cognitive symptoms), amyloid accumulation is accompanied by glial‐driven hypermetabolism, reflecting early metabolic reprogramming and inflammation; a catalyst for metabolic inflexibility. As tau pathology spreads and neuronal dysfunction increases, this compensatory hypermetabolism fails, leading to progressive metabolic decline or hypometabolism, neuronal loss, and brain atrophy that parallel cognitive impairment (clinical dementia rating; CDR = 0.5 or greater). The transition from glial‐driven hypermetabolism to brain hypometabolism represents the loss of metabolic flexibility‐ an inability of cells and networks to dynamically adapt fuel utilization to support healthy brain function, culminating in energy failure and neurodegeneration in symptomatic AD.

Fuel Types

The body can use different fuels depending on availability and energy demand. The main fuels are carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids (Liu et al. 2025). During wake‐associated activities when energy supply is typically abundant, the body primarily relies on carbohydrates, which provide a quick and easily accessible fuel source (Constantino et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2021; Irmen et al. 2025; Rynders et al. 2020; Melanson et al. 2017; Spengler et al. 2000; Moorsel et al. 2016). In the gut, carbohydrates are broken down into simple sugars such as glucose, and are released into the bloodstream to be processed by the liver or used as fuel (Goodman 2010). Glucose is then delivered to tissues throughout the body, including the brain. Glucose delivery can either be under steady‐state conditions or during periods of increased demand. Glucose is important because it not only produces ATP for energy but also serves as a biosynthetic substrate for other molecules (Constantino et al. 2025; DeBerardinis et al. 2007; Williams et al. 2020; Dienel 2019). Through different pathways, glucose can act as a building block to make nucleotides, neurotransmitters, and downstream metabolites, such as glycogen or lactate (Magistretti et al. 2018; Pellerin et al. 2012; Belanger et al. 2011; Bismut et al. 1995). This makes glucose both an efficient and preferred energy source for ATP generation as well as an essential metabolite for biosynthesis.

The body can also use proteins and lipids as fuel sources (Liu et al. 2025). Proteins are broken down into amino acids, which are normally used as biosynthetic substrates for muscles, enzymes, neurotransmitters, and other cellular components (Torres et al. 2023). They can be catabolized for fuel when energy is scarce, but this is not the body's preferred method of generating ATP because it is inefficient, generates neurotoxic ammonia, and depletes the pool of biosynthetic precursors (Liu et al. 2025; Torres et al. 2023; Ling et al. 2023). Conversely, lipids serve as one of the body's main energy reserves. During fasting or low fuel availability, stored lipids are broken down and released into the blood as free fatty acids (FFAs) (Kersten 2023). Tissues can use FFAs directly to make large amounts of ATP. While molecule per molecule, FFAs can generate more ATP than glucose, it occurs at a slower rate, consumes more oxygen, and generates more reactive oxygen species (ROS) than glucose (Quijano et al. 2016; Rosca et al. 2012; Jaswal et al. 2011). This slow but robust energy generation makes FFAs more suitable for periods of low activity and/or steady energy demand (Goodpaster and Sparks 2017; Jaswal et al. 2011; Romijn et al. 1993; Pi et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2015; Brooks 1997). The liver can also convert lipids into ketone bodies during fasting, when fat breakdown is high (Fukao et al. 2004). Ketone bodies readily enter cells through monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) and are used to generate ATP. In summary, carbohydrates are the body's primary, fast, and versatile fuel, while lipids provide the main backup during scarcity. Proteins can be used in emergencies but are less efficient. Lipids are favored over proteins because they yield more ATP, and they can be further processed into FFAs and ketone bodies to sustain energy when glucose is limited.

Cell Specific Fuel Use in the Brain

Glucose is the primary fuel source in the brain and crosses the blood–brain barrier via endothelial cell glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1). Within the brain, neurons (GLUT2, GLUT3, GLUT4), microglia (GLUT1, GLUT3), astrocytes (GLUT1), and oligodendrocytes (GLUT1) all directly take up glucose to support cellular function (Table 1) (Cunnane et al. 2020; Pellerin et al. 2012; Lamy Christophe et al. 2014; Varin et al. 2015; Ashrafi et al. 2017; Belanger et al. 2011; Asadollahi et al. 2024; Saab et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2014, 2016; Yao et al. 2023). Because the brain is largely insulin‐independent, glucose transport is mainly determined by concentration gradients between the blood, extracellular space, and intracellular compartments, making transport highly sensitive to local glucose availability and allowing for rapid adaptation (Koepsell 2020). Under normal conditions, glucose levels in the blood are significantly higher than in the brain, favoring uptake via endothelial cells (Leegsma‐Vogt et al. 2001). Although all brain cells can import glucose, the transporters they express differ in affinity, reflecting cell type‐specific metabolic demands and flexibility. GLUT1 is a high‐affinity transporter that ensures efficient uptake in cells with high energetic demand, illustrating how important glucose transport across the blood–brain barrier, which is mediated by endothelial cells and astrocytes, to maintain normal brain function (Varin et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2014, 2016; Simpson et al. 2008).

Importantly, neurons and microglia express multiple glucose transporters, suggesting that they have either higher, or more nuanced, energetic demand compared to other cell types and multiple ways of fluxing glucose into the cell, even when glucose availability is limited. GLUT3, an ultra‐high affinity transporter, allows sustained glucose uptake across a wide range of concentrations, and supports energy‐intensive processes in microglia such as surveillance, phagocytosis, and inflammatory signaling (Simpson et al. 2008; Sadeghdoust et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2019). The co‐expression of GLUT1 and GLUT3 in microglia confers metabolic flexibility and ensures a reliable supply of glucose. Neurons also express GLUT3, the ultra‐high affinity transporter, allowing rapid glucose uptake to meet the high energetic demands of synaptic activity and neurotransmission (Simpson et al. 2008). However, neurons also express GLUT2 and GLUT4, which provide further regulatory control of glucose metabolism (Simpson et al. 2008; Arluison, Quignon, Nguyen, et al. 2004; Arluison, Quignon, Thorens, et al. 2004). GLUT2, a low‐affinity transporter, functions as a metabolic sensor, decreasing transport when extracellular glucose levels fall (Roy et al. 2024; Wood and Trayhurn 2003; Mueckler and Thorens 2013; Marty et al. 2007). This reduction of glucose transport signals a need for adaptive responses, such as reduced neuronal firing or a shift to other fuels (Lamy Christophe et al. 2014; Roy et al. 2024). GLUT4 is a moderate‐affinity transporter enriched in the cortex and hippocampus that translocates to the neuronal membrane in response to insulin signaling (Ashrafi et al. 2017; Ren et al. 2014; McNay and Pearson‐Leary 2019; Benomar et al. 2006). This mechanism enhances glucose uptake during periods of energy abundance and contributes to activity‐dependent processes such as memory formation, long‐term potentiation, and sustained firing (Ashrafi et al. 2017; Ren et al. 2014; McNay and Pearson‐Leary 2019; Benomar et al. 2006). This highlights the importance of metabolic flexibility in neurons, where not only do they have robust mechanisms of transporting glucose, but they can also sense extracellular glucose and fine‐tune their activity to match energy availability. Collectively, the differential expression of high, low, and insulin‐responsive glucose transporters showcases the brain's metabolic flexibility. While astrocytes and oligodendrocytes rely primarily on one transporter to ensure their glucose supply, neurons, and microglia express multiple transporters, enabling both continuous energy supply and dynamic regulation of glucose metabolism in response to extracellular availability and cellular energetic demand. This suggests that metabolic programming in neurons and microglia is perhaps more nuanced, underscoring the critical role metabolic flexibility plays in their cellular function.

In addition to glucose, the brain can utilize monocarboxylates, notably lactate and ketone bodies, as fuel (Table 1) (Cunnane et al. 2020; Magistretti et al. 2018). Similar to glucose, monocarboxylate transport is also concentration‐dependent, ensuring that each cell can adjust substrate uptake or release according to the local metabolic environment. Lactate, an end‐product of glycolysis, is present at high levels in the brain relative to the bloodstream (Magistretti et al. 2018; Jalloh et al. 2013). This reflects the brain's high, intrinsic glycolytic capacity and ability to generate its own fuel independent of peripheral lactate supply. In contrast, ketone bodies are predominantly generated by the liver during periods of fasting as products of fatty acid β‐oxidation (Fukao et al. 2004; Kolb et al. 2021). Astrocytes can also produce ketones locally, but this supply is limited, especially when compared to the liver (Kolb et al. 2021; Takahashi et al. 2014). Both lactate and ketone bodies cross the blood–brain barrier through monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) on endothelial cells (Cunnane et al. 2020; Magistretti et al. 2018; Pellerin et al. 2012; Tsacopoulos 1996; Yao et al. 2023; Brooks 2018; Yang et al. 2022). Neurons (MCT2 and MCT3), oligodendrocytes (MCT2, MCT3, and MCT4) and microglia (MCT2 and MCT4) can all transport lactate and ketone bodies, where it is well established that lactate serves as a major energy source for neurons during periods of heightened activity (Zhang et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2016; Yao et al. 2023). Astrocytes (MCT1 and MCT4) are classically thought to supply monocarboxylates, particularly lactate, to neurons during periods of heightened activity, allowing neurons to bypass early steps of glycolysis and rapidly generate ATP (Cunnane et al. 2020; Magistretti et al. 2018; Belanger et al. 2011). However, recent transcriptomic analyses challenge this view. Astrocytic MCT expression appears relatively low, suggesting that other cell types contribute substantially to lactate and ketone body transport (Zhang et al. 2014, 2016; Yao et al. 2023). Indeed, all brain cell types express ketolytic enzymes (BDH1 and OXCT1) required for ketone metabolism, and enzymes needed for both the production and consumption of lactate (LDHA and LDHB), enabling both intracellular use and uptake from extracellular pools (Zhang et al. 2014, 2016; Yao et al. 2023). As with glucose transporters, MCTs differ in affinity and directionality. MCT1, MCT2, and MCT3 are high‐affinity transporters optimized for uptake, whereas MCT4 is a low‐affinity transporter specialized for metabolite efflux (Bosshart et al. 2019; Halestrap 2013; Dimmer et al. 2000). Strikingly, while glial cells express MCT4 to facilitate lactate release or ketone transport, neurons predominantly express high‐affinity MCTs, positioning them to readily uptake lactate when extracellular levels are high or ketone bodies when glucose availability is diminished (Zhang et al. 2014, 2016; Yao et al. 2023). This arrangement underscores the metabolic sensitivity and flexibility of neurons, to adjust fuel use and maintain energy demands with changes in glucose availability. Notably, microglia specifically exhibit high levels of MCT4 compared to other brain cell types (Zhang et al. 2014, 2016; Yao et al. 2023; Monsorno et al. 2023). This profile suggests that microglia are both highly glycolytic and key contributors to extracellular lactate release, a role previously underappreciated (Baik et al. 2019). These findings highlight the sensitivity of microglia to metabolic changes, as well as their high energetic demand and production. Taken together, the ability of all cell types within the brain to transport and utilize monocarboxylates helps sustain neuronal or glial activity, whether that is to support neuronal firing, complex tasks, or neuroinflammatory responses.

Beyond glucose and monocarboxylates, the brain can also use fatty acids as an energy source. Fatty acids are produced via lipolysis between meals and during fasting (Kersten 2023; Ruppert et al. 2025). Circulating fatty acids cross the blood‐brain barrier through endothelial cells via fatty acid transporters (FATP4 and MFSD2A) (Jensen et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2014, 2016; Yang et al. 2022; Pifferi et al. 2021; Bruce et al. 2017). Within the brain, all cell types express varying levels of fatty acid transporters (FATP1, FATP4, CD36, and MFSD2A), indicating a broad capacity to import and utilize fatty acids via β‐oxidation (Jensen et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2014, 2016; Pifferi et al. 2021; Bruce et al. 2017). Outside of being an immediate fuel source for cell types in the brain, fatty acids can undergo further modifications. Once inside the brain, excess fatty acids can be stored as lipid droplets, which provide a readily mobilizable energy reserve under normal conditions (Pifferi et al. 2021; Bruce et al. 2017; Farmer et al. 2019; Kumar et al. 2025). Recent studies suggest that neurons can even metabolize triglycerides to support heightened activity and synaptic function (Kumar et al. 2025). Lipid droplets are formed primarily by astrocytes, where they can be processed into APOE‐containing lipid particles. These particles can then be distributed to neurons, microglia, and oligodendrocytes, to maintain energy balance (Pifferi et al. 2021; Bruce et al. 2017; Farmer et al. 2019; Ioannou et al. 2019). Alternatively, fatty acids can be converted into ketone bodies by astrocytes and released via MCTs for uptake by other cell types in the brain as a fuel source (García‐Rodríguez and Giménez‐Cassina 2021; Jensen et al. 2020; Pifferi et al. 2021; Bruce et al. 2017; Farmer et al. 2019). Therefore, the brain can utilize fatty acids in a variety of ways, as a substrate for β‐oxidation or ketone production, and as energy storage in the form of lipid droplets. This multi‐pathway system underscores the brain's metabolic flexibility, ensuring that energy requirements are met across conditions, while maintaining a critical metabolic reserve to rapidly adapt to changing energy production and demand.

In summary, the brain relies primarily on glucose for energy but preserves metabolic homeostasis through a diverse group of fuels and a high degree of cell‐type cooperation. This integrated network allows neurons and glia to draw upon multiple substrates—glucose, lactate, ketone bodies, and fatty acids—and to shift among them as availability or demand changes. This metabolic flexibility sustains processes such as neuronal signaling, synaptic transmission, and glial activity, while providing a metabolic reserve that buffers the brain against fluctuations in energy supply. Importantly, each cell type exhibits distinct energetic demands and fuel preferences, yet through coordinated activity maintains the metabolic homeostasis of the whole organ. This metabolic flexibility demonstrates the brain's intrinsic capacity to adapt its fuel use and regulatory mechanisms to preserve energetic and functional stability.

Alzheimer's Disease and Metabolism

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a complex neurodegenerative disease marked by progressive cognitive decline, memory loss, and ultimately death (Long and Holtzman 2019). Currently ~7 million Americans are suffering from AD, with an estimated lifetime prevalence of AD of ~20% in women and ~11% in men over the age of 45 (Alzheimer's Association 2024; Rajan et al. 2021; Chêne et al. 2015; Brookmeyer et al. 2018; Holtzman et al. 2011; GBD 2019). Classically, AD pathology follows the amyloid (A), tau (T), and neurodegeneration (N; ATN) framework, where extracellular amyloid‐β aggregates into amyloid plaques and intracellular hyperphosphorylated tau aggregates into neurofibrillary tangles, ultimately leading to neurodegeneration (Long and Holtzman 2019; Jack et al. 2013). Advances in plasma, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and neuroimaging biomarkers have defined the temporal staging of AD. Biomarkers of amyloid plaques, including CSF Aβ40/42 ratios, CSF or plasma p‐tau217, plasma Aβ40/42, or Pittsburgh Compound B (PiB)PET, emerge 15–19 years before symptom onset, indicating that pathological changes begin decades before cognitive decline (Li et al. 2024; Ashton et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2024). Amyloid‐associated markers (CSF p‐tau217) continue to rise 12–14 years prior to symptomology (Li et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2024). At this timepoint, markers of neurodegeneration (CSF neurogranin, SNAP‐25, CSF NfL, plasma NfL) and neuroinflammation (CSF sTREM2, plasma GFAP) are also detectable, demonstrating that neuronal injury and inflammatory responses co‐occur and begin more than a decade before clinical symptoms (Li et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2024). Tau pathology (CSF p‐tau181, p‐tau205, Tau PET) develops later in disease progression, around 7–11 years before symptom onset, coinciding with neurodegeneration (MRI hippocampal atrophy) and continued neuroinflammation (CSF YKL‐40) (Li et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2024). Together, these findings show that modern biomarker assays can accurately estimate years to symptom onset (EYO) as well as provide a timeline for how pathologies change during the 20‐year presymptomatic period in AD.

Beyond the ATN framework, metabolic dysfunction is a defining feature of symptomatic AD. For decades, 2‐deoxy‐2‐[18F]fluoro‐D‐glucose positron emission tomography (FDG‐PET) has been the gold standard for imaging cerebral glucose metabolism, consistently revealing a characteristic regional hypometabolism that remains one of the most robust and reproducible markers of the disease (Jack et al. 2013, 2024). Interestingly, cerebral hypometabolism occurs after changes in amyloid and tau biomarkers and more closely parallels markers of brain atrophy and cognitive decline (McDade et al. 2020; Guo et al. 2021). Therefore, changes in FDG‐PET have long been regarded as a proxy for decreased neuronal metabolism and neurodegeneration. Neuroimaging studies in young, healthy individuals lend further credence to this view. Regions with higher FDG‐PET uptake, reflecting greater metabolic demand, largely overlap with cortical hubs and the default mode network (DMN) (Raichle et al. 2001; Raichle 2015; Vaishnavi et al. 2010; Buckner et al. 2009). These regions share several defining features, including high neuronal activity, synaptic plasticity, and functional connectivity, suggesting FDG‐PET signal is strongly associated with neuronal activity. Interestingly, these networks are also disproportionately susceptible to amyloid pathology, suggesting that their metabolic profile may either contribute to or reflect vulnerability in AD (Raichle et al. 2001; Vaishnavi et al. 2010; Buckner et al. 2009; Bakhtiari et al. 2023; Oh et al. 2016, 2014; Gordon et al. 2018; Benzinger et al. 2013; Cohen et al. 2009; Zhang and Raichle 2010; Fox et al. 2005, 2009; Allaman et al. 2010; Nicholson et al. 2010; Vlassenko et al. 2010). Additional human neuroimaging shows a strong spatial correlation between amyloid plaques and “aerobic glycolysis” (Vaishnavi et al. 2010; Vlassenko et al. 2010; Vlassenko and Raichle 2015; Vlassenko et al. 2018; Goyal et al. 2017, 2023). Described in the cancer field as the Warburg Effect, “aerobic glycolysis” occurs when glucose use exceeds oxygen consumption despite enough oxygen present for mitochondrial respiration and ATP generation (Koppenol et al. 2011). Thus, glucose is used for other processes beyond energy production, including biosynthesis, synaptic plasticity and remodeling, cellular redox maintenance, lactate production, and glycogen synthesis (Vaishnavi et al. 2010; Vlassenko et al. 2010; Vlassenko and Raichle 2015; Vlassenko et al. 2018; Goyal et al. 2017; Blazey et al. 2018; Shannon et al. 2016; Goyal et al. 2014). Together, these findings show that networks vulnerable to amyloid pathology rely heavily on glucose for normal function to support neuronal activity and functional connectivity.

There are, however, several caveats when considering FDG‐PET data. First, FDG‐PET does not capture complete glucose metabolism; instead, it reflects glucose uptake (Zimmer et al. 2022; Trotter et al. 2023). After hexokinase phosphorylates FDG, the tracer becomes trapped, preventing it from being further metabolized by the cell (Zimmer et al. 2022; Trotter et al. 2023). Thus, it is impossible to know how downstream metabolites, like glucose, lactate, or glycogen, or biosynthetic substrates, like glutamate, are impacted by changes in FDG‐PET. Furthermore, PET imaging lacks cellular resolution; therefore, FDG‐PET reflects regional glucose uptake, but does not reveal which cell types drive changes in cerebral metabolism (Zimmer et al. 2022; Trotter et al. 2023). Because most FDG‐PET studies focused on individuals who were already symptomatic, they offer limited information about metabolic alterations during the long presymptomatic period before cognitive decline. Only with the recent development of biomarkers to stage pathological changes in presymptomatic AD can we start to interrogate the link between cellular metabolism and disease progression.

Recent large‐scale brain and CSF proteomic and metabolomic studies consistently demonstrate that disruptions in multiple metabolic processes, including glucose uptake, glycolytic flux, and lipid utilization, are a defining feature of AD, occurring well before individuals manifest cognitive decline (Yu et al. 2023; van Zalm et al. 2023; Berezhnoy et al. 2023; Panyard et al. 2023). Increased levels of glycolytic enzymes (e.g., pyruvate kinase; PKM, aldolase; ALDOA, lactate dehydrogenase B; LDHB, pyruvate kinase; PKM) are observed in the CSF of individuals during the presymptomatic (amyloid+, tau+, but cognitively unimpaired) and symptomatic (MCI or AD) periods, suggesting a metabolic shift occurs in response to or facilitates protein aggregation. Recent CSF metabolomic studies complement the proteomic evidence that energy metabolism is disrupted early in AD (Yu et al. 2023; van Zalm et al. 2023; Berezhnoy et al. 2023; Panyard et al. 2023). Untargeted and targeted metabolomics consistently show that pathways associated with glucose and carbon metabolism are dysregulated (e.g., increased levels of succinylcarnitine) with amyloid and tau burden, while broader shifts in TCA cycle and mitochondrial intermediates are associated with MCI and AD. Whether metabolites or enzymes are increased or decreased depends widely on the stage of disease (amyloid+, tau+), cognitive status, or AD risk factors (APOE4+) (Panyard et al. 2023). Together, this confirms that changes in metabolic flux and therefore flexibility are associated with amyloid and/or tau pathology and changes across the AD continuum.

While FDG‐PET studies suggest that neuronal activity drives glucose need, it also suggests that processes that are metabolically demanding, such as hyperexcitability or neuroinflammation, may selectively alter cerebral metabolism and further contribute to AD progression (Johnson et al. 2020). In fact, recent studies report that regional hypermetabolism, or increased FDG‐PET, occurs in select regions prior to tau pathology, neurodegeneration, and late stage hypometabolism (Bakhtiari et al. 2023; Oh et al. 2016; Gordon et al. 2018; Benzinger et al. 2013; Adams et al. 2019; Chia et al. 2018). In a myriad of clinical studies, increased glucose uptake is associated with increased amyloid pathology, tau burden, neuroinflammation, worse memory performance, and AD risk factors, like APOE4 and TREM2 mutations (Xiang et al. 2021; Bakhtiari et al. 2023; Gordon et al. 2018; Benzinger et al. 2013; Adams et al. 2019; Hanseeuw et al. 2017; Jett et al. 2023). Preclinical studies in rodent models of cerebral amyloidosis, primary tauopathies, and AD risk factors, like human APOE4 knockin or TREM2 knockout mice, also display early brain hypermetabolism or changes in glycolytic flux (Poisnel et al. 2012; Brendel et al. 2016; Harris et al. 2016). Intriguingly, this early hypermetabolism occurs in the same regions that show elevated baseline glucose metabolism or aerobic glycolysis (e.g., DMN and cortical hubs) in human brains (Bakhtiari et al. 2023; Oh et al. 2016, 2014), suggesting that these highly active networks are especially vulnerable to metabolic competition. The precise cellular drivers of FDG‐PET hypermetabolism remains unresolved but likely involve dynamic interactions between neurons, astrocytes, and microglia, with neurons being most vulnerable to metabolic competition.

Historically, neurons and astrocytes were assumed to generate the majority of cerebral FDG‐PET signal, with neuronal synapses being particularly metabolically active (Sokoloff et al. 1977; Sokoloff 1999; Magistretti and Pellerin 1996; Voutsinos‐Porche et al. 2003; Zimmer et al. 2017; Carter et al. 2019; Rocha et al. 2022; Chételat et al. 2020). Important discoveries like the ANLS clearly support this idea, in addition to the neuroimaging studies outlined above (Pellerin and Magistretti 1994). Unequivocally, both astrocytes and neurons undergo metabolic dysfunction in AD (Cunnane et al. 2020; Acosta et al. 2017). We would argue, however, that metabolic reprogramming in glia during presymptomatic AD ultimately causes metabolic inflexibility within the brain and compromises neuronal function during symptomatic AD. This is supported by several high‐dimension, multimodal papers, like SEA‐AD's integrated atlas, that show that as disease progresses, reactive microglia and astrocytes emerge early, while excitatory and inhibitory neuronal loss occurs later (Mathys et al. 2024; Hawrylycz et al. 2024; Gabitto et al. 2024). Pathway analysis in SEA‐AD, reveals glial enrichment of stress/immune programs with metabolic components and neuronal down‐regulation of synaptic and mitochondrial/OXPHOS genes, consistent with a shift towards glial‐first metabolism and subsequent neuronal energetic vulnerability in AD. For example, astrocytes undergo profound metabolic and functional changes across the AD continuum. Similar to microglia, a subpopulation of astrocytes become disease responsive and adopt a GFAP‐high or disease associated (DAA) genetic signature in response to amyloid, with upregulation of complement, aging, lipid metabolism, glycolysis, and phagolysosomal pathways (Habib et al. 2020). These functional changes are associated with alterations in substrate use. Elevated cortical lactate levels (1H‐MRS) that correlate with GFAP and myo‐inositol levels, and increased 11C‐acetate uptake, a metabolite typically enriched in astrocytes, occur in cortical regions with amyloid plaques. Single‐nucleus/cell transcriptomics and proteomic studies further reveal upregulation of glycolytic enzymes (ALDOA, PKM, and LDHA) and downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation and glutamate transporters (GLT1/EAAT2) in AD brains. Collectively, this reflects a loss of metabolic flexibility, where astrocytes replace glucose‐dependent oxidative phosphorylation with glycolysis and acetate metabolism to support a disease reactive phenotype at the expense of homeostatic functions. Reactive astrocytes also accumulate lipid droplets, which cause cholesterol and lipid sequestration, impair lipid trafficking, and compromise membrane dynamics, and ultimately disrupting astrocyte–neuron metabolic coupling and contributing to brain hypometabolism. Therefore, this metabolic shift in astrocytes is associated with a change in function, where essential homeostatic processes in astrocytes that typically sustain neuronal activity, glutamate‐glutamine recycling, K+ buffering, and redox balance are reduced and replaced by proinflammatory, disease reactive pathways (Morabito et al. 2021). Thus, astrocytes adopt a metabolically rigid or inflexible program that weakens astrocyte–neuron metabolic coupling (ANLS) and neuronal support across disease progression.

Aside from astrocytes, recent work suggests that microglia may account for at least 10%–20% of the FDG‐PET signal despite their relatively low abundance in the brain and this percentage may increase even more in the presence of disease (Xiang et al. 2021; Dos Santos et al. 2020; Herculano‐Houzel et al. 2018; Choi et al. 2021; Krasemann et al. 2017; Keren‐Shaul et al. 2017; Mazaheri et al. 2017). Microglia are one of the most metabolically complex cell types in the brain, shifting their cellular functions based on their glucose and lipid handling. This adaptive profile indicates that metabolic shifts in microglia could drive widespread inflexibility in AD. As the brain's innate immune cells, microglia regulate tissue homeostasis, debris clearance, and immune surveillance—functions that require exceptionally high metabolic activity (Romero‐Molina et al. 2022; Jorfi et al. 2023). Genome‐wide association studies first highlighted microglial involvement in AD by identifying risk variants in several genes, including CD33, TREM2, CR1, SPI1, MS4A, ABCA7, and INPP5D (Efthymiou and Goate 2017). Amyloid plaque formation triggers morphological and functional changes in microglia, which are strongly associated with changes in microglial metabolism (Keren‐Shaul et al. 2017; Condello et al. 2015; Bolmont et al. 2008; Grubman et al. 2021). Microglia surround amyloid plaques, where they compact or wall off Aβ deposits and facilitate Aβ degradation or clearance (Keren‐Shaul et al. 2017; Romero‐Molina et al. 2022; Jorfi et al. 2023; Efthymiou and Goate 2017; Deczkowska et al. 2018). Their morphology shifts from a ramified to an amoeboid shape, accompanied by a transcriptomic switch from a homeostatic to a reactive, phagocytic state. This includes a downregulation of homeostatic markers (Iba1, P2ry12, Cx3cr1, Tmem119) and an upregulation of cytokines, complement proteins, and phagocytic and inflammatory markers (Apoe, Trem2, Clec7a, Cd68, Lpl, Spp1, Ctsd, Cst7, Tyrobp, CD9, Cd11c, CD63) (Paolicelli et al. 2022). This shift towards a pro‐inflammatory phenotype is associated with worsened pathology and cognitive decline. It is also accompanied by shifts in carbon, glucose, and lipid metabolism (Johnson et al. 2020; Krasemann et al. 2017; Paolicelli et al. 2022; Sala Frigerio et al. 2019; Lee and Landreth 2010; Clayton et al. 2021; Gratuze et al. 2021) during presymptomatic AD, suggesting that neuroinflammation drives metabolic reprogramming that may limit flexibility before any change in cognitive status (Johnson et al. 2020).

In both aging, cerebral amyloidosis, and tauopathy, reactive microglia are hypothesized to drive early FDG‐PET elevations (Xiang et al. 2021; Zimmer et al. 2022; Brendel et al. 2016, 2017; Gotzl et al. 2019; Mirzaei et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2015; Xiang et al. 2022). Microglia undergo metabolic reprogramming in response to AD pathology, to support migration, proliferation, phagocytosis, immune surveillance, and cytokine production (Monsorno et al. 2022; Xiang et al. 2021; Ulland et al. 2017; Lee et al. 2023; Bernier et al. 2020; Sabogal‐Guaqueta et al. 2023; Shippy and Ulland 2020; Mandrekar et al. 2009; Paolicelli and Angiari 2019; Devanney et al. 2020). Reactive microglia increase glucose uptake but also shift from mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to a strong reliance on glycolysis (Hu et al. 2022; Pan et al. 2022). Although glycolysis allows for rapid energy production to initially support bioenergetically demanding microglial processes, it is far less efficient than oxidative phosphorylation, yielding ~95% less ATP per glucose molecule (Shen et al. 2024). Microglial activation, which can be detected by 18 kDa translocator protein‐PET (TSPO‐PET) in vivo, rises in both mouse models of cerebral amyloidosis, patients with AD, and patients with four‐repeat tauopathies (Xiang et al. 2021). TSPO‐PET signal correlates with FDG‐PET uptake, implicating reactive microglia as a substantial source of cerebral glucose utilization (Xiang et al. 2021; Brendel et al. 2016; Choi et al. 2021; Krasemann et al. 2017; Keren‐Shaul et al. 2017; Mazaheri et al. 2017; Brendel et al. 2017; Mirzaei et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2015). Notably, biomarkers of microglial reactivity (CSF sTREM2) change years before symptom onset and correlate with early FDG‐PET hypermetabolism during amyloid accumulation (Li et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2024; Biel et al. 2023). This suggests that microglial activation precedes tau pathology and overt neurodegeneration, positioning microglia as early drivers of glucose hypermetabolism and the neuroimmune response to amyloid pathology. Therefore, reactive microglia can drive metabolic inflexibility in AD by both increasing glucose uptake as well as shifting from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis (Johnson et al. 2020). Although this glycolytic bias may initially support critical proinflammatory functions, long‐term reliance can impair ATP generation and energy‐intensive processes, such as surveillance, phagocytosis, and proteostasis. It can also inhibit the flux of TCA cycle intermediates necessary for biosynthesis of important lipids and neurotransmitters (Wang et al. 2022; Lepiarz‐Raba et al. 2023). As a result, microglia lose the ability to dynamically adjust to changing functional and energetic demands, making them metabolically inflexible. Interestingly, this metabolic inflexibility can be reversible. Several studies utilizing mouse models of amyloidosis demonstrate that targeting key glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PKM2) in microglia can reprogram their metabolism away from this glycolytic bias, restoring bioenergetic adaptability, and improving amyloid pathology, neuroinflammation, and cognition (Liu et al. 2024; Leng et al. 2022). This demonstrates that the metabolic inflexibility driven by microglia in AD can be targetable and reversible, and that re‐establishing flexibility can improve pathological outcomes.

Aside from microglia's cell autonomous contribution to metabolic inflexibility, their metabolic reprogramming has a profound effect on neuronal function by restricting resource sharing. Regions most susceptible to amyloid pathology (e.g., medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, parietal lobe, and temporal lobe) are inherently energy demanding and exhibit high levels of basal neuronal activity (Raichle et al. 2001; Vaishnavi et al. 2010; Buckner et al. 2009; Zhang and Raichle 2010; Fox et al. 2005, 2009; Allaman et al. 2010; Nicholson et al. 2010; Vlassenko et al. 2010). Increased neuronal firing requires rapid glucose and lactate delivery (Cunnane et al. 2020; Caporale et al. 2021; Gustafsson et al. 2024). Fundamentally, neurons rely on ATP‐powered sodium potassium pumps (Na+/K+‐ATPase) to preserve the ionic gradients necessary for action potential generation and synaptic transmission (Pivovarov et al. 2018). It is estimated that this comprises nearly 70%–80% of the energy budget for the brain. Therefore, restricting glucose availability to neurons will impair neuronal ATP production, disrupting synaptic signaling and network function, and favoring excitotoxic damage. Because of this, metabolic competition between neurons and microglia is high in the setting of AD. Microglia are well suited to influence neuronal function because they highly express glucose and monocarboxylate transporters and further upregulate glucose uptake, glycolytic metabolism, and glucose transporter expression in response to amyloid pathology (Cunnane et al. 2020; Xiang et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2014, 2016; Wang et al. 2019; Johnson et al. 2020; Piers et al. 2020; Sebastian Monasor et al. 2020). Therefore, reactive microglia monopolize fuels, depleting neurons of a consistent supply that normally supports neuron‐glial cooperation and neuronal homeostasis (Wang et al. 2022; Lepiarz‐Raba et al. 2023). In highly active cortical hubs and DMN regions, this competition deprives neurons of critical glucose availability. As a result, microglial‐driven hypermetabolism drives neuronal hypometabolism, heightening network vulnerability in AD (Johnson et al. 2020).

Altered glucose metabolism is not the only metabolic change that occurs in response to amyloid or tau pathology in AD. Impaired lipid metabolism, lipid dyshomeostasis and lipid droplet formation are also defining features of presymptomatic and symptomatic AD (Yin 2023; Wang et al. 2025; Tong et al. 2024). This illustrates that both lipid handling and lipid storage play a crucial role in disease progression. The brain, which has the second highest lipid content in the body (~50% of its dry weight), partially relies on lipids as a fuel source but more so, as a source of structural components for membranes, myelin, and lipid rafts that support cell signaling and synaptic dynamics (Yin 2023; Wang et al. 2025). As a fuel source, β‐oxidation efficiently breaks down fatty acids to generate large amounts of ATP‐ more than what is generated from a single molecule of glucose; however, this comes at the expense of increased oxygen consumption and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation (Quijano et al. 2016; Rosca et al. 2012; Jaswal et al. 2011; Yin 2023; Wang et al. 2025). However, β‐oxidation traditionally decreases, not increases, in the setting of AD, suggesting a global decrease in energy availability. In contrast, lipid peroxidation and ROS production are more of a defining feature of AD (Yin 2023; Tong et al. 2024). This causes damage to lipids, membranes, and mitochondria, reinforcing a pathological cycle of oxidative stress and metabolic inflexibility (Yin 2023; Tong et al. 2024). Consistent with this, numerous studies show shifts towards classes of neurotoxic lipid, where lipid metabolism in reactive microglia results in cytokine and ROS production (Yin 2023; Wang et al. 2025; Tong et al. 2024). In fact, human metabolomic and lipidomic studies which reveal distinct lipid species—notably sphingolipids, phosphatidylcholines, and lysophosphatidylcholines—correlate with tau, synaptic injury, and reactive glia, demonstrating a tight coupling between metabolic and neuroinflammatory processes (Yin 2023; Wang et al. 2025; Tong et al. 2024). This exemplifies metabolic inflexibility, as inflammatory processes are supported at the expense of homeostatic functions through shifts in lipid profiles and lipid metabolism. Another hallmark of lipid dysfunction is excessive lipid droplet formation in both astrocytes and microglia (Yin 2023; Tong et al. 2024). While this may be initially protective, it ultimately sequesters lipids, fatty acids, and cholesterol in a way that restricts their availability for use, either as a bioenergetic substrate, signaling molecule, or component of cellular membranes and organelles (Yin 2023; Tong et al. 2024). Together with declining glucose utilization, impaired lipid utilization, storage, and flux accelerates a state of bioenergetic crisis, neuroinflammation, and metabolic inflexibility in AD.

Finally, peripheral metabolism can strongly influence metabolic flexibility in the brain. Therefore, age‐related comorbidities, like type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, sleep apnea, or similar, all negatively impact peripheral metabolism and profoundly impact fuel availability, transport, and cellular cooperation within the brain (Carroll and Macauley 2019). This is largely due to alterations in peripheral insulin resistance (Rhea et al. 2024). While the role of brain insulin resistance is widely debated, peripheral insulin resistance diminishes the brain's ability to transport and adapt fuel use to maintain homeostasis and metabolic flexibility. When systemic insulin signaling is impaired, glucose uptake and utilization by peripheral tissues decline, leading to hyperinsulinemia and altered nutrient delivery to the brain. Chronically elevated insulin leads to insulin resistance in the liver and drives hepatic de novo lipogenesis, causing a lipid overload in the periphery and a downregulation of glucose utilization. This increases systemic free fatty acids which can disrupt blood–brain barrier transport of glucose and/or ketone bodies, thereby constraining substrate availability in the brain. Within the brain, reduced insulin and IGF receptor signaling downregulate PI3K–AKT–mTOR and mitochondrial pathways in neurons and glia, blunting glycolytic and oxidative metabolism. Astrocytes and microglia, in turn, adopt pro‐inflammatory, glycolytic phenotypes that further uncouple neuronal energy supply from systemic metabolism. Thus, peripheral insulin resistance propagates a feed‐forward cycle of metabolic inflexibility that alters nutrient availability and inflammatory cascades that are particularly destructive to the AD brain.

Consequences of Metabolic Inflexibility

Alzheimer's disease is more than just cerebral hypometabolism, but rather a disease of early‐stage metabolic inflexibility (Figure 1). The metabolic changes in presymptomatic AD feed forward to have pleiotropic functional consequences at the cellular, tissue, and organismal level. For microglia and astrocytes, both cell types take on secondary, pro‐inflammatory phenotypes, often neglecting or downregulating their homeostatic functions. Both microglia, and to a lesser extent, astrocytes shift towards increased glycolysis and lipid storage at the expense of mitochondrial respiration (Hirata et al. 2024; Xiang et al. 2021; Baik et al. 2019; Farmer et al. 2019; Zimmer et al. 2022; Carter et al. 2019; Choi et al. 2021; Keren‐Shaul et al. 2017; Mazaheri et al. 2017; Grubman et al. 2021; Xiang et al. 2022; Biel et al. 2023; Sebastian Monasor et al. 2020; Yin 2023; Tong et al. 2024; Zatcepin et al. 2024; Nair et al. 2019; Litwiniuk et al. 2023). For microglia, this is accompanied by a functional shift towards decreased surveillance, neuron‐glial signaling, and synaptic pruning (Mazaheri et al. 2017; Condello et al. 2015; Sebastian Monasor et al. 2020). Astrocytes, on the other hand, reduce their fuel supply or trophic support to neurons as well as decrease neurotransmitter recycling and glutamate uptake (Hirata et al. 2024; Acosta et al. 2017; Spotorno et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2023). This is supported by recent single‐nucleus transcriptomic and proteomic atlases, including the SEA‐AD and Allen Brain Atlas datasets, revealing that neurons exhibit progressive loss of oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial gene expression, while astrocytes and microglia upregulate glycolysis, lipid handling, and inflammatory pathways. Spatial multi‐omics further show that these metabolic programs are enriched near plaques and tangles, creating microenvironments of glial hypermetabolism surrounding neuronal hypometabolism. These multi‐omic signatures provide mechanistic evidence that AD involves cell‐type‐specific reprogramming of energy metabolism and confirm that glial‐driven metabolic inflexibility precedes overt neurodegeneration (Cunnane et al. 2020; Magistretti et al. 2018; Monsorno et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2025; Hirata et al. 2024; Johnson et al. 2020; Yin 2023; Wang et al. 2025; Tong et al. 2024). Relative to AD, changes in neuronal excitability feed forward to directly impact Aβ and tau dynamics which ultimately drives amyloid and tau accumulation (Cirrito et al. 2005, 2008; Bero et al. 2011; Yamada et al. 2014). For example, neuronal activity is one of the main drivers of Aβ production and tau release (Cirrito et al. 2005, 2008; Bero et al. 2011; Yamada et al. 2014). When Aβ and tau are produced in excess, they are more likely to spread and aggregate, causing amyloid plaque and neurofibrillary tangle formation (Cirrito et al. 2005, 2008; Bero et al. 2011; Yamada et al. 2014). Once these proteinopathies form, they exacerbate the neuroinflammatory response, increasing metabolic demand, inflexibility, and dysfunction which further restricts metabolic cooperation and support to neurons. Ultimately, this culminates in functional deficits, like sleep loss or cognitive decline. These relationships are summarized below in Tables 1 and 2. We provide examples of how metabolic deficits can feed forward to produce mechanistic consequences and negative functional outcomes in AD. While not an exhaustive list, it provides examples of how changes in fuel availability, cellular cooperation, or fuel switching contribute to metabolic inflexibility and functional deficits in AD.

Clinical Trials

Human clinical trials are increasingly testing metabolic interventions both before and after the onset of AD and related dementias (ADRDs), reflecting the growing recognition that metabolism is an important therapeutic target (Cummings, Zhou, et al. 2025). Agents designed to restore glucose and lipid homeostasis, such as GLP‐1 receptor agonists, metformin, and statins, are being evaluated for their ability to prevent AD or ADRD pathology or improve cognition in affected individuals (Cummings, Zhou, et al. 2025; Wang, Wang, et al. 2024; Cummings, Atri, et al. 2025; ClinicalTrials.gov 2025a; Joseph et al. 2023). Of the roughly 138 drugs currently being studied in clinical trials for AD, 10 drugs target metabolic processes, 2 drugs target lipids and APOE, 3 drugs target oxidative stress, and 1 trial utilizes ketone supplementation (Cummings, Zhou, et al. 2025; ClinicalTrials.gov 2025b). This amounts to 15 drugs and one nutritional strategy that explicitly act on metabolism (Cummings, Zhou, et al. 2025). Of these drugs, 3 have advanced to stage 3 clinical trials, underscoring their therapeutic potential (Cummings, Zhou, et al. 2025). An additional 24 drugs under investigation modulate inflammation and immune responses, processes tightly linked to metabolic regulation, bringing the number of drug trials that could influence metabolism to 39 (Cummings, Zhou, et al. 2025). Importantly, any intervention that alters neuronal activity, synaptic transmission, neurotransmitter receptor activity, cellular energetics, inflammation, or AD‐related pathology will inevitably influence brain metabolism, either by neuronal and glial activity or by dampening the metabolic responses triggered by pathology. Viewed from this perspective, nearly all the 138 drugs being tested for disease modification in AD can be considered metabolically active, even though most trials do not have metabolic endpoints. This growing body of work highlights metabolic changes as a cornerstone of AD pathogenesis and a compelling therapeutic target. Even when not the primary target, secondary or tertiary effects on metabolism may help correct the dysregulated metabolic state characteristic of AD. Collectively, these efforts signal a transition in AD therapeutics, from symptomatic management to strategies that address metabolic inflexibility and target the biological etiology of disease. Importantly, we hypothesize that disease‐modifying interventions aimed at restoring metabolic balance can reverse many AD phenotypes, a concept now being tested in multiple animal models and increasingly embraced in the field. This emerging focus places metabolism, cross‐system interactions, and lifelong metabolic health at the forefront of AD prevention and treatment, representing a crucial change in how scientists and clinicians approach AD (Table 3).

Limitations and Unanswered Questions

Despite numerous advances in our understanding of the metabolic underpinnings associated with AD progression, several limitations complicate our interpretation and translation. First, much of our mechanistic or cell‐specific knowledge is derived from in vitro, rodent models, or post‐mortem studies. In vitro systems are often simplified, only exploring the contributions of one or two cell types in isolation and often in the presence of excess glucose to facilitate cell survival. While these studies are powerful for understanding cellular metabolism, it is difficult to know how these cell‐autonomous findings relate to the whole brain or network dynamics. Rodent models help overcome some of these hurdles by providing the complexity of a mammalian system; however, no rodent model fully recapitulates the human course of disease. In addition to size, rodent and human brains differ greatly in morphology. The human brain has more cortical complexity, white matter, and glia compared to the rodent brain, all of which will have a profound impact on glucose and lipid metabolism (Herculano‐Houzel and Lent 2005; Ofer et al. 2022; Azevedo et al. 2009). One of the biggest challenges in studying metabolism with post‐mortem tissue is the effect of post‐mortem intervals on metabolic changes (Olney et al. 2025; Novitzky et al. 1988; Donaldson and Lamont 2013). While human postmortem tissue remains a cornerstone of metabolic research, metabolites such as lactate and ATP change within seconds of death, introducing biases that complicate our understanding of metabolic shifts (Novitzky et al. 1988; Donaldson and Lamont 2013). These methodological constraints leave critical gaps in how well our experimental systems capture in vivo human physiology during AD progression in the human brain. Another major caveat is understanding AD‐specific changes in the number of comorbidities that occur with aging or age‐related diseases. Type 2 diabetes, prediabetes, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic syndrome all impact glucose and lipid metabolism (Carroll and Macauley 2019; Wang, Wang, et al. 2024; Livingston et al. 2024). Not only does this increase the risk for AD and dementia, but it further complicates interpretation by layering metabolic stress onto an already vulnerable brain (Carroll and Macauley 2019; Wang, Wang, et al. 2024; Livingston et al. 2024; Nianogo et al. 2022). Finally, while metabolic imaging with FDG‐PET was once viewed as a routine tool and defining biomarker of AD, the field has shifted towards PET imaging with amyloid and tau and other inflammatory markers, leaving a gap in tools being used to directly monitor metabolism in vivo. Understanding how metabolic inflexibility contributes to AD will require novel biomarkers that couple cellular metabolism with the ATN framework, closing the gap between mechanistic insight and clinical translation.

Closing Remarks

Metabolic flexibility is crucial to healthy brain function. It depends on tightly coordinated fuel access, transport, fluxing, and cooperation across cell types, networks, and organ systems. AD disrupts this coordination and drives the brain from adaptive, shared metabolism to rigid, cell‐type‐biased programs that are proinflammatory and amplify pathology—a process we described in this review as “metabolic inflexibility.” We used this term because it captures the idea that the brain has inordinate access to different fuels or metabolic programs, but somehow loses the flexibility to maintain basic homeostatic functions. In the early stages of pathogenesis, AD is not typified by an energy failure but rather subtle cell‐autonomous shifts in metabolic programs that cause widespread damage to network dynamics and neuronal function.

In this review, we outlined how cell types across the brain differ in fuel requirements, metabolite transporters, and fuel preferences, and how these differences shape vulnerability to metabolic inflexibility. A central theme is a biphasic trajectory of metabolic changes in AD. Early glia‐driven hypermetabolism, particularly microglial metabolic reprogramming, is followed by late‐stage neuronal hypometabolism, network failure, and cognitive decline. These metabolic changes impact neuronal excitability, synaptic support, and Aβ/tau metabolism. This results in accelerating Aβ and tau pathology, and reinforcing inflammation, oxidative stress, and lipid droplet accumulation, hallmarks of the dysfunctional AD brain. Despite divergent cellular mechanisms, these consequences converge to impair network function, neuronal activity and accelerate sleep loss and cognitive decline. Importantly, metabolism is moving to the forefront of therapeutic targeting. Interventions that restore fuel delivery and metabolic flux, reduce glycolytic imbalances and oxidative stress, normalize lipid handling, or restore glial function have the potential to reverse metabolic dysfunction, improve network function, and modify disease progression. In sum, metabolic inflexibility is a key feature of and contributor to disease progression, with divergent mechanisms across cell populations that converge to cause neuronal stress and dysfunction, leading to the clinical syndrome observed in AD. Exploring options that restore metabolic flexibility offers a viable path to mitigating pathology and preserving cognition in AD.
