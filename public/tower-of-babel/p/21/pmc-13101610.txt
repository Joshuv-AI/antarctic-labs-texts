Examining the health impacts of climate change through electronic health records: A rapid review
Authors: Arun Dahil, Cameron Pinn, Lucy Smith, Nehal Hassan, Nuno Koch Esteves, Glenn Simpson, Hajira Dambha-Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTemperature extremes, including elevated heat and cold, are important environmental determinants of health, the frequency and duration of which are increasing due to climate change. Ecological and time-series studies have established links with adverse outcomes but often lacked individual-level detail. Electronic health records (EHR) provide an alternative source, yet their use in climate-health research remains inconsistent.AimFirst, to undertake a methodological examination of how the health impacts of temperature extremes are captured and coded in EHR-based studies across healthcare settings and to describe the current methods and coding practices in the existing evidence base; and second, to synthesize evidence on the health outcomes associated with extreme temperature events to identify gaps in the current literature to inform future research.MethodsWe conducted a rapid review of studies using EHR data to examine associations between temperature extremes and health outcomes across healthcare settings. The aim was to assess how health impacts of temperature extremes have been captured and coded within EHR-based research, and to identify methodological and coding-related gaps. Searches of seven databases identified eligible studies, and data were extracted on exposure definitions, outcome coding, methods, findings, and limitations, using key terms for climate change exposure and health outcomes from EHRs. The findings and limitations were narratively summarized and tabulated to highlight trends, methodological issues, and evidence gaps.ResultsOf the 1,616 studies identified, 526 duplicates were removed, leaving 1,090 for screening; 58 studies met our inclusion criteria. Extreme heat was most frequently studied, with fewer analyses of extreme cold. Common outcomes included morbidity, cardiovascular admissions, asthma, and pregnancy-related conditions. Mental health outcomes were rarely assessed, subgroup analyses were mostly age-based, and studies focused on high-income countries. Exposure metrics and coding practices varied widely, with limited reporting of diagnostic codes and individual-level mediators.ConclusionHarmonized exposure definitions, broader outcome coverage, and integration of socio-demographic and individual-level factors are needed to strengthen EHR-based climate-health research and guide targeted interventions.Keywords: Temperature extremes, Climate change, Electronic health records, Rapid review

Introduction

Temperature extremes, including prolonged periods of extreme heat or cold, are increasingly recognized as important environmental determinants of health. Their frequency and duration are projected to rise, driven by climate change, with significant implications for healthcare demand and health outcomes across diverse population groups [1]. Numerous studies have documented associations between extreme temperatures and a range of adverse health outcomes, including increases in emergency department visits, hospitalizations, and mortality [2,3]. These effects are disproportionately observed in older adults, individuals with chronic health conditions, and residents of socioeconomically disadvantaged areas [[4], [5], [6]]. Despite these findings, most existing research relies on ecological or time-series designs using aggregated health data. While these approaches have advanced understanding of short-term effects, they are limited in their ability to capture individual-level variation, account for pre-existing health conditions, or consider how patterns of healthcare utilization and behavioral responses may mediate climate-health relationships. This restricts the capacity to identify the most vulnerable subpopulations and develop targeted, evidence-based interventions.

Electronic health records (EHR) offer an alternative and potentially more powerful approach. These routinely collected datasets provide longitudinal, individual-level information on diagnoses, prescriptions, consultations, and demographic characteristics. Large-scale resources such as the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) and the Secure Anonymised Information Linkage (SAIL) Databank in the UK hold millions of patient records, enabling population-based analyses of climate-related health outcomes [7]. However, EHR-based studies in this field are hampered by substantial methodological heterogeneity. Differences in temperature metrics, exposure windows, and diagnostic coding systems, combined with inconsistent reporting of these methods, limit interpretability, comparability, and synthesis of findings. Furthermore, contemporary research has focused on a narrow range of health outcomes and high-income settings, often neglecting mental health.

This rapid review had two key aims. First, to conduct a methodological examination of how the health impacts of temperature extremes are captured and coded in EHR-based studies across healthcare settings and to describe the current methods and coding practices in the existing evidence base; and second, to synthesize evidence on the health outcomes associated with extreme temperature events to pinpoint gaps in the current literature to inform future research.

Methods

We conducted a rapid review to produce results in a timely manner, as the variables identified in this study were being used for decision-making on a separate epidemiological paper. This rapid review followed the methodological framework of Arksey and O’Malley [8], refined by Levac et al. [9], and adhered to the updated Cochrane Rapid Review methods guidance [10]. The process involved: (i) identifying eligibility criteria and relevant studies; (ii) selecting studies for inclusion; (iii) extracting and charting data; and (iv) collating, summarising, and reporting findings.

Eligibility criteria

We included studies that were (i) based on EHR or linked routinely collected health data; (ii) examined health outcomes, healthcare utilization, or interventions in the context of temperature extremes (heatwaves, cold events, extreme humidity, or other temperature-related exposures); (iii) published in peer-reviewed journals, academic books, conference proceedings or relevant grey literature; and (iv) were available in English.

Studies that did not meet these criteria were excluded.

Search strategy

Searches were developed in consultation with an information specialist from the University of Southampton and refined with input from climate change and medical experts. Initial terms were piloted and revised before final implementation. Keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) combined terms for exposures (e.g., “temperature extreme*,” “heatwave*,” “hot weather,” “cold spell*,” “freeze*,” “extreme humidity,” “climate change” “weather extremes”) with health data sources (e.g., “EHR,” “routinely collected data,” “registry data,” “health administrative data”). Variants were searched using title, abstract, and indexing fields. Searches were run from database inception to 4th July 2025 in MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), GreenFILE, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL (EBSCOhost), and Cochrane. No filters were applied to database searches.

Study selection

Results were imported into Rayyan and duplicates removed. Titles and abstracts were all screened independently by three reviewers (CP, AD, LS). Full-text screening was conducted for studies meeting the inclusion criteria by CP and AD, who screened 100% of the papers. Where eligibility was unclear, disagreements were resolved by consensus or adjudication by a third reviewer (LS).

Data extraction and charting

A standardized, piloted data extraction form was used to record study characteristics (authors, year, country, design); population demographics; exposure type, metrics, and duration; health outcomes (morbidity, mortality, hospital admissions, primary care visits, or other utilization measures); EHR dataset and meteorological data source; coding approach (diagnostic, symptom, or utilization codes, classification system used); specific codes; key findings; and reported limitations. Data were extracted by CP, AD, LS, NH, and NKE.

Data synthesis

Extracted data were summarized descriptively and grouped thematically by type of climate variable studied, health outcomes assessed, exposure definitions and metrics, codes and terminology used, sources of climatic data, and patterns in study design and population characteristics. Key findings and limitations were analyzed to identify trends, methodological issues, and evidence gaps, summarized narratively and presented in summary tables.

Results

Study selection

Database searches yielded 1,616 records; 526 duplicates were removed, leaving 1,090 for title and abstract screening. A further 845 were excluded, and 245 underwent full-text review. One hundred and eighty-seven were excluded, resulting in 58 studies for analysis (Fig. 1).

There has been an increasing trend in publications post-2020, with 45 papers (77 %) being published between 2020-2025, as shown in Fig. 3. The main study type used was a retrospective cohort design (n=33) and the ‘look-back’ periods ranged from two to 24 years, with a mean of 9.5 years.

Four studies made predictions based on forecast models [12,13,27,29], one study included a climate change intervention [34], and one study was a systematic review [68].

Populations included in the studies were mostly adult, although some studies focused on children (n=10) [2,3,5,6,10,15,18,26,67,45]. No studies referred to subgroup analysis beyond age as a factor. In terms of other sub-populations, the impact of climate extremes on veterans was also analysed (n=2) [23,29]. Many studies referred to whole cohorts, but without reporting the demographic characteristics of the individuals who comprised these cohorts.

The use of electronic health care records to record health outcomes

EHR were used to capture health outcomes across a broad range of themes, including reference to a specific illness or disease area (n = 24) [7], [8], [9], [11], [16], [20], [22], [23], [24], [27], [30], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [42], [45], [54], [57], [59], [67], health service usage/cost (n=22) [3], [7], [8], [9], [16], [21], [25], [29], [34], [36], [37], [38], [39], [42], [43], [44], [45], [54], [55], [56], [62], mortality (n = 13) [1,2,4,11,13,14,17,19,22,23,40,41,46], neonatal/pregnancy outcomes (n=13) [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [10], [15], [18], [38], [57], [64], [67], mental health (n=3, one of which referred to post-partum depression) [29], [37], [64], and outcomes after surgery (n = 1) [31]. Many studies referred to a specific illness or disease; these included cardiovascular disease (n = 8) [7], [11], [13], [21], [24], [43], [54], specific temperature-related conditions, injuries or accidents such as dehydration, heat syncope or heat stroke (n=7) [14], [19], [24], [25], [55], [56], [65], asthma (n=5) [9], [16], [26], [35], [55], respiratory diseases (n=4) [20,23,34,45], infectious diseases (n=3) [20,33,46], ischemic stroke (n=2) [8,27], gestational diabetes (n=2) [57,38], and acute kidney disease (n=1) [32]. Health outcomes related to neonatal/pregnancy outcomes included growth and/or birth defects (n=3) [2,6,15], stillbirths (n=1) [4], premature deaths among newborns (n=1) [2], placental abruption (n=1) [10], maternal morbidity (n=1) [5], and premature delivery (n=1) [3]. No study considered the impact of climate change exposure on people with multimorbidity. Fig. 4 provides a visual representation of the different health outcomes explored using EHR.

A bar graph showing health outcomes extracted from the EHRs of studies investigating the impact of extreme weather events on health outcomes.

The majority of studies used either International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9 or 10 codes to extract the data from EHR (n=31) [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [10], [11], [12], [14], [19], [21], [22], [23], [24], [27], [28], [29], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [42], [43], [54], [56], [59], [64]; 16 studies used different registries to obtain their data [1], [2], [6], [11], [13], [14], [16], [17], [19], [20], [22], [38], [46], [54], [58], [67]; two studies used SNOWMED codes [26,60]; and nine studies did not make explicit how they extracted their data [9], [15], [18], [21], [25], [39], [55], [57], [61]. There was a general missingness of coding detail across the studies, and in particular, where ICD 9/10 or SNOWMED were used, the specific codes utilized to extract the data were only included in 15 papers [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [10], [11], [14], [23], [24], [27], [32], [54], [56].

Lastly, the settings from which EHR data were extracted were primarily secondary hospital-level data, especially from emergency departments. Only one study examined primary care data, which was in relation to missed appointments [52], and one study looked at visits to a tertiary mental health clinic [46].

Climate change variables and definitions/metrics

Twelve different exposure variables were extracted from the 58 papers; these included microclimate data, thunderstorms/major storms, flooding, snowfall, solar output, wind speed, humidity, rainfall/precipitation, air pollutants, extreme cold weather, extreme hot weather, and temperature, including both extreme heat and cold weather events. The most common exposure variable, featured in 29 (50 %) of the papers, was temperature, followed by a focus on extreme heat events (n=13). Cold weather events were a less frequently focus (n = 2). The definitions and metrics used across exposure variables varied across papers. For definitions of extreme heat, studies (n = 5) used different percentile cut-off points [5], [19], [24], [28], [57], ranging from a temperature over the 75th percentile to the 99th percentile. Studies also varied in the duration used to define a heatwave event, requiring between two and four consecutive days where the temperature was at or exceeded the defined percentile-based thresholds [24], [28], [41], [57]. Similarly, variance was observed in the cut-off points for extreme cold, with temperatures ranging from below the 2.5th percentile to below the 10th percentile [7], [23], [28], [43], [57]. There was inconsistency in duration of temperature recordings across studies, which ranged from hourly (n = 4) [3,4,18,27], daily (n = 17) [7], [9], [11], [12], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [28], [34], [45], [54], [59], [64], [65], weekly (n = 3) [15], [18], [58], monthly (n = 2) [9,46], to annually (n = 2) [34], [59]. Time lag data from exposure to health outcome differed substantially between studies, ranging from as short as 24 hours [3,27], to 3-7 days [23], [24], [43], [54], 14 days [8,12], 21 days [11], 30 days [19], and up to 30 years [59]. Lastly, four papers [17,23,48,63] used a resolution grid to provide a spatial imagery of temperature, and areas selected varied from 1 × 1 km to 500 km.

Key findings by exposure variables and health outcomes

Supplementary Table 2 provides a summary of key findings for each study. Overall, these show that extreme temperatures, either cold or hot, can impact health across a range of health outcomes, and this can vary by gender and deprivation, with those who are most socially vulnerable experiencing increased risks.

In relation to neonatal outcomes, exposure to extreme temperatures leads to a poorer prognosis for neonatal patients, although these vary depending on whether it is hot or cold. Increased exposure to heat is associated with high risk of stillbirth, maternal morbidity, gestational diabetes mellitus and lower neonatal growth. Exposure to extreme cold was linked with placenta abruption and admission to the neonatal intensive care unit.

Across the papers focused on cardiovascular disease (CVD), an inconsistent pattern emerged, with some studies finding extreme heat increases CVD mortality and admissions, whilst others found that risk increased with extreme cold temperatures. Gender differences and deprivation also influenced this relationship. A more consistent pattern emerged for mental health service usage, which increased during hot temperatures.

Lastly, cold weather extremes were more likely to lead to hospital admissions for conditions such as hypothermia. An increase in mortality was consistent with extreme heat, with females and those from areas of deprivation being more at risk. Young males are more at risk of heat-related admissions to hospital, which was linked to behavioral factors.

Limitations in climate change exposure and health outcome research

A consistent set of limitations emerged across studies. Most excluded or did not take into account individual-level modifiers such as socio-demographic factors (e.g., age, sex, deprivation and ethnicity) or behavioral adaptations to temperature extremes (e.g., use of air conditioning during heat events or passive cooling). Missing data were common due to the predominance of retrospective study designs within this review, which inherently limit follow-up. Geographic coverage was often narrow, with many studies focusing on a single city or urban area, rather than on the regional or national scales. The accuracy of environmental exposure data varied by country and ecological designs limited causal inference. Across studies, ICD codes were mainly used to classify health outcomes rather than to capture climate change exposures, which were instead derived from linked meteorological datasets (e.g., data collated by state meteorological agencies).

Discussion

In this study, we examined how the health impacts of climate extremes are captured and coded in EHR-based research. We found that while publications have grown over the past five years, most originate from the USA and China, with none from low-income countries, despite higher vulnerability to climate change-related health impacts. Current research is largely based on retrospective hospital EHRs, which are heavily reliant on ICD-9/10 codes and often without consistent specification. Additionally, ICD codes were mainly used in studies to classify health outcomes rather than to capture climate change exposures. Instead, climate change exposures were defined through meteorological data, such as temperature percentiles, heatwave duration, or rainfall thresholds, and linked to the health outcomes coded in EHR. This highlights a methodological limitation in current studies, as well as a lack of coding frameworks to represent climate change-related exposures within EHR systems.

Furthermore, most studies focused on extreme heat, with limited analysis of the health effects of extreme cold and inconsistent exposure definitions. Studies focused on cardiovascular, respiratory, neonatal and pregnancy-related conditions, with limited attention given to mental health and multimorbidity conditions that are susceptible to climate change-related health impacts. In relation to care settings, relatively few studies were situated in primary or community care, missing an important component of healthcare use and early-stage condition management, where it is likely a substantial proportion of mild-to-moderate climate-related presentations occur, and behavioral risk factors are recorded.

Further, the relatively narrow research focus of the current literature may partially explain the limited inclusion of individual-level risk factors and social vulnerability variables (e.g., variables such as age, sex, ethnicity, deprivation, housing characteristics, occupation, and adaptation behaviours), which were often unavailable or under-recorded.

Overall, future research should expand the geographical scope of the current evidence base to encompass multiple locations, settings and countries (especially low-income states). There is also a need for study designs that analyze a broader and more diverse range of socio-economic and high-risk populations (e.g., older adults, outdoor workers, low-income groups, socially isolated or digitally excluded cohorts). Furthermore, current studies are primarily retrospective, highlighting the need for greater diversity in study designs, in particular, longitudinal studies. Going forward, studies should provide clarity and more consistent reporting of the specific heat or cold thresholds used, the duration of extreme weather events (i.e., heatwaves or cold spells) and the time period used (hourly, daily, or weekly). To further advance research in this field, there is a need for greater transparency and detail on the code lists used in studies, for example, publishing the full ICD/SNOMED code lists. More research is required to improve how climate exposures are captured and linked within EHR systems, enabling more accurate identification and interpretation of climate-related health hazards, especially in relation to high-risk cohorts. Future work should broaden the range of diseases studied (e.g., mental health disorders and multimorbidity) and consider outcomes beyond hospital/secondary care to include other care settings (i.e., primary care, community and social care, and public health).

Future evaluation or trial studies have the potential to move current research beyond describing associations to testing interventions that reduce climate-related health risks. Additionally, it will be important to conduct evaluations of how adaptation and mitigation policies translate into measurable health outcomes to guide equitable and evidence-based action in this field. Urban planning studies may help inform the development of robust infrastructure and built environments that prevent or reduce exposure to the effects of extreme temperatures and support the efficient delivery of healthcare to vulnerable populations during these periods. At the clinical level, exposure-informed care such as adjusting medications during heatwaves or providing pollution counselling should be tested for effectiveness. Finally, early warning systems integrating weather and clinical data could help predict heat or pollution-related admissions.

Comparison with existing literature

This review confirms the limited attention given to mental health outcomes in relation to temperature extreme exposures, which is consistent with previous findings [69]. Given that those most vulnerable to climate extremes are also at higher risk of mental health issues, this remains an important area requiring further analysis [70,71]. Our results also align with current evidence, which shows that children, particularly newborns and infants, are more vulnerable to climate change-related health impacts [72,73]. Our work also reinforces calls for further research examining climate extremes across the full developmental trajectory [74].

A notable finding of our study was the lack of methodological consistency in EHR-based climate-health research, which was also observed in other domains such as mental health [75,76]. Most studies applied retrospective time-series cohort designs, which are common in environmental health. However, this limits the ability of such study designs to account for unmeasured confounders, including individual-level mediators of exposure effects, which has also been observed in existing work [76]. Heterogeneity in climate exposure measures was rarely addressed, and few studies incorporated social vulnerability indicators such as education, housing, income, location, or access to care [77], although a previous systematic review found contrary evidence [78]. Although people with chronic conditions are more susceptible to climate extremes [79], none of the included studies examined the implications of multimorbidity, likely underestimating health impacts in these high-risk populations which a recent systematic review has highlighted [68]. Our finding that the majority of existing work in this field is focused on extreme heat events (rather than cold spells) and emergency care utilisation, has been described in previous studies [68].

Limitations

This rapid review provides a structured synthesis of how temperature-related health impacts driven by climate change have been examined using EHRs, offering a timely overview of current practices in exposure definition, outcome coding, and data utilisation. Strengths include a comprehensive, multi-database search strategy developed with an information specialist, ensuring broad coverage of peer-reviewed literature, and adherence to established rapid review frameworks and PRISMA-RR reporting standards, enhancing transparency and reproducibility.

Limitations include restriction to English-language publications, potentially excluding relevant research from non-English speaking regions; omission of grey literature; and the inherent constraints of the rapid review method, including the absence of full quality appraisal and exclusion of evidence published after the search cut-off date. In addition, the emphasis on temperature-related extremes in our search strategy may have resulted in other climate hazards (e.g., flooding, wildfires, droughts) not being fully captured, limiting the broader applicability of findings across the full spectrum of climate-health interactions, although we did use broad generic search terms such as ‘climate change’ and ‘extreme weather events’ Lastly, the lower proportion of research included in this review from low-income countries may reflect the slower adoption and coverage of EHR systems in these locations, rather than indicating a lack of attention to climate change and health research.

Conclusion

Our review findings suggest that EHR-based climate-health research is expanding but remains geographically narrow, methodologically inconsistent, and limited in its inclusion of a range of high-risk populations. Future studies should be extended to low-income and climate-vulnerable regions; incorporate primary care, community, and mental health settings; and address neglected areas such as mental health, the long term prognostic outcomes in paediatric populations, and multimorbidity. Harmonized exposure definitions and fully specified code lists are essential for transparency and comparability, while prospective designs linking EHR with environmental and socio-demographic data, and integrating individual-level mediators and social vulnerability metrics, will generate evidence that can inform targeted and equitable interventions to address both clinical and public health priorities. Future reviews could investigate the literature using alternative data sources such as individual-level studies, information regarding infrastructure and policy, as well as social and demographic information using censuses, etc. Addressing the issues raised in this review will be critical to guide effective policy and strengthen health system resilience in the face of intensifying temperature extremes resulting from climate change.
