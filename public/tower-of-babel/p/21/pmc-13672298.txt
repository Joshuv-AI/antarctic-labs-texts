Prospective observational study of atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel in patients with metastatic triple-negative breast cancer: JBCRG-C08/ATTRIBUTE
Authors: Tatsunori Shimoi, Nobuhiro Shibata, Masahiro Kitada, Michiko Tsuneizumi, Mitsugu Yamamoto, Yukinori Ozaki, Yumi Fujimoto, Sayuri Akiyoshi, Naoki Hashimoto, Miki Yamaguchi, Yutaka Yamamoto, Norikazu Masuda, Hiroshi Tada, Takashi Yamanaka, Yuichiro Kikawa, Tetsuhiko Taira, Shizuka Nakagawa, Taizo Takahashi, Mari S Oba, Naoki Niikura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe JBCRG-C08/ATTRIBUTE (UMIN000041747) study assessed atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel’s safety and effectiveness in a metastatic triple-negative breast cancer population, including IMpassion130-ineligible patients. The primary outcome was incidence of investigator-assessed adverse events (AEs). Safety and full analysis populations included 149 and 148 patients, respectively; 4.0% of the full analysis population had Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status ≥2 and 7.4% had a history/comorbidity of autoimmune disease (AID) at baseline. AEs occurred in 96.6% of the safety analysis population; serious AEs (SAEs), in 16.8%; and immune-related AEs (irAEs), in 30.9%. irAE incidence was 45.5% in patients with a history/comorbidity of AID. Notable SAEs included pneumonitis (3.4%), diarrhoea, pyrexia, adrenal insufficiency and cardiac failure (1.3% each). Two cases of Grade 5 pneumonitis occurred. Regarding effectiveness, median progression-free survival was 5.6 months (95% confidence interval, 4.2–6.7). This first analysis found no new safety concerns with atezolizumab combination therapy, although patients with AID require careful monitoring.Subject terms: Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for ~11% of all breast cancers and is defined by the absence of hormone receptor (oestrogen receptor and progesterone receptor) expression, as well as a lack of overexpression or amplification of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)1. TNBC is an aggressive subtype and is associated with poorer outcomes, risk of recurrence and poor survival following distant metastasis (which are more likely to occur in the brain and lungs) compared with other breast cancer subtypes1–7. Despite recent progress, and while chemotherapy remains the backbone of systemic treatment, novel targeted therapies and immunotherapeutic approaches have expanded the therapeutic armamentarium for TNBC in recent years8,9.

Approximately one-third of patients diagnosed with stage II or III TNBC experience metastatic recurrence within 2 to 3 years, despite receiving standard-of-care chemotherapy10. Following metastasis, median survival ranges from 12 to 18 months, underscoring the critical need for effective therapeutic strategies beyond conventional chemotherapy11. In TNBC, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) is predominantly expressed on tumour-infiltrating immune cells (ICs), suppressing anticancer immune responses. Therefore, targeting the PD-L1 pathway represents a promising therapeutic approach in this subtype12.

Atezolizumab is an anti–PD-L1 antibody that restores anti-tumour T-cell activity. Atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel demonstrated significantly improved efficacy over placebo plus nab-paclitaxel and manageable safety, in PD-L1–positive metastatic TNBC (mTNBC) in the IMpassion130 trial13,14, leading to its approval and recommendation as a first-line standard therapy in international guidelines15–17. Atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel was approved in Japan in 2019 and has since been recommended as a standard therapy for PD-L1–positive TNBC by the Japanese Breast Cancer Society Clinical Practice Guidelines18,19. However, its clinical application in diverse real-world populations, including those with characteristics that met the exclusion criteria of IMpassion130, remains insufficiently characterised.

Despite the efficacy of atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel in clinical trials, real-world evidence remains limited. Moreover, immune-related adverse events (irAEs) are known to occur during treatment as well as after discontinuation; however, detailed characterisation of these events is limited. Given the aggressive nature, poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options associated with mTNBC, it is essential to evaluate the real-world effectiveness and safety of treatment regimens20.

To address this gap, the JBCRG-C08/ATTRIBUTE study (UMIN000041747) prospectively evaluated real-world safety and effectiveness in patients with PD-L1–positive mTNBC who were scheduled to initiate atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel. The study included those who would have been excluded from clinical trials, such as those with a history of autoimmune disease (AID), those receiving atezolizumab as a second- or third-line treatment or those with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) ≥2.

Results

A total of 151 patients were enrolled between 15 February 2021 and 26 April 2024 across 79 institutions in Japan. The safety analysis population included 149 patients, after excluding two patients who had not received atezolizumab. The full analysis population comprised 148 patients, after excluding one non-conforming case. The response-evaluable population consisted of 107 patients from those with measurable disease at baseline (per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours [RECIST] version 1.1) (n = 123), after excluding those with inappropriate baseline imaging (n = 5) and those with ongoing treatment at the data cut-off (n = 11) (Supplementary Fig. 1). The median follow-up duration for the safety analysis population was 12.9 months.

Patient and disease characteristics

The median age of patients in this real-world study was 59 years (Table 1). Most patients (96.0%) had an ECOG PS of 0 or 1. Eleven patients (7.4%) had a history or comorbidity of AID prior to study treatment initiation. Atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel was administered as first-line therapy in 70.5% of cases, second-line in 22.1% and third-line in 7.4%.

Safety

In the safety analysis population (n = 149), from treatment initiation to 30 days after treatment discontinuation, adverse events (AEs) were reported in 144 patients (96.6%; 95% confidence interval [CI], 92.3–98.9). The most common AEs were neutropenia in 72 patients (48.3%; 95% CI, 40.1–56.6), alopecia in 68 patients (45.6%; 95% CI, 37.5–54.0), and neuropathy peripheral in 66 patients (44.3%; 95% CI, 36.2–52.7). From 31 days to 6 months after treatment discontinuation, AEs were reported in four patients (2.7%; 95% CI, 0.7–6.7); these included adrenal insufficiency (n = 2), pneumonitis (n = 1), and palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome (n = 1). Overall, from treatment initiation to 6 months after treatment discontinuation, AEs occurred in 144 patients (96.6%; 95% CI, 92.3–98.9). Serious (SAEs) occurring from treatment initiation to 30 days after treatment discontinuation were reported in 25 patients (16.8%; 95% CI, 11.2–23.8). Among these SAEs, 21 events were of National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) severity Grade ≥3 (14.1%; 95% CI, 8.9–20.7) (Table 2). Two patients (1.3%; 95% CI, 0.2–4.8) experienced Grade 5 pneumonitis.

The most common SAEs (reported in ≥2 patients) were pneumonitis in five patients (3.4%; 95% CI, 1.1–7.7), adrenal insufficiency in two (1.3%; 95% CI, 0.2–4.8), cardiac failure in two (1.3%; 95% CI, 0.2–4.8), diarrhoea in two (1.3%; 95% CI, 0.2–4.8) and pyrexia in two (1.3%; 95% CI, 0.2–4.8) (Table 2).

Between 31 days and 6 months after treatment discontinuation, one case (0.7%; 95% CI, 0.0–3.7) of palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome was observed as an SAE. From treatment initiation to 6 months after treatment discontinuation, SAEs were observed in 26 patients (17.4%; 95% CI, 11.7–24.5) (Supplementary Table 1).

Discontinuation of atezolizumab due to AEs occurred in 21 patients (14.1%; 95% CI, 8.9–20.7) and discontinuation of nab-paclitaxel due to AEs occurred in 26 patients (17.4%; 95% CI, 11.7–24.5). For atezolizumab, 14 discontinuations were due to irAEs. These included pneumonitis (seven events), and one event each of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) increased, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) increased, colitis, cough, gastritis, pyrexia and immune thrombocytopenia.

irAEs were reported in 46 patients (30.9%; 95% CI, 23.6–39.0) from treatment initiation to 30 days after treatment discontinuation, in four patients (2.7%; 95% CI, 0.7–6.7) from 31 days to 6 months after treatment discontinuation and in 48 patients (32.2%; 95% CI, 24.8–40.4) from treatment initiation to 6 months after treatment discontinuation (Supplementary Table 1). The most common irAEs reported in >5 patients from treatment initiation to 30 days after treatment discontinuation were pneumonitis in 11 patients (7.4%; 95% CI, 3.7–12.8), hypothyroidism in nine patients (6.0%; 95% CI, 2.8–11.2) and adrenal insufficiency in eight patients (5.4%; 95% CI, 2.3–10.3). During the period from 31 days to 6 months after treatment discontinuation, the irAEs were adrenal insufficiency (n = 2; 95% CI, 0.2–4.8; one was Grade ≥3), pneumonitis (n = 1; 95% CI, 0.0–3.7) and palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome (n = 1; 95% CI, 0.0–3.7; Grade ≥3).

Effectiveness

Objective response rate (ORR) in the response-evaluable population was 36.4% (95% CI, 27.4–46.3), with seven patients (6.5%; 95% CI, 2.7–13.0) achieving complete response and 32 patients (29.9%; 95% CI, 21.4–39.5) achieving partial response. Twenty-five patients (23.4%; 95% CI, 15.7–32.5) had stable disease, including five patients (4.7%; 95% CI, 1.5–10.6) with stable disease lasting ≥24 weeks. Thirty-nine patients (36.4%; 95% CI, 27.4–46.3) had progressive disease and four patients (3.7%) were not evaluable. Clinical benefit rate (CBR) was 41.1% (95% CI, 31.7–51.0).

In patients who received first-line treatment and had measurable disease at baseline (n = 78), the ORR was 43.6% (95% CI, 32.4–55.3) and the CBR was 47.4% (95% CI, 36.0–59.1).

The median progression-free survival (PFS) in the full analysis population was 5.6 months (95% CI, 4.2–6.7) (Fig. 3). The median PFS was 5.7 months for patients who received atezolizumab as first-line treatment and 4.6 months for those who had second-/third-line treatment (hazard ratio; 1.21, 95% CI, 0.82–1.79) (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

This multi-institutional, prospective, observational study describes the characteristics and outcomes for 149 patients treated with atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel in real-world practice. The study included patients who would have been excluded from enrolment in the IMpassion130 trial, such as patients with ECOG PS ≥2, those who previously received 1–2 lines of systemic treatment for mTNBC and those with a history or comorbidity of AID21.

The findings of the study showed a lower SAEs rate compared with the IMpassion130 trial (16.8% vs. 23.9%)14. This discrepancy likely reflects the fundamental differences in safety monitoring and reporting methodologies between a structured clinical trial and routine clinical practice. Clinical trials mandate intensive monitoring and structured AEs capture, which tends to ensure comprehensive data collection. In contrast, it is a well-established limitation that observational studies are susceptible to under-ascertainment and underreporting of AEs. Indeed, a systematic review on this topic has shown that AEs tend to be underreported in spontaneous reporting in real-world clinical practice22. Therefore, the lower SAE rate observed in our study may reflect the inherent limitations of data capture in a real-world setting. While a prospective observational study may reduce the likelihood of missing SAEs due to the severity of such events, the possibility of underreporting cannot be entirely excluded. The differences in incidence rates can be attributed to the broader data collection period in the IMpassion130 trial, which included SAEs reported up to 30 days after treatment as well as those occurring beyond 31 days14. Previous data also included patients with a negative PD-L1 status14.

The effectiveness outcomes observed in our real-world analysis were numerically lower than those reported in the IMpassion130 clinical trial14. This discrepancy likely reflects inclusion of patients with ECOG PS ≥2, prior lines of therapy, or autoimmune comorbidities, who are systematically excluded from pivotal trials but commonly encountered in practice. These results illustrate the challenge of extrapolating trial efficacy to effectiveness in heterogeneous real-world populations.

No new safety signals were observed in this real-world analysis. The study showed that irAEs can occur for up to 6 months after treatment discontinuation, extending beyond conventional trial observation periods. This real-world evidence aligns with Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer recommendations23 for long-term monitoring and underscores the need for structured survivorship care models that address delayed immune-related toxicities.

The underlying mechanism of irAE occurrence is still largely unclear. The drug elimination half-life is considered one of the mechanisms for post-treatment irAE occurrence. The elimination half-life of atezolizumab is 27 days24, and it takes 5 months for complete elimination from the body after administration. Therefore, it is considered that irAEs may occur for extended periods after treatment discontinuation. Accordingly, some studies recommend continued monitoring every 3 months for the first year after immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy, followed by biannual assessments thereafter25,26. Since irAEs can recur or appear with delayed onset after treatment discontinuation, continuous observation is necessary not only during treatment but also after treatment discontinuation.

Patients with AID experienced a higher incidence of irAEs (45.5%), consistent with previous reports27,28. Notably, most events were manageable, Grade 1–2 and did not lead to treatment discontinuation, indicating that cancer immunotherapy may be feasible in carefully selected patients through thorough risk–benefit assessment and multidisciplinary management. This finding broadens the potential treatment population beyond those typically included in clinical trials. These results indicate that while patients with a history or comorbidity of AID have a higher rate of irAEs, no new safety signals were identified. Evidence regarding the irAE profile of ICI therapy in patients with breast cancer and a history or comorbidity of AID is currently limited, and this study provides valuable insights into this clinically important population.

When administering ICIs to patients with a history or comorbidity of AID, treatment should be considered with careful assessment of risks and benefits29.

These findings align with the real-world ANASTASE study conducted in Italy, which evaluated atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel in PD-L1–positive mTNBC and reported safety and effectiveness outcomes consistent with the efficacy observed in the IMpassion130 trial, with no unexpected AEs30. Similarly, the global EL1SSAR study demonstrated comparable safety and efficacy profiles in a more selected population, further supporting the consistency of results with both this study and IMpassion13031.

Finally, while atezolizumab has been withdrawn for TNBC in some regions, our findings offer critical real-world insights for countries where atezolizumab remains available, and they underscore the importance of context-specific data to guide treatment decisions in a rapidly evolving therapeutic landscape.

This is an observational study conducted in a real-world setting; therefore, there were variations in visit timing, imaging evaluation intervals and the management of AEs. As an observational study, our analysis is subject to the inherent limitations of AE reporting in a real-world setting, including potential underreporting and under-ascertainment. AEs that are of lower grade or whose causal relationship to the study treatment is not immediately clear may be less likely to be documented. Consequently, the safety profile described in this study may not fully capture the complete incidence of all AEs.

Furthermore, the possibility of selection bias cannot be excluded, which may influence the generalisability of the findings. Given the median follow-up period of 12.9 months, many censored cases were included in the PFS and OS analyses, limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding long-term effectiveness.

Moreover, irAEs potentially associated with atezolizumab treatment were classified solely based on the clinical judgement of treating physicians, without validation through independent adjudication. Furthermore, in the case of late-onset AEs that influence subsequent treatments, it may be challenging to determine whether these irAEs were related to atezolizumab or whether results were confounded by other factors, including the effects of post-treatment therapies, as time has passed since treatment discontinuation, and the impact of subsequent treatments on outcomes has not been sufficiently evaluated.

This large-scale prospective study, which enrolled 151 patients with mTNBC, demonstrated the safety of atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel combination therapy in real-world clinical practice. Despite including patients who met the exclusion criteria of IMpassion130, atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel combination therapy had no new safety concerns, reinforcing its clinical utility in the real-world setting. However, treatment in patients with a history or comorbidity of AID resulted in a high incidence of irAEs. Treatment should be considered with careful assessment of risks and benefits.

Methods

Study design and patients

This was a multi-institutional, prospective, observational study. Eligible patients were aged 20 years or older, and had PD-L1–positive, hormone receptor-negative, HER2-negative, inoperable or recurrent breast cancer, with ≤2 prior systemic treatments for mTNBC (excluding endocrine therapy) and without a history of hypersensitivity to any component of atezolizumab, who were scheduled to receive atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel in clinical practice. PD-L1–positivity was determined using the VENTANA PD-L1 (SP142) assay (Roche Diagnostics K.K., Tokyo, Japan) and defined as PD-L1 expression on tumour-infiltrating ICs covering ≥1% of the tumour area (IC ≥1%). The planned end date of enrolment was 3 years and 10 months from the date of enrolment of the first study subject, or until the target sample size was reached, whichever occurred first. The end date of the observation period was 4 years and 10 months from the date of enrolment of the first study subject, or up to 2 years from the date of enrolment of the last study subject, whichever occurred first.

Study treatments

The study treatment was defined as treatment with atezolizumab and nab-paclitaxel. However, if either atezolizumab or nab-paclitaxel was discontinued due to AEs, and the other treatment was continued, the study treatment was regarded as continued.

The recommended dosage and administration schedule consisted of atezolizumab 840 mg administered intravenously every 2 weeks, concurrently with nab-paclitaxel 100 mg/m² administered intravenously on Days 1, 8 and 15. However, alternative dosage and administration schedules were not considered protocol deviations. The study protocol recommended referring to the IMpassion130 trial for guidance on treatment interruption, discontinuation and dose reduction. However, specific criteria were not defined, and decisions were left to the investigator’s discretion. No post-treatment therapy was specified after study treatment discontinuation.

The study protocol recommended periodic imaging assessments for tumour evaluation. However, as this was an observational study without a specific imaging schedule, tumour assessments were conducted by investigators according to routine clinical practice. The end of study treatment was defined as the day of the final dose when both atezolizumab and nab-paclitaxel were terminated. If nab-paclitaxel alone was continued after the discontinuation of atezolizumab, and nab-paclitaxel with another drug was started, the end date of study treatment was the day of administration of nab-paclitaxel monotherapy immediately before the start of the combination therapy. If the study drug was not administered for 12 weeks or longer, the last dose at this time point was considered as the end date of study treatment.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was incidence of investigator-assessed AEs. AE severity was graded according to CTCAE version 5.0. All AEs were collected from treatment initiation until 30 days after treatment discontinuation. The relationship between AEs and the study drug was determined by the investigator’s judgement. In this study, SAEs were defined according to the study protocol as AEs meeting any of the following criteria: 1) fatal; 2) life-threatening; 3) requiring inpatient hospitalisation or prolongation of existing hospitalisation; 4) resulting in persistent or significant disability/incapacity; 5) a congenital anomaly/birth defect; or 6) other medically important conditions requiring intervention to prevent one of the other serious outcomes. From 31 days to 6 months after treatment discontinuation, events judged as related to atezolizumab, which were categorised into two groups: 1) any SAEs, and 2) a predefined list of AEs of special interest, regardless of their seriousness, were collected. The list of AEs of special interest included: pneumonitis, Grade ≥3 hypoxia or dyspnoea, colitis, endocrine disorders (diabetes, pancreatitis or adrenal insufficiency), vasculitis, hepatitis, liver enzymes (AST >3 × upper limit of normal [ULN] and bilirubin >2 × ULN, ALT >3 × ULN and bilirubin >2 × ULN, AST >10 × ULN, ALT >10 × ULN), systemic lupus erythematosus, Guillain-Barré syndrome and skin reactions (vitiligo, bullous pemphigoid). irAEs were collected during treatment and after treatment discontinuation.

Secondary outcomes included PFS, ORR, CBR and OS. CBR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving complete response, partial response or durable stable disease (≥24 weeks). Effectiveness was assessed by investigators based on RECIST version 1.1.

Statistical analysis and considerations

Approximately 150 patients were planned to be enrolled to ensure a reasonable chance of observing ≥1 rare AE (expected incidence 1.1%) for the primary objective of safety. The safety analysis population comprised patients who received ≥1 dose of atezolizumab following registration. The full analysis population for effectiveness analyses comprised the safety analysis population, excluding ineligible patients and those without effectiveness assessments.

For the primary analysis, the 95% CIs for the number and proportion of patients who experienced AEs were estimated. For time-to-event outcomes, survival functions were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with survival rates at specific timepoints and 95% CIs calculated using Greenwood’s formula. For ORR and CBR, the numbers of eligible patients, as well as the numbers and proportions of responders with 95% CIs, were calculated. Confirmation of the duration of response according to RECIST was not necessary for this clinical study. Subgroup analyses were exploratory in nature and were not powered for formal statistical comparisons; therefore, no adjustment for multiplicity was performed.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). This report analysed data collected up to a cut-off set 6 months after completion of enrolment for all patients; effectiveness results should be regarded as preliminary and may change upon final analysis.
