Genomic and transcriptomic basis of morphological and life cycle diversity in the prasinophyte alga Pseudoscourfieldia marina
Authors: Olivier Crépeault, Monique Turmel, Christian Otis, Jean-François Pombert, Claude Lemieux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prasinophyte green alga Pseudoscourfieldia marina exhibits striking morphological diversity, with coccoid and flagellated forms now recognized as a single species. To investigate the basis of this divergence, we compare here the genomes and transcriptomes of two morphotypes: CCMP1203 (coccoid, non-flagellated) and UIO007 (scaly, flagellated). Although their genome architecture is largely conserved, the strains differ in ploidy and repeat content, and harbor divergent outlier chromosomes enriched in transposable elements and carbohydrate metabolism genes, suggesting roles in antiviral defense. CCMP1203 is predominantly diploid, whereas UIO007 is haploid and strongly upregulates genes for flagellar assembly and scale formation, including those encoding the complete biosynthetic pathway for the 2-keto sugar 3-deoxy-D-manno-octulosonate, a major carbohydrate component of prasinophyte scales. The presence of meiosis-specific and life-cycle genes in P. marina supports a heteromorphic haplodiplontic life cycle, with the flagellated, scaly UIO007 strain representing the haploid phase and the coccoid CCMP1203 strain the diploid phase.

Introduction

Prasinophyte algae are a morphologically diverse and ecologically significant group of unicellular green algae that occupy a pivotal position in the evolutionary history of green plants (Viridiplantae). Formerly grouped under the term “prasinophytes”, these algae are now recognized as spanning the earliest diverging lineages of the Chlorophyta1. Their deep-branching phylogenetic placement makes them essential for reconstructing the features of the last common ancestor of Viridiplantae and for understanding early events in green algal diversification2,3. To date, nine major prasinophyte lineages have been identified4, and their widespread distribution across marine environments highlights their ecological importance.

Among these lineages, the marine picoalgae Pycnococcus provasolii and Pseudoscourfieldia marina form a distinct clade (clade V), recently classified as the class Pseudoscourfieldiophyceae5. P. provasolii is a naked, coccoid alga lacking flagella6, whereas P. marina is a scaly, biflagellated alga with a distinct oval-to-truncate morphology7–9 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Pycnococcus has been observed in deep oceanic waters, particularly near the pycnocline, and appears well-adapted to low-light environments, an adaptation likely mediated by a dual-domain photoreceptor (DUC1) capable of sensing both orange/far-red and blue light10. Comparative analyses of P. provasolii and P. marina organellar genomes have recently revealed near-identical sequences, prompting a taxonomic revision that unites both strains under the species Pseudoscourfieldia marina5. This finding is particularly striking, as such pronounced morphological differences between morphotypes are rare among unicellular algae. P. marina therefore provides a unique model for investigating morphological and genomic plasticity in prasinophytes.

In this study, we sequenced and analyzed the nuclear genomes and transcriptomes of two P. marina morphotypes, strains CCMP1203 (coccoid, non-flagellated) and UIO007 (scaly, flagellated), to further investigate their genetic relationship, explore the molecular mechanisms underlying their morphological divergence, and gain deeper insights into prasinophyte evolution. Our results reveal that although the two strains share a highly conserved genomic architecture, they differ markedly in ploidy and gene expression. Notably, genes involved in flagellar assembly and scale biosynthesis are strongly upregulated in UIO007. These observations support the hypothesis that P. marina exhibits a haplodiplontic life cycle, with the flagellated form representing the haploid vegetative phase and the coccoid form corresponding to the diploid phase. Within the Archaeplastida, heteromorphic life cycles characterized by pronounced morphological differences between alternating free-living haploid and diploid phases are typical of multicellular organisms11, but have not been documented in unicellular green algae.

Comparative analysis of the P. marina CCMP1203 and UIO007 nuclear genomes

The CCMP1203 and UIO007 genomes are essentially collinear (Fig. 1). With the exceptions of the CCMP1203 chromosome 32 and UIO007 chromosome 16, hereafter designated as outliers, all chromosomes of a given strain have homologous counterparts spanning most of their entire length in the other strain (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Dot plot analysis revealed only two chromosomal inversions, each involving a different chromosome pair (Supplementary Fig. 2).

A key difference between the two genomes lies in their ploidy. Three lines of evidence indicate that CCMP1203 is predominantly diploid. First, allele phasing yielded a nearly complete alternative haplotype assembly, with 195 allelic contigs covering 83% of the primary assembly. Second, the majority of the 158,635 single-nucleotide polymorphic (SNP) and small indel sites are biallelic, with a bell-shaped allelic frequency distribution centered at 50%, indicative of equal representation of two alleles (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Third, k-mer analyses using raw reads for both genomes revealed that the CCMP1203 coverage profile displayed two distinct peaks, consistent with diploidy, whereas that obtained for UIO007 showed a single peak, consistent with haploidy (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c).

Illumina read-depth analysis revealed aneuploidy in both strains. In CCMP1203, chromosome 32 occurs as a single copy, whereas chromosome 31 exhibits mixed diploid and triploid signals, possibly reflecting aneuploid variation within the cell population (Fig. 1). Although assembly errors cannot be entirely ruled out, synteny analysis supports assembly accuracy: UIO007 chromosome 31 is fully colinear with its homolog in CCMP1203, which shows no abnormal ploidy signal, and is highly syntenic with homologous chromosomes in the CCAP 190/2 and NIES-2893 assemblies (Supplementary Fig. 4). These syntenic relationships strongly suggest that the observed triploid signal represents genuine biological variation rather than an assembly artifact. In contrast, the UIO007 genome is predominantly haploid, as indicated by its extremely low heterozygosity (3.9E–06 SNPs/kb vs. 5.0E–03 SNPs/kb in CCMP1203). Nevertheless, the UIO007 genome also displays aneuploidy, with chromosomes 30 and 43 exhibiting approximately double the average read depth (Fig. 1).

The elevated repeat content in the UIO007 genome is largely due to a higher abundance of long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons, particularly from the Ty1/Copia and Gypsy/DIRS1 families (Table 2). A total of 467 intact LTR retrotransposons were identified across its 45 chromosomes, compared to only 18 found in CCMP1203, distributed across 15 chromosomes. Notably, the outlier chromosomes, chromosome 32 in CCMP1203 and chromosome 16 in UIO007, contain more repetitive sequences than the rest of their respective genomes (Fig. 3). RNA-seq read mapping revealed striking differences in gene expression between the two outlier chromosomes. Genes on the CCMP1203 outlier show minimal or no transcriptional activity, whereas a substantial proportion of genes on the UIO007 outlier exhibit markedly higher transcript levels (Fig. 4).

Outlier chromosomes have not been reported in the genome assemblies of P. marina strains NIES 289310 and CCAP 190/212. We searched the NIES-2893 genome for genes located on the outlier chromosomes of CCMP1203 and UIO007 using reciprocal BLASTP of predicted proteins but found no orthologs. For CCAP 190/2, a similar search could not be performed because gene annotations are not available in the current version of its genome assembly (GCA_938743325.2).

Transcriptional analyses of genes potentially associated with morphological differences

Among the 8647 single-copy orthologs shared by CCMP1203 and UIO007, 4385 were differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 3369 upregulated in UIO007 and 1016 in CCMP1203 (Supplementary Fig. 5a). To provide functional context, we grouped DEGs into three expression groups based on log₂FC intervals ( ≥ 2.00, ≤–2.00, and intermediate) and classified them into KEGG functional categories. Supplementary Fig. 5b shows the proportion of genes from each KEGG category within these groups, calculated relative to the total number of genes in each group. Notably, cilium proteins contributed most to the group of genes upregulated in the flagellated UIO007 strain, alongside cytoskeletal proteins. In contrast, CCMP1203 showed higher contributions from transporter and exosome protein families.

Genes encoding flagellar proteins

We identified a set of 274 genes associated with flagellar assembly, all of which are present in both CCMP1203 and UIO007 (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 4). These genes encode essential components of the 9 + 2 axoneme, including dynein arms, radial spokes, and nexin–dynein regulatory complexes, as well as elements of the intraflagellar transport machinery. The corresponding protein orthologs are highly conserved: among the 274 proteins, 85 exhibit 100% sequence identity, with an average identity of 99.24% across the entire set (Supplementary Table 4). Genes involved in scale formation

Prasinophyte scales are synthesized in the Golgi apparatus15,16 and contain the 2-keto sugar 3-deoxy-D-manno-octulosonate (Kdo) as a major carbohydrate component16–18, yet the enzymatic machinery responsible for their biosynthesis remains poorly characterized. The three genes involved in the CMP-Kdo biosynthesis pathway in land plants (kdsA, kdsB, and kdsD)19 are conserved in P. marina, and all are upregulated in UIO007 (Fig. 6, Supplementary Table 5). As in Arabidopsis thaliana, P. marina lacks the bacterial kdsC gene, suggesting that its function may be carried out by a broad-specificity phosphatase19. While the Arabidopsis kdsB gene encodes a CMP-Kdo synthetase targeted to the mitochondria20, P. marina possesses two kdsB paralogs (47.4% identity) encoding proteins predicted to localize to the cytosol and mitochondria. In land plants, Kdo activation occurs exclusively in mitochondria, requiring CMP-Kdo to be transported across both mitochondrial and Golgi membranes to reach the site of rhamnogalacturonan II (RG-II) synthesis, a complex pectic polysaccharide of the extracellular matrix. This mitochondrial localization is thought to reflect an ancestral eubacterial pathway retained for the synthesis of a putative Kdo₂-lipid A molecule within the organelle19. Efficient transport of CMP-Kdo into the Golgi is essential for scale assembly. Our analysis revealed 12 candidate P. marina genes encoding CMP–sialic acid transporter homologs (Supplementary Table 5), including several closely related to Arabidopsis genes AT5G41760 and AT4G35335. These Arabidopsis genes, although annotated as CMP-sialic acid transporters, are hypothesized to function as CMP-Kdo transporters due to structural similarity between sialic acid and Kdo, along with the absence of sialic acid in land plants21. Six of the P. marina genes are located at conserved loci in both CCMP1203 and UIO007, while five are exclusive to the UIO007 outlier chromosome. Most are upregulated in UIO007, and their encoded proteins are predicted to localize to Golgi membranes (Supplementary Table 5).

Once inside the Golgi lumen, CMP-Kdo serves as a substrate for downstream glycosyltransferases involved in scale biosynthesis. Comparative genomic analyses between naked and scaled prasinophytes have previously revealed expansions of the carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) families GT29 and GH33 in scaly mamiellophyceans (Bathycoccus prasinos, Dolichomastix tenuilepis and Mantoniella tinhauana) relative to naked taxa (Micromonas spp. and Ostreococcus spp.), highlighting their likely role in scale biosynthesis2,22–25. Supporting the involvement of GT29 in Kdo metabolism, a recent study demonstrated that the Arabidopsis GT29 enzyme MGP2 functions as an inverting RG-II CMP-β-Kdo transferase26. P. marina encodes the largest number of GT29 (103) and GH33 (26) genes reported among scaly prasinophytes (Table 3). These genes predominantly encode proteins predicted to localize to the Golgi or extracellular space (Supplementary Table 6), with most showing upregulation in the scaly UIO007 strain. To identify additional P. marina CAZymes potentially involved in scale assembly, we examined extracellular or Golgi-localized proteins encoded by genes that are upregulated in the scaly P. marina strain and/or belong to CAZyme families expanded in scaly prasinophytes relative to their naked counterparts. Several candidates met these criteria, including members of the GT47, GT77, GT95, and GT96 families (Table 3, Supplementary Table 6), which are known to glycosylate cell wall extensins27. These hydroxyproline-rich glycoproteins serve as scaffolds for the deposition of carbohydrate polymers28. The enzymes act on the repeating pentapeptide sequence Ser-Hyp-Hyp-Hyp-Hyp found in extensins: GT96 catalyzes the monogalactosylation of the serine residue, while GT47, GT77, GT95 mediate the addition of oligoarabinosides to adjacent hydroxyproline residue27 (Supplementary Fig. 7).

We also identified GT23 family fucosyltransferases in P. marina that may contribute to scale assembly. The involvement of fucose in prasinophyte scales has been previously suggested, based on the presence of a Golgi-targeted fucosyltransferase uniquely shared by the scaly prasinophytes Bathycoccus and Dolichomastix25. In our analysis, five GT23 genes were found in the naked CCMP1203 strain, while seven were identified in the scaly UIO007 strain, with the two additional genes located on the outlier chromosome (Table 3). Both strains possess larger GT23 gene sets than those reported in Bathycoccus and Chloropicon (Table 3). Several other P. marina CAZymes exhibit irregular distribution patterns across chlorophyte taxa, and many of the corresponding genes are also overexpressed in the scaly UIO007 strain (Fig. 7). Particularly noteworthy are members of the GT13, GT14, GT19, GT43, and GT61 families, which are broadly conserved in land plants. In Arabidopsis, GT14 proteins participate in the biosynthesis of type II arabinogalactans, abundant components of the cell surface29, while GT43 and GT61 enzymes are involved in the synthesis and modification of cell wall xylans30,31. In contrast, glycoside hydrolases from the GH15, GH30, GH92, GH125, and GH141 families are absent from land plants but are common in fungi. Most of the P. marina proteins belonging to the aforementioned CAZyme families are predicted to localize to the Golgi apparatus or extracellular space (Fig. 7), consistent with roles in cell surface remodeling and scale biosynthesis.

Genomic signatures of meiosis and life cycle regulation in P. marina

Our data reveal a complex life cycle in P. marina, characterized by dominant haploid and diploid vegetative phases associated with distinct morphologies. Because sexual reproduction has not been demonstrated for this prasinophyte, it remains unclear whether transitions can occur between the two ploidy states. To address this question, we searched for genes involved in meiosis and syngamy.

Of the 33 genes comprising the meiosis toolkit described by Malik et al.32 and Gazquez et al.33, we identified 29 (including 10 meiosis-specific genes) in both CCMP1203 and UIO007 (Supplementary Table 7). Zip4, a meiosis-specific gene, as well as Mlh3, Rad1, and Rad52 were not detected. Most identified genes exhibited negative log2FC values, indicating higher expression in CCMP1203 relative to UIO007, consistent with its diploid status.

We examined the conserved gene triad encoding the FUS1/GEX2, HAP2/GCS1, and KAR5/GEX1 proteins responsible for the core cellular events that define syngamy across algae and plants34. The Hap2/Gcs1 and Kar5/Gex1 genes, which mediate gamete membrane and nuclear fusions, respectively, were identified (Supplementary Table 7). However, Fus1/Gex2, encoding the gamete adhesion protein was not detected, likely due to its low sequence conservation. Expression patterns were consistent with functional roles: Hap2/Gcs1 was strongly upregulated in the UIO007 haploid strain, as observed in minus gametes of the volvocine green alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii35, whereas Kar5/Gex1 showed a negative log2FC value, in line with its expected expression in the CCMP1203 diploid strain.

In addition, we identified homologs of the Chlamydomonas Gsm1 and Gsp1 genes encoding subunits of a heterodimeric TALE-class homeodomain transcription factor that controls the haploid-to-diploid transition by activating diploid-specific genes and repressing haploid-specific ones36 (Supplementary Table 7). Both genes are present and upregulated in the haploid UIO007 strain, suggesting that, unlike in Chlamydomonas36 but similar to their homologs in the multicellular brown alga Ectocarpus37, they do not exhibit sex-specific patterns of expression in gametes.

Our search for sex-determination genes yielded no clear homologs of Chlamydomonas mating-type specific genes, such as MID, MTD1, MTA1, and FUS138. MID is a dominant gene in the minus mating-type locus and is essential for activating minus-specific genes while repressing plus-specific genes in gametes39. Although we identified nine P. marina MID-like genes containing the RWP-RK domain of transcription factors (Supplementary Table 7), these domains were too divergent from the conserved region of volvocine MID proteins to allow unambiguous identification of a true MID homolog.

Discussion

Genomic conservation and divergence in P. marina

Despite near-identical genomic architecture, P. marina strains CCMP1203 and UIO007 exhibit profound differences in ploidy and transcriptional profiles, revealing a rare case of morphological plasticity within a single species of prasinophyte algae. The nuclear genomes of both strains are highly similar in size, chromosomal architecture, and gene content; however, key differences were observed in chromosome copy number, repetitive sequence content, and the structure of an outlier chromosome. The coccoid CCMP1203 strain is predominantly diploid, whereas the scaly, flagellated UIO007 strain is primarily haploid. Approximately 50% of single-copy orthologs were differentially expressed, with a strong bias toward upregulation in UIO007, suggesting broad transcriptional reprogramming associated with its unique cellular features. Both strains possess a full complement of flagella- and scale-associated genes, but these are predominantly upregulated in UIO007, consistent with its flagellated and scaly phenotype. These findings support the conclusion that CCMP1203 and UIO007 represent distinct morphotypes of the same species, with morphological divergence primarily driven by differential gene expression rather than pseudogenization or relaxed selection.

Chromosomal copy number variation and aneuploidy

Neither strain conforms strictly to a diploid or haploid state, as both exhibit aneuploidy in specific chromosomes. Aneuploidy is a well-documented phenomenon in cultured Mamiellophyceae40 and has also been reported for the diploid Chloropicon primus22,41 and the allodiploid Auxenochlorella UTEX 250-A42. Given that the P. marina strains analyzed here have been maintained in laboratory culture for extended periods, it remains possible that the coccoid and flagellated forms are fully diploid and haploid, respectively, in their natural environments. Notably, the predominantly diploid genome assembly of the coccoid P. marina strain CCAP 190/2 also contains two haploid chromosomes12, but these do not correspond to the aneuploid chromosomes identified in CCMP1203 and UIO007. This suggests that chromosomal copy number variation in P. marina may involve distinct genomic regions across strains, potentially contributing to phenotypic plasticity or laboratory adaptation. Future studies examining wild isolates will be essential to determine whether the observed ploidy and expression differences are maintained in natural environments or represent culture-induced adaptations.

Outlier chromosomes: evolutionary hotspots and potential viral defense

We identified two outlier chromosomes in CCMP1203 and UIO007 with distinct structural and functional features, reminiscent of the small and big outlier chromosomes (SOC and BOC) reported in Mamiellophyceae23,43,44. These chromosomes stand apart from the rest of the genome due to their reduced GC content, higher repeat density, and greater variability in gene content. Although many genes on the P. marina outlier chromosomes remain uncharacterized, those with sequence-based annotations are predominantly associated with glycoconjugate metabolism and methyltransferase activity, characteristics commonly linked to SOCs.

These chromosomes may act as evolutionary hotspots, facilitating rapid adaptation and possibly viral defense. In Mamiellophyceae, SOCs have been implicated in antiviral mechanisms, often involving chromosomal rearrangements mediated by transposable elements and alterations in glycosylation patterns45. The presence of actively transcribed LTR retroelements in the UIO007 outlier chromosome suggests a similar role in P. marina, potentially enhancing genome plasticity in response to viral pressure. Identifying and characterizing viruses that interact with P. marina will be essential to test this hypothesis and to elucidate the ecological forces driving genome dynamics and life cycle transitions in this species.

Despite the apparent absence of outlier chromosomes in the genome assemblies of P. marina strains NIES 289310 and CCAP 190/212, compelling evidence supports their presence in CCMP1203 and UIO007. The identification of telomeric sequences flanking both ends of these chromosomes strongly indicates that they are complete and distinct chromosomal units. Furthermore, the pronounced sequence divergence between the two outlier chromosomes suggests rapid evolutionary dynamics, which may explain their apparent absence in other strains through lineage-specific loss or divergence beyond recognition. Such lineage-specific divergence could reflect adaptive responses to environmental pressures, including viral predation, and may contribute to genomic innovation within P. marina.

Transcriptional regulation of flagellar machinery

The flagellar axoneme of P. marina exhibits the canonical 9 + 2 microtubule arrangement, comprising nine outer doublet microtubules surrounding a central pair of singlets8, as in the model alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii14. We identified 274 flagella-associated genes in both genomes, most of which are also present in Chlamydomonas (Supplementary Table 4), underscoring the strong conservation of the flagellar apparatus in P. marina. Notably, 93% of these genes are upregulated in UIO007, consistent with the presence of motile flagella in this strain and their absence in the non-flagellated CCMP1203.

Although most flagellar genes showed higher expression in UIO007, a small subset, including specific α- and β-tubulin isoforms, displayed divergent expression patterns. This variation may reflect isoform-specific regulation or functional specialization in distinct cellular contexts. Furthermore, expression levels of CCT chaperonins, which contribute to BBSome assembly and broader cytoskeletal organization, remained largely unchanged between strains. This suggests that not all components of the flagellar system are subject to strain-specific transcriptional regulation.

Molecular architecture of scale biosynthesis

Our findings also shed light on the molecular framework underlying scale biosynthesis in P. marina, revealing a complex and specialized network of CAZymes likely evolved to support the formation of these elaborate extracellular structures. While prior biochemical studies have implicated Kdo in scale formation16–18, our genomic and transcriptomic analyses provide a comprehensive view of the underlying biosynthetic machinery in P. marina. The conservation and upregulation of CMP-Kdo biosynthesis genes in the scaly UIO007 strain underscore the central role of Kdo metabolism in scale formation. Notably, the presence of two kdsB paralogs with distinct predicted subcellular localizations supports the hypothesis that P. marina has evolved a dual-pathway system for Kdo activation, potentially serving different cellular functions, including scale assembly. The identification of multiple putative CMP-Kdo transporters, many predicted to localize to the Golgi, suggests the existence of a dedicated transport mechanism for delivering activated Kdo to the site of scale biosynthesis.

The expansion of GT29 and GH33 CAZyme families in P. marina, their predicted Golgi or extracellular localization, and their specific upregulation in the scaly morphotype strongly support their role in assembling the Kdo-rich polysaccharide matrix of scales. This hypothesis is further reinforced by the recent demonstration of RG-II CMP-β-Kdo transferase activity in a GT29 enzyme from Arabidopsis26. In addition, our analyses point to the potential involvement of other, possibly lineage-specific, CAZyme families that may modify extensins and other glycoprotein scaffolds essential for scale architecture. These findings emphasize the molecular complexity and evolutionary innovation underlying scale formation in P. marina.

Evidence for a haplodiplontic life cycle

Our hypothesis of a heteromorphic haplodiplontic life cycle in P. marina is supported by multiple lines of evidence: genomic signatures of ploidy, high heterozygosity in CCMP1203, reports of a transient flagellated diploid stage6,46, and the presence of genes implicated in life-cycle transitions. The identification of an almost complete set of meiosis-specific genes and their higher expression in CCMP1203 suggest that meiotic processes may occur during the diploid phase. Conversely, the upregulation of Hap2/Gcs1 in the haploid UIO007 strain and the slightly higher expression of Kar5/Gex1 in the diploid CCMP1203 strain indicate potential roles in membrane and nuclear fusions during syngamy. Intriguingly, homologs of the TALE-class transcription factors Gsm1 and Gsp1, which regulate the haploid-to-diploid transition in Chlamydomonas36, were both present and upregulated in UIO007. This pattern resembles the co-expression of the TALE homeodomain life-cycle regulators OUROBOROS or SAMSARA in both gametes of the brown alga Ectocarpus prior to diploid sporophyte formation37. Co-expression of Gsm1 and Gsp1 in haploid cells suggests that activation of the heterodimeric transcription factor in diploid strains may involve mechanisms distinct from those described in Chlamydomonas. The absence of canonical mating-type genes, despite the presence of divergent RWP-RK transcription factors, further points to lineage-specific strategies for sex determination in P. marina.

Our observations parallel the well-characterized life cycle of the coccolithophore Gephyrocapsa huxleyi (formerly Emiliania huxleyi), a cosmopolitan bloom-forming marine alga that alternates between a calcified, non-motile diploid phase and a biflagellated, non-calcified haploid phase. These stages exhibit distinct ecological roles and gene expression profiles, with haploid cells showing resistance to viral infection and upregulation of motility-related genes47,48. Recent work also demonstrates differential sensitivity to solar radiation between phases, suggesting that ploidy-specific photophysiological traits enable vertical niche partitioning in the water column49.

Building on this perspective, we identified the chimeric photoreceptor gene DUC1 in CCMP1203. This gene encodes a dual-domain photoreceptor integrating phytochrome and cryptochrome signaling, enabling perception of both orange/far-red and blue light10. All protein domains reported in NIES-2893 are conserved in CCMP1203 (99.05% identity), whereas UIO007 lacks the phytochrome domain and retains only a portion of the cryptochrome domain (Supplementary Fig. 8). Expression patterns mirror this structural divergence: the full-length gene is highly expressed in CCMP1203 (log₂FC = 9.58), while the truncated version is minimally expressed in UIO007 (log₂FC = –10.70). This initially suggested a possible role in vertical niche partitioning, with diploid strains such as CCMP1203 potentially adapted to deeper or variable light environments. However, Makita et al.10 reported an intact DUC1 in another flagellated strain (NIES-1419), contradicting a systematic ecological divergence between haploid and diploid strains. Taken together, these findings suggest that truncation in UIO007 likely reflects a gene-loss event, possibly due to prolonged maintenance in culture collections since 1983. In this context, DUC1 function may have been compensated by one of the two cryptochrome genes shared with plants and other chlorophytes (RI054_26g108900 and RI054_26g109230). Comparative genomic surveys across a broader range of isolates will be essential to determine whether the absence of a functional DUC1 is widespread among natural P. marina populations.

While our genomic and transcriptomic data support a heteromorphic haplodiplontic life cycle in P. marina, the complete life cycle has not yet been completed under laboratory conditions. This leaves key questions unresolved: How do transitions between haploid and diploid phases occur? Can the haploid strain act directly as a gamete, or must it undergo further differentiation to become sexually competent? The presence of meiosis-specific genes and conserved transcriptional regulators suggests that the molecular machinery for sexual reproduction is intact, but its activation may depend on environmental cues or culture conditions not yet replicated in culture. Establishing conditions that trigger phase transitions, such as manipulating light regimes, or nutrient availability, will be critical to validate P. marina as a model for studying life cycle evolution in prasinophytes. Resolving these questions will not only clarify the biology of P. marina but also provide broader insights into the evolution of sexual reproduction and ploidy regulation in early-diverging green algae.

Conclusions

This study provides genomic and transcriptomic evidence for morphological plasticity and a heteromorphic haplodiplontic life cycle in unicellular green algae, highlighting the diversity of reproductive strategies among prasinophytes and challenging the prevailing view that such complexity is restricted to multicellular lineages. Our findings demonstrate that conserved genomic frameworks in P. marina can generate distinct morphotypes through differential gene expression. By leveraging the unique biology of this species, we advance understanding of prasinophyte scale formation and highlight the role of a specialized, evolutionarily dynamic set of CAZymes in constructing complex extracellular architectures. Considering that chromatin-level regulation, including histone modifications and chromatin accessibility50, likely underlie phase transitions and morphological specialization, future research should characterize the epigenomic landscape of P. marina to uncover the regulatory basis of its haplodiplontic life cycle. It will also be important to conduct genomic and transcriptomic analyses across diverse isolates to assess intraspecific genetic variability, thereby clarifying the evolutionary and ecological significance of this diversity and its role in shaping prasinophyte resilience in dynamic marine ecosystems.

Methods

Strains, culture conditions, and nucleic acid extraction

P. marina CCMP1203 (formerly Pycnococcus provasolii CCMP1203) was obtained from the National Center for Marine Algae and Microbiota, whereas P. marina UIO007 (formerly strain K-0017) was obtained from the Norwegian Culture Collection of Algae. CCMP1203 was cultured in L1 medium (https://norcca.scrol.net/node/3954) under 12 h light/12 h dark cycles, while UIO007 was grown in TL30 medium (https://norcca.scrol.net/node/3956) under the same conditions. For transcriptomic analysis, three biological replicates of each strain were grown in L1 medium supplemented with 20% TL30 under continuous light, and harvested during exponential growth. Scanning electron microscopy of P. marina CCMP1203 UIO007 was performed as described51.

For genomic DNA extraction, cells were lysed in 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 20 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 0.8 M NaCl, 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 1% cetyltrimethylammonium bromide, 0.5% polyvinylpyrrolidone 40,000, 10 mg/ml Proteinase K for 30 min at 65 °C, and the lysate was extracted once with an equal volume of phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) and twice with an equal volume of chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (24:1) before precipitation of DNA with two volumes of cold ethanol. For long-read sequencing, DNA fragments <25 kb were removed using the Short Read Eliminator kit (Pacific Biosciences). Total RNA was extracted using the Total RNA Kit II (Omega Bio-Tek) from cultures grown either under alternating light/dark conditions for genome annotation or under continuous light for transcriptomic analysis.

Sequencing, assembly, and annotation

Short-read DNA libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (150-bp paired-end reads) at Genome Quebec Innovation Center. Long-read libraries were generated using the Oxford Nanopore Ligation Sequencing Kit V10 and sequenced in-house on the MinION platform. RNA-seq libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on either the NovaSeq platform (for genome annotation) at Genome Quebec Innovation Center or the Element Biosciences Aviti platform (for transcriptomics) at the Plateforme d’Analyses Génomiques of Université Laval. Short reads were quality-checked with FastQC 0.12.1 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and quality-filtered using AfterQC 0.9.652 or Fastp 0.23.453, whereas long reads were filtered with Filtlong 0.2.1 (min_length = 4000, keep_percent = 90) (https://github.com/rrwick/Filtlong).

DNA assemblies were generated from long reads using CANU 2.254, and polished with five rounds of HAPO-G 1.255 using short-read data. Haplotigs in the CCMP1203 assembly were identified with Purge Haplotigs 1.1.3 (align_cov=70)56. Scaffolding and visualization were performed in Sequencher 5.4.6 (Gene Codes Corporation, Ann Arbor, MI USA).

Preliminary gene annotations were generated using Augustus 3.3.357 within the MAKER 2.31.1058 pipeline to assess assembly quality prior to the availability of RNA-seq data. Once RNA-seq data became available, reads were normalized with Trinity 2.12.059 and mapped to the genome using HiSAT 2.1.060, enabling genome annotation to be performed with BRAKER 2.1.461. Both sets of predictions were subsequently loaded into Web-Apollo v2.5.062 for consistency checks and manual curation of gene models. Ribosomal and transfer RNAs were annotated using RNAmmer 1.263 and tRNAscan-SE 2.0.464, respectively. Functional annotation of predicted proteins was performed using the A2GB pipeline (https://github.com/PombertLab/A2GB), which integrate InterProScan565 and DIAMOND searches66 against the SwissProt/TrEMBL databases67.

Sequencing and assembly statistics were compiled using MultiQC 1.2868. Long-read metrics were visualized using the custom scripts keep_longest_reads.pl 0.9b and read_len_plot.py 0.5 g, and assembly quality was assessed with QUAST 5.269. Completeness of the P. marina CCMP1203 and UIO007 genome annotations was assessed with BUSCO 5.8.270 using the chlorophyta_odb10 reference set in protein mode.

Comparative genomics

Orthogroup analyses were performed with OrthoFinder 2.3.1171 to identify shared gene families between P. marina strains and other prasinophytes. UpSet plots were generated using the UpSet 1.4.0 R package72. Chromosomal synteny was analyzed using the SYNY 1.2 pipeline73. Ploidy levels were inferred by mapping Illumina reads to genome assemblies and calling variants with VarScan2 2.4.674 via the SSRG pipeline (https://github.com/PombertLab/SSRG). Allele frequency distributions of polymorphic sites were visualized using fitdistplot in R75. Ploidy levels were also assessed by counting k-mers using Jellyfish v2.3.176, generating k-mer frequency histograms, and visualizing these distributions with GenomeScope v2.077. Repetitive elements were identified and masked using RepeatModeler 2.0.678 and RepeatMasker 4.1.579, while intact LTR retrotransposons were annotated with EDTA 2.2.280. Identification of telomeres and assessment of chromosome completeness were performed with the custom script check_for_telomeres.pl 0.4b. Integrated visualizations of nucleotide biases, ploidy, and repeats were created with Circos 0.69-981.

Transcriptomics

RNA-seq reads from CCMP1203 and UIO007 were mapped to the UIO007 reference genome using HiSAT 2.1.0 with default parameters. Gene expression quantification was performed using the custom script gene_expressed.pl 0.5, and differential gene expression analysis was then conducted with DESeq282. Genes exhibiting a log2FC ≥ 2 with an adjusted pvalue ≤ 0.05 were classified as significantly upregulated in UIO007, while those with log2FC ≤ -2 and adjusted pvalue ≤ 0.05 were considered upregulated in CCMP1203.

Transcriptional analysis of the outlier chromosomes was based on RNA-Seq read depth from three biological replicates of each P. marina strain. Read counts mapped to individual genes were normalized for gene length and expressed as RPKM (Reads Per Kb per Million mapped reads).

Functional genomics

KEGG orthology identifiers were assigned to predicted proteins using KEGG annotation tools, and functional pathways were explored with KEGG Mapper83. Flagella-associated proteins were also identified via BLASTp comparisons with Chlamydomonas reinhardtii and cross-referenced with the flagellar proteome database84. Putative CMP-Kdo transporters were identified via KEGG (K15272 group) and validated using the Transporter Classification Database85. CAZymes were classified using HMMER searches against the dbCAN3 HMM database86, and candidates potentially involved in scale formation were selected based on gene family expansion, overexpression in UIO007, and predicted Golgi localization, the latter inferred using DeepLoc 2.187.

Phylogenetics

Phylogenetic relationships among chlorophytes were reconstructed using single-copy orthologs. Protein sequences were aligned with MUSCLE 3.788, trimmed with TrimAl 1.389, and concatenated using Phyutility 2.2.690. Phylogenies were inferred with IQ-TREE 2.1.391 using the LG + C20 + F + G4 model and 100 non-parametric bootstrap replicates. GT29 and GH33 protein trees were built similarly, using WAG + F + I + G4 and LG + F + G4 models, respectively.

Statistics and reproducibility

For transcriptomic analyses, three independent biological replicates were prepared for each P. marina strain (CCMP1203 and UIO007). Each replicate represents a separate culture grown under identical conditions, ensuring that gene expression measurements reflect biological variation rather than repeated measurements from the same sample. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq282, which models count data using a negative binomial distribution and applies shrinkage estimation for dispersion and fold change. Statistical significance was determined using adjusted p-values (Benjamini-Hochberg correction), with thresholds set at adjusted p ≤ 0.05 and |log₂ fold change | ≥ 2. For allele frequency analysis, variant distributions were visualized using fitdistplot in R75, and descriptive statistics were used to infer ploidy levels. No additional statistical tests were applied to genome assembly or annotation metrics, which were assessed using standard bioinformatic tools. Genomic sequencing was conducted independently for both strains, and assembly and annotation pipelines were applied consistently. Custom scripts and bioinformatic tools were run using standardized parameters across datasets to ensure replicable results.
