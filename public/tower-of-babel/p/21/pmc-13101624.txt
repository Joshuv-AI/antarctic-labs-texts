Eco-friendly mosquito control: Assessing the larvicidal and repellent effects of Dorema aucheri extract on Anopheles stephensi, the malaria vector
Authors: Amin Hosseinpour, Mozaffar Vahedi, Azadeh Hamedi, Niloofar Soltanian, Farnaz Kazemi, Elham Rezaee, Aboozar Soltani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMosquito-borne diseases, particularly malaria, remain a significant global health challenge, with Anopheles stephensi being a primary vector in regions of the Middle East and South Asia. The increasing resistance to synthetic insecticides and their adverse environmental and health effects have intensified the search for effective botanical alternatives. This study aimed to evaluate the larvicidal and repellent effects of Dorema aucheri (Apiaceae), a medicinal plant native to Iran, against A. stephensi. The hydroalcoholic extracts of the Green and white aerial sections of D. aucheri separately tested across a range of concentrations following WHO Larvicidal bioassay guidelines, while repellency was assessed using the Klun & Debboun (K&D) method. Probit analysis was used to determine LC₅₀, LC₉₀, ED₅₀, and ED₉₀ values. The extracts demonstrated dose-dependent larvicidal activity, achieving up to 98% mortality at 400 mg/L, with the green section showing an LC₅₀ of 98.54 mg/L and the white section 104.72 mg/L. In terms of repellency, both plant fractions showed significant activity, with the green section achieving 90% repellency at 0.5 mg/cm2 and the white section 85% at the same dose. Complete protection times for 5% doses were 64 min for the green fraction and 52 min for the white. These findings suggest that D. aucheri has considerable potential as an environmentally friendly insecticidal and repellent agent, making it a promising candidate for use in integrated mosquito control programs. Further studies on its formulation and field validation are recommended to maximize its practical application.Keywords: Bioassay, Botanical insecticides, Hydroalcoholic extract, Integrated vector management, Malaria vector, Plant-based repellent

Introduction

Vector-borne diseases remain among the most significant public health challenges worldwide, disproportionately affecting tropical and subtropical regions. According to recent global epidemiological projections, vector-borne parasitic diseases continue to expand geographically due to climate change, urbanization, human mobility, and insecticide resistance, with malaria remaining a major contributor to morbidity and mortality (Wang et al., 2025). Among malaria vectors, Anopheles stephensi has gained increasing attention because of its expanding distribution beyond its traditional range in South Asia and the Middle East into parts of the Horn of Africa, posing a serious threat to malaria control programs (Abebe et al., 2025).

Malaria remains endemic in several southern provinces of Iran, where multiple Anopheles species contribute to disease transmission. A. stephensi is recognized as one of the primary urban malaria vectors in the region due to its ecological adaptability and capacity to breed in artificial water containers. The persistence of this vector complicates elimination efforts and underscores the need for innovative, sustainable control strategies.

For decades, malaria control programs have relied heavily on chemical insecticides, particularly pyrethroids, organophosphates, carbamates, and organochlorines. These compounds are widely used in indoor residual spraying (IRS) and insecticide-treated nets (ITNs). However, the extensive and prolonged application of synthetic insecticides has led to widespread resistance in mosquito populations. Recent surveillance data indicate increasing resistance mutations in several Anopheles species, significantly reducing the effectiveness of conventional control tools (Sengul Demirak and Canpolat, 2022). In addition to resistance development, synthetic insecticides raise concerns related to environmental persistence, toxicity to non-target organisms, bioaccumulation, and potential adverse human health effects.

In this context, botanical insecticides have re-emerged as promising alternatives or complementary tools within Integrated Vector Management (IVM) frameworks. Plant-derived compounds are often biodegradable, exhibit multiple modes of action, and contain complex mixtures of bioactive secondary metabolites that reduce the likelihood of resistance development (Isman, 2006; Nerio et al., 2010). Over the past decade, growing attention has been directed toward evaluating essential oils and plant extracts for larvicidal, adulticidal, and repellent properties against mosquito vectors.

Recent studies have demonstrated substantial mosquito control potential in plant species belonging to families such as Lamiaceae, Myrtaceae, Asteraceae, and Apiaceae. For example, essential oils from Syzygium aromaticum, Cinnamomum cassia, Melaleuca cajuputi, and Cymbopogon citratus have shown significant larvicidal activity against A. stephensi, with LC₅₀ values within operationally relevant ranges (Osanloo et al., 2018; Vivekanandhan et al., 2024a; Vivekanandhan, 2025a). Furthermore, several botanical extracts have demonstrated strong dose-dependent repellency comparable to synthetic standards under laboratory conditions (Moemenbellah-Fard et al., 2020; Asadollahi et al., 2019).

The Apiaceae family, in particular, is recognized for producing biologically active secondary metabolites such as coumarins, terpenoids, and phenolic compounds, many of which possess insecticidal or repellent properties (Sonigra and Meena, 2020). Within this family, the genus Dorema comprises medicinal plants traditionally used in Iranian ethnomedicine. D. aucheri, locally known as “Bilahar,” is native to mountainous regions of Iran and is rich in resinous and aromatic compounds. Despite its ethnobotanical significance, scientific data regarding its insecticidal or repellent activity against malaria vectors remain scarce.

To date, no comprehensive evaluation has simultaneously assessed both the larvicidal and adult repellent activities of hydroalcoholic extracts of D. aucheri against A. stephensi. Addressing this knowledge gap is particularly relevant in light of escalating insecticide resistance and the urgent need for environmentally sustainable vector control tools.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the larvicidal efficacy of hydroalcoholic extracts derived from the green and white aerial sections of D. aucheri against fourth-instar larvae of A. stephensi under laboratory conditions, following WHO guidelines. Additionally, the repellent activity and complete protection time (CPT) of these extracts were assessed using standardized Klun & Debboun (K&D) and arm-in-cage methodologies. By generating both toxicological and repellency data, this study seeks to determine whether D. aucheri may serve as a promising candidate for development into a plant-based bioinsecticide within integrated malaria vector management programs.

Material and methods

Collection and maintenance of plant

In spring 2020, aerial parts of D. aucheri (locally known as Bilahar) were collected from the slopes of Mount Dena (30°41′10.6″N, 51°41′19.9″E) in Kohgiluyeh and Boyer-Ahmad Province, Iran. Botanical identification was performed by a taxonomist at the Department of Pharmacognosy, Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, using morphological characteristics and regional floristic keys. A voucher specimen was authenticated and deposited in the herbarium of the Faculty of Pharmacy, Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (Voucher No.: DA-2020-01). Plant authentication followed standard taxonomic verification procedures described for medicinal plant studies (Organization, W.H., 2003). The green and white aerial sections were separated manually, chopped, and shade-dried at ambient temperature (25 ± 2 °C) for 7–10 days to prevent degradation of thermolabile constituents (Fig. 1). The dried materials were subsequently ground into fine powder using an electric grinder and stored in airtight containers until extraction.

Extraction of hydroalcoholic extract

The extraction procedure was performed using a maceration method commonly employed for phytochemical investigations (Harborne, 1998). Fifty grams of powdered material from each plant section (green and white) were separately soaked in 350 mL of 70% ethanol (analytical grade) at room temperature for 72 h with intermittent agitation to enhance solvent penetration. The solvent-to-plant ratio (7:1, v/w) was selected based on standard extraction practices for hydroalcoholic preparations.

After maceration, the mixtures were filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper to remove plant debris. The filtrates were concentrated under reduced pressure at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator to prevent thermal degradation of bioactive constituents. The semi-solid extracts were further dried under vacuum and stored at −20 °C until use. A schematic representation of the extraction and concentration process is provided in Supplementary Fig. S1.

For larvicidal bioassays, stock solutions were prepared by dissolving accurately weighed extract in dechlorinated water, followed by serial dilution to obtain final concentrations of 50, 100, 200, and 400 mg/L. Preparation of test solutions followed WHO recommendations for mosquito larvicidal testing (Organization, W.H., 2005).

Mosquito rearing

The A. stephensi mosquitoes were reared in the insectarium of the Department of Medical Entomology and Vector Control, SUMS, under controlled conditions: 25 °C ± 2 °C, 70% ± 10% relative humidity (RH), and a 12:12 light-to-dark photoperiod. A 10% sugar water solution was provided as a nutritional source for the adult mosquitoes, while the larvae were fed with Tetra fish food (Ghosh et al., 2025). The adult female mosquitoes were specifically nourished through an artificial blood-feeding technique, which involved mimicking the process of blood intake. This method ensures that the mosquitoes receive the necessary nutrients for reproduction. Both feeding strategies were carefully chosen to maintain optimal growth and development under controlled conditions. Females that were 7 to 10 days old and starved for 24 h were used for the repellency tests (Chenniappan and Kadarkari, 2012).

Larvicidal bioassay

Larvicidal activity was evaluated following the World Health Organization standard protocol for laboratory testing of mosquito larvicides (Organization, W.H., 2005). Bioassays were conducted using 250 mL glass beakers containing 249 mL of dechlorinated tap water. Twenty-five healthy fourth-instar larvae of A. stephensi were introduced into each beaker. One milliliter of the prepared test solution was added to achieve final concentrations of 50, 100, 200, and 400 mg/L. Each concentration was tested in four independent replicates (n = 100 larvae per concentration). Control groups received 1 mL of solvent without plant extract.

Larval mortality was recorded after 24 h of exposure. Larvae were considered dead if they showed no movement when gently probed with a fine glass rod. If control mortality ranged between 5 and 20%, data were corrected using Abbott's formula (Abbot, 1925) (Abbott, 1925). The experimental setup of the WHO larvicidal bioassay is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S2. Tests with control mortality exceeding 20% were discarded and repeated. Lethal concentration values (LC₅₀ and LC₉₀) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using Probit regression analysis as described by Finney (1971) (R.H.D, 1952).

Statistical analysis

Mortality and repellency data were subjected to Probit analysis to determine LC₅₀, LC₉₀, ED₅₀, and ED₉₀ values with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Probit analysis was performed using Probit-MSChart software (Chi, 1997) (Chi, 1997), based on the method described by Finney (1971) (R.H.D, 1952). The goodness-of-fit of the regression model was assessed using chi-square (χ2) values and heterogeneity factors. Dose–response relationships were considered significant when p < 0.05. All experiments were conducted in quadruplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation where appropriate.

Mosquito repellency bioassay

Repellent activity was evaluated using the Klun and Debboun (K&D) quantitative bioassay method, a standardized laboratory model for screening topical repellents (Klun and Debboun, 2000; Debboun et al., 2014). All experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions (25 ± 2 °C, 70 ± 10% RH). Female A. stephensi mosquitoes aged 7–10 days and starved for 12 h were used in repellency assays. Written informed consent was obtained from all human volunteers prior to participation. The study protocol was approved by the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee (IR.SUMS.REC.1398.522) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The repellent was applied to the skin of the arm, and the treated area was monitored for allergic responses over a 72-h period (Klun and Debboun, 2000). The testing apparatus was made of a plexiglass cube (4x5x18 cm) with four rectangular holes (3 × 4 cm) to house the mosquitoes (Klun and Debboun, 2000; Debboun et al., 2014). A schematic representation of the K&D exposure chamber and experimental configuration is provided in Supplementary Fig. S3.

For effective dose determination, four marked areas (3 × 4 cm) were delineated on the forearm. A volume of 50 μL of test solution was applied to each area and allowed to dry. The exposure chamber containing five mosquitoes per cell was placed over the treated skin, and mosquito landing and probing activity were recorded for five minutes (Debboun et al., 2014). ED₅₀ and ED₉₀ values were calculated using Probit analysis.

Complete protection time (CPT) was determined using the Arm-in-Cage (AIC) method described by Colucci and Müller (2018) (Colucci and Müller, 2018). A standardized dose (1 mL per 500 cm2) was applied uniformly on the forearm. CPT was defined as the time interval between application and the first confirmed mosquito landing or probing attempt.

Results

Larvicidal tests

Under controlled laboratory conditions, the hydroalcoholic extract of D. aucheri demonstrated larvicidal activity against A. stephensi. Mortality increased with dose, producing LC₅₀ and LC₉₀ values that classify the green and white tissues as moderately active and effectively toxic, respectively (Table 1). Larvicidal, the regression line, and dose–response results are presented in Table 2 and Figs. 2 and 3. Control mortality was zero, so Abbott's correction was unnecessary for Probit analyses. A clear concentration–response relationship was observed. Both fractions exceeded 95% mortality at 400 mg/L, with the green sections achieving this benchmark at slightly lower concentrations due to its lower LC₅₀.

Discussion

The present study provides strong evidence that the hydroalcoholic extract of D. aucheri possesses both larvicidal and repellent activities against A. stephensi, a major malaria vector in neighboring regions. The dose-dependent mortality and repellency observed in this study highlight the potential of D. aucheri as a plant-based alternative to synthetic insecticides, which aligns with global efforts to identify environmentally safe and sustainable vector control tools.

Larvicidal activity in comparison with previous studies

The larvicidal bioassays demonstrated that both green and white sections of D. aucheri effectively killed A. stephensi larvae, with mortality reaching 98% at 400 mg/L. The LC₅₀ values of 98.54 mg/L (green) and 104.72 mg/L (white) place the extracts within the moderate-to-effective toxicity range. This level of activity is comparable to several earlier botanical studies. For example, Barat Shooshtari and Ghalandari (2012) reported similar efficacy for extracts of Melissa officinalis, Lavandula angustifolia, and Rosmarinus officinalis, where higher concentrations resulted in substantial mortality of Anopheles larvae (Barat Shooshtari and Ghalandari, 2012). Similarly, Khanavi et al. (2013) showed that Iranian native plants such as Peganum harmala and Aloe littoralis displayed dose-dependent larvicidal activity against A. stephensi, though often requiring concentrations comparable to or higher than those used in the present study (Khanavi et al., 2013). In another study, Cymbopogon citratus essential oil exhibits potent insecticidal activity against Aedes albopictus, a vector of Zika and dengue. The oil achieved 100% larval mortality at 400 mg/L, with an LC₅₀ of 98.7 mg/L, demonstrating its efficacy as a larvicide. Additionally, it showed significant adulticidal activity, causing 65% mortality after 48 h. These results suggest C. citratus oil is a promising natural alternative to synthetic insecticides for mosquito control (Vivekanandhan, 2025b).

Osanloo et al. (2018) examined the essential oil of Syzygium aromaticum and its main component eugenol, demonstrating LC₅₀ values within a similar range to our findings (Osanloo et al., 2018). This reinforces the notion that D. aucheri belongs to the group of medicinal plants with strong insecticidal potential. Notably, the white fraction of D. aucheri showed slightly higher toxicity than the green section, a distinction that may be attributed to differences in secondary metabolite composition—a point that warrants future phytochemical studies.

Vivekanandhan (2025) demonstrated that Cinnamomum cassia (L.) bark essential oil has significant mosquitocidal activity, achieving 24-h mortality rates of 53.33% in larvae and 84% in adults. After 48 h, mortality increased to 96% and 97.33%, respectively. Pupal mortality ranged from 70.66% to 85.33%. Enzyme assays suggest CC-EO disrupts insect biochemical processes, as evidenced by decreased catalase and increased glutathione S-transferase levels (Vivekanandhan, 2025a).

Plant-based insecticides are gaining recognition as alternatives to synthetic chemicals. A study evaluated the insecticidal potential of Acanthospermum hispidum leaf crude extract against three major mosquito species. Extracts from Ac. hispidum leaves, obtained using hexane, petroleum benzene, chloroform, ethyl acetate, and acetone, were tested on the larvae, pupae, and adults of Anopheles stephensi, Aedes aegypti, and Culex quinquefasciatus, following WHO guidelines. The petroleum benzene extract showed the highest larvicidal and pupicidal activity. Low LC50 and LC90 values were observed for A. stephensi larvae (20.965 and 31.467 ppm), Ae. aegypti larvae (15.223 and 27.753 ppm), and Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae (29.095 and 42.737 ppm). Pupicidal activity LC50 and LC90 values were 41.234 and 51.123 ppm, 32.168 and 55.321 ppm, and 39.213 and 49.123 ppm, respectively. Smoke toxicity tests showed significant adult mosquito mortality: A. stephensi (69%), Ae. aegypti (52%), and Cx. quinquefasciatus (56%). These results indicate that A. hispidum leaf extract demonstrates promising insecticidal activity against these mosquito species, suggesting its potential as a natural mosquito control agent (Vivekanandhan et al., 2018).

Another study investigates the larvicidal efficacy of Melaleuca cajuputi essential oil against Anopheles stephensi, as well as its biochemical effects and non-target toxicity. The researchers extracted the essential oil from the leaves of Melaleuca cajuputi and analyzed its chemical composition using GC–MS. The oil showed strong dose-dependent toxicity against mosquito larvae, with 100% mortality in second instars at 200 ppm and LC₅₀ values ranging from 34.66 ppm for second instars to 84.23 ppm for fourth instars. Biochemical analysis revealed that the oil caused a significant reduction in detoxifying enzyme activities, including acetylcholinesterase and carboxylesterase, suggesting impairment in the mosquitoes' metabolic processes (Vivekanandhan et al., 2024a).

Vivekanandhan et al., 2024 evaluated the insecticidal potential of essential oils extracted from the seed cotyledons of Acacia nilotica. The oils demonstrated significant toxicity against insect pests, with Culex quinquefasciatus showing mortality rates of 60% and 98% at 24 and 48 h, respectively. For Plutella xylostella, the mortality was 60% and 96.66% at the same time points. The essential oils exhibited an LC₅₀ of 159.263 ppm/mL and LC₉₀ of 320.930 ppm/mL after 24 h, with values decreasing to 52.070 ppm/mL (LC₅₀) and 195.123 ppm/mL (LC₉₀) after 48 h for C. quinquefasciatus. For P. xylostella, the LC₅₀ was 165.900 ppm/mL and LC₉₀ was 343.840 ppm/mL at 24 h, decreasing to 62.965 ppm/mL and 236.795 ppm/mL, respectively, after 48 h (Vivekanandhan et al., 2024b).

Recent research further supports the importance of plant-derived compounds in larval control. For example, a 2019 study by Tennyson et al. found that methanolic extracts of Ageratum houstonianum exhibited strong larvicidal activity against A. stephensi, with LC₅₀ values comparable to those observed in D. aucheri (Tennyson et al., 2015). Likewise, a 2020 investigation into Ferula assa-foetida essential oil reported significant larvicidal effects, highlighting the continued relevance of Apiaceae species in vector control programs (Sonigra and Meena, 2020). The present study thus complements a growing body of evidence indicating that medicinal plants from the Apiaceae family possess considerable mosquito-control potential.

Repellent activity and comparison with existing repellents

The repellent effects of D. aucheri were similarly impressive, with the green section achieving 90% repellency at 0.5 mg/cm2. This performance is comparable to several plant-based repellents documented in previous research. Moemenbellah-Fard et al. (2020) reported potent repellency for the essential oils of Cinnamomum zeylanicum and Eucalyptus globulus against A. stephensi, with protection levels similar to those observed at higher doses in this study (Moemenbellah-Fard et al., 2020). Likewise, the systematic review by Asadollahi et al. (2019) emphasized the strong dose–response relationship of most botanical repellents—an observation clearly reflected in the current findings (Asadollahi et al., 2019).

Our study also contributes novel data to the relatively limited literature on complete protection time for plant extracts. The CPT values of 64 min (green) and 52 min (white) are consistent with earlier studies on clove oil and lemon eucalyptus oil, which often report protection durations ranging from 45 to 90 min depending on concentration (Nerio et al., 2010; El Gohary, 2021). Recent work by Adam et al. (2019) found that Ocimum basilicum essential oil 2% provided a CPT of roughly 125 min for against Anopheles species, placing D. aucheri in a similar range of efficacy, albeit with lower efficacy (Adam et al., 2019).

Furthermore, a 2022 study by Sengul Demirak & Canpolat highlighted that botanical repellents can reduce biting rates by up to 80–95%, depending on the formulation and concentration—again validating the high repellency levels observed in the present study (Sengul Demirak and Canpolat, 2022). Our results, therefore, support ongoing arguments that natural repellents remain competitive alternatives to widely used synthetic repellents like DEET, especially when safety, biodegradability, and environmental impacts are considered.

Relevance to insecticide resistance and integrated vector management

Growing insecticide resistance in Anopheles species has become a major obstacle in malaria vector control programs. Synthetic pyrethroids, organophosphates, and carbamates are increasingly less effective due to widespread resistance mutations. Botanical extracts, including those derived from D. aucheri, offer a promising complementary strategy because of their complex mixtures of bioactive compounds, which make resistance development less likely. Recent work underscores the importance of incorporating botanical bioinsecticides into integrated vector management (IVM) frameworks to mitigate resistance pressures (Sengul Demirak and Canpolat, 2022; Nerio et al., 2010; Moemenbellah-Fard et al., 2020).

The moderate-to-effective toxicity and strong repellency shown by D. aucheri suggest that it could be used as part of such IVM strategies, either as a stand-alone larvicide or in combination with microbial agents like Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (Bti). Formulation science—using encapsulation, emulsification, or controlled-release technologies—may further enhance the residual activity of D. aucheri extracts, as shown in recent studies involving nano-emulsified plant oils (Ferreira et al., 2019).

Conclusions

Overall, the findings of this study demonstrate that D. aucheri possesses meaningful larvicidal and repellent activities against A. stephensi. When compared with both classical literature and recent advances in botanical insecticide research, D. aucheri consistently aligns with the efficacy ranges of well-established plant-based mosquito control agents. Its strong dose–response trends, coupled with environmentally friendly characteristics, indicate significant potential for future development as a natural vector control tool. Further investigation into its phytochemical profile, formulation optimization, and field-based validation will be essential to realize its full applicability in public health programs.
