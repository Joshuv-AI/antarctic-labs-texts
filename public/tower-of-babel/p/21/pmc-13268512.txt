Development of a one-pot RPA–CRISPR/Cas12a assay for rapid multiplex detection of respiratory pathogens
Authors: Jiuqi Liao, Lili Teng, Chao Hui, Xin Liu, Jianhua Huang, Ying Li, Haiming Qin, Yang Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Respiratory tract infections impose a substantial clinical and public health burden, and timely etiological identification remains challenging. Rapid panel testing is therefore needed for common respiratory pathogens. In this study, we developed a closed-tube, one-pot recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA)–CRISPR/Cas12a assay using a multichamber 8-strip tube format to enable parallel fluorescence readout while reducing the risk of aerosol contamination. The assay targets 9 pathogens, including Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Staphylococcus aureus, influenza A virus, influenza B virus, respiratory syncytial virus, adenovirus, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae. Analytical performance, including limit of detection, specificity, and repeatability, was evaluated using viral recombinant plasmids and bacterial genomic DNA. Clinical evaluation was performed using available respiratory specimens and bacterial isolates, with reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction for viral targets and conventional bacterial identification for bacterial targets as reference methods. The assay provided results within approximately 50 to 60 minutes. The limits of detection for viral targets were 1 copy/μL for influenza A virus and adenovirus and 2 copies/μL for influenza B virus and respiratory syncytial virus. No cross-reactivity was observed under the tested conditions. Repeatability was satisfactory, with coefficients of variation below 15% across targets. In a clinical evaluation of 34 specimens or isolates, the one-pot assay showed 100% overall agreement with the reference methods. These findings indicate that the closed-tube, one-pot RPA–CRISPR/Cas12a platform enables rapid and parallel detection of common respiratory pathogens and may support point-of-care testing and deployment in resource-limited settings.

1. Introduction

Respiratory tract infections are among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and lower respiratory tract infections remain a major contributor to infectious disease deaths.[1,2] Mixed infections involving >1 bacterial or viral pathogen are frequently encountered, which can delay appropriate antimicrobial or antiviral therapy when etiologic diagnosis is incomplete or slow.[3,4] Conventional microbiological culture and automated identification provide high specificity but typically require hours to days, whereas quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)/reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) offers excellent analytical performance but depends on thermal cyclers and trained personnel, limiting deployment in decentralized or resource-limited settings.[5,6]

CRISPR-based diagnostics have emerged as a promising alternative because of sequence-specific recognition, isothermal compatibility, and straightforward signal readout. Cas12a exhibits target-activated collateral cleavage of single-stranded DNA reporters, enabling fluorescence detection without complex instrumentation. When combined with isothermal amplification such as recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), CRISPR/Cas systems can achieve high sensitivity within short reaction times.[7,8] However, multiplexing and contamination control remain practical challenges for 2-step workflows that require tube opening between amplification and detection.

Our findings present a closed-tube, one-pot RPA–CRISPR/Cas12a platform that has been designed for multiplex detection of 9 commonly found respiratory infections. An 8-strip tube with physical compartmentalization is utilized in the workflow to segregate RPA reagents from CRISPR reagents prior to a brief mixing step. This helps to reduce the danger of aerosol contamination while simultaneously allowing for parallel reactions to take place. Analytical performance (sensitivity, specificity, and repeatability) was tested, and clinical concordance was assessed through the comparison of respiratory swab specimens and bacterial isolates with reference methods.

Because of the severity of respiratory illnesses, emergency departments and inpatient services are under a great deal of strain, particularly during the peak of the influenza and respiratory syncytial virus epidemics. Within the context of this discussion, a prompt etiological diagnosis has the potential to impact decisions concerning patient cohorting, the beginning of antiviral therapy, and the prompt escalation or de-escalation of antibiotic treatment within the first hour of presentation. On the other hand, regular diagnostic approaches frequently rely on syndromic evaluation, delayed culture, or centralized PCR testing, all of which may not be able to keep up with the speed at which decisions are made about infection management and acute treatment.[9,10] Antigen-based tests have the potential to be helpful; nevertheless, they frequently lack sensitivity and do not cover a broad spectrum of bacterial and viral strains that can cause disease.

Multiplex nucleic acid testing helps fill these gaps by covering more species and discovering mixed infections that might not be identified otherwise, particularly in individuals with unclear symptoms or overlapping clinical presentations.[11-13] As a result, these gaps are filled. On the other hand, many commercial multiplex platforms require specialized tools, platform-exclusive cartridges, or very difficult workflows. This can make it challenging to employ these platforms in laboratories that have limited resources. There is still a need for assays that have a short turnaround time, are very sensitive and specific, work with only a few pieces of equipment, and have a closed-tube workflow that reduces the likelihood of amplicon contamination.[14-16] These assays are required in decentralized settings.

Accordingly, we developed and evaluated a one-pot RPA–CRISPR/Cas12a assay targeting 9 common respiratory pathogens, including major bacterial agents and prevalent respiratory viruses. We hypothesized that physical compartmentalization within a single reaction vessel could preserve the analytical sensitivity of amplification-assisted CRISPR detection while reducing contamination risk and simplifying hands-on steps. We further assessed cross-reactivity, limit of detection, repeatability, and concordance with reference methods using clinical isolates and respiratory swab specimens to explore the potential of this platform for near-patient testing. By enabling simultaneous detection of bacteria and viruses in a single run, such a tool could support earlier targeted therapy, improve antimicrobial stewardship, and strengthen surveillance during outbreaks when laboratory capacity is constrained.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Reagents, materials, and instruments

Primers and single-stranded DNA fluorescence-quencher reporter probes (ssDNA-FQ) were synthesized commercially. RT-qPCR reagents, in vitro transcription kits, and RNA extraction/purification kits were obtained from commercial suppliers. RPA/RT-RPA isothermal amplification and CRISPR fluorescence detection were performed using the Respiratory 9-Pathogen Detection Kit (CRISPR Fluorescence Method; MicroDiag Biomedicine/Jiangsu Weizhen Biopharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd., China; batch/lot no. 20241008; specification: 16 tests/box), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The kit was stored at −20°C and had a labeled shelf life of 12 months. Fluorescence signals were acquired on a real-time PCR instrument in end-point or kinetic mode, and a dry-bath incubator provided isothermal temperature control. Bacterial identification was performed using the BD Phoenix™ 100 system with appropriate identification/antimicrobial susceptibility panels.

Clinical bacterial isolates and respiratory swab samples were obtained from routine clinical testing repositories. Bacterial isolates included Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Staphylococcus aureus, and were identified by automated microbiological systems. Viral and atypical pathogen results (influenza A virus [IAV], influenza B virus [IBV], respiratory syncytial virus [RSV], adenovirus [ADV], and Mycoplasma pneumoniae) were determined by RT-qPCR as the reference.

Ethical approval and informed consent: Clinical sample collection and use were approved by the Ethics Committee of Huludao Central Hospital. Because this study used residual, de-identified specimens obtained during routine clinical testing, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the ethics committee in accordance with institutional requirements.

2.2. Target selection, primer, and crRNA design

For each pathogen, a conserved genomic region was selected based on sequence alignments and public database resources. Candidate targets were required to show high conservation across strains (typically > 95% within available sequences) and species specificity to avoid cross-reactivity with other respiratory pathogens or the human genome. Cas12a targeting additionally required the presence of a TTTV (V = A/C/G) protospacer adjacent motif adjacent to the protospacer.

RPA primers were designed to amplify the selected target regions. crRNAs were designed with 20 to 24-nt spacer sequences complementary to target amplicons and screened in silico using BLAST to minimize off-target binding. GC content was maintained within 40% to 60%, and homopolymer runs were avoided. The corresponding RPA primer and crRNA sequences are listed in Table 1. To improve reproducibility of target selection, we retrieved and aligned at least 20 non-duplicated complete or near-complete reference sequences for each pathogen from public databases (NCBI/GenBank and related viral sequence repositories). The retained regions showed high sequence conservation across the evaluated sequences, with estimated conservation of 98.9% for K pneumoniae, 99.1% for P aeruginosa, 98.6% for A baumannii, 99.3% for S aureus, 97.8% for influenza A virus, 98.2% for influenza B virus, 97.5% for respiratory syncytial virus, 96.7% for adenovirus, and 99.0% for M pneumoniae. Regions with obvious homology to other respiratory pathogens or to the human genome were excluded after BLAST screening.

2.3. Reference methods (bacterial identification and RT-qPCR)

Bacterial isolates were identified using the BD Phoenix™ automated identification system according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For viral and atypical pathogens, RT-qPCR was performed as the reference method using validated clinical assays. Cycle threshold (Ct) values were recorded for comparison and quality control.

2.4. One-pot RPA–CRISPR/Cas12a assay design and workflow

The one-pot assay integrates RPA amplification and CRISPR/Cas12a detection in a single closed tube using physical compartmentalization. RPA reagents (including primers and lyophilized reaction components) were preloaded in the lower chamber of each tube. CRISPR reagents (Cas12a protein, crRNA, and ssDNA-FQ reporter) were preloaded in an upper compartment within the tube cap/insert. A narrow microchannel (<0.5 mm) prevents spontaneous mixing; mixing occurs only after a brief centrifugation or vibration step.

Workflow: nucleic acids extracted from clinical samples were added to the lower chamber; RPA/RT-RPA was performed at 37°C for 15 to 30 minutes; tubes were briefly centrifuged to transfer amplified products into the CRISPR chamber; Cas12a collateral cleavage was initiated at 42°C; fluorescence was monitored using a real-time PCR instrument, and end-point fluorescence was used for result interpretation. For optional visual readout, reaction products were also applied to lateral flow strips (FAM/biotin reporter), and the test-line intensity was quantified for limits of detection (LOD) curve construction. The reaction vessel used in this study was a 0.2-mL 8-strip PCR tube modified with a cap-integrated inner insert to create separate upper and lower reagent compartments. The lower chamber had an approximate working volume of 20 to 25 μL for RPA/RT-RPA, whereas the upper insert contained approximately 5 to 10 μL of CRISPR/Cas12a detection mixture. The 2 compartments were separated before mixing by a narrow connecting channel (<0.5 mm), which allowed reagent transfer only after brief centrifugation. This compartmentalized tube was custom assembled for the present assay on the basis of the kit-associated 0.2-mL 8-strip reaction format supplied by MicroDiag Biomedicine/Jiangsu Weizhen Biopharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. The assembled reaction vessel was not used as a separately commercially catalogued diagnostic cartridge; therefore, no independent cartridge product identifier was applicable.

2.5. Analytical specificity and cross-reactivity testing

Analytical specificity was assessed using recombinant plasmids or extracted nucleic acids from target and nontarget organisms. For each target, reactions included a positive control (target template) and negative controls (nontarget templates and no-template controls). A reaction was considered positive when the fluorescence signal exceeded a predefined threshold and was at least 10-fold higher than that of the negative control.

2.6. Analytical sensitivity and limit of detection

To determine LOD for viral targets, recombinant plasmids were serially diluted (10-fold series) and tested in triplicate at each concentration. LOD was defined as the lowest concentration with 100% detection (3/3 positive).

2.7. Clinical evaluation and repeatability

Clinical performance was evaluated using bacterial isolates and respiratory swab specimens with results available from reference methods. Agreement was calculated as the proportion of concordant results between the one-pot assay and the reference method.

Repeatability was assessed by testing representative concentrations of viral targets in independent runs. Coefficients of variation (CV) were calculated as (standard deviation/mean) × 100%. A CV < 15% was considered acceptable.

2.8. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize analytical and clinical performance. Sensitivity, specificity, and overall agreement were calculated using standard definitions. All analyses were performed using standard spreadsheet tools and/or Python.

The one-pot RPA–CRISPR/Cas12a assay showed clear analytical specificity for the bacterial targets. When target DNA was present, the fluorescence signal increased progressively across reaction cycles, whereas nontarget bacterial templates and no-template controls remained close to baseline without an obvious rise. Cross-reactivity testing among bacterial targets further supported target-specific activation, with no detectable signal above the threshold for nontarget organisms under the optimized conditions. Therefore, analytical specificity was 100% in the tested cross-reactivity panel, and the qualitative results are reported both as positive/negative calls and as percentages in Table 2.

4. Discussion

4.1. Principal findings and technical innovation

During the course of this investigation, we developed a closed-tube, compartmentalized, one-pot RPA-CRISPR/Cas12a platform that is capable of detecting 9 common respiratory infections simultaneously in around 1 hour.[17,18] The fact that the chemicals for amplification and CRISPR are kept separate until they are required to be mixed for a fleeting period of time is an essential component of the design. This reduces the number of times that reaction vessels need to be opened, which in turn reduces the amount of aerosolized amplicon contamination. There is a significant obstacle to overcome in order to implement amplification-assisted CRISPR diagnostics in a routine setting. This workflow is significant because it maintains the rapid kinetics of RPA while simultaneously reducing the amount of work that requires direct human intervention. Because of this, it is simpler to utilize in decentralized labs that do not have specific workflows that are unidirectional or distinct spaces for post-amplification.

The compartmentalization technique is able to resolve a practical contradiction in CRISPR diagnostics. RPA is able to reap the benefits of rapid amplification at a constant temperature, whereas Cas12a collateral cleavage requires immediate exposure to amplified products. Postponing the interface between the amplification compartment and the CRISPR compartment until a regulated mixing phase is one of the ways that our design intends to retain speed while also assuring biosafety and workflow robustness.[19,20] It is also possible to apply this “reaction staging” concept for multiplexing since it enables amplification to take place under the same conditions each and every time, which is then followed by a standardized CRISPR activation phase. The variability that results from adding reagents one at a time by hand is alleviated as a result of this arrangement. In addition, closed-tube processing can reduce the number of errors that are made by the operator. These errors include not mixing the components sufficiently, not using the appropriate pipette, and splashing between wells, which is something that can occur when testing more than 1 target at the same time.

Integration and simplification of the workflow constitute yet another breakthrough. The assay combines nucleic acid amplification, target recognition, and signal generation into a single tube, which simplifies the requirements for the bench and reduces the amount of time it takes to get from the sample to the answer.[21,22] This structure is more suitable for outpatient clinics, emergency departments, and county-level laboratories, which may have limited space and staff training opportunities. In contrast to multi-step CRISPR procedures, this method does not necessitate the use of separate rooms for amplification and detection, nor does it necessitate the opening of the lid multiple times. When taken as a whole, these characteristics provide the impression that the one-pot design that has been described is not only suitable for analysis but also feasible for use on a broader scale.

4.2. Analytical performance and clinical relevance

It was established that the test possessed strong analytical specificity, as there was no detectable cross-reactivity among the bacterial targets that were investigated, and there was no signal in the controls that did not contain a template. The limit of detection for viral targets, which ranged from 1 to 2 copies per microliter, was within a highly sensitive range for amplification-assisted molecular diagnostics and should be interpreted as broadly comparable with recently reported integrated isothermal and CRISPR-based diagnostic platforms, rather than as evidence of definitive superiority across different assays. When it comes to clinical respiratory samples, where pathogen loads are frequently higher than this range during symptomatic infection, this level is generally sufficient. Multiplex detection, which includes both bacterial and viral pathogens, has the potential to improve early triage and therapeutic decision-making from a clinical perspective. It is possible to improve the timing of antiviral treatment and infection control measures by locating influenza viruses or RSV in a timely manner. The identification of bacterial infections that are associated with severe pneumonia may make it simpler to initiate targeted antimicrobial therapy at an earlier stage.[23-25] There is also the possibility that the multiplex format could be helpful in locating co-detections, which are becoming increasingly prevalent in respiratory infections and have the ability to influence the progression of the disease as well as the treatment that is administered for it. For literature context, recent integrated nucleic-acid diagnostic studies, including a dynamic confined-space one-pot isothermal amplification system and a highly multiplexed CRISPR-based assay for lower respiratory tract infection, provide relevant methodological benchmarks.[26,27]

Beyond the analytical sensitivity of the test, the practical benefit of a pulmonary multiplex assay is that it gives information that is helpful during the process of evaluating prospective treatment alternatives. This information is helpful in determining whether or not the test is appropriate. In many instances, the use of empirical antibiotics is necessary because it is challenging for doctors to discriminate between viral and bacterial causes of illness in a timely manner. This is one of the reasons why empirical antibiotics are utilized. It is possible to increase antimicrobial stewardship by obtaining a speedy multiplex result, which can make it easier to withhold or reduce the dosage of antibiotics upon the detection of viral infections and consistent clinical presentation. Simultaneously, a quick multiplex result can identify bacterial targets that may require early targeted therapy in high-risk patients.[28,29] This can be accomplished by screening for bacterial targets. Furthermore, the rapid detection of viruses that are significant to public health (e.g., influenza) might speed up the process of cohorting, isolating, and managing outbreaks, particularly during peak respiratory seasons when bed capacity is restricted. This is especially true during the time of year when bed capacity is limited.

When it comes to multiplex panels, where false positives could lead to patients receiving medicine that is not required, being isolated for extended periods, and experiencing anxiety, the high specificity of the assay is an especially important factor to consider. The closed-tube design may help preserve specificity by minimizing the amount of contamination transported inside the tube. Carryover contamination is a known cause of false-positive findings in amplification-based tests, and the closed-tube design may help maintain specificity. Additionally, multiplex detection makes it simpler to carry out a more complete examination of clinical syndromes. For instance, doctors can find co-detection patterns useful when evaluating secondary bacterial infections that emerge after viral illnesses or when interpreting persistent symptoms that occur when numerous pathogens are present at the same time. Both of these situations are examples of situations in which co-detection patterns might be of aid. It is possible that previous etiological clarity could hasten the beginning of appropriate medication, reduce the number of unnecessary investigations, and improve resource efficiency (for example, by reducing the amount of duplicate imaging or lengthy laboratory panels). This is despite the fact that clinical outcomes were not directly evaluated in this course of research. Within the context of this inquiry, the analytical performance that was demonstrated provides persuasive evidence that the assay is useful for the management of respiratory infections in conditions that are realistic of the real world.

4.3. Comparison with conventional tests and translational potential

Furthermore, culture is still an essential component of antimicrobial susceptibility testing, despite the fact that the proposed method significantly reduces the amount of time it takes to achieve a response in contrast to workflows that are dependent on culture. The one-pot RPA-CRISPR technique requires slightly less heat cycling than the RT-qPCR panels, and it can function with only the most fundamental level of temperature control.[30] This is in comparison to the RT-qPCR panels. Using a real-time PCR instrument to read fluorescence is a straightforward process that can be utilized for testing purposes. The chemistry, on the other hand, can be used with other kinds of readers as well, such as compact fluorescent readers. As a result of this, it might be more convenient to utilize in locations that are close to patients or at the point of care. When there are limited resources available, the compartmentalized format may also be of service in the process of stabilizing reagents, such as lyophilization, which is required for transit, storage, and usage. There is a potential that the compartmentalized format may also be of assistance in this procedure.

When it comes to normal clinical microbiology, 2 very different procedures are utilized: culture and RT-qPCR. Nevertheless, culture is a procedure that requires a significant amount of time and can provide information regarding live organisms and the susceptibility of individual species. In spite of the fact that RT-qPCR is a technology that is both speedy and sensitive, it frequently calls for the utilization of specialist thermocyclers, steady power, and adequately qualified workers. The approach that we have developed makes an effort to find a suitable middle ground by delivering sensitivity that is comparable to that of molecular testing, while also requiring less complicated temperature settings and fewer stages in the workflow. As opposed to independent isothermal methods, such as the LAMP method, the CRISPR readout adds an additional layer of sequence-specific identification to the process. Additionally, by doing so, multiplex designs have the potential to become more specific, hence lowering the probability that nonspecific amplification may appear. The one-pot system also simplifies logistics by lowering the number of consumables, the number of transfer stages, and the demand for separate pre- and post-amplification rooms. This is accomplished by reducing the number of steps involved in the transfer process. As a consequence of this, the staff might have a reduced amount of work to complete.

The versatility of the platform is rather remarkable, especially when translational considerations are taken into account. Primers-crRNA sets are capable of being updated anytime there is a change in the epidemiology of diseases (for example, when novel variations emerge or when the seasons change). This is the case in principle. It is possible to design a modular panel that can be modified without the need to rebuild the complete workflow as a result of this as it is possible to create such a panel.workflow. The incorporation of portable fluorescent equipment or readers that are powered by batteries could be of even greater aid when it comes to the deployment of these devices in outpatient respiratory clinics or community screening sites. Because the test is able to keep a constant temperature, it can be carried out with relatively simple heat blocks or incubators rather than with complex PCR machines. This is because the assay is able to maintain a consistent temperature. To summarize, the design of the closed-tube system is compliant with the requirements concerning quality management and biosafety policies. The risk of contamination events, which can lead to invalid runs, is decreased as a result of this, which in turn makes the system more reliable on a day-to-day basis. The fact that these things possess all of these traits demonstrates that they have the potential to be exploited in real life, outside of the heavily supported central laboratories.

4.4. Limitations and future directions

In order to proceed with the procedure, it is necessary to first identify a few limitations. Initially, the clinical validation was based on a restricted number of samples and a limited number of different kinds of specimens. This was due to the limited number of samples available. It is vital to conduct larger, multi-site studies to obtain a more accurate understanding of the test’s sensitivity and specificity across a variety of specimens, age groups, and sickness severity levels. This is because bigger studies are more likely to yield more accurate results. In the second place, Secondly, the current workflow is mostly made up of quality considerations. Despite the fact that it is conceivable that the kinetics of the CRISPR signal are connected to the concentration of the input, a correct evaluation would necessitate calibration in addition to careful control of matrix effects, amplification efficiency, and lot-to-lot variability. Thirdly, the quality of the sample preparation continues to affect how effectively it operates in the real world. Although it is feasible that simpler extraction or direct-to-amplification procedures will minimize turnaround time, it is important to note that these protocols require a comprehensive evaluation to determine whether or not they are biosafe and do not have inhibitor tolerance. The integration of internal controls, such as an external process control and a specimen-adequacy marker, would result in an increase in the validity of the findings. Last but not least, multiplexing causes design trade-offs, such as primer interactions and competition, which may cause the approach to be less sensitive to low-abundance targets. It will be important to continue improving primer balance and to vigilantly monitor any changes that may occur in the genomes of pathogens in order to preserve inclusiveness. For the objective of advancing toward legitimate bedside or community testing, future research should concentrate on enhancing clinical validation, optimizing sample preparation, and researching alternative portable readout technologies, respectively. This will allow for the advancement of the process.technologies.

In addition to these limitations, there are other restrictions that pertain to reference standards and the method by which clinical data ought to be interpreted, among other things. When RT-qPCR is used as the comparison method, it is possible for the results to be inconsistent due to variances in target regions, extraction efficiencies, or borderline viral loads that are close to the detection threshold. These factors can all contribute to the inconsistency of the results. For this reason, it is possible that the conclusions taken from future study could be strengthened by doing a discrepant analysis (such as retesting or sequencing confirmation). In spite of the fact that multiplex detection is favorable, it is important to keep in mind that a positive result does not necessarily indicate causality in symptomatic disease. This is an extra issue to take into consideration. This is especially true for viruses that can live on or be passed on without causing any symptoms to manifest themselves. It is proposed that future clinical studies use more comprehensive clinical metadata in order to improve the contextualization of results and evaluate the impact of quick multiplex testing on management decisions and outcomes. This objective can be accomplished by using more comprehensive clinical metadata. It is important that this metadata include the length of time that symptoms have been present, severity levels, imaging findings, inflammatory markers, and antibiotic exposure.

The stability of the reagents, the manner in which they are stored, and the degree to which they perform in a consistent manner from batch to batch are all essential factors of the implementation process. For the purpose of making formulations that are lyophilized or stable at room temperature more effective in the real world, it would be helpful to assess how well they can tolerate variations in temperature that occur during the shipment process. In addition to this, inquiries concerning quality control and regulations need to be looked at. When it comes to the deployment of clinical procedures, it is absolutely necessary to have uniform internal controls, clear interpretation thresholds, and established wrong criteria. It is possible that the genetic diversity of pathogens will change over time, which may affect the precision with which primers and crRNAs bind to one another. This is not the least of the potential consequences. When it comes to ensuring that the panel is always up to current, it is strongly suggested that it be verified with circulating strains and in silico on a regular basis. Therefore, in order to make these items a standard component of clinical practice, it will be important to make considerable steps toward addressing these difficulties. This can be accomplished by doing larger prospective studies and validation that focuses on implementation.

5. Conclusion

In a nutshell, the suggested one-pot RPA–CRISPR/Cas12a test makes it possible to detect various respiratory infections in a closed-tube format in a manner that is both accurate and specific. It seems as though the platform could be effective in settings where resources are limited or when patients need to be diagnosed as rapidly as possible in order to improve their care and control the spread of illnesses.
