Biventricular Interatrial Shunt With Desaturation After Atrial Septal Defect Repair
Authors: Oleksandr Danylenko, Sonya Babu-Narayan, Margarita Brida, Ee Ling Heng, Wei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients after atrial septal defect (ASD) repair with residual shunt, elevated pulmonary arterial pressure or arrhythmias, and those repaired at adult age should be followed on a regular basis.Case SummaryThis case describes a 43-year-old female with previous ASD repair, who presented with breathlessness, tiredness, and evidence of desaturation on exercise with a mildly positive bubble echo. While local investigations were inconclusive, diagnostic tests performed at our center revealed an inferior sinus venosus ASD-type defect with bidirectional flow, even though neither right-heart dilatation, pulmonary hypertension nor hemodynamically significant shunt was identified. The patient was offered surgical repair of the residual interatrial communication to give the patient symptomatic benefit.DiscussionThis case demonstrates a rare presentation of a post-ASD repair patient with bidirectional shunt without right-heart overload and no pulmonary hypertension.Take-Home MessagePatients with repaired congenital defects should be followed up by specialized congenital services at reasonable interval based on the lesion and the results of the repair.Key words: atrial septal defect, congenital heart defect, echocardiography, imaging, 3-dimensional imaging

History of Presentation: How the Patient Presented, Physical Examination

A 43-year-old female was referred to our tertiary center with symptoms of breathlessness and tiredness and evidence of desaturation on exercise with a mildly positive bubble echo. The patient underwent atrial septal defect (ASD) repair at early childhood (no records provided) with no important residual defects demonstrated on local cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) or transthoracic echocardiography.

Past Medical History

Before referral to our specialist adult congenital heart disease (CHD) service, the patient underwent initial workup (Video 1), which included an electrocardiogram, blood tests including B-type natriuretic peptide, a cardiopulmonary exercise test, a high-resolution computed tomography, and a computed tomography pulmonary angiography, and was even tested for mutations in the JAK2, CALR, and MPL genes to rule out the possibility of a myeloproliferative neoplasm. Her electrocardiogram was unremarkable, but the full blood count showed increased hemoglobin level of 165 g/L, and the patient desaturated during the cardiopulmonary exercise test (SpO2 decreased from 88% at rest to 67% at maximal exercise). Furthermore, no evidence of chronic thrombotic pulmonary disease, pulmonary emphysema, or any other lung abnormalities were found on computed tomography imaging, and the possibility of a myeloproliferative neoplasm was ruled out as no mutations in the JAK2, CALR, and MPL genes were identified.

Differential diagnosis included incomplete ASD closure/residual shunt, pulmonary hypertension, including chronic thrombo-embolic pulmonary hypertension, and lung disease, and the latter 2 issues were excluded by the initial workup.

Investigations

At our center, we repeated CMR and performed transesophageal echocardiography and right-heart catheterization. CMR showed that biventricular volumes and ejection fraction of both ventricles were normal in addition to normal atria size and normal post-repair pulmonary venous return. Furthermore, it was found that there was very inferior post-repair residual atrial defects with a morphology of sinus venosus ASD, communicating with the inferior vena cava (IVC) and associated with a hemodynamically nonsignificant shunt (Qp:Qs 1.0) with possibly bidirectional flow (Video 2). A turbulent flow was noted at the junction of the IVC with the right atrium, and further turbulent flow near the ostium secundum (Video 3). Transesophageal echocardiography revealed a residual inferior sinus venosus ASD-type defect with bidirectional flow in addition to a small adjoining secundum ASD. In addition, the prominent Eustachian valve was noticed and pulled towards the interatrial septum distally to the defect causing some obstruction to flow (mean gradient 2 mm Hg) and therefore facilitating right-to-left shunt across the residual inferior ASD (Figure 1, Video 4). A calculated Qp:Qs was 1.0, suggestive of hemodynamically nonsignificant shunt. Right-heart catheterization showed no evidence of precapillary or postcapillary pulmonary hypertension, and the shunt was deemed to be not hemodynamically significant (Qp:Qs 1.4).

Management (Medical/Interventional)

Based on medical history, clinical status, and results of investigations using multimodality imaging, the decision was made to offer a surgical repair of the residual interatrial communication to abolish the right-to-left shunt to give the patient symptomatic benefit.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient has been placed on the waiting list to undergo a surgical repair of the residual interatrial communication.

Discussion

ASD types include secundum ASDs, primum ASDs, superior and inferior sinus venosus defects, and unroofed coronary sinus.1 While secundum ASDs and superior sinus venosus ASDs could be treated either surgically or via the transcatheter approach, other ASD types could be only treated surgically.1,2 Our patient had surgical ASD repair at early childhood, but no medical records were presented to us regarding the type of ASD she was diagnosed with in the past and surgical repair technique she had.

Surgical repair of ASDs is well-established and is believed to be durable treatment.3,4 It is expected that patch closure precludes any residual shunt. Nevertheless, the frequency of residual defects after surgical closure is between 2% and 7.9% in the long-term follow-up3 and could be related to deficient rims, inadequacies in the surgical technique, or patch degeneration. Therefore, it is advisable to follow up patients after ASD repair, paying attention to a residual shunt, right ventricle size and function, tricuspid regurgitation, and pulmonary artery pressure by echocardiography. Only patients repaired at age <25 years without relevant sequelae or residua (no residual shunt, normal pulmonary artery pressure, normal right ventricle, no arrhythmias) do not require regular follow-up.1 Unfortunately, our patient was not followed up by adult CHD services and sought medical help from other healthcare professionals, even though no clear causes for her symptoms and desaturation were identified despite multiple investigations being performed.

The anatomy of residual defects sometimes is not straightforward, so broad utilization of different imaging modalities is essential before planning the closure approach. An important thing to mention is that expertise in adult CHD is paramount to interpret the result of investigations of patients with repaired CHD. Although it is more likely to see left-to-right shunt than right-to-left or bidirectional ones in patients after ASD repair/device closure, such cases have been described in the literature. One case demonstrated anterior malposition of the septal occluder, with the right atrial rim of the device diverting the blood from the IVC to the left atrium, causing systemic desaturation and exertional dyspnea 5 years after device closure.5 Sapin and Salley6 published a case about a patient with previous surgical repair of the secundum ASD, who developed exertional dyspnea postoperatively. It was found upon investigation that the Eustachian valve had been mistaken for the inferior rim of the defect and sutured to the upper rim of the defect. This created a channel through which blood from the IVC could be partially deferred to the left atrium.

Conclusions

This case highlights the importance of clinical vigilance while dealing with patients with repaired CHD and underscores the usefulness of cardiac multimodality imaging in patients living with congenital heart abnormalities. In addition, patients with repaired CHD should be followed up by a specialized team with expertise in CHD at reasonable interval based on the lesion and the results of the repair.
