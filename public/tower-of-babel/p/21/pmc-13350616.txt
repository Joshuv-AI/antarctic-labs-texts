Distinguishing lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy in fever of unknown origin using a diagnostic model based on PET/CT and clinical-laboratory parameters
Authors: Xinchao Zhang, Yujing Hu, Yalong Di, Congna Tian, Qiang Wei, Ruming Zhou, Jingjie Zhang, Fenglian Jing, Yanzhu Bian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFever of unknown origin (FUO) remains a complex diagnostic challenge, with infection, malignancy, and inflammatory diseases as the leading causes. A substantial proportion of FUO patients present with lymphadenopathy, among which lymphoma and benign lymph node disorders are the most critical differential diagnoses due to their markedly different management and prognosis. Although 18F-FDG PET/CT plays an indispensable role in lymphoma detection, significant metabolic overlap between malignant and benign lymphadenopathy limits its standalone diagnostic accuracy in FUO. Therefore, integrating PET/CT metabolic parameters with clinical features and laboratory markers may improve the differentiation between lymphoma and benign lymphadenopathy, facilitating timely and accurate diagnosis in FUO patients.ResultsThis study retrospectively analyzed patients with FUO who underwent PET/CT. Lymph nodes were included if their short-axis diameter was ≥ 1 cm on axial CT or if metabolic activity exceeded that of the mediastinal blood pool. Volumes of interest were manually delineated in LIFEx using a 40% SUVmax threshold. Among 203 patients (114 with lymphoma and 89 with benign lymphadenopathy), logistic regression analyses identified independent predictors across three domains: hyperpyrexia, joint pain, and rash among clinical factors; albumin (ALB), procalcitonin (PCT), and serum amyloid A (SAA) among laboratory indicators; and SUVmean, total metabolic tumor volume (TMTV), and total lesion glycolysis (TLG) among PET parameters. A combined model incorporating rash, PCT, SAA, SUVmean, and TMTV achieved superior diagnostic performance, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.965, significantly outperforming models based solely on clinical (AUC = 0.790), laboratory (AUC = 0.866), or PET (AUC = 0.845) variables (all P < 0.001). A nomogram was subsequently developed for individualized risk prediction.ConclusionsRash, PCT, SAA, SUVmean, and TMTV were independent predictors distinguishing lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy in FUO. Integrating clinical, laboratory, and PET parameters markedly improved diagnostic accuracy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13550-026-01450-8.

Background

In 1991, Durack and Street established the diagnostic criteria for fever of unknown origin (FUO), defining it as “a persistent fever of 38.3°C or higher, of unknown origin, lasting for more than three weeks despite 3 days of outpatient or inpatient evaluation” [1]. The causes of FUO are classified into five categories: infection, malignancy, inflammation, other causes, and undiagnosed causes [2, 3]. A significant proportion of FUO patients present with lymphadenopathy, with lymphoma, infections, and rheumatologic disorders being major underlying causes [4, 5]. Given the marked differences in clinical management and prognosis between lymphoma and benign lymph node diseases, the rapid and accurate differentiation between these two conditions is crucial for patient outcomes [6]. Lymphoma patients often present with a range of systemic symptoms, including fever, weight loss, and night sweats, collectively known as “B symptoms.” However, these symptoms are not exclusive to lymphoma and can also be seen in a variety of other diseases [7, 8], complicating the diagnostic process. Moreover, lymphoma patients frequently present with lymphadenopathy or involvement of peripheral organs, which may resemble benign conditions such as reactive lymphadenopathy or infectious lymphadenitis, further complicating the differential diagnosis [9–11].

In recent years, PET/CT has become an essential tool in lymphoma diagnosis, staging, and treatment evaluation [12–14]. The radiopharmaceutical used in PET/CT, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG), accumulates in metabolically active cells. Lymphoma cells typically exhibit high metabolic activity, making PET/CT highly sensitive for the early diagnosis and staging of lymphoma [15]. Despite its indispensable role in lymphoma diagnosis, the interpretation of PET/CT imaging results remains fraught with uncertainty. This is due to the overlap in metabolic activity between malignant and benign lymph node diseases. For example, conditions such as necrotizing lymphadenitis and adult-onset Still’s disease (AOSD) may show high metabolic activity, while certain indolent lymphomas, such as marginal zone lymphoma, small lymphocytic lymphoma, or low-grade follicular lymphoma, may exhibit low metabolic activity. This overlap poses a significant challenge in diagnosing FUO [5, 16–19].

The diagnostic workup of FUO is particularly challenging, requiring a comprehensive analysis of imaging and clinical data. PET/CT findings, clinical factors (e.g., patient age, fever pattern, weight loss, rash, joint pain), and laboratory results all provide crucial clues for diagnosis [20, 21]. This study aims to distinguish lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy in fever of unknown origin by evaluating clinical features, laboratory markers, and PET/CT metabolic parameters. By integrating these predictors, we seek to improve diagnostic accuracy and support timely, appropriate clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study cohort

This study retrospectively analyzed patients with FUO who underwent PET/CT examination at our hospital between January 2018 and December 2023. The diagnostic criteria for FUO were consistent with those described in the Introduction. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Approval No. 2024-LW-081). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of FUO and having undergone 18F-FDG PET/CT examination; (2) complete clinical and laboratory data available; (3) PET/CT findings indicating lymphadenopathy, defined as lymph nodes with metabolic activity higher than that of the mediastinal blood pool or a short-axis diameter ≥ 1 cm on axial CT images; (4) definitive diagnosis obtained for all cases, with benign lesions confirmed by clinical follow-up or histopathology, and malignant lesions confirmed pathologically. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) immunodeficiency or nosocomial FUO; (2) presence of non-lymphomatous solid tumors on PET/CT or a prior history of malignancy.

Clinical parameters collected included: gender, age, presence of hyperpyrexia, joint pain, rash, weight loss, hepatomegaly, and splenomegaly. Laboratory markers included: blood analysis (BA), blood cultures (BC), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), procalcitonin (PCT), ferritin (FER), serum amyloid A (SAA), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), antinuclear antibodies (ANA), rheumatoid factor (RF), immunofixation electrophoresis (IFE), and interferon gamma release assay (IGRA).

18F-FDG PET/CT examination

All PET/CT scans were performed using a Discovery Elite PET/CT system (GE Healthcare, USA). The radiochemical purity of 18F-FDG was ≥ 90%. All patients fasted for at least 6 h before scanning, and their fasting blood glucose level was ≤ 11.1 mmol/L. Each patient received an intravenous injection of 18F-FDG at a dose of 3.70–5.55 MBq/kg. Prior to scanning, 600 mL of an oral diatrizoate meglumine contrast solution was administered to fill the gastrointestinal tract. PET/CT imaging was performed 60 min after tracer injection, covering the area from the skull base to the mid-thigh. The acquisition included 6–7 bed positions, with 2.5 min per bed. PET data were acquired in three-dimensional time-of-flight (3D-TOF) mode, followed by low-dose CT scanning (120 kV, 100 mA, slice thickness 3.3 mm) for attenuation correction and anatomical localization. Fused PET/CT images were subsequently reconstructed for analysis.

Image segmentation and metabolic parameter extraction

Image segmentation and quantitative parameter extraction were performed using LIFEx software (version 7.3.0; https://www.lifexsoft.org/). Lymph nodes were selected if the short-axis diameter was ≥ 1 cm on axial CT images or if the metabolic activity exceeded that of the mediastinal blood pool. Two experienced nuclear medicine physicians (each with more than 5 years of diagnostic experience) manually delineated the volumes of interest (VOIs), applying a 40% maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) threshold. Conventional PET parameters were recorded, including, SUVmax, mean standardized uptake value (SUVmean), total metabolic tumor volume (TMTV), and total lesion glycolysis (TLG).

Model construction

Univariate logistic regression analyses were first performed for clinical variables, laboratory indicators, and PET metabolic parameters to identify factors significantly associated with the diagnosis (P < 0.05). Significant variables were then included in multivariate logistic regression to identify independent predictors. Three separate models were established based on the identified predictors: the clinical model (CSCORE), the laboratory model (LSCORE), and the PET model (PSCORE). Subsequently, independent predictors from these three models were integrated into a combined multivariate logistic regression analysis to calculate a comprehensive model score (ZSCORE), forming the combined model. Finally, a nomogram incorporating clinical, laboratory, and PET metabolic variables was developed to visualize the individualized probability of lymphoma.

Model evaluation and validation

The diagnostic performance of each model in differentiating lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the area under the ROC curve (AUC). Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling (B = 1000) to obtain bias-corrected AUC values. Differences in AUCs among models were compared using the DeLong nonparametric test (MedCal19.6.4).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM) and R statistical software (version 4.2.2) (http://www.R-project.org). Quantitative variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent-samples t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as M (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

By the end of follow-up, a total of 325 patients with FUO and lymphadenopathy were identified. Among them, 25 patients were found to have solid organ tumors, 7 patients had a previous history of malignancy, 89 patients remained undiagnosed at the end of follow-up, and 1 patient had incomplete clinical or laboratory data. Consequently, 203 patients met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the final analysis, including 114 patients with lymphoma and 89 with benign lymphadenopathy. Model construction

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses for clinical, laboratory, and PET metabolic variables are presented in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1.

Fig. 1Forest plots of clinical, laboratory, and PET predictors for lymphoma

Forest plots of clinical, laboratory, and PET predictors for lymphoma

For clinical parameters, hyperpyrexia (OR = 0.185, P = 0.002), joint pain (OR = 0.079, P = 0.001), and rash (OR = 0.074, P = 0.001) were identified as independent predictors for differentiating lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy.

The clinical model score (CSCORE) was calculated as follows:

CSCORE = 0.911 − 1.685 × (hyperpyrexia, temperature > 39 °C = 1) − 2.537 × (joint pain = 1) − 2.610 × (rash = 1).

For laboratory indicators, ALB (OR = 3.213, P = 0.036), PCT (OR = 0.105, P < 0.001), and SAA (OR = 0.120, P < 0.001) were independent predictors.

The laboratory model score (LSCORE) was defined as:

LSCORE = 2.512 + 1.167 × (ALB > 55 g/L = 1) − 2.254 × (PCT ≥ 0.06 ng/mL = 1) − 2.117 × (SAA ≥ 10 mg/L = 1).

For PET metabolic parameters, SUVmean (OR = 2.378, P = 0.042), TMTV (OR = 1.046, P < 0.001), and TLG (OR = 0.998, P = 0.003) were independent predictors.

The PET model score (PSCORE) was expressed as:

PSCORE = − 2.568 + 0.866 × SUVmean + 0.045 × TMTV − 0.002 × TLG.

When independent predictors from the clinical, laboratory, and PET models were combined in a multivariate logistic regression, rash (OR = 0.054, P = 0.006), PCT (OR = 0.098, P < 0.001), SAA (OR = 0.116, P = 0.001), SUVmean (OR = 1.410, P = 0.005), and TMTV (OR = 1.047, P = 0.007) remained significant.

The combined model score (ZSCORE) was calculated as:

ZSCORE = 0.366 − 2.914 × rash − 2.327 × PCT − 2.151 × SAA + 0.344 × SUVmean + 0.046 × TMTV (Table 5).

Model evaluation and validation

The AUC values for the clinical, laboratory, PET, and combined models in distinguishing lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy were 0.790 (95% CI: 0.727–0.844), 0.866 (95% CI: 0.811–0.910), 0.845 (95% CI: 0.788–0.892), and 0.965 (95% CI: 0.930–0.986), respectively. After bootstrap resampling, the bias-corrected AUCs were 0.790 (95% CI: 0.738–0.846), 0.866 (95% CI: 0.818–0.918), 0.845 (95% CI: 0.793–0.905), and 0.965 (95% CI: 0.946–0.991). The DeLong test demonstrated that the AUC of the combined model was significantly higher than those of the clinical, laboratory, and PET models (all P < 0.001; Fig. 2). Both the laboratory and PET models showed higher AUCs than the clinical model, but only the difference between the laboratory and clinical models reached statistical significance (P = 0.035). No significant differences were observed between the PET and clinical models (P = 0.155 and 0.596) or between the laboratory and PET models (P = 0.596).

Discussion

The etiology of FUO is broad and complex, encompassing infectious, neoplastic, and noninfectious inflammatory causes. Its complexity and diagnostic challenge demand that clinicians possess comprehensive knowledge and multidisciplinary diagnostic capabilities. Traditional diagnostic approaches include thorough medical history, physical examination, laboratory testing, and imaging studies. However, in difficult cases, relying on a single diagnostic method is often insufficient to determine the etiology. Therefore, the integration of multimodal diagnostic techniques and multidisciplinary collaboration has become a prevailing trend in the diagnosis of FUO. A considerable proportion of FUO patients present with lymphadenopathy. In this study, malignant etiologies were predominantly lymphomas, accounting for 56.2% (114/203) of all lymph node lesions. Benign etiologies included infectious and noninfectious inflammatory diseases, among which bacterial infections were most common, followed by viral and fungal infections. Noninfectious inflammatory diseases primarily consisted of AOSD, necrotizing lymphadenitis, and reactive lymphadenopathy, findings that are consistent with previous reports [5, 16, 22].

These three diseases were most frequently confused with lymphoma due to overlapping clinical and imaging features. AOSD typically manifests as fever, rash, joint pain, and generalized lymphadenopathy [23]. On PET/CT, enlarged lymph nodes with increased metabolic activity often mimic lymphoma. Laboratory inflammatory markers such as WBC, ferritin (FER), and CRP are markedly elevated in ASD but can also be elevated in lymphoma, limiting their discriminatory value. Necrotizing lymphadenitis, characterized by aseptic necrosis of lymph nodes and fever with cervical lymphadenopathy, often exhibits ill-defined lymph node masses with high metabolic uptake on PET/CT [24], further complicating differential diagnosis.

Based on these challenges, we developed a diagnostic model integrating PET/CT metabolic parameters with clinical and laboratory indicators to differentiate lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified hyperpyrexia, joint pain, and rash as independent clinical predictors; ALB, PCT, and SAA as independent laboratory predictors; and SUVmean, TMTV, and TLG as independent PET predictors. Using these nine variables, we established clinical, laboratory, PET, and combined models. The combined model achieved the highest AUC, demonstrating that integrating PET/CT metabolic parameters with clinical and laboratory data significantly enhances diagnostic performance.

Similar findings have been reported internationally. Chen et al. analyzed 524 FUO patients undergoing PET/CT and identified several clinical and laboratory parameters that improved diagnostic efficacy, including inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP, FER, LDH), immunologic indicators (IGRA, ANA), and clinical signs (weight loss, rash, splenomegaly) [22]. In our study, hyperpyrexia, joint pain, rash, ALB, PCT, and SAA were independent predictors. Rash was identified as a specific feature in both studies, while joint pain and PCT differed—likely due to variations in disease spectrum, patient heterogeneity, and analytical methods. Chen et al. stratified FUO patients by etiology (infection, malignancy, and noninfectious inflammation), whereas our study focused specifically on differentiating lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy, leading to distinct feature selection. Notably, hyperpyrexia, ALB, and SAA, which were not included in Chen’s analysis, were significant discriminators in our cohort, reflecting the distinctive pathophysiology of FUO with lymphadenopathy.

Regarding PET/CT parameters, SUVmean, TMTV, and TLG were independent predictors, whereas SUVmax was not. The likely explanation is that certain benign conditions, such as necrotizing lymphadenitis or ASD, can also show high SUVmax values, resulting in metabolic overlap with lymphoma and potential misdiagnosis. In contrast, SUVmean, TMTV, and TLG better capture the overall metabolic burden and heterogeneity of lymphoma, making them more reliable for differential diagnosis.

In Chen et al.’s study, the AUC values of prediction models for infection, malignancy, and noninfectious inflammation were 0.88, 0.93, and 0.95, respectively, confirming the diagnostic value of 18F-FDG PET/CT in FUO, which can be further improved by incorporating clinical features [22]. The strength of Chen’s study lies in its large cohort, multi-etiology categorization, and internal validation using a 7:3 split, yielding high diagnostic AUCs suitable for initial FUO screening. Our study, by contrast, refined the disease spectrum by focusing on differentiating lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy. The combined model demonstrated superior predictive performance and may represent an important contribution in this field. By integrating clinical, laboratory, and PET parameters, it improved diagnostic accuracy compared with single-modality approaches. Bootstrap resampling was used for model validation, further strengthening its stability and generalizability. Another study also reported that a scoring system combining PET/CT and clinical parameters effectively differentiated lymphoma from benign diseases, achieving an AUC of 0.93 [5]. However, that study had a smaller sample size and did not perform separate regression analyses for laboratory and clinical parameters, nor did it establish a combined model.

Wang et al. studied 253 FUO patients and found that combining sex, WBC count, and LDH increased the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity for malignancy to 83.3% and 81.0%, respectively. For infections, combining sex, WBC count, FER, and hepatosplenomegaly yielded a sensitivity of 78% and specificity of 76.2% [25]. Wu et al. further reported that lymphomas presenting initially as FUO were mainly B- or T-cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas, and that ferritin levels might predict long-term outcomes in FUO patients [4]. These studies consistently demonstrate that integrating PET/CT findings with clinical and laboratory data enhances diagnostic precision and reliability. Compared with previous studies, our analysis included a broader spectrum of clinical and laboratory variables and focused specifically on distinguishing lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy. By combining metabolic, clinical, and laboratory indicators, we aimed to construct a highly specific diagnostic model that improves accuracy, reduces misdiagnosis, and enhances patient management.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective analysis, which may have introduced selection bias and limited generalizability. Second, the combined model achieved a very high AUC, which may also reflect potential overfitting given the number of predictors included. Although bootstrap resampling was used for internal validation to enhance model stability, such internal validation cannot fully exclude the risk of overfitting. In addition, the cut-off values were derived from ROC analysis in the present cohort and may vary across different populations and institutions, which may limit their generalizability. Importantly, before clinical implementation, the model requires further validation in independent external cohorts, preferably through large-scale multicenter studies, to confirm its robustness and applicability across different clinical settings. Furthermore, this model is intended to assist clinical decision-making, particularly in guiding biopsy strategies, rather than replace histopathological confirmation, which remains the gold standard for diagnosis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, among FUO patients with lymphadenopathy, lymphoma is the most common malignancy, whereas infections, AOSD, and necrotizing lymphadenitis are the predominant benign causes. Rash, PCT, SAA, SUVmean, and TMTV were identified as independent predictors distinguishing lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy. The combined model integrating clinical, laboratory, and PET parameters significantly improved the diagnostic differentiation between lymphoma and benign lymph node diseases.
