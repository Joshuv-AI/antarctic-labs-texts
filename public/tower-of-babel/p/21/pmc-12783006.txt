Cellular expansion of hepatocellular carcinoma organoids using decellularized liver scaffolds
Authors: Shin Hwang, I-Ji Jung, Kyoung-Jin Lee, Yun-Kyu Kim, Eunyoung Tak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA decellularized liver scaffold (DLS) is a three-dimensional acellular extracellular matrix created by removing cellular components from liver tissue. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) organoids represent a useful experimental model.MethodsHCC organoids from patient-derived xenografts (PDX), liver organoids, and HepG2 cells were expanded by cultivation within a murine DLS.ResultsHCC and liver organoids were generated from HCC PDX and human liver tissues, respectively. Expression levels of hepatocyte paraffin 1 (HepPar1), epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM), alpha-fetoprotein, keratin-7, and keratin-19 were detected in normal liver tissue, HCC tissue, HCC PDX, and HCC organoids. Fifteen murine DLSs were created, and the complete absence of liver cells was confirmed histologically by Masson trichrome and periodic acid–Schiff staining. Culture of HCC organoids in the DLS resulted in the expansion of numerous HCC cells scattered throughout the DLS framework. Keratin-7 expression was abundant, indicating widely dispersed progenitor cells. In contrast, cultivation of HepG2 cells within the DLS resulted in sparse cell distribution throughout the scaffold. Human hepatocyte organoids could not be cultivated successfully within the murine DLS framework.ConclusionsThe DLS collagen framework facilitates the proliferation of HCC organoids. Thus, cultivating HCC organoids within a DLS appears to represent an effective method for rapid cell expansion, although it was ineffective for HepG2 cells and liver organoids. Further studies are required to validate the biological and oncological equivalence between seeded HCC organoids and those rapidly expanded within a DLS.Keywords: Organoid, Stem cell, Xenograft, Culture, Hepatocellular carcinoma

INTRODUCTION

Conventional two-dimensional cell culture methods face challenges in replicating inherent cellular properties and tissue unit characteristics. Small animal experimental models also have limitations regarding reliability in drug response testing, primarily due to genetic and biological differences from humans. As an alternative, experimental preclinical studies have employed patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models [1–3]. However, PDX models have drawbacks such as high maintenance costs, difficulty in cryopreservation, and challenging handling requirements. Recently, organoids have gained attention among experimental models. Following the establishment of intestinal organoids [4], various organoids have been developed from organs and related soft tissues, including the brain, stomach, pancreas, lungs, liver, tongue, and others [5–10]. Cancer organoids that preserve individual patient characteristics have been cultured from tissues and circulating tumor cells. Organoids can theoretically proliferate indefinitely, be cryopreserved in the form of biobanks, and manipulated easily using techniques similar to traditional two-dimensional monolayer cultures. However, generating a sufficient quantity of organoids for repetitive research remains challenging.

A decellularized liver scaffold (DLS) comprises only the three-dimensional extracellular matrix (ECM) of the liver, created by removing all cellular components [11–14]. Successful repopulation of a DLS with animal hepatocytes is possible through cell perfusion via the portal vein, suggesting that DLS is a suitable platform for organoid proliferation [15,16].

The aim of this study was to create PDX-derived organoids from human hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tissues and cultivate them within a murine DLS to produce numerous HCC organoids.

METHODS

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (IRB No. 2021-1611) and by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Asan Institute for Life Sciences (IACUC No. 2021-0251). The study complied with the ethical guidelines outlined in the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki, 2013.

The study comprised five steps: (1) creation of HCC PDX using human HCC tissues; (2) establishment of HCC organoids from HCC PDX; (3) establishment of liver organoids from human liver tissues; (4) creation of a DLS using whole mouse liver; and (5) cultivation of PDX-derived HCC organoids, liver cell organoids, and HepG2 cells within the DLS.

The objective of this study was to establish patient-derived organoids from human HCC xenografts and expand them within a murine DLS, enabling the scalable production of HCC organoids.

Part 1: Creation of HCC PDX Using Human HCC Tissues

An HCC PDX model was established by transplanting patient-derived HCC tissues into immunocompromised nonobese diabetic-severe combined immunodeficiency (NOD-SCID) mice. The histological characteristics of each PDX were then assessed. Briefly, a 0.2- to 0.3-g sample of patient HCC tissue was transplanted subcutaneously into a NOD-SCID mouse. After confirming tumor formation, the enlarged HCC tissue was excised, and its size and weight were measured. Subsequently, a 0.2-g piece was reimplanted into a NOD-SCID mouse to confirm regrowth. The regrown HCC PDX tissues were harvested and broken into small pieces before primary culture, after which PDX-derived HCC cell lines were established.

Part 2: Establishment of HCC Organoids From HCC PDX

A multicellular HCC organoid model was established by mixing PDX-derived HCC cells with vascular endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and astrocytes in specific proportions. PDX-derived HCC organoids were generated using the hanging drop method. After mixing HCC tissues with stromal cells and suspending them in Ham medium, 30-µL drops were seeded onto a Petri dish using a three-dimensional hanging drop plate system (3D Biomatrix; InSphero). The Petri dish was then incubated upside down for 7 days. Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium containing fetal bovine serum (FBS) and Ham medium (stem cell medium without FBS) was used for spheroid growth and differentiation. Organoid formation was monitored for approximately 10 days by photographing the structures under a microscope to measure them (Fig. 1).

Formation of hepatocellular carcinoma organoids over 10 days using the hanging drop method.

Part 3: Establishment of Liver Organoids From Human Liver Tissues

Pieces of normal liver tissue (2.0 g) obtained from patients without liver disease were broken into small pieces and cultured in culture medium. A multicellular liver organoid model was established by mixing human liver cells with vascular endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and astrocytes in specific proportions. The procedure for organoid formation was identical to that described for the PDX-derived HCC organoids.

Part 4: DLS Creation Using Whole Mouse Liver

A healthy mouse was euthanized in accordance with ethical guidelines. Sterile dissection was performed to harvest the liver, which was immediately placed in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to prevent degradation. The liver was then trimmed to fit into the decellularization setup.

A 20-gauge plastic needle was cannulated into the portal vein for perfusion with a sterile perfusion buffer (PBS with 1% penicillin and streptomycin) to prevent bacterial contamination. This solution also contained a detergent-based mixture comprising Triton X-100 and sodium dodecyl sulfate to facilitate cell lysis. Decellularization involved a combination of enzymatic and detergent-based methods to remove all cells from the liver without damaging the ECM. Continuous gentle portal perfusion was maintained for 48 hours at 4 °C. The liver was inspected every 6 hours to monitor cellular lysis, and the effluent was checked for cellular debris. The liver was then perfused with an enzymatic solution containing DNase to degrade any residual cellular DNA. Following detergent perfusion, the liver was rinsed with cold PBS to remove residual detergent. This process was repeated three to five times.

The DLS was sterilized through treatment for 2 hours with a mild solution of 70% peracetic acid, followed by washing with sterile PBS. The DLS was stored in sterile PBS at 4 °C for 9 to 12 days (Fig. 2). Histological analysis was performed with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to confirm the extent of decellularization. In total, 15 murine DLSs were successfully established.

Formation of a decellularized liver scaffold using a mouse liver.

Part 5: Culture of PDX-Derived HCC Organoids, Liver Organoids, and HepG2 Cells Within the DLS

Although DLS recellularization usually involves repopulating the liver ECM with functional liver cells to mimic the structure and function of native liver tissue, this study was simplified to focus solely on cell expansion.

The cold-stored DLS was preincubated overnight with culture medium to hydrate the DLS and provide a suitable environment for the cells. A hepatocyte-specific culture medium (Williams E medium supplemented with hepatocyte growth factor) was used for DLS culture. The DLS was cannulated via the portal vein, and a suspension of cells (PDX-derived HCC organoids, liver organoids, or HepG2 cells) was injected to seed the cells into the liver system.

After seeding, the DLS-containing dish was placed in a static culture system, mimicking the physiological environment to promote cell growth (37 °C/5% CO2, 1 atmosphere). The pH of the medium was maintained between 7.2 and 7.4. DLS culture continued for 14 days, with daily monitoring. Histological assessment of the DLS was performed after the 14-day culture period.

RESULTS

Histomorphological Comparison of Normal Liver and HCC Organoids

Comparative histomorphological analysis of the established organoids by H&E staining revealed that the PDX-derived HCC organoids consisted of densely packed cells. This characteristic was especially apparent at low magnification. Under high magnification, the cells within normal liver organoids displayed a largely circular arrangement, whereas HCC organoids contained clustered cells due to the high density of tumor cells. This observation suggests that HCC organoids consist of cells with high differentiation and proliferation capacity (Fig. 3).

Histomorphological comparison of normal liver, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tissue, and their derived organoids. PDX, patient-derived xenograft; H&E, hematoxylin and eosin stain.

Comparison of Biomarker Expression in Normal Liver Cells and HCC Organoids

Various biomarkers were expressed by the normal liver tissue, HCC tissue, HCC PDX, and HCC organoids (Fig. 4). The monoclonal antibody hepatocyte paraffin 1 (HepPar1), which recognizes mitochondrial antigens derived from hepatocytes, is highly specific for tumors of hepatocyte origin. Although HepPar1 was expressed to some extent in normal liver tissue, it was more prominent in HCC tissue; this finding suggested increased hepatocyte proliferation and confirmed that the tissue represented HCC.

Expression of biomarkers in normal liver, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tissue, and HCC organoids. Red arrows indicate expression of keratin-7 (KRT-7) and keratin-19 (KRT-19), which are primarily expressed by hepatic progenitor cells. PDX, patient-derived xenograft; HepPar1, hepatocyte paraffin 1; EpCAM, epithelial cell adhesion molecule; AFP, alpha-fetoprotein.

Epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) is a marker associated with cell migration and differentiation. High expression of EpCAM indicates poorer prognosis and lower tumor differentiation. The expression of this marker was notably higher in HCC tissue compared to normal liver tissue. Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), a characteristic marker of HCC, also showed higher expression in HCC organoids compared to HCC tissue or HCC PDX. Keratin-7 (KRT-7) and keratin-19 (KRT-19), biomarkers primarily expressed by hepatic progenitor cells, demonstrated higher expression in HCC organoids compared to HCC tissue or HCC PDX.

Histological Confirmation of the Absence of Liver Cells From the Murine DLS

Histological staining of cold-stored DLS confirmed the complete absence of liver cells. Masson trichrome staining demonstrated that the frameworks were composed of collagen fibers. Furthermore, periodic acid-Schiff staining indicated the absence of cellular components such as glycogen, glycoproteins, and proteoglycans, which are typically found in connective tissues, mucus, and basement membranes (Fig. 5).

Histological confirmation of cell absence from the decellularized liver scaffold (DLS). H&E, hematoxylin and eosin staining; MT stain, Masson trichrome staining; PAS, periodic acid–Schiff staining.

Culture of PDX-Derived Human HCC Organoids in the DLS

PDX-derived human HCC organoids (5 × 107) were seeded into the murine DLS via portal vein cannulation and cultured for 14 days. Analysis revealed numerous HCC cells scattered throughout the entire DLS framework. Histochemical staining confirmed these as HCC cells (Fig. 6). Abundant KRT-7 expression indicated widely scattered progenitor cells, possibly preadult stem cells. However, distinct ultrastructures typical of the normal liver system were not observed.

Histochemical staining of a murine decellularized liver scaffold (DLS) seeded with patient-derived xenograft–derived human hepatocellular carcinoma organoids. H&E, hematoxylin and eosin staining; MT, Masson trichrome staining; PAS, periodic acid–Schiff staining.

Culture of HepG2 Cells in the DLS

HepG2 cells (5 × 106) were seeded into the murine DLS via portal vein cannulation and cultured for 14 days. Analysis revealed sparse distribution of HepG2 cells partially filling the DLS framework (Fig. 7). The extent of cell coverage was markedly less than that achieved with PDX-derived HCC organoids. This difference may be attributed to the lower number of seeded cells (10 times less than for PDX-derived organoids), the comparatively low proliferative capacity of HepG2 cells, and the absence of progenitor cells. Histochemical staining confirmed the identity of the cells as HepG2.

Histochemical staining of a murine decellularized liver scaffold (DLS) seeded with HepG2 cells. H&E, hematoxylin and eosin staining; MT, Masson trichrome staining; PAS, periodic acid–Schiff staining.

Culture of Human Liver Organoids in the DLS

Human liver organoids (5 × 103) were seeded into the murine DLS via portal vein cannulation and cultured for 14 days. Human hepatocyte organoids did not demonstrate growth within the DLS framework (Fig. 8). This lack of growth may be attributed to the considerably lower seeding volume (10,000 times lower than for PDX-derived HCC organoids and 1,000 times lower than for HepG2 cells), largely due to limited availability of liver organoids. Additionally, no progenitor cells were identified.

Histochemical staining of a murine decellularized liver scaffold (DLS) seeded with human liver organoids. H&E, hematoxylin and eosin staining; MT, Masson trichrome staining; PAS, periodic acid–Schiff staining.

DISCUSSION

Experimental and preclinical studies of cancer have been conducted using various methodologies. Many early studies utilized two-dimensional cell cultures and/or animal models [17,18]. However, two-dimensional cell line cultures often fail to replicate characteristic tissue units, and animal experiments present limitations in reliability due to genetic and biological differences from humans. To overcome these limitations, PDX models using heterogeneous transplantation have been widely implemented [1–3]. We previously reported in vitro research using cell line culture derived from HCC PDX and conducted an in vivo investigation evaluating the drug efficacy of metformin and sorafenib using HCC PDX [19].

While the results of studies using PDX vary according to cancer type, the success rate of xenograft formation is generally low. Furthermore, several months are required for establishing PDX, leading to high maintenance costs, and their manipulation and cryopreservation are challenging. Additionally, PDX models may not be optimal for large-scale drug screening because accurately reproducing the patient-specific cancer microenvironment is hindered by contamination from the microenvironment of the xenograft-bearing animal [20–22].

To address these limitations, recent studies have utilized organoids [4–10], which closely resemble living organs. Indeed, organoids can be derived from virtually every human organ. Organoids initially gained attention as clinical research platforms through modeling in patients with cystic fibrosis, a disease caused by mutations in the CFTR gene. In normal rectal organoids, swelling occurred in response to forskolin treatment, whereas rectal organoids from patients with cystic fibrosis did not exhibit this response. However, this lack of edema response could be rescued by drugs that restored CFTR gene function. Consequently, organoids derived from patients with cystic fibrosis have been employed to develop therapeutics capable of restoring CFTR functionality [23–26]. More recently, organoids have been utilized for drug response testing, new drug screening, and establishing research models for various diseases caused by single-gene mutations, including certain cancers.

The development of tissue-specific organoid models represents a breakthrough addressing the lack of suitable experimental and disease models. Pancreatic cancer, for example, has a poor prognosis due to delayed diagnosis and the absence of effective therapeutic options, largely resulting from difficulties in establishing a model suitable for research and drug testing. However, a recent study described the culture of pancreatic cancer organoids from patient-derived tissues, reproducing patient-specific histological characteristics and physiological activities. Xenotransplanted pancreatic cancer organoids closely resembled the primary cancer. Furthermore, tests involving drugs that inhibit the histone methyltransferase activity of HK27me3 have demonstrated the feasibility of developing targeted therapies [27,28]. Similarly, patient-derived organoids from malignant glioblastoma—a brain tumor with a poor prognosis due to cancer cell heterogeneity and a complex microenvironment—effectively replicated the heterogeneity of cancer stem cells, highlighting their potential in developing new diagnostic and therapeutic methods [29].

Previous studies involving DLS and organoids have primarily focused on liver disease models. Liver organoids cultured within DLS can exhibit behaviors and organizational patterns that closely mimic normal liver tissue. Their primary applications include liver disease modeling, regenerative medicine, and drug metabolism research. They also provide valuable insights into hepatotoxicity and bioartificial liver systems due to their relatively stable and predictable phenotypes [30,31]. In contrast, the theoretical application of DLS with HCC organoids aims to evaluate responses to HCC treatments. While no such studies have yet been reported, DLS has been explored as a three-dimensional scaffold for HCC cell culture [32,33].

Cancer organoid-based drug screening has become feasible due to the capability of culturing cancer organoids in vitro. A preclinical drug testing platform that can verify patient-specific drug sensitivities is essential for the use of genomic data to tailor patient treatments. Both patient organoid-based drug screening platforms and PDX models can be useful in this context. For instance, these models can support the establishment of individualized treatment strategies and help determine effective therapeutic responses by comparing outcomes from existing PDX models and patient-derived organoid-based drug screenings. At the core of organoid-based drug screening platforms is the execution of large-scale drug screening programs utilizing biobanks. By culturing cancer organoids derived from numerous patients, biobanks can be established, enabling clinical research to validate new therapeutic agents [34,35]. Cancer organoid-based platforms may also bridge the gap between cancer treatments based on dielectric information and clinical applications [35,36].

In Korea, liver cancer ranks sixth in overall cancer incidence, with HCC accounting for most cases (76%) [37]. HCC has a very poor prognosis because recurrence frequently occurs even after radical treatment. Additionally, HCC is often advanced at the time of diagnosis, making curative treatment impossible in many cases. Developing a multicellular tumor organoid model using HCC cells can meaningfully facilitate the development of treatments, as organoids can mimic the tumor environment and exhibit characteristics closely resembling those of the primary tumor. Establishing a multicellular tumor organoid from patient-derived liver cancer cells is essential for future HCC research, given the disease’s heterogeneous treatment responses and varied prognoses compared to other cancers [38].

In this study, we generated HCC PDX and compared them with HCC organoids. Organoids were successfully derived from normal liver tissue and PDX-derived HCC tissue. We confirmed that cell proliferation and density were higher in organoids than in PDX tissue. Furthermore, HCC organoids better reflected the proliferation and differentiation capabilities of HCC and exhibited stronger stem cell features. Regarding biomarker expression, AFP, KRT-7, and KRT-19 were expressed at higher levels in HCC organoids. This higher biomarker expression indicates that HCC organoids better represent the differentiation and proliferation capabilities of HCC and reflect its stem cell characteristics.

DLSs are engineered tissues stripped of their cellular components, leaving only the ECM structure [11–14]. These structures are employed in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine applications, particularly liver regeneration and transplantation. In the early 2000s, the liver was recognized as a promising organ for decellularization because of its regenerative capacity and complex architecture, which includes various cell types (e.g., hepatocytes, endothelial cells, and stellate cells) embedded within a specialized ECM. By the 2010s, protocols for whole-liver decellularization had been refined; these protocols were employed in the present study. Notably, successful decellularization of entire rat and pig livers has enabled their use as scaffolds for cell repopulation. Perfusion decellularization has emerged as the preferred method; in this technique, the liver is perfused with detergents and enzymes via the vascular system, providing more uniform decellularization and better preservation of the ECM architecture. Concurrently, studies began exploring repopulation of DLSs with liver cells such as hepatocytes, endothelial cells, and stellate cells to create functional liver tissue. In the 2020s, research has focused on improving the functionality of DLSs [39], including optimizing cell seeding and culture conditions, improving scaffold vascularization, and better mimicking the complex liver microenvironment. Successful repopulation of a DLS with animal hepatocytes has been achieved through cell perfusion via the portal vein, indicating the potential of DLSs as platforms for organoid proliferation. Additionally, researchers have combined DLSs with stem cells (such as induced pluripotent stem cells) or organoid-based models to develop more sophisticated models for drug testing, disease modeling, and eventual transplantation [11–14,39–41]. These previous studies guided our attempt to expand HCC organoids using a murine DLS framework.

In this study, we seeded PDX-derived human HCC organoids (5 × 107) into a murine DLS via portal vein cannulation and cultured them for 14 days. The DLS became filled with numerous HCC cells distributed throughout the entire scaffold. KRT-7 expression was abundant, suggesting that progenitor cells (possibly preadult stem cells) were widely dispersed within the DLS. Thus, the collagen framework of the DLS appeared to facilitate the proliferation of HCC organoids. However, we did not observe the typical macroscopic and microscopic histological characteristics of normal liver ultrastructure, likely due to the intrinsic properties of HCC.

HepG2 cells (5 × 106) were also seeded into the murine DLS and cultured for 14 days. The DLS became partially filled with sparsely scattered HepG2 cells, with the cell-filled areas markedly smaller than those observed after the seeding of HCC organoids. This difference may be attributed to the considerably lower number of seeded cells (10 times fewer than for PDX-derived HCC organoids), the lower proliferative capacity of HepG2 cells, and the absence of progenitor cells. These growth patterns resembled those observed in conventional two-dimensional and three-dimensional cultures. Thus, it is reasonable to conclude that DLS does not promote rapid three-dimensional expansion of HepG2 cells.

In addition, we seeded human liver organoids (5 × 103) into the murine DLS and cultured them for 14 days. We found that human hepatocyte organoids could not be cultivated within the murine DLS. This failure might be attributed to the substantially lower number of seeded cells (10,000 times fewer than for PDX-derived HCC organoids and 1,000 times fewer than for HepG2 cells) and the scarcity of progenitor cells. Such cultivation failure may also result from using too few organoids and an insufficient number of stem cells within the hepatocyte organoids. These results suggest that the direct transformation of a DLS into a miniature liver organ using hepatocyte organoids is not feasible under current conditions.

This study had several limitations. First, the cells used were of human origin, but the organoids were cultured within a murine DLS. Second, the number of seeded cells was insufficient to drive noticeable replication in the models using HepG2 cells and liver organoids. Third, we did not perform detailed validation studies to confirm the biological and oncological equivalence between the initially seeded HCC organoids and those rapidly cultivated within the DLS. Fourth, this study employed a xenogeneic (human-mouse) model; thus, biological equivalence was not validated.

Collagen frameworks within DLS structures appear to facilitate the proliferation of HCC organoids. Therefore, culturing HCC organoids within a DLS seems to represent an effective method for rapid cellular expansion. However, this culture method was ineffective for HepG2 cells and liver organoids. Further studies are required to validate the biological and oncological equivalence between the initially seeded HCC organoids and those rapidly cultivated within the DLS. Nonetheless, the data suggest that HCC organoids may serve as a suitable platform for personalized medical treatments and future research.
