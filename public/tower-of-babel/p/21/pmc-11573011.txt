Silicone sling surgery for congenital ptosis: Tarsal tunnel technique
Authors: Neelam Pushker, Manish Mahabir, Sahil Agrawal, Mandeep Singh Bajaj
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This was a prospective, interventional, comparative study conducted on patients with congenital simple severe ptosis. A novel method of silicone rod fixation to the tarsus (tarsal tunnel technique, group 1) was done compared with a conventional technique of silicone rod fixation (suture fixation technique, group 2) in frontalis suspension surgery. A total of 30 patients were randomized into two groups of 15 patients each. Postoperatively, MRD1, vertical palpebral aperture, and eyelid fold height were comparable in both groups till the last follow-up with stability in eyelid position. Patient satisfaction scores showed similar results with good-fair satisfaction grading in 13 patients in group 1 and 11 patients in group 2 at 6 months follow-up. No significant complications occurred in either group. Tarsal tunnel fixation of silicone rods is a novel method in sling surgery with optimal cosmetic and function outcomes. Further long-term studies are needed to validate the results of the technique.

Frontalis suspension surgery is a routinely done procedure for the correction of severe congenital ptosis with poor levator function.[1] Various synthetic materials have been studied, with the most popular being silicone rods. Synthetic materials have been reported to be associated with a higher risk of recurrence of ptosis, extrusion, infection, and granuloma formation.[2] The underlying reasons described for the recurrence of ptosis are slippage of the knot, lack of fixity of sling material, cheese wiring of the sutures, and breakage of sling material.[34]

The present study was conducted to describe a novel method of silicone rod sling fixation using the tarsal tunnel technique for the correction of severe congenital ptosis with the intent to provide a better fixation of the silicone rod with a tough structure, that is, tarsus, to provide stability to the eyelid position.

This was a prospective, randomized, interventional pilot study conducted at a tertiary care hospital after obtaining ethical clearance. Informed written consent was obtained. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethics clearance was obtained from the institute’s ethics committee.

Two randomized groups of patients were recruited, one in the suture fixation technique (group 1) and the other in tarsal tunnel technique (group 2), respectively. Patients with simple severe congenital ptosis with levator action between 0 and 4 mm with age >3 years were recruited.

Surgical Technique

Surgery was performed by the same surgeon in both groups (N.P.). Local or general anesthesia as indicated was given. Cleaning and draping of the surgical area were done. In both groups, sling surgery was done using the open method wherein a skin incision was given along the desired eyelid crease. The skin incision was given at the same height as the normal eyelid in unilateral ptosis and at 6–8 mm from the eyelid margin in bilateral ptosis. Dissection of the orbicularis muscle was done to expose the superior half of the tarsal surface. Modified Crawford’s double triangle method was used to pass the silicone sling. Two stab incisions were given just above the brow, and a small deep submuscular pocket was dissected inferior to the cut for burying the knot.

Suture fixation technique (Group 1)

In the suture fixation technique, two silicone rod slings were passed through the pre-tarsal space and secured to the tarsal plate using three non-absorbable 6-0 nylon sutures taking partial thickness bites, in the tarsus, 3–4 mm from the superior tarsal border [Fig. 1].

Tarsal tunnel technique (Group 2)

Three vertical partial-thickness stab incisions were given in the tarsus, 3–4 mm from its superior border. The MVR blade was used to make a partial thickness tunnel in the tarsus connecting the incisions. A silicone rod was passed through the tarsal tunnel with the help of its attached needle probe, making a loop at the center incision which was later cut into two halves. The eyelid was everted to look for perforation of the tarsus and exposure of the silicone rod [Fig. 2].

The eyelid position was kept at the superior limbus, maintaining its contour. A modified frost suture was passed from the lower eyelid for upward traction to keep the eye closed in the immediate postoperative period. Topical antibiotics and lubricants were given. All the patients were regularly followed up. The following parameters were studied in both groups: baseline margin reflex distance 1 (MRD1); vertical palpebral aperture (VPA); height of eyelid fold in primary gaze; and presence of lagophthalmos and any complications at baseline, 6 weeks, 12 weeks, and 6 months follow-ups. Subjective assessment of patient satisfaction on a visual analog scale (VAS) of 1–10 was done at 6 months follow-up and graded as good satisfaction (score: 8–10), fair satisfaction (score: 5–7), and poor satisfaction (score: 1–4). Objective assessment of ptosis correction was also done at 6 months follow-up as mentioned in Table 1. Recurrence of ptosis was defined as a change in the subjective patient satisfaction score (VAS) from “good” or “fair” to “poor satisfaction” at 6 months follow-up.

A total of 30 patients were included, with a mean ± SD age of 11.67 ± 5.53 years (range: 6–24 years) in group 1 and 10.77 ± 4.82 (range: 5–19 years) in group 2. Five patients had bilateral ptosis in group 1 (20 eyes studied), whereas all patients in group 2 had unilateral ptosis (15 eyes studied). The preoperative value of ptosis in group 1 was 6.3 ± 1.19 (mm ± SD), which improved at 6 weeks follow-up to 2.8 ± 0.92 (P < 0.01). The preoperative value of ptosis in group 2 was 6.0 ± 1.11 (mm ± SD), which improved at 6 weeks follow-up to 2.7 ± 0.87 (P < 0.01). These remained stable in both groups till the last follow-up. There was no significant difference in MRD1 and VPA between the groups during the follow-ups, and a gradual increase in the height of VPA was observed with time. On analyzing the asymmetry in MRD1 and eyelid fold height between the eyes at 6 months follow-up in both groups, a minimal asymmetry was found with a mean ± SD of MRD1 being 1.23 ± 0.69 in group 1 and 1.07 ± 0.24 in group 2, and eyelid fold height was 0.15 ± 0.36 in group 1 and 0.08 ± 0.27 in group 2 [Table 2]. According to the objective criteria mentioned in Table 1, asymmetry in MRD1 was good-fair in 14 patients in each group. Lagophthalmos was observed to be significantly more in group 1 at 12 weeks follow-up with a declining trend noted in both groups. We also studied the intergroup patient satisfaction at 6 months follow-up and observed statistically similar results with good-fair satisfaction grading in 13 patients in group 1 and 11 patients in group 2. To define the recurrence of ptosis, a drop in patient satisfaction level (VAS) from good or fair to poor was found in one patient each in both groups at 6 months follow-up.

Intraoperatively, the surgeon observed that in group 2, though the depth of the tunnel was not uniform, the anchoring of the silicone rod with tarsus was observed to be strong in all cases. Perforation of the tarsus on the conjunctival side occurred while making the tunnel in three eyes, and a new tunnel was successfully made without perforation [Fig. 3]. Two patients in group 1 and three patients in group 2 had intraoperative lash ptosis for which skin excision with anterior lamellar repositioning was done to correct it. One patient in group 1 had extrusion of silicone rod from supra-brow incision for which repositioning was done. No other significant postoperative complications occurred in either group.

Discussion

Various studies have been done to improve the fixity of synthetic material in sling surgery. In a study, authors used polytetrafluoroethylene (Gortex), which is a porous monofilament suture known to bio-integrate with the surrounding tissues, with satisfactory results.[5] In another study, authors used the modified Crawford’s method using interlocking of the two suspension slings at the lower end by direct suturing of the sling to the tarsal plate.[6] In yet another study, authors made a customized suspension sling with a plate and holes and fixed it to the tarsus by using three 5-0 nylon sutures.[7] Many surgeons do the tarso-frontalis sling, making small stab incisions only in a pentagon or triangular fashion. This is less invasive and gives good results.

The present study was conducted to compare a novel surgical technique for the fixation of silicone sling material to the toughest structure of the eyelid, that is, the tarsus, by making a tunnel in the tarsal plate (group 2), with a described technique of fixation of sling material to the tarsus, that is, by using sutures (group 1). Both techniques need eyelid skin crease incision and hence the open method for exposing the tarsal surface. The open method of sling surgery helps in achieving the desired eyelid crease and fold, especially in unilateral cases; in addition, skin excision with or without anterior lamellar positioning helps in taking care of lash ptosis.[8] We used modified Crawford’s double triangle technique, which has a three-point pulling force on the eyelid and has been described to provide good eyelid contour.[910] Intraoperatively, the only problem faced by the surgeon in group 2 was the inability to make a uniform depth tunnel because the tarsus is a thin and fibrotic structure, but the anchoring of silicone rod with tarsus was observed to be strong.

In the present study, on intergroup comparison of postoperative MRD1 and VPA, we observed statistically similar results over a follow-up period of 6 months. The intergroup asymmetry in MRD1 and eyelid fold height was minimal and comparable in both groups till 6 months of follow-up. Lagophthalmos was observed to be significantly more in group 1 at 12 weeks follow-up with a declining trend noted in both groups. We also studied the intergroup patient satisfaction at 6 months follow-up and observed statistically similar results with good-fair satisfaction in the majority of patients. The complications noted in both groups were minor.

In conclusion, the tarsal tunnel technique of sling fixation has shown optimal outcomes with no technique-related postoperative complications. The described novel technique is unique, but the main limitation of the study is short-term follow-up. Furthermore, studies using the technique with long-term follow-up would help in defining the outcomes of the technique.
