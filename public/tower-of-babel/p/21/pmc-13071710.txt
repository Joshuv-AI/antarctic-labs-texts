A novel maximum wall thickness (Tmax) category for cystic lung adenocarcinomas: a retrospective study focusing on pathological invasiveness and prognosis
Authors: Beinuo Wang, Jin Pu, Jian Zhou, Zhenghao Dong, Xiang Lin, Yu Tong, Chunchao Xia, Hu Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCystic lung adenocarcinoma (CLA) is a major subtype of cystic lung cancer (CLC). Clinical T staging for CLA is ambiguous due to cystic components. This issue impedes precise assessment. Previous research has confirmed the significant predictive value of cystic morphology. This study explores a new category for better CLA prognostic stratification, aiding precision treatment.MethodsDiagnosed CLAs were classified into preinvasive (adenocarcinoma in situ, or minimally invasive adenocarcinoma) and invasive adenocarcinomas. Radiologic analysis focused on cystic-airspace features. Logistic and Cox regression were used to identify independent risk factors for pathological invasiveness and prognosis. A novel category incorporating maximum wall thickness (Tmax) was proposed, and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis validated this approach.ResultsA total of 219 patients were included in the study. Pleural traction [odds ratio (OR) =4.76] and Tmax (OR =62.03) were independent risk factors for pathological invasiveness of CLAs. Irregular cystic morphology [hazard ratio (HR) =3.77] and Tmax (HR =7.39) were independent prognostic factors for CLAs. Multiloculation (HR =0.38) was associated with a potential protective effect. A novel Tmax category was first proposed: Tmax① ≤0.5 cm; 0.5 cm < Tmax② ≤1.0 cm; 1.0 cm < Tmax③ ≤1.3 cm; Tmax④ >1.3 cm. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated highly significant prognostic stratification in 5-year disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) across Tmax subgroups.ConclusionsThe Tmax variable will serve as the most critical indicator for evaluating the invasiveness and prognosis of CLAs. A novel Tmax category has been established. Its predictive model effectively reflects pathological invasiveness and prognostic outcomes in patients. This Tmax category is proven beneficial for clinical surgeons in conducting staged diagnoses and other applications.

Highlight box

Key findings

• A novel category based on maximum wall thickness (Tmax) was introduced for cystic lung adenocarcinoma (CLA), enabling precise evaluation of pathological invasiveness and prognosis.

What is known and what is new?

• The presence of cystic airspace limits the assessment of clinical T staging. Previous studies have confirmed the predictive value of cystic lung cancer morphology, but some issues remain, such as a lack of standardization and subjectivity.

• The innovation of this study lies in identifying that Tmax plays the most critical role in both pathological invasiveness and prognosis, and, for the first time, establishing a measurable Tmax category.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Kaplan-Meier analysis reveals significant differences in 5-year disease-free survival and overall survival between Tmax subgroups, validating its prognostic value for clinical decision-making.

• A prospective cohort should be established, and the category should be validated in multicenter studies to enhance its applicability and aid surgical planning for patients with CLAs.

Introduction

Cystic lung cancer (CLC), a distinct subtype of primary lung cancer, predominantly presents as air-containing cavities or peripheral nodular lesions with cystic components on computed tomography (CT) imaging (1,2). A key clinical challenge is distinguishing CLC from benign cystic lung diseases on imaging, such as infected bullae and pulmonary cysts. It is reported that the CLC diagnostic accuracy decreased by 23% (3,4). This directly impacts the precision of clinical treatment decisions.

Epidemiologically, CLC accounts for approximately 0.5–4% of primary lung cancers, among which cystic lung adenocarcinoma (CLA) is the predominant pathological subtype, representing 60–80% of CLC cases (5,6). The presence of cystic components introduces notable complexity to the accurate application of the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system—particularly in the assessment of lesion size and malignancy extent—which in turn leads to ambiguities in clinical decision-making throughout the entire management pathway, from preoperative surgical strategy formulation to postoperative follow-up and adjuvant therapy planning (7,8). Currently, the existing imaging-based classification systems for CLC remain fragmented and provide limited clinical guidance. With the trend toward minimally invasive and precision lung cancer treatments, it is essential to explore prognostic stratification schemes for CLAs (9,10).

Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that imaging features of CLAs possess substantial prognostic value, with key diagnostic factors including cystic morphology, wall thickness, loculation, and CT density (5,11). This study analyzed retrospective CLA cases to develop a multivariate logistic regression for pathological invasiveness assessment and a Cox proportional hazards model for prognosis prediction. The maximum wall thickness (Tmax) emerged as the most significant independent risk factor for pathological invasiveness and prognosis. Consequently, we propose a novel Tmax category comprising four types: Tmax① ≤0.5 cm; 0.5 cm < Tmax② ≤1.0 cm; 1.0 cm < Tmax③ ≤1.3 cm; Tmax④ >1.3 cm. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2025-aw-1186/rc).

Methods

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. It was approved by the Ethics Committee of Biomedical Research of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, Chengdu, China (No. 2024-1856). Owing to the retrospective nature of the study, the ethics committee exempted informed consent.

Patients and clinicopathological characteristics

We collected cases from January 2010 to December 2022 at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, West China Hospital, Sichuan University, where preoperative chest CT reports contained Chinese keywords related to cystic airspaces (“Nangzhong”, “Nangxing”, “Nangqiang”, “Kongdong”, “Kongpao”, “Kongqiang” or “Qiqiang”). A thoracic surgeon and a radiologist independently reviewed these CT images to identify patients with cystic airspaces. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The inter-observer agreement of interpreted CT features between two reviewers was provided (Table S1). Case exclusion adhered to the following specific criteria: (I) cases labeled with the aforementioned keywords were confirmed as false positives caused by lesions adjacent to normal bronchi upon review of consecutive axial CT lung window images; (II) poor lung image quality (such as severe motion artifacts or extensive pulmonary fibrosis) accompanied by numerous indistinguishable bullae, making it difficult to accurately identify and evaluate potential cystic tumor lesions; (III) cases where even joint image reading could not clearly differentiate between “bullae with infection” and “cystic lung cancer”; (IV) cases unanimously deemed as necrotic cavities formed by rapidly growing squamous cell carcinoma. After this initial screening, only 10 non-adenocarcinoma cases were identified (6 squamous cell carcinomas, 2 adeno-squamous carcinomas, and 2 mixed-type carcinomas). Given the extremely small sample sizes of non-adenocarcinoma subtypes and their vastly different imaging manifestations, there was a high risk of increased bias due to insufficient subgroup representation. Therefore, these cases were excluded. Finally, only patients with resected CLAs were included. A flow diagram with inclusion and exclusion criteria was presented (Figure 1).

Baseline and survival data were retrieved from the Western China Lung Cancer Database (WCLCD), including: (I) demographics: age, sex, height, weight, smoking history, family history of cancer; (II) malignant CT signs: lobulation, spiculation, pleural traction; (III) survival outcomes: disease-free survival (DFS), overall survival (OS); (IV) clinical T (cT) staging for lung cancer, in accordance with the TNM 9th edition, was determined preoperatively via contrast-enhanced chest CT. Staging was based on measuring the longest diameter of the entire lesion at its largest axial dimension. Pathological TNM staging (pTNM) refers to the assessment of the primary tumor (pT), regional lymph nodes (pN), and distant metastasis (pM) based on histopathological examination of surgically resected specimens and postoperative pathological examination (12).

Radiological features and morphological categories

All measurements were taken on thin-slice CT (1 mm) using electronic calipers on picture archiving and communication system (PACS). The lung window (window width 1,500; window level −600 HU) is used to clearly display the measurements. The mediastinal window (window width 350–400 HU; window level 40–50 HU) was used only as an adjunct to evaluate solid components, while all thickness-related measurements were performed on the lung window to ensure consistency. Radiological features were recorded, including location, lesion component, lesion length, loculation, cyst length, wall thickness, cyst morphology, and wall morphology. In this study, “solid component” refers to a homogeneous area on lung window images that exhibits higher density than the adjacent lung parenchyma and completely obscures bronchial and vascular structures. The location was classified as lobar distribution and anatomical position (peripherally detected within the outer third of the lobe). The lesion component was classified as pure ground-glass opacity (GGO), part-solid, and solid. Lesion length was the maximal diameter of the lesion. Loculation was described as unilocular and multilocular, and cyst length was defined as the maximal diameter of the cystic airspace (if multilocular, the longest distance between cystic airspaces was measured). For each lesion, axial images were reviewed first to identify the slice showing the maximal wall thickening. When the maximal thickening was not clearly captured on axial images due to an oblique lesion orientation or complex morphology, multiplanar reconstructions (coronal and sagittal) were used to localize the maximal-thickness site. Wall thickness was the maximal and minimal vertical distance measured from the continuous boundary of the cystic air cavity to the outer tangent of the continuous boundary at the outermost edge of the lesion. The cyst morphology was classified as oval or irregular, and the wall morphology was classified as smooth or irregular.

Pathology and gene evaluation

Recorded pathological features, including Histological subtypes, differentiation grade, N stage, and genetic testing reports. Histological subtypes of CLC were confirmed according to the 2021 World Health Organization classification (13). For invasive adenocarcinoma (IAC), histologic grade was assigned according to the predominant growth pattern: lepidic-predominant (low grade, well differentiated), acinar- or papillary-predominant (intermediate grade, moderately differentiated), and micropapillary- or solid-predominant (high grade, poorly differentiated). Invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma (n=3) was excluded from the analysis due to its distinct histologic subtype and limited sample size (14).

Follow-up strategy

Survival data for patients with CLAs were extracted from the WCLCD. The department has a dedicated follow-up team and provides standardized preoperative education that emphasizes the importance of follow-up, resulting in high overall patient compliance. The follow-up protocol was structured as follows: after surgery, telephone follow-up was conducted every 3 months. For the subsequent 2 years, telephone follow-up was conducted every 6 months. After 3 years, telephone follow-up was conducted every 24 months. Patients were considered alive until the date of the latest follow-up record or the date of death. The follow-up period was calculated from the surgery date to the last follow-up date, with censoring at death. Two patients were lost to follow-up, and one additional patient was excluded because preoperative CT images were missing.

The main endpoints of this study were 5-year OS and 5-year DFS. OS was defined as the time from radical resection to death from any cause. DFS was defined as the time from radical resection to the first documented recurrence, whether local or metastatic. Recurrence specifically refers to the progression of tumor lesions that resemble the original CLA.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.2.1 software, with a significance level of α=0.05. Inter-group differences were evaluated using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, depending on the theoretical frequency distributions. Variables with P values less than 0.1 in univariable analysis were included in non-parametric correlation analyses and in the variance inflation factor (VIF) tests to evaluate multicollinearity. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied for variable screening, choosing the λ value that minimized mean squared error. Logistic and Cox regression analyses used variables identified via stepwise selection based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC) to identify independent factors affecting pathological invasiveness and prognosis in CLAs. The concordance index (C-index) of the logistic invasion diagnostic model and the Cox prognostic model were validated using 5-fold cross-validation and bootstrap resampling. Nomograms were constructed using the R package “rms”. The cutoff points for Tmax subtypes were determined using the R package “survminer” to maximize survival differences between groups. DFS and OS were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Survival curves were generated using the R package “survival”.

Results

Baseline of the study cohort

A total of 219 patients undergoing surgical resection for CLAs were included. The mean age of patients with CLAs was 56.1±10.7 years, with males (51.6%) predominating over females (48.4%), and 70.3% were non-smokers. Anatomical locations of 216 cases (98.6%) were peripheral. Radiological features included: 77 cases (35.2%) with pure GGO, 72 cases (32.8%) with part-solid components, and 70 cases (31.9%) with solid components. Pathological subtypes comprised 31 cases (14.1%) of adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS)/minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA) and 188 cases (85.8%) of IAC. Tmax category distribution was: 72 cases (32.8%) for Tmax①, 97 cases (44.2%) for Tmax②, 19 cases (8.6%) for Tmax③, and 31 cases (14.1%) for Tmax④. Lobectomy was performed in 136 cases (62.1%). Postoperative genetic testing in 199 cases (90.8%) revealed epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations (44 cases, 20.0%), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) mutations (8 cases, 3.6%), ROS proto-oncogene 1 (ROS1) mutations (24 cases, 10.9%), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression (15 cases, 6.8%), and other mutations, four cases involving Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), K16, 56-gene, and exon 5 p.R175H missense mutations) (Table 1).

CT imaging features analysis of CLAs

This study first analyzed the CT imaging features of CLAs. Key features requiring emphasis included lesion size, Tmax, lesion components, and the presence or absence of malignant CT features. Among the pathological subtypes of CLAs, 3 AIS cases exhibited small cyst diameters (<5 mm), pure GGO density, and thin cyst walls (Figure 2A). Twenty-eight MIA cases showed slightly larger cyst diameters but with partial cyst wall thickening and increased cyst wall density (Figure 2B). In 188 IAC cases, heterogeneous cyst diameters, markedly thickened cyst walls (>5 mm), and multiple irregular solid components within or adjacent to cystic structures were observed (Figure 2C). These solid CLAs frequently exhibited malignant CT signs such as spiculation and lobulation, suggesting higher invasiveness. The imaging characteristics of solid-predominant CLAs with thick cyst walls strongly correlated with aggressive pathological behavior.

Features analysis and invasive risk prediction model for CLAs

A comparative analysis was conducted on the included variables between the preinvasive and invasive CLAs group (Table S2). Variables with area under the curve (AUC) >0.8 included lesion length, Tmax, cystic length, and cystic wall morphology (Figure 3A). The irregular cyst morphology, irregular wall morphology, and the solid component demonstrated significant positive correlations (highlighted in blue, P<0.01), suggesting a shared pathological basis among these features. Additionally, the cyst length, the difference between lesion length and cyst length, and the Tmax exhibited significant positive correlations (highlighted in blue, P<0.01) with these CT image characteristics (Figure 3B). VIF tests confirmed multicollinearity acceptability, with the maximum VIF observed for the Tmax (VIF =2.996) (Table S3).

LASSO regression and stepwise logistic regression were performed for variable selection (Figure S1A,S1B). Ultimately, we identified pleural traction [odds ratio (OR) =4.76, P=0.001] and Tmax (OR =62.03, P<0.001) as the two independent risk factors for pathological invasiveness of CLAs (Table 2). The mean C-index for the logistic regression model using five-fold cross-validation was 0.871, demonstrating highly discriminative performance across different training-validation data splits with minimal performance variability (Figure S1C). Bootstrap internal resampling was performed with 1,000 iterations to generate an empirical distribution of the C-index, yielding a Bootstrap-validated C-index of 0.862 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.767–0.957] (Figure S1D). The data demonstrated that a nomogram was constructed to visualize the quantitative contributions of each indicator to the invasive prediction probability (Figure S1E). Certainly, the nomogram established in this study has not yet been validated in an independent external dataset, and its generalizability needs to be confirmed.

Prognostic factors analysis of CLAs

A comparative analysis of the included variables was performed between invasive CLAs with DFS ≥5 years and those with DFS <5 years (Table S4). We used a Cox proportional hazards regression to screen independent risk factors influencing CLA prognosis. LASSO regression was applied for variable selection. Analysis revealed that only the Tmax was retained as a significant predictor (Figure 4A,4B). Subsequently, variables associated with poor histopathological grade were incorporated into the Cox regression (Table 3). Three variables were ultimately identified: multiloculation [hazard ratio (HR) =0.382, P=0.02], irregular cyst morphology (HR =3.772, P=0.007), and Tmax (HR =7.393, P<0.001) (Table 4).

The mean C-index for the Cox prognostic model using five-fold cross-validation was 0.828, demonstrating highly discriminative performance across different training-validation data splits with minimal performance variability (Figure 4C). Bootstrap internal resampling was performed with 1,000 iterations to generate an empirical distribution of the C-index, yielding a Bootstrap-validated C-index of 0.836 (95% CI: 0.759–0.905) (Figure 4D). The data demonstrated that a nomogram was further constructed to visualize the 3-year and 5-year DFS probabilities, providing a clinical reference for prognosis assessment (Figure 4E). Indeed, its generalizability requires further research.

Correlation between the Tmax category and TNM staging/pathology

Changes in Tmax subtypes demonstrated highly significant differences in both preoperative and postoperative T staging. Among these cases, 35.1% exhibited pathological T staging (pT) that was higher than cT staging. Specifically, 119 cases (64.32%) showed cT = pT, while 65 cases (35.1%) had pT > cT, with most pT upgrades attributed to pathological visceral pleural invasion (VPI) (57 cases of T1x→T2a, accounting for 87.70% of pT > cT cases). And the risk of VPI increased markedly with thicker cyst walls, rising from 23.9% in Tmax① to 86.7% in Tmax④. All Tmax① cases were stage I, while stage I prevalence decreased progressively to 90%, 78.9%, and 53.3% in Tmax②, Tmax③, and Tmax④ groups, respectively. N0 rates declined progressively across Tmax subgroups: 100% (Tmax①), 93.3% (Tmax②), 89.5% (Tmax③), and 60.0% (Tmax④).

Pathological differentiation demonstrated distinct patterns: Tmax①: exclusively moderate-to-high differentiation, with no low differentiation. Tmax② and Tmax③: high differentiation rates decreased to 23.9% and 14.4%, respectively. Tmax④: exclusively moderate-to-low differentiation, lacking high differentiation. Genetic testing revealed EGFR mutations (22.7%), ROS-1 mutations (11.9%), PD-L1 expression (7.6%), and ALK rearrangements (3.8%), with no significant correlation between these mutations and Tmax subtypes (Table 5).

In this study, 219 CLA patients with a median follow-up of 55 months were analyzed. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed highly significant differences in 5-year DFS and 5-year OS among Tmax subtypes (Log-rank test, P<0.001). Among these, Tmax① exhibited the best prognosis, with 100% 5-year DFS and OS rates, and no events were recorded in the risk table. Conversely, Tmax④ had the poorest prognosis, with 5-year DFS and OS rates of 45.2% and 48.4%, respectively, along with a median survival time of 50 months. The survival curve for this subtype showed a sharp decline. Discussion

In this study, we aimed to explore a new category for CLA risk stratification. The main reason is that current imaging-based classification systems for CLC remain complex and face significant challenges in clinical implementation, especially for cT staging. Here, we focus on CLAs, which account for 60–80% of CLC cases (15,16). To assist surgical planning and clinical treatment, we analyzed clinicopathological features associated with pathological invasiveness and prognosis and developed a novel category based on Tmax.

TNM staging aims to map tumor biology to objectively measurable phenotypes, such as invasiveness, tumor size, and lymph node status, thereby providing a framework for treatment planning and predicting long-term outcomes (12). However, in CLAs, conventional T staging has notable limitations. The key metric, maximum tumor diameter, often fails to capture CLA-specific invasive patterns, including circumferential cyst wall thickening and loculation. Moreover, previously proposed classification schemes for CLC face practical challenges in real-world application: first, morphological progression lacks a standardized quantitative assessment framework; second, multiple imaging features frequently coexist in complex lesions, making it difficult to delineate stage-related differences in invasiveness in a consistent and reproducible manner (11,17,18).

Against this background, we first focused on pathological invasiveness in CLAs. Prior studies have shown that AIS and MIA are associated with 5-year DFS rates of nearly 100% (13,19). Analysis of imaging features suggests that oval cystic morphology, smooth cyst walls, and pure ground-glass components may characterize CLA at the AIS/MIA spectrum. A prediction model incorporating pleural traction and Tmax demonstrated excellent discrimination for invasive CLAs, achieving a mean C-index of 0.871. Notably, pathological invasiveness should not be directly equated with metastatic potential; nevertheless, within this cohort, it served as a useful marker for risk stratification, and extrapolation to other settings should be approached with caution.

We then evaluated prognostic factors for CLAs. Irregular cyst morphology and sTmax emerged as independent predictors of poorer prognosis, whereas multiloculation showed a weakly protective association (HR =0.382, 95% CI: 0.169–0.866; P=0.02). This finding may indicate that, although multiloculation can coexist with invasive pathology, it is not necessarily a marker of worse survival in CLAs. One possible explanation is that multilocular architecture, with fibrous septa partitioning the cystic space, may impose additional barriers to contiguous spread; however, this interpretation remains speculative and warrants validation in external cohorts. Notably, the solid-component feature was not retained in the final models for either invasiveness prediction or prognosis. This suggests that, in CLAs, the predictive contribution of a solid component may be attenuated when Tmax is included, because solid components in cystic lesions often arise in regions of increased wall thickness and their prognostic information may be partially captured by Tmax (16,20,21).

Given that the Tmax of the cyst is a common and critical independent risk factor for pathological invasiveness and prognosis, we applied the previously described four-tiered Tmax stratification, incorporating cT staging with postoperative pathological (p) TNM staging to validate its rationality through comparative analysis. The cT stage is typically based on preoperative non-invasive imaging to assess tumor size, while the pTNM stage is determined through pathological examination of the surgically resected primary tumor, regional lymph nodes, and distant metastases—serving as the gold standard for evaluating tumor invasion and prognosis. Analysis showed that Tmax categories significantly correlate with T staging (P<0.001), N staging (P<0.001), and pathological differentiation (P=0.035). In 64.32% of cases, the cT matched the pT stage; however, 35.1% exhibited pT > cT, contradicting Sheard et al.’s hypothesis that cystic airspace components might cause over-staging (7). Notably, VPI was common in CLA (42.7%) and was the primary reason for pT upstaging (57/65 cases). Genetic analysis revealed EGFR mutations in 22.7% of cases, significantly lower than Ma et al.’s 58.5% (11), with no notable differences across Tmax subtypes. The disparity between Western populations (~25% KRAS mutations) and higher EGFR prevalence in Asians might reflect ethnic differences or sampling limitations. Survival analysis demonstrated distinct prognostic outcomes across Tmax subtypes, with Kaplan-Meier curves showing highly significant differences in both 5-year DFS (P<0.001) and 5-year OS (P<0.001). Tmax④ patients had the poorest prognosis, with 5-year DFS of 45.2% and OS of 48.4%, and a median survival of 50 months.

We further observed that when Tmax >0.5 cm (exceeding Tmax①), recurrence may occur, with metastasis alone being the most common recurrence pattern, accounting for 58.1% (18/31 cases). The local recurrence combined with metastasis accounted for 32.3% (10/31 cases), while local recurrence accounted for only 9.7% (3/31 cases) (Table S5). Besides, high Tmax was defined as a wall thickness >1 cm, corresponding to Tmax③ and Tmax④, encompassing 50 patients, all with a pathological stage of at least Ib (pT2aN0M0). Among them, 22 cases (44.0%) experienced recurrence within 5 years post-surgery, and 16 cases (32.0%) ultimately died from lung cancer. These findings suggest that for patients with Tmax >0.5 cm, vigilance for potential metastasis is warranted, and systemic evaluation (e.g., bone scan or PET-CT) may be actively considered to avoid delayed diagnosis. Additionally, high-Tmax patients should be closely monitored for common lung cancer metastatic sites such as the brain, bones, and liver during postoperative follow-up.

In summary, consistent with prior reports, cyst wall thickening and solid components appear to drive the malignant potential of CLAs; Tmax offers a reproducible quantitative metric for these features and may help refine preoperative risk stratification beyond size alone (21). In clinical practice, Tmax may serve as a reproducible preoperative metric for cystic lung lesions. Radiologists may consider reporting Tmax alongside lesion diameter to facilitate standardized risk communication. Surgeons may use the Tmax category as an adjunct to diameter-based T assessment during preoperative counseling and operative planning, particularly for cystic lesions where fluid components may lead to underestimation of size. Oncologists may incorporate the Tmax category as supplementary information when discussing postoperative risk and surveillance intensity in accordance with established guidelines.

There are still certain key limitations in this study, and suggestions for future research are also presented. First, this study focuses on the most common CLA subtype in clinical practice, and the predictive efficacy of other subtypes in CLC remains unknown. Secondly, this study is a retrospective analysis conducted at a single medical center, and the limitations of its research design and data sources may compromise the generalizability of its conclusions and introduce the risk of systematic error. Future studies should expand the cohort size in prospective design and establish multicenter collaborations to validate the universality and stability of the Tmax category across various pathological subtypes of CLC, ensuring its applicability across diverse populations and clinical centers. Third, CT imaging analysis should incorporate volumetric data alongside diametric measurements to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of tumor characteristics and better reflect overall tumor morphology and growth patterns.

Conclusions

We proposed a novel four-tiered category based on Tmax (with cutoffs at 0.5, 1.0, and 1.3 cm) for the evaluation and prognostic stratification of CLAs. It can be integrated into routine clinical practice, providing a new tool for the treatment and prognostic evaluation of CLAs.
