Predictors of interruption in medical follow-up among patients with complex adult congenital heart disease
Authors: Ryotaro Maeda, Naohiko Nakanishi, Tomoyuki Nagai, Hideo Tsubata, Michiyo Yamano, Tetsuhiro Yamano, Takeshi Nakamura, Yoko Kawai, Yo Kajiyama, Kazuyuki Ikeda, Shuhei Fujita, Shinichiro Oda, Tomoko Iehara, Satoaki Matoba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMany patients with adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) experience interruptions in medical follow-up, leading to a worsening life prognosis, particularly among those with moderate-to-severe complexity. However, the risk factors associated with interruption remain unclear.MethodsIn this questionnaire-based study, we sent postcards containing a QR code linking to the questionnaire to 1226 patients who underwent surgery for congenital heart disease between 1997 and 2023 and were >15 years old at registration. We analyzed the causative factors for interruption in medical follow-up among patients with moderate or severe ACHD complexity.ResultsOf 785 patients who received questionnaire, 211 patients provided valid responses. Among them, 144 patients with moderate-to-severe complexity were included in the analysis to identify factors associated with interruption in medical follow-up. We found that 19.4 % of patients experienced interruptions. 64.0 % of patients discontinued hospital visits between 15 and 19 years old. Multivariate analysis revealed that the perceived need for continued hospital visits was an independent factor associated with interruption.ConclusionsOur study identified inadequate communication regarding the necessity of continued follow-up as an independent factor contributing to the interruption of medical care. These findings highlight the need for better education for both ACHD specialists and general healthcare providers.Keywords: Adult congenital heart disease, Transition, Medical follow-up, Interruption

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is a common condition occurring in approximately 1 % of newborns [1]. With advances in cardiac surgery, approximately 90 % of patients with CHD survive into adulthood [2]. In Japan, the number of patients with adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) exceeds 500,000 with a growing proportion of those having moderate-to-severe complexity [3].

Most CHD surgeries are “reparative” rather than “curative,” [4] resulting in various postoperative residual conditions and sequelae. Lifelong care is essential, and a seamless medical transition from pediatric to adult is critical to prevent gaps in management [5]. Nevertheless, approximately one-quarter of patients with ACHD experience interruptions in medical follow-up [6], which can lead to poor prognosis particularly in those with moderate-to-severe complexity [4,7]. Previous studies have identified male sex and non-severe disease complexity as patient-related factors of follow-up interruption [8,9]. However, the specific risk factors for interruption in patients with moderate or severe ACHD complexity remain unclear.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the causative factors for interruption in medical follow-up among this high-risk population to improve the medical system for ACHD care.

Methods

Study population

In this single-center questionnaire study, 1226 patients who underwent CHD surgeries at our institution between January 1997 and December 2023 and were aged >15 years at registration were invited via postcards containing quick response (QR) codes linked to the questionnaire. Among them, 437 had unknown addresses, and four had died. Of the remaining 785 who received postcards, 571 did not respond, and three declined participation; ultimately, 211 patients provided valid responses (response rate: 26.9 %). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution (ERB-C-3240). Participation was voluntary under an opt-in informed consent protocol, and only respondents who provided consent were included.

Data collection

The names of diseases and surgeries were confirmed by chart review. Diseases were categorized into three complexity groups (mild, moderate, and severe) according to the guidelines [10]. The questionnaire included items such as age, sex, understanding of the disease, and past surgery, number of past surgeries, presence of oral medication, and level of independence in daily life, as well as the Japanese Transition Readiness Assessment Questionnaire (TRAQ-J) [11,12] and the EuroQol five-dimensional questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) [13] to assess transition readiness and quality of life. It also addressed current hospital visits and reasons for interruption in medical follow-up. Interruption in medical follow-up was defined as the discontinuation of regular CHD follow-up, either when patients did not attend their scheduled appointments or when no further appointments were arranged (including the permitted termination from their physician).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviation or medians with interquartile range (IQR). Differences between groups were analyzed using Fisher's exact test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors for interruption of medical follow-up, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro version 18.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of patients with all complexities

The mean age was 27.0 ± 12.1 years; 86.3 % were <40 years, and 43.1 % were male. Of the patients, 31.8 % had mild, 46.5 % moderate, and 21.8 % severe disease complexity. The mean number of surgeries was 1.67 ± 0.98. Overall, 39.3 % had experienced an interruption in medical follow-up. 36.0 % of patients had not been informed about the need for continued hospital visits, and 63.1 % wished to learn more about their medical conditions. Regarding physical disability, 81.0 % of patients were independent in their daily lives.

Characteristics of patients with moderate or severe complexity and causes of interruption in care

144 patients with moderate or severe complexity were classified by follow-up status (discontinuation and continuation groups). The mean age was 28.5 ± 13.9 years, and 44.4 % were male. Among these patients, 19.4 % experienced interruption in medical follow-up. The reasons for interruption were physician permission to discontinue hospital visits (76.9 %), discontinuation of care during the transition (11.5 %), and patients’ decision (7.7 %) (Fig. 1). 64.0 % of patients discontinued hospital visits between 15 and 19 years old, a critical period for transition to adult care.

Among 785 patients who received postcards, 211 patients provided valid responses (response rate of 26.9 %). 144 patients with moderate or severe complexity were classified by follow-up status. Among these patients, 19.4 % experienced interruption in medical follow-up, and 64.0 % discontinued hospital visits between 15 and 19 years of age. Patients in the discontinuation group had better understanding about their disease and had a smaller number of surgery than those in continuation group. In addition, explanation of continued hospital visit necessity were less frequently provided in the discontinuation group (25.0 % vs. 99.1 %, P < 0.01). Regarding the transition readiness and health-related QOL, patients in the discontinuation group had better QOL (EQ-5D-5L score: 0.98 ± 0.05 vs. 0.91 ± 0.13, P < 0.01), were more independent in daily life (92.9 % vs. 73.9 %, P = 0.041), and had good readiness for transition (TRAQ-J score: 103.3 ± 10.7 vs. 90.9 ± 28.6, P = 0.067).

Univariate and multivariate analyses for interruption of care in patients with moderate or severe complexity

Univariate logistic regression analysis revealed several factors significantly associated with interruption of medical follow-up in patients with moderate or severe complexity: moderate complexity, lack of understanding of the disease name, lack of understanding of the surgical procedure, undergoing ≥2 surgeries, lack of explanation about the need for continued hospital visits, lack of transition to ACHD care, no medication use, independence in daily life, and high EQ-5D-5L score (P < 0.05, respectively).

In multivariate analysis, only a lack of explanation regarding the need for continued hospital visits remained a strong independent predictor of follow-up interruption (OR 328.4; 95 % CI 34.4–12016; P < 0.01) (Table 1).

Discussion

This study explored factors that interrupt medical follow-up in patients with moderate or severe ACHD complexity. The major findings were that (1) the most common reason for discontinuing hospital visits was physician permission, and (2) lack of explanation about the need for continued hospital visits was the strongest independent factor associated with interruption of follow-up in patients with moderate or severe ACHD complexity.

Interruption of medical follow-up has become an important issue in ACHD care. However, detailed analyses in the Japanese healthcare setting have been limited. We found that 19.4 % of patients with moderate-to-severe ACHD complexity experienced interruption of follow-up, and more than half of them stopped hospital visits during the transition period. Notably, over 70 % of these patients reported being told by doctors that further follow-up was unnecessary. Interruption of medical follow-up in this population may lead to sudden cardiac death, heart failure, arrhythmia, infective endocarditis, and high-risk pregnancy or childbirth, often requiring advanced medical intervention and associated with a poorer prognosis [4,7,8]. It has been reported that interruption in medical follow-up is independently associated with increased mortality in a large ACHD cohort, and patients at high risk of interruption should be specifically targeted and receive counselling to mitigate their increased risk of death [14].

The univariate analysis showed that patients with severe complexity or multiple surgeries were more likely to continue follow-up, consistent with previous findings [8,9]. Furthermore, being informed about the need for continued hospital visits and receiving appropriate transition to adult care were associated with continued follow-up. Multivariate analysis revealed that lack of explanation about the need for continued hospital visits was the strongest independent factor associated with the interruption of medical follow-up. This finding underscores that the responsibility for follow-up interruption lies largely with medical professionals rather than with the patients themselves. Therefore, adequate education for both ACHD specialists and general healthcare providers is essential to develop a medical system that ensures continuous care for patients with ACHD from childhood through adulthood.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a limited sample size, particularly for patients with moderate or severe ACHD complexity. Second, potential selection bias may exist because the questionnaire required a certain level of cognitive ability for completion, and only respondents who returned it were analyzed; non-respondents may have had different characteristics. Such selection bias could affect the generalizability of our findings. Third, detailed information on which provider issued permission for discontinuation of follow-up and the exact timing was unavailable. Finally, as some participants had not yet reached adulthood or completed the transition to adult care, interruption of medical follow-up did not necessarily indicate a failed medical transition.

Conclusions

Our study identified lack of explanation regarding the need for continued hospital visits as the strongest factor associated with interruption of medical follow-up in patients with moderate-to-severe ACHD complexity. This finding suggests that follow-up was often interrupted because medical professionals failed to emphasize its importance. As the responsibility for interruption of medical follow-up largely lies with medical professionals rather than with patients, adequate education is essential for both ACHD professionals and general healthcare providers.
