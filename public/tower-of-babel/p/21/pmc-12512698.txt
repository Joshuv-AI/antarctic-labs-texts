Determinants and barriers of modern family planning uptake among postpartum mothers at selected regional referral hospitals in Uganda: a cross-sectional study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostpartum family planning (PPFP) is important in reducing the risk of complications associated with closely spaced pregnancies. Family planning (FP) programmes usually promote the use of modern methods rather than traditional methods as the latter have a higher failure rate. However, only 28% of postpartum women in Uganda use modern FP. We identified determinants and barriers of modern FP uptake among postpartum mothers at selected regional referral hospitals (RRH) in Uganda in October 2023.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study among 499 postpartum mothers aged 18–49 years, and attending immunization clinics in seven selected RRH in October 2023. We defined modern FP uptake as use of a contemporary contraception method: condoms, intrauterine devices (IUD), male or female sterilization, injectables, oral contraceptives and implants. We conducted interviews to collect data on sociodemographic characteristics, reproductive health and obstetric history and modern FP uptake. We summarized participant characteristics using frequency distributions, and identified determinants of uptake using modified Poisson regression.ResultsAmong 499 postpartum mothers, most were aged 25–34 years (45%), unemployed (33%), and had attained secondary education (45%). The majority had heard about modern FP (85%), received counselling regarding PPFP (64%), and were using modern FP methods (46%). Mother being formally employed (aPR = 1.5, 95% CI: 1.1–2.1), having an employed partner (aPR = 4.14, 95% CI: 1.31–13.05), exclusive breastfeeding (aPR = 0.8, 95% CI: 0.65–0.99), receiving counselling on FP (aPR = 2.9, 95% CI: 2.1–4.02) were associated with modern FP uptake. Among 270 mothers who did not take up modern FP, spouse not being around (17%), fear of side effects (13%), and not being decided on method (10%) were the commonest reasons for non-uptake of modern FP.ConclusionUptake of modern FP among postpartum mothers was suboptimal given that all postpartum mothers should be using FP. Mother being employed, partner employment, exclusive breastfeeding and receiving counselling on FP were associated with uptake of modern FP. Spouse not being around, fear of side effects and not being decided on FP method, were the commonest reasons for non-uptake of modern FP. We recommended intensifying FP counseling services in addition to health education for mothers and their partners on the benefits of using modern FP to improve modern FP uptake among postpartum mothers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02137-4.

Introduction

Globally, about 41% of total pregnancies are unintended with 44% of these occurring within 12 months after delivery (postpartum period) in low and middle-income countries [1]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, approximately 56% of women are susceptible to unintended pregnancy during the postpartum period [1]. Given the unpredictable time of the return of fertility after childbirth, postpartum women can get pregnant right before the return of their menstruation [2].The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends attempting the next pregnancy at least 24 months after birth or six months after a miscarriage [3].The shorter the birth-to-pregnancy interval, the higher the risk to women and their children. Short intervals predispose to a higher risk of preterm birth, low birth weight, congenital abnormalities for infants, as well as pregnancy-related complications, such as anaemia, pre/eclampsia and obstetric fistula [3].

Increasing accessibility to contraceptive methods among postpartum women, also known as postpartum family planning (PPFP) is an important strategy to reduce the risk of closely spaced pregnancies and their associated complications [4]. PPFP can avert more than 30% of maternal deaths and 10% of child mortality [5]. Recognizing this, Uganda Ministry of Health (MoH) recommends PPFP as a pivotal intervention to ensure optimal outcomes for both women and infants [4]. Using a modern FP method during the postpartum period is not only effective for pregnancy planning and child spacing [6] but also improves maternal and child health outcomes [7].

According to the Uganda FP advocacy strategy and costed implementation plan 2020/21–2024/25, PPFP is meant to increase the levels of FP uptake [8]. FP programmes usually promote the use of modern methods rather than traditional methods as the latter have a higher failure rate [5]. According to the report by WHO on medical eligibility criteria for contraceptive use, the recommended modern FP methods for women in the postpartum period include: progestin-only pills, condoms, implants, intrauterine devices (IUD), and bilateral tubal ligation (BTL) [9].

Despite the benefits of PPFP, its use in developing countries is estimated to be 41% [5]. Moreover, previous work in low-income countries has shown that despite a substantially expressed need to delay or prevent pregnancy during the postpartum period, modern FP is generally low [10]. Findings from a Ugandan population-based cross-sectional study on predictors of modern contraceptive use during the postpartum period among women were that 28% of postpartum women use modern FP [11]. Our present study leverages on that to provide a more concise estimate of current PPFP use among mothers of children less than 12 months and possible reasons for PPFP non-uptake. We assessed determinants and barriers of modern FP uptake among postpartum mothers at selected RRHs in Uganda for the period from October 2022 to September 2023.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study in seven conveniently selected RRHs across the four regions of Uganda. Uganda has seventeen RRHs. However, we conducted the study at only seven, namely: Gulu, Lira, Soroti, Mubende, Fort Porstal, Moroto, and Mbale (Fig. 1). We conveniently selected these RRHs because they were duty stations for the investigators. These facilities are high volume serving a total population of almost two million people monthly(National FP Advocacy Strategy & Costed ImplementatioSn [12]). They offer general and specialized health services such as immunization, FP, obstetrics and gynecological services among others.Fig. 1Study sites

Study sites

Study population

An eligible participant was a mother aged 18–49 years who had given birth within the previous 12 months (October 2022–September 2023), and attending immunization clinics at any of the seven selected RRHs in October 2023. Mothers who declined to consent, or were mentally incapacitated, or ill were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling procedures

Using the Fleiss formula, we computed sample size based on findings from a cross-sectional study of 397 postpartum mothers aged 18–49 years attending a national referral hospital in Uganda, 80% power, 95% level of confidence, design effect of 2, and contraceptive counseling during antenatal care (adjusted odds ratio = 9.05; 95% CI 2.65–30.93) as the predictor(National Family Planning Advocacy Strategy & Costed Implementation [12]) to obtain 499 participants for our study. We recruited 499 participants and distributed them across the seven selected RRHs using proportion-to-size probability sampling. Participants at each selected RRH were selected systematically until the required sample size was reached. The distribution of the 499 participants was as follows: Gulu-75, Lira-134, Soroti-72, Mubende-50, Fort Portal-91, Moroto-26, and Mbale-51.

Data collection and study variables

We collected data using a pretested, interviewer-administered questionnaire embedded into Kobo collect tool-box. We collected data on socio-demographic characteristics (mothers’ age, partners’ age, mothers’ religion, partners’ religion, marital status, mothers’ occupation, partners’ occupation, mothers’ education level and partners’ education level); reproductive health and obstetric history (number of pregnancies, ever heard abortion or miscarriage, number of antenatal care visits for most recent pregnancy, place of delivery for most recent pregnancy, mode of delivery); knowledge and practices (breastfeeding, partner support during antenatal care visit, ever heard about modern FP, source of modern FP information and counselling received to start modern FP); modern FP (FP uptake, and reasons for non-uptake). Modern FP uptake (the outcome variable) was defined as use of a contemporary contraception method including: condoms, intrauterine devices (IUD), male or female sterilization, injectables, oral contraceptives and implants [13].

Data analysis

We analyzed data using STATA version 14 (StataCorp Texas USA). We summarized categorical exposure, modern FP uptake, barriers for uptake using frequency distributions. Age was summarized using median, range and inter quartile range (IQR).We determined associations between exposures and modern FP uptake using modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors, and presented effect measures as prevalence ratios (PR) with accompanying 95% confidence intervals (CI). At bivariate analysis, exposures with a P-value ≤ 0.2 were considered for multivariate analysis. At multivariate analysis, exposures with P-value ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. We assessed for interaction and confounding using Mantel–Haenszel statistic.

Ethical considerations

The Office of the Associate Director for Science, US-Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)-Uganda, and the US-CDC human subjects review board determined that this study was non-research aimed at informing MoH policy and actions towards improved PPFP uptake. We obtained a non-research determination clearance from US-CDC who are the funders of the study. The Uganda Ministry of Health and respective RRHs provided administrative clearance before execution of this study. We sought written consent from participants to take part in the study. We ensured confidentiality and privacy by de-identifying the data collected and used password protected computers only accessed by the study team to store the data.

Discussion

Despite the national target of 100% for all post-partum mothers as eligible for FP, the uptake of modern FP was sub-optimal with less than half of postpartum mothers using modern FP methods. While FP programmes promote the modern methods, we found that a few mothers were using traditional FP methods. Mother being employed, partner employment, exclusive breastfeeding and receiving counselling increased uptake of modern FP. Spouse not being around, fear of side effects and not being decided on method, were the commonest reasons for non-uptake of modern FP during the post-partum period.

In our study, we observed suboptimal modern FP uptake. Our findings are similar with those of a survey on modern FP uptake in 68 out of 146 districts in Uganda where uptake was found to be low [14]. However, uptake among postpartum women could be due to the readily available information on FP in urban areas, where the study sites are also located, that encourages mothers to take up FP based on perceived benefits and method of choice [15]. Integration of PPFP services within the continuum of care in Uganda could be considered to prevent closely timed births within the population thereby reducing maternal and infant mortality rates [16].

In this study, mother’s occupation was significantly associated with increased uptake of modern FP among postpartum mothers. Modern FP uptake was 53% more prevalent among employed postpartum mothers compared to the unemployed. This finding is similar to others conducted in sub-Sahara Africa which found that employed women are more likely to take up modern FP [2]. This might be due to inconveniences that come with pregnancy such as sudden leg cramps, fatigue, depression, anxiety, gestational diabetes which may interfere with productivity of employed mothers hence increasing their desire to take up modern FP more readily [3]. In addition, employed women are usually more educated than the unemployed, and often have additional access avenues to information on the benefits of modern FP from their peers, workmates and social groups other than the health facility[2]. Therefore, healthcare workers need to plan targeted interventions such as community sensitization on local media to avail more avenues for unemployed postpartum mothers.

Partners occupation was associated with uptake of modern FP. Modern FP uptake was over 3 times more prevalent among postpartum mothers whose partners had a source of income compared to those whose partners were unemployed. Similarly, a study on factors affecting the use of FP among the youth in Karamoja Region in Uganda indicated that partners’ occupation was highly associated with FP uptake [2].This may be due to the fact that men with a source of income can provide support to their women inform of transport to health facilities and or even pay for any sort of charges that may be required. Sources of income can determine access and willingness to pay for health services including FP. Despite this, male partner involvement in FP services is low in Uganda. Male disapproval of use of FP by their female partners and misconceptions about side effects are barriers to FP in Uganda [17]. Community based approaches to FP sensitization in form of community education campaigns and dialogues, may be an important step towards male involvement in the use of modern FP methods.

Our findings indicated that breast feeding was significantly associated with uptake of modern FP among postpartum mothers. Both postpartum mothers who exclusively and those who did not exclusively breast feed their children had lower prevalence of modern FP uptake compared to those who did not breastfeed their children at all. Similar findings were obtained from studies in Uganda, Sub-Saharan Africa & Asia where breastfeeding was associated with reduced uptake of modern FP [18]. This could be because breastfeeding mothers were aware of the benefits of lactation amenorrhea as a form of a FP method [5]. More still, women who were breastfeeding could have had fears regarding side effects of modern FP such as hormonal methods reducing milk production, or disseminating into milk sucked by their infants [4].

Our findings indicate that receiving counselling about FP increased prevalence of modern FP uptake by three times. This is similar to findings from a national referral hospital in Uganda where women who had received contraceptive counselling during antenatal care were 9 times more likely to take up contraceptives [1]. Counselling on FP leads to an increased likelihood to use contraception [19]. However, in most developing countries where accessing functional facilities is challenging, many women are unable to return for their postpartum visits and thus unable to receive FP counselling to adopt a method [20]. To address these challenges, incorporation of comprehensive and clear information on the benefits and side effects of modern FP during antenatal, postnatal and immunization visits could reduce misconceptions and support informed FP choices, and hence influence mothers to take up modern FP during the postpartum period.

Similar to other medical interventions [21], modern FP methods face barriers in addressing concerns related to side effects [11]. Our study found that a woman’s spouse not being around, fear of side effects and not being decided on FP method were the commonest barriers to uptake of modern FP. In Uganda, FP side effects have been identified as the primary reason for discontinuation of FP use or not using FP in the postpartum period marking this concern as one of the major obstacles to FP use in Uganda [22]. In addition, the fear of side effects and failure to decide on method of modern FP may be attributed to lack of clear information about different modern FP methods. Efforts should be directed towards addressing concerns related to side effects of modern FP.

Study limitations

Our study had some limitations. Firstly, RRHs where we conducted the study, are located in urban areas hence most of the beneficiaries might reside in proximal geographic locations to the RRHs, and might have more access to FP services compared to women in rural areas. This might over estimate modern FP uptake. On the other hand, RRHs are public facilities that are usually utilized by people who are unable to afford private facilities. This might also relate to social class, level of income and health awareness, hence under estimating modern FP uptake. Secondly, we relied on mothers’ self-reports on modern FP uptake without proof of FP card. Due to this, we could have overestimated modern FP uptake.

Conclusion

Less than half of postpartum mothers took up modern FP. Uptake was associated with mothers’ occupation, partners’ occupation, breastfeeding and receiving counseling to start modern FP in the postpartum period. Spouse not being around, fear of side effects and not being decided on the FP method were the predominant barriers to taking up modern FP in the post-partum period. We recommend targeted health education among unemployed mothers and partners on the benefits of using modern FP during the postpartum period. In addition, healthcare workers should educate breastfeeding mothers on the benefits of using modern FP. Furthermore, intensified counselling among post-partum mothers should be conducted to address barriers to modern FP uptake.
