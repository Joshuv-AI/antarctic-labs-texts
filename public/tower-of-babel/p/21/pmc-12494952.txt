Parahippocampal activation reveals countermeasure use in a sentence-based complex trial protocol guilty knowledge test with P900 source localization
Authors: Eun Jin Go, Eunhye Park, Young Youn Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated guilty knowledge detection using the Complex Trial Protocol (CTP) within the Guilty Knowledge Test (GKT) framework. Korean sentence stimuli were employed to examine the effects of countermeasures. The study involved three groups: a simple guilty (SG) group (n = 15), a countermeasure (CM) group (n = 15), and an innocent (IN) group (n = 15). Participants in the SG and CM groups engaged in a mock crime, while the IN group did not. Sentences were structured in a ‘subject-object-verb’ format, with crime-related information embedded in the object. The object element varied by stimulus condition (probe or irrelevant), whereas the verb (‘steal’) was constant across trials. Event-Related Potentials (ERP) analyses revealed late positive activity around 900 ms (P900) for probe verbs in both the SG and CM groups. However, source localization using standardized low-resolution electromagnetic tomography (sLORETA) indicated significant parahippocampal activation exclusively in the CM group. This suggests that the P900 in the CM group may reflect contextual recollection or memory modulation strategies employed to suppress recognition. Overall, the findings indicate that while CTP is effective in detecting guilty knowledge, sentence complexity and countermeasure strategies influence the expression and neural sources of late ERP components.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18076-w.

Introduction

Lying is when individuals deliberately try to deceive others by presenting false information as true1, and this tendency increases in situations where the perceived benefits outweigh the risks of punishment2. Given its legal and societal relevance, deception detection is a key concern in forensic contexts, prompting extensive research into reliable methods for lie detection. One widely used technique is the Event-Related Potential (ERP)-based Guilty Knowledge Test (GKT), also known as the Concealed Information Test (CIT)3,4. This paradigm utilizes the three-stimulus paradigm (3SP), a classic oddball framework comprising infrequent probe stimuli (crime-relevant), frequent irrelevant stimuli (crime-irrelevant), and infrequent target stimuli to maintain attention. The primary goal is to detect differential brain responses that indicate recognition of concealed information.

The P300, a positive deflection around 300 ms, is typically larger for probes in guilty individuals, reflecting involuntary recognition of salient stimuli5. While initial studies reported high diagnostic accuracy6, subsequent findings have demonstrated susceptibility to deliberate countermeasures under certain protocols7–10. However, more recent work has reported accuracies exceeding 84% in advanced CTP paradigms11.

To address this vulnerability, Rosenfeld and colleagues developed the Complex Trial Protocol (CTP), which separates the implicit probe recognition task and the explicit target discrimination task within a single trial12. This dual-task structure was designed to minimize cognitive interference and reduce the effectiveness of countermeasures. The CTP has demonstrated improved resistance to both physical and mental countermeasures, such as covertly recalling specific autobiographical information during irrelevant stimulus presentation12–16. More recent extensions of the CTP framework have provided additional evidence of its diagnostic utility. For example, Deng, et al.11 introduced an item-source CTP variant capable of discriminating guilty individuals from both knowledgeable and unknowledgeable innocents. Similarly, Rosenfeld, et al.17 found that financial incentives did not affect P300 responses in a CTP-based malingering detection paradigm, underscoring its robustness. Lu, et al.18 reported that detection accuracy may be reduced in collaborative crimes, highlighting the challenges of shared memory retrieval in group-based scenarios.

In parallel, a later positive ERP component—P900—has been observed during countermeasure use, particularly when the probe is recognized as an exempt item that does not require a specific response16,19. This component appears around 900 ms post-stimulus and has been interpreted as reflecting higher-order evaluative processing or contextual integration14,16. While P300 reflects recognition of salient stimuli, P900 may represent additional post-recognition processing, especially under complex task demands.

Of particular interest in our study is the parahippocampal gyrus, a medial temporal lobe structure implicated in contextual and episodic memory retrieval. Prior research has demonstrated its involvement in reconstructing detailed environmental and event-related context20,21. In tasks requiring suppression or modulation of memory—such as in deception or countermeasure use—activation in the parahippocampal region has been reported22,23. Thus, parahippocampal activity may serve as a neural marker for cognitive strategies employed during guilty knowledge suppression or contextual recollection.

In this study, we aim to extend previous work by Jung, Kang and Kim24, who implemented a sentence-based GKT in Korean and found that P300 responses varied based on sentence structure. Unlike English, where critical information appears earlier in the sentence, Korean sentence structure places the verb at the end, necessitating full sentence comprehension before a response can be made. Our study replicates and builds upon their work by incorporating the use of countermeasures and applying source localization analysis using standardized low-resolution electromagnetic tomography (sLORETA)25 to investigate neural generators underlying deception and countermeasure use.

We hypothesize that object-related probes will activate parietal regions linked to sensory integration and memory retrieval, while verb-related probes will engage frontal regions involved in decision-making and cognitive control. Furthermore, we expect the P900 component observed in the countermeasure group to be associated with increased parahippocampal activity, reflecting the retrieval and modulation of contextual information. By localizing these ERP components, our study aims to advance the understanding of cognitive and neural correlates underlying deception and to evaluate the diagnostic utility of the P900 in the context of countermeasures.

Materials and methods

Participants

Sixty undergraduates (29 women and 31 men) from an introductory psychology class voluntarily participated in this study. They ranged in age from 18 to 27 [mean = 20.4, SD = 2.09, F(2, 57) = 0.752, p = .476], all right-handed, with normal or corrected vision, and with no history of alcohol and drug abuse, psychiatric disorder, or neurological deficit. In order to assess personal and psychological characteristics of the participants, we collected the following scores: Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)26, Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)26, lie scale of the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory-2 (MMPI-2)27, Machiavellianism Ⅳ scale (Mach Ⅳ)28, and self-monitoring scale29. The participants were randomly assigned to three groups: (a) A simple guilty (SG) group, in which they acquired guilty knowledge through a mock crime that had the participants steal some mail from ‘the Ministry of Justice’ and tear it off to toss it away; (b) An innocent (IN) group, who were simply instructed to make a list of the mail; and (c) A countermeasure (CM) group, which carried out the same mock crime that the SG group had, but then performed mental countermeasures during GKT. No significant group differences were found in either demographic data or self-report scores (Table 1). The final sample included 15 participants per group after applying exclusion criteria to ensure sufficient data quality for both ERP and sLORETA analyses. Participants were excluded if they had fewer than 30 artifact-free probe trials, committed excessive task errors (e.g., > 20% incorrect responses on target trials), or experienced technical issues such as poor electrode connectivity or amplifier malfunction. These criteria ensured the reliability of averaged ERP waveforms and the validity of source localization. The resulting sample size (n = 15 per group) is consistent with prior deception studies using sLORETA, including Jung, et al.24, which successfully identified neural activation patterns with comparable group sizes. The experimental process was thoroughly explained before the participants provided written informed consent. The experimental process was thoroughly explained to all participants, and written informed consent was obtained before the study began. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and the experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyonggi University.

Stimuli

In this experiment, we employed the CTP14, adapting it by using sentences instead of single-word stimuli. Each trial began with the presentation of sentence stimuli, followed by strings of numbers (e.g., ‘11111’ ~ ‘55555’) that served as target or nontarget stimuli (Fig. 1). The sentence stimuli were structured in a subject-object-verb order in Korean, designed to probe crime-related information that would only be known to participants who enacted the mock crime. For instance, the sentence “dang-sin-ee (you) bup–mu–bu-mun-seo-rul (a document from the Ministry of Justice) hum-chyeoss-sum-ni-gga? (stole? )” translates to “Did you steal the document from the Ministry of Justice?” in English.

The elements of each sentence, along with the subsequent target or nontarget stimuli, were displayed in white text on a black background on a computer monitor positioned 1 m from the participants. The screen was calibrated to provide a vertical visual angle of approximately 2.29° and a horizontal angle of 3.4°. In Korean syntax, the verb is positioned at the end of the sentence after the object, contrasting with the typical English sentence structure where the verb follows the subject before the object.

The sentence stimuli were comprised of one probe sentence that contained information related to the guilty scenario, four irrelevant sentences, and four filler sentences. While the subject and verb remained consistent across the probe and irrelevant sentences, the object varied. For example, the object in the probe sentence, “the Ministry of Justice,” was substituted with “the Ministry of Education,” “the Ministry of Gender Equality,” “the Ministry of Unification,” and “the Ministry of Environment” in the irrelevant sentences. The critical crime-related information (probe or irrelevant) was embedded in the object element of the sentence. The verb element (‘steal’) remained constant across all trials. Filler sentences were constructed with a different verb, “see,” instead of “steal,” to ensure participants remained attentive throughout the sentence presentations. Participants were instructed not to respond to the filler sentences, which served as control trials to maintain their focus. To ensure reliable ERP measurements in our study, particularly given the extended and cognitively demanding nature of the tasks, the inclusion of filler sentences was crucial for maintaining participant engagement and mimicking real-world scenarios where irrelevant information often competes for attention, thereby enhancing the ecological validity of the task24.

Procedure and tasks

Scenario assignment

Participants were first brought into the laboratory, where they were asked to select one of two sealed envelopes, each containing either a guilty or innocent scenario. Participants who selected the guilty scenario were divided into two groups: the SG group (n = 20, 9 women, mean age = 19.90 years, SD = 2.20) and CM group (n = 20, 11 women, mean age = 20.65 years, SD = 2.21). These participants were instructed to commit a mock crime, which involved collecting and opening mail from “the Ministry of Justice” addressed to Professor Kim at the registrar’s office. Upon recognizing the names of the recipient and sender, they were to destroy the document by tearing it up.

Participants who selected the innocent scenario were assigned to the IN group (n = 20, 11 women, mean age = 20.55 years, SD = 1.99). They were given similar instructions but were not required to perform any illegal acts. Instead, they were asked to create a list of the mail received for Professor Kim, without opening it.

Countermeasure instructions for the CM group

Participants in the CM group received additional instructions regarding the use of mental countermeasures during the GKT. Following the method suggested by Rosenfeld and Labkovsky14, these participants were trained to silently recall either their own name or their mother’s name when presented with irrelevant stimuli. This technique, known to enhance the P300 magnitude, leverages the impact of autobiographical information on cognitive processing30. At the experiment’s conclusion, CM participants were asked to rate the effectiveness of their countermeasures on a scale from 0 to 10 and identify the irrelevant stimuli they targeted. In the CM group, participants were instructed to apply mental countermeasures to 2 out of the 4 irrelevant items, resulting in 10 countermeasure trials per participant.

Experimental task design

The experiment consisted of four blocks, each containing 85 sentences, including 15 probe sentences, 60 irrelevant sentences, and 10 filler sentences. Target and nontarget stimuli were also presented 85 times per block. The presentation of sentences and targets/nontargets was pseudorandomized, ensuring that probe sentences were not predictably followed by targets. Each participant viewed a total of 60 trials: 20 probe, 20 irrelevant, and 20 target sentences, presented in random order.

Each element of the sentence was presented for 500 ms, with a 700-ms interval between the subject, object, and verb. After the sentence’s final element (the verb), there was a 1200-ms interval during which participants were instructed to respond using a four-button response pad. This response was labeled as the “I saw it” response. Participants were advised to vary their button presses to prevent repetitive patterns and maintain focus. For target and nontarget stimuli, specific responses were required: the leftmost button was designated for targets, and the rightmost button for nontargets, with button assignments counterbalanced across participants.

Practice and memory verification

Before beginning the electroencephalograph (EEG) recording, participants completed a practice block of 12 trials to ensure they understood the task and were fully engaged. For the P300 component analysis, 20 trials per stimulus condition were used. This number of trials was chosen to ensure the reliability and stability of the P300 waveform, following standard practices in ERP research, which recommends around 20–30 trials per condition for robust data31. After the EEG session, participants were questioned about their memory of the mock crime scenario to confirm the validity of the experimental manipulation.

EEG recording

We used 64 Ag/AgCl electrodes embedded in an elastic cap (Neuroscan, Charlotee, USA) to record the EEG. All participants were in a soundproof, electrically shielded experimental room when the EEG was recorded. The scalp electrodes were referenced to the left and right mastoid and embedded according to the international 10–20 system. We monitored eye blinks and eye movements by measuring horizontal and vertical electrooculogram (EOG) with two pairs of electrodes placed vertically on the left eye and horizontally on both sides of the eyes. Impedances were held below 5 kΩ. EEG was continuously recorded with a 0.05–100 Hz analog bandpass and sampled at a rate of 1000 Hz. After recordings were finished, the data were divided into 1300-ms epochs including a 100-ms prestimulus baseline correction period. EEG signals were time-locked to the onset of the object element in each sentence, as this element contained the relevant stimulus information. Among the EEG epochs, if the amplitudes exceeded ± 100 µV, those EEG epochs were automatically eliminated from averaging. Trials with incorrect responses, artifacts, and bad channels were also excluded by means of visual inspection. For each participant, two sentence elements, the object and the verb, were averaged and digitally filtered with a 0.1–30 Hz bandpass filter. Since the subject “you” was the same in all types of sentences, it was not analyzed. One participant in the IN group and three in the CM group were removed from analysis because of excessive eye or head movements.

For both P300 and P900 analyses, a 100-ms prestimulus baseline correction was applied. Despite the P900 occurring later in the epoch (700–1200 ms), this early baseline period ensures that both components are assessed using a uniform and uncontaminated reference interval. This approach aligns with standard practices in ERP research and ensures comparability between components16,31.

To ensure ERP data quality, artifact rejection was conducted through visual inspection and an amplitude threshold of ± 100 µV. This approach aligns with prior ERP studies in deception research, including Jung et al.24, which successfully applied similar criteria in sentence-based paradigms involving sLORETA analysis. Stimulus-locked epochs were baseline corrected using a 100 ms prestimulus window. P300 and P900 components were quantified using fixed latency windows (350–630 ms and 700–1200 ms, respectively), based on established ERP literature31,32 and adapted for the sentence-based complexity of the current task. On average, participants retained 18.1 (± 1.4) probe trials, 18.7 (± 1.0) irrelevant trials, and 19.2 (± 0.9) target trials after artifact rejection.

Source localization

sLORETA source localization was conducted using the standard MNI-152 template brain provided by the software, as individual structural MRIs were not collected in this study. This standard template approach has been commonly used in prior deception and ERP-based studies24. We used a Fastrak 3D digitizer (Polhemus, Colchester, VT, USA) to identify the scalp locations of each electrode. Source localization was carried out using Curry V6.0 software (Neuroscan, Charlotte, USA), in which the template MRI scan was spatially matched to the electrode coordinates. To estimate current density images for each stimulus type, we applied a current source-density analysis method, sLORETA25. Nasion and bilateral preauricular points were used for anatomical co-registration. Nasion and bilateral preauricular points were used for anatomical co-registration of the electrode array to the MNI-152 template brain. Although individual MRIs were not collected, this step improves spatial alignment between the sensor configuration and the standard head model, ensuring more accurate source localization in the absence of subject-specific anatomy.

Independent component analysis (ICA)33,34 was implemented in Curry V6.0 using the Infomax algorithm to identify and remove artifacts such as eye blinks and muscle noise. ICA was performed at 50-ms intervals around the peak mean global field power of the P300 and P900 components. Components with a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) lower than 0.9 were excluded. A three-compartment boundary-element method (BEM) model was also implemented using Curry V6.0, based on the MNI-152 template head model. The BEM model comprised approximately 4000 triangular nodes to model the volume conductor35. Standard tissue conductivities were set at 0.33 for the brain, 0.0042 for the skull, and 0.33 for the scalp. As with the ERP analysis, both object and verb elements were submitted to sLORETA analysis. Current density values were localized using the Talairach atlas36, and residual variances were held below 20%.

Group-level statistical parametric maps (SPM{t}) for sLORETA results were computed using a voxel-wise uncorrected threshold of p < .005, with a minimum cluster size of 20 contiguous voxels, and rendered on the MNI-152 template brain. This cluster-extent approach is commonly used in ERP source localization research to balance Type I and II error risks when sample size is moderate. Cortical regions exhibiting increased current density were identified based on Talairach coordinates36.

Statistical analysis

We used mixed-design analysis of variance (mixed ANOVA) in order to analyze the behavioral data, reaction time, and response accuracy. The stimulus condition (probe and irrelevant; additionally, for the CM group, both countered and uncountered irrelevant) was treated as a within-subject factor, and the groups (SG, IN, and CM) were treated as a between-subjects factor. Target/nontarget stimuli were excluded in statistical analysis since no countermeasure was used.

Behavioral data were collected in the form of reaction times and response accuracy during the task. Reaction times were measured from the onset of the verb stimulus to the participant’s response. Accuracy was defined as the correct identification of target and non-target stimuli. Both reaction times and accuracy rates were averaged across trials for each condition (probe, irrelevant, target, filler) and group (SG, CM, IN). The data were screened for outliers, with reaction times exceeding ± 2.5 standard deviations from the mean being excluded from further analysis using mixed ANOVA, with stimulus type as the within-subject factor and group as the between-subject factor. The significance level was set at p < .05 for all analyses.

The P300 peak amplitude was analyzed within the 350 to 630 ms poststimulus window, and the P900 peak amplitude was analyzed within the 700 to 1200 ms window. These time windows were selected based on established ERP literature, which typically places the P300 component within the 300–600 ms range poststimulus, with an extension to 630 ms to capture any late peaks or variations specific to the sentence-based stimuli used in this study31,32.

ERP analyses in this study focused on mean amplitudes within predefined latency windows rather than peak latency measures, given the semantic and structural complexity of the sentence-based stimuli. A mixed-design ANOVA was conducted on the mean amplitudes, incorporating both within-subjects factors (three electrode sites: Fz, Cz, and Pz, and the stimulus condition) and a between-subjects factor (group). A Greenhouse-Geisser correction37 was applied where appropriate to adjust for violations of sphericity. Statistical significance was set at a level of 0.05 for all effects.

We used SPM (Statistical Parametric Mapping, Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, London, UK; http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) implemented in MATLAB 7.10 (Mathworks, Natick, USA) for the statistical analysis of current density images. Prior to the analysis, we did spatial preprocessing of current density images of each stimulus condition from the sLORETA analysis. During the coregistration process in matching mean current density to the template MRI, we made a voxel-to-voxel affine transformation matrix. We smoothed current density images by convolving an isotropic Gaussian Kernel with 5 mm full width half-maximum, then did spatial normalization.

Based on the ERP results, two-sample t-tests were conducted for group comparisons of the normalized P300 and P900 current density images for the probe stimulus. When doing the two-sample t-tests, we estimated global effects by assuming the mean voxel value. In each test, the grand mean scaled value was 50 and used an analysis of covariance for global normalization. For P300, we estimated significant clusters with a p < .01 (uncorrected) containing at least 200 contiguous voxels, while significant clusters with a p < .005 (uncorrected) containing at least 20 contiguous voxels were estimated for P900. We also did paired t-tests in the conditions that involved global normalization and t-contrasts between images of stimulus conditions, probe (1) and irrelevant (-1). We did global normalization by proportional scaling with a grand mean scaled value of 50. Only significant clusters (uncorrected p < .01) containing at least 20 voxels were estimated for P300, and a p < .005 (uncorrected) with at least 20 contiguous voxels were estimated for P900. The global mean was set as one in each test.

Results

Behavioral results

No significant group difference in response accuracy [F(2, 54) = 1.20, p = .310, ηp2 = 0.042] and mean reaction time [F(2, 54) = 2.22, p = .118, ηp2 = 0.076] was found. Table 2 shows the response accuracy and mean reaction time for each stimulus condition. Even though there was no statistically significant effect in reaction time and response accuracy, overall reaction time in the CM group was longer than were the reaction times in the SG and IN groups. Such increased reaction time might have resulted from the use of countermeasures for the CM group.

In the SG group, probe stimulus elicited the largest P300 amplitudes at Pz, whereas irrelevant stimuli did not, but such a trend was not found at any electrode sites in the IN and CM groups. The P300 in the SG group appeared at around 350 ms and lasted for about 630 ms poststimulus. The P300 amplitude around 350 to 630 ms poststimulus for the object element at 3 electrode sites (Fz, Cz, and Pz) was analyzed by mixed ANOVA. There were main effects of the group [F(2, 57) = 4.57, p = .014, ηp2 = 0.138] and stimulus condition [F(1, 57) = 19.84, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.258]. A significant group and stimulus condition interaction [F(2, 57) = 14.47, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.337] was also found. Such a result indicates that, as Fig. 2 shows, the probe in the SG group elicited more positivity at all electrode sties than the irrelevants did, but the probe in the CM and IN groups did not. P300 peak amplitude in the SG group was largest at the parietal region (at the Pz site) as previous studies reported24,38–40. We did further statistical analysis of paired t-tests for the difference between the probe and irrelevant stimuli at Pz. It showed a significant amplitude difference in the SG group [t(19) = 7.01, p < .001, d = 1.57], but there were no differences in the IN [t(19) = 0.089, p = .390, d = 0.20] and CM groups [t(19) = 0.818, p = .424, d = 0.18]. It seems that the difference of P300 between probe and irrelevant stimuli demonstrates the guilty knowledge because of recognition of the probe. Furthermore, there was no difference of P300 peak amplitude between probe and irrelevant in the CM group, even though the probe was also meaningful for them as well. Figure 3 presents the amplitudes of all stimulus conditions in the three groups. We found that the probe amplitude in the CM group elicited as large as that in the SG group. However, because of the use of countermeasures when irrelevants were presented, the irrelevant amplitudes were also increased. Hence, using countermeasures made it difficult to detect the guilty knowledge from the object element. Although the grand-averaged waveform in Fig. 2 may suggest a subtle negative shift in the 300–630 ms window at Pz, statistical analyses (Fig. 3) confirmed significantly larger mean P300 amplitudes for probe stimuli compared to irrelevant stimuli, particularly in the SG group. This apparent discrepancy can occur due to inter-individual variability in latency and waveform morphology, which may cause the grand-average waveform to appear blunted or shifted. Therefore, the statistical comparison of average individual amplitudes provides a more reliable basis for inference than the visual inspection of grand-average plots alone.

It was expected that P900 peak amplitude would be observed in the CM group, based on findings from previous studies. However, there was no significant difference between probe and irrelevant stimuli in the 700–1200 ms window for the object element in the CM group. (Fig. 2). However, because the probe amplitude elicited larger than irrelevant only in the SG group, a main effect of stimulus condition [F(1, 57) = 7.04, p = .01, ηp2 = 0.110] and a main effect of electrode sites [F(2, 114) = 17.44, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.234] were found. There was also an interaction effect between group and stimulus condition [F(2, 57) = 3.91, p = .026, ηp2 = 0.121]. Based on the ERP results, sLORETA analysis was conducted at 50-ms intervals around the peak mean global field power of P300 and P900 for each stimulus. Significant activation of mean current density for the probe and irrelevant stimuli of the object and verb elements were identified for each group using SPM8. Among the ERP results, there was a significant amplitude difference of P300 only in the SG group for the object element (Fig. 2). Table 3 shows the statistically significant activation of the current density area in comparison between the stimulus conditions within the SG group as well as comparison of the probe stimulus in SG group to that in other groups. Corresponding to the ERP data, because the probe amplitude in the CM group elicited as large a probe amplitude in the SG group, the result indicates that the increases of cortical source elicited by probe P300 in both groups were similar. Hence, both groups contained the same guilty knowledge. Therefore, although the object element was presented, it seems the temporal and parietal lobes are mostly responsible in the SG group as processing the guilty knowledge.

Discussion

In our study, we adapted the CTP to include full-sentence stimuli instead of traditional single-word stimuli to increase ecological validity and explore the impact of more complex stimuli on concealed information detection. This methodological shift to sentence-based stimuli was intended to mirror real-world scenarios more closely and necessitated a nuanced analytical approach that considered not only the P300 response but also its interaction with other ERP components influenced by sentence comprehension. Such complexity was hypothesized to enhance the protocol’s resistance to countermeasures by increasing cognitive load, a theory supported by recent study suggests that complex stimuli can enhance the diagnostic utility of ERP-based lie detection by challenging cognitive and countermeasure capabilities41.

We analyzed the cortical source of P300 and P900 in this study using P300-based GKT with complex trial protocol, in which the P300 component reflected a detection of concealed information, whereas the P900 component was expected to indicate countermeasure use. A Korean sentence was used as a stimulus presented in ‘subject-object-verb’ structure. The ERP data showed that using countermeasures on two of four irrelevant stimuli reduced the difference in amplitude of P300 between the probe and irrelevant of the object element, leading to the difficulty in distinguishing the guilty knowledge in the CM group. In the SG group, on the other hand, the probe stimulus evoked higher P300 than did the irrelevant stimuli in the parietal region. Probe and irrelevant stimuli in the IN group seemed to be processed similarly. Moreover, P300 and P900 elicited by the probe of the verb element in the CM group were increased in the frontal region, compared to the IN group. Further source localization analysis of the P900 component for the verb also found the significant activation increase in the parahippocampal gyrus in the CM group, compared to the SG group.

In ‘subject-object-verb’ structure of stimuli in Korean, the object elements consist of key information from mock crime while the verb had the same meaning ‘steal’. The ERP data in this study showed that the difference in amplitude of probe P300 in the object element was reduced due to the use of countermeasure. This result contrasts with the previous studies that used the CTP which showed resistances in countermeasures12–14. Although the use of countermeasures made it difficult to discriminate guilty knowledge in the CM group via the object element, the probe of the verb element, which was followed by the probe object element, was sufficient to indicate the subject’s guilty knowledge and the use of a countermeasure, respectively.

For the behavior result, all three groups showed similarities in task accuracy and mean reaction time. Although no statistically significant effect was found, in both probe and irrelevant, the CM group showed a trend toward a longer mean reaction time than did the IN and SG groups. This might lead to the careful assumption that the CM group spent more time in responding because of increased demand in the task, corresponding to the previous studies’ finding that a subject’s reaction time can also be used as an index of countermeasure use7,12,42. Nevertheless, Rosenfeld et al.7 mentioned that solely using reaction time as an indicator of countermeasure use can be limited, because it might get affected by a subject’s intent and the level of concentration. Thus, if a subject is well trained with high motivation, reaction time can be shortened.

According to Jung, Kang and Kim24, which is known to be the first research examining cortical activation in the P300-based GKT using Korean sentences, different sentence elements were responsible for distinct patterns of cortical activation. The complement (adverb) and object (noun) were associated with parietal activation, whereas verbs were associated with frontal activation24. Similarly, the between-groups two-sample t-test results for the probe of P300 of the object element in the SG group compared to the IN group identified significant activation increases in the left fusiform gyrus, right superior parietal lobule, right inferior temporal gyrus, left middle occipital gyrus, left middle temporal gyrus, and left transverse temporal gyrus. Temporal P300 sources have also been reported by previous studies38–40,43,44, along with the temporoparietal junction often reported as the P300 source in ERP studies45. Moreover, this study has confirmed the frontal source for probe of P300 of the verb element against irrelevant in the SG group. These results clarify that the parietal and temporal activations for the object element in the SG group reflect attentional resource allocation and context updating processes32 during the guilty knowledge processing. According to Jung et al.24, frontal activation in verb is likely to indicate unique information processing in the guilty group since they encode crim-relevant information in their episodic memory after experiencing a mock crime and plan to respond incorrectly (inhibition response) to the probe stimulus intentionally24.

For the CM group, no differences in cortical activity in the probe P300 between the SG and the CM group was found, nor in the comparison of current densities in probe against irrelevant of P300 of the object element in the CM group. Such a result was expectable based on the ERP result, which showed no difference in amplitudes between probe and irrelevant of P300 of the object in the CM group (Fig. 2). The amplitudes of probe of P300 in the CM group and the SG group were also similar (Fig. 3). The CM group successfully concealed the guilty knowledge from the object element by providing more attention (i.e., using a countermeasure) on irrelevant stimuli. On the verb element, however, it seemed the guilty knowledge was distinguishable where the probe of P300 was significantly larger than were the irrelevants. These results imply that the guilty knowledge affected the P300 evoked by the probe of the verb element in both SG and CM groups, although the same verb (“steal”) was used regardless of the type of the stimulus. Thus, post-processing occurred in both groups after the crime-related word in the form of a noun (hence, the object element in the current study) was processed. As Jung et al.24 has reported the frontal activation for the verb element, it also seems that the frontal region was responsible for the probe stimulus in both SG and CM groups and elicited P300 for the guilty knowledge. Moreover, this suggests that even though participants in the CM group used countermeasures on the irrelevant stimuli, the probe stimulus was still important to them, allowing the P300 to be elicited; therefore, the guilty knowledge can be detected. The significant cortical activity increases for P300 of the verb element in the SG group were observed in the left and right medial frontal gyrus, left and right superior frontal gyrus, and left superior temporal gyrus. However, no difference in activation was found when the probe of P300 of the verb in the CM group was compared to that in the IN group.

As the ERP result of the verb element, both SG and CM groups showed a similar pattern of positivity in amplitude around 700 to 1200 ms poststimulus. However, further sLORETA analysis revealed that the sources of the cortical activation between the SG and the CM group were distinct. Probe minus irrelevant paired t-test within the CM group for the amplitude around 700 to 1200 ms poststimulus of the verb element showed significant activation increase in the left and right parahippocampal gyrus, right superior frontal gyrus, and right middle frontal gyrus. This result also corresponded to the between-groups two-sample t-test. Compared to the SG group, a statistically significant activation increase in current density was observed in the right and left parahippocampal gyrus in the CM group, which indicated that the parahippocampal gyrus was responsible for processing the extra attention required in the CM group, that is, the process in applying a countermeasure and its related cognitive procedure. On the other hand, the source of the activation for the amplitude around 700 to 1200 ms poststimulus in the SG group was found to be mostly in the right and left frontal gyrus (Table 5; Fig. 5). The contribution of the frontal activation for the amplitude around 700 to 1200 ms poststimulus in the SG group might be related to the deceptive response involving working memory, response monitoring, and attentional processes46. Since the sources between P300 and P900 seemed distinct in the CM group, we exclusively identified the positivity of the probe around 700 to 1200 ms poststimulus found in the CM group, P900 component, indicating the use of countermeasures during GKT.

In the domain of memory research, the parahippocampal gyrus is well-recognized for its role in contextual processing, including functions such as recollection, episodic memory, associative memory, and source memory23,47–50. The findings of Tendolkar et al. (2008) demonstrated that activation in the parahippocampal cortex increases with the retrieval of more extensive contextual information, indicating the region’s potential involvement in processing detailed and complex memories. Our study builds upon these insights by investigating the neural underpinnings of countermeasure use during a GKT using the CTP with Korean sentence stimuli. Specifically, the observed parahippocampal activation in the CM group during the P900 component suggests that participants engaged in more sophisticated cognitive processing, likely involving the retrieval and integration of contextual information related to the crime. This is in contrast to simpler recognition processes that might occur in the absence of countermeasures. The necessity to apply a countermeasure on specific irrelevant stimuli while simultaneously recognizing crime-related information likely increased cognitive demands, leading to enhanced parahippocampal activity during the processing of the verb element. This study is important because it provides novel insights into the cognitive strategies employed during countermeasure use, highlighting the parahippocampal gyrus’s role in managing these complex cognitive demands. Unlike previous studies, which primarily focused on the P300 component in deception detection, our research emphasizes the significance of the P900 component and its association with parahippocampal activation. By linking the P900 to advanced cognitive strategies involving contextual memory retrieval, our findings suggest that the P900 may serve as a valuable index for detecting countermeasure use, offering a deeper understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying deception and countermeasures in the GKT framework.

In this study, we expected higher cognitive demands on the CM group than on the SG group (i.e., responding according to the instruction on certain irrelevant stimuli). Therefore, while the CM group used the countermeasure on the specific irrelevant stimuli, they also implicitly recognized crime-related information simultaneously caused them to reconsider the stimuli with more contextual details during postprocessing of the verb element.

Deception is known to involve cognitive control as in inhibiting the truthful response to convert the true information into false information, a lie51–54, and such cognitive control involves executive control and working memory55,56. Thus, according to the sLORETA analysis in this study, the P300 probe stimulus evoked the temporal, parietal, and frontal areas. The temporal source is known to be responsible for the retrieval of memories about episodic and personal experience, especially when preparing to lie51, whereas the parietal and frontal sources are involved in working memory, as in planning how to respond during the GKT. Moreover, P300 originated from the frontal area has been known to represent attention mechanisms, along with P300 evoked from the temporal-parietal area associated with subsequent memory processing32. Working memory was also reported with its association in the frontoparietal cortex55,57,58. Given that, temporal activation in this study may reflect retrieval of complex detailed memories in reasoning about personal beliefs, whereas frontal and parietal activation involves high level of cognitive control and executive functioning. During a deceptive response, the CM group seemed to prepare to lie by retrieving episodic experience (i.e., the mock crime) and be more attentive to the probe stimulus, contributing more cognitive resources.

In summary, this is the first study to examine the statistically significant brain activation of the P900 source in the P300-based GKT using Korean sentences with CTP. It seems that when the countermeasure is used, CTP with Korean can only detect concealed information in the verb, but not the object element. In other words, CTP is not efficient in discriminate guilty group from the object element if countermeasure is used. As expected, different brain sources were associated with probe P900 of the verb element in the CM group, who were instructed to use the countermeasure, compared to the SG and the IN group. The source of the activation of probe P900 of the verb element in the CM group was found predominantly in the frontal and parahippocampal gyrus. Since the CM group implicitly processed the crime-related information and simultaneously performed the specific task of using the countermeasure on certain irrelevant stimuli, the contextual information in episodic memory during the mock crime seemed to be retrieved to ensure that the probe stimulus was the absolute stimulus that did not require the countermeasure, which contributes to the cortical activation of the parahippocampal gyrus. This study also showed the possibility of detecting guilty knowledge even when subjects used countermeasures, by using the P300 component of the verb element in CTP, although the same verb was used for both probe and irrelevant stimuli.

However, several limitations in this study should be noted. First, the number of participants was small and drawn from a population of students, indicating that the results might not be generalizable to other demographics. Additionally, although our sample size was consistent with prior ERP and sLORETA deception studies, modest group sizes may have limited statistical power and robustness. Second, low ecological validity might be an issue regarding whether tearing and discarding the mail that does not belong to one in the mock crime scenario is enough to cause tension in the participants. Third, we used the standard MNI-152 template for sLORETA source localization, as individual structural MRIs were not collected. While common in ERP studies, this limits anatomical precision and should be addressed in future research using subject-specific MRI scans. Fourth, statistical maps in the sLORETA analysis were generated using a voxel-wise uncorrected threshold (p < .005) with a minimum cluster size of 20 voxels. Although this cluster-extent thresholding is typical in exploratory neuroimaging, it may increase the risk of false positives. Furthermore, the use of a modified version of the CTP which involved sentence-based stimuli rather than the traditional single-word stimuli, may have introduced increased cognitive load and complexity that could affect the protocol’s resistance to countermeasures. This adaptation, while designed to increase ecological validity, may have influenced the P300 response in ways that differ from the standard CTP. Furthermore, while we employed a peak amplitude analysis for the P300 component, which is a common approach in ERP studies, it is acknowledged that this method, combined with the 1200 ms response interval, may have limited our ability to capture the full P300 recovery to baseline and any subsequent negative deflections. Additionally, while the P900 component identified in this study shows promise as a marker for countermeasure detection, it is important to recognize that advanced countermeasures may effectively neutralize the probe and prevent the production of the P900 response, potentially diminishing its diagnostic utility. Future GKT studies using Korean sentences could elaborate on more practical manipulations and measures to test the neural mechanisms underlying the higher cognitive demands imposed by such modifications, and how these might impact the detection of concealed information. Exploring alternative or complementary ERP components may also be necessary to enhance the robustness of deception detection protocols, especially in scenarios involving sophisticated countermeasures. Lastly, although the object-related ERP waveform in Fig. 2 resembles an N400-like morphology, we interpret this as reflecting the complex semantic integration required by sentence-based stimuli, especially in Korean syntax where the critical verb appears at the end. This pattern is consistent with findings from Jung et al.24, who reported similar ERP morphologies in a sentence-based GKT. Therefore, the absence of a canonical P300 peak does not negate recognition-related processes; rather, it reflects the demands of linguistic processing inherent in the paradigm. Future studies may benefit from using automated peak detection or peak-to-peak analysis to address inter-individual variability and improve measurement precision. Nevertheless, given the technical constraints and our alignment with previous ERP deception studies, we used a fixed time window approach, which provided consistent comparability across participants.

In conclusion, our study represents a significant step forward in understanding the neural correlates of deception and countermeasure use within the GKT framework. By employing Korean sentence stimuli and the CTP, we were able to demonstrate that the parahippocampal gyrus plays a crucial role in processing contextual information during the application of countermeasures, as evidenced by the P900 component. This finding suggests that advanced cognitive strategies involving contextual memory retrieval are critical in countermeasure scenarios, offering a deeper insight into the neural mechanisms that underlie deception detection. While the P300 component of the verb element was effective in detecting guilty knowledge, the use of countermeasures revealed distinct activation patterns in the parahippocampal gyrus, highlighting the potential of the P900 as a diagnostic tool. Future research should explore the robustness of the P900 in detecting countermeasures, especially in more complex and ecologically valid scenarios, to enhance the efficacy of ERP-based deception detection protocols.
