Butyrate epigenetically licenses sustained epithelial-T cell crosstalk for intestinal immune tolerance
Authors: Tianming Yu, Wenjing Yang, Anthony J. Bilotta, Suxia Yao, Jeong-Heon Lee, Qihong Zhao, Jia Zhou, Tamas Ordog, Parambir S. Dulai, Yingzi Cong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intestinal epithelial cells (IEC) are the primary cell type in direct contact with stimuli from the luminal microbiota, playing a critical role in host-microbe interactions. However, how IECs communicate with underlying immune cells to maintain homeostasis remains poorly understood. In addition, the mechanisms by which IECs sense microbiota-derived stimuli to initiate this crosstalk are not yet fully understood. Here, we demonstrate that oral administration of the gut microbiota metabolite butyrate induces sustained IL-10 production in CD4⁺ T cells, conferring long-lasting protection against intestinal inflammation even after treatment withdrawal. This persistent immunoregulatory effect is also observed in germ-free mice, indicating the establishment of a butyrate-conditioned intestinal environment. By metabolomic profiling, we identify N1-acetylspermidine as a metabolite contributing to the IL-10-inducing activity of butyrate-treated IEC-conditioned medium. Mechanistically, butyrate induces sustained transcriptional and epigenetic activation of the acetylpolyamine biosynthetic enzyme Sat1 in IECs, which catalyzes the acetylation of spermidine to generate N1-acetylspermidine, and this activation persists after butyrate withdrawal. Together, our findings reveal a mechanism by which butyrate sensing establishes persistent crosstalk between IECs and T cells, thereby maintaining intestinal immune tolerance through epigenetic regulation and reprogramming of epithelial metabolism.

Introduction

Well-developed mucosal immune responses to the gut microbiota are critical for maintaining intestinal homeostasis. Among the key regulatory mechanisms, the production of interleukin-10 (IL-10) by T cells plays a pivotal role in suppressing excessive immune activation1,2. IL-10, a potent immunosuppressive cytokine, is produced not only by regulatory T (Treg) cells but also by effector T cells. Notably, IL-10 production by effector T cells is considered a self-limiting mechanism to prevent a detrimental, exaggerated T cell response in the intestines3,4. Polymorphisms in the IL-10 locus confer risk for inflammatory bowel diseases, and a deficiency in either IL-10 or the IL-10 receptor leads to severe intestinal inflammation in mice5,6 and humans7,8. Interestingly, mice with IL-10 deficiency in CD4 T cells, despite intact IL-10 expression in other cell types, develop spontaneous colitis resembling the phenotype in global IL-10-deficient mice, indicating a crucial role for T cell production of IL-10 in intestinal tolerance1. However, the mechanisms regulating IL-10 production by gut T cell production remain incompletely.

Intestinal epithelial cells (IEC) are the primary cell type in direct contact with stimuli from the luminal microbiota and play a crucial role in microbe-host interactions9. While much research has focused on the defense mechanisms and barrier functions mediated by IECs10–12, these cells also coordinate immune responses from immune cells located in the lamina propria and underlying lymphoid tissues. Although we have a good understanding of the various immune cell populations in the gut and the close relationship between the epithelium and commensal microbes, our knowledge of how IECs regulate T cell function during homeostasis and how this regulation is disrupted during intestinal inflammation remains limited. Additionally, the specific stimuli from gut microbiota that IECs recognize to communicate with T cells are not yet fully understood.

Accumulating evidence suggests that nutrition and bacterial metabolites may significantly impact the immune response, particularly in the context of inflammatory disease in the gut and beyond 13–15

. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), which are produced solely by gut bacteria from indigestible, fiber-rich diets, can influence gene transcription by inhibiting histone deacetylases (HDAC) and by binding to metabolite-sensing receptors such as GPR41, GPR43, and GPR109A16,17. SCFAs have been associated with the promotion of Treg development18–20 and the production of IL-10 by effector T cells3,21. Though SCFAs, especially butyrate, can reach high concentrations (millimolar level) in the gut lumen, particularly in the colon, their levels decrease sharply to micromolar ranges in systemic circulation due to significant absorption and metabolism by epithelial cells22–25. While previous studies have shown that butyrate can directly regulate T cell differentiation and function3,21, it remains unclear whether SCFAs also exert indirect effects on mucosal T cells through intestinal epithelial cells.

In this report, we demonstrate that SCFA butyrate promotes a long-lasting effect on IECs, facilitating their crosstalk with T cells. This interaction promotes T cell IL-10 production, thereby enhancing immune tolerance via small molecular metabolites produced by the host. This finding underscores the crucial role of IECs in sensing SCFAs to regulate intestinal immune tolerance by stimulating T cell production of IL-10.

Results

Transient in vivo butyrate treatment induces long-lasting IL-10⁺ T cells and protects against colitis

In previous reports, we demonstrated that oral treatment with butyrate increased intestinal IL-10-producing CD4+ T cells in mice3. To investigate whether this effect persists after treatment ends, we administered butyrate in drinking water to mice for two weeks. This was followed by a two-week washout period during which the mice received regular drinking water (Fig. 1A). We collected lamina propria from the colons to determine CD4+ T cell IL-10 at three time points: baseline (0 week), post-treatment (2 week), and post-withdrawal (4 week). Notably, the frequency of IL-10⁺ CD4⁺ T cells in the lamina propria remained significantly elevated even after butyrate withdrawal compared to baseline (Fig. 1B). Importantly, the proportion of IL-10⁺ T cells observed after the washout period was comparable to that seen in mice analyzed immediately after butyrate treatment (Fig. 1B). This suggests that a transient in vivo exposure to butyrate is sufficient to induce a long-lasting immunoregulatory T cell phenotype. While butyrate also increased the frequencies of IL-17A+, IFN-γ+, and IL-22+ CD4+ T cells immediately post-treatment, these effects diminished after butyrate withdrawal and returned to baseline levels (Supplementary Fig. 1A–C). A similar trend was observed for Foxp3+ CD4+ T cells, which increased after exposure to butyrate, similar to the IL-10+ CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Notably, butyrate also enhanced the population of Foxp3⁻ IL-10⁺ CD4⁺ T cells, reflecting the overall increase in total IL-10-producing CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 1D). We compared butyrate levels in small intestinal and colonic tissues from WT mice under baseline conditions. Butyrate levels were markedly lower in the small intestine than in the colon (Supplementary Fig. 2A), consistent with the established physiology that butyrate is predominantly enriched in the colon25,26. Given that orally administered butyrate is largely absorbed and metabolized by small intestinal IECs, we further asked whether transient butyrate exposure also promotes sustained IL-10-producing T-cell responses in the small intestine. We found that the frequency of IL-10-producing CD4+ T cells was increased in the small intestines from butyrate-pretreated mice compared to the control mice (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Next, we investigated whether transient butyrate treatment protects against colitis. WT mice received butyrate in drinking water for two weeks, followed by a two-week washout period. After this, the mice were subjected to colitis induced by dextran sulfate sodium (DSS), which they received in drinking water at a concentration of 2% for seven days, followed by a three-day recovery period (Fig. 1C). Although butyrate had already been withdrawn for two weeks before DSS treatment, mice that had been pretreated with butyrate exhibited reduced weight loss (Fig. 1D) and lower levels of calprotectin (S100A8/A9) and proinflammatory cytokine (IL-6) secreted from colons (Fig. 1E, F). Additionally, they showed less histological inflammation in the colons (Fig. 1G). Notably, the production of IL-10 by CD4+ T cells in the colonic lamina propria was increased in the butyrate-pretreated group (Fig. 1H). There was no difference in colonic IL-17A+ and IFN-γ+ CD4+ T cells between the butyrate-pretreated and control group (Fig. 1I, J). Meanwhile, there was an increase in colonic Foxp3+ CD4 + T cells in the butyrate-pretreated group (Fig. 1K). To further assess whether this protective effect persists under more severe inflammatory conditions, we performed an additional DSS experiment with a higher DSS concentration (3.5%) (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Even under this more severe inflammatory condition, transient butyrate exposure continued to confer significant protection. This was evidenced by attenuated weight loss (Supplementary Fig. 3B), reduced colonic expression of S100A8/A9 and IL-6 (Supplementary Fig. 3C, D), and lower histopathological scores (Supplementary Fig. 3E). Transient butyrate treatment also increased frequencies of IL-10+ CD4+ T cells and Foxp3+ CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 3F, I) without affecting the frequencies of IL-17A+ CD4+ T cells in the colons (Supplementary Fig. 3G). These findings are consistent with those observed in the lower-dose DSS model. Notably, a significant reduction in IFN‑γ⁺ CD4⁺ T cells was additionally observed in mice exposed transiently to butyrate in the higher-dose DSS model (Supplementary Fig. 3H).

To determine whether the protective effect of transient butyrate exposure depends on IL-10 signaling, we treated control mice and transient butyrate-exposed mice with an anti-IL-10 receptor blocking antibody during DSS-induced colitis (Fig. 1L). IL-10 receptor blockade abolished the protective effect of butyrate, as transient butyrate exposure no longer reduced body weight loss (Fig. 1M), S100A8/A9 (Fig. 1N), and IL-6 expression (Fig. 1O), or histopathological scores (Fig. 1P) compared with the control group. These data indicate that intact IL-10 signaling is required for the protective effect of transient butyrate exposure in this model.

Together, these data suggest that transient butyrate treatment induces long-lasting IL-10-producing CD4+ T cells, thus protecting against colitis in mice.

Altered gut microbiota does not mediate the sustained IL-10⁺ T cell response induced by transient butyrate treatment

It is well established that microbiota can modulate immune responses27–29. We previously reported that butyrate treatment altered the microbiota composition in WT mice, as reflected by differences in β-diversity, whereas bacterial richness (α-diversity) remained unchanged30. To determine whether butyrate-induced microbiota changes are required for the sustained induction of IL-10⁺ T cells, we transferred fecal microbiota from control or butyrate-treated mice into germ-free (GF) recipients (Fig. 2A). Our analysis of bacterial abundance revealed no differences between GF mice colonized with the microbiota from control mice and those from butyrate-treated mice, as measured by universal 16S rRNA gene expression (Fig. 2B). Additionally, GF mice receiving fecal microbiota from either group displayed similar levels of IL-10-producing CD4⁺ T cells in colonic lamina propria (Fig. 2C). To further investigate whether the effect of transient butyrate treatment requires the microbiota, we tested it in GF mice (Fig. 2G). We found that transient butyrate exposure still increased and maintained IL-10 production in colonic CD4+ T cells in GF mice (Fig. 2H). These findings indicate that the sustained increase in IL-10+ CD4+ T cells after transient butyrate treatment can occur in the absence of the microbiota.

Transient butyrate treatment conditions the intestinal environment to promote IL-10+ T cells and suppress colitis

To investigate whether butyrate induces IL-10+ T cells through a direct effect on T cells, we simultaneously administered butyrate and transferred WT CD4+ T cells into Rag1-/- mice (Fig. 3A). We observed an increase in IL-10+ CD4+ T cells in the colonic lamina propria 10 days after the transfer (Fig. 3B). There were no differences in Th17, Th1, and Treg cells between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 4A). To further determine whether a butyrate-conditioned tissue environment contributes to the sustained IL-10⁺ T cell response, we administered butyrate to Rag1-/- mice for two weeks, followed by a two-week washout period. We then transferred WT CD4+ T cells into these pre-conditioned Rag1-/- recipients (Fig. 3C). Ten days after the transfer, the percentage of IL-10-producing CD4⁺ T cells in the colonic lamina propria of butyrate pre-conditioned Rag1-/- recipients was significantly higher compared to untreated Rag1-/- controls (Fig. 3D). Again, Th17, Th1, and Treg cells did not show any differences between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 4B). To further characterize the butyrate-conditioned environment in this model (Fig. 3C), we examined additional cellular compartments in Rag1-/- recipients with or without transient butyrate exposure. We did not observe significant differences in the frequencies of colonic F4/80+ CD11b+ macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 5A) or CD11c+ MHCII+ dendritic cells (Supplementary Fig. 5B) between the two groups. In contrast, transient butyrate exposure reduced the expression of stem cell markers (Lgr5 and Olfm4, Supplementary Fig. 5C) and the Paneth cell marker Lyz1 (Supplementary Fig. 5D), while increasing the expression of the enterocyte marker Alpi (Supplementary Fig. 5E), without affecting the goblet cell markers Muc2 and Tff3 (Supplementary Fig. 5F) or the enteroendocrine cell marker Chga (Supplementary Fig. 5G). These findings are consistent with the known effects of butyrate on epithelial differentiation31,32. In addition, depletion of gut bacteria with antibiotics in Rag1-/- recipients did not abolish the ability of transient butyrate exposure to promote IL-10 production by transferred CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 5H, I).

In our previous study, we demonstrated that SCFAs directly promote IL‑10 production in T cells through signaling via their intrinsic receptor GPR433. To test whether T cell-intrinsic GPR43 is required for the sustained IL-10-promoting effect observed after transient butyrate pretreatment, we used nucleofection to deliver a CRISPR-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex, including Cas9 protein and single-guide RNA (sgRNA), into CD4+ T cells to delete the Gpr43 gene (sgGpr43). We used non‑targeting control sgRNAs (sgCtrl) as a control. The knockout efficiency was validated in Supplementary Fig. 6A. We next co-transferred sgCtrl and sgGpr43 CD4+ T cells into Rag1-/- recipients under three conditions: untreated control mice, mice preconditioned with butyrate before T-cell transfer (indirect treatment), and mice receiving butyrate beginning at the time of T-cell transfer (direct treatment), as shown in Fig. 3E. Gpr43-deficient T cells produced significantly less IL-10 than control T cells in mice receiving butyrate beginning at the time of transfer, but not in mice pretreated with butyrate before transfer (Fig. 3F). These results indicate that the sustained IL-10-promoting effect after transient butyrate exposure is mediated at least in part by a butyrate-conditioned intestinal environment.

To determine whether a butyrate-conditioned environment confers protection against intestinal inflammation, we utilized a CD4⁺ CD45RBhi transfer colitis model. Rag1-/- mice received butyrate treatment for two weeks, followed by a two-week washout, after which they were transferred with CD4⁺ CD45RBhi T cells. The mice were then euthanized three weeks post-T cell transfer (Fig. 3G). Mice that were pretreated with butyrate showed significantly less weight loss (Fig. 3H), decreased secretion of S100A8/A9 and IL-6 from the colons (Fig. 3I, J), reduced histological inflammation (Fig. 3K), and an increased colonic IL-10⁺ T cells (Fig. 3L) compared to untreated controls. The frequency of colonic Th17 cells and Th1 cells was lower in the butyrate-pretreated mice, while there was no difference observed in colonic Th22 cells (Fig. 3M–O). These results indicate that transient butyrate exposure establishes a lasting intestinal environment that promotes IL-10 production in transferred T cells and provides protection against colitis.

Molecules produced by IECs in the sensing of butyrate promote T cell production of IL-10

We speculated that IECs mediate the indirect, long-lasting effects of butyrate on T cell IL-10 production. To this end, we treated murine small intestinal epithelial cells (MSIE), an immortalized epithelial cell line established from the intestines of normal mice33, with increasing concentrations of butyrate (1, 1.5, or 2 mM) for 48 h, then collected the conditioned media (CM). Mouse splenic CD4+ T cells were activated and cultured with either 20 or 30% volume of CM from butyrate-treated MSIE or from untreated MSIE (Fig. 4A). The T cells cultured with CM from butyrate-treated MSIE showed a dose-dependent increase in IL-10 production, as measured by flow cytometry (Fig. 4B). Consistently, IL-10 levels in T cell culture supernatant, measured by ELISA, also increased in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4C). To determine whether the observed effect was due to residual butyrate in the butyrate-treated MSIE CM, we quantified butyrate levels both before and after the culture of MSIE. Initially, butyrate was added to the culture at a concentration of 1.5 mM; however, after 48 h, over 98% of the butyrate was consumed by the epithelial cells, leaving only 16–24 μM in the supernatant (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). When the butyrate-treated MSIE CM was diluted into the T cell culture at a volume of 30%, the final butyrate concentration decreased to approximately 5–7 μM (Supplementary Fig. 7A). To determine if this residual amount of butyrate could explain the observed induction of IL-10, we activated and cultured mouse splenic CD4⁺ T cells with an additional 10 μM of butyrate in 30% MSIE control CM. This low concentration of butyrate was not sufficient to induce IL-10 expression (Supplementary Fig. 7C). In addition, we cultured control and Gpr43-deficient T cells with either butyrate-treated CM. Loss of GPR43 did not impair IL-10 induction by butyrate-treated IEC CM (Supplementary Fig. 6B). These findings suggest that the effect of butyrate-conditioned epithelial supernatant is not mediated by residual butyrate, but rather by soluble molecules secreted by butyrate-treated epithelial cells.

To further validate the role of IECs in mediating the immunoregulatory effects of butyrate on T cells and to explore the underlying mechanism, we utilized enteroids as an ex vivo epithelial model. First, enteroids were isolated from the small intestines of specific-pathogen-free (SPF) mice and GF mice. They were then cultured with or without butyrate for 48 hours, and the CM were collected. Consistent with the results obtained using MSIE cells, CM from butyrate-treated enteroids derived from SPF mice significantly increased the frequency of IL-10-producing CD4⁺ T cells compared to CM from untreated controls (Fig. 4D). Notably, the CM from butyrate-treated enteroids from GF mice produced a similar effect, with no significant difference compared to those from SPF mice (Fig. 4D). This suggests that soluble immunoregulatory molecules secreted by IECs in response to butyrate do not require prior microbial priming. We also directly compared the baseline IL-10-inducing activity of CM from untreated SPF and untreated GF enteroids. CM from untreated SPF enteroids induced a modest but significant increase in IL-10+ T cells compared with medium from untreated GF enteroids (Fig. 4D), suggesting that prior in vivo exposure to microbiota-derived factors can partially prime epithelial cells.

To determine whether the IL-10-inducing activity of butyrate-treated IEC conditioned medium could be explained by nonspecific epithelial injury, we compared butyrate with staurosporine (STS), a potent inducer of epithelial apoptosis34,35. MSIE cells treated with 1.5 mM butyrate maintained viability, whereas STS induced marked apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 8A). Importantly, conditioned medium from STS-treated MSIE cells failed to induce IL-10 production in CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 8B), indicating that epithelial cell death alone does not recapitulate the effect of butyrate. Because enteroids are more sensitive to butyrate than MSIE cells, we next performed dose-titration experiments in enteroids. We found that low-dose butyrate (0.5 mM) did not increase apoptosis in enteroids (Supplementary Fig. 8C), yet CM from enteroids treated with low-dose butyrate still significantly promoted IL-10 production in CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 8D). Together, these data indicate that the IL-10-inducing activity of butyrate-conditioned IEC medium is not simply a nonspecific consequence of epithelial injury or reduced viability.

We next analyzed epithelial programs in both enteroids and MSIE cells after butyrate treatment. In enteroids, butyrate reduced expression of the proliferation marker Mki67 (Supplementary Fig. 8E) and the stem cell markers Lgr5 and Olfm4 (Supplementary Fig. 8F), while increasing expression of the enterocyte-associated markers Vil1 and Cdh1 (Supplementary Fig. 8G). Butyrate also altered several other epithelial lineage-associated markers, including decreased Muc2 and increased Tff3 and Chga, whereas Lyz1 was unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 8H–J). In contrast, MSIE cells showed much more limited responses, with only a modest reduction in Mki67 expression(Supplementary Fig. 8E). Together, these findings indicate that butyrate induces more pronounced epithelial reprogramming in enteroids than in MSIE cells.

Given the known role of GPR43 in IEC-mediated SCFA signaling, we next determined whether GPR43 is involved in this process. Enteroids were generated from WT mice and GPR43-/- mice, and butyrate-treated CM was collected from both. We found that butyrate-treated enteroid CM from GPR43-/- mice did promote IL-10 expression in CD4+ T cells compared to its control; however, it showed significantly decreased effects compared to the butyrate-treated enteroid CM from WT mice (Fig. 4E). This indicates that GPR43 is partially involved in the production of immunoregulatory molecules in IECs induced by butyrate.

To determine whether molecules produced by mouse IECs also affect human T cells, we treated human peripheral blood CD4⁺ T cells with CM from butyrate-treated MSIE. We found that this significantly promoted IL-10 production in human CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. 4F). Additionally, both FOXP3+ IL-10+ and FOXP3⁻ IL-10+ CD4+ T cell populations were increased by the butyrate-treated CM (Fig. 4G). Concurrently, the levels of Th17 and Th1 cells decreased in the cultured human CD4 T cells (Fig. 4H). These findings suggest that the active molecules in butyrate-treated IEC CM have cross-species activity.

To further investigate the functional state of CD4⁺ T cells exposed to molecules from butyrate-conditioned epithelial cells, we performed RNA sequencing on T cells cultured with CM from both butyrate-treated and untreated MSIE. The differential gene expression analysis revealed a distinct transcriptional profile in T cells exposed to butyrate-treated CM (Fig. 5A). This includes the upregulation of GzmB, Il10, Il13, Prdm1 (the gene coding Blimp1), Il5, and Gata3, along with the downregulation of Rorc, Il2, and Foxp3. Heatmap analysis highlighted significant genes associated with transcriptional factors to IL-10 and T cell function (Fig. 5B). Notable increases were observed in Hif1a, Prdm1, and Maf, which encode the transcription factors known to promote IL-10 expression 2,36. To validate these findings at the protein level, we performed flow cytometry to assess IL-10 production and the expression of key transcription factors in CD4⁺ T cells. The results showed that T cells cultured with butyrate-treated CM had a significant increase in IL-10 production in various T cell subpopulations, including IL-10⁺ IFN-γ⁺, IL-10⁺ GzmB⁺, and IL-10⁺ IL-13⁺ cells (Fig. 5C–E). Furthermore, IL-10-producing T cells showed increased levels of transcriptional regulators Blimp-1, MAF, and HIF1α (Fig. 5F–H). Intestinal epithelial cell-derived N1-Acetylspermidine induced by butyrate promotes the development of IL-10-producing T cells

To further understand the soluble molecules responsible for the induction of T cell IL-10 production, we first fractionated the IEC-CM based on molecular weight. Supernatants from butyrate-treated or control MSIE were passed through a 3 kDa centrifugal filter to separate low-molecular-weight (<3 kDa) components from high-molecular-weight (>3 kDa) components. Interestingly, only the <3 kDa fraction from the butyrate-treated MSIE CM retained the ability to induce IL-10⁺ CD4⁺ T cells, similar to the unfractionated total CM, while the >3 kDa fraction had no effect (Fig. 6A). Next, we used liquid-liquid extraction to further separate the <3 kDa fraction into aqueous and organic phases. When mouse splenic CD4⁺ T cells were cultured with each phase, IL-10 induction was only observed in cells exposed to the aqueous phase of the butyrate-treated IEC CM, while the organic fraction showed no activity (Fig. 6B). To assess the thermal stability of the active components, we subjected the <3 kDa fraction of butyrate-treated IEC CM to boiling before adding it to the T cell cultures. The heat treatment did not impair its capacity to induce IL-10-inducing T cells (Supplementary Fig. 9A), indicating that the active molecules are heat-stable. To identify the active molecules within the <3 kDa fraction of the butyrate-treated IEC CM, we conducted untargeted metabolomics profiling. CM from butyrate-treated and control MSIE cells were analyzed by LC-MS. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct differences in metabolite compositions between the two groups (Fig. 6C). Among the significantly altered metabolites, (N1 or N8)-acetylspermidine was notably enriched in the butyrate-treated IEC CM, whereas several metabolites, including AMP and 2’-deoxycytidine, showed significantly decreased levels (Fig. 6D). As expected, butyrate/isobutyrate (4:0) was higher in the butyrate-treated IEC CM, consistent with residual extracellular butyrate after treatment, which is absent in the control CM, highlighting the relative differences. We also performed untargeted metabolomics on intracellular extracts from butyrate-treated and control MSIE cells (Supplementary Fig. 9B). In contrast to the CM, intracellular butyrate/isobutyrate (4:0) levels were reduced in butyrate-treated MSIE cells (Supplementary Fig. 9C, D), likely reflecting active uptake and rapid metabolic utilization of butyrate by IECs. Importantly, (N1 or N8)-acetylspermidine, which was enriched in the butyrate-treated IEC CM, was also significantly upregulated within the MSIE cells (Supplementary Fig. 9C). Moreover, butyrate broadly altered metabolic pathways involving amino acids, peptides, and lipids in IECs (Supplementary Fig. 9D). In contrast, the levels of spermidine and spermine were significantly reduced, indicating that these polyamines are actively consumed or converted upon exposure to butyrate (Supplementary Fig. 9C, D). Together, these findings indicate that butyrate reprograms epithelial cell metabolism and promotes the production of (N1 or N8)-acetylspermidine both intracellularly and in the extracellular environment. Since untargeted metabolomics cannot distinguish between N1-acetylspermidine and N8-acetylspermidine, we utilized targeted LC-MS to quantify each isomer individually. This analysis revealed a significant increase in N1-acetylspermidine, but not in N8-acetylspermidine, in the CM of butyrate-treated IEC compared to controls (Fig. 6E).

Importantly, N1-acetylspermidine promoted IL-10 production in cultured CD4+ T cells (Fig. 6F). Slc12a8 has been implicated in the uptake of polyamine-related metabolites37,38. To further investigate whether N1-acetylspermidine contributes to the activity of butyrate-treated IEC conditioned medium, we generated Slc12a8-deficient CD4+ T cells using Cas9-CRISPR-RNP editing (Supplementary Fig. 10A) and compared their responses with control CD4+ T cells cultured with control or butyrate-treated conditioned medium. Slc12a8-deficient T cells showed a reduced IL-10 response to butyrate-treated CM compared with control T cells (Fig. 6G), supporting a role for polyamine-related metabolite uptake in this response. However, this reduction was only partial, suggesting that additional factors may also contribute to the activity of butyrate-treated IEC CM.

We next compared the effects of N1-acetylspermidine alone, butyrate-treated CM alone, and the combination of both on IL-10 induction in CD4+ T cells. We found that butyrate-treated CM induced IL-10 more robustly than N1-acetylspermidine alone, and that the combination of N1-acetylspermidine with butyrate-treated CM further increased the frequency of IL-10+ T cells compared with either condition alone (Supplementary Fig. 11A). These findings suggest that N1-acetylspermidine does not fully account for the IL-10-inducing activity of butyrate-treated IEC CM and that additional immunomodulatory component(s) are likely present in the CM. Because residual butyrate remained detectable in butyrate-treated IEC CM, we further investigated whether the residual level of butyrate could enhance the effect of N1-acetylspermidine on IL-10. CD4+ T cells were treated with N1-acetylspermidine alone, butyrate alone (10 μM), or the combination of both metabolites. Co-treatment with N1-acetylspermidine and 10 μM butyrate did not further enhance IL-10 production beyond the effect of N1-acetylspermidine alone (Supplementary Fig. 11B), suggesting that residual butyrate in the conditioned media is unlikely to be a major driver of the IL-10-inducing activity observed under these in vitro conditions.

Together, these data identify N1-acetylspermidine as a functionally relevant metabolite enriched in butyrate-treated IEC conditioned medium that contributes to, but does not fully account for, its IL-10-inducing activity.

Butyrate induces sustained transcriptional and epigenetic activation of Sat1 in IECs

To elucidate the underlying mechanism of increased N1-acetylspermidine production, we performed a transcriptional profiling of polyamine pathway-related genes in butyrate-treated MSIE cells and enteroids. Notably, Sat1, which encodes the enzyme catalyzing the acetylation of polyamines spermidine and spermine to form N1-acetylspermidine, was significantly upregulated in both butyrate-treated MSIE cells and enteroids, while Srm, which encodes spermidine synthase, was downregulated (Fig. 7C and Supplementary Fig. 12A, B). Given that Sat1 is a rate-limiting enzyme in the production of N1-acetylspermidine41, we focused our further analysis on its expression. We validated SAT1 protein expression and found that SAT1 protein was markedly increased in both MSIE cells and enteroids following butyrate exposure (Fig. 7D). To assess whether this regulation occurs in vivo, we isolated the primary small intestine IECs from mice used in Fig. 1A by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Quantitative PCR analysis (Fig. 7E) and western blot analysis (Fig. 7F) confirmed a robust and sustained induction of Sat1 expression at both 2 and 4 weeks, suggesting a long-lasting transcriptional imprint of butyrate on IECs. Furthermore, in butyrate-preconditioned Rag1-/- mice before T-cell transfer, we detected increased Sat1 expression in colonic IECs (Supplementary Fig. 13A) but not in lamina propria cells in the absence of CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 13B). We also examined whether SAT1 expression persists in MSIE cells after passaging. SAT1 protein remained elevated in passaged butyrate-treated MSIE cells compared with controls (Supplementary Fig. 13C), supporting that butyrate-induced changes in IECs can persist beyond the initial exposure period. To determine whether butyrate can induce Sat1 in IECs independently of microbiota, we examined Sat1 expression in primary IECs from GF mice following butyrate treatment. Consistent with the findings in SPF mice, Sat1 expression in primary IECs of GF mice was increased at both 2 and 4 weeks after butyrate treatment (Supplementary Fig. 13D), indicating that butyrate can induce this epithelial program in the absence of microbiota. To test whether SAT1 activity functionally contributes to the IL-10-inducing activity of butyrate-treated IEC conditioned medium, we suppressed SAT1 in MSIE cells using diminazene aceturate (DA), an inhibitor of Sat142,43. Co-treatment of MSIE cells with DA and butyrate significantly reduced the ability of butyrate-treated conditioned medium to induce IL-10+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 13E), supporting a functional contribution of SAT1 activity to this response.

To further examine whether this epithelial polyamine pathway contributes to IL-10 induction in vivo, we transferred WT or Slc12a8-deficient T cells into Rag1-/- mice that had been pretreated with or without butyrate (Supplementary Fig. 10B). Under these conditions, the increase in IL-10 observed in WT CD4+ T cells was reduced in Slc12a8-deficient T cells in butyrate-preconditioned recipients (Supplementary Fig. 10C), supporting a role for the polyamine-Slc12a8 axis in butyrate-induced sustained IL-10 production in vivo.

Previous research has shown that spermidine increases CD4+ T cell differentiation to Tregs and promotes IL-10 production44. To investigate whether butyrate also upregulates the polyamine biosynthesis pathway and N1-acetylspermidine, we determined the expression of genes involved in the polyamine pathway in CD4⁺ T cells treated directly with butyrate using our previously published RNA-sequencing dataset. In contrast to our observations in IECs, Sat1, the key enzyme responsible for generating N1-acetylspermidine, was downregulated in T cells following butyrate treatment (Supplementary Fig. 14).

To investigate whether transcriptional changes in polyamine metabolism induced by butyrate were associated with chromatin remodeling, we performed ATAC-seq on primary IECs from the mice described in Fig. 1A. Genome-wide analysis of transcription start site (TSS)-centered peaks revealed a widespread increase in chromatin accessibility following butyrate treatment, which was partially retained after treatment withdrawal (Fig. 7G). Notably, when examining the Sat1 locus, we observed a clear increase in accessibility surrounding TSS at 2 weeks, which persisted at the 4-week mark (Fig. 7H, I). This suggests that butyrate induces a stable epigenetic imprint.

Butyrate is well known for its ability to epigenetically modulate host cellular functions through HDAC inhibition45. To determine whether HDAC inhibition underlies the ability of butyrate-treated IECs to promote IL-10 production in T cells, we treated MSIE cells with or without the HDAC inhibitor (TSA) for 48 h to generate CM. We found that TSA pretreated MSIE CM significantly enhanced IL-10 production in T cells, similar to CM derived from butyrate-treated MSIE cells (Fig. 7J). This suggests that HDAC inhibition is closely involved in the generation of immunoregulatory factors by IECs in response to butyrate. To further determine whether HDAC inhibition alone fully explains the effect of butyrate on IECs, we pretreated MSIE cells with TSA for 2 days, and then treated them with or without butyrate for an additional 2 days. CM collected after TSA pretreatment followed by butyrate exposure further promoted IL-10 production in CD4+ T cells compared with conditioned medium collected from TSA-pretreated cells without subsequent butyrate exposure (Supplementary Fig. 15A), indicating that the effect of butyrate on IECs is not solely mediated by HDAC inhibition.

We next investigated whether other epigenetic regulators, such as Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2 (Ezh2), a histone methyltransferase that catalyzes H3K27me3 and has been implicated in IEC function46,47, might also be involved. Recent studies suggest that epithelial Ezh2 serves as an epigenetic determinant in experimental colitis, with its inhibition leading to reduced intestinal inflammation48,49. We found that Ezh2 expression was downregulated in IECs after exposure to butyrate (Supplementary Fig. 15B). To assess the functional significance of this downregulation, we treated MSIE cells with or without the Ezh2 inhibitor (GSK 126) for 48 h and then collected CM. However, GSK126 did not have a similar effect on inducing IL-10 expression in T cells (Supplementary Fig. 15C), indicating that Ezh2 inhibition alone does not recapitulate the immunoregulatory effects of butyrate.

Taken together, these findings support that butyrate induces sustained epigenetic and metabolic remodeling in IECs, including activation of the Sat1 and polyamine axis, which functionally contributes to the long-lasting IL-10-inducing activity of butyrate-conditioned IECs.

Discussion

Although it has been suggested that gut microbiota play a role in regulating IEC to maintain immune tolerance in the intestines, it is unknown whether such regulation can persist. It also remains unclear whether this regulation is enduring. Additionally, the potential for gut microbiota to induce long-lasting immune tolerance effects in IECs is still unknown. In this study, we demonstrated that the immunomodulatory effect of butyrate, a metabolite derived from the microbiota, on IL-10 production by T cells persists after butyrate supplementation is withdrawn, and that this sustained effect contributes to protection against intestinal inflammation in an IL-10-dependent manner. Our findings further support the notion that IECs mediate this long-lasting effect on T-cell IL-10 production and that this effect can occur independently of the microbiota. CM from butyrate-treated IECs robustly induced IL-10-producing T cells in both murine and human systems. Metabolomic profiling of the IEC CM revealed a significant increase in N1-acetylspermidine following butyrate treatment. Mechanistically, we found that butyrate epigenetically regulates the expression of the polyamine acetylation gene Sat1 in IECs.

SCFAs, particularly butyrate, are well-established modulators of gene expression primarily via inhibition of HDAC45. Butyrate’s HDAC-inhibitory activity leads to increased histone acetylation and transcriptional activation of target genes, including those involved in immune regulation50. Previous studies have shown that butyrate concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 2 mM are sufficient to exert HDAC-dependent effects on CD4⁺ T cells in vitro, modulating their function directly 3,18,21,51. However, in vivo, the majority of SCFAs are rapidly absorbed by IECs, reducing the amount of free butyrate that can directly access underlying T cells. In our current in vitro setting, the residual butyrate present in butyrate-treated IEC CM was approximately 10 μM, which was insufficient to induce IL-10 production when applied directly to T cells. Consistent with this, loss of T cell-intrinsic GPR43 did not impair IL-10 induction by butyrate-treated IEC CM, and Gpr43-deficient T cells showed impaired IL-10 induction only in mice receiving butyrate beginning at the time of transfer, but not in butyrate-preconditioned recipients. These findings suggest that, in this setting, the sustained IL-10-promoting effect observed after transient butyrate exposure is not primarily explained by direct butyrate signaling in T cells. Our findings suggest that butyrate can act indirectly, by reprogramming IECs to produce immunoregulatory metabolites such as N1-acetylspermidine, which in turn promote IL-10 production in T cells. However, our data do not exclude the possibility that direct butyrate action on T cells also contributes, at least in part, to the sustained increase in IL-10 production observed after transient butyrate exposure.

Given that IECs are the first cellular interface with luminal SCFAs, they are ideally positioned to undergo epigenetic reprogramming in response to butyrate. Importantly, IECs are continuously renewed every 3–5 days from intestinal stem cells located deep within the crypts52. The sustained immunomodulatory effects observed in our study imply that butyrate may influence the epigenetic landscape of the stem cells, thereby imprinting long-term changes in epithelial function that persist beyond the period of butyrate exposure. Sat1 expression was increased in primary IECs from both SPF and GF mice after butyrate treatment, supporting microbiota-independent induction of this epithelial program. SAT1 protein expression also remained elevated after passaging butyrate-treated MSIE cells, consistent with persistence beyond the initial exposure period. In addition, although TSA-treated IEC CM promoted IL-10+ T cells less effectively than butyrate-treated CM, subsequent butyrate exposure after TSA pretreatment further enhanced this activity, suggesting that HDAC inhibition contributes to, but does not fully explain, the effect of butyrate. Butyrate also altered epithelial growth and differentiation programs, particularly in enteroids, where it reduced proliferation and stem cell-associated markers while promoting enterocyte-associated features. However, the IL-10-inducing activity of butyrate-conditioned medium was not recapitulated by epithelial apoptosis alone, indicating that this effect is not simply a nonspecific consequence of epithelial injury or altered viability. Further investigation into the epigenetic modifications within intestinal stem cells will be essential to fully elucidate the mechanisms underlying this long-term imprinting. It will broaden the concept of epigenetic memory in the intestinal epithelium, opening new avenues for understanding how transient microbial metabolites can exert durable effects on host immunity.

Recent studies have identified polyamine metabolism as a critical metabolic checkpoint that integrates nutrient availability, autophagy, and translational control to shape T-cell fate and function44,53,54. Upon activation, T cells upregulate polyamine biosynthesis and transport to support proliferation and transcriptional reprogramming 53,54. These metabolites stabilize DNA and RNA structures and promote the translation of activation-related transcripts through spermidine-dependent hypusination of eukaryotic initiation factor 5 A (eIF5A)54,55. Spermidine, in particular, has been shown to promote the differentiation of Foxp3⁺ Tregs by inducing autophagy and enhancing the translation of autophagy-related genes via hypusination44. In contrast, acetylated polyamines, such as N1-acetylspermidine, are generally considered less biologically active due to their reduced positive charge, which weakens their interaction with nucleic acids. This chemical modification increases membrane permeability and facilitates their export from cells, contributing to the dynamic regulation of intracellular polyamine levels 56. Interestingly, our study reveals that N1-acetylspermidine promotes IL-10 production in T cells, suggesting a previously unrecognized immunoregulatory role. One possibility is that N1-acetylspermidine may function as a reservoir form of spermidine, capable of being taken up by T cells and converted back to its active form to support cellular functions such as autophagy and cytokine production. Our data suggest that N1-acetylspermidine partially contributes to the IL-10-inducing activity of butyrate-treated IEC CM. While N1-acetylspermidine promoted T cell IL-10 production, Slc12a8 deficiency only partially reduced the IL-10 response to butyrate-treated CM in vitro and reduced the increase in IL-10 in butyrate-preconditioned Rag1-/- recipients in vivo, supporting a contributory but not exclusive role for polyamine-related metabolite uptake. In addition, butyrate-treated CM induced IL-10 more strongly than N1-acetylspermidine alone, and the combination of N1-acetylspermidine with butyrate-treated CM further enhanced IL-10 production, suggesting that additional epithelial-derived factor(s) are also likely to contribute. While the precise mechanism remains to be elucidated, this finding highlights the potential for acetylated polyamines to contribute to immune regulation and expands our understanding of polyamine dynamics in mucosal immunity.

Although our in vivo data support the involvement of IECs in sustained IL-10 induction in CD4⁺ T cells after transient butyrate exposure, the current experiments do not yet definitively demonstrate that butyrate directly targets IECs in vivo to drive this response. Additional studies using epithelial-specific genetic models will be needed to establish this causal relationship. A limitation of the early 10-day T cell adoptive transfer mouse model is that donor splenic CD4⁺ T cells were analyzed at an early time point after transfer into Rag1-/- recipients, before full accumulation and differentiation within the intestinal environment, which likely contributed to the relatively low frequency of IL-10⁺ T cells.

In summary, our data support a model in which butyrate epigenetically reprograms the polyamine metabolic pathway in IECs, leading to sustained production and export of N1-acetylspermidine. Together with other epithelial-derived factor(s), this metabolite supports a long-lasting enhancement of IL-10 production by gut-resident T cells, thereby promoting immune tolerance and contributing to intestinal homeostasis. Although additional studies will be required to define the full set of mediators and the precise mechanism underlying this persistence, these findings reveal a mechanism by which microbial metabolites shape host immune responses through epithelial-immune cell interactions.

Methods

Mice

C57BL/6 J wild-type (WT), B6.129S7-Rag1tm1Mom/J, B6.Cg-Tg(Prdm1-EYFP)1Mnz/J, and B6.SJL-Ptprca Pepcb/BoyJ (CD45.1) mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory. Gpr43-/- (Ffar2tm1Lex) mice were obtained from Bristol-Myers Squibb. All mice were maintained in specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facilities at the University of Texas Medical Branch or the Center for Comparative Medicine at Northwestern University. Germ-free (GF) C57BL/6NTac mice were bred and maintained in dedicated GF facilities at the University of Texas Medical Branch or the Center for Comparative Medicine at Northwestern University. Mice were used at 8–12 weeks of age. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of either the University of Texas Medical Branch or Northwestern University. Both genders were used in this study. Within each in vivo experiment, mice were age- and sex-matched, and treatment and control groups consisted of mice of the same sex.

Mouse model

Butyrate treatment

WT, GF, or Rag1-/- mice were administered 200 mM sodium butyrate (Cat# 303410, Sigma-Aldrich) in drinking water for 2 weeks. Control mice were given regular drinking water. For the butyrate regimen, the 2‑week supplementation period was followed by a two-week washout period during which they were provided with regular drinking water.

Antibiotic treatment

Rag1-/- mice were administered a broad‑spectrum antibiotic cocktail in drinking water, including 1 g /L ampicillin (Cat# BP1760-25, Fisher Bioreagents), 1 g /L kanamycin (Cat# BP906-5, Fisher Bioreagents), 0.5 g /L vancomycin (Cat# 296990050, ACROS), and 1 g/L metronidazole (Cat# 210341000, ACROS). To achieve efficient depletion of the gut microbiota, mice were fed with an antibiotic cocktail in drinking water for 2 weeks before the start of the experiment, and antibiotic treatment was maintained continuously until the end of the experiment.

Fecal microbiota transplants

WT mice were treated with or without 200 mM sodium butyrate in drinking water for 2 weeks. Fecal samples (100 mg) were collected from each group and suspended in 1 mL of PBS containing 0.05% L-Cysteine hydrochloride (Cat# C1276, Sigma-Aldrich), followed by thorough homogenization. The homogenates were centrifuged, and the supernatants were filtered through a 70-µM cell strainer to remove particulate matter. All procedures were performed on ice to preserve microbial viability. GF recipients were colonized via oral gavage with 200 μL of freshly prepared bacterial suspension once a week for 2 consecutive weeks. Mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation 3 weeks after initial gavage.

CD4+ T cells adoptive transfer model

Splenic CD4+ T cells were isolated from WT or CD45.1 mice using magnetic separation and transferred into Rag1-/- mice (1 × 106/mouse) by tail vein injection. Mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocationat ten days post-transfer.

DSS-induced colitis model

WT mice were administered 2% or 3.5% (w/v) DSS (Cat# 160110, MP Biomedicals) in drinking water for 7 days and followed by an additional 3 days of regular drinking water. For in vivo blocking of IL-10/IL-10R signaling, mice were intraperitoneally treated with anti-IL-10R antibody (25 mg/kg, Bioxell, Cat# #BE0050, Clone#1B1.3 A) every other day throughout the DSS treatment and recovery phase.

CD45RBhi T cell-induced colitis model

Splenic CD4+ T cells from WT mice were labeled with anti-PE/CD45RB antibody, and then the CD45RBhi subset (gated top 10% of CD45RB expression) was sorted using a Sony SH800 Cell Sorter, then transferred into Rag1-/- mice (2 × 105/mouse) by tail vein injection. Mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation at three weeks post-transfer.

Tissue processing and histology

Mouse colons were prepared using the Swiss-roll technique, followed by fixation, dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at 5 µm thickness. Sections were mounted onto glass slides, rehydrated through graded alcohols, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological analysis. Histopathological scores were determined by summing individual scores across multiple parameters. For the DSS-induced colitis model, scoring was based on two parameters (maximum score: 13): inflammatory cell infiltrate, submucosal change, architecture, and affected area. For the T cell transfer-induced colitis model, pathology scoring was based on six parameters (maximum score: 15): lamina propria inflammation, goblet cell loss, abnormal crypt architecture, crypt abscesses, mucosal erosion/ulceration, and submucosal changes.

Mouse CD4+ cells isolation and culture

Mouse spleens were mechanically dissociated and passed through a 100-µM cell strainer. After lysing the red blood cells, the cells were incubated with anti-mouse CD4 magnetic beads (Cat# 551539, BD Biosciences) for 30 minutes. CD4+ T cells were then isolated using magnetic separation. Purified CD4+ T cells were cultured in an RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin, sodium pyruvate, β-mercaptoethanol, and 10% FBS at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were activated in anti-mouse CD3 antibody (Cat# BE0001-1, Bio X Cell, 5 μg/mL)-coated plates in the presence of soluble anti-mouse CD28 antibody (Cat# BE0015-1, Bio X Cell, 2 μg/mL) under neutral conditions.

CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene knockout

Mouse CD4⁺ T cells were electroporated with ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes consisting of Cas9 protein and guide RNAs using the P3 Primary Cell 96-well Nucleofector Kit (Cat# V4SP-3096, Lonza) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, Cas9 protein (Alt-R S.p. Cas9 Nuclease V3, Cat# 1081059, Integrated DNA Technologies) was complexed with single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting the indicated genes and incubated at room temperature for 10 min to allow RNP formation. CD4+ T cells were resuspended in 20 µL of P3 nucleofector solution containing the RNP complexes and electroporated using the Lonza 4D‑Nucleofector X Unit with program DN100. Knockdown efficiency was validated by Western blot. Target-specific sgRNAs were synthesized from Synthego and non‑targeting control sgRNAs were purchased from the same vendor. All sgRNA sequences are provided in the Supplementary Table 1.

Mouse small intestinal epithelial cell culture and conditioned medium preparation

Mouse small intestinal epithelial (MSIE) cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 5 ng/mL murine IFN-γ (Cat# 575306, Biolegend), Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium /ITS (Cat# 25-800-CR, Corning), penicillin/streptomycin, and 5% FBS at 33 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For experimental treatments, cells were shifted to 37 °C. MSIE cells were cultured with sodium butyrate, staurosporine (Cat# 81590, Cayman), diminazene aceturate (Cat# 25-800-CR, Cayman), Trichostatin A (Cat# HY-15144, MedChemExpress) at the indicated concentrations and time points, as described in the figure legends.

Conditioned medium (CM) was collected after 48 hours of culture. To fractionate CM by molecular weight (<3 kDa and >3 kDa), samples were centrifuged at 4000 × g for 60 min at room temperature using Amicon® Ultra-15 3 K centrifugal filters (UFC9003, Millipore), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The >3 kDa fraction was retained above the filter, while the <3 kDa fraction passed through. The >3 kDa fraction was recovered by rinsing the filter with RPMI medium to match the original CM volume. For liquid-liquid extraction of the <3 kDa CM, chloroform and methanol were added to create a biphasic system (CM:methanol:chloroform = 1:1:2). Samples were vortexed and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 8 min at room temperature to promote phase separation. The upper aqueous phase (containing polar metabolites) and the lower organic phase (containing lipids) were carefully collected. The organic phase was evaporated to dryness using a rotary evaporator and reconstituted in RPMI medium to the original CM volume. For heat treatment assays, <3 kDa CM was incubated at 98 °C for 2 h in a heat block. All CM preparations were sterile filtered through a 0.22 µM filter before use.

Mouse enteroid isolation and culture

Jejunal segments (approximately 10 cm) were dissected from mice, flushed with ice-cold PBS, and minced. Tissue fragments were washed and incubated in dissociation buffer (PBS containing 2 mM EDTA, 10 mM HEPES, and 10% FBS) at 4 °C for 45 min. After washing, crypts were released using a sucrose/sorbitol-based shaking buffer and filtered through a 70-μM strainer. Crypts were counted, pelleted by centrifugation, and resuspended in a 1:1 mixture of Matrigel (Cat# 354277, Corning) and Intesticult medium (Cat# 06005, STEMCELL Technologies). Approximately 500 crypts were seeded per well in 24-well plates and incubated at 37 °C. After Matrigel polymerization, wells were overlaid with culture medium, which was refreshed every 2–3 days. For passaging, enteroids were dissociated with TrypLE Express (Cat# 12604013, ThermoFisher) and reseeded at a density of 3 × 10⁴ cells per well. One day after the first passage, enteroids were ready for experimental treatments.

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cell isolation and human CD4+ T cell activation

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from healthy volunteers using EDTA-coated collection tubes. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants under Institutional Review Board-approved protocols STU00203172 and STU00221354. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation with Ficoll-Paque. PBMCs were cultured in an RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin, sodium pyruvate, β-mercaptoethanol, and 10% FBS. For activation, cells were stimulated with Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Cat# 11131D, ThermoFisher) in the presence of 20 ng/mL rhIL-2 (Cat# 589106, Biolegend).

Small intestinal epithelial cells isolation

Mouse small intestines were excised and sliced into 1 cm pieces. After washing with ice-cold PBS, the tissues were incubated with precooled 5 mM EDTA and 1 mM DTT (Cat# R0861, ThermoFisher) in PBS buffer, gently agitated in the rotor in the cold room for 1 h. Cell suspensions were isolated from the interface between 20 and 40% Percoll layers. After centrifuging, the cell pellets were incubated in 2 mL prewarmed TrypLE Express for 3 min. After enzymatic dissociation, cells were stained with anti-PE-Cy7/CD31, anti-PE-Cy7/TER-119, anti-PE-Cy7/CD45.2, and anti-APC/EpCAM antibodies. Purified mature IECs were sorted as APC⁺ PE-Cy7- populations using a Sony SH800 Cell Sorter.

Lamina propria cells isolation

Mouse colons and small intestines were isolated and washed with PBS. Tissue was incubated with 2.5 mM EDTA in PBS with agitation for 30 min at 37 °C. The remaining tissues were washed and then digested in dissociation buffer (0.5 mg/mL Collagenase IV and 2 µg/mL DNase in RPMI 1640 medium) using the gentleMACSTM Dissociator system (Miltenyi Biotec). Cell suspensions were filtered through a 100-µM cell strainer, and lamina propria cells were separated from the interface between the 40 and 75% Percoll layers.

Flow cytometry

For analysis of myeloid populations in the colonic lamina propria, cells were stained with a Live/Dead viability dye followed by surface marker staining. For assessment of cytokine production and transcription factor expression, cells were stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (50 ng/mL, Cat# P8139, Sigma-Aldrich) and ionomycin (750 ng/mL, Cat# I24222, ThermoFisher) for 2 h, followed by treatment with GolgiStop (1 μL/mL, Cat# 554724, BD Biosciences) for an additional 3 h to inhibit cytokine secretion. Cells were then washed with PBS and stained with a Live/Dead viability dye and appropriate surface markers. For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were fixed using IC Fixation Buffer (Cat# 88-8824-00, Invitrogen) for 30 min, washed, and incubated with cytokine-specific antibodies in Permeabilization Buffer. For combined intracellular cytokine and nuclear transcription factor staining, cells were fixed, permeabilized, washed, and stained using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Cat# 00-5523-00, Invitrogen). MSIE cells and enteroids were dissociated, washed with PBS, and examined for apoptosis using APC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with PI (BioLegend, Cat# 640932). All samples were acquired using the BD Symphony A5 flow analyzer and analyzed by FlowJo software version 10. Antibody information is provided in the reporting summary. Gating strategies are shown in Supplementary Fig. 16. The applications and working dilutions of all antibodies are summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Mouse fecal pellets were collected and homogenized at a concentration of 20 mg/mL in a collection buffer (PBS containing 25 mM EDTA and 0.05 mg/mL soybean trypsin inhibitor). The resulting supernatant was used to quantify calprotectin levels using the Mouse S100A8/S100A9 Heterodimer DuoSet ELISA kit (Cat# DY8596-05, R&D Systems), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For IL-6 measurement, colonic punch biopsies were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ for 24 h. The culture supernatant was then analyzed using the ELISA MAX™ Standard Set Mouse IL-6 kit (Cat# 431301, BioLegend). Briefly, samples were added to plates pre-coated with capture antibodies and incubated for 2 h. After washing, the plates were incubated with the detection antibodies, followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary reagents. Signals were developed using TMB substrates, and the reaction was stopped by adding the stop solution. Optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a BioTek Synergy HTX Multimode Reader.

Western blot

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer containing protease, phosphatase inhibitors, and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. Protein levels were quantified with a BCA kit. Samples were loaded onto Bio-Rad Mini-PROTEIN TGX precast gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated with anti-mouse SAT1 antibody (1:2000, Cat# 83319-1-RR, Proteintech), anti-GPR43/FFAR2 polyclonal Antibody (1:200, Thermofisher, Cat# AFR-032-200UL), or anti-SLC12A8 polyclonal Antibody (1:500, Proteintech, Cat# 12242-1-AP) in TBS with 0.5 % (v/v) Tween 20 (TBST) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Following incubation with ECL substrate, chemiluminescent signals were generated on the membranes and subsequently captured using a ChemiDoc imaging system. Membranes were stripped using Restore™ PLUS Western Blot Stripping Buffer to remove previously bound antibodies. After stripping, membranes were re-blocked and incubated with β-Actin (D6A8) Rabbit mAb (1:2000, Cat# 8457 L, CST). Signal intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to β-actin to control for protein loading. The applications and working dilutions of all antibodies are summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Fecal DNA was extracted from mouse samples using the MoBio PowerFecal kit (Cat# 12830-50, MoBio) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 100 ng of extracted DNA was used for amplification of bacterial 16S rRNA genes via universal primers by real-time PCR. The abundance of 16S rRNA genes was quantified and normalized relative to the SPF mice group. Total RNA was extracted from MSIE cells and enteroids using TRIzol reagent (Cat# 15596026, ThermoFisher) and then reverse-transcribed into cDNA (Cat# 95047-500, Quanta Bio) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene expression was quantified by real-time PCR and normalized to β-actin expression. Primer sequences are provided in the Supplementary Fig. 2.

Quantification of residual butyrate in conditioned medium by LC-MS

Residual butyrate levels in CM from butyrate-treated MSIE cells were quantified using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) following derivatization with 3-nitrophenylhydrazine (3-NPH). A standard curve was generated using butyric acid analyte solutions at ten different concentrations. D7-butyric acid was used as an internal standard and added to all samples and standards before derivatization. LC-MS data were analyzed using MultiQuant software (SCIEX), and butyrate concentrations were normalized to the volume of CM.

Semi-quantification of Acetyl-spermidine in Conditioned Medium by LC-MS/MS

CM samples were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography coupled to triple-quadruple tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS). Selective reaction monitoring (SRM) was performed for the protonated precursor ions and corresponding product ions of N1‑acetylspermidine (m/z 188 → 100) and N8‑acetylspermidine (m/z 188 → 114). Peak areas were integrated, and data acquisition and analysis were performed using Xcalibur 4.1 and TraceFinder 4.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively.

Bulk RNA-seq

MSIE cells (6 × 105) were treated with or without 2 mM butyrate for 12 h. Enteroids were exposed to 1.5 mM butyrate or a vehicle control for 24 h. Mouse splenic CD4+ T cells (4 ×105) were treated with 30% CM derived from MSIE cells with 2 mM butyrate or control for 36 h. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA samples were submitted to Novogene Co., Ltd. (Sacramento, CA, USA) for standard mRNA sequencing Briefly, poly(A)⁺ mRNA was enriched, fragmented, and reverse-transcribed into cDNA, followed by adapter ligation and PCR amplification. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw FASTQ files were processed to remove low-quality reads and adapter sequences, resulting in high-quality clean reads (Q30 > 93%). Clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38/mm10) using a well-annotated reference index.

Untargeted metabolomics

MSIE cells and <3 kDa fraction of MSIE conditioned media were submitted to Metabolon Inc. (Durham, NC, USA) for untargeted metabolomic profiling using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). Sample preparation was conducted on an automated MicroLab STAR® system (Hamilton Company). Recovery standards were added for quality control, and proteins were precipitated with methanol under vigorous shaking. The resulting extracts were fractionated for analysis under various chromatographic and ionization conditions. Extracts were analyzed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system coupled to a Thermo Scientific Q-Exactive high-resolution mass spectrometer equipped with a heated electrospray ionization source and Orbitrap mass analyzer operating at 35,000 mass resolution. Four independent analytical methods were employed: two reverse-phase (RP) methods in positive ion mode optimized for hydrophilic and hydrophobic compounds, one RP method in negative ion mode, and one hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) method in negative ion mode. Raw data were processed using Metabolon’s proprietary software for peak detection, identification, and quantification. Metabolites were identified by matching retention indices, accurate mass (±10 ppm), and MS/MS spectra against a reference library containing over 5400 authenticated standards and recurrent unknowns. Quantification was performed based on peak area integration, and data were normalized using run‑day median normalization to correct for instrument variability. For MSIE cell pellet samples, metabolite intensities were further batch‑normalized, missing values corresponding to blanks were imputed, normalized to total protein content as determined by a Bradford assay, and natural logarithm transform before analysis. For MSIE culture medium samples, data were batch‑normalized, missing values were imputed, and natural logarithm transform without protein normalization. Rigorous quality control measures included pooled matrix samples, process blanks, and internal standards to monitor extraction efficiency, chromatographic performance, and mass accuracy.

ATAC-seq

1 × 106 IECs were sorted from the small intestines of mice with butyrate regimen. ATAC-seq was performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Sacramento, CA, USA) following their standard assay for the transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing workflow. Briefly, nuclei were isolated from sorted IECs and incubated with Tn5 transposase to fragment accessible chromatin and simultaneously insert sequencing adapters. The resulting DNA fragments were purified and PCR-amplified to generate sequencing libraries. Libraries were quality-checked using Bioanalyzer and quantified before paired-end sequencing on the HiSeq/NovaSeq platform. Raw reads were filtered to remove adapter contamination and low-quality sequences, yielding high-quality clean reads. Clean reads were mapped to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38/mm10) using standard alignment parameters. Peak calling was performed using MACS2, and differential chromatin accessibility was analyzed according to Novogene’s bioinformatic pipeline.

Bioinformatics analysis

All bioinformatics analyses and data visualizations were performed in R 4.4.0.

For RNA-seq data, raw read counts were normalized and variance-stabilized using the rlog transformation in DESeq2. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with DESeq2 using an adjusted p value threshold and log₂ fold change cutoffs as indicated in the figure legends. Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2 to display DEGs, and heatmaps were produced based on Z-scored rlog-transformed expression values using pheatmap.

For untargeted metabolomics data, normalized metabolite intensities were analyzed for differential abundance using the limma package. Differential abundance analysis was performed on natural log-transformed metabolite intensities using limma linear modeling with empirical Bayes moderation. Fold-change estimates were converted to log2 scale for visualization. Significantly altered metabolites were defined as adjusted P < 0.05 together with the absolute fold-change cutoffs indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Principal component analysis (PCA) and volcano plots were generated with ggplot2 to visualize sample clustering and significantly altered metabolites. Pathway enrichment and hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated using pheatmap and clusterProfiler.

For ATAC-seq data, fragment-extended and normalized signal bigWig files were generated, and group-wise average matrices were constructed using rtracklayer. Aggregated ATAC-seq signals surrounding transcription start sites (TSSs; ±3 kb) of expressed genes were visualized as heatmaps using EnrichedHeatmap. Genomic signal tracks at the Sat1 locus (±5 kb) were visualized and exported using IGV, and average signal intensity profiles across the TSS ± 3 kb region of Sat1 were plotted using ggplot2.
