Desktop Fabrication of Plasmonic Nanoantennas with 10 nm Precision Using Field-Emission Scanning Probe Lithography
Authors: Yufan Jin, Jie Bian, Likang Wang, Zhenda Lu, Yan-Qing Lu, Weihua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We demonstrate a precise, stable, and cost-effective method for fabricating plasmonic nanostructures using scanning probe lithography (SPL). This approach enables the accurate and reproducible fabrication of plasmonic nanoantennas with line widths down to 40 nm and gap size approximately 25 nm. Furthermore, geometric parameters such as length and width can be continuously tuned with a precision of 10 nm. This method eliminates the need for expensive vacuum electron beam lithography equipment and can be performed using a simple, desktop ambient atomic force microscope (AFM). Both the materials and the pattern transfer processes utilized are standard, providing a novel, convenient, and low-cost pathway for the fabrication of nanophotonic devices.

Introduction

With the rapid development of micro- and nano-optics, the dimensions of optical structures and devices have entered the submicron regime. Specifically, for plasmonic and metasurface devices, their performance is extremely sensitive to structural geometric dimensions, often demanding fabrication precisions down to the 10 nm level. , Today, the customized fabrication of these plasmonic nanodevices relies heavily on expensive vacuum equipment such as electron beam lithography (EBL) and focused ion beam (FIB) milling, which considerably limits the wide application of the technique. The development of novel, low-cost, convenient, and high-precision compact direct-writing technologies has therefore become increasingly crucial.

AFM-based SPL represents an important alternative technological pathway, as it enables lithography under ambient atmospheric conditions using desktop-scale equipment at a low-cost. Its patterning line width depends on the tip apex size, allowing for sub-50 nm resolution (even down to sub-10 nm). , The morphological scanning capabilities of scanning probe microscopy (SPM) provide in situ characterization, greatly enhancing system usability and stitching accuracy. Meanwhile, the cost of current commercial AFMs can be as low as 30,000 USD, while the cost of open-source AFM systems is even lower (∼1000 USD). Over the past decades, various SPL techniques have been developed. ,− Some can be directly integrated into current nanofabrication workflows, such as thermal scanning probe lithography (t-SPL), which can carve 2.5D patterns into a resist and use it as a mask to transfer patterns into functional material layers. However, the positive resist scheme typically used in t-SPL requires cumbersome, multiple processing steps for nanophotonic structures like nanoantennas.

Another promising alternative is field-emission SPL (FE-SPL), which is analogous to EBL in both principle and function. It achieves localized exposure and subsequent patterning by directly injecting electrons into the resist via a conductive tip. FE-SPL operates without the need for a high-vacuum environment, and the incident electron energy is typically low. This helps suppress the proximity effect and mitigates damage to sensitive substrates, offering a viable alternative to EBL for the facile fabrication of nanostructures. However, because thinner resists are advantageous for achieving smaller line widths and increasing the resist thickness can destabilize the field-emission process, previous reports indicate that the resist layers employed in FE-SPL are typically very thin, generally around 10 nm. , Such thin resist layers are inadequate for subsequent etching processes, which restricts the application of FE-SPL in the fabrication of functional device structures. To address this challenge, this study explores the FE-SPL exposure process using thicker resists (40 nm and above) along with the subsequent pattern transfer techniques, and demonstrates its application in the manufacturing of plasmonic nanoantennas.

Experimental Section

Figure illustrates the fabrication process of plasmonic nanostructures based on FE-SPL. The workflow is similar to that of standard EBL, encompassing the main steps of film deposition, spin-coating, pattern writing, development and pattern transfer (etching). The primary distinction is the use of a conductive probe instead of an electron beam during the patterning step.

FE-SPL based Fabrication of plasmonic nanoantennas. (a) Process flow. (b) System diagram of the AFM used in this work. (c) SEM image of a typical gap antenna fabricated with FE-SPL technique. (d) The scattering spectrum of the antenna in c.

FE-SPL is compatible with a wide range of resists, ,, as many resists can undergo cross-linking reactions under the influence of strong localized electric fields. In this study, we employed a phenolic resin-based negative-tone resist (AR-N 7520, Allresit). The target resist thicknesses were obtained by diluting the solvent to various concentrations and adjusting the spin-coating speeds. Prior to use, the resist was baked at 85 °C for 3–5 min to remove residual solvent. Since the height of plasmonic antennas is generally 40 nm or more, the resist thickness was also set above 40 nm to ensure the precise transfer of patterns into the plasmonic layer via etching. However, because excessively thick resist significantly increases the required writing voltage and degrades stability, the resist thickness was kept below 70 nm in our experiments.

Pattern writing was performed using an NT-MDT AFM system (NTEGRA Prima, NT-MDT, Russia) operating in contact mode. Commercial conductive probes (HA_CNC/W2C+, ScanSens, tip apex radius ≈ 35 nm) were utilized. Because the direct-writing process requires a relatively high bias voltage to drive field-emission electrons into the resist to induce cross-linking, , a high-voltage amplifier was connected between the low-voltage output of the controller (0–8 V) and the tip apex (Figure (b)). The specific direct-writing parameters in actual operation, such as scanning speed and writing voltage, must be optimized based on parameters like resist thickness and substrate conductivity. Typical operating voltages range from 20 to 60 V, with scanning speeds between 4 and 10 μm/s.

After the patterning and development steps, the remaining resist structures served as etching masks, and the patterns were transferred into the gold film layer via ion beam etching (IBE). Experimental measurements indicated that the etching selectivity ratio between the gold film and the resist was approximately 2:1; meaning the etching rate of the gold film was roughly twice that of the resist. Following the metal layer etching, a small amount of residual resist on top of the antennas was removed using an inductively coupled plasma (ICP) process, yielding the final gold nanostructures. Figure (c),(d) illustrate the results of a typical gap antenna fabricated with FE-SPL. A nanogap of approximately 25 nm was successfully obtained.

It is worth noting that the AFM functions not only as a direct-writing fabrication tool but also as an in situ characterization instrument. Specifically, the capability of AFM to image charge distributions enables the inspection of exposure states without development. Meanwhile, its morphological scanning capability facilitates the characterization of the resist pattern topography after development, thereby allowing researchers to determine exposure completeness, verify appropriate line widths, and identify any missing features. Compared to relying solely on the final SEM inspection, this intermediate-stage characterization can significantly shorten the parameter optimization cycle.

Results and Discussion

Precision Length Control

Using the aforementioned method, we fabricated a series of plasmonic nanobars with varying lengths (Figure (a)–(d)). All nanobars were designed with an identical width of 40 nm. The designed lengths were 80, 100, 120, 140 nm, respectively. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images reveal that the antenna structures in each array are uniform. The antenna lengths were accurately fabricated according to the designed dimensions.

Typical plasmonic nanostructures fabricated by FE-SPL. (a–d) SEM images of Au nanobars with different geometrical parameters. Their widths are uniformly 40 nm, and the lengths in panels a, b, c and d are 80, 100, 120, and 140 nm, respectively. The insets are the zoom-in images of the nanobars. (e) Scattering spectra of the nanoantennas corresponding to a–d.

This precise dimensional variation is directly reflected in the spectral response. Here, we measured the scattering spectra of individual antennas with an inverted microscope (IX 73, Olympus) equipped with a homemade dark-field setup. The signals were collected by an imaging spectrograph (Shamrock 500i, Oxford Instruments) and recorded by a back-illuminated charge-coupled device (DU401A BR-DD, Oxford Instruments). As shown in Figure , the resonant wavelengths of the antennas from a to d shift sequentially. It is well-established that for nanobars, length exhibits a linear relationship with resonant frequency. The regular shift of the resonance peak positions in the figure indicates that the antenna lengths vary in an arithmetic progression, demonstrating that the experimental results align with theoretical expectations and further validating the fabrication accuracy.

Precise Tuning of Line Width

In addition to length, the ability to precisely tune the line width of structures is equally critical for the fabrication of plasmonic micro/nanostructures. Because current direct-writing technologies rely on the discretization of target patterns, gradual tuning of nanostructure dimensions has been a challenge. Specifically for FE-SPL, achieving fine geometric parameter adjustment remains somewhat difficult due to limitations imposed by the tip size (∼35 nm in our case).

Interestingly, we found that the line width in FE-SPL can be finely tuned by controlling the exposure parameters. Here, the scanning speed and the voltage applied to the tip are the primary factors influencing the direct-writing results. Figure (a) shows one typical example. We wrote an array of nanodot structures under different writing voltages. From left to right, the writing voltage increases sequentially, while structures in the same column share identical writing parameters. It is evident that the size of the nanodots gradually increases as the writing voltage rises. Concurrently, we tested gap antenna structures; by adjusting the exposure time, we can progressively narrow the antenna gap (as shown in Figure (b)).

Dependence of line width on scanning parameters. (a, b) SEM images of nanodots and gap antennas fabricated under varying writing voltages. (c) Line width as a function of writing voltage at different scanning speeds. Scale bar in (a) and (b) denotes 1 μm and 500 nm, respectively.

Using this approach, we systematically evaluated the impact of writing voltage and speed on line width using the nanobar structure as a model. The results are summarized by Figure (c). Monotonic dependence of line width on voltage and scanning speed is observed. For a constant resist thickness (50 nm in this case), by increasing the writing voltage and decreasing the scanning speed, the line width can be tuned from 30 to 200 nm, while the line width deviation is maintained at approximately 10 nm. In other words, while preserving the direct-writing trajectory, continuous line width tuning can be achieved by adjusting scanning parameters with a precision of 10 nm.

High Reliability and Reproducibility

In practical applications, the primary challenges facing FE-SPL are its stability and reproducibility. , Because it operates in an ambient atmosphere, the solid–solid contact point between the tip and the resist is on the nanometer scale. Furthermore, a voltage of tens of volts is directly applied across this gap, creating an extreme interaction region at the microscopic level with pressures reaching ∼1 GPa and electric fields exceeding 108 V/m. Maintaining the stability of such an extreme multifield coupled system is highly challenging. Fortunately, we experimentally found that despite these factors, when good substrate conductivity (substrate sheet resistance <15 Ω/sq) and an appropriate sample thickness are maintained, the FE-SPL direct-writing process can sustain high stability and reproducibility at relatively low voltages (below 70 V). Additionally, to ensure batch-to-batch consistency, we expose a standard pattern and calibrate the lithography performance using the in situ characterization capability of the AFM whenever the probe or substrate is replaced, thereby guaranteeing highly uniform results.

To demonstrate the stability of this FE-SPL process, we fabricated a series of nanobar arrays (5-by-5). The design dimensions within the same array were identical; the nanobar width in all arrays was 40 nm, and the lengths were 40, 80, 120, and 160 nm, respectively. As shown in Figure , the dimensions of the nanobars exhibit excellent uniformity within each array. The variations were kept below 10 nm, which is consistent with the results from the previous line width tests.

Other Key Parameters: Resist Thickness, Humidity, and Tip Coating Materials

To achieve the highly reproducible fabrication demonstrated above, understanding and controlling the multifield coupled parameters within the system is crucial. During the FE-SPL exposure process, a highly localized electric field is established between the conductive AFM tip and the conductive substrate across the resist layer. Because the radius of the tip apex is on the nanometer scale, the applied bias voltage is concentrated within this localized tip–resist–substrate junction, generating an intense local electric field. Once this electric field exceeds a critical threshold, electrons are injected from the tip into the resist, thereby inducing a cross-linking reaction. In this process, factors such as the resist thickness, the tip–resist contact quality, and the intrinsic properties of the probe collectively influence the final electron injection outcomes. We therefore further investigated related key parameters in the FE-SPL, such as resist thickness, ambient humidity and tip coating material.

First, we investigate the effect of resist thickness on the threshold exposure voltage. To study the influence of direct-writing voltage on exposure at various thicknesses, we prepared a series of substrates with different resist thicknesses by adjusting the dilution ratio and spin-coating parameters. For each resist thickness, nanobar array patterns were written on the sample using varying voltages (20–60 V, in 2 V increments). The results reveal the existence of a distinct threshold voltage. Below this threshold voltage, stable direct writing cannot be achieved even if the scanning speed is further reduced. Conversely, once the threshold voltage is reached, complete structures can be successfully patterned even at the highest scanning speed. This indicates that the induced cross-linking reaction is predominantly controlled by the exposure voltage.

Figure (a)–(d) present a typical result for a resist thickness of 50 nm. It is evident that when the writing voltage is below the threshold voltage (34 V), the writing process is unstable, leading to a substantial number of missing structures. Upon reaching the threshold, the patterned structures become complete. Further increasing the writing voltage results in significant structural broadening. We evaluated four resist thicknesses: 40, 50, 60, and 70 nm. As depicted in Figure (e), the threshold exposure voltage exhibits an approximately linear increase with resist thickness. This finding suggests that a thicker resist layer significantly attenuates the localized electric field between the tip and the sample, thereby necessitating a higher applied bias to achieve the material reaction threshold.

Relationship between resist thickness and threshold exposure voltage. (a–d) Fabrication results of nanobars under different direct-writing voltages (32, 34, 36, and 38 V). (e) Threshold of writing voltage versus resist thickness. Scale bars: 1 μm.

It should be noted that there is an upper limit to this compensation method. When the applied bias exceeds 70 V, the tip begins to suffer from a certain degree of damage, ultimately manifesting as localized structural overexposure, boundary expansion, morphological distortion, or even sample dielectric breakdown. A possible reason is that high voltage exerts an electrostatic force on the cantilever directed toward the sample surface, leading to system instability or causing defects that induce breakdown and damage the system. The above results demonstrate that thickness optimization is constrained not only by the threshold voltage but also by the stable operating voltage window of the tip, which in turn determines the effective process thickness range.

Ambient humidity is another critical parameter in FE-SPL. This is because water molecules aggregate in the tip-resist gap to form a water meniscus. Considering that resist exposure depends on the injected charge, which in turn directly relies on the tip-resist contact, maintaining stable contact during operation is the key to FE-SPL technology. Under appropriate humidity, this meniscus facilitates stable electrical contact without causing current spreading that would compromise system resolution.

To study the effect of humidity on direct-writing performance, we used the same tip to expose array patterns on the same resist sample under different humidity conditions, keeping all other parameters constant. We then employed Kelvin Probe Force Microscopy (KPFM) to characterize the charge distribution corresponding to the exposure dose, thereby evaluating the direct-writing results. It is worth emphasizing that this characterization method is nondestructive and requires no development, providing immense convenience for optimizing FE-SPL parameters.

The results are shown in Figure . When the relative humidity is 40%, uniform and clear patterns are formed. However, at lower humidity (34%), the lattice becomes highly uneven; due to unstable tip-resist contact, a large number of structures fail to be written. Conversely, when the humidity is too high (46%), the patterns begin to blur. This is caused by an excessively thick water film, which leads to the diffusion of the direct-writing current.

Effect of ambient humidity on writing results. (a–c) KPFM scan images of the direct-writing results (prior to development) at humidity levels of 34%, 40%, and 46%. Scale bars: 1 μm.

Another factor related to contact is the prebaking of the resist. Our experiments revealed that failing to use prebaking to remove the diluent leads to line width fluctuations and a decrease in array uniformity.

It should be pointed out that during the ambient scanning probe patterning process, the role of ambient humidity may not be limited to modifying the electrical contact between the tip and the resist. Previous studies have demonstrated that the nanoscale water meniscus formed between the tip and the sample surface can serve as a medium for localized chemical or electrochemical reactions, thereby influencing the materials modification and pattern formation processes. ,

In addition, the material properties of the metal coating on the tip itself impact the direct-writing results. Generally, conductive coatings for probes should possess high hardness, wear resistance and high electrical conductivity. Tips coated with Ti or WC are optimal choices, whereas Au coatings are less stable due to their lower hardness. Furthermore, differences in the work functions of the conductive coating layer affect the injection efficiency of field-emission electrons. As a result, optimal process windows must be determined individually for different tip materials. For the same reason, the material of the substrate itself also influences the writing parameters. Our experiments indicate that the sheet resistance of the material beneath the resist needs to be less than 15 Ω/sq; if the resistance is too high, stable patterns cannot be obtained.

Insufficient cantilever stiffness may also lead to instability, as the cantilever bends under the influence of electrostatic forces when the writing voltage is applied. In our experiments, we typically employ cantilever probes with a spring constant of ∼1.0–1.5 N/m. Probes with this stiffness can also operate in tapping mode in conjunction with KPFM functionality to inspect the charge distribution directly after writing without development, facilitating the optimization of the direct-writing process.

Conclusion

We have developed a plasmonic nanostructure fabrication technology based on FE-SPL, enabling the precise, reliable, and convenient manufacturing of plasmonic nanostructures using low-cost AFM platforms. Using this method, we successfully fabricated nanobars and gap antennas with continuously tunable widths and lengths. Line widths as small as 40 nm were achieved, with parameter tuning precision down to 10 nm. To realize these fabrication capabilities, we systematically investigated key process parameters in FE-SPL, including resist thickness, ambient humidity, writing voltage, and scanning speed, identifying the parameter window for stable and reliable system operation.

This method eliminates the need for expensive vacuum electron beam lithography equipment and can be implemented using standard desktop commercial AFMs. Table summarizes a side-by-side comparison of FE-SPL with mainstream nanofabrication techniques, including electron beam lithography (EBL), focused ion beam (FIB) milling, and thermal scanning probe lithography (t-SPL). While EBL and FIB offer ultimate sub-10 nm resolutions and excellent accuracy, they heavily rely on harsh high-vacuum environments and extremely expensive equipment (often exceeding one million USD). Similarly, t-SPL achieves high resolution but requires specialized heated-probe setups and often involves complex pattern transfer processes due to the use of ultrathin positive resists. , In contrast, the FE-SPL method developed in this work offers a remarkable balance. Although its throughput is inherently limited by the serial scanning nature of the AFM, and its minimum line width is slightly larger than that of EBL, its fabrication capacity is adequate for most plasmonic and metasurface applications. Most importantly, it drastically reduces the equipment requirements and costs, enabling researchers to perform robust nanofabrication using merely a standard desktop AFM under ambient conditions.
