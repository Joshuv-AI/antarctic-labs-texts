Shrub mitigate the negative impacts of drought on the ecological adaptability of Syntrichia caninervis in the desert regions of Northwest China
Authors: Shujun Zhang, Ao Yang, Benfeng Yin, Qing Zhang, Xinyu Zhang, Xiaobing Zhou, Jin Chen, Ruilin Chen, Jing Zhang, Yuanming Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Syntrichia caninervis, a representative stress-tolerant moss, thrives under extreme conditions such as extreme temperatures, drought, and intense radiation, making it a top candidate for extraterrestrial colonization. However, whether the ecological adaptation traits of S. caninervis are consistent across different distribution areas and microhabitats and the interactive relationships among functional traits remain unclear. This study employed a plant trait network (PTN) approach to examine the relationships among 25 functional traits of S. caninervis within and outside shrub habitats to assess its ecological adaptability in temperate desert.ResultShrub microhabitats increased the biomass, leaf morphology, and photosynthetic efficiency of S. caninervis by improving the local hydrothermal environment while reducing the physiological stress response. The trait network of S. caninervis under shrub presented a relatively high edge density and average clustering coefficient and relatively low network diameter, average path length, and degree of modularity. These findings suggest that shrubs enhance trait network connectivity and functional collaboration of S. caninervis, effectively mitigating drought stress and optimizing resource utilization efficiency. Central trait analysis revealed that, under shrub conditions, Fv/Fm and soluble sugars were central traits, whereas exposed area, Fv/Fm and POD were central traits. As precipitation decreased and temperature increased, the leaf area and photosynthetic activity of S. caninervis initially tended to increase but then decreased. Simultaneously, its trait network connectivity strengthened, with both modularity and local tightness gradually increasing, reflecting its adaptation to drought and high-temperature stress through trait synergy and functional modular specialization. Structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis revealed that climate dominated the ecological adaptability of S. caninervis through both direct and indirect pathways, whereas shrubs significantly alleviated the negative impacts of drought stress by improving hydrothermal conditions. This study not only reveals the physiological adaptation strategies of moss species in extreme environments but also provides new insights into the adaptive mechanisms of moss species in desert ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07384-y.Keywords: Temperate desert, Microhabitats, Biological soil crust, Ecological adaptability, Trait network

Introduction

As a poikilohydric plant, moss is highly sensitive to environmental changes, rapidly responding to external fluctuations [1, 2]. Xerophytic moss survives harsh conditions through complex morphological and physiological adjustments, such as altering structure, accumulating osmoregulatory substances, and enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity to improve stress resistance, and optimizing water-use strategies [3, 4]. These abilities enable moss to effectively cope with drought, temperature fluctuations, and intense radiation in extreme environments, while also influencing biogeochemical cycles and surface microenvironments [5, 6], and mitigating the impact of drought on arid ecosystems [7]. Syntrichia caninervis, a widespread desert moss, is highly tolerant of extreme aridity, ultralow temperatures (−196 °C), and high radiation (4000 Gy), making it a potential pioneer species for Mars colonization [8]. Therefore, further research on the ecological adaptability of S. caninervis is not only important for understanding its survival strategies in earth’s extreme environments but also has important value for future human extraterrestrial migration.

Deserts, as an ecosystem highly sensitive to environmental changes, serve as natural laboratories for studying plant ecological adaptation mechanisms in extreme environments because of its harsh climatic conditions and scarce resources [5, 9]. Moss gains ecological dominance in these regions through coordinated population, morphological, and physiological strategies [10, 11]. For example, desert moss often forms cushion-like clusters [12], reducing leaf area [13], increasing leaf thickness and costa width, and pressing leaves against stems to enhance adaptation to drought and radiation [14, 15]. Specialized hair points on leaves efficiently capture water from the surrounding environment [16]. Compared to leaves without hair points, those with trichomes exhibit a 24.87% increase in water adsorption capacity, significantly enhancing water-use efficiency [17]. Physiological traits are more sensitive than morphology to environmental shifts. The photosynthetic capacity of moss can recover to 80–90% of normal levels within a short period after rehydration [18, 19], but the levels of osmoregulatory substances and antioxidant enzyme activity increase with reduced water or rising temperatures [11]. Microhabitats also create small-scale climates, shielding moss from extreme heat and drought, which influences its morphology and physiology. Vegetation cover increases moss coverage and biomass while reducing osmoregulatory substances and antioxidant enzyme activity [20, 21]. In summary, moss morphology and physiology are jointly shaped by biotic (vegetation) and abiotic (climate) factors.

Shrubs, key vegetation in arid regions, form “wet island” and “fertility island” effects, alleviating water stress and intense radiation for xerophytic moss, impacting their morphology and physiology [22, 23]. Shrub microhabitats reduce surface evaporation, extending moss hydration periods [24], increase moss height, biomass, and photosynthetic capacity [25], and lower osmoregulatory substances and antioxidant enzyme activity, easing physiological stress [26, 27]. In conclusion, the growth of moss is influenced by both large-scale and local-scale environmental variables. The morphological and physiological traits of mosses are closely linked to ecosystem processes [24]. However, existing studies on shrub‒moss interactions have focused mainly on single moss traits, and comprehensive analyses of population dynamics, morphology, and physiology are lacking. In particular, whether the adaptability of moss to hydrothermal variations differs across spatial scales, and whether such differences are influenced by the buffering effect of shrubs remain insufficiently explored.

Ecological adaptability is a multivariate process [28], reflecting integrated responses of population, morphology, and physiology to environmental change [29]. Plants adjust their population structure, morphology, and physiology to increase their resource-use efficiency and reduce stress impacts under varying climate, soil, and resource conditions [30, 31]. Common methods used to assess adaptability include analyzing morphological traits (e.g., leaf size), physiological metrics (e.g., photosynthetic efficiency, osmoregulatory substances), and ecological traits (e.g., population structure) [32, 33]. Plants possess multiple traits that work together to promote specific functions, and the synergies and trade-offs between traits reflect the plant’s response to external conditions on the basis of physiological needs [34]. However, much of the current research on plant traits still focuses on the response of a particular organ or trait to the environment [35, 36], and the role of potential coordination and trade-offs between traits in plant ecological adaptations is often overlooked [37, 38]. Plant trait network analysis offers a systematic perspective for revealing the multidimensional relationships of plant adaptability [39]. Tighter networks (connectivity) enhance the efficient acquisition and use of resources by plants, whereas modular structures (complexity) enable plants to flexibly adapt to external environments [40]. Tropical forests, with more favorable climate conditions and abundant resources, exhibit denser and more complex networks, with higher trait coordination and resource use efficiency [41]. However, owing to differences in species selection and trait assemblages, the parameters of network traits may exhibit opposite trends. For example, in resource-limited environments, there is greater connectivity between plant traits [42]. Therefore, analyzing plant trait networks to comprehensively evaluate the complex relationships among various traits can help provide a more thorough understanding of plant ecological adaptation strategies under environmental change [43].

S. caninervis is a drought-tolerant moss species widely distributed in desert regions, and its ecological adaptability is highly sensitive to environmental heterogeneity. Prior studies have emphasized physiological and biochemical aspects such as photosynthetic capacity and osmotic regulatory substances while neglecting the multidimensional synergistic adaptation mechanisms among its population, morphology, and physiology. This study employs PTN to address the knowledge gap in shrub-moss interactions by revealing multidimensional synergies among population, morphology, and physiology—specifically, how trait coordination underpins ecological adaptation. We hypothesize that (1) the combined “wet island” and “fertility island” effects of shrubs increase S. caninervis morphological traits and photosynthetic activity, alleviating the negative impacts of drought stress; (2) the presence of shrubs enhances connectivity among S. caninervis traits, improving its resource acquisition and utilization; and (3) with increasing precipitation, the trait network topology of S. caninervis becomes tighter and more complex, strengthening trait synergy and improving resource-use efficiency. To test these hypotheses, we selected Northwest China’s desert region as the study area, which targeted S. caninervis, a moss widely distributed in temperate deserts. We collected S. caninervis samples from diverse microhabitats across environmental gradients and analyzed their population, morphological, and physiological traits to evaluate their ecological adaptability and identify key drivers of their adaptability.

Results

Shrub effects on population and morphology across environmental gradients

Shrubs significantly altered the S. caninervis population and morphological traits. Compared with exposed areas, shrub microhabitats presented reduced S. caninervis population density (−17.13%), leaf width/leaf length (−5.42%), and costa/leaf width (−17.13%) but increased biomass per unit area (+ 20.57%), individual biomass (+ 64.15%), height (+ 42.31%), leaf length (+ 15.81%), leaf width (+ 9.51%), hair-point length (+ 27.41%), and costa width (+ 3.46%) (p < 0.05, Fig. 1, S1). With increasing MAP and MAT, the S. caninervis population density decreased and then increased (Fig. S2), whereas the biomass, height, leaf length, width, costa width, and hair-point length increased and then decreased (Fig. S3 and S4). Notably, shrub microhabitats delayed the response threshold of the S. caninervis population and morphological traits to increasing MAP but advanced the response to increasing MAT.

Shrub effects on fluorescence and physiology across gradients

Compared with the exposed area, the shrub microhabitats increased Fv/Fm by 5.11% (p < 0.05, Fig. S5) while reducing the osmoregulatory substances (proline − 14.71%, soluble sugar − 37.01%, soluble protein − 35.56%), MDA (−32.98%), and antioxidant enzyme activity (CAT − 13.98%) (p < 0.05, Fig. S5). With increasing MAP and MAT, both the Fv/Fm and Y(II) of S. caninervis inside and outside the shrub microhabitats exhibited unimodal patterns. The response thresholds of Fv/Fm and Y(II) in S. caninervis from the exposed area to MAP changes occurred earlier than those in shrub microhabitats did, while their responses to MAT elevation showed relatively lagged adaptations. Conversely, soluble sugars, starch, total nonstructural carbohydrates, and antioxidant enzyme activity (POD, CAT) decreased but then increased, whereas proline and SOD increased progressively (Fig. 2, S6).

Trait network variation inside and outside shrub

The trait network topology of S. caninervis varied across microhabitats (Fig. 3). Compared with the exposed area, the shrub microhabitats presented greater edge density and clustering coefficients, with a lower network diameter, mean path length, and modularity (Table S1). These findings suggest that shrub habitats enhance S. caninervis trait network connectivity through increased trait synergy, whereas the S. caninervis trait network in the exposed area is more complex because of greater modularity. As MAP decreased and MAT increased, the S. caninervis networks presented a decrease in average path length and diameter but an increase in modularity and clustering coefficients (Table S1). This suggests that the connectivity and modularity of the networks are enhanced under conditions of lower precipitation.

Trait networks of S. caninervis under different precipitation gradients in shrub areas (A) and exposed areas (B). The abbreviations for the S. caninervis traits are provided in Table S2

The trait importance within the S. caninervis networks varied by microhabitat (Fig. 4). In shrub microhabitats, soluble protein, soluble sugars, Fv/Fₘ, and biomass had high degrees and closeness, whereas soluble sugars and leaf length presented high betweenness. In the exposed area, Fv/Fₘ, MDA and POD exhibited high degrees and closeness. Moreover, population density consistently showed a low degree, closeness, and betweenness across both microhabitats.

Key drivers of ecological adaptability

SEM was used to examine how climate, shrubs, and their interactions directly or indirectly affect the spatial variation in S. caninervis ecological adaptability through morphological and physiological traits (Fig. 5A). The results revealed that when random site effects were accounted for, these five factors explained 73% of the spatial variation in S. caninervis adaptability. Variance decomposition further revealed that climate and shrubs jointly dominated adaptability, with climate having the greatest effect (56.96%), followed by shrubs (43.03%) (Fig. 5B, C).

Discussion

Shrub presence mitigates the negative effects of drought on S. caninervis

This study revealed that shrubs in the arid deserts of Northwest China significantly alleviate the adverse effects of drought on S. caninervis by altering its morphological and physiological traits, which aligns with Hypothesis 1. Shrubs enhance soil moisture and resource availability [44, 45], reduce photoinhibition [46], and promote increases in plant height (42.31%), leaf length (15.81%), and leaf width (9.51%) in S. caninervis. Concurrently, reductions in osmotic adjustment substances (soluble protein by 35.63%, proline by 14.71%) and antioxidant enzyme activities (POD by 20.40%, CAT by 13.98%) were observed. Moss, including S. caninervis, typically favor low-light environments [47], and shaded, humid microhabitats beneath shrubs reduce environmental stress [48]. These conditions not only increase light-harvesting capacity [49], but also diminish reliance on osmotic adjustment substances and antioxidant enzyme activities [50]. Furthermore, a significant density-dependent relationship exists between population density and individual biomass in S. caninervis, where individual biomass decreases with increasing density (Fig. S7). This density effect manifests divergent strategies across microhabitats: under shrubs, individuals prioritize resource acquisition to promote morphological development and growth, whereas in open areas, high-density populations leverage capillary water transport induced by crowding to improve hydraulic efficiency and drought adaptation [51].

Water availability and temperature are critical limiting factors for plant growth in arid and semiarid regions, profoundly shaping vegetation adaptation strategies [52]. Our findings indicate that S. caninervis population density initially decreases and then increases with increasing MAP and MAT, whereas leaf morphological traits, biomass per unit area, individual biomass, and plant height follow a unimodal pattern (peaking at intermediate MAP/MAT). This contrasts with previous observations of monotonic declines in plant morphological metrics under drought [10, 53, 54]. This unique response arises from the species’ poikilohydric nature and cushion-forming growth habit: in regions with relatively high MAP, frequent small precipitation events (< 5 mm) force repeated transitions between dormancy and reactivation, depleting carbon reserves [55, 56]. Density-dependent self-thinning and metabolic imbalances induced by small precipitation events synergistically suppress individual development [57]. Physiological traits exhibit similar trends, with chlorophyll fluorescence activity and photochemical efficiency peaking under moderate moisture conditions [58]. Osmotic adjustment substances and antioxidant enzyme activities accumulate significantly under both low and high MAP. Under low MAP, photoinhibition triggers carbohydrate and protein hydrolysis (while suppressing synthesis) to maintain osmotic balance [59], whereas reactive oxygen species (ROS) overaccumulation activates antioxidant defenses, increasing the MDA content and antioxidant enzyme activity [11, 60]. Under high MAP, frequent small precipitation events deplete metabolic energy, suppress fluorescence activity and increase osmoregulatory substances and antioxidant enzyme activity responses [61, 62]. Additionally, reduced leaf size and photosynthetic capacity at high MAP may further drive these physiological shifts. Notably, the response thresholds of the S. caninervis population and its morphological and physiological traits to increasing precipitation are delayed in shrub microhabitats, whereas the tolerance to increasing temperature is greater in exposed areas. These findings underscore S. caninervis’s “morphophysiological” synergy in maintaining ecological adaptability under varying environmental pressures.

Trends in trait network connectivity of S. caninervis under climate change

The topological structure of plant trait networks reflects species’ environmental adaptation strategies: highly connected networks facilitate trait coordination to improve resource acquisition efficiency, whereas modular architectures enhance localized functional flexibility, collectively forming a trade-off mechanism for heterogeneous environments [39, 63]. Compared with exposed areas, shrub microhabitats foster greater trait connectivity in S. caninervis, a structure that enhances resource-use efficiency through functional trait coordination, aligning with theories of trait integration in resource-rich environments and validating Hypothesis 2 [41, 64]. Specifically, Shrub shading mitigates environmental stress by reducing radiation and enhancing soil water and nutrient availability, thereby creating more favorable growth conditions. Under these environments, S. caninervis exhibits improved photosynthetic efficiency and greater carbon assimilation, reallocating additional carbon to morphological investments such as increased leaf area and plant height, thereby promoting stronger trait coordination and higher connectivity within its functional network. In contrast, exposed area populations exhibit lower connectivity and higher modularity, favoring the formation of modular networks for localized stress responses (e.g., antioxidant modules), which enhances their ability to regulate and cope with multiple environmental stressors [65, 66]. This suggests that in resource-limited exposed areas, relatively weak resource acquisition and utilization result in sparse, complex trait networks with limited functional synergy.

At regional scales, plant trait network connectivity and complexity vary with species’ environmental adaptation. Global studies indicate that extreme climates drive high modularity and connectivity in plant trait networks, with nonwoody plants exhibiting stable modularity across climates [42] —a pattern corroborated here. In temperate deserts, S. caninervis trait networks show increasing connectivity and modularity rising temperatures precipitation (p > 0.05), reflecting enhanced trait coordination under intensifying aridity, which diverges from Hypothesis 3. This contrasts with reports of increasing connectivity and complexity in tree leaf trait networks along a humid thermal gradient from boreal to tropical forests [41]. The unique adaptation observed here likely stems from desert ecosystem specificity and the ecological strategy of S. caninervis. In high-temperature, low-precipitation environments, dual mechanisms operate: heightened network connectivity optimizes resource allocation, whereas strengthened intramodule linkages stabilize core metabolism by minimizing cross-module signal interference. This enhanced connectivity and complexity reflect S. caninervis’ adaptive optimization of functional modules and resource use to survive extreme conditions.

Central traits in S. caninervis trait networks

“Central traits” in plant networks act as hub nodes, regulating core physiological functions through their topological centrality; their variation cascades to higher-level plant properties [67]. The central traits of S. caninervis differ markedly between microhabitats: soluble protein, soluble sugars, Fv/Fₘ and biomass dominate shrub networks, whereas Fv/Fₘ, MDA and POD are central in exposed areas. As a poikilohydric species, S. caninervis exhibits pronounced sensitivity of fluorescence activity (Fv/Fm) to environmental shifts, with efficient photosynthesis fuelling morphological development [18]. The strong connectivity of photosynthetic traits enables their dominance in network integration across different microhabitats. It is worth noting that the presence of shrubs optimizes growth-related processes, enabling Syntrichia caninervis to prioritize energy allocation toward morphological development, with soluble sugars and biomass becoming the central traits in shrub microhabitats. In exposed areas, harsher conditions elevate ROS levels, reshaping the network topology and positioning MDA and POD as central hubs for mitigating oxidative damage, indicating that Syntrichia caninervis prioritizes oxidative defense in exposed areas. This plasticity highlights the ability of S. caninervis to optimize its physiological function by modulating central traits under contrasting environmental pressures.

Conclusions

This study systematically elucidates the multidimensional adaptation strategies of S. caninervis in the arid desert ecosystems of Northwest China by integrating its population, morphological, and physiological traits. The presence of shrubs not only significantly improved the growth environment of S. caninervis but also positively influenced its ecological adaptability. Under shrub canopies, S. caninervis presents enhanced morphological traits (greater leaf length, width, and costa width) and photosynthetic capacity, alongside lower population density and reduced reliance on osmotic adjustment substances and antioxidant enzyme activity. Concurrently, the heightened connectivity among traits under shrubs reflects prioritized individual growth and improved resource-use efficiency in water-sufficient environments. The relationships between the morphological and physiological traits of S. caninervis and climatic factors (precipitation and temperature) are not uniformly linear. Notably, under low water availability, S. caninervis enhances survival by increasing trait network connectivity, modularity, and localized functional integration. Crucially, climate and shrub microhabitats jointly regulate the ecological adaptability of S. caninervis, with shrubs mitigating the negative impacts of drought stress. In summary, S. caninervis demonstrates remarkable adaptive plasticity across various hydrothermal conditions through flexible adjustments in population structure, morphology, and physiological function. Under aridity, the synergistic regulation of population density, morphological traits, and physiological mechanisms provides critical support for survival. This study advances the understanding of S. caninervis adaptation strategies and offers a theoretical foundation for predicting the responses of desert ecosystems to climate change.

Materials and methods

Study area

We selected the Tengger and Gurbantunggut Deserts in Northwest China, where S. caninervis is widely distributed, as study areas. (1) The Gurbantunggut Desert (44°11′–46°20′ N, 84°31′–90°00′ E), located in the Junggar Basin of Xinjiang, China, covers approximately 4.88 × 10⁴ km², making it the country’s largest fixed and semi-fixed desert. Based on meteorological data from 2006 to 2020, the region exhibits a typical arid climate with mean annual precipitation (MAP) range of 80–150 mm, mean annual temperature (MAT) range of 6–10 °C, and annual potential evaporation exceeding 2000 mm [68]. Its unique geography and extreme conditions support drought-tolerant shrubs and small trees, including Haloxylon ammodendron, Haloxylon persicum, Ephedra przewalskii, Calligonum mongolicum. These vascular plants, interspersed with S. caninervis dominated moss, form a key surface landscape in this region [69]. (2) The Tengger Desert (37°32′–40°00′ N, 102°15′–105°41′ E), spanning Inner Mongolia, Gansu, and Ningxia, occupies about 4.3 × 10⁴ km². This region experiences 125–160 mm MAP and has MAT of 7.0–9.7 °C. Vegetation is sparse and species-poor, dominated by drought-adapted shrubs such as Sarcozygium xanthoxylon and Caragana intermedia, alongside mosses like Didymodon vinealis, ryum argenteum. S. caninervis occurs sporadically, mainly along the southeastern margin [11, 70].

Experimental design

From June to July 2021, we collected S. caninervis samples along environmental gradients at 14 sites in Northwest China’s deserts (Fig. 6A). The sampling sites were interdune lowlands with shrubs (mainly Ephedra przewalskii) and well-developed mosses. At each site, we established three 30 m × 30 m plots spaced ≥ 100 m apart, with two 10 m × 10 m subplots randomly placed within each plot. Within each subplot, we collected one S. caninervis sample from the exposed area (at least 1 m from the shrubs) and one from under the shrubs (Fig. 6B). The identity of S. caninervis was confirmed by Benfeng Yin, and voucher specimens were deposited at the Xinjiang Institute of Ecology and Geography, Chinese Academy of Sciences, under accession number XJBI00161330 to ensure traceability. To minimize damage to mosses, we sprayed water Lightly before sampling via PVC tubes. Six subplot samples served as replicates per site, yielding 12 samples per site and 168 samples across 14 sites. The samples were returned to the laboratory for population, morphological, and physiological analyses. To avoid the effects of prior precipitation on physiological traits, all the samples were air-dried indoors for 30 days. S. caninervis samples were then rehydrated and incubated in a controlled chamber. A 16-h light (12,000 lx)/8-h dark (0 lx) cycle was used for incubation, with a temperature of 24 °C (day)/15°C (night) and a humidity of 80% ± 5%. Water was added every 12 h to maintain full hydration. After 48 h, physiological measurements were taken.

Population and morphological measurements

Representative moss patches in PVC tubes were photographed, and area (S) was calculated using Image software. S. caninervis plant numbers (N) were counted, samples cleaned by rinsing roots with deionized water to remove soil, oven-dried (105 °C for 15 min, then 70 °C for 48 h), and dry weight (M) recorded. Population density (ρ = N/S), biomass per unit area (BM = M/S), and individual biomass (IBM = BM/ρ) were calculated.

Thirty representative plants from the shrub and exposed area microhabitats were selected post-rehydration, mature leaves taken from the upper third, photographed with an Olympus SZX10 microscope, and measured for height, leaf length, width, hair-point length, and costa width. Ratios (leaf width/length, costa width/leaf width, hair-point length/leaf length) were computed, averaging five plants per sample [13].

Physiological and biochemical measurements

Chlorophyll fluorescence was measured using a PAM-2500 fluorometer (Walz, Hernz, Germany). Samples in PVC tubes were dark-adapted for 1 h in shading boxes. Initial fluorescence (F0) was measured at 650 nm (0.1 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ red light), maximum fluorescence (Fm) at 10,000 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ (0.8 s pulse), steady-state fluorescence (Ft) at 250 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ actinic light, and maximum fluorescence after light adaptation (Fm′) post-pulse. Fv/Fm = (Fm - F0)/Fm and Y(II) = (Fm′ - Ft)/Fm′ were calculated [18].

The proline, soluble sugar, and soluble protein contents were measured via the ninhydrin, anthrone, and Coomassie brilliant blue methods, respectively, with the absorbance measured via a spectrophotometer (PE, Waltham, USA) [71]. Starch was quantified via the anthrone-sulfuric acid method. The total nonstructural carbohydrate content was estimated as the soluble sugar plus starch content [72]. The MDA content was measured via the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) colorimetric method. SOD, POD, and CAT activities were assessed via the hydroxylamine, guaiacol, and visible Light methods, respectively, with absorbance at 450 nm, 470 nm, and 510 nm on a spectrophotometer [73].

Statistical analysis

Data normality and variance homogeneity were tested prior to analysis. One-way ANOVA with least significant difference (LSD) multiple comparisons was applied to population, morphological, and physiological data across microhabitats, followed by plotting. Concurrently, we assessed the responses of S. caninervis population, morphological, and physiological traits to increasing MAP and MAT via linear and nonlinear (quadratic and generalized additive models, GAMs) regressions, selecting models with lower AIC values.

For plant trait network analysis, we treated traits as nodes and correlations as edges. We calculated correlation coefficient matrices using mean trait values per site, with Pearson correlation absolute values (|r|) indicating relationship strength. Owing to the small sample size (n = 14 sites), we set |r| >0.2 and p < 0.1 as significance thresholds [41, 43]. Adjacency matrices (A = [a, j], where a, j ∈ [0, 1]) were constructed, assigning 1 to relationships above thresholds and 0 below. Finally, we used the R package “igraph” to visualize networks and calculate topological parameters (Table S3) [43].

To assess the roles of climate and shrubs in driving S. caninervis adaptability, we used the “piecewiseSEM” package in R 4.2.1 to build piecewise structural equation modeling (SEM) to evaluate their direct and indirect effects. Biomass per unit area indicated S. caninervis adaptability [74]. PCA generated composite variables from climate, morphological, and physiological trait [75]. We used piecewise SEMs to test direct climate and shrub effects and indirect effects through morphological and physiological traits. Models were assessed with Fisher’s C (0.05 < p < 1) and the AIC, adjusted on the basis of path significance. Variance decomposition via “glmm.hp” quantified climate and shrub contributions [76].

All analyses were conducted in R 4.1.2 (R Core Team, 2023), using packages “dplyr,” “ggplot2,” “patchwork,” “igraph,” “tidyverse,” “Hmisc,” and “vegan” for data analysis and visualization.
