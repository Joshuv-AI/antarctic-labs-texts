Machine learning-based construction of Immunogenic cell death-related score for improving prognosis and personalized treatment in glioma
Authors: Guoyin Li, Yukui Zhao, Yubo He, Zhaoqiang Qian, Yiwen Liu, Xiaoyan Li, Lili Li, Zhiqiang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunogenic cell death (ICD) is capable of activating both innate and adaptive immune responses. In this study, we aimed to develop an ICD-related signature in glioma patients and facilitate the assessment of their prognosis and drug sensitivity. Consensus clustering and non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) were performed to classify patients into subgroups. A least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression model was constructed to establish an ICD-related risk score (ICDS). CIBERSORT and ESTIMATE algorithms were employed to evaluate the infiltration of immune cells. Flow cytometry, CCK-8, EdU, and Transwell assays were used to detect cell proliferation and migration abilities. qPCR, Western blotting, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence were utilized to detect mRNA and protein expression levels. The ICDS proved effective in predicting the prognosis of glioma patients in both the training and two validating cohorts. The ICDS exhibited significant advantages when compared to the 71 previously published signatures. Patients with a high ICDS score demonstrated marked enhancement in immune cell infiltration and expression of immune checkpoint inhibitor-related genes. Furthermore, SERPINH1, one of the 14 key genes used to establish the ICDS, was abnormally overexpressed in gliomas and activate JAK/STAT signaling, thereby promoting glioma cell proliferation and migration. Introduction

Worldwide, gilomas are the most prevalent primary malignant tumor in the brain, accounting for approximately 80% of all malignantbrain tumors in adults1. According to the 2016 WHO classification, grade II and III are referred to as lower-grade brain gliomas (LGG) and grade IV is referred to as glioblastoma (GBM)2. The median overall survival (OS) for LGG is approximately 78.1 and 37.6 months for WHO II and III, respectively, whereas GBM has a much shorter median OS of only 15 months3. Studies have shown that glioma patients with mutations in the isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) gene tend to have a better prognosis4. Gliomas are characterized by high invasiveness, heterogeneity and tendency to recur5. The early infiltrative nature of gliomas hinders the effectiveness of complete surgical resection, while the heterogeneity of cancer cells in terms of their phenotype, genotype, and function poses a significant challenge in treating GBM. Despite great advances have been made in the diagnosis and treatment of glioma in recent decades, the prognosis of patients is still not optimistic.The blood-brain barrier prevents most anti-tumor drugs from entering the brain, posing a huge challenge for the treatment of gliomas6. There is an urgent need to develop more effective treatments to improve glioma patient outcomes.

In theory, immunotherapy holds great promise as an ideal protocol for treating gliomas, as immune cells can cross the blood-brain barrier and selectively kill tumor cells while sparing normal brain cells. However, the efficacy of immunotherapy, including vaccines, adoptive cell therapy, CAR-T cells, and immune checkpoint blockade, is unfortunately limited in most cases of glioblastoma7. Immunotherapy, which is based on boosting the immune response, shows higher response rates in “immune hot” tumors characterized by abundant tumor-killing T cells and pro-inflammatory genes8,9. However, GBM is considered an “immune cold” tumor as it lacks tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs, < 5%) and is rich in immunosuppressive myeloid cells7. In gliomas, programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase limit the presentation of antigens10,11, and interleukin (IL)−10 and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) reduce the activity of immune cells12,13. It is urgent to explore new markers for immunotherapy and develop immune-related prognostic models to address these challenges.

Immunogenic cell death (ICD) is a type of regulated cell death that is sufficient to activate an adaptive immune response in an immunocompetent setting14. ICD relies on specific stress signals and involves the generation of reactive oxygen species and induction of endoplasmic reticulum stress15. ICD encompasses the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) from dying tumor cells. These DAMPs are recognized by innate pattern recognition receptors such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and NOD-like receptors (NLRs), leading to the activation of tumor-specific immune responses, thereby stimulating the long-term efficacy of anticancer drugs by combining direct cancer cell killing with anti-tumor immunity14,16. ICD can be induced by chemotherapy, photodynamic therapy, photothermal therapy and radiotherapy17. Due to their significant efficacy18, low toxicity19, and combination therapy advantages20, ICD-inducing drugs have become a promising approach in tumor treatment. In recent years, the deepened understanding of the molecular mechanisms of ICD has promoted the development of ICD-related risk models and their application in patient classification and prognosis assessment.

In this study, we assessed the expression of 34 ICD-related genes and developed an ICD-related risk score (ICDS) to identify distinct glioma subtypes with varying prognoses, pathway enrichment features, and characteristics of immune cell infiltration. In addition, the impact of ICDS on the sensitivity to 83 kinds of drugs was also investigated. We compared ICDS with 71 published prognostic models and found that ICDS exhibited superior performance in both the training and validation cohorts. In addition, we also developed a nomogram integrating ICDS and clinical indicators to quantitatively assess patient prognosis. Finally, both bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation suggested that Serpin Family H Member 1 (SERPINH1) might promote the malignant phenotype of gliomas by activating the JAK/STAT signaling pathway.

Materials and methods

Public data acquisition and processing

The CGGA_301, CGGA_325, and CGGA_693 datasets were obtained from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) database (http://www.cgga.org.cn/). Data cleaning and processing were performed by Perl and R 4.1.3 software. All datasets used in this study were downloaded from publicly available databases; therefore, additional ethical approval was not required.

Construction of ICDscore

First, following the approach reported by Qin et al.21, we stratified patients into two subgroups using both consensus clustering and NMF (Non-Negative Matrix Factorization) methods based on the expression levels of the 34 ICD-related genes. We then identified differentially expressed genes (|logFC| ≥ 2; FDR ≤ 0.01) between the two subgroups and selected the intersecting genes for subsequent analysis. Subsequently, we conducted Cox regression analysis to identify genes associated with prognosis (p < 0.01). Finally, the genes associated with prognosis were input into a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression model to identify the core genes and their corresponding expression coefficients, which were then used to develop the ICD-related risk score (ICDS). A new score was calculated for each glioma patient using the following formula: score = ∑i Coefficient(Gene i) × Expression(Gene i). To facilitate comparison across different datasets, the ICDS was calculated with the formula as follows: ICDS = (score-Min)/absolute (Max)22. The CGGA_325 cohort was utilized as the training set, while the CGGA_301 and CGGA_693 cohorts were employed as the validation sets.

Immune profile analysis

In this study, the “estimate” and “limma” packages in R 4.1.3 software were employed to characterize the tumor microenvironment (TME) of patients in the CGGA_325 dataset. The “reshape2”, “ggpubr”, and “limma” packages were utilized for the analysis of immune cell infiltration and immune function in glioma patients.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA for glioma patients was conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package in R. The gene set database “c5.go.symbols.gmt” (retrieved from MSigDB) was selected for this analysis. To visualize the results, the “GseaVis” package in R was utilized.

Development and evaluation of the nomogram

Clinical features including age, gender, IDH mutation status, and tumor grade were integrated with ICDS to develop a nomogram. The nomogram’s accuracy was evaluated using ROC curve analysis, calibration curve analysis, and the C-index.

Cell culture and SiRNA transfection

Human glioma cells LN229 and U251 were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Institute of Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The siRNAs were synthesized by Shanghai Gima Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. Transfection was performed using Lipo8000™ (Beyotime, C0533, China). The sequences of siRNAs are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

CCK-8 assay

LN229 and U251 cells were inoculated into 96-well plates and treated with CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, C0038, China) at 0, 1, 2, and 3 days, respectively. The absorbance of the cells at 450 nm was then measured using an enzyme labeling instrument.

Patients and specimens

Patients in the department of pathology, Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital (Taiyuan, China) provided 95 human glioma tissues. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. The Ethics Committee of the Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital approved the use of clinical specimens and human glioma cells (2021 − 196). All experimental methods were strictly conducted in compliance with the relevant ethical guidelines and regulatory requirements.

Western blotting, immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence assays

Exponentially growing cells were treated with RIPA buffer to extract total protein and quantify the protein using a BCA protein quantification assay kit (Beyotime, P0010, China). Western blotting (WB), immunohistochemistry (IHC), and immunofluorescence were performed using primary antibodies against SERPINH1 (proteintech, 10875-1-AP, China), MYD88 (proteintech, 23230-1-AP, China), LY96 (proteintech, 11784-1-AP, China), PDIA3 (proteintech, 15967-1-AP, China), GAPDH (proteintech, 80570-1-RR, China). HRP-conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (proteintech, SA00001-2, China) was used as secondary antibodies for Western blotting. Cy3–conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (proteintech, SA00009-2, China) was used as secondary antibodies for immunofluorescence. Hoechst 33,258 (Beyotime, C1018, China) was used to stain the cell nucleus. Glioma tissues were processed into tissue microarrays and scanned and digitized the IHC results. The specific methods are described in our previous report23.

Statistical analysis

The Mann-Whitney U test was applied to analyze the differences between two groups. Wilcoxon test was performed to compare the proportion of tumor-infiltrating immune cells. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to analyze the correlation between two variables. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were carried out to identify the independent prognostic factor for OS. The predictive accuracy of the ICDS for OS was assessed by conducting time-dependent ROC curve analysis. R 4.1.0 were used for data processing and visualization. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Unsupervised clustering of ICD-related genes in gliomas

Based on the expression profiles of 34 ICD-related genes21, consensus clustering and nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) methods were employed to stratify glioma patients in the CGGA_325 dataset. Based on the consensus matrix for K values from 2 to 10 and the consensus clustering cumulative distribution function (CDF) plot, K = 2 was identified as the best parameter for the consensus clustering method (Figs. 1A-B). A total of 144 and 169 glioma patients were assigned to the C1 and C2 clusters, respectively, and Kaplan–Meier (KM) analysis showed patients in cluster C2 had a longer overall survival (OS) (Fig. 1C). For the NMF method, a rank of 2 was determined as the most suitable number of subgroups according to the cophenetic coefficient and the consensus matrix for rank = 2 to 10 (Figs. 1D-E). The 188 glioma patients were divided into group G1, which exhibited a longer OS (Fig. 1F). PCA further confirmed that both methods could effectively distinguish between patients subgroups (Figs. 1G-H). The aforementioned results indicate that glioma patients can be effectively distinguished according to the expression profiles of ICD-related genes.

Construction and validation of ICD-based signature in gliomas

As shown in Fig. 2A and 482 differentially expressed genes were identified by consensus clustering and NMF methods, respectively, in the CGGA_325 dataset (logFC ≥ 2, FDR ≤ 0.01). The 339 genes identified by both methods were used for further analysis. Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that 250 genes were associated with patient prognosis (p ≤ 0.01). Subsequently, we subjected these genes into LASSO Cox regression model to generate 14 key genes to geneate the ICDS (Fig. 2B-C). The ICDS of each sample was calculated by the following formula: score = (−0.0014 × CABP1) + (−0.1189 × CACNG2) + (−0.0784 × CALN1) + (0.0155 × CCL18) + (0.7515 × CD70) + (−0.0767 × GABRG2) + (−0.0805 × GRIN3A) + (−0.0062 × KCNC2) + (0.0184 × PDZK1IP1) + (−0.2459 × PRLHR) + (−0.0192 × PTPRT) + (0.0513 × SERPINH1) + (−0.0094 × SYCE1) + (−0.0976 × TNR). Patients in both the training and validation (CGGA_693 and CGGA_301) sets were divided into high-risk and low-risk subgroups based on ICDS (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Figs. 1 A, 2 A). Both the scatter plots and the bar plots all indicated that the high-risk subgroups had a higher mortality rate (Fig. 2E-F, Supplementary Figs. 1B-C, 2B-C). In addition, both the principal component analysis (PCA) and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) both confirmed that ICDS was effective in distinguishing glioma patients (Fig. 2G-H, Supplementary Fig. 1D-E, 2D-E). We found that ICDS was significantly inversely associated with OS in glioma patients (Fig. 2I, Supplementary Figs. 1 F, 2 F). We analyzed the expression profiles of 14 core genes in the above three cohorts and found that CD70, SERPINH1, CCL18 and PDZK1IP1 were highly expressed in patients from high-risk subgroups, while the other genes exhibited the opposite trend (Fig. 2J, Supplementary Fig. 1G, 2G).

To explore the impact of ICDS on the prognosis of glioma patients, we conducted the KM analysis and found that patients in the high-risk subgroups had a shorter OS in both the training and validation sets (Fig. 3A-C). Subsequently, we plotted the time-dependent ROC curves, and the results showed that the AUC values for ICDS at 1, 3, and 5 years were all greater than 0.7 in the three datasets (Fig. 3D-F). In addition, the calibration curves also confirmed the reliability of ICDS in predicting patient outcomes (Fig. 3G-I). The above-mentioned analysis results exhibited high consistency in both the training and validation sets. Furthermore, we retrieved glioma mRNA risk models from the PubMed website and excluded studies that did not provide complete model information. Ultimately, 71 signatures were selected for benchmarking the ICDS (Supplementary Table 2). These models were related to various biological processes, including autophagy, ferroptosis, metabolic plasticity, cuproptosis, immune response, pyroptosis, endocytosis, DNA methylation, and inflammation. Through comparative analysis with these models, ICDS demonstrated remarkable performance by ranking 1 st, tied for 2nd, and tied for 4th in C-index for CGGA_325, CGGA_693, and CGGA_301 datasets, respectively (Fig. 4). ICDS demonstrated excellent and stable performance in both the training set and validation sets, while most comparative models only perform well in a single dataset. Additionally, unlike models focusing on single molecular pathways, ICDS systematically integrates multidimensional features through 34 ICD-related genes, including immunogenic cell death, release of DAMPs, and immune cell infiltration, which is more consistent with the clinical heterogeneity of glioma immunity. Based on this, we believe that ICDS will be an excellent model for assessing the prognosis of glioma.

Tumor microenvironment landscape of ICDS-based classification

The primary factors contributing to mortality among glioma patients are tumor recurrences and secondary resistance. Extant research has validated that the tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a pivotal role in the progression of gliomas as well as the development of drug tolerance. Previous studies have confirmed that tumor purity stands as an influential prognostic indicator for glioma24,25. In the CGGA_325, CGGA_693 and CGGA_301 datasets, we found that patients in the high-risk (H-ICDS) subgroups had a higher stromal score, immune score, and ESTIMATE score than those in the low-risk (L-ICDS) subgroups (Fig. 5A-C), while tumor purity exhibited an opposite trend (Fig. 5D-F). Subsequently, we performed KM analysis on the CGGA_325, CGGA_693, and CGGA_301 datasets, and the results showed that patients with higher tumor purity (H-TP) had a better prognosis (Fig. 5G-I). Considering of tumor purity and ICDS, we found that patients in the H-TP & L-ICDS subgroups had the best prognosis (Supplementary Figs. 3 A-C). Drawing upon these findings, we infer that ICDS is closely related to tumor purity and prognosis of glioma.

GSEA analysis was performed on the CGGA_325 dataset to delve into the underlying molecular mechanisms that account for the disparities between patients from H-ICDS and L-ICDS subgroups. We discovered that genes in the H-ICDS subgroup exhibited enrichment in activation of immune response, acute inflammatory response, and adaptive immune response (Fig. 6A). However, genes in the L-ICDS group were enriched in behavior, calcium ion-regulated exocytosis, and neurotransmitter exocytosis (Fig. 6B). We also analyzed the correlation between the infiltration levels of 22 types of immune cells and the expression levels of the 14 core genes. The results showed that CD8+ T cells, macrophages M0, and B cells naive were significantly correlated with most of the core genes (Fig. 6C). In addition, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the immune cell landscape in the CGGA_325 dataset, and found that the infiltration ratios of aDCs, B cells, CD8+ T cells, DCs, iDCs, macrophages, mast cells, pDCs, T helper cells, Tfh, Th1 cells, Th2 cells, TILs, and Tregs were conspicuously elevated in the H-ICDS subgroups (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, our study revealed that the immune function of patients such as APC_co_inhibition, APC_co_stimulation, CCR, checkpoint, cytolytic_activity, HLA, inflammation − promoting, MHC_class_I, parainflammation, T_cell_co − inhibition, T_cell_co-stimulation, type_I_IFN_Reponse, and type_II_IFN_Reponse was markedly upregulated in the H-ICDS subgroup (Fig. 6E). Studies reported that PDCD1, CTLA4, HAVCR2, TIGIT, LAG3 and their related genes played key roles in the immune evasion of cancer cells26. We found that HAVCR2, CEACAM1, LGALS9, LAG3, CLEC4G, PDCD1, CD274, PDCD1LG2, CD96, PVR, CTLA4, CD60, and CD66 were significantly up-regulated in the H-ICDS subgroup (Supplementary Fig. 4). Furthermore, the correlation heatmap showed that there was a significant positive correlation between ICDS and the expression levels of most of the aforementioned immune checkpoint genes (Supplementary Fig. 5). The above results indicate that ICDS is closely associated with immune cell infiltration and immune function in gliomas.

Establishment of a nomogram based on ICDS and clinical characters

To investigate the association between clinical characteristics (age, gender, tumor grade, and IDH mutation status) and ICDS, we compared ICDS among patients in different subgroups. We found that ICDS increased with grade (Supplementary Fig. 6 A). Additionally, patients with IDH wild-type had higher ICDS than those with IDH mutant type (Supplementary Fig. 6B). The ICDS of patients over 60 years old was higher than that of under 60 (Supplementary Fig. 6 C). We found no significant difference in ICDS between males and females (Supplementary Fig. 6D). To confirm that ICDS is a better prognostic indicator than existing clinical features, we first conducted univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses on the CGGA_325 dataset. We found that grade, IDH mutation status, and ICDS were all independent prognostic factors for glioma (Fig. 7A-B). We plotted the 1-, 3- and 5-year ROC curves for ICDS and the aforementioned clinical indicators, and found that the AUC values of ICDS were consistently greater than those of other clinical indicators, but ICDS + Clinical consistently had the largest AUC value (Fig. 7C). The above results suggested that combining ICDS with clinical features will have a stronger predictive ability for glioma prognosis. Subsequently, we developed a nomogram using ICDS and the aforementioned clinical indicators to precisely quantify the 1-, 3- and 5-year survival rates of glioma patients (Fig. 7D). In the CGGA_325 dataset, the AUC values of the nomogram for predicting 1, 3, and 5 years survival rates all surpassed 0.79, indicating a robust predictive capability (Fig. 7E). Furthermore, the calibration curves also confirmed the reliability of the nomogram (Fig. 7F). Therefore, we consider the nomogram to be an excellent indicator for quantitatively evaluating the prognosis of gliomas.

In gliomas, SERPINH1 acts as an oncogene and enhances JAK/STAT signaling

Among the 14 key genes used to develop the ICDS, SERPINH1 showed a significant positive correlation with the ICDS (Supplementary Fig. 8). Furthermore, we discovered that in the datasets of GTEx & TCGA_LGG, GTEx & TCGA_GBM, GSE50161, GSE29796, and GSE4290, the expression of SERPINH1 was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues (Fig. 8A-E). In the GSE4290, CGGA_301, CGGA_325, and CGGA_693 cohorts, we also observed that the expression level of SERPINH1 significantly increased with the elevation of glioma grade (Fig. 8F-I). KM analysis indicated that patients with low SERPINH1 expression had longer OS in the CGGA_301, CGGA_325, CGGA_693, GSE74187, and TCGA_LGG datasets (Fig. 8J-N). The aforementioned results implied that SERPINH1 might be closely related to the initiation and progression of glioma.

To elucidate the role of SERPINH1 in glioma, we silenced SERPINH1 in U251 and LN229 cell lines using siRNA (Fig. 9A-D). The CCK-8 assay indicated that the proliferative capacity of glioma cells was significantly reduced after SERPINHI knockdown (Fig. 9E-F). The results of flow cytometry showed that after SERPINHI1 knockdown, there was a significant decrease in S-phase cell numbers, accompanied by a significant increase in the number of cells in the G1 phase (Fig. 9G-J). The transwell assay results showed that the migration ability of glioma cells was significantly reduced after SERPINH1 knockdown (Fig. 9K-L). The EdU staining results also indicated that the in vitro proliferative capacity of glioma cells was significantly reduced after SERPINH1 knockdown (Fig. 9M-N). Based on the above results, we conclude that SERPINH1 plays a carcinogenic role in glioma.

To further explore the tumor-promoting mechanism of SERPINH1 in gliomas, GSEA was performed on the CGGA_325 dataset. The results indicated that the genes in the group with high SERPINHI1 expression were enriched in the JAK/STAT signaling pathway (Fig. 10A). The results of the correlation analysis indicated that SERPINHI showed a significant positive correlation with both STAT3 and MCL1 in the CGGA_301, CGGA_325, CGGA_693, GSE13041, and GSE74187 datasets (Supplementary Fig. 9; R ≥ 0.45; p < 0.001). Subsequently, we conducted WB and immunofluorescence experiments in U251 and LN229 cell lines to verify the reliability of the correlation analysis results. WB assays revealed that the expression levels of STAT3, p-STAT3, and MCL1 were significantly reduced after knocking down SERPINH1 (Fig. 10B). Immunofluorescence results were consistent with WB assay findings (Fig. 10C-D). Furthermore, we also detected the protein levels of the aforementioned proteins in human glioma tissues via IHC. The results indicated that SERPINHI was also significantly positively correlated with STAT3, p-STAT3, and MCL1 at the protein expression level (Fig. 10E-H). Combining the above results, we propose that SERPINH1 exerts a tumor-promoting effect in glioma by enhancing JAK-STAT3 signaling.

Discussion

Glioma is a common and fatal malignant brain tumor. Currently, the treatment options include surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, but the results are still unsatisfactory, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 10% for patients with glioblastoma27. The immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, tumor heterogeneity, and lack of predictive biomarkers are the major challenges faced in the treatment of gliomas. In recent years, the realm of glioma treatment has witnessed a surge in the exploration and application of immunotherapy. Among the most extensively researched strategies, immune checkpoint inhibitors, chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy, cytokine-based approaches, and tumor vaccines have emerged as leading immunotherapeutic modalities, offering promising avenues for combating glioma28. Immunotherapy has provided remarkable benefits to patients with cancers such as melanoma, liver cancer, and non-small cell lung cancer, and has also shown promise in glioma treatment29–32. However, the effectiveness of immunotherapy in treating gliomas is still limited, and further research is urgently needed. Uncovering the immune evasion mechanisms of gliomas to enhance the overall response rate in glioma patients, and developing reliable biomarkers to screen patients who may benefit from immunotherapy, remain critical issues that need urgent address.

In recent years, ICD has emerged as a highly promising approach for activating anti-tumor immunity, which is characterized by activation of the immune response against dying tumor cells and release of DAMPs. DAMPs are instrumental in facilitating dendritic cells (DCs), the generation of immunostimulatory factors, the activation of APCs (Antigen-Presenting Cells), and the co-stimulation of T cells33. The aforementioned characteristics endow ICD with a function analogous to cancer vaccines, enabling it to convert the “cold” tumor microenvironment into a “hot” one. However, mice with genetic defects in Toll-like receptor4 (TLR4) or Myd88 failed to elicit tumor-specific T-cell immunity when exposed to ICD inducers34. In this work, we developed a biomarker based on ICD-related genes to evaluate the prognosis and immunotherapy sensitivity of glioma patients. We found that based on the expression of ICD-related genes in the CGGA_325 cohort, glioma patients could be classified into two distinct prognostic groups using both consensus clustering and NMF methods (Fig. 1A and H). Subsequently, we employed a series of bioinformatics tools to develop an ICD-related signature, ICDS, which offers guidance for both the prognosis assessment and drug screening of patients (Figs. 2A-C). In the training dataset (CGGA_325) and two external validation datasets (CGGA_693 and CGGA_301), glioma patients with high ICDS had longer OS (Figs. 3A-C). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed ICDS as an independent prognostic factor, unaffected by age, tumor grade, or IDH mutation status (Figs. 7A-B), and the constructed nomogram integrating ICDS and clinical indicators showed robust predictive performance with AUC values > 0.79 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (Fig. 7E), while its calibration curve highly matched actual survival data (Fig. 7F), underscoring the superior quantitative evaluation value of combining ICDS with clinical indicators over single indicators or existing models. In addition, we compared the ICDS with 71 published models that are closely related to many biological processes, such as immune cell infiltration, various forms of cell death, DNA methylation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and metabolism. Furthermore, we compared the ICDS with 71 published signatures that are closely related to numerous biological processes, such as immune cell infiltration, programmed cell death, DNA damage repair, epigenetic modifications, and metabolism. The univariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that ICDS exhibited outstanding predictive capabilities in both the training and validation cohorts, surpassing most published signatures and demonstrating an advantage in predicting the prognosis of glioma patients (Fig. 4). The reasons for the varying rankings of ICDS across different cohorts may include the following: (1) uneven distribution of tumor grades; (2) differences in the proportion of IDH-mutated patients; (3) variations in sequencing platforms used; (4) batch effects. This study for the first time explored the clinical translational potential in drug sensitivity prediction, revealing a significant negative correlation (R > 0.7, p < 2.2e-16) between ICDS and the sensitivity to 7 of 83 tested antitumor drugs (Supplementary Fig. 7). This suggests that low-ICDS patients may derive greater benefit from these medications, providing potential guidance for clinical personalized medication.

ICD-related mRNA risk models have been established and refined in diverse types of cancer, such as melanoma35,36, gastric adenocarcinoma37, clear cell renal carcinoma38, prostate cancer39, cervical cancer40, lung adenocarcinoma41, esophageal cancer42, and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma43. Patients in the ICDS high-risk groups of glioma and the aforementioned tumors have been confirmed to have a poor prognosis. However, patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma classified in the ICDS high-risk group demonstrated a trend towards longer survival44. The variations in the microenvironments of different types of tumors may serve as the primary factors contributing to the disparities in ICDS and prognoses. The results of bioinformatics analysis revealed that glioma patients in the ICDS high-risk group exhibited a strong correlation with high activity of immune response signals, high infiltration of immune cells, and high expression levels of immune checkpoint genes (Fig. 6; Supplementary Fig. 4). These characteristics suggested that glioma patients in the ICDS high-risk group might benefit more from immunotherapy. In the CGGA_325 cohort, patients in the ICDS high-risk group had low tumor purity and a high proportion of CD8 T cells (Figs. 5D-F and 6D). Previous studies have confirmed that a high proportion of CD8 T cells in glioma patients is associated with poor prognosis, indicating the presence of an immunosuppressive TME and T cell exhaustion in gliomas. Furthermore, in the precise stratification of immunotherapy-responsive populations, high-ICDS patients exhibited characteristics of “immune hot tumors”—low tumor purity, high immune cell infiltration (Figs. 5D-F and 6D), and upregulated expression of immune checkpoint genes which align with existing findings that immune checkpoint inhibitors demonstrate better efficacy in highly infiltrated tumors. This indicates that ICDS can serve as a biomarker to assist in screening patients who may benefit from immunotherapy, addressing the deficiency of existing models in immune microenvironment correlation analysis.

SERPINH1, a key gene identified in this study for the development of ICDS, is essential for the proper folding and secretion of collagen45. The SERPINH1 gene is located on chromosome 11q13.5, which is one of the most frequently amplified regions in human cancers. SERPINH1 may promote tumor growth and invasion by regulating the extracellular matrix network, making it a potential biomarker and therapeutic target46. We found that SERPINH1 was abnormally overexpressed in gliomas, and its expression level was positively correlated with the grade of the disease (Figs. 8A-I). Our results were consistent with the reports by Wu et al. and Zhao et al. on gliomas47,48. Existing studies have shown that the expression of SERPINH1 is inhibited by miR-29 in cervical cancer, breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, and gastric cancer45. Zhu et al.‘s research indicated that SERPINH1 could promote the proliferation and invasion of breast cancer, and its expression was positively regulated by the TGF-β signaling pathway46. The expression and function mechanism of SERPINH1 in gliomas remain unclear. Based on our in vitro experiments, SERPINH1 functions as an oncogene in gliomas, as knocking down SERPINH1 significantly downregulated the proliferation and migration capabilities of glioma cells (Fig. 9). The GSEA analysis indicated that the genes of patients with high SERPINH1 expression were enriched in the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, which drove a series of physiological and pathological processes, including proliferation, metabolism, immune response, inflammation, and malignant tumors49. The dysregulation of the JAK/STAT pathway is associated with various solid tumors including breast cancer, prostate cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, glioblastoma, thyroid cancer, and so on50. The results of correlation analysis indicated that SERPINH1 exhibited a significant positive correlation with STAT3 and its downstream molecule MCL1 across five independent cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 9). The IHC results also demonstrated a significant positive correlation between SERPINH1 and STAT3, p-STAT3, and MCL1 in glioma tissues (Figs. 10E-H). Furthermore, after silencing SERPINH1 in glioma cells, the protein expression levels of STAT3, p-STAT3, and SERPINH1 were all downregulated (Fig. 10B). In summary, SERPINH1 may regulate the JAK/STAT signaling pathway to modulate the innate immune response and ICD in gliomas, but further work is needed to confirm this correlation.

In conclusion, this study has successfully developed an Immunogenic Cell Death-Related Score (ICDS) that demonstrates dual utility in prognostic assessment and therapeutic guidance for glioma patients. The ICDS outperforms other mRNA signatures and has the potential to efficiently guide clinical decision-making and surveillance for glioma patients.
