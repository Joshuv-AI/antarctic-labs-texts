NAT10 promotes glycolysis and cell stemness of ovarian cancer cells via ac4C acetylation of PGAM1 mRNA
Authors: Qian Zhang, Yuan Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOvarian cancer represents one of the most prevalent gynecological malignancies with a poor prognosis. Targeting glycolytic pathways has emerged as a novel cancer therapeutic strategy. N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10)-mediated N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification plays a regulatory role in cancer glycolysis. Phosphoglycerate mutase 1 (PGAM1) functions as a critical glycolytic enzyme and potential therapeutic target in oncology. This study investigated the functional role and underlying mechanisms of NAT10 in ovarian cancer progression. Cellular glycolysis was assessed through glucose uptake measurements, lactate production quantification, and extracellular acidification rate analysis. Cell stemness characteristics were evaluated using sphere formation assays and western blot analysis. Molecular mechanisms were explored via quantitative real-time PCR, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP), ac4C-specific RIP, dot blot analysis, and dual-luciferase reporter assays. Elevated NAT10 expression and ac4C modification levels were observed in ovarian cancer cells. NAT10 silencing significantly inhibited both cell stemness properties and glycolytic activity. Mechanistically, NAT10 enhanced PGAM1 mRNA stability through ac4C modification. Re-expression of PGAM1 reversed the functional effects induced by NAT10 depletion in ovarian cancer cells. Furthermore, in vivo tumor growth experiments demonstrated that NAT10 promotes tumorigenesis. Our findings demonstrate that NAT10 facilitates ovarian cancer progression by mediating ac4C modification of PGAM1. Introduction

Ovarian cancer ranks as the third most prevalent gynecological malignancy and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related mortality in women globally1. Approximately 140,000 women succumb to ovarian cancer annually, with ~ 70% of cases diagnosed at advanced stages, resulting in a 5-year survival rate below 30%2. As a highly heterogeneous tumor with marked metastatic potential, ovarian cancer primarily causes mortality through recurrence and acquired drug resistance3. Although therapeutic advances including targeted therapies and immunotherapies have been implemented, their clinical efficacy remains limited4. A deeper understanding of molecular pathogenesis is urgently required to identify novel therapeutic targets. Metabolic reprogramming constitutes a fundamental hallmark of cancer progression, particularly the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis), which is closely associated with tumor growth and chemoresistance5. Malignant cells predominantly utilize aerobic glycolysis to convert glucose into pyruvate, which is subsequently reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), thereby acidifying the tumor microenvironment and promoting tumor progression6. Targeting key enzymes within the glycolytic pathway has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy in oncology7.

More than 170 distinct RNA epigenetic modifications have been identified to date8. These RNA modifications, including m1A, m5C, m6A, m7G, and ac4C, primarily regulate RNA metabolism through post-transcriptional modifications9,10. Accumulating evidence suggests that RNA modifications play critical roles in the initiation and progression of various malignant tumors11. N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modifications, which are conserved chemical modifications that occur in eukaryotic tRNAs, rRNAs, and mRNAs12 that have recently been studied in multiple types of cancer, such as pancreatic, bladder, and gastric cancers13, but have not yet been seen in ovarian cancer. N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10) is considered an RNA ac4C modifier because it is a protein with an acetylase domain and RNA binding domain. NAT10 mainly acts as acetyltransferase to promote tumorigenesis and progression, such as hepatocellular carcinoma14, multiple myeloma15, as well as lung cancer16, and participates in the biological processes of ferroptosis17, glycolysis18, fatty acid metabolism19, cell proliferation, invasion and metastasis20. However, the specific role of NAT10-mediated ac4C modification in ovarian cancer pathogenesis remains poorly understood.

Herein, we investigated the oncogenic role of NAT10 in ovarian cancer progression both in vitro and in vivo. Our findings further demonstrate that NAT10-mediated ac4C modification of phosphoglycerate mutase 1 (PGAM1) mRNA enhances PGAM1 stability, which promotes glycolysis and enhances cell stemness in ovarian cancer cells, thereby driving tumor progression. This study identifies the NAT10-ac4C-PGAM1 axis as a critical regulatory mechanism in ovarian carcinogenesis and highlights its potential as a therapeutic target for ovarian cancer treatment.

Methods

Clinical sample collection

Thirty-six tumor tissue specimens and corresponding adjacent non-tumor tissues from patients with ovarian cancer were collected during the surgery and immediately stored at − 80 degrees for use. Patients were enrolled in this study after signing written informed consent and all tissue samples were verified by pathological examination. The Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan Medical University approved this study.

Cell culture

Non-carcinogenic human normal ovarian epithelial cell line IOSE-80 (Suyan Biotech, Guangzhou, China) and ovarian cancer cell lines CaOV3 and OV90 (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; ATCC) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ATCC), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere.

Cell transfection and treatment

Short hairpin (sh) RNA targeting NAT10 (shNAT10), shRNA negative control (shNC), PGAM1 overexpressing plasmids, and vector were purchased from Shanghai GeneChem. These plasmids were transfected into ovarian cancer cells using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from ovarian cancer cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Next, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). Primers were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotech. The mRNA levels of target genes were measured by qPCR using SYBR Green PCR MasterMix (Solarbio Life Science, Beijing, China). The comparative 2−ΔΔCT method was used to analyze relative mRNA expression in each group.

Ac4C dot blot

Total RNA extracted from ovarian cancer cells was denatured and loaded onto a Hybond-N + membrane. RNAs on the blotted membrane were cross-linked using UV light at 150 mJ/cm2 in a Stratalinker 2400 (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA, USA). Following this, the samples were pre-stained with 0.02% methylene blue solution for 10 min. The membrane was then blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-ac4C antibody (1:1000 dilution; Abcam, ab253039, Cambridgeshire, UK). Subsequent incubation with a Sondary antibody was performed, and signals from the dot blots were visualized using an imaging system.

Cell viability

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Ovarian cancer cells were seeded in 96-well plates, treated with 10 μL CCK-8 reagent, and further incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured at each time point using a microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Offenburg, Germany).

Glucose uptake and lactate production

Glucose uptake levels were determined using a Glucose Uptake Colorimetric Assay Kit (Abcam, catalog number ab136955), and lactate levels were measured using a Lactate Assay Kit (BioVision, Milpitas, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR)

Ovarian cancer cells were incubated until reaching 90% confluence. Real-time ECAR was measured using the XF96 extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, North Billerica, MA, USA). For ECAR analysis, glucose, oligomycin, and 2-DG were sequentially added, and the results were expressed as mpH/min.

Sphere formation assay

Ovarian cancer cells were seeded into 6-well ultra-low cluster plates at a density of 1 × 23 cells/well and incubated with serum-free DMEM/F12 medium containing 4 μg/mL insulin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 2% B27 (Gibco, VA, USA), 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (Sigma-Aldrich), and 20 ng/mL b-fibroblast growth factor (Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 days. The sphere formation rate was quantified.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

The interaction between NAT10 and PGAM1 in ovarian cancer cells was assessed using RIP assays with the MagnaRIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Briefly, ovarian cancer cells were washed, centrifuged, and lysed in RIP lysis buffer on ice. Subsequently, the cell lysates were incubated with anti-NAT10 (#66548, CST, Danvers, MA, USA) or anti-IgG (#2729, CST, Danvers, MA, USA) antibody-coated magnetic beads at 4 °C overnight. Following purification, RNA was extracted from the immunoprecipitated complexes and analyzed by qPCR.

Ac4C RIP

The ac4C RIP assay was performed to assess ac4C enrichment of PGAM1 in ovarian cancer cells using the GenSeq ac4C RIP Kit (Cloudseq Biotech, catalog number GS-ET-005, Shanghai, China). Briefly, total RNA was extracted and randomly fragmented to a size of approximately 200 nt. A 252 µL reaction mixture containing 2 µL ac4C antibody, magnetic beads, fragmented RNA, and 200 µL enzyme-free water was incubated together. Subsequently, the RNAs were purified, and the mRNA levels were analyzed by qPCR.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The wild-type (wt) and mutant-type (mut) sequences of PGAM1 were individually cloned into the pGL3-Basic luciferase reporter plasmid (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Subsequently, sh-NC or sh-NAT10, along with the wild-type or mutant-type reporter plasmid and the pRL-TK vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), were co-transfected into ovarian cancer cells using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured according to the manufacturer’s protocols for the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega), and the firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Bioinformatic analysis

The potential acetylation sites of PGAM1 mRNA were predicted using the PACES database (http://www.rnanut.net/paces/).

Detection of RNA stability

NAT10-knockdown ovarian cancer cells were treated with 5 μg/mL actinomycin D (Act-D; Abbott Molecular, Abbott Park, IL, USA) for 1, 4, 8, and 12 h. The relative expression levels of PGAM1 in total RNA were then quantified by qPCR.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from ovarian cancer cells, and protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). Proteins were resolved via 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. Following transfer, the membrane was blocked using a rapid protein-blocking solution and incubated overnight at 4 °C with a 1:1000 dilution of primary antibody. The next day, the membrane was washed three times with TBST and incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, ab172130, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, protein bands were visualized using a ChemiDoc™ XR + imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Animal study

6-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Nanjing GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at 20–26 °C with 40–60% humidity in a 12-h light/dark cycle. Mice were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 6 per group). Under 3% isoflurane anesthesia via inhalation (Sigma-Aldrich), ovarian cancer cells transfected with shNC or shNAT10 were subcutaneously injected into the dorsal flanks of mice at a density of 5 × 10⁶ cells per mouse. Tumor volumes were measured using vernier calipers starting 1 week post-injection and monitored weekly for four consecutive weeks, calculated using the formula: volume (mm3) = 0.5 × length × width2. 35 days post-injection, mice were euthanized via inhalation of 5% isoflurane (Sigma-Aldrich), and tumors were excised for further analysis. Tumor weights were also recorded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan Medical University. All animal experiments were complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) analysis

HE staining was performed using the HE Staining Kit (G1120, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for histopathological analysis. Tumor tissues were processed for paraffin embedding and sectioned into 5-μm-thick slices as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Following standard dewaxing and rehydration protocols, sections were stained with hematoxylin for 7 min and eosin for 5 min. Subsequently, the slides were dehydrated through graded ethanol, air-dried, and mounted with neutral mounting medium. Histological images were captured using an Olympus optical microscope.

Statistical analysis

All data (n ≥ 3 independent experiments) were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data distribution for each group was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (α = 0.05). Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test (for two-group comparisons) or one-way ANOVA (for multi-group comparisons), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For comparisons involving two groups, homogeneity of variance was evaluated using F-test. For comparisons involving multiple groups, homogeneity of variances was confirmed using Levene’s test. Non-parametric alternatives were employed if data significantly deviated from normality or lacked homogeneity of variance. A p value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Results

The level of ac4C modification and NAT10 expression are significantly up-regulated in ovarian cancer

To investigate the roles of mRNA modifications in ovarian cancer, we quantified ac4C levels and NAT10 mRNA expression in ovarian cancer tissues and their paired adjacent normal tissues using dot blot assays and qPCR, respectively. Ac4C levels were significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1A). Similarly, tumor tissues exhibited significantly higher NAT10 expression than non-tumor samples (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, ovarian cancer cell lines showed marked upregulation of both ac4C and NAT10 (Fig. 1C, D).

The level of ac4C modification and NAT10 expression are significantly up-regulated in ovarian cancer. (A) The level of ac4C modification in 3 pairs of ovarian cancer tumors and adjacent normal tissues was detected by dot blot. (B) The NAT10 mRNA levels in 36 pairs of ovarian cancer and adjacent normal tissues were analyzed by qPCR. (C–D) The levels of ac4C modification and NAT10 mRNA in ovarian cancer cell lines and IOSE-80 cells were detected by dot blot and qPCR respectively. Data are shown as mean ± SD of independent experiments.

Silencing of NAT10 suppresses glycolysis and cell stemness in ovarian cancer cells

To investigate the functional role of NAT10 in ovarian cancer, NAT10 was knocked down following shNAT10 transfection into ovarian cancer cells (Fig. 2A). Knockdown of NAT10 significantly reduced cell viability (Fig. 2B). Consistently, NAT10 depletion suppressed glucose uptake and lactate production in ovarian cancer cells (Fig. 2C, D). Furthermore, ECAR assays revealed a marked decline in glycolytic capacity following NAT10 silencing (Fig. 2E). Additionally, NAT10 knockdown impaired ovarian cancer cell spheroid formation (Fig. 2F). Western blot analysis demonstrated that NAT10 silencing downregulated the expression of stemness marker proteins Nanog and SOX2 in ovarian cancer cells (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these findings indicate that NAT10 inhibition suppresses ovarian cancer cell stemness and glycolytic metabolism.

Silencing of NAT10 suppresses glycolysis and cell stemness in ovarian cancer cells. (A) The transfection efficiency was detected using qPCR. (B) The cell viability was detected by CCK-8 assay. (C–D) Effects of NAT10 knockdown on glucose uptake and lactate production in ovarian cancer cells. (E) The glycolytic capability of ovarian cancer cells was evaluated using the ECAR curve. (F) The sphere formation rate of ovarian cancer cells. (G) The protein expression of Nanog and SOX2 was measured using a western blot. Data are shown as mean ± SD of independent experiments. (n = 3).

NAT10 promotes tumor growth in vivo

To assess the in vivo tumorigenic potential of NAT10, ovarian cancer cells transfected with shNC or shNAT10 were used to generate xenograft tumor models. Tumor volume and weight were significantly reduced in the NAT10-knockdown group compared to controls, demonstrating that NAT10 silencing markedly suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 3A–C). HE staining revealed that tumor tissues in the shNC group exhibited high cellularity, marked cellular atypia, frequent mitotic figures, and disorganized architecture—characteristic features of aggressive neoplastic growth. In contrast, tumors from the shNAT10 group displayed significantly reduced cellularity, decreased nuclear pleomorphism, diminished mitotic activity, and increased necrotic areas (Fig. 3D). Our findings confirmed that NAT10 silencing impairs ovarian cancer progression by attenuating malignant cellular behaviors.

NAT10 promotes tumor growth in vivo. (A–C) The tumor size, volume, and weight in shNAT10 and shNC groups. (D) HE staining of xenograft tumors. Data are shown as mean ± SD of independent experiments. (n = 6).

Nat10 facilitates the ac4C acetylation of PGAM1 mRNA

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying NAT10 function in ovarian cancer, we assessed ac4C modification levels and PGAM1 mRNA expression in NAT10-knockdown cells. Ac4C RIP assays revealed reduced ac4C modification at PGAM1 mRNA, while qPCR confirmed decreased PGAM1 mRNA levels following NAT10 silencing (Fig. 4A, B). The interaction between NAT10 and PGAM1 was further validated by RIP (Fig. 4C). Potential acetylation sites (at the cytosine base at position 797) on PGAM1 mRNA were predicted using the PACES database (Fig. 4D). Luciferase assays demonstrated that NAT10 silencing significantly attenuated luciferase activity in wild-type PGAM1 cells but had no effect on mutant-type PGAM1 cells (Fig. 4E). Additionally, PGAM1 mRNA stability was diminished in NAT10-knockdown cells (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that NAT10 stabilizes PGAM1 mRNA via ac4C modification in ovarian cancer.

Nat10 facilitates the ac4C acetylation of PGAM1 mRNA. (A) The ac4C RIP assay detected ac4C acetylation of PGAM1 mRNA in ovarian cancer cells upon NAT10 knockdown. (B) The mRNA level of PGAM1 was measured by qPCR. (C) RIP assay confirming the interaction of NAT10 and PGAM1 mRNA. IgG was utilized for internal control. (D) The potential acetylation site of PGAM1 mRNA was predicted using the PACES database. (E) Luciferase assay was conducted to examine the binding relationship between NAT10 and PGAM1. (F) The stability of PGAM1 mRNA was measured by qPCR after ovarian cancer cells were treated with actinomycin D at 1, 4, 8, and 12 h. Data are shown as mean ± SD of independent experiments. (n = 3).

PGAM1 reverses the cell stemness and glycolysis of ovarian cancer cells induced by NAT10 silencing

Rescue experiments were performed to determine the role of PGAM1 in NAT10-mediated ovarian cancer cell processes. Ovarian cancer cells transfected with PGAM1 overexpression vectors exhibited elevated PGAM1 mRNA levels (Fig. 5A). Overexpression of PGAM1 reversed the suppression of cell viability caused by NAT10 silencing (Fig. 5B). Subsequently, PGAM1 overexpression counteracted the inhibitory effects of NAT10 knockdown on glucose uptake and lactate production in ovarian cancer cells (Fig. 5C, D). ECAR assays showed that NAT10 silencing reduced glycolytic activity, which was restored by PGAM1 overexpression (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, NAT10 knockdown suppressed spheroid formation and downregulated the stemness markers Nanog and SOX2, but these effects were reversed by PGAM1 overexpression (Fig. 5F, G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that NAT10 promotes ovarian cancer cell stemness and glycolysis by stabilizing PGAM1.

(B) The cell viability was detected by CCK-8 assay. (C–D) Levels of glucose uptake and lactate production in ovarian cancer cells. (E) The glycolytic capability of ovarian cancer cells was evaluated using the ECAR curve. (F) The sphere formation rate of ovarian cancer cells. (G) The protein expression of Nanog and SOX2 was measured using a western blot. Data are shown as mean ± SD of independent experiments. (n = 3).

Discussion

NAT10, an oncogene implicated in cell proliferation and migration, is abnormally upregulated in numerous human cancers21. In gynecological malignancies, NAT10 promotes cervical cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and migration by stabilizing HNRNPUL1 mRNA through ac4C modification22. Similarly, NAT10-mediated ac4C modification of MDR1 and BCRP drives breast cancer progression23. Notably, NAT10 is closely associated with ovarian cancer, a disease characterized by high replication stress levels, as it contributes to oncogene-induced replication stress24. Additionally, NAT10 is essential for the growth of Stem-A, a molecular subtype of epithelial ovarian cancer25. However, the precise biological function of NAT10, particularly its role in ac4C modification, in ovarian cancer remains unclear. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that both ac4C mRNA modification and its regulator NAT10 are elevated in ovarian cancer. Functional experiments reveal that NAT10 promotes ovarian cancer cell stemness and glycolysis in vitro and tumorigenesis in vivo. Importantly, these oncogenic effects depend on NAT10’s ac4C modification activity. Our findings establish that NAT10-mediated ac4C modification regulates ovarian cancer progression by modulating glycolytic metabolism.

Glycolysis is a hallmark of cancer progression, characterized by elevated activity of glucose uptake enzymes and lactate-producing transporters. Inhibition of glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation has been shown to suppress ovarian cancer cell growth and induce apoptosis26. For instance, the long non-coding RNA LINC00629 interacts with and destabilizes c-Myc, thereby inhibiting glycolysis and suppressing ovarian cancer progression27. Notably, NAT10-mediated ac4C modification has emerged as a critical regulator of glycolytic metabolism in cancer cells18. Studies have demonstrated that the NAT10/ac4C/FOXP1 axis enhances glycolysis and lactate secretion, promoting the malignant progression of cervical cancer28. Furthermore, NAT10-mediated ac4C modification supports colon cancer cell stemness by stabilizing key transcripts29.Building on these findings, we demonstrate for the first time that NAT10-mediated ac4C modification drives both glycolytic metabolism and stemness in ovarian cancer cells.

Our findings revealed that PGAM1 mRNA is a direct ac4C target of NAT10. PGAM1 encodes a critical glycolytic enzyme that catalyzes the interconversion of 2-phosphoglycerate and 3-phosphoglycerate. Accumulating evidence indicates that PGAM1 is aberrantly upregulated in multiple cancers and plays a pivotal role in tumor progression, establishing it as a promising therapeutic target30. For instance, Qiu et al. demonstrated that PGAM1 drives aerobic glycolysis-mediated growth and metastasis in hepatocellular carcinoma31. Ke et al. showed that PGAM1-mediated glycolysis promotes breast cancer progression32. Besides, MicroRNA-324-3p suppresses osteosarcoma progression by inhibiting PGAM1-dependent aerobic glycolysis33. Downregulation of PGAM1 impairs prostate cancer cell growth, migration, and invasion34. Critically, PGAM1 accelerates glycolytic metabolism in ovarian cancer cells through pyruvate and lactate production35. In this study, we identified that NAT10 binds to PGAM1 mRNA and enhances its stability via ac4C modification, thereby upregulating PGAM1 expression. Furthermore, we observed that PGAM1 overexpression rescues the inhibitory effects of NAT10 knockdown on glycolysis and cell stemness in ovarian cancer cells. Collectively, these data establish that NAT10-mediated ac4C modification stabilizes PGAM1 mRNA, amplifying glycolytic metabolism and driving ovarian cancer progression.

This study should also acknowledge some limitations. First, the clinical sample size is relatively modest, and validation in larger, independent cohorts, particularly stratified by histologic subtypes and stages, is needed to confirm the clinical correlation between NAT10/ac4C levels and patient survival, chemotherapy response, or disease stage. Second, the functional validation relied primarily on cell lines and xenograft models, which may not fully recapitulate the complexity of the human tumor microenvironment. Orthotopic or patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models could provide more clinically relevant insights. Third, although PGAM1 was identified as a key downstream target, other potential ac4C-modified transcripts contributing to ovarian cancer pathogenesis remain unexplored. Future studies should employ transcriptome-wide ac4C sequencing (ac4C-seq) to comprehensively identify NAT10-regulated targets beyond PGAM1. Additionally, while our experiments demonstrated the functional role of NAT10 in glycolysis and cell stemness, the study did not investigate the potential interplay between NAT10 and other metabolic pathways or tumor microenvironment factors.

Therapeutically, this work identifies NAT10 and its ac4C-mediated regulation of PGAM1 as promising targets for ovarian cancer intervention. Given the documented role of PGAM1 in paclitaxel resistance and our demonstration of NAT10-driven glycolysis/stemness, inhibiting this axis may overcome chemoresistance—a major clinical challenge. Small-molecule NAT10 inhibitors (e.g., Remodelin) already show efficacy in other cancers and warrant testing in ovarian cancer models, particularly in combination with standard chemotherapy. Furthermore, detecting ac4C levels in patient serum or tumor biopsies could serve as a novel diagnostic or prognostic biomarker. Validating these findings in clinical cohorts and developing targeted inhibitors against NAT10’s acetyltransferase activity represent critical next steps toward translating this mechanistic insight into improved ovarian cancer therapies.

In summary, our findings delineate a previously unrecognized oncogenic axis in ovarian cancer: NAT10-mediated ac4C acetylation stabilizes PGAM1 mRNA, thereby enhancing PGAM1 expression, fueling glycolysis, promoting cell stemness, and driving tumor progression. This study provides the first evidence linking NAT10/ac4C modification directly to metabolic reprogramming and stemness in ovarian cancer through PGAM1 regulation. While PGAM1 emerges as a critical effector within this pathway, the potential contribution of other ac4C-modified transcripts warrants future exploration using transcriptome-wide approaches. The identification of the NAT10-ac4C-PGAM1 axis not only expands our understanding of the molecular underpinnings of ovarian cancer pathogenesis but also unveils novel and promising therapeutic targets (NAT10, ac4C modification, PGAM1) for intervention. Targeting this axis, particularly given NAT10’s role in PGAM1-driven glycolysis and stemness potentially linked to chemoresistance, may offer a strategic avenue to overcome treatment barriers in this lethal malignancy.
