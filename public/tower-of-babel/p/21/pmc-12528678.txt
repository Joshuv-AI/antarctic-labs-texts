A geography of indoors for analyzing global ways of living using computer vision
Authors: Martina Mazzarello, Mikita Klimenka, Rohit Sanatani, Javad Eshtiyagh, Yanhua Yao, Paolo Santi, Fabio Duarte, Richard Florida, Carlo Ratti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Globalization is claimed to have a homogenizing effect, reducing pronounced local cultural differences. Indoor living spaces are among the most vivid expressions of local culture, yet they remain underexplored in this context. Our visual AI framework, utilizing a unique dataset of over 400,000 Airbnb images, investigates the diversity in living spaces across 80 cities. By employing deep learning classification models, Gradient-Weighted Class Activation Mapping techniques, and statistical analysis, we demonstrate that both geographic proximity and the extent of globalization significantly correlate with the visual characteristics of indoor spaces (R = 0.23–0.30 and R ≈ 0.47, respectively). Our results indicate that despite global pressures and trends towards cultural homogenization, local identities and cultural distinctions nevertheless remain.

Introduction

The question of whether and how global pressures reduce or eliminate local cultural distinctiveness has been the subject of considerable research. The theory of ’cultural imperialism’ argues that Western media and economic dominance undermine local traditions and marginalize non-dominant cultures1. McDonaldization’ describes the global standardization of cultural practices, exemplified by fast-food chains2. The similar construct of “IKEAization” refers to the global diffusion of Scandinavian design, creating shared communities of taste while maintaining standardized functionality3,4. Hybridization theory, by contrast, suggests that globalization fosters the mixing and blending of cultural elements, leading to the emergence of new cultural forms and identities5. Robertson’s concept of “glocalization” discusses the interplay between global and local influences, suggesting that local cultures adapt and coexist with global trends rather than being entirely subsumed6. On the other hand, Friedman’s “The World is Flat” posits that globalization has created a level playing field, reducing the uniqueness of local cultures7. Finally, the theory of “regionalism” underscores the resilience and adaptability of local traditions in coexisting with global influences, offering a counterbalance to homogenizing forces.

The omnipresence of social networks and global markets has reshaped and often homogenized cultural references, from music to food to home furniture. Indoor environments, including physical layout, configuration, and objects, serve as enduring markers of local cultural identity and societal values8–10. Since people spend 90% of their time indoors11, how living spaces are arranged—such as furniture layout, materials, and colors—shapes how people live every day12–15. They are cultural signifiers that reveal insights into the lives and values of those who inhabit these spaces16–18. Historically, these cultural signifiers were specific to certain times and geographies—whether a Manhattan apartment in the 1960 s, an early 20th-century English townhouse, or a Japanese home modeled on tatami mats—reflecting deeply embedded cultural values that persist despite global influences8,10.

Local culture includes unique practices, materials, and aesthetics tied to specific geographies and histories. In contrast, globalization refers to the homogenization resulting from increased interconnectedness and the exchange of ideas, goods, and cultural practices across borders. While scholars have discussed the dialectics between global forces and local practices for decades now6,19, there appears to be a void in terms of assessing its implications at a large quantitative scale. Recent work on urban studies has been taking advantage of cutting-edge computer vision techniques to analyze the plethora of visual data produced in cities worldwide20–23. Some examples related to the current study include finding hidden styles in city dwellings by analyzing geolocated Airbnb rental images in ten major cities24, unveiling the potential of image recognition techniques for visual information mining in hospitality and tourism25,26, and connecting cities geographically distant through the visual similarities of pictures posted on social media27. Building on28, whose AI-based framework analyzes visual diversity in urban neighborhoods, we expand the concept to interior spaces, addressing a previously unexplored domain. Despite the mentioned studies, the interaction between globalization and local cultures, particularly in intimate spaces like indoor environments, has not been fully investigated.

To test the propositions regarding the persistence of local distinctiveness in the face of globalization, we gather and study a unique dataset of more than 400,000 Airbnb images across 80 global cities, ranging from large metropolitan areas to small urban settings. Airbnb provides a unique lens for studying the interplay between globalization and local culture. As a global platform, it captures spaces that cater to international tourists while simultaneously reflecting local design practices, materials, and cultural traditions. Although the images on the platform represent short-term rentals rather than primary residences—and may reflect curated or commercially optimized interiors—they offer a globally consistent source of data. We acknowledge that such listings may differ from how people design their own homes. However, the scale and geographic coverage of Airbnb images make them uniquely valuable for this kind of global study. Moreover, we performed a robustness check using listings from other platforms (Craigslist, Zillow, Zigbang, Ohou), which showed that the patterns we observe in Airbnb data are consistent with broader interior trends. This robustness strengthens the validity of using Airbnb as a dataset to explore global-local cultural dynamics in interior spaces.

This study uses visual AI to analyze indoor living spaces based on design, appearance, and object prevalence across regions. We combine deep learning for image classification, GradCAM for visual analysis, and statistical methods to correlate findings with external factors. This method identifies patterns of similarity and difference in how indoor spaces are designed and link these patterns to global variables such as the KoF globalization index and airline traffic.

We investigate the extent to which indoor spaces retain their local cultural identities amidst the homogenizing forces of globalization. Our analysis encompasses both external factors, such as geographic proximity and degree of globalization, and indoor features, including the diversity in room aesthetics, the assortment of items within these spaces, and the presence of distinctive objects that contribute to visual classification. In addition, we also explore the impact of socioeconomic connectivity, which we choose to represent through airline traffic between cities.

The central inquiry of this research is as follows: Do Airbnb listings exhibit global homogeneity, or do they retain distinctive local characteristics? Should patterns of similarity emerge, what factors predominantly contribute to these patterns? Are they influenced more significantly by geographic proximity, the degree of globalization, or the size of the city?

This study deepens our understanding of globalization’s influence on cultural expression within indoor environments, an underexplored yet vital domain. The findings emphasize regional variations and the persistence of local cultural identities amidst homogenizing global forces, showcasing the value of AI-driven methodologies in cultural analysis.

Results

Our investigation examined the indoor features like visual qualities and object occurrences observed in Airbnb listings globally and examined the role of external factors such as geographic location and degree of globalization in shaping the interiors’ aesthetic uniqueness. Leveraging deep learning for image analysis and object recognition algorithms allowed us to evaluate 400,000 images across a strategic selection of 80 cities. For each of the eight geographic regions of North America, South America, Europe, Africa, West Asia, South Asia, East Asia, and Oceania, by selecting the five most populous cities in which Airbnb is active. Then for each of these large cities, we select a local city nearby with a relatively small population that has a sufficiently large number of Airbnb listings for training and testing purposes.

The main finding of our study is that there is still a pronounced retention of local cultural attributes in living spaces, defying the common belief in globalization’s homogenizing influence. Firstly, we establish that geographic proximity is a critical factor. We find geographically closer cities tend to exhibit more visual similarities, with a significant correlation (R = 0.23) between geographic distance and visual difference. This phenomenon is not confined to local and relatively isolated cities but extends to cosmopolitan centers, which also exhibit correlations (R = 0.26) between distance and visual difference, and thus indicate similar retention of local attributes.

In addition to geographic proximity, we explored the role of globalization — measured using the KOF index29 — as possible a determinant of visual characteristics in living spaces. We also examined the role of socio-economic connections between cities, measured using the volume of direct flights between large city pairs. Our findings suggest a marked impact of degree of globalization on the visual difference between indoor spaces (R = 0.47 ± 0.10).

We will present, moreover, how our analysis delves into city-specific object taxonomies, revealing global differences and identifying distinct indoor elements that define the most visually divergent cities. The influence of location and degree of globalization on the visual profile of interiors

The analysis of the latent space representation indicates that physical distance significantly influences the visual similarity between living spaces. This phenomenon presents one of the first empirical demonstrations of the “first law of geography” in the context of the visual qualities of indoor living spaces—everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related than distant things30. Furthermore, the difference in globalization between cities also plays a crucial role in shaping the visual similarities and distinctions among these spaces across various urban environments. To further examine these influences, we will present an analysis that highlights how these factors differently affect room types and the visual profiles of interiors in both large metropolitan areas and smaller cities.

Locational analysis—visual similarity and geographic proximity

In our study, we analyze the relationship between the physical location of living spaces and their visual characteristics by examining two specific types of distances. The first, geographic distance (also known as great-circle distance), refers to the shortest distance between any two points (cities) on Earth as measured along the surface of the sphere31. This is how we quantify the physical separation between city centers. The second, Euclidean feature-space distance, involves measuring the Euclidean straight-line distance between points in a multi-dimensional space32 where each dimension represents a specific visual feature of the interior spaces. This method helps us to quantify the visual similarity or difference between these spaces. Through comprehensive analysis comparing every possible pair of city centroids, our findings reveal consistent and statistically significant correlations between these two measures of distance. While the strength of this relationship, as indicated by Pearson’s correlation coefficients, varies among different room types, we observe correlation (R) values as high as 0.30 as shown in Figure 3. This provides strong evidence that the geographic location significantly influences the visual appearance of these spaces, suggesting that spaces closer together geographically tend to share more visual similarities than those farther apart.Fig. 3Relationships between geographic distance and visual similarity for every large city (top) and small city (bottom) pair.

Visual similarity and degree of globalization

While physical proximity has been shown to influence the visual similarity of living spaces, the correlation factor is moderate, hinting at the existence of additional factors that might influence visual similarity. We use the KOF Index of Globalization29 as a well-established measure of degree of globalization of a particular country, encompassing diverse metrics like trade flows, travel volumes, investment and cultural contacts. Given that KOF index is calculated for countries, not particular cities, in this section we label our visual data by country class, not city.

Our findings show a consistent and statistically significant positive correlation (R = 0.47, 95% CI: ±0.10) between the difference in globalization between cities and the feature-space distances observed between them (Figure 4). Visual identifiers of indoor features

While the findings discussed above reveal important macro-trends pertaining to indoor visual qualities across the world, an added focus of this work was also to uncover qualitative and explanatory aspects behind such quantitative findings. The analysis of Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (GradCAM)33 highlights which visual elements were most influential in determining city-specific classifications, revealing that certain objects—either through their presence or unique appearance—serve as markers of visual identity. These findings support the notion that while global influences are present, localized design traits still play a key role in shaping visual profiles.

Figure 5 summarizes the relative importance of distinct objects within living rooms of cities. Essentially, we identify two primary types of elements that contribute to a room’s visual identity across different room types. In the first type, it is the presence of certain elements, either alone or in combination with others, that makes the room identifiable as belonging to a specific city. While individual objects such as flowers, cushions, or pictures are indeed common, their particular combination, frequency, spatial arrangement, and integration within the interior contribute to a city’s distinct visual signature. These patterns, surfaced by the model’s activation weights, offer a nuanced understanding of how design cues vary subtly yet meaningfully across regions. In some cases, like Los Angeles, these combinations can also include outdoor elements such as palm trees visible through windows. The second type of element is defined not only by its presence but by its specific visual appearance—for example, architectural features such as walls, windows, doors, or flooring. In these cases, it is not simply that a wall is present in Chennai or a window in Paris, but that the particular look or style of these elements contributes to a city’s distinct visual identity. It is important to note that the visual features that define a city’s indoor spaces do not always follow expected patterns based on geography, culture, or economy. Cities that are similar in these ways may still have different visual identities, and vice versa. These features act as stand-alone indicators of local style. While they help us understand how interior design differs across places, linking them directly to geographic or social factors would require more detailed mappings, which are beyond the scope of this study.

Comparing the impacts of geographic proximity and difference in globalization

Geographic proximity and difference in globalization play distinct roles in shaping the visual identity of interior spaces. Scatterplots in Appendix A.6 validate that there is no correlation between geographic distance and difference in globalization for the cities in our dataset. Geographic distance predominantly affects the overall visual profile of interiors, influencing aspects such as style and layout. In contrast, globalization is more closely tied to the specific objects found within these spaces.

To demonstrate this, we explored the relationship between both geographic distance and differences in globalization and their respective impacts on visual and object-related differences in interior spaces. It is important to note that the visual differences we identify through our neural network approach involve both the appearance of objects and their presence within a scene. In other words a neural network classifier makes a decision about classifying an image, it considers both how objects look and what kind of objects are present in the scene. In order to describe the differences between images in terms of only how the objects look, we need a different variable. To do that, we computed pure visual difference between images - a measure of difference that considers the differences obtained from a neural network classifier and removes the influence of the object difference. This was achieved by computing a residual variable as described in Appendix A.3. Discussion

Our findings challenge the common view that globalization removes local differences. By analyzing more than 400,000 Airbnb images across 80 cities, we show that despite global design influences, local visual identities persist. Our classification model achieved an accuracy of 0.43–0.47 (Appendix A.4), across 40 different classes. This is significantly above random chance (0.025) and shows that visual cues in interiors remain strong enough to distinguish cities with a reasonable level of accuracy. These patterns emerge from the coexistence of two dynamics. On one hand, cities that are geographically closer tend to exhibit more visually similar interiors (R ≈ 0.23–0.30), particularly in terms of stylistic elements such as textures, colors, or materials. On the other hand, cities with similar globalization scores (measured via the KoF Index) show a stronger correlation in object composition (R ≈ 0.47), indicating that global forces increasingly shape the presence of shared furnishings and decor elements.

This duality reveals that both geographic and global forces shape indoor design in distinct but overlapping ways: geographic proximity primarily influences how objects look—their visual style and appearance—while globalization more strongly determines which objects are present in a room. This interpretation is supported by residual and AIC-based analyses (Appendix A.3), which show that proximity better explains visual differences, whereas globalization more significantly predicts object composition.

The increasing availability of visual data from streaming platforms, social media, and short-term rentals offers new opportunities to analyze cultural patterns and human behavior. The vast dataset enables more granular investigations into how cultural imprints shape indoor spaces.

Despite Airbnb’s focus on short-term rentals, robustness checks using alternative datasets—Craigslist, Zillow, and Zig- bang—confirm that its visual patterns align with broader real estate trends. These findings confirm Airbnb’s representativeness, demonstrating its reflection of broader cultural trends and providing valuable insights into globalization’s impact on indoor living spaces. Consequently, Airbnb offers valuable insights into globalization’s impact on indoor spaces (See Appendix A.7).

At the same time, our results show that globalization plays an equally important role in shaping how homes look. Cities with similar globalization scores exhibit more visually similar interiors (R ≈ 0.47), particularly in terms of object composition. This suggests that while local design traits persist, they are layered with increasingly common global trends and items. Still, geography also plays a strong role—cities that are closer together often have more similar interiors. This shows that both global and local factors matter in shaping indoor design. Even moderate correlations observed consistently across global data can reveal meaningful patterns in how space is visually configured.

In addition to the comprehensive measure of globalization represented by the KoF index, we have explored the impact of socioeconomic connectivity between cities on their visual differences as one of the components of globalization. We used air traffic as a proxy for socioeconomic connectivity, given its established link to globalization35–37. However, factors such as layovers, passenger types, and market dynamics complicate the link between air traffic and economic connectivity. Nevertheless, We observed a connection between flight volume and visual similarity and included these experiments in the Appendix A.2 to supplement our study.

Similarities between cities like Madrid and London may stem from historical and linguistic ties, as well as the widespread influence of globalized retail networks, a previously discussed concept of IKEAization. This phenomenon connects to geographic proximity and socioeconomic ties while also encompassing broader factors, presenting opportunities for future research.

While this study demonstrates the potential of visual AI in analyzing cultural dynamics, several limitations remain. Current visual AI models struggle to detect subtle personal items, such as decorative objects or cultural artifacts. Distinguishing between ’composition’ (element arrangement) and ’appearance’ (aesthetic attributes) is crucial to understanding the balance between function and style. This distinction is crucial for understanding how cultural identity is shaped by stylistic presentation and spatial arrangement. Local knowledge may often be implicitly captured in the visual qualities of indoor spaces and not explicitly in objects. For example, specific features such as the traditional layout of a Japanese tatami room may not be directly labeled but are inherently distinguishable through their unique visual and compositional characteristics. The consistency of visual patterns across these platforms supports the robustness of our findings. Addressing these challenges will require refined algorithms and expanded datasets in future work. Prior research in design and architecture has often explored decorative elements such as materials or color schemes, but typically through qualitative or small-scale analysis. Our approach captures these features implicitly through latent representations, allowing us to work at scale. However, explicitly integrating interpretable features—such as recurrent color palettes or common materials like wood, brick, or fabric—could make the analysis more robust and transparent. This remains a promising direction for future studies.

These observations open avenues for further investigation into how functional requirements and cultural practices influence design susceptibility to global influences.

This study demonstrates the power of visual AI in analyzing cultural dynamics on a global scale. We combine AI methods (Latent space analysis and Grad-CAM) with globalization metrics to show how both global and local factors shape how homes look around the world. Our approach contributes to the discourse on ‘glocalization’ and ‘hybridization,’ illustrating how interior design embodies the balance between cultural resilience and global influences.

Our findings also reveal room-specific variations in resistance to globalization, as evident in differing strengths of correlation between geographic distance and visual similarity. Kitchens tend to retain stronger local characteristics, often reflecting cultural traditions and daily rituals, while living rooms exhibit greater uniformity, likely driven by standardized design trends. These observations open avenues for further investigation into how functional requirements and cultural practices influence design susceptibility to global influences.

Finally, this study transcends its methodological focus on visual AI and situates itself within the broader discourse on cities as cultural and social ecosystems. By showing how globalization and local traditions shape indoor spaces, this study highlights cities as dynamic hubs where global and local influences intersect. These findings highlight the role of urban spaces in balancing global influences with cultural resilience. This research offers a framework for understanding how cities balance global and local influences, providing insights that extend beyond computational methods to inform urban and cultural studies.

Methods

Indoor features

Indoor features refers to the visual qualities of living spaces and differences between their appearances. We used three ways to explore these indoor features, including Visual Difference, Object Difference, and Grad-CAM.

Exploring Visual Differences between indoor spaces is our main method of analyzing indoor features, which we subsequently compare with external factors covered later in the Methods section. By training a neural network on images from the dataset across the world, we obtained a model that captured essential visual distinctions of cities and was capable of identifying a city type from a given image. Instead of using the trained network in a classification task, we instead study how the network describes images visually relative to each other. To do so, we ran model prediction inference on each test image and extracted a penultimate layer from the neural network, which corresponded to the placement of a certain image in a space of latent features. We used dimensionality reduction to plot how images from different cities visually relate to each other, and observe the visual identity of images across cities relative to each other, similar to38 (Figure 7).Fig. 7The unsupervised clustering approach.

The unsupervised clustering approach.

Visual difference

A robust comparison of diverse images requires developing a common set of parameters that the visual data points can be evaluated upon. Such parameters can include distribution of colors and textures24, count of specific objects39, visual clutter40, or any other visual or indoor layout conditions41 that can help us draw meaningful conclusions about the difference between images. However, the introduction of highly specific comparison parameters such as color or object count imposes bias on the study as it limits the comparison to a specific parameter. For example, comparing apartments in different continents by wall color is one of the many experiments we may design which asserts the assumption of walls as a defining cultural feature of indoor spaces. We instead seek to first observe universal differences across indoor spaces and further explore the factors driving that difference. In this case, deep learning models are suitable for encoding such comparison frameworks as they are capable of learning highly complex relations between visual data points across billions of dimensions42.

While deep convolutional neural networks are most often used for specific end-to-end tasks such as classification or object detection, they are also capable of acting as dimensionality reduction mechanisms and are capable of representing a complex RGB input as a datapoint in an n-dimensional latent space that the network learns over training43. While we cannot extract the meaning of the latent space dimensions, such a reduction technique allows us to track how visually similar or dissimilar particular two images are, given how far they are positioned from each other in the latent space representation44.

In order to compute visual similarity in this method, we first learn the latent features relevant exclusively to our dataset. For this, we trained our own ResNet-50 network45 to distinguish between 40 image classes, each representing a separate city in the dataset. We trained separate models on small and large cities (40 cities each) in order to generate separate latent-space representations for both city types. Instead of using the output classification heads of the neural network, we instead extract its penultimate layer (see Figure 7) to obtain the latent-space embedding associated with a specific input image46, which represents the position of that visual data point in a latent space. These latent space vectors contain 2048 dimensions as designed by the architecture of ResNet-50. The visual difference between two samples was calculated as the Euclidean distance between the samples in this high-dimensional latent space. We further reduced the embedding from 2048 to 2 dimensions using t-SNE, a common dimensionality reduction algorithm47. We also conducted experiments with no t-SNE reduction and did not find a significant difference in our results. For convenience, our visualizations are based on reduction to 2 dimensions.

We conducted training and dimensionality reduction separately for each of the room types (Figure 8). The coloring of the cities by distance shows that while the network was not aware of the regional clustering of input classes, similar cities tend to cluster closer together.Fig. 8Dimensionality reduction output from a pre-trained network, dots colored by city.

Dimensionality reduction output from a pre-trained network, dots colored by city.

Object difference

While a dimensionality reduction based on training neural network classifiers from image input allows us to record the degree of difference between individual cities, such ‘black-box’ models do not explain what the contributing factors to those differences are. We used semantic segmentation (ADE20K)48,49, to identify which object categories appear in each image. This was not used as a measure of style itself but to help interpret which types of objects contribute to visual variation across cities. Our primary measure of visual similarity is based on latent space embeddings from deep neural networks, which capture visual features such as texture, color, and composition. We are able to parse each image into semantic regions corresponding to each of the 150 classes in the ADE20K object dataset. If there are some pixels detected belonging to a specific object class, we add their count per given image. We subsequently sum the occurrences of all objects across images per room type and aggregate the sums in a data frame for each city. We subsequently normalize the values per row to account for potential differences in data points between cities. As a result, we obtain a table that displays the strength of occurrence of each specific object in a given city, which allows us to obtain 150-dimensional embeddings of object occurrences for each city (See Figure 9 (a)). GradCAM

GradCAM33 is an insightful approach to investigate how a neural network makes its classification decision. Equipped with this attention visualization tool, we can trace what specific regions in the picture the network focuses on. Using a segmentation network pre-trained on the ADE20K dataset50, we are able to separate image regions belonging to different types of furniture. By overlaying attention maps over segmentation masks, we were able to calculate the relative degree of attention per each of the 150 object classes segmented from images. By dividing each attention sum by the respective area of the detected object and further normalizing the results, we are able to compare by how much the total attention towards a specific object or a category outweighs its spatial weight per image. This allows us to arrange object categories by their relative importance in a weight distribution (See Figure 9(b)). By running such analyses across all images belonging to a specific city, we are able to see what specific objects categories are the most characteristic for a particular city in the dataset. Figure 5 displays an array of such comparisons stacked and arranged by objects of average most importance. The gradient from red to yellow visualizes the ‘decay’ of relative importance of specific object categories to the network. Upon general observation, the gradient trends are similar across the cities, with a few common objects achieving high importance. While the same objects generally earn most of the attention, the specific ranking of the objects is also disrupted due to local differences. For example, curtains in Turkey or kitchen furniture in Boston ranks significantly higher than its relative average rank, signifying that this object category is of specific importance for the network as it assigns a city class to a given image. This allows us to quantitatively compare cities to each other by examining the similarity of their object decay curves. While such decay curves can be compared to each other in their unique representation, they may also reflect unique differences in characteristic objects between cities.

External factors

For a systematic inquiry into the influences driving visual similarity and difference amongst cities, two external factors are analyzed – geographic proximity, and the difference in the degree of globalization. These are analyzed for every possible city pair, considering the set of large and small cities separately. For both sets of n=40 cities, the analysis is carried out on n(n-1)/2 = 780 pairs.

For an inquiry into the effect of geographic proximity, the correlation between geographic great-circle distance31 and Euclidean feature-space distance32 is examined, based on every pair of city centroids. This provides an indication of the degree to which location influences visual similarity within living spaces.

The difference in the degree of globalization is studied using the KOF Index of Globalization29. However, instead of looking at the degree of globalization itself, we consider the difference in the degree of globalization (represented as the difference in the KOF values) between pairs of cities. That allows us to effectively study the connection of difference in globalization against visual differences. Feature-space distance, introduced earlier in our study and described further in this section, measures the visual dissimilarity between spaces by quantifying the distance between their representations in a multi-dimensional space, where each dimension corresponds to a visual characteristic of the interiors. This metric allows us to objectively compare the visual profiles of living spaces across different cities. The KOF index of globalization, however, is a single-dimension variable, describing whether the globalization of a country is high or low in one specific dimension. In order to explore the impact of globalization on these visual differences, we thus calculated the difference in KOF index between cities of a particular country.

Finally, because the KOF Index is assigned by country, not by city, we assign each city with a KOF Index of a country it is located in. We also conducted supplementary exploration of socioeconomic connectivity represented by air traffic, but we leave the details of this to the Appendix A.2.

Representativeness of Airbnb data

To investigate the representativeness of Airbnb data, we conducted a robustness check using additional rental platforms: Craigslist and Zillow (USA) and Zigbang and Ohou (South Korea). For four cities — Seoul, Chicago, New York, and Los Angeles — we collected 1,670 images from these platforms and compared it to Airbnb data. Using latent space representations derived from our trained neural network, we calculated the average distances of both Airbnb and non-Airbnb samples to their respective city centroids. We found that the differences in these average distances were well within the standard deviation of the Airbnb samples, indicating that the visual qualities of Airbnb data closely align with those of other rental platforms. These results suggest that Airbnb listings are representative of broader rental markets and not exclusively shaped by the global tourism market. Detailed results of this analysis are provided in Appendix S6.
