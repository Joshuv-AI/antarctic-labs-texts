Co-treatments of vitamin D and mesenchymal stem cells effectively alleviate the diabetic kidney disease through attenuating the SIRT1-mediated pathways
Authors: Xinfeng Zhao, Sirui He, Xin Luo, Xiaru Zhang, Xueyi Jiang, Yueqing Liang, Tiantian Tang, Kemin Qi, Yang Wang, Yi Zhang, Ping Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic kidney disease (DKD) is still as a common chronic micro-vascular complication of type 1 diabetic (T1DM). Although many studies had verified the therapeutic potential of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) on the clinical treatment of the related complications associated with T1DM, especially the DKD, by preserving the balances of immune micro-environment and so on. However, the immunomodulatory capabilities of MSCs are still controversial on the the efficacy of DKD treatment primarily due to the variations on the immune micro-environment, which could be modulated by a variety of elements, such as the vitamin D (VD). Otherwise, its underlying molecular mechanisms have not yet been elucidated. Herein, the purpose of our study was to explore the synergistic effects of co-treatments with VD and MSCs in vivo and in vitro on the progression of DKD by attenuating the SIRT1-mediated pathways.MethodsFour-week-old male mice were fed with different VD diets for four weeks and established the T1DM models by administering the STZ (40 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection, which were then treated with BMSCs (bone MSCs) injection through the tail vein. After the euthanasia, the renal functions, pathological productors and VD metabolites were evaluated by the related biochemical indicators, flow cytometry analysis, staining techniques and immunofluorescence in the serum and/or renal tissues. Meanwhile, the mechanisms of SIRT1-mediated pathways underlying the amelioration of co-treatment with VD and MSCs on the renal injuries were examined by qRT-PCR, Western blotting and immunohistochemistry assays, which were then verified by the MPC5 cells in vitro with SIRT1-specific knockdown and over-expression under the treatment of high glucose (HG).ResultsTotally, co-treatments of VD and MSCs could more effectively ameliorate the disorders of glucose metabolism and renal injuries (KIM1, Podocin, ZEB1 and/or ZEB2) by boosting the concentrations of VD metabolites both in the STZ-induced T1DM mice models and MPC5 cells with HG treatment in vitro (P < 0.05). Moreover, the mechanism results indicated that co-treatments of VD and MSCs could improve the DKD such as the renal inflammation and fibrosis by attenuating the SIRT1 to inhibit the activation of TGFβ1/Smad pathways both in vivo and in vitro (P < 0.05). Furthermore, over-expression of SIRT1 could partly abolish the inflammation and fibrosis in vitro by attenuating the SIRT1-mediated TGFβ1/Smad3 pathways, which might be aggravated under the down-regulated expressions of SIRT1 in vitro (P < 0.05).ConclusionsOur findings had detailed the mechanisms underlying the amelioration of VD and MSCs related SIRT1-mediated pathways agonist on the DKD-like pathology both in vivo and in vitro, which could highlight the important potential of SIRT1 as a promising therapeutic target for the progression of DKD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04579-w.Keywords: Type 1 diabetes mellitus, Diabetic kidney disease, Vitamin d, SIRT1, TGFβ1/Smad pathways

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), as a major public health challenge of childhood, is caused by the destruction of pancreatic β cells and/or complete insulin deficiency with various types of related complications [1], in which epidemiological studies have revealed that about 30%-40% T1DM patients could develop with the diabetic kidney disease (DKD) within 20–30 years of disease diagnosis, with approximately 50% of them may progress to the end-stage renal diseases (ESRD) by posing a serious threat to the T1DM patient’s quality of life [2, 3]. Meanwhile, early-stage DKD is mainly characterized by the increased aggravation of tubulointerstitial lesions, accompanying with the accumulation of inflammation and fibrosis [4]. Given the global health burden of early-stage DKD, it is extremely crucial to prioritize the prevention and early intervention in the primary care setting to hold great clinical significance. Recently, preclinical researches have demonstrated the involvement of DKD pathogenesis is initiated by the diabetes-related disturbances on the glucose metabolisms [5, 6]. Consequently, its treatment is involved in managing the hyperglycemia as the insulin, SGLT-2 and/or DPP-4 inhibitors and etc [4, 5]. Despite the current clinical therapies, there are still large residual risks of renal injuries in the onset and progression of DKD. Therefore, a widespread innovation is urgently expected to improve their health outcomes for the DKD patients.

Recently, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), as a heterogeneous population of multipotent stromal cells, exhibit a certain potential on involving in the immunomodulatory and immuno-suppressive mechanisms. Thereby, many studies also proved that both multiple MSCs transplantations and MSCs-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs), emerging as a promising superior therapeutic option, could promote renal function recovery more effectively by enhancing the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR)-mediated NF-κB/NLRP3/IL-1β signaling pathways, preserving the balances of immune microenvironment and so on, which also have been verified by the animal studies and in vitro [7–9]. Otherwise, there are relatively few clinical case-studies on the MSCs treatments of renal injuries on the development of DKD with the consistent beneficial effects [10]. In general, the underlying roots were that the immunomodulatory capabilities of MSCs were not constitutive but rather licensed by the related inflammatory cytokines, the activation of MSCs might vary depending on the micro-environment and different types of inflammation within the residing tissues [5]. Therefore, the states of micro-environment could influence the functionality of MSCs on the progression of DKD, which could be modulated by a variety of elements, such as the vitamin D (VD) [12]. Meanwhile, an increasing number of studies have indicated that comparing to the healthy individuals, the contents of VD metabolites, especially the 25(OH)D, are significantly lower among the T1DM patients, even monitoring the levels of VD metabolites could serve as a potential indicator for assessing the risks of DKD onset [11, 12]. Otherwise, it has also been found that an appropriate amount of VD supplementation can partially improve the pancreatic function and reduce the renal damages of DKD in the T1DM mice models [13, 14]. Subsequently, current clinical and basic researches indicate that VD intervention has multiple effects on the renal injuries in the DKD patients, as evidenced by the reduced proteinuria, improved renal function indicators, and delayed renal pathological changes [15]. However, further researches are still needed to determine the optimal dosage, timing, and target populations for specifically clinical applications of DKD. Although the specific mechanism remains unclear, the relevant mechanisms by which VD could improve the renal injuries on the progression of DKD are primarily manifested in the immune regulation and so on [16–20]. It provides a new hypothesis suggesting that co-treatments with VD and MSCs could more effectively alleviate the renalinjuries by modulating the MSCs related inflammation micro-environment, thereby to influence the progression of DKD.

Despite the pathophysiological processes that occur on the progression of DKD are still unclear, silent information regulator 1 (SIRT1)-mediated pathways have been shown to attenuate renal injury by modulating the oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, fibrosis, energy metabolism and etc. These pathways could involve in the inhibition of many downstream related genes on the TGFβ1-Smad, NF-κB, STAT3, p53 pathways and so on to ultimately exacerbate the relatred inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction to worsen the renal injury [21, 22]. Indeed, numbers of researches have shown that SIRT1, as a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) dependent enzyme, is responsible on deacetylating the related proteins to play its role on the therapeutic targets for renal/liver fibrosis by inhibiting the related pathways [21–23]. Furthermore, elevated SIRT1 expression could inhibit the expression of α-SMA, COL4A1, and fibronectin, which could be mediated by the TGF-β1/Smad3 pathway [22, 23]. Overall, there is an significant interaction between SIRT1 and Smad2/3 pathways, which are jointly involved in the progression of DKD. Moreover, MSCs transplantations could exhibit a significant capacity to diminish renal injury among the rats by regulating the SIRT1-related pathways, thereby maintaining inflammatory responses and differentiation potential [23]. Emerging observational data also suggest that 1,25(OH)2D3, through VDR-mediated gene transcription, plays a pivotal role on preventing the onset of DKD by up-regulating the SIRT1 expressions both in vivo and in vitro [24, 25]. However, it is unclear whether over-expression of SIRT1 could protect against the renal injury of DKD by the co-treatments with VD and MSCs.

Therefore, this present study was to evaluate the synergistic effects on the renal injuries of DKD by the co-treatment of VD and MSCs in vivo and in vitro, then we specifically examined the intriguing possibility that they could improve the DKD by blocking the SIRT1-mediated TGF-β1/Smad pathways. It could provide the important insights for the innovatively clinical treatments of DKD.

Materials and methods

Animal models

Forty four-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China) and subsequently divided into four groups (Control, T1DM, MSCs-T1DM and VD-MSCs-T1DM groups, n = 10/group). Then, they were housed at the Animal Center from the Military Medical Research Institute under the standard conditions with a 12 h:12 h light-dark cycle at a room temperature of 23–25℃. Meanwhile, the mice in the Control, T1DM, and MSCs-T1DM groups were fed with a normal VD reproductive diet (1000 IU/kg diet) and the mice in the VD-MSCs-T1DM group were treated with a VD supplemented reproductive diet (5000 IU/kg diet) for four consecutive weeks, in which the detailed ingredients of these diets were listed in the Supplementary Table 1. Moreover, the above mice in the T1DM, MSCs-T1DM and VD-MSCs-T1DM groups were intraperitoneally injected with the freshly prepared streptozotocin (STZ, no.V900890-1 g, Sigma, USA) in a 0.1 mol/L citric acid buffer solution at a dose of 40 mg/kg body weight for seven consecutive days to establish a T1DM mice model, then the successful induction of these T1DM mice models was subsequently verified by measuring the contents of fasting blood glucose (FBG) at three different time points through the tail vein detection on the 7 to 9 days post-STZ injection, in which the FBG should be more than 11.1mM. Whereas the mice in the control group mice were received an equivalent volume of 0.1 mol/L citric acid buffer solution as the controls. Following the successful induction of T1DM mice models, the mice in the MSCs-T1DM and VD-MSCs-T1DM groups were underwent under the treatment regimen consisting of five intravenous tail injections of BMSCs (5 × 105 cells in the 100µL PBS solution) over a period of 15 days, while each injection was administered at three-day intervals. Meanwhile, the mice in the Control and T1DM groups were simultaneously received 100µL PBS solution as the controls. After excluding the mice that were unsuccessful in the T1DM model and died following the tail vein injection of STZ and BMSCs, the mice from each group (n = 8/group) were finally chosen to participate in the subsequent researches. Totally, the detailed animal model was clearly illustrated in the Fig. 1A. Besides, the BMSCs utilized in this study were obtained, cultured, identified and induced differentiation towards to the adipogenesis and osteogenesis as described previously [26], in which the full-length blots/gels were also presented in the Supplementary Fig. 1.

At the whole procedure, the body weights and FBG concentrations from the tail vein were monitored weekly. After 14 days of the last BMSCs injection, the mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal dose of the ketamine/xylazine mixture including the 90 mg/kg of ketamine (no. K2753, Sigma) and 4.5 mg/kg of xylazine (no. 46995, Sigma) to extract the eyeball blood, which were then euthanized by the carbon dioxide inhalation (CO2) at the end of study design point using a CO2 line-connected box at a gas infusion rate of 2.0 L/min. Once all the mice were cease their respiration for about 10 min, the euthanasia was then confirmed by the cervical dislocation. Immediately, the renal and pancreas tissues were collected, in which some were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde for histological examination, while the others were frozen in the liquid N2 and finally stored at -80℃ refrigerator for the further determination. Meanwhile, their serum samples were obtained at 3000 r/min for 15 min after stewing 30 min at the room temperature and stored at -80℃ refrigerator until the usage.

The whole animal procedures mentioned above were conformed and approved by the Guide for the Care and Usage Committee of Laboratory Animals by the Beijing Academy of Military Medical Sciences. Moreover, they were approved by the Ethics of Animal Experiments in the Academy of Military Medical Sciences in China (no. IACUC-DWZX-2019-704). Meanwhile, the whole procedures had been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Determination of IPGTT and ITT in the STZ-induced T1DM mice models

Two weeks before the mice execution, the intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT) were utilized to assess the glucose metabolism status. Exactly, the mice in the Control, T1DM, MSCs-T1DM and VD-MSCs-T1DM groups were fasted for 16 h prior to the experiments and recognized as the concentrations of FBG at the 0 min. After 30 min adaptation, 20% glucose (2 g/kg, 10µL/g) (no. V900116, Sigma) was intraperitoneally injected into the mice. Subsequently, the contents of FBG were respectively measured and recorded at the 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min post-glucose administration in the IPGTT. Moreover, the concentrations of glucose were assessed before insulin injection as the 0 min after a 2-hour fast as a part of intraperitoneal ITT. After administering the insulin (2IU/kg) (no. Novolin®R Penfill®, novo nordisk) via intraperitoneal injection, the contents of FBG were respectively monitored at the 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min.

Flow cytometry analysis of spleen inflammatory cytokines

The spleen samples (n = 8/group) were immediately obtained when the mice were euthanized to process the flow cytometry analysis of inflammatory cytokines, in which the spleen samples were excised and minced using the scissors to pipit in the PBS, pass through a 200-mesh strainer and centrifuge at 3000 rpm for 15 min to isolate the splenetic lymphocytes. Then, they were stained with the anti-mouse CD3-PE (no. 12-0031-82, eBioscience, USA) and CD8-FITC (no. 11-0041-85, eBioscience, USA) for 30 min at the room temperature in the dark, which were fixed and permeabilized using the diluent (no. TNB-1020-l050, Bio-Sciences, USA) and stained with the anti-mouse IFNγ-APC (no. 20-7311-U100, Bio-Sciences, USA) and IL17-APC (no. 17-7177-82, eBioscience, USA) for darkly 30 min at the 4℃. To establish the negative control, the unstained and stained splenetic lymphocytes were prepared with the fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) control stain sets and measured by a FACS CantoII (FACScan®, BD Biosciences, USA). Then, the related data was analyzed using the FlowJo® 10 software (Tree Star Inc., USA).

Assessment of biochemical indicators both in the serum and renal tissues

The homogenate supernatant of renal tissues (approximately 100 mg) were homogenized and obtained by 600uL 0.9% sterile saline, then the biochemical indicators in the serum and renal supernatant, including the total cholesterol (TC, no. A111-1-1), triglyceride (TG, no. A110-1-1), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, no. A113-1-1), insulin (no. H203-1-2), 8-hydroxy-2-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG, no. H165-1-2), kidney injury molecule 1 (KIM1, no. H436-1-2), VD3 (no. H191-2-2) and 25(OH)D (no. H191-1-2) were determined by the commercial assay kits from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. Meanwhile, the contents of renal protein were assessed using the BeyoBCA protein concentration rapid assay kit (no.P0398L, Beyotime, China) to calculate the contents of above biochemical indicators in the renal tissues.

Histological analyses of pancreatic and renal tissues

The fixed pancreatic and renal tissues in the Control, T1DM, MSCs-T1DM and VD-MSCs-T1DM groups were dehydrated by an immersion in the 100% ethanol for 24 h and processed for the paraffin embedding. Then, 5 μm paraffin sections both in the pancreatic and renal tissues were stained with the haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to measure the inflammatory status under a light microscope at 100× magnification. To determine the renal fibrosis, the fixed renal tissues were stained with Periodic acid-silver methenamine (PASM), Masson trichrome and Sirius red staining under a light microscope at 100× magnification according to standard procedures [20]. All the above histological experiments were performed by the Servicebio technology Co., Ltd in China.

Immunohistochemical detection of related indicators in the pancreatic and renal tissues

The 5 μm paraffin sections of pancreatic and renal tissues were dew-axed using the xylene and rehydrated reagents through being graded with a series of ethanol solutions. They were then incubated in the citrate buffer (PH = 6.0) at 95℃ for the antigen retrieval and cooled for 1 h at the room temperature. After treating the sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min in a dark room to block the endogenous peroxidase activity, they were blocked for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4℃ with the primary antibodies of insulin (no. GB11022-100, 1:1000, Servicebio) in the pancreatic tissues, and TGFβ1 (no. GB11179-100, 1:800, Servicebio), a-SMA (no. GB13044-50, 1:500, Servicebio), ZEB1 (no. GB11513-50, 1:1000, Servicebio) and ZEB2 (no. GB11509-50, 1:1000, Servicebio) in the renal tissues. Subsequently, the secondary antibody of HRP goat anti-Rabbit lgG (no. GB23303, 1:200, Servicebio) was incubated with the above sections for 1 h at the room temperature. To finally visualize the expression levels of target proteins, they were counter-stained with the DAB and hematoxylin by a quantitative analysis with the Image-Pro Plus software (Version 6.0.0.260, Media Cybernetics, USA). All above immunohistochemical detection were then determined and analyzed by the Servicebio technology Co., Ltd in China.

Cell culture and different treatments in vitro of MPC5 cells

The immortalized mouse podocyte cell line (MPC5) was procured from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academic of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in the low-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) with 5.5 mM glucose (Gibco, San Diego, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, San Diego, USA). For the further investigation, the MPC5 cells as the T1DM model were established by stimulating with the totally 30 mM high glucose (HG) (no. V900116, Sigma) (24.5 mM HG and 5.5mM in the low-glucose DMEM) in the presence of 10% FBS with (VD + HG group) and without 100nM 25(OH)D3 treatment (HG group) for 48 h to simulate the hyperglycemic environment in vitro. Meanwhile, the MPC5 with 24.5 mM mannitol (no. IM0040, Solarbio) in the low-glucose DMEM was as the Control group without the treatment of HG and 25(OH)D3 [27]. Besides, the above interventaions of HG and 25(OH)D3 were obtained by utilizing the analyses of different dose-response relationships to examine the outcomes from the proliferation, differentiation and mRNA expressions of associated genes in the MPC5 cells, which was shown in the Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3. Moreover, the trans-well chambers (3 μm pore size, Costar polycarbonate filters) were utilized for the co-cultures of MPC5 (2 × 105 cells/well in the lower chamber) and BMSCs (2 × 104 cells/well in the upper chamber), and stimulated with 30 mM HG 100 nM 25(OH)D3 in the VD + MSCs + HG group and without 100 nM 25(OH)D3 treatment as the MSCs + HG group for 48 h. Then, the morphology of MPC5 cells in the all groups were captured by an inverted microscope (IX83, Olympus, Japan).

CCK8 detection of cell proliferation and differentiation of MPC5 cells

The MPC5 cells under the different treatments of 25(OH)D3, HG and MSCs were inoculated in a 96-well plate (Corning, USA) with five replicate wells in the control, HG, VD + HG, MSCs + HG and VD + MSCs + HG groups. After 24 h treatment, the complete 90 µl DMEM culture medium was replaced in each well, added 10 µl CCK8 detection solution (no. C0043, Beyotine, China) and incubated in a 37℃ incubator for 1.5 h. Then, the absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm wavelength using the microplate reader and repeated every 12 h until 48h detection period.

Over-expression of SIRT1 in the MPC5 cells by lentivirus transfection under different treatments in vitro

After the cultivation for 12 h, the MPC5 cells were cultured in the low-glucose DMEM with 10% FBS. Then, the vector and lentivirus SIRT1-shRNA (The sequences were shown in the Supplementary Table 2) (multiplicity of infection, MOI = 10, GenePharma, Shanghai, China) was transferred into the MPC5 cells, mixed with Lipofecta mine TM 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and incubated for 20 min at the room temperature in the dark. Meanwhile, the polybrene (10 µg/mL, Solarbio, Shanghai, China) was mixed with the above growth medium to increase the transfection efficiency. After 48 h cultivation, 2 µg/mL puromycin (Solarbio, Shanghai, China) was added to the above medium, in which the stably transfected cells were screened for two weeks to consider as the over-expressing-SIRT1 MPC5 cells. Moreover, the negative scrambled shRNA was used as the NC group. The transfection efficiency of over-expressing SIRT1 was subsequently evaluated using the fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan), flow cytometry, qRT-PCR and Western Blotting.

In these experiments, the MPC5 cells were only treated with 30mM glucose as the positive controls (HG group), while the NC-shRNA SIRT1 and shSIRT1 over-expressed MPC5 cells under the treatment of 30mM glucose were respectively designed as the HG + NC and HG + SIRT1high groups. Moreover, over-expressed SIRT1 MPC5 cells under the treatments of 30mM glucose and 100nM 25(OH)D3 were as the HG + VD + SIRT1highgroup. Meanwhile, they were treated without (HG + MSCs + SIRT1highgroup) and with 100nM 25(OH)D3 (HG + VD + MSCs + SIRT1highgroup) by co-culturing with the MSCs.

SIRT1 knockdown in the MPC5 cells by the SiRNA transfections under different treatments in vitro

To achieve the SIRT1 down-regulation MPC5 cells, the SIRT1-siRNA with green fluorescent protein (GFP) was transferred using the Lipofecta mine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA), while the NC-siRNA was the no-load plasmid with only the GFP as the controls. All the sequences were shown in the Supplementary Table 2. Both the NC-siRNA and SIRT1-siRNA were constructed and harvested by the Gen-epharma (GenePharma, Shanghai, China). Exactly, 2 × 105 MPC5 cells/well were seeded in a 6-well plate with three duplicates for 24 h and incubated with 2mL Opti-MEM medium containing with 8.5µL SIRT1-siRNA and 6µL lipofecta mine transfection reagents for 6 h. After the 24 h cultivation, the transfection efficiency was evaluated by the fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan), flow cytometry, quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR) and Western Blotting respectively.

In this experiment, the MPC5 cells were as the Control group, whereas the NC-siRNA and siSIRT1 knockdown MPC5 cells were respectively as siSIRT1-NC and siSIRT1 groups. Moreover, the siSIRT1 knockdown MPC5 cells were treated with 100nM 25(OH)D3 as the siSIRT1 + VD group. Meanwhile, they were treated without (siSIRT1 + MSCs group) and with 100nM 25(OH)D3 (siSIRT1 + VD + MSCs group) by co-culturing with the MSCs.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the renal tissues and MPC5 related cells using the Trizol reagent (TaKaRa, Japan). Their cDNA samples were then reversely transcribed by the Transcript® One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (no. AT341-02, TransGen Biotech, China), and performed by the qRT-PCR method using the Top Green qPCR SuperMix (AQ131-01, TransGen Biotech, China) under the CFX96 TouchTM Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) to determine the mRNA expressions of related genes on the adipogenic differentiation (PPARγ and C/EBPα), osteogenic differentiation (Runx2 and ALP), VD metabolism (VDR, CYP27B1 and CYP24A1), renal injuries (KIM1, Podocin, ZEB1 and ZEB2), inflammation (TNFα, IL1β, ccl2, Mcp1, iNOS, Foxp3 and IL17A), fibrosis (COL1A1, COL4A1, COL5A1, Fibronectin1 and α-SMA) and SIRT1-mediated TGFβ1-Smad pathway (SIRT1, TGFβ1, TGFβR1, TGFβR2, Smad2 and Smad3), with the GAPDH as an internal reference gene. Afterward, their expressions were performed in the triplicates and calculated to the internal reference gene using the 2−▲▲CT and/or 2−▲CT method, in which the related primers were presented in the Supplementary Table 3.

Western blotting

The protein samples were extracted from the renal tissues (about 100 mg) and MPC5 related cells using the 200µL RIPA lysate (no. P0013B, Beyotime, China), which were then re-suspended and lysed at the 4℃ for 45 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4℃ for 30 min. Then, the supernatant samples were obtain to determine their concentrations by the BeyoBCA protein concentration rapid assay kit (no. P0398L, Beyotime, China). Subsequently, the above proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to the polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Bedford, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk in the 10% TBST for 2 h at the room temperature, the PVDF membranes were incubated overnight at 4℃ with the specific primary antibodies, namely PPAR-γ (no. 2435, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), C/EBPα (no. 2295, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), ALP (no. sc-365765, Santa Cruz, 1:100), Runx2 (no. 8486, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), VDR (no. 12550, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), CYP27B1 (no. sc-515903, Santa Cruz, 1:100), CYP24A1 (no. K002997P, Solarbio, 1:500), IL1β (no. GB11113, Servicebio, 1:1000), iNOS (no. GB115703, Servicebio, 1:1000), COL1A1 (no. 72026, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), α-SMA (no. GB111364, Servicebio, 1:1000), TGFβ (no. 3711, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), Fibronectin1 (no. GB114491, Servicebio, 1:1000), Smad2/3 (no. 3102, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), P-Smad2 (no. 18338, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), P-Smad3 (no. 9520, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), SIRT1 (no. 9475, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), α-Tubulin (no. 11224-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:2000), and GAPDH (no. 5174, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:2000). After washing by the 10% TBST triplicates for each 15 min, the membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-mouse (no. 7076, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:3000) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (no. ab6721, Abcam, 1:10000) secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at the room temperature, and washed off with the 10%TBST in triplicates. Then, the target proteins bands were detected using the ECL detection system (Millipore, MA, USA) and analyzed the grayscale values by utilizing the Image J software.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS 21.0. The animal experiments were performed with 8 mice/group, while the cell studies were repeated thrice independently with minimum of three replicates. Then, all values were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD), in which the Percent-Percent plot was chosen to determine the normality of data. Then, the differences among these two groups were determined through the independent-samples t test, while they were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for the continuous variables with normal distribution among the three and more groups, which were then followed by the Student-Newman-Keuls test between each two groups. These differences were considered statistically significant as P < 0.05.

Results

Basic characterization of BMSCs and STZ-induced T1DM mice models under the different treatments

As shown in the Fig. 1A, the STZ-induced T1DM mice models were constructed by the intraperitoneal STZ injection at a dose of 40 mg/kg for seven consecutive days among the eight-week-old C57BL/6J mice. After the T1DM mice model was established and verified, 5 × 105 BMSCs in the 100µL PBS solution were injected via the tail vein over a period of 15 days, in which each injection was administered at three-day intervals. Then, their serum, kidney, spleen, and pancreas tissues were separated and collected for further detection. Simultaneously, the BMSCs utilized for the aforementioned treatments of T1DM were isolated, acquired and evaluated as described previously [26]. As shown in the Fig. 1B, the morphology of BMSCs were exhibited a long-spindle shape under a microscope, which were accompanied by high expressions of CD90, CD29 and Sca-1, and low expressions of CD45, CD34, CD31, MHC-II, and CD11b by the flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 1C). Moreover, there were adipogenesis and osteogenesis potential of BMSCs, as evidenced by the Oil Red O and ALP staining, qRT-PCR and Western Blotting analysis (Fig. 1D to Fig. 1F). All these results proved that the BMSCs used in this study were complied with their international standards [26].

Co-treatments of VD and MSCs could ameliorate the disorders of glucose metabolism in the STZ-induced T1DM mice models

It recorded that there were no significant differences on the body weights (Fig. 2A and B) and FBG (Fig. 2D) before and at the STZ injection among the mice in the Control, T1DM, MSCs-T1DM and VD-MSCs-T1DM groups, accompanied by the contents of FBG at three different time points to verify the success of STZ-induced T1DM mice models (Fig. 2E). As shown in the Fig. 2C, the body weights were lower in the T1DM and MSCs-T1DM groups than those both in the Control and VD-MSCs-T1DM groups (P < 0.05), while no significant differences were observed between the VD-MSCs-T1DM and Control groups (P > 0.05). Moreover, comparing with the T1DM group, the body weight was higher in the MSCs-T1DM groups at the time of mice euthanasia (P < 0.05). Meanwhile, the monitoring results of FBG after the MSCs treatments showed that the contents of FBG were higher among the T1DM, MSCs-T1DM and VD-MSCs-T1DM groups than those in the Control group (P < 0.05). Moreover, comparing with the T1DM group, both the MSCs-T1DM and VD-MSCs-T1DM groups had lower contents of FBG from more than four days after the BMSCs injection (P < 0.05). Discussion

The progression of T1DM exerts the distinct impacts on the renal injury, manifesting as DKD performance under the long-term hyperglycemia, which could in turn cause the intra-cellular osmotic imbalances, cellular damages, inflammation and fibrosis of renal cells as the ZEB1, ZEB2, KIM1 and Podocin due to persistent hyperglycemia [28]. Recently, numerous studies have explored the roles of fibrosis and inflammatory markers on the progression and its complications of T1DM, especially the DKD, leading to severe renal injury in the T1DM experimental animals by activating the related signaling pathways [27–30], among which the SIRT1, as a sensor of cellular energy and metabolism, is widely expressed in the tubular cells and podocytes, and involved in the progression of DKD by suppressing the inflammatory and/or fibrosis responses through the deacetylation of histone, transcription factors, and other modified transcription factors [31, 32]. So far, regardless of the etiology, the roles of SIRT1 on counteracting the renal injury had been demonstrated in the SIRT1-deficient mice and in vitro by modulating the TGFβ1 signaling pathways [33]. Several studies also had demonstrated there was an inverse relationship between SIRT1 and TGFβ/Smad signaling pathways on the occurrence of renal injuries, in turn both resveratrol or its agonist SRT3025 could also mitigate the renal inflammation and reduce the accumulations of COL4A1 and fibronectin by deacetylating the activation of Smad3 [33–35]. Meanwhile, the study on the Rosa roxburghii showed that it could delay the renal injuries by regulating the SIRT1 mediated TGFβ/Smads signaling pathways [36]. Here, we also verified the consistent conclusions both in the STZ-induced T1DM mice models and in vitro, in which SIRT1 inhibition could activate the TGFβ1/Smad pathways on the progressions of DKD as the higher renal injury indicators including the ZEB1 and KIM1, which could be reformed by the up-expressions of SIRT1 and aggravated through the SIRT knockdown.

On the progression of DKD, the dysregulation of inflammatory cytokines could further exacerbate the damages of pancreatic β cells, ultimately leading to the insufficient insulin secretion [37, 38]. This process is driven by the autoimmune pathology, rather than a deficiency and/or abnormalities of MSCs [37–39]. Due to the MSCs exceptional healing abilities, they are currently used in the clinical therapies for a number of ailments as a unique possible therapeutic intervention [38, 40]. Even with the clinical treatments of MSCs transplantations and MSCs-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs), it is not possible to completely halt the progression of DKD and promote the renal function recovery more effectively, as the persistent presence of autoimmune regulation continuously harms the renal cells [7– [9, 41]. Moreover, Ji et al. also proved that there were the unique cellular and molecular mechanisms of MSC-sEV on treating the DKD fibrosis at a single-cell levels [40]. Furthermore, preclinical researches of clinical MSCs therapy had yielded the significant potential and safety on the treatment of DKD by many underlying mechanisms, in which the MSCs could suppress the expressions of TGF-β/Smad pathways, inhibite the NLPR3 translation, affect the releases of inflammatory cytokines, activate the mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy, improve the quality of mitochondria and so on [40, 42]. Although the MSCs have the certain efficacy on regulating the immune functions and promoting the tissue repairs in the clinical treatments of DKD [40, 43]. There were still some varying results, indicating that some renal injuries on the progression of DKD had been unrelated to MSCs when considering various factors such as the pathogenesis, progression, survival time, and homing ability of MSCs in vivo and so on [44]. These significantly different results were manly for the complex and multi-directional immune regulatory impacts of MSCs to prevent their effective therapeutic effects on the progressions of DKD [5, 45, 46]. However, its specific mechanism is not yet fully elucidated, meaning that the clinical treatments of DKD may necessitate the exploration of more effective combination therapy strategies incorporating with the MSCs.

At the same time, the combined applications of MSCs and other therapies could exhibit a synergistic efficacy to achieve the optimally therapeutic outcomes, so we still need to optimize the MSC therapy to improve the therapeutic effects on the progression of DKD [41]. As was mentioned above, the effects of VD on the MSCs transplatation were mainly reflected on the changes of their differentiation and proliferation ability through increasing the expressions of early osteoblast markers as the Runx2 and COL1A1, and inhibiting the expressions of adipogenic induction related transcriptal factors as PPARγ, C/EBPα and etc to activate the VDR-related signaling pathways [47–49]. Totally, there are numerous potential mechanisms that could be included as follows. Firstly, VD had anti-inflammatory effects on inhibiting the activity of NF-κB and reducing the productions of inflammatory factors. Moreover, some researches showed that the DKD patients who were supplemented with VD could decrease the contents of inflammatory regulators [50, 51]. Furthermore, Cruciani S et al. proved that VD also could modulate the inflammation and autophagy during the MSCs differentiation [52]. However, there was no evidence on the roles of co-treatments of VD and MSCs to alleviate the renal injury. So our results firstly indicated that the co-treatments of VD and MSCs could effectively ameliorate the disorders of glucose metabolism and renal injuries by boosting the levels of VD metabolites both in the STZ-induced T1DM mice models and MPC5 cells under the treatment with HG in vitro. To date, VD is tightly intertwined with the SIRT1-mediated signaling pathways through regulating the VDR in the numerous physiological and pathological processes on the development of DKD. So the results from recent mechanism researches indicated that co-treatments of VD and MSCs could improved the renal inflammation and fibrosis by attenuating the SIRT1 involving TGFβ1/Smad pathways. Moreover, SIRT1 over-expression could abolished the renal injuries in vitro by attenuating the SIRT1-mediated pathways, which could be aggravated under the down-regulated expressions of SIRT1. Nevertheless, there were still some limitations in our study, in which the key shortcomings were as follows. Firstly, although our results had confirmed the renal inflammation and fibrosis could be attenuated by the SIRT1 mediated TGFβ/Smad pathways through in vitro and in vivo experiments, but there was still no data on the acetylated Smad3 and gene-specific analyses to rule out the influences of other pathways and genes, which still requires further exploration in the future. Secondly, the immunohistochemistry results of VDR and SIRT1 in the renal tissues were remained less ideal and clarity in the podocyte and then further exploration is still necessary in our future endeavors.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, it had detailed the mechanisms underlying the amelioration of VD and MSCs related SIRT1-mediated pathways agonist on the DKD-like pathology both in vivo and in vitro. Therefore, co-treatments of VD and MSCs could be used as promising the DKD therapy by down-regulation of SIRT to suppress the activation of TGFβ1/Smad signaling pathways, which could highlight the important potential of SIRT1 as a promising therapeutic target for the clinical treatment of DKD.
