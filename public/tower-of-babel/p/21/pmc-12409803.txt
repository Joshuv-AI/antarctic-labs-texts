Preserving the tribal identity: A policy imperative for indigenous well-being and conservation of the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve in South India
Authors: Geetha Veliah, Padma Venkatasubramanian, Irene Sambath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis short communication explores how identity erosion among Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs) in the Nilgiris district of Tamil Nadu, India, impacts their wellness and ecological stewardship. It aims to highlight culturally specific disruptions across generations and recommend policy responses that are identity-affirming and context-sensitive.MethodsThis exploratory qualitative study used an epistemological approach to understand tribal perceptions through in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with elders and youth from select members of the PVTGs in the Nilgiris. A semi-structured interview guide covering six wellness domains was developed through deductive and inductive methods. Data were collected over four months, transcribed, translated, and thematically analyzed using manual coding. Institutional ethical clearance was obtained, and informed consent was secured from all participants and community leaders.ResultsThe study found that well-intentioned mainstream developmental interventions, such as non-contextual education and biomedical health models, have disrupted cultural transmission, resulting in identity erosion and loss of sacred landscapes. Many participants expressed concern over mental health issues, substance use, climate vulnerability, and inadequate community representation in governance. Mobile technologies and media consumption have contributed to cultural disorientation among tribal youth.ConclusionPreserving tribal identity is essential to ensuring community wellness and conserving the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve. Policy efforts must shift from generalized welfare to culturally grounded, participatory frameworks that integrate traditional knowledge systems and support Indigenous autonomy.Keywords: Tribal identity, Indigenous wellness, Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve, Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups, Traditional knowledge systems, Policy brief

Background

The Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve (NBR), located in Tamil Nadu, India, is home to six Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs) - the Todas, Kotas, Irulars, Kurumbas, Kattunayakans, and Paniyas. Over generations, these PVTGs have been shaped by the ecological, and political transformations, beginning with colonial encroachment in the 19th century [1,2]. British land policies displaced tribes from ancestral territories, restricted forest access, and converted grasslands into tea plantations. These shifts dismantled once-egalitarian socio-cultural systems, and imposed new hierarchies. Supporting this historical account, W. Francis in his 1908 Madras Gazetteer has documented on colonial attitude toward PVTG utilitarianism, and how colonial land policies displaced Nilgiri tribes, eroded traditions and altered ecological balance [2].

The post-1990s globalization, tourism, glamorization of modernity, material consumption, formal employment, residential schooling facilities, mainstream media, and peer influences have overtly reshaped the tribal lifeways, through processes of cultural homogenization and the introduction of consumerist values [3]. The emerging identity constructs among younger generations are often at odds with the communal, subsistence-based values of their elders. While such aspirations provide avenues for socioeconomic engagement and empowerment, they also forge tensions within families and communities as traditional roles, languages, and ecological stewardship practices are gradually devalued. Addressing these imbalances is a policy imperative, as safeguarding the NBR calls for strengthening the identities of those who have conserved it for centuries.

This short communication explores how mainstream development frameworks, often implemented without cultural contextualization, undermine tribal autonomy, wellness, and identity in the Nilgiris. Drawing on community narratives and thematic insights, the study identifies critical policy gaps and proposes identity-affirming pathways to support Indigenous well-being and ecological stewardship. The layered disruptions of displacement, cultural dilution, conservation issue, and the weakening intergenerational knowledge transfer behooves an urgent crafting of policies to re-center tribal identity, governance, and autonomy.

Methods

This qualitative, exploratory study employed an epistemological approach [4] aligned with interpretivism and indigenous ways of knowing, using conversational/narrative testimony to understand the lived experiences and perceptions of wellness among PVTGs in the Nilgiris district of Tamil Nadu, India. In-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs) were guided by a semi-structured interview tool developed through deductive (literature-based) and inductive (pilot interview) methods covering six wellness domains: physical, mental, social, spiritual, environmental, and economic derived from literature review and pilot interviews across tribes to ensure cultural and thematic relevance. Participants were purposively selected based on willingness and availability, and interviews were conducted in natural settings by the lead researcher, affiliated with the institution, with support from a local key informant to ensure trust, rapport-building, and cultural sensitivity. As saturation was not the goal, interviews were conducted until diverse perspectives were obtained. No incentives were provided.

Interviews and FGDs (60–90 min) were conducted in Tamil, audio-recorded with consent, transcribed using Transkriptor, and translated into English with culturally specific terms checked for meaning by the Tamil-speaking researcher. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of SRM Medical College Hospital and Research Centre IEC with approval Number 2912/IEC/2021, with permissions from local authorities and community leaders. Written informed consent was obtained, confidentiality maintained by assigning pseudonyms and restricting data access to the primary researcher. Inductive thematic analysis involved manual coding of transcripts, organizing responses by tribe and respondent, identifying recurring themes and subthemes, and grouping them into broader categories to generate policy-relevant insights. Coding was done manually by the lead researcher due to the scale of the study. Inter-coder reliability was not applicable but rigor was ensured via participant validation and member checking. The Results section integrates participant narratives, thematic interpretation, and relevant literature to situate community voices within broader academic and policy contexts, enhancing both analytic depth and applicability.

Results and discussion

The research engaged 72 respondents (47 elders, 25 youth) from six PVTGs across the Nilgiris, with gender representation to capture diverse perspectives. Data were collected through IDIs with Todas, Kotas, Irulars, Kurumbas, Kattunayakans, and Paniyas, and three FGDs with Todas, Irulas and the Paniyans; 48 % were above 55 years. Thematic analysis yielded context-specific challenges faced by PVTGs that are not just symptoms of neglect, but systemic outcomes of centuries of marginalization, ecological displacement, and cultural loss. While health and livelihood concerns remain vital, they are facing a wider crisis of identity loss, land alienation, and marginalization in policymaking [4]. These trends in the interconnected challenges resonate with Marmot's social determinants of health [5], emphasising the role of structural inequities on well-being. This positioning underscores that PVTG wellness cannot be dissociated from identity, territory, and governance, an understanding well documented within the global Indigenous scholarship on decolonizing health systems. The following issues reflect the compounding effects of historical and ongoing disruption:

Healthcare delivery mismatch

A community that thrived on holism is subject to the biomedical paradigms promoted by the Government while facing exclusion of tribal healing practices, spiritual aspects of illness and local preferences for community-based care [4]. Additionally, there has been a gradual loss of traditional healers and a clamouring for modern medicine by the younger generations.

An Irula woman 80 years old opined, “100 years ago we did not have hospitals we delivered healthy babies at home, nowadays the younger generation prefer Government hospitals for delivery services.”

Economic factors intensify this generational divide, such as, long travel to hospitals incurring transport costs, wage loss, and treatment expenses. Several participants, especially the elders advocated integrating traditional medicine/herbs into primary practice, establishing mobile health units, training youth as cultural health liaisons, and developing sustainable herbal medicine enterprises. This approach reflects Antonovsky's Salutogenesis concept of drawing health from cultural coherence and meaning [6], Halbert Dunn's higher level of wellness [7] and mirrors models in First Nations communities in Canada [8,9] and the Māori in New Zealand [10].

Mental health crisis and substance abuse

Increasing prevalence of substance abuse [11], and psychological distress [12] as a result of cultural disconnect, identity issues, and unemployment among the tribal youth are often unaddressed due to stigma and lack of culturally competent care. This is particularly so of young boys and men who are school dropouts with poor self-esteem which further compounds their mental illness.

A young Paniya boy 18 years of age lamented, “The government needs to do something about the boys in our community. Many of them drop out of school and start to do wage work and the money they earn is spent on alcohol. In the recent few months (October 2023) we have had few suicides among the young adults”.

This crisis could be averted by community-based wellness programs blending storytelling, land-based activities, and peer mentorship grounded in tribal identity and traditions. Comparable initiatives among First Nations communities in Canada, Native American youth in the U.S. and Aboriginal Australians have demonstrated reduced stigma, fostered resilience, and strengthened community bonds [[13], [14], [15]].

Loss of sacred landscapes

Encroachment by tea plantations, non-native afforestation, unregulated infrastructure projects, and categorization of forest as “reserved forests” have severed the connection between tribe, territory, spirituality, and psychological well-being [16]. Rituals tied to specific landscapes, such as dolmens, seasonal festivals, and seed-sowing ceremonies lose their ecological meaning when they are alienated from their lands/forests.

A Kota priest 65 years of age recounts, “Restricting access to our forests has had deep impact in us pursuing our traditional herbal medicines. Furthermore, we are unable to teach our traditions to the next generation.”

Conservation efforts are undermined, their role as custodians of local biodiversity is diminished, eroding centuries-old systems of sustainable land management. There is a need for co-managed conservation frameworks that restore tribal access and embed Indigenous ecological principles into formal environmental policies, as detailed by the Joint Forest Management initiatives passively adapted in India [17].

Climate vulnerability

Food systems, traditional agricultural practices, ceremonies linked to harvesting season, and foraging activities have been disrupted due to climate and weather change such as noticeable shifts in rainfall patterns, shorter and warmer winters, erratic monsoons, and prolonged hot spells as well as due to lack of access to the forests and use of chemical-based fertilizers to enhance crop-yield compromising both physical survival and cultural rituals that are deeply connected to ecological activities [18].

A Kota leader reported, “Lot of changes in weather. When I was young, we would leave coconut shells filled with water outside during winter and by morning it would become ice. Not anymore.”

The loss of berries, medicinal herbs, and ceremonial grass undermines the performance of ancestral rites. Integrating traditional ecological knowledge into climate adaptation planning, reviving indigenous crop varieties, conserving microclimates within sacred groves, and aligning agricultural support schemes with subsistence requirements as well as ritual calendars, can strengthen community resilience.

Cultural disintegration through education

Mainstream curriculum marginalize tribal language, storytelling, or ecological learning, accelerating intergenerational disconnect, loss of linguistic identity, and pride in ancestral heritage [19]. Intergenerational disconnection is furthered by boarding schools that isolate children away from their mandh/settlement for extended periods, also inter-mingling among other tribes dilutes their traditional practices including diet.

A Kota woman leader 45 years of age stated, “Education is important and is encouraged by the government as well, this leaves us very little time to teach our children some of our traditions which we learned with our elders teaching us and through observing.”

The transfer of place-based ecological wisdom could be sustained by contextualizing school curricula to include tribal history, indigenous ecological science, and mentorship buddy programs pairing students with elders during long school vacations.

Tourism without sanctity

The ‘sanctity’, the deep spiritual, ancestral, and cultural significance they hold of the tribal space (land), that serve as sites for seasonal rituals, rites of passage, and community decision-making, is often overlooked in the promotion of the Nilgiris as a tourist spot [20]. Furthermore, commercialized tourism dilutes these living archives of tribal heritage by commodifying rituals, transforming public spaces into performance venues, building resorts, expanding roads, prioritizing tourist demands over internal cultural needs.

A young Kota man exclaimed, “we used to have tourists during the season (May-July) in those days, but nowadays we have them all the time.”

From an ecological stewardship point of view, unrestricted tourism increases visitor numbers in fragile ecosystems, resulting in soil erosion, littering, and disturbing the endemic vegetation and wildlife, displacing controlled foraging, rotational grazing, and forest fire prevention. A way forward would be tribal-led tourism governance models where visitor numbers, access to sacred spots, and interpretive content are regulated by the community.

Digital and media pressures

The penetration of global media and mobile technologies has introduced new aspirations among tribal youth, often centered on urban lifestyles [21], that conflict with traditional roles and values, deepening cultural disorientation. This cultural conflict occurs in various forms: waning attendance at annual festivals, falling interest in arranged marriages in some tribes, and unwillingness to preserve ancestral practices such as agriculture where economic prospects are perceived as limited.

Culturally grounded digital storytelling, community-based social networking platforms, and training in indigenous crafts or eco-tourism for online markets would channel youth aspirations [22]. Such thoughtful tactics could blend modern skills with heritage preservation, ensuring that technological exposure strengthens rather than compromise identity.

Weak leadership and representation

There is an absence of formalized tribal leadership among each of the tribes and representation in local governance and health planning bodies. Despite strong oral traditions and customary governance, tribal voices remain peripheral in policymaking processes. Given that the six tribes are geographically dispersed across different parts of the Nilgiris, bringing leaders together on a common agenda is logistically challenging.

A young Irula educated woman observed, “Our leaders are not as strong as they should be. They do not command the respect that the previous generations earned. We need a strong leader to help us progress.”

Government-recognized institutionalized tribal councils comprising both elders and young members should function more effectively and deliver realistic outcomes. Comparative experiences from Māori iwi councils in New Zealand and Alaska Native tribal governments illustrate successful integration [23]. Digital platforms in local languages designed for low-bandwidth locations could offer an alternate practical way to facilitate dialogues.

The integrated path forward must be forged across three reinforcing levels discussed below. These categories draw upon globally successful Indigenous strategies and highlight the need for categorized identity preserving solutions that are practical and replicable.

System-level transformation: This includes embedding culturally competent models in healthcare [24], promoting contextual education systems [25], preserving sacred landscapes through enforcement [26], legal and conservation frameworks [27], and regulating tourism to align with tribal consent and cultural values [28]. Globally, Indigenous-led health reforms in countries like Norway (Sámi people) and New Zealand demonstrate how system-level change anchored in Indigenous self-governance, language preservation, and culturally responsive services can simultaneously address health disparities while protecting cultural continuity [29].

Community and individual empowerment: Revitalizing tribal identities [30] and traditional knowledge systems, offering tribal youth culturally sensitive mental health and substance use recovery initiatives [31], preserving traditional ecological livelihoods [32], and expanding access to community information sharing that are critical actions to reinforce cultural continuity at the grassroots [33].

Stewardship and leadership: This involves the institutionalization of tribal councils allowing the younger generation to be engaged [34], investment in Indigenous leadership training, co-management of environmental and heritage spaces, and meaningful representation in local and regional policy bodies.

Together, these strategies recognize that identity is not a passive inheritance but a dynamic source of resilience. It has been ingrained in these communities that cultural sovereignty and equitable development are not opposing goals - they are mutually reinforcing pillars of Indigenous wellness.

Conclusion: reclaiming identity as policy

To truly preserve the Wellness of the Nilgiris, we must recognize that the tribal identity is not a cultural leftover but a living, evolving, spiritual force that epitomises ecological stewardship, intergenerational knowledge, and collective well-being. Their identity has to be elevated and celebrated. The Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve cannot be conserved without the tribes who have safeguarded it for generations. Policies must transcend welfare and must be culturally grounded to elevate and celebrate tribal identity and its autonomy, the right to manage their lands, determine their development pathways, and sustain their governance systems. Reclaiming identity is not only an act of justice but a strategy for sustainable development, climate resilience, and cultural continuity. The time to act is NOW!
