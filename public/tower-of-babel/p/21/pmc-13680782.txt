Papilla‐Preserving Gingival Fiber Retention Osseous Surgery in the Anterior Maxilla: A Novel Clinical Technique
Authors: Abdusalam E. Alrmali, Hom‐Lay Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo describe papilla‐preserving gingival fiber retention osseous surgery (PP‐FibReOS), a biologically driven resective approach intended to eliminate periodontal pockets while maintaining interproximal connective tissue and papillary stability in esthetically demanding maxillary anterior sites.Clinical TechniquePP‐FibReOS was implemented in two challenging anterior maxillary scenarios: functional crown lengthening and surgical pocket elimination in a stage IV, grade C periodontitis case. The key feature of this protocol uses a split‐full‐split buccal flap with selective coronal tissue removal, combined with deliberate preservation of a de‐epithelialized interproximal supracrestal fiber complex attached to the palatal flap, which is designed to support papillary volume during healing. Osseous recontouring is performed conservatively, with the flap repositioned in a double‐layered papillary complex closure just coronal to the bone crest. At 36‐month follow‐up, both cases showed shallow probing pocket depths (PPD) of 1–3 mm and absence of bleeding on probing, together with stable interdental papilla positions, high clinician‐reported esthetic scores (pink esthetic score [PES] 11–12), and patient satisfaction ratings of 10/10.ConclusionWithin the limits of this two‐case proof‐of‐concept report, PP‐FibReOS may offer a promising alternative for maxillary resective surgery when preservation of papillary architecture is a primary objective. Larger controlled studies are needed to clarify its indications, limitations, and long‐term predictability.Clinical SignificancePP‐FibReOS provides a possible approach for selected anterior maxillary defects in which pocket elimination or functional crown lengthening must be balanced against preservation of interdental soft‐tissue dimensions. In patients with a thick periodontal phenotype, adequate supracrestal connective tissue, and predominantly horizontal or shallow angular defects, a fiber‐retentive palatal flap combined with conservative osseous reshaping may help maintain stable, shallow probing pocket depths while limiting increases in clinical crown length and visible loss of papillary fill.

Introduction

Periodontal surgical therapy aims to establish a healthy, maintainable periodontium and favorable restorative conditions by eliminating pathologic pockets or creating adequate supragingival tooth structure, while preserving soft‐tissue support and esthetics, particularly in the maxillary anterior region [1, 2, 3]. Classical resective approaches, such as apically positioned flap (APF) surgery with osseous recontouring, are effective in eliminating periodontal pockets and establishing a positive bony architecture, or in providing the clinical crown length required for prosthetic rehabilitation, but often at the cost of increased clinical crown length and loss of interdental papillae [4, 5, 6, 7]. These esthetic sequelae are especially problematic in patients with high smile lines or triangular tooth forms, where even limited recession can result in visible black triangles.

Consequently, techniques that allow pocket elimination or functional crown lengthening while preserving supracrestal connective tissue (SCT) and papillary volume are of considerable clinical interest [8]. In the early 1970s, Levine introduced periodontal flap surgery with gingival fiber retention as a biologically oriented alternative to conventional resective procedures [9]. The core concept was to preserve a band of SCT fibers inserted in root cementum apical to the junctional epithelium, rather than removing all soft tissues coronal to the alveolar crest [9]. Levine proposed that maintaining these inserted collagen fibers could provide a stable connective‐tissue barrier, limit apical migration of the epithelial attachment, and support a more coronal postsurgical gingival margin [9, 10].

Subsequent clinical reports extended the use of fiber‐retention procedures to the esthetic maxillary anterior region. Dello Russo demonstrated that fiber retention could be applied successfully in patients with early to moderate periodontitis, enabling effective pocket elimination while maintaining interdental papilla height and minimizing root exposure [8]. More recently, Carnevale proposed fiber retention osseous resective surgery (FibReORS) as a novel conservative approach for eliminating shallow infrabony defects [11, 12, 13]. In contrast to classical osseous resective surgery, which uses the most apical interproximal bony crest as the reference for ostectomy, FibReORS uses the level of the connective tissue fiber attachment as the new “bottom” of the defect. Clinical follow‐up studies have suggested that shallow probing depths achieved with FibReORS can be maintained long‐term under supportive periodontal care, with low rates of disease recurrence [11, 12, 13].

Despite these advances, there remains a clinical gap in the management of esthetic anterior cases both periodontitis‐related and prosthetically driven where combined horizontal and shallow infrabony components or disharmonious gingival margins are present. To address this gap, papilla‐preserving gingival fiber‐retention osseous surgery (PP‐FibReOS) is proposed as a proof‐of‐concept variant within the broader family of fiber‐retentive resective procedures. Conceptually, PP‐FibReOS adapts Levine's gingival fiber‐retention flap to contemporary anterior indications ranging from chronic periodontitis to prosthetically driven crown lengthening, but differs from previously described approaches by (1) prioritizing preservation of de‐epithelialized SCT attached to the palatal flap, (2) intentionally constructing a double‐layered papillary complex through superimposition of a de‐epithelialized interproximal papilla beneath a buccal split‐thickness papilla, and (3) combining these soft‐tissue maneuvers with a split‐full‐split flap design and minimal ostectomy with predominately osteoplasty‐oriented osseous reshaping tailored to high‐esthetic‐risk anterior defects. The aim of this two‐case report is therefore to describe the surgical steps and 36‐month clinical outcomes of PP‐FibReOS in an advanced periodontitis case and a prosthetically driven functional crown‐lengthening case involving the maxillary anterior region (Table 1).

Clinical Technique: PP‐FibReOS

PP‐FibReOS was developed as a biologically oriented modification of osseous resective surgery that integrates the key concepts of Levine's gingival fiber‐retention flap [9, 10] and Ramfjord's [14] emphasis on preserving interproximal tissue while reshaping bone into a maintainable physiologic architecture. In PP‐FibReOS, SCT fibers are deliberately preserved and incorporated into the palatal flap rather than being left as an interproximal tissue “island,” as in Levine's original design [9], with the intent of helping control soft‐tissue position and limit postsurgical recession in the esthetic zone. At the same time, minimal interproximal soft‐tissue scalloping and osseous recontouring follow Ramfjord's principle of establishing a cleansable, physiologic contour, while respecting the coronal level of the retained SCT fiber apparatus [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. By treating the interproximal bone connective tissue complex as a functional unit and minimizing vertical bone removal beneath the papillae, PP‐FibReOS aims to combine effective pocket elimination with preservation of interproximal soft‐tissue support, thereby reducing the risk of papillary collapse and black triangle formation while still providing the access and long‐term maintainability expected from osseous resective surgery.

Case Selection and Indications

PP‐FibReOS was reserved for patients in whom the soft tissue and bony architecture offered a favorable biologic starting point. Ideal candidates exhibited a thick periodontal phenotype, horizontal or only shallow angular defects, and SCT dimensions sufficient to allow fiber preservation without compromising the interproximal soft‐tissue complex. The technique was primarily considered in high‐esthetic‐risk anterior sextants and in functional crown‐lengthening situations where maintaining interdental papillae was a critical treatment objective.

Conversely, PP‐FibReOS was avoided in thin periodontal phenotypes, in the presence of deep, narrow intrabony defects, or where severe interproximal attachment loss and markedly reduced papillary support were already present. Patients who were current smokers, had poor plaque control, or displayed unstable periodontal conditions were likewise not considered suitable candidates, given the technique's reliance on stable soft‐tissue conditions and rigorous maintenance.

Technique Description

Step 1: Presurgical Preparation and Anesthesia

Nonsurgical therapy (supra‐ and subgingival debridement, plaque control reinforcement, correction of local plaque‐retentive factors) is completed and residual pockets with bleeding on probing are confirmed at re‐evaluation. Standard medical and periodontal risk assessment is performed, and surgery is scheduled when inflammation is below approximately 10%. On the day of surgery, the patient rinses with 0.12%–0.2% chlorhexidine; local anesthesia is achieved (Figures 1E, 4A and 8A).

Step 2: Buccal Split‐Full‐Split Flap Design in the Esthetic Zone

On the buccal aspect, a 0.5 mm internally beveled, scalloped incision is made across the anterior segment, positioned to preserve a band of attached gingiva and respect existing papillary form. Vertical releases are avoided when possible. A split‐thickness flap is reflected coronally, transitioning to full thickness over the crestal bone and then back to split thickness apically (split‐full‐split), allowing thinning of the marginal tissues while maintaining a SCT cuff that will be de‐epithelized and reflected with the palatal flap (Figures 2A,B and 4B).

Step 3: Palatal Full‐Thickness Flap and Papilla De‐Epithelialization

On the palatal aspect, a 0.5–1 mm internally beveled, scalloped incision is made across the midpalatal surface of the involved teeth. Only a full‐thickness flap is reflected to provide access for debridement and osseous reshaping from the palatal side. The interproximal papillae are carefully de‐epithelialized either immediately before or after flap elevation, creating a vascular connective tissue bed capable of integrating with the buccal papilla. The de‐epithelialized interproximal papilla is then gently detached from the adjacent root surfaces using a small universal curette and reflected together with the palatal flap (Figures 2C–E and 4C–E).

Step 4: Osseous Surgery and Double‐Layered Papillary Complex

Granulation tissue and pocket epithelium coronal to the crestal bone are removed to establish a clean root surface. Because a portion of the supracrestal fiber apparatus remains attached to the palatal flap, only minimal interproximal ostectomy is performed, and osseous recontouring is limited to what is necessary to achieve physiologic architecture and optimal flap adaptation. The de‐epithelialized palatal papilla is then rotated beneath the buccal split‐thickness papilla and sutured to it apically, creating a double‐layered interproximal soft‐tissue complex designed to provide structural support for papillary volume and resist collapse of the embrasure. This double‐layered papillary complex represents the central biologic and mechanical principle of the PP‐FibReOS technique (Figures 2F,G, 4F,G).

Step 5: Flap Repositioning, Suturing, and Postoperative Care

The buccal split‐thickness flap is adapted over the preserved connective tissue fibers and de‐epithelialized interproximal papillae and stabilized with either a single external vertical mattress or a continuous crossing‐mattress periosteal suture technique [20] (Figures 2H, 4H, 8B–D). The choice of suturing technique was adapted to the extent and configuration of the treated segment. Single external vertical mattress sutures were used for isolated or short‐span defects, where they provided secure buccolingual stabilization of the interproximal complex and allowed fine adjustment of the apical repositioning of individual papillae. In contrast, a continuous crossing mattress periosteal suture was selected for longer segments involving multiple adjacent defects, as this configuration facilitated uniform apical repositioning of the flap, distributed tension along the treated area, and secured the facial tissue positioning alongside interproximal papillae. The flap is positioned at or slightly apical to the original gingival margin, with intimate interproximal adaptation. The buccal split‐thickness flap is advanced to cover the de‐epithelialized papilla, with the buccal papillary tissue overlapping the palatal connective‐tissue papilla as extensively as is compatible with passive flap positioning. This creates a double‐layered interproximal complex in which the buccal layer provides the external contour and the palatal connective‐tissue layer serves as the internal scaffold, helping to provide a stable connective‐tissue barrier, limit apical migration of the epithelial attachment, and support a more coronal postsurgical gingival margin. Postoperative care includes chlorhexidine rinses, systemic analgesics (and antibiotics when indicated), suture removal after 7–10 days, and enrollment in a 3–6 monthly supportive periodontal care program (Figures 3A,B, 4I, 8E).

Case Applications

This technique was applied in two anterior maxillary situations at high esthetic risk: an advanced periodontitis case requiring pocket elimination and subsequent prosthetic rehabilitation (Figures 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) and a crown‐lengthening case with supracrestal tissue attachment violation (Figures 7, 8, 9, 10). The inclusion of both a severe Stage IV Grade C periodontitis case and a prosthetically driven crown‐lengthening case reflects an intentional proof‐of‐concept design aimed at illustrating the shared indication spectrum of PP‐FibReOS rather than providing internally homogeneous outcomes. In both scenarios, the technique was applied only where a comparable set of prerequisites was present, namely, several millimeters of SCT, predominantly horizontal bone loss or a compromised interproximal papilla, and a critical need to preserve papillary volume and anterior esthetics. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this heterogeneity limits internal consistency and does not permit firm conclusions regarding specific case types; larger series with more narrowly defined inclusion criteria will be required to delineate the most appropriate indications for PP‐FibReOS within both periodontal and prosthetically driven contexts. In addition, the restorative and prosthetic phases, including the design of provisional and definitive restorations, likely contributed to soft‐tissue maturation and stability and should be considered integral components of the overall treatment concept.

Discussion

The present report describes papilla‐preserving gingival fiber retention osseous surgery (PP‐FibReOS) as a biologically oriented resective approach intended to eliminate anterior periodontal pockets while preserving SCT and interproximal soft‐tissue support in esthetically critical sites. In both applications of the technique, shallow residual probing depths and stable CAL were observed over 3 years without clinically relevant increases in crown length or the development of obvious black triangles. These observations suggest that, when local anatomy is favorable, PP‐FibReOS may help reconcile the traditional objectives of resective surgery (access, pocket elimination, and stable architecture) with contemporary demands for papilla preservation and maintenance of gingival symmetry around definitive restorations.

The proposed biologic rationale for PP‐FibReOS should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than as a demonstrated outcome of this report. In particular, the concept that preserved SCT functions as a biologic scaffold that supports interproximal soft‐tissue volume and limits papillary collapse, while consistent with prior fiber‐retention literature, was not directly tested in these two cases. Likewise, statements regarding the potential behavior of retained Sharpey‐type fibers and their integration into the healing connective tissue are extrapolated from histologic observations in previous studies and should not be viewed as histologic evidence specific to PP‐FibReOS [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Earlier fiber‐retention work by Levine et al. indicated that preserving SCT fibers can help control postsurgical marginal position and limit recession, while subsequent clinical applications in the anterior maxilla showed that, in suitably selected cases, this strategy supports pocket resolution with maintenance of papillary height. In this context, PP‐FibReOS can be viewed as a contemporary adaptation that uses these biologic principles to address combined functional and esthetic demands in the anterior maxilla, particularly where several millimeters of supracrestal tissue and favorable defect morphology are present [9, 10]. FibReORS further demonstrated that referencing the preserved fiber apparatus for osseous reshaping allows pocket elimination with more conservative vertical ostectomy [11, 12, 13]. PP‐FibReOS builds on the same preservation concept but shifts the emphasis toward a papilla‐focused, double‐layered soft‐tissue design: by retaining palatal supracrestal fibers, limiting interproximal bone removal, and overlaying a buccal split‐thickness papilla, the technique aims primarily to stabilize papillary volume and gingival symmetry while still delivering the access and shallow residual pockets expected from resective surgery.

When compared conceptually with conventional APF surgery with osseous recontouring, PP‐FibReOS may therefore offer several potential advantages in appropriately selected anterior cases. First, preservation of the interproximal supracrestal fiber apparatus and avoidance of aggressive vertical ostectomy beneath the papillae may better support long‐term papillary height, even if complete embrasure fill is not achieved in every site. Second, the split‐full‐split buccal flap design, combined with precise adaptation over the de‐epithelized SCT fiber band, appears to limit apical displacement of the gingival margin, thereby minimizing unwanted increases in clinical crown length while still achieving pocket elimination. Third, the ability to obtain shallow residual pockets while maintaining relatively stable gingival contours may mitigate the classic trade‐off between disease control and esthetic appearance that often constrains resective surgery in the anterior maxilla.

From a prosthodontic perspective, the soft‐tissue stability observed around definitive restorations in the treated cases underscores the importance of integrating fiber‐preserving resective surgery into an interdisciplinary treatment plan. Maintenance of relatively stable gingival margins and papillary form facilitated a favorable emergence profile and a natural smile line, illustrating how a carefully executed resective procedure can support restorative outcomes in patients with high esthetic demands. Even though some embrasures may not be completely filled, reducing the number and visibility of black triangles may substantially improve patient satisfaction compared with conventional resective approaches.

This report has several important limitations that warrant a cautious interpretation of the findings. First, PP‐FibReOS was applied only in carefully selected anterior cases with favorable supracrestal tissue height and defect configuration, which limits the generalizability of the results to more complex morphologies or sites with reduced attached gingiva. Second, the study lacked a parallel control group treated with conventional APF or regenerative procedures, so any apparent advantages of PP‐FibReOS remain hypothesis‐generating rather than demonstrative of superiority. Third, the small sample size and relatively short follow‐up period preclude firm conclusions about long‐term stability of papillary volume, soft‐tissue symmetry, and periodontal support. In addition, the restorative and prosthetic phases including the design of provisional and definitive restorations likely contributed to soft‐tissue maturation and stability and may be considered integral components of the overall treatment concept. Finally, the surgical protocol involved a high level of operator experience, and it is unclear whether similar outcomes could be routinely achieved in broader clinical practice without additional training and without careful case selection of patients able to maintain meticulous plaque control and comply with supportive periodontal care [21, 22]. Future studies should compare PP‐FibReOS with conventional APF and regenerative techniques in similar defect types, ideally incorporating standardized esthetic indices and patient‐reported outcomes. In that context, the adjunctive use of biologic agents may be explored to determine whether they further enhance soft‐tissue healing and maturation when combined with PP‐FibReOS [23, 24, 25].

Conclusions

Within the limitations of a two‐case proof‐of‐concept report involving anterior maxillary sites with 3‐year follow‐up, papilla‐preserving gingival fiber‐retention osseous surgery (PP‐FibReOS) may offer a feasible approach in situations where pocket reduction or functional crown lengthening must be balanced against the need to preserve papillary support in the esthetic zone. In the cases presented, conservative osseous recontouring combined with deliberate preservation and coronal repositioning of supracrestal gingival fibers was associated with stable shallow probing depths, maintenance of clinical attachment, and largely preserved interdental papillary fill, without obvious increases in clinical crown length or conspicuous black triangles at medium‐term follow‐up. These observations should be regarded as illustrative rather than definitive, and they underscore the need for larger, controlled clinical studies directly comparing PP‐FibReOS with conventional APF surgery and regenerative procedures in similar defect types, in order to clarify its indications, limitations, and long‐term predictability.
