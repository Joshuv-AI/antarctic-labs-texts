Transient Myocardial Thickening in an 11‐Year‐Old Cat Infected With Feline Infectious Peritonitis and Treated With GS‐441524
Authors: M Dhunputh, M Petibon, A Avé, B Castro, S Besançon, P Verwaerde, V Saponaro, Rita Payan-Carreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case report provides a thorough description of the clinical assessment and management of an extremely rare case of transient myocardial thickening associated with feline infectious peritonitis (FIP) and highlights the overlap of clinical signs secondary to FIP or congestive heart failure. An 11‐year‐old male Norwegian cat was presented with a 3‐week history of weakness, dysorexia, tachypnea, dyspnea, and weight loss. Thoracic point‐of‐care ultrasound (POCUS) revealed severe bilateral pleural effusion without cardiac chamber enlargement. Blood work including serum protein electrophoresis (demonstrating polyclonal gammopathy) was suggestive of FIP. Analyses of pleural effusion consisted of real‐time PCR being positive for FCoV nucleic acids with a high viral load and slightly equivocal increased NT‐proBNP. Together with the noncardiogenic nature of the effusion, these findings were consistent with a diagnosis of FIP. Three days after the presentation, the cat exhibited progressive dyspnea secondary to newly collected pleural effusion with markedly increased NT‐proBNP. At that time, echocardiography revealed left ventricular wall thickening and left atrial enlargement, thus suggesting hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or myocarditis with left‐sided congestive heart failure. Cardiac troponin I concentration was increased. Nine days after admission, the patient was discharged on cardiac therapy and nucleoside analog GS‐441524. Fifteen days after initiation of GS treatment, echocardiography showed a decrease in left ventricular wall thickness. No long‐term complications or recurrences of FIP or myocardial hypertrophy were observed at the 1.5‐, 4‐, and 18‐month follow‐ups, with normalization of cardiac troponin I concentration and echocardiographic variables, thus supporting the hypothesis of FIP‐related transient myocardial thickening. To the authors′ knowledge, this is one of the rare reports of transient myocardial thickening in the FIP setting, following GS‐441524 treatment. Cardiac POCUS and NT‐proBNP dosage in pleural effusion should systematically be performed in order to unmask congestive heart failure.Keywords: cardiac Troponin I, echocardiography, feline infectious peritonitis, myocarditis, pleural effusion

1. Introduction

This report details the clinical assessment of a rare case of transient myocardial thickening associated with feline infectious peritonitis (FIP) and points out the importance of cardiac biomarkers for a correct therapeutic approach. Feline myocarditis is a form of myocardial disease characterized by the presence of myocardial necrosis or degeneration and inflammation [1]. Two echocardiographic phenotypes are typically described: hypertrophic [2–4] and, less commonly, a dilated form [5]. A particular variant of myocarditis is characterized by a transient hypertrophic phenotype [6–9]. A few cases of suspected coronavirus‐associated myocarditis have already been reported in the veterinary literature, both with SARS‐CoV‐2 [10] and FIP [2, 5, 11, 12]. A recent study described the outcome of 40 cats diagnosed with FIP, including four with signs compatible with myocarditis and normalization of echocardiogram after the GS‐441524 treatment [13]. However, in these cases, cardiac troponin I concentration (cTnI) was assessed only at presentation, and follow‐up was based solely on normalization of cardiac chamber dimensions. This report describes one of the rare cases of complete resolution of suspected FIP‐related myocarditis following GS‐441524 treatment, with a normal echocardiogram and cTnI at follow‐up.

2. Case Description

An 11‐year‐old intact male Norwegian cat weighing 6.2 kg was presented to the neurology service of the veterinary hospital for small animals of the National Veterinary School of Alfort, where he had been followed for idiopathic epilepsy for 4 months. He exhibited tachypnea, weakness, and weight loss and therefore was referred to the emergency unit for a full clinical assessment.

On physical examination, the cat presented with tachypnea and restrictive dyspnea. He also showed mild dehydration (6%) and slight hypothermia (37.2°C). Thoracic auscultation revealed bradycardia (90 bpm), muffled heart sounds, and moderate crackles. The cat was placed in an oxygen‐enriched cage and received furosemide (2 mg/kg IV) and butorphanol (0.2 mg/kg IV). Systemic blood pressure was measured at 100 mmHg by the Doppler method.

Severe pleural effusion on both thoracic sides was visualized on thoracic POCUS which did not show left atrial enlargement at this time (left atrial‐to‐aortic ratio [LA : Ao = 1.1]; upper limit = 1.6 [14]). Thoracocentesis, performed on the right hemithorax, yielded approximately 100 mL of aseptic neutrophilic yellow exudate without any bacterial growth (cellularity = 16, 690/mm3; protein level = 85 g/L; specific gravity = 1.035). Evaluation of NT‐proBNP concentration in the pleural fluid showed a slight equivocal increase (209 pmol/L; RI < 100 pmol/L) [15]. Thoracic radiographs revealed an alveolar pattern in the cranial lung lobes, suggesting atelectasis or pneumonia (Figure 1). Due to the absence of cardiac abnormalities, the cardiac cause of pleural effusion was ruled out. Abdominal ultrasound revealed a pancreatic‐duodenal and hepatic adenopathy along with splenomegaly.

Thoracic radiograph in the right lateral recumbency in an 11‐year‐old Norwegian male cat presented with dyspnea. Extended opacification of cranial lung lobes with an alveolar pattern, according to the presence of bronchograms, suggesting atelectasis or pneumonia.

Hematology and biochemistry revealed neutrophilia (13, 130/mm3; RI = 2300 − 10, 290/mm3), moderate nonregenerative, normocytic, normochromic anemia (26.9%; RI = 30.3% − 52.3%), marked hyperproteinemia (106 g/L; RI = 64 − 96 g/L), slight hypoalbuminemia (21 g/L; RI = 25 − 35 g/L), and severe hyperglobulinemia (85 g/L; RI = 28–51 g/L) with an albumin/globulin ratio of 0.2. Moderate hyperbilirubinemia and hypokalemia were also noted (17.7 mg/L; RI = 1.1 ± 3.7 mg/L and 3.4 mmol/L; RI = 3.6–5.5 mmol/L, respectively). Total thyroxine was 25 nmol/L (normal < 50 nmol/L). Based on hyperproteinemia, hyperbilirubinemia, and the presence of noncardiogenic pleural effusion, FIP was considered the most likely diagnosis. In order to confirm this hypothesis, serum protein electrophoresis was performed and revealed polyclonal gammopathy with marked hypoalbuminemia (Figure 2). Furthermore, real‐time PCR for FCoV nucleic acids was performed on the pleural effusion and revealed a high viral load, thus strengthening the suspicion of FIP (2.74 × 104).

Serum protein electrophoresis performed at presentation in an 11‐year‐old Norwegian male cat exhibiting pleural effusion, slight hypoalbuminemia, severe hyperglobulinemia, and an albumin/globulin ratio of 0.2, in order to support the hypothesis of FIP infection. The electrophoresis revealed polyclonal gammopathy with marked hypoalbuminemia and was followed by a positive real‐time PCR result for FCoV nucleic acids, confirming the hypothesis.

At this time, the treatment consisted of sodium lactate solution (2 mL/kg/h) for rehydration, phenobarbital (6 mg/kg q24h PO) for the pre‐existing epilepsy, potassium gluconate (468 mg q12h PO), and mirtazapine pomade for improving the appetite.

Three days afterward, the cat was still tachypneic; thoracic POCUS confirmed pleural effusion and revealed, this time, a moderate left atrial enlargement. Fluid therapy was discontinued, and a second thoracocentesis was performed, removing 100 mL of pleural effusion with breathing improvement. At this time, NT‐proBNP concentration in pleural effusion was markedly increased (434 pmol/L), thus suggesting the onset of left congestive heart failure (CHF).

A complete cardiac assessment was then performed. Persistent bradycardia (HR: 110 bpm) was classified as sinus bradycardia, interrupted by periods of ventricular escape rhythm. Echocardiography revealed, on the right parasternal short‐axis view, a left ventricular wall thickening, with a free wall (LVFWd) and interventricular septum diastolic (IVSd) thickness measuring 6.6 and 6.2 mm, respectively (RI = 3.2–5.3 mm and RI = 3.1–5.4 mm, respectively) [16]. Normal left ventricular dimensions in diastole that were slightly decreased in systole (left ventricular internal diameter in diastole = 16 mm, RI = 13.7–21.3 mm; and in systole = 5.1 mm, RI = 6.2 − 13.7 mm) along with a slightly increased shortening fraction (68%; RI = 28%–62%) were also observed [16] (Figure 3B). Left atrial enlargement (LA mediolateral diameter [LAd] = 18.9 mm; RI = 9.9 − 15.8 mm; LA : Ao = 1.7) [16] (Figure 4A,B) and thickening of the midseptum in the long axis (7.2 mm in diastole) were also appreciated, together with mild mitral regurgitation on color Doppler and mild pericardial effusion in the right parasternal view. All the mentioned findings were consistent with a hypertrophic phenotype in ACVIM Stage C [14]. As FIP was just diagnosed, the hypothesis of myocarditis was considered likely; therefore, cTnI was tested revealing a concentration of 0.93 ng/mL (upper normal limit = 0.06 ng/mL). The treatment was adjusted by adding clopidogrel 18.75 mg q24h PO, furosemide 1.6 mg/kg q12h IV, and pimobendan 0.2 mg/kg q12h PO. Steroid therapy was added (dexamethasone 0.1 mg/kg q24h IV), for 4 days.

The treatment adjustment allowed clinical improvement in 48 h consisting of regained appetite and sternal (versus lateral) recumbency, normalization of heart rate (160 bpm), blood pressure (130 mmHg), and breathing pattern, with resolution of pleural effusion. One short seizure episode occurred and was managed by midazolam administration (0.2 mg/kg IV), with no further seizure episodes reported afterward. On the 7th day of hospitalization, echocardiography revealed the improvement of left atrial dilation, and the furosemide dose was then progressively decreased to 0.8 mg/kg q12h. Nine days after admission, due to progressive general improvement, the cat was discharged on treatment for heart failure (pimobendan, furosemide, and clopidogrel at the last doses), phenobarbital for epilepsy, oral supplementation with potassium gluconate, and nucleoside analog GS‐441524, a specific treatment for FIP, for 84 days at the dose of 10 mg/kg q24h SC.

At 2‐week follow‐up, on antiviral treatment, the cat did not exhibit any clinical signs, and echocardiography revealed a decrease in left ventricular wall thickness, now falling within the reference intervals (LVFWd and IVSd = 5.1 and 4.6 mm, respectively) and normalization of left atrial size (LAd = 14.5 mm; LA : Ao = 1.2). Therefore, clopidogrel was discontinued, and furosemide and pimobendan doses were further decreased to 0.8 and 0.2 mg/kg q24h PO, respectively.

At 1.5‐month follow‐up, still on antiviral treatment, biochemistry and hematology revealed a normal neutrophil count and normalization of proteinemia (globulin and albumin). A new cTnI dosage showed a concentration within the normal range (0.02 ng/mL). Abdominal ultrasounds showed resolution of adenopathy.

At 4‐month follow‐up, 1 month after antiviral treatment discontinuation, echocardiography did not show any cardiac abnormalities: LVFWd and IVSd measuring 4.9 and 4 mm, respectively (Figure 3D). Therefore, furosemide and pimobendan were definitively discontinued. At the time of writing, 1 and a half years after the first presentation, the cat is still alive with no clinical signs of FIP or of heart failure.

3. Discussion

The outcome illustrated here resembles the one described in the cases of transient myocardial thickening, which is often associated with increased cTnI concentrations [6–10]. It usually occurs in young cats and is often preceded by recent events such as neutering surgery or other kinds of stress [6–9, 17, 18], while some cases have also been described in infectious settings [3, 6–8, 17]. It has already been compared to Takotsubo cardiomyopathy in humans, an acute/fulminant stress–induced deformation of a failing heart [19–24]. Hence, it is possible that transient myocardial thickening is a reversible form of feline myocarditis.

Myocarditis in cats on echocardiography is usually characterized by symmetrical thickening of the myocardium, with the left (and potentially the right) ventricle involved [3, 25]. Rarely, a restrictive phenotype can be observed, as in humans, and less frequently, a hypokinetic/dilated phenotype has been reported [26]; the latter represents the most common myocarditis‐associated phenotype in humans and dogs [1, 27]. Due to this variety of echocardiographic phenotypes, the diagnosis of myocarditis cannot be based only on echocardiography but should be confirmed by other tests. Currently, cTnI appears to be a useful test to support the diagnosis of myocarditis in cats, as being often severely increased in the case of this condition [4, 6, 7, 10, 28]. Cardiac troponin I is also severely increased in the case of decompensated hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [29]. In the cat of the present report, the cTnI value was not as high as mostly reported in cats suffering from myocarditis, thus not enough to differentiate myocarditis from HCM. However, the resolution of the hypertrophy and left CHF, as well as the decrease in cTnI value after starting the antiviral treatment, allows to hypothesize that the acute myocardial injury was simultaneous with FIP; although a previous echocardiogram was not available for comparison. In humans, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and endomyocardial biopsy are useful to confirm myocarditis, the second being the gold standard [26, 27], but those tests were not proposed to the owner in the present report due to the clinical improvement of his cat during the hospitalization and the final risk–benefit analysis. Moreover, biopsies are not routinely performed in cats [30, 31].

The distinction between primary HCM and myocarditis is difficult. In vivo, some useful diagnostic criteria for myocarditis are “absence of any previous history of heart disease, the per‐acute onset of clinical signs characterized by lethargy, inappetence […], tachypnea/dyspnea secondary to the presence of congestive heart failure […], elevation of cardiac troponin‐I […], evidence of myocardial disease on echocardiography” [10]. In the present report, all the criteria of myocarditis were met, and HCM was definitely ruled out based on normalization of wall thickness and troponin concentration after antiviral treatment and the lack of left CHF relapse without therapy, during all the follow‐up period (1.5 years).

Various infectious pathogens are described in association with myocarditis in feline medicine: Toxoplasma gondii [3, 6], Salmonella typhimurium [32], Bartonella spp. [33–37], Proteus mirabilis [38], Streptococcus spp. [28, 39], feline immunodeficiency virus [25], parvovirus [40, 41], and coronavirus [2, 5, 12, 42]. A limitation of the present report is represented by the lack of differential diagnostic tests for other infectious agents.

However, remission of infectious myocarditis is rare in veterinary literature, in the absence of a specific therapy [2, 5, 12, 42]; some cases describe disappearance of echocardiographic and biological alterations after treatment [6, 7, 10]. The same is for untreated FIP‐related myocarditis. Interestingly, in two recent studies, cats infected with FIP and having received the antiviral treatment seemed to show a reversible myocarditis. The latter was strongly suspected based on hypertrophy or troponin concentration increase at presentation, along with a favorable outcome after antiviral treatment [13, 43]. These two recent studies suggest that the antiviral treatment can cure FIP‐related myocarditis if combined with the left CHF therapy; however, they lack a clinical key point of a reversible feline myocarditis : the transient myocardial thickening. Indeed, the study from Cerna et al., (published only as an abstract at time of the final revision of the present paper), described a mild elevation in cTnI, returning to normal value after treatment in six cats but nothing about an echocardiographic follow‐up. The study by Buchta et al. reports a cTnI increase at presentation in four infected cats, but only equivocal myocardial hypertrophy upon echocardiography [13]. Moreover, in these four cats, pleural effusion cannot clearly be considered secondary to left CHF due to the lack of NT‐proBNP dosage in contrast to the present report. Indeed, NT‐proBNP dosage, especially if sampled in pleural effusion, can be a very useful and simple‐to‐perform ancillary test to confirm CHF in the FIP setting. In this particular context, its usefulness consists of permitting a differential pathophysiological diagnosis of pleural effusion [15]. In the present report, the significant increase of this biomarker, 3 days after presentation, indicated that pleural effusion was not only the consequence of FIP exudation but also the consequence of CHF due to an extremely increased myocardial wall stress, thus justifying the use of inodilators and diuretics along with antiviral treatment. This is even more important since the kidney is a target organ of the FIP virus; therefore, improper use of nephrotoxic drugs as diuretics could worsen the renal status of the patient. Another interesting finding in this case was the initially persisting sinus bradycardia. Due to its appearance before left atrial dilation and disappearance after resolution of cardiogenic pleural effusion, bradycardia was probably related to mixed causes: myocarditis and heart failure. Bradycardia is not infrequent in cats suffering from myocarditis and/or transient myocardial thickening and is considered related to the decompensatory shock [6]. In humans, myocarditis can be associated with symptomatic bradycardia [44]. Another possible cause could have been hypoxia, resulting from pleural effusion [45]. Concerning the high antiviral dose used for the cat of the present report, it was chosen as a seizure episode occurred during hospitalization, and neurological FIP was not completely ruled out at this time, even if the cat was known to suffer from the idiopathic form [46].

The present case is the first clearly describing the involvement of CHF associated with transient myocardial thickening that, along with transient cTnI elevation, was most probably the manifestations of FIP‐related myocarditis.

4. Conclusions

This report confirms that specific antiviral treatment can lead to a full recovery of FIP‐related myocarditis and suggests that NT‐proBNP should be routinely performed in this setting, to confirm CHF and justify the use of diuretics. At the time of writing, the cat was still alive without any FIP‐ or CHF‐related clinical signs and had a normal echocardiogram.
