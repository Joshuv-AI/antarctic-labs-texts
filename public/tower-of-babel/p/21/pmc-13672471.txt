Serial determination of caffeine and active metabolites in a case of severe caffeine poisoning causing recurrent cardiac arrest after hemodialysis: a case report
Authors: Saeko Kohara, Yoshito Kamijo, Ryoko Kyan, Hiroko Abe, Taiki Yamataka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Caffeine is highly dialyzable, and cases of severe caffeine poisoning causing recurrent symptoms after hemodialysis (HD) and the serial evaluation of active metabolite and caffeine levels have rarely been reported. A previously healthy 17-year-old female with cardiac arrest due to ventricular fibrillation was transferred by ambulance to our emergency department. Laboratory tests revealed severe lactic acidosis and hypokalemia. As cardiac defibrillation was ineffective, veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was initiated after tracheal intubation. Eleven hours after admission, ingestion of highly caffeinated tablets (12.1 g of caffeine in total) was revealed, and HD was performed for 4 h, achieving hemodynamic stability. She developed recurrent cardiac arrest 26 h after admission. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved with 1 mg of intravenous adrenaline, and continuous hemodiafiltration was performed, resulting in circulatory stability. Complications included abdominal compartment syndrome, right lower limb compartment syndrome, and hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. She was transferred to a rehabilitation facility on day 111. Serum concentrations of caffeine and its active metabolites were determined using liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry. Serum caffeine concentrations on admission and at the time of the second cardiac arrest were 233 µg/mL and 44 µg/mL, respectively, with serum concentrations of active metabolites (paraxanthine, theobromine, and theophylline) re-elevated at the time of the second cardiac arrest. It should be kept in mind that recurrence of toxic symptoms may occur due to delayed elevation of active metabolites, particularly in critically ill patients with severe complications.Keywords: Caffeine intoxication, Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, Hemodialysis, Cardiac arrest, Active metabolites

Introduction

In Japan, reports of acute caffeine poisoning caused by massive, caffeinated tablet ingestion have been increasing, and severe and fatal cases have been observed [1]. As caffeine is highly dialysable, few studies of recurrent blood level elevations after hemodialysis (HD) or recurrence of toxic signs or symptoms have been reported [2]. Herein, we report a case of a patient with severe caffeine poisoning causing cardiac arrest, in which HD under circulatory support by veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was performed. The patient’s hemodynamics stabilized; however, a second cardiac arrest occurred. In the present case, active caffeine metabolites (paraxanthine, theobromine, and theophylline) and caffeine levels were serially measured. Based on the findings of the present case, delayed caffeine metabolism and the accumulation of active metabolites may contribute to delayed death or sudden deterioration even after apparent stabilization following HD. These observations also have important forensic implications for the evaluation of the cause of death and postmortem interpretation in cases of caffeine poisoning.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old female without past medical history was found unconscious in the bathroom at home by her family, who immediately called an ambulance. Traces of vomiting were noted; however, the duration of unconsciousness was unknown. When the ambulance arrived, she was found to have cardiac arrest and was transported to the emergency center while receiving cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Upon arrival, the patient had ventricular fibrillation, which changed to pulseless electrical activity after 200 J of electric defibrillation. VA-ECMO and mechanical ventilation after tracheal intubation were initiated. Shortly after the initiation of VA-ECMO, spontaneous cardiac activity returned, but her blood pressure remained unstable and low. A 12-lead electrocardiogram revealed wide QRS complexes. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated severe lactic acidosis and hypokalemia: pH, 6.508; base excess, − 36.6; lactate, 200.1 mg/dL; and K+, 3.1 mEq/L. Head computed tomography (CT), whole-body contrast-enhanced CT, and coronary angiography showed no significant abnormalities. Qualitative urinary drug screening using an immunochromatographic assay was conducted, which tested for amphetamines, barbiturates, benzodiazepines, cocaine, cannabis, methylenedioxymethamphetamine, morphine-related opioids, oxycodone, phencyclidine, propoxyphene, and tricyclic antidepressants; all results were negative. The cause of the cardiac arrest was unclear at this point. Potassium supplementation was initiated, and continuous intravenous infusion of norepinephrine (up to 0.37 µg/kg/min) was administered. Approximately 5 h after admission, abdominal distension was observed, and the intravesical pressure was elevated to 38 mmHg. Repeat abdominal contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated delayed descending aorta enhancement and bowel wall edema with poor enhancement in the colon and rectum, leading to an abdominal compartment syndrome (ACS) diagnosis. Afterward, the intravesical pressure stabilized, and the ACS was conservatively managed. As the blood pressure stabilized with norepinephrine administration, VA-ECMO was discontinued 10 h after admission.

At 11 h after admission, it was revealed that the patient had ingested 121 Estaron-mocha® tablets (9.2-mm diameter, 3.9-mm thick tablet, immediate-release, SS Pharmacy, Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), each containing 100 mg of caffeine (12.1 g in total). HD was performed at a blood flow rate (QB) of 180 mL/min and a dialysate flow rate (QD) of 500 mL/min for 4 h. After the HD, the norepinephrine was gradually tapered. She also developed right lower leg compartment syndrome, for which a fasciotomy was performed. Although her hemodynamics had remained stable for a period, the patient developed hypotension leading to recurrent cardiac arrest 26 h after admission. At that time, laboratory testing revealed no abnormalities in serum potassium or cardiac enzyme levels, or other related parameters. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved with 1 mg of intravenous adrenaline, and continuous hemodiafiltration was performed at a QB of 80 mL/min, a QD of 600 mL/h, and a filtration flow rate (Qf) of 200 mL/h for 5 days. Her circulatory status subsequently improved. A tracheostomy and right lower limb amputation were performed on days 8 and 9, respectively. Mechanical ventilation was discontinued on day 10, but the patient remained comatose. Brain magnetic resonance imaging was suggestive of hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. During hospitalization, she developed aspiration pneumonia, a urinary tract infection, and status epilepticus. On day 111, she was transferred to a rehabilitation facility, with persistent central nervous system dysfunction indicated by Glasgow Coma Scale scores of E3, V1, and M4.

Toxicological analysis

Serum caffeine and active metabolite levels (paraxanthine, theobromine, and theophylline) were measured using liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry. Calibration curves for all analytes were linear over the concentration range of 0.1–250 µg/mL. The lower limit of quantification for each compound was 0.1 µg/mL. Deuterated diazepam (diazepam-d5) was used as the internal standard for quantification. The blood caffeine level on admission was markedly elevated at 233 µg/mL. Discussion

Caffeine exerts central nervous system and myocardial stimulatory effects via catecholamine release from the adrenal medulla, phosphodiesterase inhibition, and competitive adenosine receptor antagonism [3]. In severe caffeine poisoning, impaired consciousness, status epilepticus, tachyarrhythmias, and cardiac arrest may occur. At blood caffeine levels ≥ 25 µg/mL, poisoning may occur. At blood caffeine levels ≥ 140 µg/mL, severe poisoning may occur, and blood purification methods such as HD are recommended to remove the substance [4]. Jones et al. reported that the mean (± standard deviation), median, and range of caffeine concentrations in postmortem blood were 187 (± 96 µg/mL), 180 µg/mL, and 33–567 µg/mL, respectively [5]. In addition, Kamijo et al. investigated 101 patients with acute caffeine intoxication and reported that seven patients (6.9%) developed cardiac arrest and three died; notably, cardiac arrest occurred in patients who had consumed approximately 6.0 g of caffeine or whose serum caffeine concentrations on admission were ≥ 200 µg/mL [1]. To the best of our knowledge, there are no published reports that have systematically evaluated blood concentrations of caffeine metabolites in fatal caffeine poisoning; although metabolites have occasionally been detected in fatal cases [6], detailed quantitative analyses were not performed. In the present case, the serum caffeine concentration on admission was markedly elevated at 233 µg/mL. When interpreted considering the aforementioned forensic and clinical literature, this concentration is consistent with an exposure capable of causing lethal arrhythmias and cardiac arrest.

Caffeine is metabolized to paraxanthine (80%), theobromine (10%), and theophylline (4%). These metabolites, as well as caffeine, are pharmacologically active: paraxanthine exerts twice the adenosine receptor antagonistic activity of caffeine, and theophylline exerts approximately 2–3 times the adenosine receptor antagonistic activity of caffeine and stronger phosphodiesterase inhibitory activity than caffeine [7–10]. Yamazaki et al. [11] defined the “blood xanthine concentration” as [blood caffeine concentration] + [blood paraxanthine concentration] × 2 and suggested that this value may correlate more strongly with the poisoning severity than blood caffeine levels alone, and that residual caffeine metabolism may result in a delayed increase in active metabolite levels, potentially prolonging intoxication symptoms. However, there have been no reports in which the serum theobromine, theophylline, paraxanthine, and caffeine levels were measured serially, and the role of these active metabolites in toxic symptoms was suggested.

In the present case, the patient developed a lethal arrhythmia when the blood caffeine level was 233 µg/mL. After a 4-h HD session, her hemodynamics stabilized. However, the patient experienced a second cardiac arrest 26 h after admission, when the blood caffeine level had decreased to 44 µg/mL, but the paraxanthine, theobromine, and theophylline levels were elevated to 5.3, 1.3, and 0.71 µg/mL, respectively. There were no other causes for the second cardiac arrest, such as hyperkalemia. In addition to the toxic blood caffeine levels (> 25 µg/mL), the delayed increase in those of active metabolites may have partially contributed to the second cardiac arrest. Although the active metabolite levels were not markedly raised, the critically ill patient with severe complications, such as ACS, may have become more susceptible to xanthine cardiotoxicity.

Furthermore, while the blood caffeine level decreased rapidly with HD, the decrease was gradual after the HD was discontinued, and the toxic level (> 25 µg/mL) persisted. In this case, the patient developed ACS. It has been reported that in conditions that reduce intestinal peristalsis, delayed drug absorption can occur [12]. Taking this into consideration, it is possible that the caffeine remained in the gastrointestinal tract, leading to delayed absorption. In similar cases, where an increase in metabolites is a concern and delayed caffeine absorption is suspected, recurrence of intoxication symptoms after initial improvement may occur. Taking measures such as repeating HD or preparing for sudden deterioration is necessary. Postmortem redistribution of caffeine has been reported to be minimal [13]. The elimination half-life of caffeine shows interindividual variability due to genetic factors and may be prolonged in the presence of hepatic dysfunction. In cases of massive ingestion, as in the present case, or when complicated by ACS, delayed metabolism may occur. In addition, delayed increases in caffeine metabolites can be observed after hemodialysis, and measurement of these metabolites may be necessary when delayed death occurs following hemodialysis. Although measurement of caffeine metabolites is not currently mandatory in cases of acute caffeine poisoning or caffeine-related death, evaluation combining serum caffeine concentrations with metabolite concentrations may be required depending on the clinical context.

In this case, the patient did not regain consciousness, and therefore, the reason for the massive ingestion of Estaron Mocha® could not be identified. In Japan, cases involving intentional self-harm or suicide attempts using caffeine-containing products have been increasing, and information on such use is readily accessible through internet searches. In our previous study [14], we reported that more than 80% of patients with severe caffeine poisoning had ingested caffeine for self-harm or suicidal purposes. Furthermore, prior reports of fatal caffeine intoxication have indicated that blood caffeine concentrations tend to be higher in cases involving intentional self-harm or suicide compared with other modes of exposure [5]. In this case, highly caffeinated tablets were found in the patient’s private room, and the markedly elevated serum caffeine concentration suggests ingestion of a large amount over a short period. Taken together, these findings suggest that the overdose likely occurred in the context of intentional self-harm or a suicide attempt. Given the ease of access to highly caffeinated products, the number of patients engaging in self-harm or suicide attempts using caffeine may increase in the future. From a forensic perspective, measurement and careful interpretation of blood concentrations of caffeine and its metabolites represent important elements in the evaluation of such cases.

Conclusion

We measured the serum caffeine and active caffeine metabolite levels in a case of severe caffeine poisoning, and delayed active metabolite elevation may have partially contributed to the recurrence of toxic symptoms. Notably, recurrence of toxic symptoms may occur due to delayed active metabolite elevation, particularly in critically ill patients with severe complications.
