Position Statement by the Saudi Heart Failure Working Group of the Saudi Heart Association on the Management of Advanced Heart Failure in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Ibrahim Jelaidan, Abeer Bakhsh, Ammar G. Chaudhary, Ebtisam Alkhattaby, Fahmi AlKaf, Feras Khaliel, Ismail Raslan, Mohammed Alreshidan, Mosaad Alhussein, Waleed Alhabeeb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure (HF) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in Saudi Arabia. Despite remarkable progress in guideline-directed medical therapy, a significant proportion of patients progress to advanced HF and require evaluation for heart transplantation or mechanical circulatory support (MCS). In response to this growing burden, the Saudi Heart Association (SHA) developed recommendations to guide identification, timely referral, and optimal management of candidates for advanced HF therapies in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA multidisciplinary panel of 10 HF experts from Saudi Arabia convened for a series of structured virtual meetings to review local and international evidence. The panel integrated Saudi-specific clinical practice patterns and healthcare considerations into the deliberation process. Consensus was achieved through discussion and majority agreement. Recommendations were formulated using the SHA methodology, supported by a comprehensive literature review of published evidence.Results and conclusionsThe SHA consensus establishes a structured, comprehensive framework for recognizing advanced HF, optimizing guideline-directed therapy, and determining candidacy for heart transplantation or MCS in tertiary and quaternary centers in Saudi Arabia. The document emphasizes timely prognostic stratification using clinical indicators and standardized tools such as INTERMACS and SCAI classifications. Referral pathways and multidisciplinary coordination are delineated to facilitate efficient evaluation and timely intervention. These recommendations aim to minimize delays in referral, expand access to advanced HF therapies and improve patient outcomes across Saudi Arabia.

1. Introduction

Cardiovascular disease, particularly heart failure (HF), remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1–3]. Precise national data are lacking, but extrapolation of regional estimates of severe/advanced HF to the local population in Saudi Arabia suggests that roughly 32,000–55,000 individuals may be living with advanced disease at any given time [4–6]. Although the prevalence of HF in Saudi Arabia is comparable to global estimates, the burden of mortality and disability associated with HF is disproportionately high in the Kingdom [1–3]. Patients in Saudi Arabia typically present at a younger age and exhibit a higher prevalence of metabolic comorbidities, particularly diabetes mellitus and obesity [7–14]. Further compounding this burden is poor public awareness and understanding of HF in Saudi Arabian populations [15,16]. Consistently, quality of life among Saudi patients with HF is also significantly impaired, reflecting persisting gaps in clinical management, treatment adherence, and access to specialized care [17–21].

In practice, a subset of HF patients inevitably experience disease progression despite optimal guideline-directed therapy. Advanced HF represents a stage of high-risk refractory cardiovascular deterioration, characterized by persistent New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III–IV symptoms, major functional limitation and recurrent hospitalizations despite optimal therapy (Table 1). Contemporary frameworks recognize a broader ambulatory advanced HF population, captured in the recently proposed “C2D” stage, presenting with persistent congestion, recurrent decompensation, declining functional capacity, and intolerance to guideline-directed medical therapy despite clinical stability [22]. Accordingly, management of these patients necessitates timely referral to specialized advanced HF programs for consideration of palliative care, mechanical circulatory support (MCS) and/or heart transplantation.

Importantly, durable LVAD therapy and heart transplantation should be viewed as complementary rather than competing therapeutic strategies within the continuum of advanced HF care. Heart transplantation remains the gold-standard therapy for eligible patients, offering the highest rates of long-term survival beyond 5 years [23,24]. Nevertheless, outcomes with contemporary durable left ventricular assist device (LVAD) have improved substantially, with increasingly favorable survival and distinct rather than uniformly greater adverse event profiles compared with heart transplantation [25–30]. Emerging data also support the concept of “net prolongation” in selected younger patients, whereby LVAD prior to transplantation may increase overall longevity [31].

Saudi registries underscore a high risk of progression to advanced disease in local populations [9,32,33]. However, advanced HF therapies are underutilized and significant disparities persist in access to specialized care such as LVADs and heart transplantation outside major centers in Saudi Arabia. Limited donor availability due to low organ donation rates, and poor public awareness further constrain heart transplantation [34–36], increasing reliance on LVAD therapy. Moreover, timely identification and referral of patients progressing to advanced HF remain suboptimal, despite growing recognition of the severity and trajectory of the disease.

In response to this substantial burden and the critical need for earlier and more coordinated intervention, the Saudi Heart Association (SHA) convened an expert panel to develop contextualized consensus recommendations aimed at optimizing advanced HF management. By addressing barriers such as delayed referral, limited donor availability, and reliance on LVAD programs, this consensus seeks to improve care pathways and outcomes for patients with advanced HF across the Kingdom.

2. Methods

2.1. Expert panel and consensus methodology

A multidisciplinary panel of ten experts representing key specialties involved in advanced HF management was convened under the auspices of the SHA. The panel formed the Saudi Heart Failure Working Group, which developed consensus recommendations as part of an official SHA Position Statement on the management of advanced HF. The panel included specialists in advanced heart failure and adult transplant care, cardiac surgery, and cardiac imaging and nursing. The panel reviewed current evidence and Saudi-specific clinical practices and recommendations across two structured virtual meetings. Discussions were guided by the SHA’s established methodology for consensus development.

Recommendations were formulated through collective appraisal of evidence and expert opinion, with consideration of local healthcare system characteristics, infrastructure, and patient profiles. Differences in opinion were addressed through open discussion, and consensus was established by majority agreement. Panel members held equal voting rights in formulating recommendations, which were not influenced by the SHA or funding bodies.

Draft statements were subsequently reviewed and approved by all panel members to ensure accuracy, scientific rigor, and relevance to the Saudi context. The final manuscript was also reviewed and approved by all authors and formally endorsed by the SHA prior to submission.

2.2. Literature review

A comprehensive literature review was performed using PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Scopus without restriction on publication date or language. Search terms included but were not limited to combinations of “advanced heart failure”, “heart transplantation”, “left ventricular assist device”, “mechanical circulatory support”, “INTERMACS”, “SCAI shock”, “prognosis”, “medical therapy”, “Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator”, “Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy”, “mitral repair”, “palliative care”, “multidisciplinary” and “Saudi Arabia.”

Relevant international and regional guidelines, position statements, registries, and major clinical trials were included. Additional references were identified from bibliographies and expert contributions. Priority were given to high-quality evidence, including systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and randomized controlled trials, supplemented by real-world data and local experience.

All findings were discussed within the expert panel to inform the development of practical, locally applicable consensus recommendations aligned with international standards.

3. Therapeutic options in advanced HF: heart transplantation, MCS, and supportive care

3.1. Heart transplantation

Heart transplantation remains the gold standard therapy for advanced HF, improving survival from less than two years without advanced therapy to over a decade, while also restoring quality of life [37–39]. The most recent registry data report post-transplant survival in adult patients of 92.1% at 1 year and 80.3% at 5 years [40,41]. Median survival is approximately 11 years internationally, and among patients transplanted in recent eras who survive the first year, median survival may extend to at least 18 years [40,42]. In Saudi Arabia, heart transplantation outcomes are comparable to international standards despite its high-risk patient population [43,44].

Challenges to transplantation include patient selection, contraindications (Table 2), lifelong immunosuppression, and limited donor availability. Although advanced age is a relative contraindication, carefully selected older patients can benefit [45–47]. In Saudi Arabia, the younger onset of HF and restricted donor pool have resulted in a lower transplant age threshold (approximately 60 years) than commonly seen in Western populations [43,48]. Nevertheless, this varies by center, and older yet well-selected patients may be considered for heart transplantation when eligible.

Despite these challenges, it is strongly recommended that eligible patients (Table 2) be listed for heart transplantation whenever feasible. Proactive measures should be taken to minimize waiting-list morbidity and mortality. These include temporary or durable MCS which can stabilize hemodynamics and improve quality of life, with the potential for recovery of cardiac function.

3.2. MCS

MCS encompasses mechanical ventricular assist devices that can be used to either temporarily or permanently augment or replace native cardiac output, thereby restoring systemic perfusion.

3.2.1. Temporary MCS

Short-term or temporary MCS devices are pumps that provide ventricular support, primarily in the context of cardiogenic shock, as an immediate rescue intervention to restore end-organ and systemic perfusion. Ideally, short-term MCS can serve as bridge to recovery (BTR) of sufficient function to allow weaning of MCS. However, some patients may continue to deteriorate, warranting discontinuation of MCS and heading towards a palliative direction.

In patients with INTERMACS profiles 1–3, short-term MCS can also support sufficient recovery of brain, neurological, end-organ and cardiac function to allow candidacy for advanced HF therapies (bridge to decision [BTD]) [49,50]. Bridging to heart transplantation via temporary MCS can either target transplantation directly (BTC/BTT), or indirectly through durable LVAD (bridge to bridge [BTB]).

Temporary MCS is also increasingly used before durable LVAD implantation, particularly in patients presenting with severe cardiogenic shock or progressive end-organ dysfunction who are initially unsuitable for immediate durable device implantation [51–53]. In this context, temporary MCS allows hemodynamic stabilization, optimization of right ventricular, renal, hepatic, and neurological function, and improved patient selection prior to definitive LVAD therapy [51,52,54]. This strategy has become increasingly relevant in the Saudi setting, where durable LVAD implantation is expanding beyond transplant centers into tertiary advanced HF programs without on-site heart transplantation capability. Accordingly, temporary MCS may facilitate safe referral and stabilization pathways toward durable LVAD implantation as destination therapy (DT) or as part of a bridge-to-bridge strategy.

Major temporary MCS complications profile include bleeding, vascular and access complications, hemolysis, cerebrovascular events, limb ischemia, sepsis, and left ventricular distension [55].

3.2.2. Durable LVADs

Durable LVADs are long-term MCS systems used to provide sustained hemodynamic support in patients with advanced HF, either as a bridge to transplantation or as destination therapy in those ineligible for heart transplantation. Early first-generation pneumatic and pulsatile devices provided ventricular support for thousands of patients awaiting heart transplantation but were associated with suboptimal durability and outcomes [56]. Consequently, second-generation devices adopted a continuous axial flow model which led to improved survival rates and device performance [26,57,58]. However, axial flow pumps remained limited by thrombotic and mechanical complications, leading to the development of third-generation centrifugal magnetically levitated pumps.

Within this third-generation class, the HeartWare ventricular assist device (HVAD) introduced a hybrid magnetic and hydrodynamic suspension model which led to noninferior outcomes compared to second-generation devices [25]. However, it was withdrawn from the market in 2021 due to increased neurological events and pump malfunctions identified in post-marketing surveillance, and is no longer in clinical use. Similarly, the HeartMate II axial-flow pump is no longer implanted; both devices are included in this section for historical context, with contemporary outcome estimates in this section primarily reflecting current-generation magnetically-levitated systems.

The most widely used contemporary device is the fully magnetically levitated HeartMate 3 LVAD, which represents the current standard of durable mechanical circulatory support. Its design incorporates full magnetic levitation, wide blood-flow pathways, and intrinsic pulsatility modulation (Fig. 1), features that collectively improve hemocompatibility and reduce shear stress. These engineering advances have translated into superior clinical performance, including reduced pump thrombosis and fewer reoperations for device malfunction [59].

Data from randomized trials and the INTERMACS registry demonstrate that modern continuous- flow LVADs significantly improve survival and quality of life in patients with advanced HF [25–28,60]. Among these devices, magnetically levitated pumps, most prominently the HeartMate 3, offer outcomes that approach, although do not yet fully match, those of heart transplantation [60,61]. Notably, 5-year survival with the HeartMate 3 is 64% over 2013–2022 in the STS INTERMACS registry, approaching the 69% 5-year survival observed with heart transplantation and substantially exceeding the 44% reported for non-magnetically levitated devices [60,61]. Furthermore, registry data indicate that younger LVAD recipients experience lower mortality and fewer adverse events, supporting the consideration of earlier device implantation when clinically appropriate [24].

Overall, durable LVAD reduces mortality and improves functional status [30,62], and can be applied across multiple indications (Table 3) depending on INTERMACS profile (Fig. 2). However, complications such as driveline infection, thromboembolic events, bleeding, right ventricular failure, aortic regurgitation, and device malfunction remain key limitations, requiring structured follow-up, anticoagulation management, and patient education programs (Table 4).

Historically viewed solely as a bridge to transplantation, durable LVADs are now also established as potential destination therapy (DT) in eligible patients, given improving long-term outcomes (approaching 60% 5-year survival) [61,63]. Among ambulatory patients, those in INTERMACS Profile 4 have the highest burden of advanced HF and are more likely to benefit from LVAD [64–66]. LVAD use as BTT or DT should be prioritized for patients with INTERMACS 2–4 profiles who are not eligible to or listed for heart transplantation, while those with stable advanced HF (profiles 5–7) may be managed conservatively unless clinical deterioration ensues [64–66]. Notably, myocardial recovery is achievable with LVADs in select patients [67–69], allowing device explantation and delisting from transplantation. In these cases, close monitoring by HF specialists is recommended for early detection of deterioration and for maintenance of stable cardiac function through optimized, maximally tolerated therapy.

Contemporary data revealed that young patients derive comparable survival from durable LVAD and heart transplantation [29], providing support for the consideration of an LVAD-first strategy in appropriately selected patients. In this same population, durable LVAD therapy may also be considered as a bridge to transplantation strategy to maximize total longevity by combining the durable mechanical-support era with subsequent transplant survival, a concept recently described as ‘net prolongation’ of life [31]. While this hypothesis remains under investigation and requires individualized patient selection, it underscores the evolving role of contemporary LVAD therapy as a complement to, rather than a substitute for, heart transplantation.

3.3. Supportive care

3.3.1. Inotropic support

Patients awaiting transplantation often experience limited tolerance to medical therapy and diuretic resistance due to progressive right ventricular failure, hypotension, or secondary organ dysfunction which often require treatment adjustment [70,71]. In such cases, continuous intravenous inotropic support may be required to maintain perfusion and preserve end-organ function [72–74]. Although chronic inotrope therapy does not improve survival [75] and may even increase mortality in some patient subsets [76], it remains valuable as a BTD or BTC (LVAD or heart transplantation) [77–80]. Long-term routine inotrope use is not recommended due to risks of arrhythmia, infection, and dependence [70,71].

3.3.2. Palliative and end-of-life care

Patients who are not candidates for advanced therapies and those who are awaiting transplantation should be referred to palliative care [81]. Evidence demonstrates that end-of-life care for HF, whether home-based or provided by specialized teams, reduces hospitalizations and improves quality of life, patient satisfaction and other patient-centered outcomes [82–84]. For example, the PAL-HF trial demonstrated that an interdisciplinary palliative-care intervention added to evidence-based HF care produced significant improvements in quality of life, anxiety, depression, and spiritual well-being compared with usual care alone [85]. Despite these benefits, palliative care remains underutilized in advanced HF [86,87]. Early integration and proactive advocacy are therefore essential to optimize outcomes and align care with patient values. A multidisciplinary team approach that emphasizes shared decision-making, symptom management, psychosocial support, and respect for cultural and religious beliefs is vital to maintaining quality of life during the end-of-life period. Clinicians should also be encouraged to initiate timely discussions on advanced care planning, including preferences for place of death and decisions regarding device deactivation, within a compassionate, patient-centered framework.

4. Identification, referral and management of advanced HF in Saudi Arabia: recommendations of the Saudi heart association

4.1. An overview of advanced HF care in Saudi Arabia

Saudi Arabia’s healthcare system is primarily led by the Ministry of Health, which serves as the principal public provider and payer. This is complemented by specialized governmental institutions, including military, security, and university hospitals, and the expanding private sector [88]. In alignment with Vision 2030, the Saudi Ministry of Health has introduced a cluster-based model that integrates primary healthcare centers, general hospitals, and tertiary/specialized facilities within defined geographic regions.

Advanced heart failure care in Saudi Arabia is organized across four levels of clinical capability. Primary and secondary centers (e.g. community clinics, district hospitals, and general hospitals) deliver early identification, comprehensive evaluation, optimization of guideline-directed medical therapy, and timely referral. In practice, tertiary and quaternary capabilities overlap substantially in the Kingdom; Tertiary centers, typically regional cardiac referral hospitals, provide most components of advanced HF management, including dedicated advanced-HF clinics, specialized imaging and invasive hemodynamic assessment, temporary mechanical circulatory support, and durable LVAD therapy with variable institutional capacity. Quaternary centers, currently represented by the three heart-transplant programs in Riyadh, additionally provide heart transplantation alongside the full spectrum of advanced HF therapies.

Overall, tertiary and quaternary cardiac services are concentrated in high-capacity referral hospitals that serve as regional hubs. These centers provide the multidisciplinary expertise and infrastructure required for optimal advanced HF care, encompassing cardiac surgery, heart transplantation coordination, perioperative management, and long-term LVAD follow-up. This hub and spoke approach is increasingly used in Saudi Arabia and has also garnered support in international cardiology guidelines [89,90] due to its potential to streamline early patient transfer and timely interventions in well-resourced centers, culminating in improved outcomes and reduced costs [91–96].

Currently, three heart transplant programs operate in the Kingdom, all located in Riyadh, reflecting a centralized service model. Since the first heart transplant in 1986, over 600 procedures have been performed in Saudi Arabia, according to the Saudi Center for Organ Transplantation. The Saudi Center for Organ Transplantation serves as the Kingdom’s central authority for coordinating, regulating, and developing organ donation and transplantation services. Operating under national health governance, it standardizes transplant practices, promotes equitable access, and aims to improve outcomes for patients with end-stage organ failure. The center conducts regular evaluations of transplant facilities to ensure compliance and quality, organizes professional training and educational initiatives, and leads public awareness campaigns on organ failure, donation, and transplantation. Saudi Center for Organ Transplantation also maintains the national registry of patients, donors, and transplant recipients, facilitating donor identification, inter-center coordination, and longitudinal follow-up. In the context of heart transplantation, a strict heart allocation policy is in place to ensure equitable and efficient allocation of available organs and ensure optimal outcomes [97]. Allocation decisions are based primarily on medical urgency, donor–recipient compatibility, waiting time, and organ utilization, with additional consideration for exceptional cases evaluated by a heart transplantation sub-committee. Candidates are prioritized into four urgency tiers, with the highest priority assigned to patients at imminent risk of dying due to cardiogenic shock, those on temporary or durable mechanical circulatory support with life-threatening complications, or patients receiving maximal mechanical circulatory support. Wait-list duration varies in Saudi Arabia, but data from a high-volume heart transplant center indicate an average wait of 98 days [43].

However, heart transplantation in Saudi Arabia remains constrained by limited donor availability, driven by low organ donation rates and suboptimal public awareness [34–36]. A community-based survey conducted between 2015 and 2016 reported that fewer than half of respondents (43.6%) were willing to consent to heart donation, while nearly one-third (31.8%) expressed concern that agreement to organ donation might adversely affect the prioritization of life-saving medical care [34]. More recent surveys corroborate this persistent hesitancy, demonstrating that although general attitudes toward organ donation are broadly positive, acceptance and knowledge remain moderate and are strongly influenced by educational attainment and religious considerations [35,36]. Collectively, these barriers substantially limit heart transplantation activity and increase reliance on LVAD therapy as an alternative advanced HF strategy in the Kingdom.

Durable LVADs are more widely accessible in Saudi Arabia and less centralized to the capital city, being offered by several tertiary care centers in Riyadh and other regions such as Dammam and Makkah. Current national experience remains substantially lower than global counterparts, with an estimated cumulative total of ~330 LVAD implantations performed since 2016. By comparison, contemporary INTERMACS data indicate an average of approximately 2800 LVAD implantations annually over three years (2022–2024) [98] across a population of ~340 million, corresponding to ~0.8 LVAD implants per 100,000 population per year. If a similar level of advanced HF care utilization were observed in Saudi Arabia (~35 million population), this would translate to an expected volume of approximately 280–300 LVAD implantations annually. This suggests that many patients with advanced HF are either managed conservatively with poor prognosis or are not identified and referred in a timely manner for advanced therapies.

4.2. Considerations before referral for advanced HF care

4.2.1. Optimization of guideline-directed medical therapy

Optimization of therapy prior to referral is a cornerstone of advanced HF management and includes medical, device, and interventional approaches consistent with the SHA guidelines on the management of HF guidelines [70,71]. Optimization can also encompass novel modalities aimed at reducing hospitalization and improving quality of life in symptomatic patients at high risk of deterioration, such as remote pulmonary artery pressure monitoring using implantable sensors (e.g., CardioMEMS) [99,100].

Patients should receive maximally tolerated, evidence- based pharmacotherapy prior to consideration of LVAD or heart transplantation and should continue to receive such therapy as tolerated while listed for advanced interventions and following LVAD implantation [101–103]. Nevertheless, intolerance to guideline-directed medical therapy, reflected by the inability to initiate or up-titrate recommended agents, is common in patients with advanced HF and is recognized as a defining clinical feature and adverse prognostic marker of advanced disease [104]. Moreover, most landmark randomized GDMT trials excluded patients with advanced HF or included very few patients with severe symptoms, limiting the generalizability of these results to this population. Notably, the LIFE trial, the only dedicated randomized trial in patients with advanced HFrEF and recent NYHA class IV symptoms, demonstrated that sacubitril/valsartan was not superior to valsartan in reducing NTproBNP levels or improving clinical outcomes, while showing higher rates of hyperkalemia and limited tolerability in a substantial proportion of patients [105]. These findings highlight the challenges of extrapolating GDMT benefits observed in ambulatory HFrEF populations to patients with advanced disease. Nevertheless, the 2019 SHA guidelines on the management of HF and its 2023 focused update provide detailed guidance on recommended medical therapy across the disease spectrum [70,71].

4.2.2. Optimization of device and interventional therapy

Optimization of device therapy, which includes cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs), is important to prevent progression to advanced diseases in eligible patients but should not delay referral to advanced HF centers. CRT improves symptoms and survival compared with optimal medical therapy alone [106]. In patients with moderate or severe (NYHA Class III–IV) disease, CRT reduces HF hospitalization irrespective of concomitant ICD use [107,108]. Therefore, CRT is recommended in eligible HF patients according to the SHA guideline indications [70,71].

ICDs are frequently present in candidates for advanced HF therapies, but new-onset or progressive arrhythmias may warrant implantation prior to LVAD or transplantation. However, listing for transplant/LVAD alone is not an indication for ICD implantation. According to SHA HF guidelines [70,71], all eligible patients should receive ICDs when indicated. ICD therapy is generally not recommended in patients with NYHA IV symptoms refractory to pharmacologic therapy, except in those who are candidates for CRT, LVAD, or transplantation.

Select patients with advanced HF, particularly ambulatory heart transplantation candidates, may still benefit from ICDs given the demonstrated reduction in sudden cardiac death, even in the presence of an LVAD [109]. This approach can enhance survival during bridging to transplantation, particularly in patients already on MCS (continuous flow LVAD) [110,111]. Therefore, ICDs may be considered to reduce the risk of mortality and sudden cardiac death in ambulatory patients listed for heart transplantation when prolonged waiting times (>3 months) are anticipated. Conversely, inotrope-dependent or hospitalized patients awaiting imminent LVAD or transplant, as well as patients ineligible for advanced therapies, should not undergo ICD implantation due to procedural risks and limited benefit. Hence, individualized risk–benefit assessment remains essential in ICD decision-making among patients referred for advanced HF therapy.

Valvular, coronary, or structural abnormalities frequently coexist in advanced HF. Appropriate interventions, such as coronary revascularization or valve repair/replacement, should be undertaken in line with guideline recommendations after optimization of medical and device therapy [70,71].

4.2.3. Prognostication and referral for advanced HF

Despite the high clinical and economic burden of HF, referral to advanced HF therapies remains suboptimal. Limited patient awareness of available treatment options can contribute, but delays are more often related to provider-level factors, particularly misconceptions regarding advanced HF therapies and uncertainty about the optimal timing of referral to specialized centers [112].

The early identification of patients likely to benefit from advanced therapies, coupled with timely referral to specialized HF programs, is critical to improving outcomes. Recognition of disease progression relies on a comprehensive assessment integrating clinical, functional, laboratory, and imaging parameters, many of which are established predictors of poor prognosis and should prompt referral to advanced HF care [113–115].

Prognostic stratification frameworks provide a structured means of guiding triage, prioritizing referrals, and informing treatment planning (Fig. 2). Decision-support tools such as the I-NEED-HELP mnemonic [116] have demonstrated real-world value in facilitating the recognition of patients with major clinical features of advanced HF who may require escalation of care [117–119].

The Interagency Registry for Mechanically Assisted Circulatory Support (INTERMACS) profiles (Table 5) were initially developed for the classification of patients with a potential indication for durable MCS devices [120]. They are now widely used to stratify risk and predict outcomes across the spectrum of advanced HF therapies, including urgent transplantation, LVAD implantation, and management of ambulatory patients [64,121,122]. Similarly, the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) shock stages offer a complementary classification system for defining the severity of cardiogenic shock (Table 6). Validation studies have confirmed their prognostic utility across multiple clinical contexts, underscoring the need for early referral of patients in higher-risk stages (C–E) to centers equipped to deliver advanced HF interventions [123].

4.3. Recommended framework for effective management of advanced HF in Saudi Arabia

Effective management of advanced HF requires a coordinated multidisciplinary approach that bridges local HF clinics with specialized tertiary/quaternary centers. Multidisciplinary HF care reduces hospitalizations and mortality while also improving functional recovery, a benefit that extends to heart transplantation and post-cardiac surgery care [124–129]. Timely referral to advanced HF has proven to be crucial to achieve optimal patient outcomes [116,130,131]. To achieve this, clear referral pathways are needed to ensure that patients with refractory HF are identified early and promptly escalated to centers capable of delivering advanced therapies such as LVAD implantation or heart transplantation.

A stepwise management framework for identifying patients with advanced disease, optimizing evidence-based treatments, and determining candidacy for advanced HF therapies is summarized in Fig. 2. The left panel outlines the primary/secondary HF care pathway, emphasizing comprehensive clinical evaluation which includes symptoms, functional status, laboratory testing, imaging, and risk stratification, to identify patients across the HF spectrum. While patients with mild HF generally continue standard follow-up and therapy optimization, those with moderate to severe HF (NYHA class III–IV) or deteriorating clinical status require closer surveillance for features suggestive of disease progression.

Once advanced HF is confirmed (as defined in Table 1 and detailed in SHA HF guidelines [70,71]), clinicians in primary or secondary facilities should carefully assess for reversible causes (e.g. arrhythmia, ischemia, thyroid dysfunction) and ensure that all guideline-directed medical, device, and interventional therapies have been maximally utilized prior to referral. Confirmation of irreversible advanced HF should be followed by prognostic stratification (see section 4.2.3), referral and appropriate treatment.

A central element of the primary/secondary care pathway is the systematic assessment for “warning signs” of advanced HF, including recurrent hospitalizations, persistent congestion, end-organ dysfunction, low systolic blood pressure, and intolerance to guideline-directed medical therapy. The presence of one or more of these features should prompt timely referral to an advanced HF center, rather than continued escalation of conventional therapy in isolation. Patients without warning signs may continue optimized medical therapy with routine monitoring for clinical deterioration. Patients with classic, deteriorating, or refractory cardiogenic shock corresponding to SCAI stages C and D, and selected patients in stage E also require prompt evaluation by a specialized multidisciplinary team and consideration of short-term MCS as a bridge to decision, recovery, transplantation, or durable support. In contrast, palliative care is emphasized for individuals with limited life expectancy or ineligibility for advanced interventions due to severely impaired quality of life.

The right panel depicts quaternary HF care, structured around INTERMACS profiles to guide urgency and therapeutic strategy. Patients with severe hemodynamic compromise (INTERMACS 1) are considered for short-term MCS as a bridge to decision, recovery, transplantation, or durable support. Subsequent reassessment determines reversibility of cardiac dysfunction and candidacy for heart transplantation. In patients with irreversible disease who are ineligible for HT, durable LVAD implantation as destination therapy or bridge to transplant is considered, contingent on the absence or reversibility of contraindications. Patients with progressive circulatory and clinical deterioration (INTERMACS 2–4) can be considered for either heart transplantation or durable LVAD as appropriate. Ambulatory patients with less critical profiles (INTERMACS 5–7) but with objective markers of poor prognosis should undergo structured evaluation by a specialized team for eligibility to advanced HF therapies (heart transplantation and LVAD). LVAD therapy and expectant management can both be considered for those with contraindications to transplantation or progressive clinical deterioration, while close follow-up is sufficient for low-risk patients.

Overall, the expert consensus underscores the importance of early recognition of advanced HF, proactive referral to specialized centers, and dynamic reassessment of therapeutic goals. By integrating ambulatory warning signs with inpatient severity stratification, this approach aims to avoid delays in advanced therapy referral, optimize patient selection for LVAD or transplantation, and ensure appropriate incorporation of palliative care when advanced interventions are not suitable.

5. Future directions

Moving forward, national efforts should focus on strengthening oversight, transparency, and system-wide coordination of advanced HF care in Saudi Arabia. The SHA recommends the development of a centralized national registry to capture key performance metrics (Table 7) and enable benchmarking across institutions for advanced HF therapies. Suggested indicators include median time from identification of advanced HF to referral for LVAD or heart transplantation, annual volume of advanced HF referrals from primary and secondary centers, wait-list duration and mortality, and post-intervention outcomes such as 1-, 2-, 3- and 5-year survival following heart transplantation or LVAD implantation. Routine reporting of national transplant and LVAD volumes would further enhance accountability and quality improvement as this information is not currently readily available from the Kingdom.

Additional priorities include strengthening organ donation efforts through public awareness initiatives, enhanced donor registration, and exploration of ethically appropriate strategies to expand the donor pool, including donation after circulatory death in accordance with the policies and regulations of the Saudi Center for Organ Transplantation [132]. Standardization and regulation of transfer pathways between primary, secondary, and tertiary centers based on geography, service availability, and bed capacity are needed to ensure equitable and timely access to advanced HF therapies. Clear governance structures should also address care pathways for patients receiving LVAD therapy as a bridge to transplantation at VAD-only centers, including formal agreements with transplant-capable centers to ensure BTT intent at device implantation and reduce inequities in access to heart transplantation [133,134]. Sustained investment in workforce development and specialized training is also essential to support the continued expansion of advanced HF services in Saudi Arabia. Strengthening local training pathways and multidisciplinary expertise falls within the framework of Saudi Vision 2030 and will be critical to ensuring sustainable, high-quality advanced HF care across the Kingdom.

Given the limited number of heart donors, optimization of the Saudi Center for Organ Transplantation’s national wait-list and prioritization system is essential to improve coordination between transplant centers and ensure equitable resource allocation. Awareness campaigns are also needed on a national level to address stigmas and misconceptions and encourage donor registration. Finally, decentralization of advanced HF care is crucial to address the geographic disparity of transplant-capable centers which are currently concentrated in the Kingdom’s capital city.

Coupling structured clinical pathways with robust national metrics and coordinated oversight will permit standardization of care while also catalyzing continuous system-level improvement. Implementation of these recommendations has the potential to reduce delays in referral, improve equity of access, and ultimately enhance survival and quality of life for patients with advanced HF across Saudi Arabia.

6. Conclusion

Advanced HF represents a critical stage of disease progression associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. In Saudi Arabia, early recognition and timely referral remain major challenges, compounded by limited donor availability, uneven distribution of advanced HF centers, and variable awareness among healthcare providers.

This SHA position statement provides a structured framework to optimize advanced HF management across the Kingdom. It emphasizes early prognostic stratification using standardized tools such as the INTERMACS and SCAI classifications, timely identification of patients requiring escalation of care, and clear referral pathways linking primary and secondary facilities with advanced HF centers. Key pillars of this approach are the optimization of guideline-directed medical and device therapy prior to referral, and systematic assessment of candidacy for heart transplantation or durable MCS.

Multidisciplinary collaboration is central to these recommendations, ensuring coordinated decision-making among cardiologists, surgeons, HF nurses, and other specialists. Efforts are also needed on a national level to strengthen oversight, continuous quality improvement and system-wide coordination. Implementation of these recommendations can facilitate earlier intervention, improve consistency of care, and enhance patient outcomes and quality of life. By promoting structured pathways and multidisciplinary coordination, the SHA aims to bridge existing gaps and advance the standard of care for patients with advanced HF throughout the Kingdom.
