Discovery and Biological Evaluation of 3‑(1,2,3-Thiadiazol-5-yl)-1-arylimidazolidine-2,4-diones as Potential Plant Growth Regulators
Authors: Tatiana A. Kalinina, Varvara S. Evchik, Konstantin L. Obydennov, Pavel A. Slepukhin, Osman N. Kanwugu, Olga E. Cherepanova, Tatiana V. Glukhareva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A simple method for the synthesis of 1,2,3-thiadiazol-5-ylimidazolidine-2,4-dionesstructural analogues of the plant growth regulator thidiazuronhas been developed. The effects of 8 new compounds, as well as thidiazuron (TDZ), 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BAP), and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), on cucumber seed germination and seedling morphology have been studied. Two 1,2,3-thiadiazol-5-ylimidazolidine-2,4-diones, A1 and B1, were discovered to exhibit growth-regulating effects similar to those of TDZ and considered promising candidates for further investigation. At a concentration of 5 mg/L, these compounds significantly inhibited the growth of lateral roots and retarded the elongation of the primary root, without negatively affecting the vegetative parts of the seedlings. Evaluation of the fungicidal activity of the 1,2,3-thiadiazol-5-ylimidazolidine-2,4-diones at a concentration of 50 μg/mL against 8 strains of phytopathogenic fungi revealed 4 compounds with moderate activity (I > 50%) against Alternaria solani and 2 compounds against Plenodomus lingam.

Introduction

It is difficult to imagine modern agriculture without the use of plant growth regulators (PGRs). Chemical regulation of ontogenesis allows for a more complete realization of plant potential, facilitates the regulation of flowering and ripening periods, and improves both the quality and quantity of yield while also enhancing resistance to various types of stress. , An important advantage of PGRs is their high biological activity and their efficacy when applied in low concentrations. This is important in terms of saving resources and decreasing adverse environmental impacts. In addition, PGRs reduce the need for pesticides and improve their efficacy, while also mitigating the toxic effect of pesticides on plants. , The rising incidence of agricultural diseases is one of the driving forces behind the growth of the PGR market, as these diseases significantly impair plant health, productivity, and growth. PGRs promote disease resistance, stress tolerance, balanced growth, and accelerated healing of plant lesions caused by pathogens. −

PGRs are also indispensable tools in in vitro plant cultivation techniques and are now an integral part of modern agricultural development strategies. They play a vital role in crop improvement through selective breeding, genetic modification, micropropagation, and selection of mutants with beneficial characteristics.

With increasing global demand for food and producers focusing on sustainable farming practices, the need for PGRs is expected to continue rising. The PGR market is projected to grow from USD 3.3 billion in 2024 to USD 4.6 billion by 2029, representing an average annual growth rate of approximately 7.2% in value terms during the forecast period. In this regard, there is a pressing need to develop and implement novel, original molecules, as well as functional derivatives of known synthetic PGRs and phytohormones, to expand the arsenal of tools available for plant growth regulation and crop protection. ,

Cytokinins, along with auxins, gibberellins, abscisic acid, and ethylene, are considered classic plant hormones. Among these, cytokinins are particularly important plant hormones due to their role in regulating key plant processes, including cell division and the development of shoots, roots, and reproductive organs, as well as seed filling and senescence. Although natural cytokinins are very effective, they are also very expensive. Consequently, synthetic cytokinins and derivatives of natural cytokinin are frequently used for the same purposes, with some synthetic analogues even outperforming natural cytokinins. In this regard, thidiazuron (TDZ) is one of the most potent, effective, and widely used synthetic plant growth regulators, with pronounced cytokinin-like activity. TDZ is often classified as a cytokinin, but in addition to cytokine effects, it also exhibits auxin-like effects in plants. , This leads to a wide range of applications for this PGR in both in vitro and in vivo systems. It is known that TDZ is used to prevent yellowing of leaves, regulate photosynthetic activity of plants, interrupt bud dormancy, promote fruit ripening, and stimulate proliferation of adventitious shoots. However, TDZ has particularly attracted considerable attention due to its effective role in plant cell and tissue culture. TDZ is a highly efficient bioregulator of plant morphogenesis in the tissue culture technique. Its use induces diverse responses ranging from induction of callus to embryogenesis or organogenesis.

In addition, TDZ is used as an elicitor, promoting the production of secondary metabolites, which play an important role in plant adaptation to the environment. , Although TDZ can mimic the effects of auxins and cytokinins, either individually or concurrently, it is structurally distinct from both of these PGR groups, being a phenyl-substituted 1,2,3-thiadiazolyl urea (Figure ).

Structure of 6-BAP, IAA, and TDZ.

Thidiazuron was first synthesized in 1967 by Schering Corporation in Germany, initially used as a cotton defoliant, and eventually registered in the United States in 1982. Today this PRG continues to be actively used in scientific research due to its unique biological effects and the fact that its mechanism of biological action is still not fully understood. ,, There are several hypotheses regarding the mechanism of action of TDZ. It has been shown that TDZ inhibits cytokinin oxidase (CKX), the enzyme responsible for cytokinin degradation. In addition, TDZ is thought to stimulate the expression of specific genes that regulate diverse metabolic pathways, including those involved in the biosynthesis and accumulation of endogenous phytohormones.

An effective approach for the development of new PGRs with increased biological activity, reduced costs, and simplified synthesis is the chemical synthesis of PGR analogues. However, studies devoted to the synthesis and study of the properties of thidiazuron analogues are limited in the literature. ,−

Despite the fact that TDZ is similar in structure to other cytokinin-like PGRs of the urea series, for example, diphenylurea derivatives and 4-pyridylphenylurea, its biological properties differ significantly. ,, Thus, the presence of the 1,2,3-thiadiazole cycle is an important feature of this PGR. Notably, various 1,2,3-thiadiazole derivatives have been shown to activate plant biosynthetic pathways including those associated with stress tolerance and pathogen resistance. Among the most effective inducers of systemic acquired resistance (SAR) in plants are 1,2,3-thiadiazole derivatives such as bion, tiadinil, and methiadinil. , This heterocycle moiety is present in several fungicidal molecules discovered through in vitro and in vivo screenings against phytopathogenic fungi. , Also, 1,2,3-thiadiazole derivatives exhibit a wide spectrum of other biological activities. −

Previously, we synthesized 1,2,3-thiadiazol-5-ylimidazolidine-2,4-dione I by the cyclocondensation of 1,2,3-thiadiazolyl ureas II under reflux in acetic anhydride (Figure ). Within the series of the obtained heterocyclic assemblies I, we identified compounds that stimulated the germination of Pinus sylvestris L. seeds, as well as compounds with moderate fungicidal activity against a number of phytopathogenic fungi. It is known that imidazolidine-2,4-diones have fungicidal properties. , For example, the drug iprodione is used to protect plants from fungal diseases (Figure ).

Design of the target compounds.

In the present study, we developed a simple method for synthesizing a wide range of new analogues of thidiazuron and iprodione: 1,2,3-thiadiazol-5-ylimidazolidine-2,4-diones A and B (Figure ). The effects of these heterocyclic assemblies on cucumber seed germination were studied to identify compounds promising for further study of growth-regulating properties in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, the fungicidal activity of the obtained substances was also studied.

Chemistry

At the first stage, ester of N-arylamino acids was synthesized by alkylation of aniline derivatives with either ethyl bromoacetate for compounds 4 or methyl chloropropionate for compounds 5. N-Arylglycines 6 and N-aryl alanines 7 were obtained by hydrolysis of esters 4 and 5 in the presence of NaOH (Scheme ). The reaction of 1,2,3-thiadiazole-acylazide 8 with N-arylamino acids 6 or 7 leads directly to the formation of imidazolidinediones A and B without the release of intermediate urea 9 (Scheme ). The synthesis of acyl azide 8 was carried out using a previously developed method by diazotization of 1,2,3-thiadiazolyl hydrazides. The 1H NMR, 13C NMR, IR, and EI-MS data of the target compounds are presented in the Supporting Information. The 1H NMR spectra of products A1–19 and B1–15 contain signals of the methyl group protons in position 4 of the 1,2,3-thiadiazole ring in the region 2.61–2.62 ppm. For series A1–19, signals of the methylene group protons of the imidazolidine ring appear as a singlet in the region of 4.60–4.68 ppm. For compounds B1–15, the signals of the methine proton appear in the region 5.08–5.13 ppm as a quartet, while signals of the methyl group in position 5 of the imidazolidine ring appear in the region 1.34–1.44 ppm as a doublet. The signals of the aromatic substituent protons in the downfield region were also characteristic. It should be noted that in the mass spectra of 1,2,3-thiadiazolyl imidazolidine-2,4-diones, in addition to the peak of the molecular ion, prominent fragment peaks corresponding to [M–N2]+ and [M–N2–CO]+ were also observed.

The molecular structures of compounds A1 and B15 were additionally confirmed by X-ray diffraction analysis (Figures S103–S106 and Table S1). Compound A1 crystallizes in orthorhombic space group Pna21, while compound B15 crystallizes in monoclinic space group P21/c. The exocyclic C–N bond lengths are 1.421 Å for A1 and 1.392 Å for B15, indicating that the electron lone pair of the nitrogen atom is practically not involved in conjugation with the thiadiazole ring, as observed in a number of previously published structures with bond lengths of 1.32–1.37 Å. − In both structures, the length of the endocyclic C(2)–N(1) bond is 1.356 Å, and the atoms of the imidazolidinedione fragment are located practically in the same plane with an RMSD of 0.064 Å for A1 and 0.052 Å for B15. The angle between the root-mean-square planes of the thiadiazole ring and imidazolidinedione is 41.04(14) Å for A1 and 50.09(9) Å for B15.

Most of the compounds at 50 μg/mL did not effectively inhibit the mycelia growth of the target fungi. Among the imidazolidine-2,4-dione derivatives, compounds A4, A5, A19, and B4 were found to exhibit moderate fungicidal activity (more than 50%) against A. solani and compounds A2 and B5 against P. lingam. However, the level of activity was not significantly high enough to consider these compounds as promising candidates for further study as potential fungicides.

Structure–activity analysis showed that the presence of a methyl group in the imidazolidine ring had no clear effect on the fungicidal activity. In contrast, substituents on the benzene ring exerted a more pronounced influence on the biological activity. The inhibitory activity is especially different in a series of compounds containing various substituents in the benzene ring against A. solani (from 1.6 to 53.1%) and P. lingam (from 0.8 to 52.1%). It was noted that the compounds containing substituents in the ortho-position of the benzene ring, especially 2,6-disubstituted products, have the highest activity against A. solani. It should also be noted that in the series of compounds B1–15, we studied enantiomeric mixtures of compounds. Consequently, it can be assumed that the activity may be higher for one of the enantiomers than for the racemate.

Plant Growth Regulation Activity

TDZ is typically used at low concentrations to stimulate various processes of plant development at the organ and cellular levels. However, it has also been previously established that at high concentrations TDZ exerts pronounced effects on plant morphology and cellular architecture. ,

We previously showed that TDZ at a concentration of 5 mg/L alters the morphology of pine and tomato seedlings. To identify TDZ analogues with comparable activity in the present work, we assessed the effect of TDZ and 8 obtained 1,2,3-thiadiazol-5-ylimidazolidine-2,4-diones A1, A3, A10, A12, B1, B3, B10, and B12 (Figure ) at this concentration (5 mg/L) on the growth processes of cucumber seeds (Cucumis sativus). , The synthetic cytokinin 6-BAP and auxin IAA were used for comparison and were also included in the assay. Synthetic cytokinin 6-BAP exhibits all typical cytokinin effects, such as stimulation of cell division, regulation of shoot and root development, release of apical dominance and promotion of lateral bud outgrowth, enhancement of seed germination, and delay of organ senescence. The mechanism of action of 6-BAP on plant development mimics that of natural cytokinins: upon binding to membrane-localized histidine kinases receptors (AHK2/3/4), it triggers a signaling cascade through phosphotransfer proteins (AHP) into the nucleus, where it activates transcription factors such as B-ARR. These transcription factors regulate the expression of genes involved in the cell cycle and morphogenesis. Functionally, 6-BAP stimulates cell division in meristems and suppresses apical dominance by antagonizing auxins and indirectly activating IPT genes, thereby inducing shoot branching. In addition, 6-BAP slows down aging by suppressing senescence-associated genes (SAG12) and enhancing antioxidant defenses. IAA is the main natural auxin and plays a key role in the regulation of plant growth and development. It mediates these functions via at least two distinct signaling pathways. The nuclear pathway, involving TIR1/AFB receptors, mediates transcription, whereas the cell surface ABP1-TMK1 auxin perception triggers a global ultrafast phosphorylation response. Physiologically, IAA regulates cell division and differentiation and stimulates cell elongation by activating proton pumps and loosening the cell wall. Additionally, IAA accelerates the formation of xylem and roots, controls vegetative growth processes, enforces apical dominance, suppresses the development of axillary buds, and regulates the processes of tropism (photo- and gravitropism). Furthermore, IAA influences flowering and fruiting of plants, modulates photosynthesis and pigment formation, and contributes to the biosynthesis of various metabolites and plant defenses against biotic stress factors.

When the model was constructed in the course of discriminant analysis, several linear parameters related to the root system were identified as contributors. Notable among them included the number of lateral roots (Wilks’ λ = 0.076), the width or diameter of the primary root (Wilks’ λ = 0.052), and the thickness of the stem (Wilks’ λ = 0.049) (Table S4, Supporting Information). In contrast, the features describing the development of seedling cotyledons were found to be insignificant on the fifth day (Table S4, Supporting Information).

The largest linear dimensions of the primary root on the fifth day were recorded in seedlings treated with the B12 and 6-BAP lines and in the control. In contrast, the shortest roots were observed in the A1 line, with seedlings treated with IAA, TDZ, and B1 also showing similarly reduced lengths. The difference between the minimum and maximum root length exceeded a 2-fold variation by the fifth day. The minimum level of variability of this feature was also recorded in the A1 and TDZ lines (Table S2, Supporting Information), and the maximum in the A10 line, where the length of the primary root by the fifth day varied from 3 to 67 mm.

The largest diameter of the primary root on the fifth day was noted in the IAA, A12, and B1 lines, and the minimum in the control and 6-BAP (Table S2, Supporting Information). Although in the IAA-, TDZ-, and B1-treated groups, the primary root length was approximately half that of the control, this length reduction was accompanied by a compensatory increase in root diameter (Table S2, Supporting Information). Among all root traits, the number of lateral roots showed the highest variability (Table S2, Supporting Information). By the end of the fifth day, the variability of this parameter among each group ranges from 0% (A1, TDZ) to 91.32% (6-BAP). The maximum number of lateral roots was noted in the A3 line with insignificant trait variability (Table S2, Supporting Information). In the B12 line, the mean number of lateral roots was 13.3, with nearly double the variability compared to the A3 line. The control also showed considerable variation (55.98%), with an average of only 7.33 lateral roots. Root branching, measured as the number of lateral roots per 1 cm of primary root, was highest in the IAA and A3 lines (0.689 and 0.429, respectively) and lowest in B1 (0.009). In the control, branching frequency was more than four times lower than in the A3 line. The B12 line also showed a high branching frequency (0.36), close to the maximum observed in this study.

The largest stem widths on the fifth day were observed in the lines that also exhibited the shortest main root lengthsnamely, B1, A1, TDZ, and IAA (Table S2, Supporting Information). Along with the increased stem width, a decrease in trait variability to 12.7% was also observed in the B1 and A1 lines. Only in TDZ-treated seedlings did the variation in stem width increase to 19.89%. In comparison, the control group seedlings did not have the variation in stem width increase to (18.8%).

The calculated Mahalanobis distances showed no statistically significant separation between groups, and when all the groups were classified, they clustered into a single group (Table S5, Supporting Information). Nevertheless, the largest pairwise distances were observed between B1–A3 (36.46), A1–A3 (34.04), TDZ–A3 (33.44), B1–B12 (33.66), and A1–B12 (31.58), which indicated differences in the formation of the morphological structure of the sprouts of the pairs. In contrast, a number of treatment lines showed a morphological similarity. For example, seedlings of groups A1, B1, and TDZ have a short root system with almost completely absent or completely absent lateral roots, as well as a short but wide stem (Table S2, Supporting Information).

Canonical discriminant analysis of the centroids distribution showed that lines A1, B1, and TDZ stood out significantly from the rest (Figure ). On the other hand, lines A10 and B12 were positioned closest to the control. Lines A3 and B12 and A12 and B10 were also closely grouped, whereas line 6-BAP was relatively isolated. To evaluate developmental trends beyond day 5, linear measurements were repeated on day 7 and used to construct another discriminant model. According to the results obtained, all of the main developmental trends observed on the fifth day persisted on day 7. The parameters that remained significant in the model included diameter of the primary root (Wilks’ λ = 0.059), number of lateral roots (Wilks’ λ = 0.065), and stem width (Wilks’ λ = 0.065) (Table S6, Supporting Information). Notably, root length was excluded from the model due to an insufficient discriminative value. By day 7, the linear parameters of the cotyledons were included in the constructed model, though they contributed only modestly to the discriminant function (Table S3, Supporting Information). At this time point, the longest root lengths were observed in the control, B12, and 6-BAP lines, while the shortest were recorded in seedlings treated with A1, B1, and TDZ (Table S3, Supporting Information). The highest number of lateral roots was observed in the control, B12, A3, and IAA lines.

Distribution of centroids in the field of canonical variables on day 5.

When calculating the average number of rootlets per 1 cm of the primary root length, no significant differences were observed between treatments. The highest number of lateral roots per 1 cm of the primary root was noted in the IAA (0.376) and A3 (0.271) lines, while the lowest were observed in the control (0.25) and TDZ (0.25). In the A1, B1, and TDZ lines, lateral branching was not observed by the end of 7 days. The stem thickness remained greatest at the A1, B1, and TDZ lines, where the observed averages (2.78–3.32 mm) were almost twice as high as in the control (1.80 mm). Reduced variability in stem thickness also persisted in lines A1, B1, and TDZ, with an average variation level of 15.5% (Table S3, Supporting Information). The tested compounds did not significantly affect cotyledon size. Thus, the length of the cotyledons varied slightly, from 10.73 mm (TDZ) to 14.17 mm (IAA), and the trait variability ranged from 9.78% (B3) to 19.28% (TDZ). The lowest variability in cotyledon length, noted in B3-treated seedlings, was nearly half that of control (17.62%).

Mahalanobis distance calculations based on data obtained on day 7 revealed greater deviation of the A1, B1, and TDZ lines from control, with distances of 45.59, 50.06, and 44.63, respectively. In contrast, the smallest intergroup distance was recorded between A12 and B3 (0.59) (Table S7, Supporting Information). Between the pairs of other lines, there is also a general tendency for a slight increase in the distances. In the canonical plot (Figure ), the control clustered in the lower left quadrant, while the A1, B1, and TDZ lines formed a distinct cluster on the opposite side, consistent with the shared morphological features of seedlings. Also distinguished in the general group are pairs of lines B3-A10, B10-A12, and B12-A3. The IAA and 6-BAP treatments remained relatively isolated from the other lines.

Distribution of centroids in the field of canonical variables on day 7.

To study the effects of the compounds A1, B1, and TDZ on root cell morphology, a tissue maceration method was used. As shown in Figure , root cells of the control group are arranged in a strict sequence and are elongated in the axial direction. In contrast, roots in the TDZ line appear shorter and wider, with the cells exhibiting a more square-like shape and a noticeable displacement relative to each other. A similar picture is observed in the A1- and B1-treated lines. Although long-term studies have yet to fully elucidate the mechanism of TDZ action in plants, recent studies suggest that one mode of action may involve the regulation of metabolite biosynthesis through the shikimic acid pathway, particularly during the synthesis of endogenous plant growth regulators.

Cell structure of the primary root of seedlings (magnification 15 × 40) treated with aA1, bB1, cTDZ, and dwater (control).

It is known that the linear parameters of the root system and the processes of lateral rootlet formation during ontogenesis are strongly interconnected and have a significant impact on the plant’s ability to absorb water and transport photosynthetic products. For this reason, it is important that the root system develop harmoniously. Studies in Arabidopsis mutant lines have shown that cytokinin shortens the period of cell elongation, decreases the rate of cell division, and at high concentrations negatively affects lateral root development. , It is plausible that the decrease in the linear sizes of seedlings in the TDZ, A1, and B1 lines may be associated with the inhibition of gibberellic acid production by the plant itself, which is reflected in the distinctive phenotype of seedlings characterized by short roots without lateral branching.

In a study aimed at determining the effect of TDZ on the growth of rose shoots, the authors obtained results similar to ours. In that study, the shoot cells were found to be short and wide. Subsequent analysis showed a reduction in the rate of axial growth, which manifested as decreased cell elongation. Radial growth was activated, that is, the growth of root tissue in width, which we also observed in our preparations. However, in contrast to the control samples, which displayed well-organized rows of elongated cells, our TDZ-, A1-, and B1-treated roots lacked this regular cell arrangement. Instead, we observed displacement along the root axis and cell expansion in width (Figure ).

The stability of the linear dimensions of the cotyledons is most likely associated with their role in phytochrome-mediated perception of red and far-red light signals. This may explain why we did not observe significant changes in the structure of the vegetative part of the seedlings. Nevertheless, previous reports have documented an increase in the thickness of the cotyledons when plant seedlings are exposed to high concentrations of TDZ.

Conclusion

Thirty-four novel 3-(1,2,3-thiadiazol-5-yl)-1-arylimidazolidine-2,4-diones derivatives were designed, synthesized, and structurally characterized by IR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and MS-EI. The antifungal activity of these compounds was preliminarily assayed at the concentration of 50 μg/mL. While most of the compounds exhibited low antifungal activity against the seven tested phytopathogenic fungi and one oomycete, 4 compounds demonstrated moderate antifungal activity against A. solani and 2 compounds against P. lingam. However, compounds B4 and B5 were studied as enantiomeric mixtures; therefore, further studies are needed to identify the most active one. The growth-regulating activity of 8 selected imidazolidine-2,4-dione derivatives was evaluated in a cucumber seed germination assay at a concentration of 5 mg/L. Two compounds, A1 and B1, which are structurally most similar to TDZ (containing a phenyl substituent in the imidazolidine ring), showed effects on seed germination comparable to those of the commercial PGR thidiazuron. These compounds were found to inhibit the growth of lateral roots and suppress the elongation of the primary root. These findings suggest that compounds A1 and B1 have potential as plant growth regulators; however, further in-depth investigation is required to elucidate their full biological activity and mechanism of action. Thus, it is necessary to evaluate the effects of compounds A1 and B1, as well as other imidazolidine-2,4-dione derivatives, across different plant species and at lower concentrations. In addition, their potential application in in vitro plant cultivation and micropropagation merits further investigation. Also, toxicological assessment will be essential to determine the safety profiles of these compounds. For imidazolidine-2,4-diones B1–B15, a study of the biological activity of enantiopure compounds is also recommended. Ultimately, a comprehensive study of these compounds in comparison with TDZ and other PGRs will provide valuable insights into assess their potential for sustainable horticulture and agriculture.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Instruments

All chemical reagents used were analytical grade. Melting points (mp) were measured by using a Stuart SMP 3 apparatus (Staffordshire, ST15 OSA, UK). The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were obtained using a Bruker Alpha (ATR, ZnSe) spectrometer (Ettlingen, Germany). 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance II (Karlsruhe, Germany) spectrometer (400 MHz for 1H, 100 MHz for 13C) or a Bruker Avance NEO (Karlsruhe, Germany) spectrometer (600 MHz for 1H and 150 MHz for 13C) with dimethyl sulfoxide-d 6 (DMSO-d 6) as the solvent. The NMR and IR spectra of all synthesized compounds are provided in the Supporting Information (Figures S1–S102). Mass spectra were obtained with a Shimadzu GCMS-QP 2010 “Ultra” (Kyoto, Japan) in electron ionization (EI) mode (electron energy, 70 eV). Elemental analyses were performed with a PerkinElmer 2400Series II CHNS/O analyzer (Shelton, CT, USA). The progress of the reactions and the purity of the compounds were monitored by TLC on TLC Silica gel 60 F245 aluminum sheets (Merck KGaA) in an ethyl acetate–hexane system (1:5 or 1:2).

Synthesis

General Synthetic Procedure for Compounds 4 (Scheme )

To a solution of corresponding aniline derivatives 1 (10.0 mmol) and sodium acetate (10.0 mmol) in ethanol (10 mL), ethyl bromoacetate 2 (10.0 mmol) was added. The reaction mixture was heated with stirring to 50 °C for 8–10 h. The completion of the reaction was verified through thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analysis. The solution was evaporated under reduced pressure, and chloroform was added. The organic phase was washed with water and dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. Ethanol was added to the residue. The precipitate obtained was filtered and washed with ethanol.

General Synthetic Procedure for Compounds 5 (Scheme )

Intermediate 5 was synthesized according to previously reported methods. Aniline derivatives 1 (10.0 mmol) were dissolved in 15 mL of dry DMF. Then, 1.0 eq. of potassium carbonate and 2.0 eq. of potassium iodide were added, and 12.0 mmol of methyl-(RS)-2-chloropropionate was added dropwise. The reaction mixture was heated with stirring to 80 °C for 16–20 h. The precipitate was filtered and washed with DMF. The filtrate was then poured into 100 mL of water and extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 100 mL). The combined organic fractions were washed with water (3 × 100 mL) followed by 1 N HCl (2 × 100 mL) and dried over anhydrous Na2SO4. Thereafter, the solvent was distilled off. The compound was used in the next steps of the synthesis without further purification.

General Synthetic Procedure for Compounds 6 and 7 (Scheme )

Intermediate 4 or 5 (1 mmol) was dissolved in 15 mL of ethanol, and an aqueous solution of NaOH (1.1 mmol) was added dropwise. The resulting mixture was heated to 50 °C for 4 h. Upon verification of the completion of the reaction through TLC analysis, the reaction mixture was cooled by an ice bath and 1 N HCl was added in drops until the pH was neutral. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 h. The precipitate obtained was filtered, washed with water and dried at room temperature.

General Synthetic Procedure for Compounds A1–A19 and B1–B15 (Scheme )

To a solution of 4-methyl-1,2,3-thiadiazole-5-carbonyl azide 8 in 10 mL of dry 1,4-dioxane, the corresponding N-arylglycine 6 or N-aryl-(RS)-alanine 7 was added. The reaction mixture was refluxed for 4 h. Upon verification of completion of the reaction through TLC analysis, the solution was cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, the solvent was evaporated under reduced pressure, and ethanol was added to the obtained residue. The precipitate formed was filtered and washed with ethanol to give the target compound A or B.

Fungicidal Activity Assay

Compounds A1–19 and B1–15 were evaluated for their in vitro fungicidal activities against 7 plant pathogenic fungi and 1 oomycete at a concentration of 50 μg/mL, using the mycelial growth inhibition method previously described. The tested phytopathogens included Alternaria solani Sorauer MFP601021, Botrytis cinerea Pers. MFG 60449, Colletotrichum coccodes JS 161-1, Fusarium solani (Mart.) Sacc. MFG 70523, Plenodomus lingam (Tode:Fr.) Höhn. MF Br17-044, Rhizoctonia solani RCAM01785, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, and the oomycete Phytophthora infestans (Mont.) de Bary. P. infestans was isolated at Nankai University (Tianjin, China). C. coccodes was purchased from the Russian National Collection of Industrial Microorganisms (Moscow, Russia). Other fungal strains were purchased from the Russian Collection of Agriculture Microorganisms (Saint Petersburg, Russia). Potato dextrose agar (peeled potatoes, 200 g; dextrose, 20 g; agar, 20.0 g; distilled water, 1000 mL) was used as a nutrient medium for fungal growth.

The solutions of the compounds were prepared at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL by dissolving 5 mg of the compound in 1 mL of DMSO followed by the addition of 9 mL of water. A total of 1 mL of the tested solutions was added to Petri dishes containing 9 mL of preheated culture medium and homogeneously mixed under sterile conditions in the laminar flow cabinet. Uniform fungal discs (4 mm diameter) were aseptically excised from a 7 day old culture of the test fungus using a sterile cork borer. Discs of mycelia were placed at the center of Petri dishes containing the culture medium at room temperature. The negative control plates were prepared with a culture medium and DMSO only. All treatments, including controls, were conducted in triplicate. Fungal cultures were incubated at 25 °C (48 h for R. solani; 120 h for A. solani and P. lingam; 72 h for other fungi). The diameters of the fungal colonies were measured at the end of the incubation period. Inhibition of the pathogen development was determined according to the formula of Royse and Ries: I (%) = [(C – T)/(C – 4 mm)] × 100, where I (%) is the degree of inhibition of mycelial growth, T (mm) is the mean value of the diameter of the colonies in the presence of a given concentration of each compound, and C (mm) is the mean diameter of the colonies in the absence of the compound under the same conditions. The standard deviation was calculated.

Plant Growth Regulation Assay

The growth-regulating properties of imidazolidine-2,4-diones A1, A3, A10, and A12 and B1, B3, B10, and B12 were studied on commercial C. sativus L. seeds of the Vyaznikovskie-37 variety (Russkiy ogorod, Russia) at a concentration of 5 mg/L. Cucumber seeds were surface sterilized by immersion in 70% ethanol for 30 s and then soaked in 0.1% sodium hypochlorite solution for 2 min. The disinfected seeds were then rinsed with sterilized distilled water 5 times. All procedures were carried out under aseptic conditions in a laminar flow cabinet. Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) as well as PRGs such as 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BAP) and thidiazuron (TDZ) were selected as reference substances. Water, filter paper, and dispenser tips were autoclaved (121 °C for 20 min); Petri dishes and all glassware were sterilized in a hot air oven (160 °C for 2 h). For each treatment, 7 mL of an aqueous solution containing the test compound at the required concentration along with 0.1% DMSO was added. The control Petri dishes contained only 7 mL of sterile water only. The seeds were placed in Petri dishes on filter paper, 10 pcs per dish. All treatments were performed in triplicate (3 Petri dishes for the fifth day measurements and 3 for the seventh day). A total of 60 cucumber seeds were sown for each compound. Petri dishes were incubated in the Binder growth chamber (Germany) at a temperature of 25 °C and humidity of 50%, with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod at 3100 lx (Fluora growth lamps). Measurements were taken on the fifth and seventh days for the following parameters: stem length and width, root length and width, total sprout length, cotyledon length and width, and number of lateral roots. The average for all experimental treatments is presented in Tables S2 and S3 (Supporting Information). The experimental results were analyzed using the STATISTICA 8.0 software package. Analyses included ANOVA, the Kruskal–Wallis test, and the Tukey test. Discriminant analysis was also conducted by using Mahalanobis distance-based classification. Descriptive statistics included the mean, standard deviation (SD), standard error of the mean, and coefficient of variation (CV).

To study the effect of compounds A1, B1, and TDZ on the growth of individual root cells, the tissue maceration method was used. The root tissues were temporarily kept in a solution of KOH for 1 h, then mounted onto a glass slide, covered with a coverslip, and examined under a Carl Zeiss Axioscope-1 microscope (15 × 40) in five visual fields. The photographs were measured in the program AxioVizion CarlZeiss.
