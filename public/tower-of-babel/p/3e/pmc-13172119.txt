Explainable vision transformer framework for multi-class classification and prognostic interpretation of oral cancer in histopathology images
Authors: Chandrakanta Mahanty, Chin-Shiuh Shieh, Mong-Fong Horng, Anusha Nallamalla, S Gopal Krishna Patro, Shafat Khan, Trmesgen Engida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral cancer, particularly Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma (OSCC), remains a major global health concern due to its high prevalence, late diagnosis, and limited prognostic precision in conventional histopathological evaluation. Although deep learning has been showing promising results in automated cancer classification, most current models, especially CNN-based architectures, generally lack interpretability, generalization capability, and prognostic insight, hence limiting their clinical applicability. To address these shortcomings, this work introduces an Explainable Vision Transformer framework (MMX-ViT) for multi-class classification and prognostic interpretation of oral cancer in histopathology images. The proposed model fuses convolutional feature extraction with transformer-based global attention using an Adaptive Cross-Fusion Module (ACFM), allowing efficient multi-scale learning of cellular and tissue-level features. The MMX-ViT model was trained and evaluated on a publicly available oral cancer histopathology dataset, extended in this study into four diagnostic categories, and compared with eight state-of-the-art architectures. It reached a high classification performance of 98.45%, with an AUC of 0.99, thus surpassing all the baseline methods. Explainability analysis based on Grad-CAM + + , SHAP, and Transformer Attention Rollout techniques demonstrated that biologically relevant areas of attention were identified by the model, such as dysplastic nuclei, keratin pearls, and invasion zones in stroma, with an XCI (Explainability Consistency Index) value of 94%. The model proposed here represents a major progress towards the establishment of reliable and interpretable AI-based diagnosis of oral cancer.

Introduction

Oral cancer is one of the most frequently diagnosed malignancies worldwide, with approximately 377,000 new cases and 177,000 deaths reported annually [1]. OSCC accounts for over 90% of oral cancer cases and is associated with high recurrence rates, invasive behavior, and poor prognosis when detected at advanced stages [2]. Despite advances in treatment, the 5-year survival rate remains around 50–60%, largely due to late diagnosis and subjectivity in histopathological assessment [3]. While‌ histopathology remains the diagnostic gold standard, its accuracy is questioned due to inter-observer variability, structural heterogeneity, and subjective bias, which points to the continuing necessity for automated and clinically reliable diagnostic systems [4].

Advances in AI and DL have recently allowed automated histopathological image analysis. CNN-based models such as ResNet, DenseNet, and EfficientNet have demonstrated excellent performance in cancer classification tasks [5]. Nonetheless, their insufficient capability to capture global contextual dependencies and their black-box nature limit clinical interpretability and therefore adoption. Conversely, ViTs use self-attention mechanisms to simultaneously focus on local cellular features and long-range tissue context, thus they are highly compatible with histopathological analysis [6].

Most earlier AI-driven research in the oral cancer area has been devoted to two-class or broad multi-class categorization, thus neglecting clinical categories like Dysplastic and graded OSCC, and has seldom dealt with prognostic explainability. To overcome these drawbacks, the present work puts forward MMX-ViT, an interpretable hybrid Vision Transformer architecture for multi-class oral cancer recognition and prognostic evaluation. It fuses multi-scale convolutional features with transformer-based global attention and features extensive explainability and prognostic modules.

Literature survey

The following section contextualizes MMX-ViT and highlights the novelty of our approach by reviewing key advances and lingering limitations in the literature on deep learning for oral cancer classification and explainable vision transformers.

Al-Ali et al. [7] proposed CLASEG, a multi-task framework combining EfficientNet-B3 for classification with Mask R-CNN (ResNet-101) for segmentation of 14 oral lesion categories, achieving 74.49% accuracy. Liu et al. [8] introduced a lightweight 19-layer CNN for lip and tongue cancer detection, achieving 99.54% accuracy on the OCI dataset. Kabir et al. [9] presented LightSE-MobileViT, a hybrid CNN–Transformer model integrating squeeze-and-excitation blocks with MobileViT, which achieved 98.39% accuracy. Shah et al. [10] proposed OCANet, a U-Net–based architecture achieving 98.24% accuracy, 94.09% DSC, and 88.84% mIoU on OCDC and ORCA datasets. Sundari et al. [11] introduced a modified Xception network with channel attention and CLAHE preprocessing, achieving 96.59% accuracy. Cimino et al. [12] presented an explainable pipeline combining Faster R-CNN with case-based reasoning, attaining 83% detection accuracy and 92% classification accuracy, and formalizing diagnostic workflows to improve transparency and clinician trust. Dharani et al. [13] proposed a metaheuristic-driven ensemble combining EfficientNet-B5 and ResNet50V2 with squeeze-and-excitation and hybrid spatial–channel attention, optimized using the Tunicate Swarm Algorithm. Yaduvanshi et al. [14] introduced a Modified Local Binary Pattern descriptor integrated with SVM and deep CNN classifiers, achieving 94.44% accuracy at 400 × resolution, highlighting the continued relevance of handcrafted features for interpretable and resource-efficient screening. Parola et al. [15] combined Informed DL with Case-Based Reasoning, achieving 85% accuracy.

A number of studies focused on developing low-cost and mobile solutions for screening. Shamim et al. [16] used transfer learning with DCNNs to distinguish pre-cancerous tongue lesions from clinical images with a highest overall accuracy of 97.5%, whereas Ariji et al. [17] showed that metastatic lymph node segmentation from CT scans could be obtained reliably, yielding an AUC of 0.95, higher than a radiologist's performance. Sutha et al. [18] suggested a VGG16–CNN fusion with LBP features for working with hyperspectral images to achieve 96.45% accuracy at a significantly lower computational cost. The review by Alabi et al. [19] provided a more comprehensive overview of DL applications in OSCC diagnosis and prognosis and highlighted their potential contribution to precision oncology. Models like OralNet [20], DenseNet201-based smartphone screening [21], and CADOC-SFOFC [22] proved that the non-invasive and telemedicine-based methods of diagnosis were very accurate; on the other hand, Tapak et al. [23] focused on the use of autoencoders and survival modeling for the discovery of prognostic genes.

Huang et al. [24] developed a metaheuristic-optimized CNN applying the Improved Squirrel Search Algorithm to feature selection and weight tuning on the Oral Cancer Lips and Tongue dataset. Lin et al. [25] presented a smartphone-based concept using HRNet and a carefully planned image-capturing protocol to report an 83.6% F1-score and 96.6% specificity, thus showing that telemedicine-based screening is a viable option. Welikala et al. [26] benchmarked ResNet-101 and Faster R-CNN on the MeMoSA dataset and recorded F1-scores of 87.07% for lesion detection and 78.30% for referral classification, respectively, thus confirming strong cross-national generalization. Yang et al. [27] exploited CNNs to analyze OCT images, reached an accuracy of 96.76%, and obtained improved interpretability from visualizing lesion boundaries. Song et al. [28] built a Bayesian deep learning model that produces diagnostic uncertainty measures to help clinicians with their decision-making. Warin et al. [29] introduced a hybrid architecture combining DenseNet-121 and Faster R-CNN for classification and detection tasks, Li et al. [30] demonstrated the practicality of portable endoscopic AI systems by employing U-Net and ResNet-34 for supporting oral cancer screening at home with a self-administered device.

Many existing methods rely on small, institution-specific datasets with heterogeneous staining and imaging conditions, limiting generalizability across clinical settings. Moreover, while CNN and transformer models have achieved high accuracy, they often lack sufficient explainability, reducing clinician trust. Most prior studies focus primarily on binary classification, overlooking fine-grained differentiation between pre-cancerous and cancerous stages that is vital for early diagnosis. Additionally, prognostic modeling and confidence estimation are rarely incorporated, constraining clinical decision support. Materials and methods

Baseline models

Swin transformer

It was chosen to be the benchmark for hierarchical transformers. Moreover, it presents a self-attention mechanism based on shifted window that restricts the computation of attention to local non-overlapping windows and progressively merges patches through hierarchical down-sampling The Swin Transformer was the main transformer baseline for MMX-ViT which allowed the assessment of the advantages that came from the integration of adaptive cross-fusion and dual-resolution attention [31].

TransMIL

The pathology-specific transformer approach based on the Multiple Instance Learning paradigm was represented by its inclusion. It treats whole-slide images as unordered sets of patches and pools the features via attention. TransMIL, in contrast to patch-level CNN classifiers, assigns slide-level labels by dynamically weighting the contribution of each instance, this allowing more interpretability in modeling tissue heterogeneity [32].

ConvNeXt

It symbolizes a modern CNN architecture developed to rival transformers’ performance without sacrificing the convolutional efficiency. Taking its cue from the Swin Transformer design, ConvNeXt incorporates large kernel convolutions (7 × 7), inverted bottlenecks, depthwise separable layers, and layer normalization. By this combination, the network is able to achieve not only broader receptive fields and better abstraction of features but also to maintain translation invariance [33].

ResNet-50

The ResNet-50 architecture was chosen as a standard deep CNN baseline that represents traditional residual learning. By employing residual connections, the model has been able to overcome the vanishing gradient issue and thus, the training of the network has reached up to 50 layers without any problem. The hierarchical feature extraction through the residual mapping of ResNet-50 has made it a commonly accepted standard for the medical image analysis [34].

EfficientNetV2

The EfficientNetV2 architecture showcases compound scaling, where the three factors (network width, depth and resolution) are simultaneously optimized for achieving the best quality and efficiency. Furthermore, it uses fused-MBConv blocks, squeeze-and-excitation modules and progressive learning methodologies for quicker convergence. It has been selected as a high-efficiency CNN baseline, which is perfect for clinical edge devices [35].

YOLOv8

YOLOv8, which is an anchor-less version from the YOLO (You Only Look Once) line, was modified for patch-level histopathology classification. The architect uses separate detection heads, mosaic data augmentations, and feature pyramid backbones (CSPDarknet) to efficiently capture both spatial and textural cues. Even though it was originally designed for object detection, the encoder-decoder structure along with cross-stage partial blocks used in YOLOv8 allow for significant feature localization in tissue ROIs [36].

ShuffleNetV2

This model served as a basic convolutional model of low weight with an emphasis on speed and mobile usage optimization. It uses both channel split–shuffle operations and pointwise group convolutions, which together manage efficient feature interaction and keep the number of FLOPs low. ShuffleNetV2 gives helpful diagnostic insights for the comparative evaluation of model size versus classification accuracy [37].

MobileNetV3-large

This model integrates several elements such as inverted residual bottlenecks, squeeze-and-excitation blocks, and Swish activation functions for maximum mobile inference. It provides a balance between efficiency and power, offering strong performance even on very low computational hardware [38].

These eight models provide a comprehensive architectural framework ranging from lightweight CNNs to hierarchical transformers against which the MMX-ViT framework was benchmarked.

Proposed MMX-ViT framework

To overcome the drawbacks of traditional CNNs in capturing long-range dependencies and the excessive data requirements of transformer-only setups, we introduce a new hybrid structure called MMX-ViT. The MMX-ViT architecture comprises four major components:a multi-scale CNN encoder for localized morphological feature extraction,a multi-head transformer encoder for capturing inter-patch dependencies,an ACFM that integrates hierarchical CNN and transformer representations, andan explainability sub-network powered by Grad-CAM + + , SHAP, and Attention Rollout mechanisms for post-hoc interpretability.

The proposed MMX-ViT framework (Fig. 1) follows a six-stage pipeline for oral cancer histopathology classification and prognostic interpretation. Initially, H&E-stained images undergo Macenko color normalization, resizing, and data augmentation to mitigate staining variability and improve generalization. The processed images are passed through a multi-scale CNN encoder to capture localized morphological features, which are then partitioned into patches and embedded for a Vision Transformer encoder that models long-range spatial dependencies via multi-head self-attention. An ACFM dynamically integrates CNN and transformer features, balancing local and global representations through learnable weights. The fused features are forwarded to a fully connected Softmax-based classification head for multi-class prediction. Finally, an integrated explainability module generates Grad-CAM + +, SHAP, and Attention Rollout visualizations to highlight decision-critical regions. The strong spatial agreement between these maps and expert annotations yielded an XCI of 94%, confirming close alignment with pathologist reasoning. Experimental design

Dataset description

The present study utilized the Histopathologic Oral Cancer Dataset, which is publicly available through the Kaggle repository [39]. The dataset contains 1224 H&E images of oral mucosal tissue, both normal and malignant, captured at two different magnification levels: 100 × and 400 × . Every biopsy sample was subjected to a standard fixation with 10% neutral-buffered formalin, then embedded in paraffin, and sectioned with a microtome at 3-μm thickness for staining under the H&E routine to reveal the morphologies of the epithelial, stromal, and nuclear structures. Dataset Description

Expert re-annotation and multi-class label derivation

The‌ original dataset contains diagnostic labels in binary form which distinguish between normal epithelium and OSCC. For allowing the study of clinically relevant multi-class decisions, in this study, two independent experienced oral pathologists re-annotated all abnormal images into three classes: dysplastic epithelium, low-grade OSCC, and high-grade OSCC based on WHO histopathological criteria for oral epithelial dysplasia and OSCC differentiation.

At the image level, grading was done based on epithelial architecture, cytological atypia, keratinization, mitotic figures, and invasion patterns. Any disagreement was resolved by consensus review. Along with the binary labels that were originally used, the multi-class labels represent an additional layer of annotation by experts introduced only for this study. Inter-observer agreement was statistically measured with Cohen's κ yielding a κ of 0.85 which is indicative of strong agreement and supports the reliability of the multi-class annotations. In order to avoid data leakage and to maintain the statistical independence, all patches that were taken from the same ROI or whole-slide image were exclusively assigned to a single dataset split (training, validation, or testing) and during evaluation, they were not considered as independent samples.

Data preprocessing and patch generation

Macenko's color normalization algorithm was applied to correct the staining variation between the slides so colors would be represented consistently throughout the whole dataset. Morphological opening plus Gaussian smoothing were applied for getting rid of small particles but at the same time keeping nice histological detail.

We increase the granularity of the dataset and, at the same time, reduce computational complexity by dividing each 2048 × 1536 image into non-overlapping patches of 512 × 512 pixels. Patches containing less than 30% tissue are removed from the dataset to avoid non-informative regions dominated by the background.

Data augmentation

Extensive augmentations were used to increase the variability and improve generalization given the inherent class imbalance, with data set size limitations. Synthetic transformations included the following: rotations (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°), horizontal and vertical flips, and elastic deformations to simulate natural variability in the orientation of the tissues. Photometric augmentations used in this study included color jittering with brightness ± 15%, contrast ± 10%, Gaussian blurring with σ = 1.2, and random zoom scaling.

Data splitting and stratification

The model evaluation was done in a very robust way by carrying out a patient-level stratified split which also eliminated the chances of data leakage. Specifically, all images belonging to a single patient were exclusively assigned to one of the three subsets (training, validation, or testing) in such a manner that the tissue samples of that patient would not be seen in any of the phases more than once. The split of the data was done in a ratio of 70:15:15, stratifying per magnification in order to maintain the class proportions.

Experimental setup and reproducibility

All experiments were performed on a high-performance workstation configured with an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU (48 GB VRAM), Intel Xeon W-2295 CPU, and 256 GB RAM, using PyTorch 2.2.1. Model initialization utilized pretrained weights from ImageNet-21K for both ConvNeXt-ViT and AMS-ViT encoders, resulting in faster convergence. Optimization during training was done through AdamW, a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻4, cosine annealing scheduler, batch size of 16, and up to 150 epochs, with early stopping. Hyperparameters were dynamically refined using the Position-Revised Nuclear Reaction Optimization algorithm, amplifying the stability and convergence further. For the prognostic performance, Pearson's correlation coefficient and MAE were computed. Interpretability was quantified as the XCI, which captured the overlap between Grad-CAM + + and expert annotation. Statistical significance and validation

Statistical significance analysis was performed in detail to compare the proposed MMX-ViT model with the existing architectures in terms of robustness and reliability (Table 6). It comprised one-way ANOVA, Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test, and variance estimation with five-fold cross-validation for all nine models. The one-way ANOVA test was conducted to check if the mean classification accuracies of all the models compared had a statistically significant difference. Indeed, this analysis revealed a very significant difference among the models, F(8, 36) = 41.73 with p < 0.001, thus indicating that at least one model's mean accuracy was different from the others. Subsequently, Tukey’s HSD multiple pairwise comparisons revealed that the proposed MMX-ViT model achieved a statistically significant performance improvement, p < 0.01 against all CNN-based and lightweight architectures such as ResNet50, ConvNeXt, EfficientNetV2, ShuffleNetV2, YOLOv8, and MobileNetV3-Large. However, the performance differences between Swin Transformer and TransMIL and MMX-ViT remain statistically significant, p < 0.05, hence further discussion of the supremacy of MMX-ViT among advanced transformer baselines is justified. Statistical Validation of Model Performance

Tukey's HSD test showed that the MMX-ViT model was assigned to a statistical group, Group A, which was separated from all the other architectures, thus implying that it had a significant advantage over the rest of the models. Several models, including ConvNeXt, ResNet-50, YOLOv8, and MobileNetV3-Large, among others, have been assigned to Groups D to F and as can be seen they had much lower means but larger variances, thus demonstrating inconsistency in the models' results. Swin Transformer and TransMIL were allocated different statistical groups, B and C, respectively, thus, even if they were performing equally, the fact that MMX-ViT had a measurable and reproducible advantage was proven. The very low standard deviation, σ < 0.4%, across the folds is a sign of the model’s robustness in relation to the variability of data partitioning and therefore its suitability for clinical deployment.

The accuracy distributions that were used for one-way ANOVA and Tukey's HSD analysis were generated via five-fold cross-validation; five accuracy values per model (n = 5) were thus obtained. All models were assessed with the same fixed data splits for the sake of a fair comparison, whereas training and optimization were carried out independently in each fold. To check ANOVA assumptions, Levene's test was used and confirmed variance homogeneity (p > 0.05). To be open about it, fold-wise accuracy statistics (mean ± standard deviation) for all models are given in the Appendix (Table 7).

Discussion

The experimental results reveal that MMX-ViT advances oral cancer histopathology analysis to an unprecedented level by combining within a single architecture excellent diagnostic accuracy, prognostic interpretation, and explainability. In a head-to-head comparison against eight state-of-the-art models, MMX-ViT obtained the highest performance. Integrated explainability methods offer transparent visual evidence by showing the areas of greatest diagnostic relevance, e.g., dysplastic nuclei, keratin pearls, and epithelial–stromal interfaces, in the model outputs. This closely matches pathologists’ visual reasoning and thus reduces the “black-box” issue, which is a common concern in deep learning-based systems. The high XCI score of 94% signifies a strong concordance between model attention and expert annotations, which is key for trust and interpretability in everyday clinical ‌settings. The prognostic regression head which is an extended modality of the model generates a quantitative malignancy score highly consistent with expert grading (r = 0.94, p < 0.001), thus it can be used for risk stratification, surveillance, and deciding on therapeutic plans without the need of substituting clinical judgment.

Limitations and future directions

While the proposed framework exhibits strong diagnostic and interpretive performance, several challenges remain. First, while a balanced dataset was used, broader validation across multi-institutional multi-scanner cohorts is essential for ensuring generalizability under variable staining and acquisition protocols. Second, although Grad-CAM + +, SHAP, and Attention Rollout provide qualitative interpretability, future work should integrate quantitative explainability validation metrics evaluated through pathologist-in-the-loop studies. Multi-modal fusion of histological, genomic, and clinical metadata toward comprehensive prognostic models is another potential area of future improvements, which would enable precision oncology applications.

Conclusion

This paper proposes MMX-ViT, an explainable Vision Transformer framework for the multi-class oral cancer histopathology analysis, which is designed to unify high diagnostic accuracy with clinical interpretability. By fusing CNN-based local feature extraction and transformer-based global context modeling through the ACFM mechanism, MMX-ViT captures both the cellular and tissue-level characteristics across four classes. The model attained superior results with an overall accuracy of 98.45% and an AUC of 0.99 and outperformed nine state-of-the-art architectures. Biologically meaningful explainability by Grad-CAM + +, SHAP, and Transformer Attention Rollout mechanisms was offered within an ECI of 94%, which warrants that the model focuses on the desired regions as interpreted by the pathologist. From a clinical viewpoint, MMX-ViT enhances the confidence in diagnosis by visualization of key malignant regions to support early detection, grading, and the prognostic risk assessment in digital pathology workflows. Though highly effective, future studies will go beyond validating this idea across multi-institutional datasets and explore domain adaptation and multi-modal integration to improve generalization.
