Surgical Management of Chronic Distal Tibiofibular Syndesmotic Instability: A Scoping Review of Techniques and Outcomes
Authors: Jacob W McDevitt, Eric H Durudogan, Frederic J Washburn, Milap S Patel, Muhammad Y Mutawakkil, Anish R Kadakia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Chronic distal tibiofibular syndesmotic instability is a diagnostically challenging condition associated with persistent pain, functional limitation, and progressive tibiotalar arthritis. Despite a growing range of surgical options, no consensus exists regarding optimal technique, patient selection, or expected outcomes, and no validated classification system has been established to guide treatment.Methods:A scoping review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines and the Arksey and O’Malley methodologic framework. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library were searched through February 2026 with no date restrictions. Eligible studies included skeletally mature patients undergoing surgical treatment for chronic syndesmotic instability, defined as 6 or more months from injury to intervention, with a minimum 6-month follow-up.Results:Twelve studies encompassing 122 patients met inclusion criteria. All were Level IV or V evidence. Surgical techniques included tendon graft reconstruction (n = 42), arthroscopic-assisted stabilization (n = 44), suture-button or suture anchor fixation (n = 25), and distal tibiofibular arthrodesis (n = 11). AOFAS scores improved by 20 to 50 points across technique categories. Complication and unplanned reoperation rates were low. Return to sport was achieved in all athletic cohorts. Arthroscopic instability grade influenced the ceiling of functional recovery. Complication and reoperation rates were low.Conclusion:The available evidence, although limited to low-level retrospective studies, suggests that chronic distal tibiofibular syndesmotic instability may be surgically treatable across a range of clinical presentations, and functional improvement has been reported even in longstanding cases. The evidence is consistent with a phenotype-driven treatment approach, though prospective comparative data are lacking: ligament reconstruction or dynamic stabilization for patients without significant arthritis, and distal tibiofibular arthrodesis as a joint-sparing option for those with post-traumatic degenerative changes or fibular malunion. Severity of instability at the time of surgery appears to influence the ceiling of functional recovery, suggesting that earlier operative intervention in appropriate candidates may be beneficial, although this remains to be confirmed in prospective studies. Standardized classification and outcome reporting remain the primary barriers to advancing evidence-based care for this condition.Keywords: chronic syndesmotic instability, distal tibiofibular joint, syndesmosis reconstruction, distal tibiofibular arthrodesis, suture-button fixation, scoping review

Introduction

The distal tibiofibular syndesmosis is a critical stabilizing structure of the ankle mortise, maintaining the relationship between the distal tibia and fibula and preserving normal tibiotalar joint mechanics.1,2 The syndesmotic complex consists of the anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament, posterior inferior tibiofibular ligament, interosseous ligament, and transverse ligament, which together resist fibular translation and rotation. Disruption of this complex alters ankle biomechanics, and even subtle syndesmotic instability can significantly increase tibiotalar contact pressures and predispose patients to pain, dysfunction, and post-traumatic arthritis.3,4

Syndesmotic injuries occur in approximately 1% to 18% of ankle sprains and are frequently associated with ankle fractures, particularly pronation-external rotation injuries and Weber B and C fracture patterns. 5 Acute injuries are typically managed with reduction and stabilization using syndesmotic screws or suture-button fixation, and outcomes are generally favorable when injuries are recognized and treated promptly. 6 However, a subset of patients develop chronic syndesmotic instability because of missed diagnosis or inadequate stabilization following the initial injury.7,8

Chronic syndesmotic instability is commonly defined as an injury-to-treatment interval of 6 months or greater and is characterized by persistent ankle pain, swelling, instability, functional limitations, and reduced ability to return to sport or activity. 9 The clinical presentation spans a broad spectrum and can be broadly categorized into 2 phenotypes: patients with chronic ligamentous instability in the absence of significant arthritis, who may be candidates for ligament reconstruction or dynamic stabilization, and those with concomitant post-traumatic arthritis, fibular malunion, or nonunion, for whom distal tibiofibular arthrodesis represents a distinct salvage strategy. This distinction has direct implications for surgical planning and expected outcomes. Also, diagnosis can be challenging, as standard radiographs may appear normal and symptoms often overlap with lateral ankle instability. Advanced imaging such as stress radiographs, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and arthroscopy are frequently required to confirm the diagnosis. 2

A variety of surgical techniques have been described for the management of chronic syndesmotic instability, including ligament reconstruction with tendon grafts, synthetic ligament augmentation, arthroscopic-assisted stabilization, screw re-fixation, fibular osteotomy, and distal tibiofibular arthrodesis in cases with associated arthritis. 10 Despite increasing recognition of this condition, the existing literature is limited, heterogeneous, and spans a wide range of injury severity, surgical approaches, and patient populations. No consensus exists regarding optimal surgical technique, patient selection criteria, or expected outcomes, and no validated classification system for chronic syndesmotic instability has been established to guide treatment decisions.

Given this breadth of clinical and methodologic variability, a traditional systematic review with pooled analysis is not appropriate for this body of literature. Therefore, the purpose of this scoping review was to map the existing evidence on surgical management of chronic distal tibiofibular syndesmotic instability; to characterize the range of patient presentations and surgical techniques reported in the literature; to identify key gaps in knowledge and areas of methodology and research improvement; and to provide standardized treatment algorithms.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines and followed the methodologic framework described by Arksey and O’Malley. 11 A review protocol was not prospectively registered prior to the search.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined to capture the full breadth of published evidence on surgical management of chronic distal tibiofibular syndesmotic instability. The population included skeletally mature patients with chronic distal tibiofibular syndesmotic instability, defined as an interval of 6 months or greater from initial injury to surgical intervention. The intervention included any surgical technique for reconstruction or stabilization of chronic syndesmotic instability, including synthetic ligament augmentation, tendon graft reconstruction, screw fixation, arthroscopic repair, fibular osteotomy, or distal tibiofibular arthrodesis. Given the scoping nature of this review, no comparator was required, and all study designs were eligible for inclusion regardless of whether a comparison group was present.

Studies were included if they reported clinical outcomes following surgical treatment of chronic syndesmotic instability in skeletally mature patients and were published in English. A minimum follow-up of 6 months was required to allow for meaningful postoperative functional assessment. Studies were excluded if they focused on acute syndesmotic injuries (less than 6 months from injury to treatment), biomechanical or cadaveric studies, pediatric populations, or syndesmotic fixation performed solely in the context of acute fracture management without a chronic instability component. Narrative reviews, editorials, letters, and expert opinion articles were also excluded.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A systematic literature search was performed using PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. The final search was conducted in February 2026, and no date restrictions were applied. The search strategy included combinations of keywords and MeSH terms related to syndesmotic injury, chronic syndesmotic instability, distal tibiofibular instability, syndesmosis reconstruction, and tibiofibular arthrodesis (Supplemental Table 2). The full search strategy for each database is provided in the Supplemental Appendix. Reference lists of all included studies were manually reviewed to identify additional eligible studies. Gray literature and conference abstracts were not included.

Study Selection

Study selection was performed in 2 stages. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by 2 reviewers using predetermined eligibility criteria. Studies deemed potentially eligible underwent full-text review. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus. A PRISMA-ScR flow diagram was generated to document the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and included at each stage of the review process.

During full-text review, 2 publications by Grass et al (2003) 12 and Grass and Zwipp (2006) 13 were identified as potentially representing overlapping patient cohorts. Both studies were retained because they reported distinct outcome measures and time points, and exclusion of either would substantially reduce the available evidence base. Readers should interpret findings from these 2 series with caution, as patient-level overlap cannot be excluded and may introduce duplication of outcomes. These studies are reported separately throughout the review and interpreted with the acknowledgment that patient overlap cannot be excluded.

Kim and Shin (2021) 14 was retained despite describing acute surgical intervention because the study population underwent operative stabilization specifically to prevent the development of chronic syndesmotic instability, a progression explicitly cited by the authors as the primary risk of non-operative management. The surgical technique, return-to-sport outcomes, and complication profile reported in this study are directly relevant to the clinical framework of this review, and the study’s acute etiology is clearly noted throughout.

Data Abstraction

Data were extracted using a standardized data collection form. Given the scoping intent of this review, data abstraction was designed to comprehensively characterize the range of clinical presentations, surgical approaches, and reported outcomes across the literature rather than to enable pooled quantitative analysis. Extracted variables included study design, level of evidence, sample size, patient demographics, injury etiology, preoperative severity of instability, time from injury to surgery, specific surgical technique employed, concomitant procedures, postoperative rehabilitation protocol, validated functional outcomes, pain scores, radiographic parameters, complications, reoperation rates, and return to sport or activity. Given the absence of a validated classification system for chronic syndesmotic instability, preoperative severity was not defined by a single metric but rather interpreted from each study’s reported parameters, including arthroscopic instability grade where available, radiographic measurements such as tibiofibular clear space, tibiofibular overlap, and medial clear space, stress examination findings, and qualitative descriptors used by the original authors. These heterogeneous characterizations are reported as described in the source studies and should be interpreted accordingly. Data extraction was performed by one reviewer and verified by a second reviewer, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Quality Assessment

Given that most included studies were observational case series, methodologic quality and risk of bias were assessed using the Modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale adapted for case series. Case reports were evaluated using CARE reporting guidelines. Studies were categorized as low, moderate, or high risk of bias based on selection methods, comparability, outcome assessment, confounding, and follow-up reporting. Consistent with scoping review methodology, no studies were excluded based on quality assessment. Instead, study quality was incorporated into the interpretation of findings and is reported transparently to contextualize the evidence base.

Synthesis

Due to substantial heterogeneity in study design, surgical techniques, patient populations, injury severity, outcome measures, and follow-up duration, quantitative synthesis and meta-analysis were not performed. Findings are presented descriptively, organized first by surgical technique category and then by the specific clinical and radiographic characteristics of included patient populations. Each included study is described individually to preserve the contextual detail necessary for meaningful interpretation, with particular attention to preoperative injury severity, the specific fixation strategy employed, and reported functional and radiographic outcomes. This approach was selected to accurately reflect the breadth and heterogeneity of the current literature rather than to obscure it through inappropriate aggregation.

Results

Study Selection and Characteristics

The literature search identified 77 unique records. Following title and abstract screening and full-text review, 12 studies met inclusion criteria comprising 122 patients with chronic distal tibiofibular syndesmotic instability (Figure 1, Supplemental Table 3). All included studies were Level IV or V evidence. Nine evaluated ligament reconstruction or stabilization procedures, 2 evaluated distal tibiofibular arthrodesis as a salvage procedure, and 1 described treatment of symptomatic tibiofibular synostosis. Follow-up ranged from 11 to 49 months. The included literature reflects substantial heterogeneity in patient presentation, injury severity, surgical technique, and outcome reporting (Table 1).

Findings by Study

Among reconstruction studies, Grass et al (2003) 12 and Grass and Zwipp (2006) 13 both reported on peroneus longus ligamentoplasty for chronic instability following pronation-external rotation fracture-dislocation, with mean Karlsson scores of 88 at follow-up and substantial radiographic normalization of tibiofibular parameters; potential cohort overlap between these 2 series cannot be excluded. Morris et al (2009) 19 described free hamstring autograft reconstruction in 8 patients with heterogeneous injury etiologies, achieving a mean AOFAS of 85.4 and return to work in 7 of 8 patients, although 1 required subsequent ankle arthrodesis. Wagener et al (2011) 17 combined arthroscopic debridement with suture anchor reconstruction in 12 patients, improving AOFAS from 72 to 92 with 11 of 12 achieving excellent or good results. Yasui et al (2011) 15 reported anatomical AITFL reconstruction using a doubled gracilis autograft with interference screw fixation in 6 patients, 3 of whom required concomitant fibular osteotomy to achieve adequate reduction, with AOFAS improving from 53 to 95. Colcuc et al (2016) 20 classified 32 patients arthroscopically into 3 instability grades and treated all with suture-button fixation, demonstrating statistically significant AOFAS improvement across all grades (P < .005 for each grade), though grade III patients achieved lower final scores than grades I and II. Kim and Shin (2021) 14 reported suture anchor augmentation in 22 elite athletes, with 100% return to play at a mean of 103 days to official match participation. Rozis et al (2022) 18 and Nishikawa et al (2026) 16 each reported small series or single cases of suture-button or screw fixation with favorable short-term outcomes.

Among salvage procedures, Olson et al (2011) 22 reported distal tibiofibular arthrodesis in 10 patients with chronic instability and post-traumatic arthritis, improving AOFAS from 37 to 87 with normalization of medial clear space and talar tilt; no patient required tibiotalar arthrodesis at final follow-up. Jeong et al (2018) 21 reported a single case with AOFAS improvement from 45 to 90 at 49 months following arthrodesis. Margulis et al (2020) 23 described a unique case of tibiofibular synostosis treated with resection and ligamentoplasty, resulting in resolution of impingement and return to manual labor.

Across all 12 studies, surgical techniques fell into 4 broad categories: tendon graft reconstruction (Grass 2003, Grass and Zwipp 2006, Morris 2009, Yasui 2011, Colcuc 2016 Grade III subset, Rozis 2022, Margulis 2020; n = 43), arthroscopic-assisted stabilization (Wagener 2011, Colcuc 2016 Grades I and II subset; n = 44), suture-button or suture anchor fixation (Kim and Shin 2021, Nishikawa 2026; n = 25), and distal tibiofibular arthrodesis (Olson 2011, Jeong 2018; n = 11). Pooled functional outcomes and complications by technique category are summarized in Table 4. Tendon graft reconstruction produced postoperative AOFAS scores ranging from 85 to 95 where reported, with Karlsson scores of 88 in both Grass cohorts. Arthroscopic-assisted stabilization yielded AOFAS improvements of 20 to 33 points depending on arthroscopic instability grade. Suture-button and suture anchor constructs demonstrated excellent return-to-sport outcomes with low complication rates, though the suture anchor cohort was composed exclusively of acute injuries. Distal tibiofibular arthrodesis produced the largest absolute AOFAS improvements, averaging 47 to 50 points, in the most functionally impaired preoperative cohorts. Complication and unplanned reoperation rates were low across all technique categories. Given the recognized limitations of AOFAS as a standalone outcome measure, all additionally reported functional metrics are presented in Tables 2 and 4, including Karlsson scores, FAOS subscales, SF-36, Maryland Foot Score, VAS pain scores, return to sport, return to work, and return to manual labor where reported.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Overall methodologic quality was low. No study achieved low risk of bias. Common limitations included retrospective design, small sample sizes, heterogeneous patient populations, inconsistent use of validated outcome measures, and confounding from concomitant procedures. Industry conflicts of interest were present in 2 studies involving suture-button constructs (Supplemental Table 1).

Discussion

This scoping review identified 12 studies encompassing 122 patients reporting on surgical management of chronic distal tibiofibular syndesmotic instability. Despite representing the lowest levels of evidence, the literature suggests a consistent signal of clinically meaningful functional improvement across a wide range of surgical techniques, injury severities, and patient populations. AOFAS scores improved by approximately 20 to 50 points postoperatively across studies, complication rates were low, and reoperations directly attributable to syndesmotic reconstruction were uncommon. The review demonstrates not simply that surgery works but that it works across a remarkably heterogeneous spectrum of presentations, from subtle isolated ligament disruption in elite athletes to long-standing diastasis with post-traumatic arthritis, suggesting that the pathology is more surgically forgiving than the paucity of literature might imply. 24

A key implication of this review is a framework for matching surgical approach to clinical phenotype. Patients with chronic instability in the absence of significant arthritis appear well-served by ligament reconstruction or dynamic stabilization. Tendon graft reconstruction, most commonly using the peroneus longus, was associated with consistently favorable outcomes with mean Karlsson scores in the high 80s and high rates of return to work and activity.12,13,19 When outcomes are examined by technique category, tendon graft reconstruction and arthroscopic-assisted stabilization produced consistently favorable results across heterogeneous patient populations, with suture-button and suture anchor constructs offering comparable functional outcomes and faster return to sport in athletic cohorts. Distal tibiofibular arthrodesis, reserved for the most severe phenotype, was associated with the greatest absolute functional gains from the lowest preoperative baselines, reinforcing its role as a meaningful salvage option rather than a procedure of last resort. Suture-button and internal brace constructs achieved comparable functional outcomes and facilitated early return to sport in athletic populations, although reinjury was reported in a small subset of high-demand athletes, suggesting that implant selection alone may be insufficient in the highest-demand patients.14,16,20 The arthroscopic grading system described by Colcuc et al 20 is notable in this context: although all instability grades improved significantly, Grade III patients achieved lower final AOFAS scores than Grades I and II, indicating that severity of instability at the time of surgery influences the ceiling of functional recovery even when meaningful improvement is still achieved. This has direct implications for counseling patients preoperatively and underscores the value of arthroscopic staging as both a diagnostic and prognostic tool. 17

For patients with chronic instability complicated by post-traumatic arthritis, fibular malunion, or fibular nonunion, distal tibiofibular arthrodesis represents a distinct and underutilized treatment strategy. These patients represent a different clinical phenotype entirely, and their outcomes should not be interpreted alongside those of reconstruction patients. Olson et al 22 reported AOFAS improvement from 37 to 87 in a cohort who had uniformly declined tibiotalar arthrodesis, with normalization of medial clear space and talar tilt and no progression to tibiotalar fusion at a mean of 41 months. This positions distal tibiofibular arthrodesis as a meaningful joint-sparing option in a population that might otherwise face ankle arthrodesis or arthroplasty, and it deserves greater attention in the treatment algorithm than the current literature affords it.

The case of tibiofibular synostosis described by Margulis et al 23 further illustrates that chronic distal tibiofibular pathology does not always manifest as instability. Painful impingement and loss of motion from progressive synostosis represent the pathologic opposite of diastasis, yet arise from the same injury spectrum. Recognition of this phenotype is clinically important, as it requires resection rather than stabilization, and conflating it with instability would lead to inappropriate treatment.

This review also highlights the wide range of time from injury to surgery, spanning from 6 months to >20 years. The available evidence does not support a strict surgical window, and the cases with the longest delays still achieved meaningful functional improvement. However, Kent et al have demonstrated that delayed stabilization of dynamically unstable syndesmotic injuries is associated with worse functional outcomes, and the arthrodesis cohort in this review, representing the most severe end of the chronicity spectrum, had the lowest preoperative AOFAS scores of any group.22,25 Taken together, these findings are consistent with the notion that earlier intervention in appropriate candidates may preserve greater functional potential and reduce the risk of requiring salvage-level procedures, although the available evidence is insufficient to support a definitive recommendation regarding surgical timing.

The heterogeneity of this literature is a finding in itself, not merely a limitation. The absence of a validated classification system for chronic syndesmotic instability means that studies ranging from subtle partial tears to complete diastasis with malunion are grouped under the same diagnostic label, making comparison between techniques effectively impossible with existing data. The variability in outcome measures, AOFAS, Karlsson, FAOS, Maryland Foot Score, SF-36, and Weber score used across 12 studies, compounds this problem. Until the field establishes a shared classification framework and adopts standardized outcome reporting, the literature on this topic will remain difficult to synthesize regardless of study volume. The arthroscopic grading system proposed by Colcuc et al 20 represents the most structured attempt at stratification to date and merits prospective validation as a foundation for future comparative work. A robust classification system for chronic syndesmotic instability should incorporate at minimum the degree of arthroscopic instability, radiographic parameters including tibiofibular clear space and medial clear space, presence or absence of post-traumatic arthritis, fibular alignment, and chronicity of injury, as these variables appear most influential in determining surgical approach and expected functional outcomes based on the available evidence.

This review has several limitations. All included studies were Level IV or V evidence, and the predominance of small retrospective case series and case reports limits generalizability. Additionally, the minimum 6-month follow-up threshold, while pragmatically necessary given the available literature, may be insufficient for a chronic pathologic condition; future prospective studies should adopt a minimum of 1-2 years as standard. Potential cohort overlap between the Grass et al 12 (2003) and Grass and Zwipp 13 (2006) series cannot be excluded. Industry conflicts of interest were present in studies involving suture-button constructs, and the literature search was restricted to English-language publications.26-28

Conclusion

The available evidence, although limited to low-level retrospective studies, suggests that chronic distal tibiofibular syndesmotic instability may be surgically treatable across a range of clinical presentations, and functional improvement has been reported even in longstanding cases. The available evidence is consistent with a phenotype-driven treatment approach, although prospective comparative data are lacking: ligament reconstruction or dynamic stabilization for patients without significant arthritis, and distal tibiofibular arthrodesis as a potential joint-sparing option for those with post-traumatic degenerative changes or fibular malunion. Severity of instability at the time of surgery appears to influence the ceiling of functional recovery, suggesting that earlier operative intervention in appropriate candidates may be beneficial, though this remains to be confirmed in prospective studies. The heterogeneity of the existing literature reflects the absence of a validated classification system and standardized outcome reporting, which remain the primary barriers to advancing evidence-based care for this condition.
