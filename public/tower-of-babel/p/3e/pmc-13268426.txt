Mendelian randomization evidence for a causal link between Epstein–Barr virus antibody levels and head and neck cancer risk
Authors: Jin Qiu, Ming-Feng Li, Yun-Miao Guo, Cai-Feng Yue, Li-Juan Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) has been consistently detected in head and neck cancer (HNC) tissues, suggesting its potential involvement in disease pathogenesis. These observations underscore the need to further investigate the plausible causal relationship between genetically predicted EBV antibody levels and the risk of developing HNC. We performed a 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. Genetic instruments for 5 EBV antibody levels including immunoglobin G, EBV nuclear antigen 1 (EBNA-1), EBV early antigen diffuse (EA-D), viral capsid antigen, and BamHI Z EBV replication activator were derived from a publicly available genome-wide association study dataset. Summary-level genome-wide association study data for HNC and oral and oropharyngeal cancer were obtained independently. Causal inference was performed using inverse-variance weighted (IVW), weighted median, and MR-Egger regression. Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied for multiple testing. Reverse MR analysis, Cochran Q test, MR-Egger intercept test, leave-one-out analysis, and funnel plot were subsequently conducted to assess the robustness of the results. The results of MR analysis revealed that higher genetically predicted EA-D antibody levels were significantly associated with oral and oropharyngeal cancer (IVW odds ratio [OR] = 1.00146, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.00066–1.00227, P = .00035, FDR-adjusted P = .004). The association between EBV EA-D antibody levels and overall HNC was of borderline significance (OR = 1.00132, 95% CI = 1.00012–1.00253, P = .032, FDR-adjusted P = .079). Conversely, elevated levels of EBV EBNA-1 antibody were inversely associated with the risk of HNC (IVW OR = 0.99886, 95% CI = 0.99809–0.99963, P = .00373, FDR-adjusted P = .012,) and oral and oropharyngeal cancer (IVW OR = 0.99897, 95% CI = 0.99836–0.99958, P = .00099, FDR-adjusted P = .005). No significant causal associations were observed for other EBV antibody levels. Reverse MR analysis did not provide evidence for a causal effect of HNC on genetically predicted EBV antibody levels. Sensitivity analyses indicated no major outliers. This study provides suggestive evidence of associations between genetically predicted EBV antibody levels and HNC risk. The inverse associations of EBNA-1 with both HNC subtypes and the positive association of EA-D with oral/oropharyngeal cancer remained significant after FDR correction. The observed effect sizes were modest, and the biological significance of these associations remains uncertain. Further validations in independent populations and mechanistic studies are warranted.

1. Introduction

Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), the first human tumor virus identified in 1964,[1] has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various malignancies including Burkitt lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma,[2] gastric cancer,[3,4] and certain subtypes of Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphomas.[5] EBV infection is highly prevalent worldwide, with seroprevalence estimated to approach 90% in the global population, as routinely detected through the presence of EBV-specific antibodies in serological assays.[6] Commonly measured EBV serological markers include immunoglobin G (IgG) antibodies against viral capsid antigen (VCA), early antigen, and EBV nuclear antigen 1 (EBNA-1), as well as the immunoglobin M antibody against VCA. Although EBV deoxyribonucleic acid has been detected in head and neck cancer (HNC) tissues at varying frequencies,[7–9] its precise role in the pathogenesis of HNC remains incompletely understood. The inconsistent prevalence and heterogeneous findings across studies highlight the need for robust evidence to clarify the potential causal relationship between genetically predicted EBV antibody levels and HNC risk.[10]

It is important to distinguish between direct measures of viral infection or reactivation and serological biomarkers that reflect the host immune response to EBV exposure. Antibody levels against EBV antigens may capture genetic variation in the host immune response rather than direct viral effects per se. This distinction is critical for the accurate interpretation of causal inferences in Mendelian randomization (MR) studies.

To address this knowledge gap, the present study employed MR, an established analytical approach in observational epidemiology that leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes.[11] Utilizing summary statistics from large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS), the present study aimed to assess the potential causal association between genetically predicted EBV antibody levels, reflecting the host immune response to EBV exposure, and the risk of developing HNC, with a particular focus on oral and oropharyngeal cancer.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

In this study, a 2-sample MR analysis was performed to assess the potential causal relationship between genetically predicted EBV antibody levels and the risk of HNC. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with EBV-related antibody levels were chosen as genetic IVs. This MR analysis was grounded in 3 core assumptions: the IVs are strongly associated with the exposure of interest, namely EBV-related antibodies; the IVs are not associated with any confounding factors that could potentially bias the exposure-outcome relationship; the IVs influence the outcome (development of HNC) exclusively through the exposure (EBV-related antibodies) without any direct or indirect effects other than those mediated by the exposure. To enhance the reliability and robustness of the causal inferences, a series of sensitivity analyses were conducted to validate the stability of the results.

2.2. GWAS summary data for EBV and HNC

We retrieved GWAS summary datasets from the GWAS Catalog database for 5 serum anti-EBV antibodies, including Anti-EBV IgG seropositivity,[12] EBV Epstein–Barr virus early antigen diffuse (EA-D) antibody levels, EBV EBNA-1 antibody levels, EBV VCA p18 antibody levels, and EBV BamHI Z EBV replication activator (ZEBRA) antibody levels. These datasets collectively comprised 41,179 samples. For HNC, we obtained summary statistics from the integrative epidemiology unit OpenGWAS project database.[13] The HNC GWAS dataset included a total sample of 372,855 participants (1106 cases and 371,975 controls). The oral and oropharyngeal cancer dataset included a total sample of 373,122 participants (839 cases and 372,016 controls). The exposure and outcome datasets were derived from independent GWAS studies with no sample overlap. A detailed summary of the selected datasets is provided in Table 1. Since all data were sourced from publicly available databases, ethical approval was not required for this study.

2.3. Selection of genetic IVs

A rigorous screening protocol was implemented to identify SNPs that satisfied the 3 core assumptions of MR analysis as valid IVs. First, the SNPs were required to exhibit a robust association with the exposure (EBV-related antibody levels), with a genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10−6 to ensure sufficient instrument strength. This threshold balances stringency with the need for adequate genetic instruments, consistent with previous MR studies of serological traits where instrument availability is limited compared to conventional GWAS.[14] Second, to mitigate the influence of linkage disequilibrium among selected SNPs, we enforced an linkage disequilibrium thresholds (R2 < 0.001) within a 10,000-kb window, thereby ensuring the independence of IVs for each exposure. Third, potential confounding SNPs were identified and excluded by cross-referencing with the PhenoScanner database.[15] To maintain data consistency, exposure and outcome datasets were harmonized by resolving palindromic and ambiguous SNPs with discordant allele coding. Additionally, the F-statistic for each SNP was computed to assess instrument strength, and SNPs with an F-statistic < 10 were excluded.[16] Only SNPs that met all these stringent criteria were retained for subsequent 2-sample MR analyses.

2.4. Statistical analyses

A 2-sample MR approach was employed to evaluate the causal effect of genetically predicted EBV antibody levels on the risk of HNC. All effect sizes are reported per standard deviation (SD) increase in genetically predicted antibody levels. The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, the most widely validated and commonly utilized analytical approach in MR studies,[17] served as the primary analytical strategy. To enhance result robustness and corroboration, complementary MR methods including MR-Egger regression and the weighted median approach were additionally applied.[18]

Given the multiple testing across 5 antibody traits and 2 outcomes (10 comparisons), we applied Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction to control for multiple testing. An FDR-adjusted P < .05 was considered statistically significant. As a sensitivity analysis, we also performed Bonferroni correction (significance threshold: P < .05/10 = .005).

A comprehensive suite of sensitivity analyses was conducted to assess the validity of causal inferences: Cochran Q test was performed, with P < .05 indicating the presence of statistically significant heterogeneity among genetic instruments[19]; the Mendelian randomization pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) global test was applied, where P > .05 suggests no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy[20]; leave-one-out analyses were performed to determine whether the observed causal association was driven by any individual SNP.[21] To validate the directionality of the inferred causal relationship, a reverse MR analysis was conducted,[22] with HNC designated as the exposure and genetically predicted EBV antibody levels as the outcome. All 2-sample MR analyses were implemented using the R package “TwoSampleMR” within the open-source statistical software R (version 4.2.1).[23]

The IVW method, serving as the primary analytical approach, revealed that genetically predicted EBV EA-D antibody levels showed a positive association with overall HNC (odds ratio [OR] = 1.00132, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.00012–1.00253, P = .03166, FDR-adjusted P = .079, per SD increase) and oral and oropharyngeal cancer (OR = 1.00146, 95% CI = 1.00066–1.00227, P = .00035, FDR-adjusted P = .004, per SD increase). After FDR correction for multiple testing (10 comparisons), the association between EA-D antibody levels and oral/oropharyngeal cancer remained statistically significant, whereas the association with overall HNC was no longer significant (FDR-adjusted P = .079).

In contrast, genetically predicted EBV EBNA-1 antibody levels exhibited an inverse association with overall HNC (OR = 0.99886, 95% CI = 0.99809–0.99963, P = .00373, FDR-adjusted P = .012, per SD increase) and oral and oropharyngeal cancer (OR = 0.99897, 95% CI = 0.99836–0.99958, P = .00099, FDR-adjusted P = .005, per SD increase). Both associations remained statistically significant after FDR correction. No significant associations were observed for the other 3 EBV antibody levels (Anti-EBV IgG seropositivity, VCA p18, and ZEBRA) with HNC or oral and oropharyngeal cancer after FDR adjustment.

The results of all 3 MR methods were visualized in scatter plots (Fig. S1), where a positive slope indicated a direction of association between EBV antibodies and cancer risk. Funnel plots (Fig. S2) were used to assess potential bias in the IVs, though interpretation was limited by the relatively small number of IVs. Forest plots (Fig. S3) provided detailed breakdowns of individual SNP effects and their aggregated contributions across the MR analyses.

Notably, the effect sizes observed in this study were modest, with ORs close to 1 (ranging from 0.99886 to 1.00146 per SD increase). While the associations for EBNA-1 and EA-D (with oral cancer) achieved statistical significance even after FDR correction, the biological and clinical relevance of such small effect sizes remains uncertain and requires further investigation.

3.2. Sensitivity analysis of the MR results

To strengthen the robustness and reliability of causal inferences, comprehensive sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate potential biases and validate the stability of the 2-sample MR results.[25] As summarized in Table 3, heterogeneity tests revealed statistically significant heterogeneity for the associations between EBV EA-D antibody levels and HNC (Cochran Q P = .036978 for MR-Egger and P = .060591 for IVW) and between EBV EBNA-1 antibody levels and HNC (Cochran Q P = .037923 for MR-Egger and P = .040824 for IVW). These findings indicate the presence of heterogeneity among genetic instruments for these specific exposure-outcome pairs. For the other antibody traits (Anti-EBV IgG, VCA, and ZEBRA) with both HNC and oral and oropharyngeal cancer, P > .05 indicated no statistically significant heterogeneity.

Evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR-Egger intercept test and MR-PRESSO global test.[26] For Anti-EBV IgG, EA-D, VCA, and ZEBRA antibody levels, no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was detected across both outcomes, as evidenced by MR-Egger intercept P > .05 and MR-PRESSO global test P > .05. However, for EBV EBNA-1 antibody levels with HNC, potential horizontal pleiotropy was suggested by the MR-Egger intercept test (P = .0456), although the MR-PRESSO global test did not detect significant pleiotropy (P = .0456 for MR-Egger vs P > .05 for MR-PRESSO). Despite this borderline evidence of pleiotropy for EBNA-1, the weighted median estimate was consistent with the IVW estimate for this exposure-outcome pair (OR = 0.99848 vs OR = 0.99886 for HNC), suggesting that pleiotropy may not have substantially biased the primary analysis.

No significant outlier SNPs were identified by MR-PRESSO for any of the exposure-outcome pairs. Leave-one-out analyses were performed to assess the impact of each individual SNP on the overall causal associations (Fig. S4). The consistency between the leave-one-out results, with no single SNP dramatically altering the overall estimates, supports the robustness of our findings. However, the small number of instruments for some exposures (particularly EA-D with 8–9 SNPs) limits the statistical power of these sensitivity analyses. In the reverse MR analysis (with HNC or oral and oropharyngeal cancer as the exposure and genetically predicted EBV antibody levels as the outcome), no significant causal relationships were observed, supporting the directionality of the primary findings.

4. Discussion

This study investigated the causal relationship between genetically predicted EBV antibody levels and HNC using MR analysis. By leveraging GWAS data and employing a robust 2-sample MR methodology, we aimed to determine whether genetically predicted EBV antibody levels are causally associated with the risk of HNC. Our primary findings demonstrate suggestive evidence of associations between EBV EA-D and EBNA-1 antibodies and the risk of HNC, whereas reverse MR analysis provided no evidence of a reverse association. These results contribute to the understanding of the potential role of EBV immune response in HNC and highlight the need for further mechanistic investigations.

Our MR analysis revealed suggestive associations between specific genetically predicted EBV antibody levels and HNC and oral and oropharyngeal cancer. Specifically, elevated EA-D antibody levels were significantly associated with increased risks of HNC (OR = 1.00132, 95% CI = 1.00012–1.00253, P = .03166) and oral and oropharyngeal cancer (OR = 1.00146, 95% CI = 1.00066–1.00227, P = .00035). After FDR correction for multiple testing (10 comparisons), the association between EA-D antibody levels and oral/oropharyngeal cancer remained statistically significant (FDR-adjusted P = .004), whereas the association with overall HNC was no longer significant (FDR-adjusted P = .079), suggesting that this finding should be interpreted with caution. The observed effect sizes are modest (ORs close to 1), and the biological significance of these associations remains uncertain. While the narrow CIs indicate precision of estimates, the clinical relevance of such small effect sizes requires further investigation. EA-D is an early antigen expressed during EBV reactivation,[27] and antibody elevations may reflect periods of viral activity. However, it is important to note that genetically predicted antibody levels reflect host immune response variation rather than direct viral infection or reactivation per se, and this distinction limits the extent to which our findings can be interpreted as evidence regarding EBV infection or viral activity.

In contrast, higher EBNA-1 antibody levels were associated with reduced risk of HNC (OR = 0.99886, 95% CI = 0.99809–0.99963, P = .00373 and oral and oropharyngeal cancer (OR = 0.99897, 95% CI = 0.99836–0.99958, P = .00099). Both of these associations remained statistically significant after FDR correction (FDR-adjusted P = .012 and P = .005, respectively). This protective effect may stem from the role of EBNA-1 in latent EBV infection and immune surveillance.[28] However, given that antibody levels reflect host immune response rather than direct viral effects, alternative explanations cannot be excluded. Elevated levels of EBNA-1 antibodies could signify a robust immune response that suppresses EBV reactivation, thereby reducing oncogenesis risk.[29] Alternatively, antibody-associated genetic variants may capture host immune response traits rather than reflecting direct viral effects on carcinogenesis.[30] For other EBV-related antibodies (e.g., anti-VCA p18 and anti-ZEBRA), no significant causal associations with HNC were observed. This null finding may reflect differences in antigen immunogenicity, temporal patterns of antibody production during infection, or their distinct roles in immune modulation.[31] Methodological factors, such as the selection of genetic instruments and antibody measurement specificity, might also have contributed to these results.[32]

The present study provides novel insights by leveraging MR analysis to infer causality rather than relying solely on associative evidence. By using genetic variants as IVs for EBV antibody levels, we effectively mitigated confounding and reverse causation, which commonly appear as limitations of traditional observational studies. However, it is important to note that our findings reflect associations with genetically predicted antibody levels, which may capture host immune response traits rather than direct viral effects. This distinction limits the extent to which our results can be interpreted as evidence regarding EBV infection or viral reactivation per se. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as evidence regarding genetic determinants of antibody response and their relationship to HNC risk, rather than as direct evidence of viral infection or viral-driven oncogenesis.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, potential MR biases such as weak instrument bias and residual pleiotropy may have influenced the results,[33] despite our use of sensitivity analyses. All F-statistics exceeded the threshold of 10, indicating sufficient instrument strength and minimal weak instrument bias. Second, the generalizability of findings to diverse populations remains uncertain due to variations in EBV prevalence and host genetic backgrounds. Third, the observed effect sizes are modest (ORs close to 1), and their biological and clinical relevance remains uncertain. The distinction between antibody biomarkers and actual viral infection should be carefully considered when interpreting these findings. Importantly, our genetic instruments predict antibody levels, which may reflect host immune response variation rather than direct viral infection or reactivation. This distinction limits the extent to which our findings can be interpreted as evidence regarding EBV infection per se. Fourth, after FDR correction for multiple testing, the association between EA-D antibody levels and overall HNC was no longer statistically significant, highlighting the need for cautious interpretation and further validation in independent cohorts. The borderline significance of this association in the original analysis (P = .032) and its loss of significance after correction suggests that this finding may be a false positive and should be interpreted with caution. Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies and experimental models to validate these associations and elucidate the biological mechanisms linking EBV antibody profiles to HNC pathogenesis. Investigating the interplay between EBV reactivation dynamics, immune surveillance, and genetic susceptibility will be critical for advancing precision prevention strategies.

5. Conclusions

In summary, this study provides suggestive evidence of associations between genetically predicted EBV antibody levels and the risk of HNC. The inverse associations between EBNA-1 antibody levels and both HNC subtypes remained significant after FDR correction for multiple testing, as did the association between EA-D antibody levels and oral/oropharyngeal cancer. However, the association between EA-D and overall HNC was not significant after FDR correction. While these findings contribute to our understanding of HNC etiology, the observed effect sizes were modest, and their biological significance remains uncertain. Importantly, our genetic instruments predict antibody levels reflecting host immune response rather than direct viral infection; thus, our findings should be interpreted as evidence regarding antibody response and HNC risk, rather than as direct evidence of viral-driven oncogenesis. Further validation in independent populations is required to confirm these associations and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms.
