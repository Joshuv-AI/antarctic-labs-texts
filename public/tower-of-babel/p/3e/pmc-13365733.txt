Diagnostic accuracy of the droplet digital PCR POLE mutation test in endometrial cancer: comparison with Sanger sequencing and NGS
Authors: Yung-Taek Ouh, Hyun-Woong Cho, Kyung-Jin Min, Jae-Kwan Lee, Yikyeong Chun, Jin Hwa Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) for detecting polymerase epsilon (POLE) mutations in endometrial cancer (EC), comparing it with traditional Sanger sequencing and next-generation sequencing (NGS).MethodsTumor DNA from 132 patients with EC was analyzed initially using both ddPCR and Sanger sequencing. Discordant cases (n=20) underwent further analysis using NGS as the reference standard. This study compared the sensitivity, specificity, and overall concordance of ddPCR against Sanger sequencing results, validated by NGS.ResultsAmong the 20 discrepant samples, ddPCR detected POLE mutations in 15 cases (75%). All ddPCR results matched exactly with NGS findings, including the detection of 11 low-frequency mutations missed by Sanger sequencing. ddPCR also corrected 2 false-positive results initially identified by Sanger sequencing. No false negatives were detected by ddPCR.ConclusionddPCR demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity in detecting POLE mutations compared to Sanger sequencing and showed perfect agreement with NGS. With its rapid turnaround, simplicity, and cost-effectiveness, ddPCR is highly suitable for routine clinical use, potentially enhancing patient management and outcomes in EC.Keywords: Endometrial Neoplasms, High-Throughput Nucleotide Sequencing, Mutation, DNA, Polymerase Chain Reaction

INTRODUCTION

Endometrial cancer (EC) is one of the most common malignancies in women, with over 417,000 new cases diagnosed worldwide in 2020, making it the sixth most frequently diagnosed cancer in women [1]. Notably, EC incidence has steadily increased in many countries in recent years [2], largely driven by the rising prevalence of obesity and other risk factors such as early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, and diabetes [3]. This epidemiologic trend is concerning because EC encompasses a heterogeneous group of tumors with variable clinical behavior [4,5]. While many patients present with early-stage, low-grade disease that is curable with surgery, a significant subset have high-risk features or aggressive histologic subtypes that portend poor outcomes [6,7]. Effective prognostic stratification of EC is therefore critically important to guide appropriate therapy.

In the past decade, there has been a paradigm shift in EC classification with the recognition of distinct molecular subtypes that transcend traditional histopathological categories. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project in 2013 performed an integrated genomic analysis of EC and identified 4 molecular subgroups: polymerase epsilon ultramutated (POLEmut), microsatellite instability hypermutated (mismatch repair deficient [MMRd]), p53-abnormal (p53abn, copy-number high), and no specific molecular profile (NSMP, copy-number low) [8,9]. The prognostic significance of these molecular subtypes is strong and exceeds that of conventional grade alone. Notably, POLEmut tumors have remarkably favorable clinical outcomes despite being high-grade endometroid carcinomas, whereas p53abn (usually serous or high-grade) carcinomas carry the highest risk of recurrence and death [10]. The other 2 subgroups (MMRd and NSMP) demonstrate intermediate prognoses. Numerous studies have validated this molecular classification as a powerful predictor of outcome. For example, the Vancouver Proactive Molecular Risk Classifier for EC (ProMisE) trial confirmed that classifying ECs into these 4 subgroups produces clearly divergent survival curves (p<0.001), with POLEmut cases showing the best survival and p53abn cases the worst [11].

Following the seminal TCGA study, efforts were made to translate the molecular classification into a practical clinical algorithm [12]. One such effort was the development of the ProMisE, which uses a combination of targeted sequencing and immunohistochemistry (IHC) to assign the TCGA subtypes [11]. In ProMisE, tumor tissue is evaluated by sequencing for polymerase epsilon (POLE) exonuclease domain mutations and by IHC for MMR protein loss and abnormal p53 expression, thereby recapitulating the TCGA-defined POLEmut, MMRd, p53abn, and NSMP groups. This approach was shown to be feasible and reproducible—Talhouk et al. [11] successfully applied ProMisE to 319 cases, identifying the 4 distinct prognostic groups with 100% subtype assignability and significant outcome separation. Other groups, such as the PORTEC consortium, have developed similar surrogate marker algorithms, and collectively these studies established the clinical validity of molecular classification in EC.

Major clinical guidelines, including European Society of Gynaecological Oncology/European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology/European Society of Pathology 2021 and European Society for Medical Oncology 2022, now incorporate molecular classification to guide risk assessment and adjuvant therapy [13]. The 2023 Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging system further integrates molecular profiles, highlighting their importance in clinical decision-making. However, routine clinical adoption of molecular classification faces challenges, notably in detecting POLE mutations, which traditionally rely on costly, complex, and time-consuming sequencing methods (next-generation sequencing [NGS] and Sanger sequencing). Droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) offers a promising alternative, providing rapid, sensitive, and cost-effective detection of POLE mutations [10]. Recent studies indicate high concordance between ddPCR and sequencing methods, with ddPCR detecting mutations missed by Sanger sequencing [10].

This study evaluates the diagnostic accuracy of the ddPCR POLE mutation test compared to Sanger sequencing and NGS, using a representative EC cohort. The findings provide crucial insights into ddPCR's clinical utility, potentially streamlining molecular classification and informing therapeutic strategies in routine clinical practice.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

1. Patient selection and samples

This study included 132 patients diagnosed with EC who underwent surgical resection at our institution. All tumor samples were formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues. The biospecimens and data used in this study were provided by the Biobank of Korea University Guro Hospital. Genomic DNA was extracted from FFPE tumor sections using standard column-based methods. Molecular testing for POLE exonuclease domain mutations was initially performed using both Sanger sequencing and ddPCR. From this cohort, 20 cases were identified with discrepant results between Sanger sequencing and ddPCR. These 20 samples subsequently underwent NGS analysis for final confirmation of POLE mutation status. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Guro Hospital (2024GR0225), with a waiver of informed consent due to the retrospective use of de-identified samples. The 20 samples selected for additional NGS confirmation represented only those with discordant results between Sanger sequencing and ddPCR, which could introduce a selection bias. All analyses were performed within 6 months of sample collection to minimize potential DNA degradation.

2. Sanger sequencing for POLE

Conventional Sanger sequencing was used to screen for POLE exonuclease domain mutations in exons 9–14 of the POLE gene, which encompass the major hotspot regions [14]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was performed on tumor DNA using primers flanking these exons (covering codons P286, S297, V411, A456, S459). The PCR products were then sequenced using the BigDye® Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit and run on an ABI 3730XL capillary sequencer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to standard protocols. Sequencing chromatograms were analyzed for the 5 known pathogenic POLE hotspot mutations (p.P286R, p.S297F, p.V411L, p.A456P, and p.S459F), including any other coding variants in the amplified regions. A case was considered “POLE mutation-positive” by Sanger if a heterozygous variant corresponding to a known pathogenic hotspot was identified on the electropherogram.

3. ddPCR assay for POLE mutations

All 132 cases were initially tested for POLE mutations using a ddPCR platform for higher sensitivity. This study employed the QX200 ddPCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) in conjunction with a commercially available mutation assay kit (Droplex POLE Mutation Test; Gencurix, Seoul, Korea). This assay is designed to detect 5 recurrent pathogenic point mutations in the POLE exonuclease domain: p.P286R, p.S297F, p.V411L, p.A456P, and p.S459F [14]. These 5 hotspot mutations account for the great majority of known POLE-ultramutated cases in EC and are the key targets recommended for molecular testing in current endometrial carcinoma classification guidelines. The ddPCR was performed according to the kit manufacturer’s protocol and previously established methods [10]. Briefly, each reaction mixture (20 µL) contained the Droplex POLE mutation primer/probe mix (targeting the 5 mutations) and 10–20 ng of template DNA. Droplet emulsions were generated using the QX200 droplet generator, partitioning each sample into ~20,000 nanoliter-sized droplets. PCR amplification was carried out in a 96-well plate on a thermal cycler under the recommended cycling conditions (initial enzyme activation, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation and annealing/extension, then droplet stabilization). After PCR, droplets were read individually on the QX200 droplet fluorescence reader and analyzed with QuantaSoft software (Bio-Rad). For each of the 5 target mutations, fluorescent probes distinguish mutant alleles from wild-type; droplets were classified as positive or negative based on fluorescence amplitude thresholds determined by control samples. The ddPCR output was the absolute copy number of mutant DNA fragments per 20 µL reaction and the mutation allele fraction (termed mutation index [MI]) for each target. A sample was interpreted as “POLE mutation-positive” in the ddPCR assay if the specific mutation probe yielded at least the minimum number of positive droplets above the assay’s limit of blank (generally corresponding to ≥6 mutant copies per 20 µL, or MI ≥0.3%) [10]. Each run included no-template negative controls and positive control DNA for assay validation.

4. NGS (TruSight Oncology 500)

NGS was performed on tumor DNA samples with discordant ddPCR and Sanger sequencing results using the TruSight Oncology 500 (TSO500) panel (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), which includes the POLE gene. For each case, 100 ng of FFPE-extracted DNA was fragmented (~200 bp) using a Covaris M220 ultrasonicator. Library preparation followed the manufacturer’s protocol, including end-repair, adapter ligation, PCR amplification, and hybrid capture enrichment. Prepared libraries (24 per run) were quantified, pooled, and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 550 with paired-end reads. Base calls were converted to FASTQ files and analyzed using the TSO500 Local Run Manager software, which includes alignment, variant calling, and confidence filtering. To evaluate POLE mutations, 2 filtering strategies were used: 1) a PASS filter for high-confidence pathogenic variants and 2) a more sensitive LowSupport filter retaining hotspot variants with variant allele frequency (VAF) >0.1%, aligning with ddPCR sensitivity. This approach enabled robust detection and cross-platform comparison of both high- and low-frequency POLE mutations.

5. Data analysis and comparison of methods

The POLE mutation status results obtained by ddPCR were compared with those from Sanger sequencing and NGS for the 20 selected cases. Concordance and discrepancies between methods were recorded for each of the 5 hotspot mutations. Given prior evidence that ddPCR can detect low-frequency POLE mutations with high sensitivity and concordance (≈96.7%) relative to traditional sequencing, NGS results were utilized as the gold standard to confirm mutations identified by both ddPCR and Sanger sequencing. This study specifically noted any cases where ddPCR detected mutations missed by Sanger sequencing or vice versa. Statistical analysis was primarily descriptive. Concordance rates were calculated as the percentage of cases with matching results across methods. Furthermore, 2-by-2 comparisons were made between each method (ddPCR vs. Sanger, ddPCR vs. NGS, and Sanger vs. NGS), and Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to assess agreement beyond chance. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (v25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value <0.05 was considered significant for any statistical tests of discordance.

RESULTS

The clinical and pathological characteristics of patients stratified by molecular classification (POLEmut, MMRd, p53abn, and NSMP) are summarized in Table 1. Patients with p53abn subtype were significantly older (mean age, 65.41±9.88 years) compared to those with the MMRd subtype (mean age, 57.07±7.83 years; p=0.039). A statistically significant difference in FIGO stage distribution was also observed among the groups (p=0.002). Specifically, advanced stage (FIGO stage III–IV) was more frequent in the p53abn subgroup (47.1%) compared to POLEmut (6.7%), MMRd (7.4%), and NSMP (9.6%) subgroups. Histologically, all tumors in the POLEmut (100%), MMRd (100%), and most tumors in the NSMP (98.6%) subgroups showed endometrioid histology. In contrast, only 52.9% of tumors in the p53abn subgroup were endometrioid, with the remainder (47.1%) being non-endometrioid histologies (p<0.001). A higher proportion of high-grade (grade 3) tumors was significantly associated with the p53abn subgroup (64.7%) compared to POLEmut (0%), MMRd (11.1%), and NSMP (4.1%) subgroups (p<0.001). The p53abn subgroup also showed a higher rate of deep myometrial invasion (≥50%), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (p=0.085). Lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) was significantly more prevalent in the p53abn subgroup (70.6%) compared with POLEmut (20.0%), MMRd (29.6%), and NSMP (13.7%) subgroups (p<0.001). Similarly, the rate of lymph node metastasis was significantly higher in the p53abn subgroup (23.5%) relative to the other subtypes (POLEmut 6.7%, MMRd 7.4%, NSMP 4.1%; p=0.006). Regarding adjuvant treatment modalities, no statistically significant difference was noted across the groups (p=0.154). However, recurrence rates were significantly different (p<0.001), with recurrence observed in 47.1% of patients with p53abn, compared to 0% in POLEmut, 3.7% in MMRd, and 4.1% in NSMP subgroups. Likewise, mortality was significantly associated with molecular subgroups (p<0.001), with a notably higher death rate in patients with the p53abn subtype (43.8%) compared to no deaths in the POLEmut and MMRd subgroups and 6.1% in the NSMP subgroup. Overall, these findings highlight the distinct clinicopathologic profiles and prognostic outcomes associated with each molecular subtype of EC.

Table 2 shows the comparative analysis of clinicopathological features between the POLEmut subgroup and other molecular subtypes in EC. Advanced FIGO stages (III–IV) appeared less frequently in the POLEmut subgroup (6.7%) compared to other subtypes (14.5%), though this difference was not statistically significant (odds ratio [OR]=0.420; 95% confidence interval [CI]=0.052–3.407; p=0.692). Histologically, all tumors in the POLEmut subgroup exhibited endometrioid histology (100%), while other histological types were observed in 7.7% of the other molecular subtypes (OR=1.139; 95% CI=1.066–1.216; p=0.597). Regarding myometrial invasion depth, deep myometrial invasion (≥50%) was less frequently observed in the POLEmut subgroup (22.2%) than in other molecular subgroups (39.5%), although this was not statistically significant (OR=0.438; 95% CI=0.085–2.241; p=0.474). Similarly, LVSI presence did not significantly differ between the POLEmut subgroup (20.0%) and others (25.6%; OR=0.725; 95% CI=0.191–2.745; p=0.761). Lymph node metastasis rates were also comparable between POLEmut cases (75.0%) and other subtypes (67.9%; OR=1.421; 95% CI=0.129–15.635; p=1.000). Adjuvant treatment usage showed virtually identical proportions between POLEmut (46.7%) and other molecular subgroups (47.0%; OR=0.986; 95% CI=0.336–2.897; p=1.000). No recurrence or mortality was observed within the POLEmut group during the study period, whereas recurrence occurred in 10.3% and mortality in 10.4% of patients in other molecular subtypes. However, these differences did not reach statistical significance (recurrence OR=1.143; 95% CI=1.068–1.223; p=0.359; death OR=1.158; 95% CI=1.075–1.247; p=0.356).

Table 3 presents a comparison of POLE mutation detection results obtained from ddPCR, Sanger sequencing, and NGS using a standard high-confidence variant calling filter. Among the 20 evaluated samples, 15 (75%) harbored pathogenic POLE mutations, while 5 (25%) showed no detectable pathogenic variants across all methods. Pathogenicity classification was based on annotations from established genomic databases, including ClinVar and COSMIC. Complete concordance among ddPCR, Sanger sequencing, and NGS was observed in 4 samples, which carried well-characterized pathogenic mutations—P286R (n=2) and V411L (n=2). These cases exhibited relatively high VAFs (2.57%–46.25%) and mutation indices (MIs: 1.12%–26.20%). In 5 additional samples, pathogenic variants (P286R, V411L, or S459F) were detected by ddPCR and NGS but not by Sanger sequencing; these variants demonstrated intermediate VAFs (1.71%–6.63%) and MIs (0.90%–8.09%). Furthermore, 6 samples showed pathogenic mutations—P286R (n=3), V411L (n=2), and A456P (n=1)—that were detected only by ddPCR and NGS, with relatively low VAFs (0.18%–3.16%) and MIs (0.11%–2.93%). These low-frequency variants were not identified by Sanger sequencing, highlighting its limited sensitivity. In contrast, 5 samples were consistently negative for pathogenic mutations across all 3 platforms, supporting the specificity of ddPCR. Two samples initially reported as harboring pathogenic mutations (V411L and S459F) by Sanger sequencing were reclassified as false positives, as neither ddPCR nor NGS confirmed these findings. Additionally, 3 variants identified by Sanger sequencing—2 P286S and 1 S297Y—were determined to be non-pathogenic and outside the detection scope of the ddPCR assay. These classifications were corroborated by NGS. Further details regarding the original and revised Sanger sequencing interpretations, including their concordance with ddPCR, are presented in Table S1. This table highlights instances where initial Sanger results were either corrected or reclassified upon re-evaluation, reinforcing the need for confirmatory testing in cases with borderline or ambiguous findings. Overall, these results emphasize the superior sensitivity and specificity of ddPCR compared to Sanger sequencing, particularly for low-frequency mutations, and demonstrate its high concordance with NGS when applying standard filtering criteria. To address potential concerns about overestimation of concordance due to selective NGS testing, this study further detailed the cross-platform results in Table S2. This table stratifies all 20 adjudicated cases by TSO500 filter tiers (PASS vs. LowSupport), variant allele frequencies, and ddPCR target copies, providing full transparency of case-level outcomes. Notably, the LowSupport tier identified 6 additional low-VAF hotspot variants that were all concordant with ddPCR, while the PASS tier maintained high specificity without discordant positives. These data indicate that incorporating the research-tier LowSupport calls improved interpretive sensitivity without inflating the predefined PASS-based concordance metrics. Together, the stratified analysis in Table S2 reinforces that our ddPCR–NGS concordance reflects methodological robustness rather than overestimation.

Table 4 summarizes the comparison of POLE mutation detection results between ddPCR and NGS using a more sensitive variant filtering strategy, which retained hotspot variants with a VAF >0.1%. Under this permissive filter, all 15 pathogenic mutations identified by ddPCR were also detected by NGS, demonstrating perfect concordance between the 2 methods. These included low-frequency variants that were missed by Sanger sequencing but consistently captured by both ddPCR and filtered NGS. Importantly, no additional pathogenic variants were identified by NGS beyond those already detected by ddPCR, and all 5 mutation-negative cases remained concordant between the 2 platforms. These findings confirm that ddPCR reliably detects low-frequency POLE mutations with sensitivity comparable to that of NGS under enhanced filtering conditions.

To further validate the consistency between ddPCR and NGS, a correlation analysis was performed between the MI measured by ddPCR and the VAF from NGS. A strong linear relationship was observed (r=0.9588, p<0.001), as illustrated in Fig. S1.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility of the Droplex ddPCR POLE mutation test in comparison with Sanger sequencing and NGS in EC. These results demonstrate that ddPCR offers a highly sensitive and specific approach for detecting clinically relevant POLE exonuclease domain mutations. Notably, ddPCR exhibited complete concordance with NGS, particularly in detecting low-frequency variants, while Sanger sequencing failed to identify several clinically important mutations. Additionally, clinicopathological and survival analyses reinforced the favorable prognosis associated with POLEmut tumors, emphasizing the significance of accurate POLE mutation detection in clinical practice.

This study offers several notable strengths in the context of existing literature. First, this study performed a 3-platform comparison of POLE mutation testing—ddPCR vs. Sanger sequencing vs. NGS—on the same cohort of tumors, which is unprecedented in EC research. Previous studies have typically evaluated ddPCR against a single sequencing method (e.g., ddPCR vs. Sanger) or used NGS sparingly for confirmation [10]. By directly comparing all 3 platforms, this study provides a robust validation of ddPCR’s clinical accuracy. This design allowed us to confirm that ddPCR can detect POLE mutations that traditional Sanger often misses while accurately matching the results of sensitive NGS assays. Second, we grounded our analysis in real-world data from a single-institution cohort (n=132), identifying POLE mutations in approximately 10%–11% of cases, which closely aligns with the mutation frequency (10.8%) reported in a larger previous study by Kim et al. [10] This similarity reinforces the representativeness and validity of this dataset in reflecting the broader patient population. For example, Kim et al. [10] analyzed 240 cases with a ddPCR assay and reported POLE mutations in ~10.8%, a prevalence like the ~10% rate in this series, underscoring the representative size and diversity of our sample. Furthermore, TSO500 data were analyzed under 2 manufacturer-defined tiers: PASS (validated clinical thresholds; limit of detection ≈5% VAF) and LowSupport (a research-tier analysis with extended sensitivity after FFPE noise correction; nominal VAF >0.1%). PASS governed clinical reporting according to the predefined analysis plan, whereas LowSupport did not override PASS results but rather augmented interpretation by 1) corroborating ddPCR low-copy positives within the 5-hotspot space and 2) flagging low-VAF loci for careful review. In our cohort, this dual-filter approach successfully rescued 6 low-VAF hotspot calls—A456P×1, V411L×2, and P286R×3—in agreement with ddPCR, while maintaining the high specificity of the PASS tier. Therefore, incorporating the LowSupport layer strengthened cross-platform adjudication and analytic transparency without altering the prespecified primary outcomes. Fourth, the latest clinical frameworks were integrated into this study, using the updated FIGO 2023 staging system and the ProMisE molecular classification for EC. Incorporating FIGO 2023 staging (which uniquely factors in tumor molecular features) ensures the results align with contemporary risk stratification models [15]. Likewise, classifying tumors by ProMisE subtypes (POLE-ultramutated, MMR-deficient, p53-abnormal, NSMP) situates these findings within a proven prognostic paradigm. Notably, to our knowledge, this is among the first studies to apply the 2023 FIGO criteria alongside ProMisE; this up-to-date approach increases the clinical relevance of the conclusions. Finally, the multifaceted scope of this work – spanning molecular diagnostics and prognostic classification – builds on prior foundational research (e.g., TCGA and subsequent studies) that advocated integrating molecular subclasses into EC management. Future studies should consider expanding ddPCR-based testing to include additional mutations relevant to EC (e.g., TP53, PTEN mutations).

Recent studies have further emphasized that comprehensive molecular profiling not only refines prognostic stratification but also informs emerging therapeutic strategies in EC. Particularly, novel insights into actionable molecular pathways—such as DNA damage repair deficiency, PI3K/AKT signaling, and immunologic responsiveness—are reshaping treatment paradigms and enabling personalized management based on molecular subtype [16,17]. Integrating ddPCR-based POLE testing within this evolving framework may therefore facilitate rapid, cost-effective molecular triage, ensuring that patients with POLE-ultramutated tumors, who typically have an excellent prognosis, are appropriately managed with de-escalated therapy while others can benefit from molecularly guided targeted or immunotherapeutic approaches.

These findings are broadly consistent with previous reports, including Kim et al. [10] and Joe et al. [14], while also offering new insights. POLE mutations were identified in approximately 10%–11% of cases, aligning with Kim et al.’s [10] 10.8% detection rate using ddPCR and falling within the 10%–16% range reported in other cohorts. ddPCR showed superior analytical sensitivity compared to Sanger sequencing. Several cases were missed by Sanger but correctly identified by ddPCR and confirmed by NGS, echoing Kim et al.’s [10] finding, where 8 Sanger-negative cases were ddPCR-positive and mostly validated by NGS. Joe et al. [14] reported similar high concordance (~96.7%) between ddPCR and NGS, further supporting ddPCR's reliability even in the absence of Sanger.

Importantly, this study found no cases where ddPCR missed a mutation detected by NGS, reinforcing its robustness when mutations fall within its 5-target design. In contrast, Sanger produced 2 probable false-positive results in the dataset, which were not corroborated by ddPCR or NGS—highlighting a potential for overcalling or sequencing artifacts. Beyond methodological limits, pre-analytical and FFPE-related factors—such as DNA degradation, low input, or primer mis-priming—may have contributed to spurious signals. These orthogonal negatives were documented in the Results and Discussion as sufficient evidence for transparent reclassification. These observations align with broader evidence that ddPCR combines high sensitivity with strong specificity for hotspot mutation detection [18,19]. Collectively, this work complements existing literature while providing direct 3-platform comparisons, further validating ddPCR as a practical and efficient alternative to Sanger sequencing in the molecular diagnostics of EC.

This study has several limitations that suggest directions for future research. First, being a retrospective single-institution study, it may reflect local demographics and practices, limiting generalizability. Multicenter validation is needed for broader applicability. Second, although ddPCR was applied to all 132 cases, only 20 discordant samples underwent NGS, limiting statistical power for evaluating ddPCR performance. While effective for resolving discordance, the small NGS subset restricts comprehensive sensitivity analysis. Third, the hotspot-first ddPCR approach intentionally focused on the clinically dominant pathogenic spectrum, as the 5 canonical hotspots (P286R, V411L, S297F, A456P, and S459F) account for approximately 70%–90% of pathogenic POLE-mutated ECs across cohorts and assays. However, this design inherently omits the remaining ≤10%–30% of additional pathogenic variants (M295R, F367S, D368Y, L424I, P436R, and M444K), including information on co-mutational context and tumor mutational burden, which are accessible only through broader NGS profiling. Finally, clinical follow-up was neither standardized nor long-term across all patients. Although no recurrences or deaths occurred in the POLEmut group, consistent with its known favorable prognosis, the limited events and short follow-up precluded statistical significance. An extended follow-up is needed to confirm long-term survival differences. Future prospective multicenter studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these findings across diverse populations and settings.

In conclusion, the Droplex ddPCR POLE mutation assay demonstrated superior sensitivity and diagnostic accuracy compared to traditional sequencing methods, aligning closely with comprehensive NGS. This study highlights the practical advantages of ddPCR, including shorter turnaround times, reduced complexity, and lower cost, making it a valuable alternative to NGS for routine clinical implementation. Further prospective studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate the clinical impact of integrating ddPCR-based POLE testing into standard molecular risk stratification protocols. Such studies should also explore long-term survival outcomes and cost-effectiveness analyses to facilitate widespread adoption in clinical practice.
