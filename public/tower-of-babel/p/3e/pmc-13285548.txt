African swine fever virus pE199L, as a mitophagy receptor, suppresses antiviral innate immunity to promote viral replication
Authors: Xiaoxuan Li, Binbin Ren, Danyang Zhang, Miao Dan, Dongying Liu, Ziwei Zhang, Jiakai Zhao, Yuchen Nan, Julian A Hiscox, James P Stewart, Zixiang Zhu, Qin Zhao, Yani Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe African swine fever virus (ASFV) employs sophisticated strategies to promote viral replication in the host; however, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Here, we demonstrated that the ASFV encoded pE199L protein acts as a potential mitophagy receptor that disrupted innate immunity through structural mimicry. The pE199L protein localized to mitochondria via its C terminal hydrophobic domain (155–199 aa) and induced mitochondrial fission by promoting DNM1L/Drp1 phosphorylation. Importantly, pE199L contained three LC3-interacting regions (LIRs: W35–I38, F157–L160, F193–L196) that directed autophagic degradation of key immune adaptors. Specifically, pE199L mediated mitophagic clearance of TBK1 (the CGAS-STING1 pathway) and MAVS (the RLR-MAVS pathway), thereby inhibiting type I interferon production and enhancing viral replication. This dual degradation mechanism was confirmed through rescue experiments using autophagy inhibitors and functional assays with LIR mutants. Introduction

African swine fever (ASF) is an acute, highly contagious, and fatal disease caused by African swine fever virus (ASFV) infection. To date, ASFV has spread extensively across many countries in Africa, Europe, and Asia [1,2]. The recent emergence of milder and recombinant ASFV strains is further complicating the epidemiological landscape [3,4]. Currently, except for Vietnam, there are no effective ASF vaccines in most countries. Therefore, prevention and control strategies rely primarily on enhanced biosecurity measures to contain the spread of the virus [5]. ASFV is an enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus that is the only member of the family Asfarviridae. Its genome is approximately 170–193 kb in length and encodes 150–200 proteins, including 68 structural proteins and more than 100 nonstructural proteins [6,7]. The large genome coding capacity and complex virion structure of ASFV present significant challenges in understanding its biology and pathogenesis [5]. Consequently, the biological functions of many key ASFV proteins have not been fully elucidated, limiting progress in the development of better prevention and control strategies.

Viral infection represents an ongoing battle between a virus and its host [8]. To circumvent host’s defense, viruses have evolved multiple strategies that specifically target interferon-mediated immune responses [9,10]. MAVS (mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein) and TBK1 (TANK binding kinase 1) are central components of the host innate immune defense against viruses [11]. Upon recognition of viral RNA by RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs) or detection of viral DNA through the CGAS-STING1 pathway, MAVS (localized on the mitochondrial outer membrane) and STING1 (located in the endoplasmic reticulum) function as key adaptor proteins that activate downstream signaling cascades leading to TBK1 phosphorylation [12,13]. The commonly activated IRF3 then translocates to the nucleus, where it drives the expression of type I interferons (IFN-I) and proinflammatory cytokines, which are essential for establishing antiviral immunity [14,15]. Studies have shown that various viruses employ diverse mechanisms to suppress host IFN-I responses, thereby evading antiviral innate immunity during infection [16–18]. ASFV MGF505-7 R, pB318L and CD2v inhibit CGAS-STING1-mediated IFN-I production by targeting STING1 [19–21]. ASFV p17 suppresses IFN-I production by targeting both the RLR-MAVS and the CGAS-STING1 pathways [22]. While numerous ASFV proteins have been shown to suppress host innate immunity, gene-deleted vaccines incorporating these factors retain pathogenicity [23,24]. These findings necessitate continued efforts to identify novel viral immunomodulators.

Mitophagy, a selective form of autophagy, specifically degrades dysfunctional or excess mitochondria [25,26]. PINK1 (PTEN induced kinase 1) recruits PRKN/PARK2 (parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase) to damaged mitochondria, where it promotes ubiquitin-dependent degradation of mitochondrial proteins, ultimately triggering mitophagy [27–29]. Moreover, receptor-driven mitophagy is directly mediated by proteins featuring LC3-interaction regions (LIRs), including PHB2 (prohibitin 2), BNIP3 (BCL2 interacting protein 3), BNIP3L/Nix (BCL2 interacting protein 3 like), and FUNDC1 (FUN14 domain containing 1) [30]. Intriguingly, multiple viruses have evolved mechanisms to co-opt this pathway. For example, the matrix protein M of human parainfluenza virus 3, respiratory syncytial virus nonstructural protein 1 and influenza A virus PB1-F2 protein directly bind MAP1LC3/LC3 (microtubule associated protein 1 light chain 3) to induce mitophagy [31,32]. Recently, emerging evidence has indicated that mitophagy, as a protective immune mechanism, restricts viral replication [32,33]. Conversely, viruses could exploit mitophagy to evade immune surveillance and promote replication [34,35]. Given this dual role and importance of mitophagy in viral infection, the effect of ASFV using mitophagy on viral replication requires further investigation. While ASFV p17 protein is known to cause MAVS degradation through mitophagy, thereby inhibiting innate immunity to promote viral replication [36], it remains unknown whether additional ASFV-encoded factors utilize mitophagy pathways to modulate host’s defense and potentiate replication. In particular, whether ASFV encodes a protein that functions as a mitophagy receptor that is analogous to influenza A virus PB1-F2 in inducing mitophagy to facilitate viral replication remains unknown.

ASFV pE199L (also designated j18L) is a multifunctional late-phase expressed protein localized to viral factories, where it facilitates virion assembly and core entry [37,38]. In addition to these roles, pE199L triggers mitochondrial structural collapse, membrane potential dissipation, and excessive fragmentation, thereby activating mitochondria-dependent apoptosis [39]. Furthermore, this protein promotes autophagy by engaging host protein PYCR2 [40]. While these mechanisms highlight the multifunctionality of pE199L, its potential role in influencing mitochondria-mediated innate immunity remains unexplored. In this study, we demonstrated that ASFV pE199L exploits mitophagy through molecular mimicry of cellular receptors, concurrently suppressing antiviral signaling. Crucially, pE199L mediates TBK1 and MAVS degradation through mitophagic clearance. Collectively, these findings establish pE199L-driven mitophagy as a novel immune evasion mechanism essential for ASFV pathogenesis.

Results

ASFV infection and pE199L induced extensive mitophagy

Our investigation demonstrated that ASFV infection triggered extensive mitochondrial crista disassembly, with double-membrane autophagic vacuoles encapsulating swollen mitochondria (Figure 1(A)). Confocal immunofluorescence microscopy revealed severe fragmentation of the mitochondrial network in ASFV-infected cells, which contrasted sharply with the tubular morphology observed in mock-infected controls (Figure 1(B) and Data S1A. We subsequently infected PAM cells with different amounts of ASFV and observed a dose-dependent increase in LC3-II, along with progressive reductions in the levels of the polyubiquitin-binding protein SQSTM1/p62 (sequestosome 1) and the mitochondrial markers TOMM20 (translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20) and MT-CO2 (mitochondrially encoded cytochrome c oxidase II) (Figure 1(C)). The pattern was also corroborated by reduced mtDNA:nDNA ratios via qPCR analysis (Figure 1(D)). Further evaluation of mitophagy flux through dual-labeling confocal microscopy (labeling mitochondria with MitoTracker Red and lysosomes with LysoTracker Green) revealed a significant increase in the colocalization of mitochondria and lysosomes in ASFV-infected cells (Figure 1(E) and Data S1B). Collectively, these results confirmed that ASFV induced extensive mitophagy (Figure 1(F)).

To identify ASFV proteins that mediated mitophagy, we screened seven viral candidates (pK205R, p12, pA151R, pE199L, p72, MGF-505-7 R, and p30) by plasmid transfection in HEK293T cells. In this screen, p17-a previously reported inducer of mitophagy via the SQSTM1-TOMM70 pathway [36]-was included as a positive control. The results revealed that pE199L expression increased LC3-II conversion and decreased the expression of SQSTM1, TOMM20 and MT-CO2 (Figure 2(A)). Dose-dependent transfection of pE199L-expressing plasmids resulted in progressive LC3-II accumulation concomitant with reduced mitochondrial protein levels (Figure 2(B)). Notably, chloroquine (CQ) treatment, which can block autophagosome-lysosome fusion, reversed pE199L-induced depletion of SQSTM1, TOMM20 and MT-CO2 (Figure 2(C)), indicating that pE199L possibly induced extensive mitophagy. We hypothesized whether this degradation led to functional consequences for cellular homeostasis. Thus, we further evaluated the effects of pE199L on mtDNA content, cellular ATP levels, and cellular viability. Indeed, transfection of pE199L led to a significant reduction in mtDNA content, cellular ATP levels and cell viability (Figure 2(D-F)), consistent with the loss of functional mitochondria. To further verify whether pE199L induces extensive mitophagy, we conducted further analysis through immunofluorescence, and the results showed that GFP-tagged LC3 colocalized with the RFP-tagged mitochondrial in pE199L-expressing cells (Figure 2(G) and Data S2A), indicating that sequestrated mitochondria could be within pE199L-induced autophagosome. Crucially, the pmRFP-GFP-Mito reporter presented acidic-quenched GFP signals with persistent RFP fluorescence, confirming complete mitolysosomal fusion (Figure 2(H) and Data S2B). Conversely, siRNA-mediated pE199L knockdown in ASFV-infected PAM cells abrogated the viral induction of LC3-II and restored the expression of SQSTM1, TOMM20 and MT-CO2 (Figure 2(I)). Collectively, these data established pE199L as the primary ASFV effector driving extensive mitophagy (Figure 2(J)).

pE199L-induced mitophagy requires DNM1L/Drp1-dependent mitochondrial fission

Some previous reports have shown that viral targeting of mitochondria disrupts fission-fusion dynamics to generate fragmented, dysfunctional organelles primed for mitophagy [33,41]. Next, we characterized the impact of pE199L on mitochondrial integrity. Compared with control cells, HEK293T cells expressing pE199L presented pronounced mitochondrial fragmentation networks (Figure 3(A) and Data S3A). Similar to the CCCP-treated cells, the cells presented a significant decrease in the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) (Figure 3(B)). Given reports linking viral infection to DNM1L/Drp1-S616 phosphorylation leading to mitochondrial fission [41,42], we assessed this pathway in pE199L-expressing cells. Immunoblotting revealed that the levels of p-DNM1L/Drp1 (S616) were significantly elevated in pE199L-transfected cells (Figure 3(C)), confirming that pE199L activated DNM1L/Drp1-dependent fission. Critically, both siRNA-mediated DNM1L/Drp1 knockdown and pharmacological inhibition with Mdivi-1 (a selective inhibitor of DNM1L/Drp1 activity) blocked pE199L-induced LC3-II accumulation and prevented the depletion of SQSTM1, TOMM20 and MT-CO2 (Figure 3(D,E)), confirming that mitochondrial fission was indispensable for pE199L-mediated mitophagy (Figure 3(F)).

The pE199L C terminus anchors mitochondria for mitophagy activation

As shown by the above immunofluorescence results, pE199L colocalized with mitochondria (Figure 2(E)), suggesting mitochondrial targeting. To further validate this finding, we infected PAM cells with ASFV and observed consistent colocalization of pE199L with mitochondria (Figure 4(A) and Data S4A). Bioinformatics revealed a C terminal transmembrane domain (TMD) flanked by a positively charged residue in pE199L (Figure 4(B)), recapitulating the topology of tail-anchored proteins localized to the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondrial outer membranes [43–46]. To map the mitochondrial targeting signal, we generated GFP-tagged pE199L truncations. Confocal microscopy confirmed the diffuse cytoplasmic distribution of the 1–154 aa constructs versus the mitochondria-restricted localization of the 155–199 aa fragment (Figure 4(C) and Data S4B). Subcellular fractionation revealed that residues 1–154 aa were predominantly cytosolic, whereas the 155–199 aa fragment was enriched in the mitochondrial fraction (Figure 4(D)). These results indicated that the 45 C terminal amino acids of pE199L, including the putative TMD and a short 27 amino acid positively charged tail, mediated mitochondrial targeting. To determine whether mitochondrial localization was necessary for pE199L-induced mitophagy, we transfected HEK293T cells with pE199L and truncated pE199L. Functional assessment demonstrated that while full length pE199L and its C terminal fragment (155–199 aa) induced LC3-II accumulation and decreased the expression of SQSTM1, TOMM20 and MT-CO2, the N terminal construct (1–154 aa) failed to deplete mitochondrial markers despite increasing LC3-II (Figure 4(E)). These findings established the C terminal domain (155–199 aa) as both necessary and sufficient for mitochondrial targeting and mitophagy induction (Figure 4(F)).

Structural mimicry enables ASFV pE199L to hijack LC3 as a potential mitophagy receptor

As mentioned above, pE199L induced loss of MMP and increased mitochondrial fission (Figure 3(A,B)). Loss of the MMP stabilizes PINK1 on the outer mitochondrial membrane and activates PRKN to ubiquitinate mitochondrial substrates, which triggers PINK1-PRKN-mediated mitophagy [28,47]. Virus infection can induce mitophagy mainly through either the PINK1-PRKN-dependent pathway or PINK1-PRKN-independent pathways. Therefore, to further understand the underlying mechanism by which the pE199L protein induces mitophagy, we first examined whether the regulation of pE199L protein on mitophagy activated the PINK1-PRKN pathway. Then, PRKN was analyzed in pE199L-transfected HEK293T cells by western blot. The results revealed significant PRKN accumulation on mitochondria in pE199L-expressing cells compared with controls (Figure 5(A)), suggesting that pE199L activated this pathway. To investigate whether the pE199L protein also triggers mitophagy via PINK1-PRKN-independent mechanisms, we knocked down PINK1 or PRKN using siRNA to define their roles in pE199L-induced mitophagy. PINK1 or PRKN deficiency failed to block pE199L-induced LC3-II conversion or SQSTM1, TOMM20 and MT-CO2 reduction (Figure 5(B,C)). Crucially, in PRKN-null HeLa cells [48], pE199L still robustly triggered mitophagy (Figure 5(D)). These data indicated that pE199L protein not only induced PINK1-PRKN pathway-mediated mitophagy, but also triggered non-PINK1-PRKN pathway-mediated mitophagy.

In addition to the PINK1-PRKN pathway, canonical LIR motifs, which are defined by the conserved W/Y/FxxL/I sequence, enable multiple cellular proteins, including PHB2, FUNDC1, NBR1, SQSTM1 and BNIP3L, to function as autophagy receptors [32,49,50]. Bioinformatics analysis via iLIR software identified three conserved LIR motifs (WxxI, FxxL, and FxxL) within pE199L (Figure 5(E)). It suggested pE199L might interact with LC3 and co-IP assays confirmed the binding between MYC-tagged pE199L and His-tagged LC3 (Figure 5(F)). Importantly, introducing point mutations (W35A I38A, F157A L160A, and F193A L196A) disrupted this interaction (Figure 5(G)), establishing LIR motifs as essential for pE199L-LC3 complex formation. A functional assessment of LIR in both WSL and HEK293T cells revealed that transfecting with LIR-mutant pE199L attenuated mitophagy induction, as evidenced by reduced LC3-II conversion and partially restored the expression of SQSTM1, TOMM20 and MT-CO2 (Figure 5(H) and Figure S1(A)), indicating that the LIR motifs in pE199L were crucial for the activation of mitophagy.

To elucidate the role of the LIR within the pE199L protein during ASFV infection, we successfully generated a recombinant virus, ASFV-pE199LLIRmut, harboring specific point mutations in its LIR motifs via homologous recombination (Figure S2(A)). The recombinant virus was purified through fluorescence observation combined with limited-fold dilution (Figure S2(B)). PCR amplification and sequencing analysis of the purified viral stock confirmed the successful introduction of the intended point mutations and the reporter gene cassette into the viral genome (Fig. S2C). The growth profile of ASFV-pE199LLIRmut was similar to that of WT ASFV in PAMs with a slight decrease replication level (Fig. S2D). A co-IP was performed to analyze the interaction between pE199L and endogenous LC3 in PAMs infected with either WT ASFV or ASFV-pE199LLIRmut. pE199L interacted with endogenous LC3 in WT ASFV-infected PAMs, but this interaction was abolished in PAMs infected with ASFV-pE199LLIRmut (Figure 5(I)). Subsequently, we further evaluated the effects of WT ASFV and ASFV-pE199LLIRmut viruses on mitophagy by western blot. Compared to WT ASFV, infection with ASFV-pE199LLIRmut resulted in a significantly reduced level of mitophagy, confirming our hypothesis (Figure 5(J)). Collectively, these findings established that pE199L acted primarily as a potential autophagic receptor, which hijacked LC3 through its structurally conserved LIR motifs to induce mitophagy. pE199L might trigger mitophagy that activated PINK1-PRKN, but its primary mechanism-via LIR-mediated LC3 binding-remains independent and dominant (Figure 5(K)).

pE199L inhibits the CGAS-STING1 and RLR-MAVS-mediated IFN pathways

As a double-stranded DNA virus, ASFV can activate the CGAS-STING1 innate immune signaling pathway [22,51,52]. We therefore initially evaluated whether pE199L antagonized this cascade. In HEK293T cells co-transfected with CGAS and STING1, the IFNB/IFN-β-Luc reporter and pRL-TK, pE199L expression dose-dependently suppressed CGAS-STING1-mediated IFNB promoter activity (Figure 6(A)). Consistent with these findings, pE199L dose-dependently reduced the transcription of IFNB, ISG15, IFIT2/ISG54 and IFIT1/ISG56 induced by CGAS-STING1 co-expression (Figure 6(B)). This suppression extended to poly(dA:dT)-stimulated CGAS activation, where pE199L inhibited both IFNB promoter activity and ISGs transcription (Figure 6(C,D)).

There is evidence that DNA viruses can also degrade the mitochondrial antiviral protein MAVS to suppress innate immunity [34,41]. Previous studies have shown that highly AT-rich ASFV genomic DNA is sensed by DNA-directed RNA polymerase III (Pol-III), thereby activating the RLR-MAVS pathway in host cells and triggering an antiviral immune response [22,53]. Stimulation with poly(I:C) revealed that pE199L significantly inhibited IFNB promoter activation (Figure 6(E)). qRT-PCR analysis confirmed the concomitant suppression of poly(I:C)-induced IFNB, ISG15, IFIT2/ISG54 and IFIT1/ISG56 transcription (Figure 6(F)). Crucially, siRNA-mediated pE199L knockdown significantly enhanced IFNB production during ASFV infection (Figure 6(G)). Consistent with this, ASFV-pE199LLIRmut reduced inhibition of IFNB compared to WT ASFV (Figure 6(H)). These findings established pE199L as a potent antagonist of CGAS-STING1 and RLR-MAVS signaling and was a key contributor to ASFV-induced immunosuppression (Figure 6(I)).

pE199L inhibits CGAS-STING1-mediated IFN signaling by degrading TBK1

To delineate the ability of pE199L to target the CGAS-STING1 pathway, we co-transfected HEK293T cells with MYC-pE199L alongside Flag-tagged CGAS, STING1, TBK1 or constitutively active IRF3-5D. Luciferase reporter assays revealed that pE199L suppressed IFNB promoter activity induced by CGAS, STING1 or TBK1 but not IRF3-5D (Figure 7(A)), indicating that TBK1 was a probable target. Confocal microscopy and co-IP confirmed the pE199L-TBK1 interaction (Figure 7(B,C)). To determine whether this interaction was direct, we expressed and purified recombinant His-TBK1 and GST-pE199L proteins in E. coli, and assessed their binding using GST pulldown assays. As shown in Figure 7(D), GST-pE199L, but not GST alone, specifically pulled down His-TBK1, confirming a direct interaction between pE199L and TBK1. Crucially, in both WSL and HEK293T cells, pE199L overexpression dose-dependently reduced the TBK1 protein level (Figure 7(F) and Figure S3B), without affecting TBK1 mRNA level (Figure 7(E) and Figure S3(A)). These results indicated that pE199L probably promoted TBK1 posttranslational degradation. The degradation of cellular proteins mainly depends on the ubiquitin-proteasome or autophagy-lysosome pathways [54]. Pharmacological inhibition in both cell lines demonstrated that the lysosomal inhibitor CQ and the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA, but not the proteasome inhibitor MG132, rescued TBK1 degradation (Figure 7(G) and Figure S3(C)), implicating autophagy clearance. Subcellular fractionation analyses in WSL and HEK293T cells revealed that pE199L promoted TBK1 mitochondrial translocation (Figure 7(H) and Figure S3(D)). Co-IP further revealed that the TBK1-LC3 interaction was enhanced by pE199L (Figure 7I), indicating the formation of a tripartite complex. siRNA-mediated pE199L knockdown in ASFV-infected cells restored TBK1 expression (Figure 7(J)). Furthermore, we assessed the impact on TBK1 protein using both WT ASFV and ASFV-pE199LLIRmut. As shown in Figure 7(K), TBK1 protein expression was elevated upon infection with ASFV-pE199LLIRmut compared to WT ASFV. Collectively, these consistent results from both WSL and HEK293T cells demonstrated that pE199L scaffolded TBK1 translocation to mitochondria and recruited LC3 to facilitate the autophagic degradation of TBK1, thereby evading antiviral innate immunity (Figure 7(L)).

pE199L inhibits MAVS-mediated IFN signaling by degrading MAVS

Because MAVS is a key antiviral protein located in the mitochondria, we investigated whether pE199L similarly targeted MAVS in the RLR pathway beyond TBK1 degradation. In both WSL and HEK293T cells, overexpression of pE199L dose-dependently reduced MAVS protein levels in transfected cells (Figure 8(A) and Figure S4A). This degradation was rescued by the lysosomal inhibitor CQ and the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA but not by the proteasome inhibitor MG132 in both cell lines (Figure 8(B) and Figure S4(B)), confirming the occurrence of autophagy clearance. Crucially, siRNA-mediated pE199L knockdown restored MAVS expression during ASFV infection (Figure 8(C)). Furthermore, we assessed the impact on MAVS protein using both WT ASFV and ASFV-pE199LLIRmut. As shown in Figure 8(D), MAVS protein expression was elevated upon infection with ASFV-pE199LLIRmut compared to WT ASFV. These findings established that pE199L-induced mitophagy also targeted MAVS for degradation, thereby evading the type I interferon signaling pathway (Figure 8(E)).

pE199L exploits mitophagy to suppress interferon responses to promote ASFV replication

To investigate the functional role of ASFV pE199L-mediated dual immunosuppression in viral replication, we first evaluated its effect on ASFV propagation using CQ and Mdivi-1 in both PAM and WSL cells. Both inhibitors significantly reduced the expression of the ASFV p72 protein and viral DNA in PAMs (Figure 9(A,B)) and WSL cells (Figure 9(C,D)). Notably, overexpression of pE199L in inhibitor-treated WSL cells substantially enhanced viral replication (Figure 9(C,D)). To explore whether the impact of ASFV replication was related to innate immunity, we measured IFNB expression following stimulation with poly(dA:dT) under identical conditions. Consistent with the immunomodulatory role of mitophagy, CQ and Mdivi-1 countered the ASFV-mediated suppression of IFNB production (Figure 9(E)). Conversely, the overexpression of pE199L in WSL cells led to a significant reduction in IFNB expression (Figure 9(F)). Collectively, these data demonstrated that ASFV pE199L exploited mitophagy to suppress type I interferon responses, thereby promoting viral replication (Figure 9(G)).

Discussion

The global spread of ASF continues to inflict devastating economic losses on swine production worldwide. While ASFV is known to evade host immunity through multiple viral proteins [52,53], our study defined a novel immune mechanism mediated by the viral protein pE199L. We demonstrated that pE199L acted as a potential mitophagy receptor by exploiting canonical LIR motifs to engage LC3, thereby hijacking the autophagy machinery. Critically, this interaction orchestrated the selective autophagic degradation of pivotal antiviral adaptor proteins, including TBK1 (CGAS-STING1 pathway) and MAVS (RLR-MAVS pathway), through lysosomal clearance. This dual-pathway disruption represented a previously unrecognized immune evasion strategy that directly facilitated viral replication. Our findings not only identified pE199L as the first ASFV-encoded potential mitophagy receptor but also revealed how targeted mitophagy dismantled cytosolic immune sensing, revealing new therapeutic vulnerabilities.

While prior studies established ASFV’s strategic manipulation of autophagy through distinct viral proteins at various infection stages [55–57], the involvement of mitophagy has remained unexplored. Importantly, a recent study reveals that ASFV employs a sophisticated temporal strategy to manipulate autophagy. It initially promotes full autophagic flux to alleviate inflammatory responses and support viral replication, but later shifts to blocking autophagosomal degradation to prevent the clearance of viral components and delay host cell death [55,58]. This shift from the proautophagic phase to the antiautophagic phase likely involves the coordinated action of multiple viral proteins that act in succession. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies showing that ASFV induces profound mitochondrial fragmentation and triggers extensive mitophagy, which is mechanistically linked to viral disruption of mitochondrial dynamics. Crucially, we identified pE199L as the primary viral effector driving this mitophagic response, as confirmed through gain-of-function and loss-of-function approaches. Earlier reports localize pE199L to viral factories or describe it as a membrane-associated protein with lipid bilayer affinity, but its functional role remained undefined [37,38]. We demonstrated that the C terminal hydrophobic domain of pE199L specifically targets mitochondria, probably due to structural homology between mitochondria and the cell membrane, a targeting strategy consistent with that employed by other viral proteins [45,46]. Specifically, pE199L likely undergoes stage-dependent trafficking during infection progression and exhibits different localization changes. Notably, our identification of pE199L as a potential mitophagy receptor fundamentally recontextualized its known pro-apoptotic role [39,40]. This protein actively exploited the mitophagic machinery to subvert organelle quality control and revealed a sophisticated viral immune evasion strategy.

Prior work establishes the role of ASFV p17 in mitophagy via the SQSTM1-TOMM70 interaction [36]. Consistent with previous results for p17 shown in Figure 2(A), serving as a positive control, which further confirmed protein pE199L induced mitophagy. Our findings revealed that pE199L employed a dual-pathway strategy. First, pE199L drove mitochondrial fission through DNM1L/Drp1-S616 phosphorylation, generating fragmented organelles that activated the PINK1-PRKN pathway via PINK1 stabilization and PRKN recruitment, which is a canonical route culminating in mitochondrial ubiquitination and clearance [59]. Second, pE199L-induced mitophagy persists in PRKN-deficient systems, indicating pathway independence. pE199L contained structurally conserved LIR motifs that mediated direct binding to LC3, functionally resembling mitophagy receptors such as FUNDC1 and BNIP3L [60,61]. This direct LC3 interaction enabled it to initiate mitophagy independently. In contrast, the hepatitis B virus HBx protein triggers mitophagy indirectly, primarily by engaging host mitophagy receptors such as BNIP3L to mediate the process, rather than acting as a direct receptor itself [62]. These observations suggest that pE199L may act as a potential mitophagy receptor, analogous to IAV PB1‑F2 and RSV NS1 proteins [32,33].

Mitophagy plays dual physiological roles in cellular homeostasis and innate immune regulation, with viruses frequently exploiting this pathway for immune evasion [32,63]. While ASFV proteins such as A137R, MGF110-9 L and MGF505-7 R target TBK1 for autophagic degradation to suppress CGAS-STING1 signaling [19,51,57] and pS183L/MGF-360-4 L inhibits RLR-MAVS via MDA5 targeting [56,64], our study established pE199L as the viral effector capable of simultaneously disrupting both pathways, which aligned with DNA viral strategies exemplified by VZV/EBV [34,35]. Crucially, pE199L orchestrated the mitophagy degradation of two pivotal antiviral adaptors: TBK1 (CGAS-STING1 pathway) and MAVS (RLR-MAVS pathway), a finding consistently observed in both the HEK293T and WSL cells. This bifunctional mechanism involved mitochondrial translocation of TBK1 facilitated by pE199L scaffolding, followed by LC3 recruitment for lysosomal clearance, whereas MAVS degradation occurred through direct mitophagy targeting. MAVS, as the core adaptor protein in the RLR signaling pathway, is essential for antiviral innate immunity against RNA viruses [65]. Therefore, by simultaneously suppressing both the CGAS-STING1 and RLR-MAVS pathways, pE199L established a “double-lock” mechanism that effectively blocked compensatory signaling and ensured robust inhibition of type I interferon production. This dual suppression is likely to broadly impair the host’s defense not only against ASFV itself but also against RNA viruses, potentially enhancing the replication of concurrent or subsequent RNA pathogens and facilitating broader transmission. This dual-target strategy underscores the evolutionary sophistication of ASFV in dismantling the host’s multilayered innate immune defense.

In order to confirm that pE199L as a potential mitophagy receptor and is essential for innate immunity, we attempted to delete or mutate the pE199L protein to block the interaction between pE199L and LC3 from inducing mitophagy. However, pE199L is also indispensable for viral replication, as its absence severely impairs membrane fusion and core release [38]. Therefore, we failed to generate a pE199L-deficient ASFV. In this study, we employed siRNA‑mediated knockdown and recombinant LIR‑mutant viruses to block the interaction between pE199L and LC3 and to investigate the role of pE199L in mitophagy and innate immunity. Consistent with the transfected pE199LLIR mut, both knockdown of pE199L and infection with the ASFV-pE199LLIRmut reduced mitophagy and a reduced inhibition of IFN-I signaling. Nevertheless, neither intervention completely abolished mitophagy nor fully restored IFN‑I signaling, suggesting the involvement of additional ASFV proteins that coordinately regulated these processes. For instance, ASFV protein MGF-360‑4 L inhibits IFN signaling by recruiting the selective autophagy receptor SQSTM1, thereby suppressing MDA5‑mediated innate immune activation [56], while the viral protein p17 promotes mitophagy by enhancing the SQSTM1‑TOMM70 interaction [36]. Thus, ASFV likely employs multiple proteins to ensure its replication through fine‑tuned regulation of mitophagy and innate immunity.

Overall, this study established ASFV pE199L as a master regulator of mitophagy that coordinated the induction of mitochondrial fission through DNM1L/Drp1 activation and functioned as a canonical mitophagy receptor. This dual mechanism enabled targeted degradation of TBK1 and MAVS-pivotal adaptors of the CGAS-STING1 and RLR-MAVS pathways-effectively dismantling antiviral immunity to facilitate viral replication (Figure 10). Our findings contributed to understanding the function of pE199L and its role in the viral pathogenesis, providing valuable insights for the development of antiviral interventions and vaccines targeting ASF.

Model of ASFV pE199L-mediated suppression of innate immunity through mitophagy to increase viral replication. ASFV pE199L drives mitophagy by dual mechanisms: by promoting DNM1L/Drp1-dependent mitochondrial fission and by functioning as a viral mitophagy receptor that directly recruits LC3, facilitating the degradation of TBK1 and MAVS, key adaptors of the CGAS-STING1 and RLR-MAVS pathways, thereby inhibiting interferon responses and enabling robust ASFV replication.

Materials and methods

Antibodies and reagents

The following primary antibodies were used: anti-GAPDH monoclonal antibody (mAb) (60004–1-Ig; 1:5000 for WB), anti-MYC mAb (60003–2-Ig; 1:5000 for WB), anti-Flag mAb (66008–4-Ig; 1:5000 for WB), anti-His mAb (66005–1-Ig; 1:5000 for WB), anti-LC3 polyclonal antibody (pAb) (14600–1-AP; 1:2000 for WB), anti-SQSTM1/p62 pAb (18420–1-AP; 1:2000 for WB), anti-PINK1 pAb (23274–1-AP; 1:2000 for WB), anti-PRKN pAb (14060–1-AP; 1:2000 for WB), anti-MT-CO2 pAb (55070–1-AP; 1:5000 for WB), anti-TOMM20 pAb (11802–1-AP; 1:5000 for WB), and anti-MYC pAb (10828–1-AP; 1:1000 for IF), all of which were purchased from Proteintech Group. For immunoblotting, the following horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies were used: goat anti-mouse IgG (HS002-01; 1:5000 for WB) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (HS001-01; 1:5000 for WB) from TransGen Biotech. For immunofluorescence, the following fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were obtained from Immunoway Inc: Alexa Fluor 350-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (RS3008; 1:400 for IF), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (RS3208; 1:400 for IF), and Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (RS3411; 1:400 for IF). p72 and pE199L mAb was prepared and stored in the laboratory (1:500 for WB, 1:200 for IF).

The following chemical reagents were used in this study: dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; D2650), MG132 (M7449), chloroquine (CQ; C6628), 3-methyladenine (3-MA; M9281), mitochondrial division inhibitor 1 (Mdivi-1; M0199), and carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP; C2759), all of which were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Additionally, naked poly(dA:dT) (86828–69-5) and low-molecular-weight poly(I:C) (tlrl-picw-250) were obtained from InvivoGen.

Cells and viruses

Porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs) were isolated from the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of 28-day-old piglets via a previously described protocol [66]. The cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, 12,633,020) supplemented with L-glutamine, 10% (v:v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 10,270,106), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime, C0222). The HEK293T (CRL-1573) and HeLa (CRL-12401) cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection/ATCC and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, 12,491,015). The WSL cell line was kindly provided by Associate Professor Zhang Shiqiang (College of Veterinary Medicine, Northwest A&F University).

The CN/GS/2018 ASFV, a genotype II strain, was isolated and preserved by the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. All experiments with ASFV were conducted in the Biosafety Protection-Level 3 Laboratory (BSL-3) of the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute.

Plasmids and mutagenesis

Plasmids encoding His-LC3, GFP-LC3 and pmRFP-GFP-Mito (a mitochondrial-targeted RFP-EGFP fusion) were maintained in our laboratory. The IFNB promoter-driven firefly luciferase reporter plasmid (IFN-Beta_pGL3) was obtained from Addgene (102597; deposited by Nicolas Manel). The pRL-TK plasmid (Promega, E2231), which expresses Renilla luciferase, was used as an internal control for normalization in dual-luciferase assays. The ASFV pE199L gene was codon optimized for expression in HEK293T cells by GENEWIZ (Suzhou, China) and cloned and inserted into the pCAGEN-MYC vector (NovoPro, V013274) to produce a C terminally MYC-tagged fusion protein. To generate fluorescent fusion constructs, full-length pE199L and its truncated variants (residues 1–154 aa and 155–199 aa) were inserted in-frame downstream of the GFP coding sequence in the pCAGEN vector (NovoPro, V017505). A GFP-only vector served as a negative control. LIR motif mutants of pE199L (W35A, I38A, F157A, L160A, F193A, and L196A) were generated by site-directed mutagenesis using overlap extension PCR with the primers listed in Table S1. The resulting mutant fragments were digested with EcoRI and EcoRV and ligated into the similarly digested pCAGEN-MYC vector (NovoPro, V013274) using T4 DNA ligase. The full-length coding sequences of porcine CGAS, STINGI, TBK1, IRF3, and MAVS were amplified via RT-PCR from total RNA extracted from PAMs and subsequently cloned and inserted into the pCAGEN vector (NovoPro, V017505) to generate C terminal Flag-tagged versions. The constitutively active form of IRF3, pCAGEN-Flag-IRF3-5D, was generated by site-directed mutagenesis using overlap extension PCR with pCAGEN-Flag-IRF3 as the backbone plasmids and primers designed to introduce five phosphomimetic mutations (S394D, S396D, S400D, S403D, and T405D). Recombinant plasmids pET28a-TBK1 and pGEX6P-1-pE199L were constructed using pCAGEN-Flag-TBK1 and pCAGEN-Myc-pE199L as the backbone plasmids, respectively. The resulting fragments were cloned into BamHI/XhoI-linearized pET-28a (NovoPro, V011005) or HindIII/BamHI-linearized pGEX6P-1 (NovoPro, V010912) using T4 DNA ligase. All sequences of primers used in this study are provided in Table S1, and all constructed plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Construction and analysis of recombinant ASFV

To generate a recombinant ASFV harboring point mutations within the LC3-interacting region (LIR) of the pE199L gene (ASFV-pE199LLIRmut), a homologous recombination-based strategy was employed. First, a transfer plasmid, pUC57-mCherry-p72-pE199LLIRmut, was constructed. This plasmid contained a left and a right homology arm (approximately 1 kb each) flanking the pE199L locus. The pE199L open reading frame was engineered via overlap PCR to incorporate three sets of double alanine substitutions (W35A I38A, F157A L160A, and F193A L196A) within its predicted LIR motifs. A reporter cassette, consisting of the mCherry gene driven by the ASFV p72 late promoter, was inserted immediately downstream of the mutated pE199L gene. The integrity of the entire construct was verified by Sanger sequencing. For virus recombination, PAMs were infected with the parental ASFV at a low multiplicity of infection (MOI of 0.1) and subsequently transfected with the pUC57-mCherry-p72-pE199LLIRmut transfer plasmid using jetPEI@-Macrophage DNA Transfection Reagent (Polyplus, 1,010,000,043). The recombinant virus was isolated based on mCherry fluorescence observed under a fluorescence microscope and purified by limited-fold dilution. Genomic DNA was extracted from the purified virus stock, and the successful introduction of the point mutations and the presence of the mCherry cassette were confirmed by PCR with gene-specific primers followed by sequencing of the amplicons. To assess the replication kinetics of the mutant, PAMs were infected with either the WT ASFV or the ASFV-pE199LLIRmut at an MOI of 0.1. Virus titers in the cell culture supernatants were determined at the indicated time points post-infection via the TCID50 assay on PAMs. The PCR primers used for constructing the transfer vector and detecting the primers are listed in Table S1.

Luciferase reporter assay

To evaluate the effect of pE199L overexpression on IFNB promoter activity, HEK293T and WSL cells were transiently transfected with MYC-tagged pE199L, IFN-Beta_pGL3, pRL-TK internal control plasmid and plasmids encoding relevant signaling molecules. After 24 h of transfection, the cells were stimulated with poly(dA:dT) or poly(I:C) for 12 h respectively, and a negative control group was set up. Luciferase activity was measured via the Dual-Luciferase® Assay Kit (Promega, E2980), with firefly luciferase values normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Co-immunoprecipitation and western blot analysis

Transfect the specified plasmid into HEK293T cells, or infect PAMs with either the WT ASFV or the ASFV-pE199LLIRmut (MOI = 1). Subsequently, the cells were lysed on ice for 20 min in NP-40 lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013) and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 2 min, after which the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies for immunoprecipitation. Whole-cell lysates or immunoprecipitated complexes were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk in PBS’T (PBS [Solarbio, P1020] containing 0.1% Tween 20 [Solarbio, T8220]) and subsequently incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibodies. After three washes, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized via an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

GST affinity-isolation assay

To examine the direct interaction between pE199L and TBK1, a GST affinity-isolation assay was performed. Recombinant proteins GST-pE199L and His-TBK1 were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) and purified from the soluble fractions using glutathione-Sepharose 4B beads (GE HealthCare, 17–0756-05) and Ni-NTA (Qiagen, 30,210), respectively. For GST affinity-isolation assay, glutathione-Sepharose 4B beads were incubated with 10 μg of purified GST-pE199L or GST protein (as a control) in binding buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% [v:v] NP-40 [Beyotime, P0013], supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail [Sigma-Aldrich, P8340]) at 4°C for 2 h. After washing, the beads were incubated with purified His-TBK1 protein overnight at 4°C. After thoroughly washing with PBS’T, the formed complexe were analyzed by western blot analysis.

siRNA oligonucleotide transfection

GenePharma designed and synthesized custom siRNA oligonucleotides based on the GenBank reference sequences for the ASFV pE199L, PINK1, PRKN, and DNM1L/Drp1 mRNAs. The cells were transfected with either gene-specific siRNA or negative control siRNA (siNC) via Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 13,778,030) for 24 h. The knockdown efficiency was assessed via quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR) and western blot. All the siRNA sequences of primers used are listed in Table S2.

Indirect immunofluorescence assay and confocal microscopy

PAMs were plated in 24-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and infected with ASFV at an MOI of 1 for 24 h. After infection, the cells were washed three times with PBS and stained with MitoTracker® Red (Invitrogen, M22425) or/and LysoTracker® Green (Invitrogen, L7526) probes for 30 min at 37°C. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, T8200) for 15 min. Following blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Solarbio, A8020) for 1 h at 37°C, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG for 1 h. Mitochondrial morphology and colocalization between mitochondria and lysosomes were visualized via a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

To analyze the colocalization of pE199L with LC3 and mitochondria, the cells were cotransfected with pCAGEN-MYC-pE199L and pEGFP-LC3 for 24 h. After transfection, the cells were washed with PBS and stained with MitoTracker® Red (Invitrogen, M22425) for 30 min at 37°C. Fixed cells were immunostained with an anti-MYC polyclonal antibody overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with an Alexa Fluor 350-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a Leica SP8 confocal system.

Transmission electron microscopy

PAM cells were infected with ASFV at an MOI of 1 for 24 h and then fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS (Solarbio, P1020) for 12 h at 4°C. After primary fixation, the samples were postfixed in 2% osmium tetroxide for 3 h and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30% to 100%) for 20 min at each concentration. The cells were then embedded in LR White resin and polymerized. Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate for 5–10 min and examined via an FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit transmission electron microscope (Tecnai G2; GATAN, Pleasanton, CA, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Cells cultured in 24-well plates were transiently transfected with the indicated plasmids for 24 h and treated with different agonists or infected with viruses. Total RNA was extracted with RNAiso Plus (Takara, 9108) to analyze the mRNA levels of IFNB, ISGs and TBK1. cDNA was synthesized via the PrimeScript® RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, RR037). qRT‑PCR was performed via SYBR Green Power Mixture (with ROX; GenStar, A313) on an Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR system. Gene expression was quantified via the 2−ΔΔCT method, with GAPDH used as the reference. All primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

For mtDNA analysis, total cellular DNA was isolated via the QIAamp DNA Micro Kit (Qiagen, 56,304). The mitochondrial DNA-to-nuclear DNA (mtDNA: nDNA) ratio was determined via the 2−ΔΔCT method, with beta-globin (HBB) serving as the single-copy nuclear reference gene. The primer sequences are provided in Table S3.

To quantify ASFV genomic DNA, viral DNA was extracted from infected cells via the QIAamp MinElute Virus Spin Kit (Qiagen, 57,704). Absolute quantification of the ASFV p72 gene was performed via quantitative real-time PCR with gene-specific primers and a hydrolysis probe. The primer and probe sequences are listed in Table S4.

Subcellular fractionation

Cytoplasmic and mitochondrial fractions were isolated from HEK293T and WSL cells via a Mammalian Cell Mitochondrial Isolation Kit (TransGen, DE401-01) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the cells were resuspended in mitochondrial lysis buffer and centrifuged at 700 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was further centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to pellet the mitochondrial fraction. The supernatant from this step was collected as the cytoplasmic protein fraction. The protein concentrations of both the mitochondrial and the cytoplasmic fractions were measured using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (TaKaRa, T9310A) for normalization. Equivalent amounts of proteins (10 μg) from each fraction were analyzed by western blot using the indicated antibodies.

Detection of the mitochondrial membrane potential

The mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed via a JC-1 assay kit (Solarbio, M8650) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates and transfected with a plasmid encoding pE199L or the corresponding empty vector control. At 24 h post-transfection, the cells were incubated with 300 nM JC-1 for 20 min at 37°C. The fluorescence intensities of JC-1 monomers and aggregates were analyzed via a flow cytometry (Beckman, USA).

Determination of ATP content

The intracellular ATP levels were measured using an enhanced ATP assay kit (Beyotime, S0027) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates and transfected with a plasmid encoding pE199L or the corresponding empty vector control. At 24 h post-transfection, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed on ice using the lysis buffer provided in the kit. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C, the supernatant was collected. Subsequently, the ATP content in each sample was detected by mixing the supernatant with the ATP detection working solution, and luminescence was immediately measured using a luminometer (PerkinElmer, USA).

Cell viability assay

The effect of pE199L on cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, C0038) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HEK293T cells were seeded in 96-well plates and transfected with a plasmid encoding pE199L or the corresponding empty vector control. At 24 h post-transfection, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent, followed by incubation at 37°C for 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the vector control group.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) from at least three independent experiments, each performed with three biological replicates. Statistical significance was determined via Student’s two-tailed t-test or one-way ANOVA (analysis of variance) with GraphPad Prism software version 8.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA). Significance levels are denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; ns, not significant.
