Orthogonal Biosynthesis of Pyrrolamides through Dehydration by a Pathway-Specific Glycoside Hydrolase-Like Enzyme in Gram-Positive Bacteria
Authors: Daichi Kanki, Sumire Kurosawa, Ayako Yoshida, Takayoshi Awakawa, Saori Kosono, Makoto Nishiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pyrrolamides, defined by a pyrrole-2-carboxamide core, represent a family of natural products produced by Streptomyces species. They exhibit potent yet promiscuous bioactivities, including antimicrobial, antifungal, antiviral, and anticancer effects. The characteristic pyrrole moiety is derived from 4-acetamidopyrrole-2-carboxylate, which was previously proposed to originate most likely from N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphate. This unusual transformation of a primary metabolite sugar into an aromatic peptide building block is of interest mechanistically; however, the pathway preceding pyrrole formation remains unclear. We herein report the discovery of manlipyrrolamides A–F from Bacillus manliponensis, a new class of pyrrolamides with distinctive structural features. Gene disruption and in vitro assays allowed us to identify the functions of key enzymes in the pyrrole formation pathway. Man17, a nucleotidyltransferase, functions as a gatekeeper that activates the precursor N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphate, through guanosine diphosphate (GDP) derivatization for subsequent modifications. Man18, a glycosyltransferase-like enzyme, and Man15, a glycoside hydrolase-like enzyme, function as a hydrolase and dehydratase, respectively, forming a pyrrole from a mature GDP-linked sugar. After pyrrole formation, a xanthine dehydrogenase-like enzyme complex is utilized in oxidation to produce carboxylate, distinct from the previously proposed Streptomyces pathway. The pathway-specific enzyme Man15, which is widely distributed in Gram-positive bacteria, is encoded in diverse biosynthetic gene clusters, indicating that this unprecedented transformation is utilized for unknown classes of pyrrolamides. The use of a GDP derivative suggests an orthogonal strategy distinct from that of the canonical UDP-linked sugar pathway. The present study reveals novel sugar skeletal conversion in secondary metabolism, which provides novel insights into bacterial metabolic strategies.

Introduction

Pyrrolamides constitute a family of natural products featuring a pyrrole-2-carboxamide core in their structures. These linear peptidic compounds are produced by Streptomyces species, first represented by congocidine (also known as netropsin), isolated from Streptomyces ambofaciens in the 1950s (Figure a). , After the characterization of congocidine, distamycin, anthelvencins, pyrronamycins, and their derivatives have mainly been characterized by bioactivity-guided screening (Figure S1). − Within their limited structural diversity, pyrrolamides share two characteristic features linked to their biological activities and DNA-binding properties: (i) iterative pyrrole-2-carboxamide units that interact with DNA nucleotide bases and (ii) terminal cationic groups that engage in ionic interactions with the phosphate backbone of DNA (Figure a). ,− These compounds bind preferentially to the AT-rich regions of the DNA minor groove and exhibit potent, yet broad spectrum bioactivity, including antimicrobial, antifungal, antiviral, anticancer, and antitumor effects. , Moreover, recent synthetic efforts have generated pyrrolamides with distinct structural features from those of natural pyrrolamides, exhibiting potential bioactivity as gyrase inhibitors. − Therefore, pyrrolamides merit further investigation as potential therapeutic leads. However, genome mining of pyrrolamides has remained underutilized mainly because of an ambiguous understanding of their biosynthesis.

A number of pyrrole moieties in primary and secondary metabolites were previously shown to be biosynthesized from amino acids, which undergo proper modifications sometimes accompanied by condensation with other carbon sources (Figure b). − For example, the monomeric pyrrole unit of tetrapyrrole, which forms the core moiety of metal-binding cofactors, such as heme and cyanocobalamin, is synthesized from two molecules of δ-aminolevulinic acid. In secondary metabolism, two of the three pyrroles in coumermycin A1 are synthesized from l-proline, while one is formed from the condensation of l-threonine and putative oxaloacetate. As a rare example that does not incorporate an amino acid for pyrrole formation, one of the three pyrrole rings in prodigiosin was shown to be formed by the decarboxylative addition of pyruvate with 2-octenal followed by transamination. On the other hand, in congocidine biosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism genes may be related to pyrrole ring formation, implying the unique origin of the pyrrole moiety. The key precursor of the pyrrole moiety, 4-acetamidopyrrole-2-carboxylate (10), has been deduced to most likely originate from an N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphate (GlcNAc-1-P, 7) in three steps: (i) the NDP activation of 7 and modifications by NDP sugar-modifying enzymes, (ii) five-membered pyrrole ring formation by a glycosyltransferase-like enzyme followed by nonenzymatic dehydration, and (iii) the oxidation of alcohol to carboxylate by oxidases (Figures c and S2). However, apart from step (iii), no intermediate or enzymatic reactions have been identified in steps (i) and (ii), and even the precursor, 7, remains unclear. Furthermore, the construction and aromatization of the five-membered ring have yet to be elucidated because no analogous enzymatic reactions have been reported. This ambiguous understanding of biosynthetic pathways makes it challenging to identify the biosynthesis of 10.

During our genome-mining efforts targeting uncharacterized metabolic pathways, , we found a previously uncharacterized biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC), the man cluster, in the genome of Bacillus manliponensis JCM 15802 (Figure a). We herein report the discovery of six previously unknown pyrrolamides, manlipyrrolamides A–F (1–6), which possess structural features distinct from those of known pyrrolamides (Figure a). Multifaceted analyses including gene disruption, subsequent intermediate identification, and in vitro enzyme assays revealed that the pyrrole formation pathway starts with the Man17-catalyzed GDP derivatization of GlcNAc-1-P (7), followed by a series of modifications, in which Man18 and Man15 play key roles. Notably, while homologues of Man15 (glycoside hydrolase (GH)-like) have been observed in other pyrrolamide BGCs, their roles were not considered to be directly related to the chemical transformation pathway. ,, The present results establish Man15 as a pathway-specific enzyme, as well as a potential marker for identifying novel pyrrolamide BGCs. This is the first biochemical analysis of a conversion from a sugar moiety to a pyrrole ring, which expands our understanding of sugar skeletal transformation.

Results and Discussion

Activation of the man Cluster and Characterization of Manlipyrrolamides A–F

A comparative analysis of gene clusters with similar syntenies revealed that the man cluster comprises three genomic regions: one small open-reading frame (ORF) (man1, Region A) and two regions consisting of 27 ORFs (man2-man28, Region B) and 31 ORFs (man29-man59, Region C) (Figures a and S3). It was noteworthy that two types of sequences comprising the three regions, A–B–C and A–C–B, were found in the genome database. However, we could not find any BGC that consists of only one or two regions. Furthermore, BGCs, in which most of the genes encode homologous enzymes from Regions B and C, were found, although with relatively low synteny. Based on these observations, we defined the man cluster as Regions A–B–C with 59 stand-alone ORFs spanning 60.9 kb (Figures a, S3 and Table S4).

To investigate the metabolic output of the man cluster, we developed a conjugation-based transformation system for B. manliponensis using an Escherichia coli Δdcm strain as a donor strain, enabling targeted gene deletion, followed by metabolomic analysis. We knocked out the genes man18 and man55 in the cluster; however, an LC–MS analysis revealed no change in compound production, indicating that the man cluster is silent under the laboratory culture conditions that we commonly used. Therefore, we attempted to activate the cluster by overexpressing cluster-encoded transcriptional regulators.

Two genes, man28 and man36, were annotated as helix-turn-helix (HTH)-type transcriptional regulators (Figure a and Table S4). A Pfam domain analysis indicated that Man28 harbored an N-terminal HTH (winged HTH) DNA-binding domain, followed by WYL and WCX (WYL C-terminal extension) domains at its C terminus (Figure S4). Proteins with this architecture (HTH-WYL-WCX) frequently act as transcriptional regulators in bacteria; the WYL domain has been implicated to function in ligand or nucleic acid binding and serves as a sensor module, while members of this family may function as activators or repressors. − In contrast, the OmpR/PhoB-type DNA-binding domain, present in Man36 (Figure S4), is classically associated with transcriptional activators, particularly in two-component response regulator systems, − although histidine kinase, which usually functions in sensing specific environmental stimuli and activating the regulator, has not been found in the man cluster so far.

Overexpression constructs for man28 and man36 were generated under the control of the spac promoter (P spac). An LC–MS analysis of culture supernatants using an ODS column revealed that man36 overexpression led to the production of 1–4, while man28 overexpression did not significantly affect the production of metabolites (Figure b). In contrast, the disruption of man28 increased the production levels of most manlipyrrolamides except for 1, when man36 was overexpressed (Figure S5). Given that 2–4 were the predominant products under the condition tested, Man28 and Man36 were suggested to function as a repressor and an activator of the man cluster, respectively.

LC–MS analyses showed a series of peaks differing primarily by 14 Da, with minor 2 Da shifts, indicating variations in the carbon chain and the oxidation states of the metabolites produced. The culture supernatant was subjected to a two-phase EtOAc extraction. The organic layer was concentrated and purified by reverse-phase HPLC, yielding six compounds including the above four compounds, designated manlipyrrolamides A–F (1–6) (Figure S6).

High-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry suggested the molecular formula of 1 as C18H20N4O3. Although typical α-proton signals of the carbonyl groups were absent in the 1H NMR spectrum, HMBC correlations from exchangeable protons to acyl carbon signals indicated that they possessed a peptidic backbone. Substructures a–d were deduced by the interpretation of 1D and 2D NMR data (Figure c and Table S15). The COSY spectrum showed that substructure a contained two spin systems (H4–H8 and NH–H2), and HMBC correlations between them (H1–C3, H2–C3, H4–C2, and H8–C2) confirmed their connectivity, identifying the unit as a phenethylamine moiety. Substructure b was assigned as a 3-amino-2-propenoyl unit based on COSY correlations, suggesting an enamine moiety and HMBC cross coupling from H2′ and H3′ to C1′. The olefinic geometry between C2′ and C3′ was assigned as E based on a coupling constant between H2′ and H3′ (J = 14.0 Hz). Substructure c was deduced to be a pyrrole ring because HMBC correlations were observed from an exchangeable proton to four sp2-hybridized carbon atoms. Additional HMBC correlations from another exchangeable proton to three of the pyrrole carbons (C3″, C4″, and C5″) and from a proton on the pyrrole ring to a carbonyl carbon (C1″) allowed the assignment of a carbonyl carbon and amino group substitution on the pyrrole ring and their relative positions. Substructure d was identified as an acetyl group, supported by an HMBC correlation from the methyl protons to a carbonyl carbon (H2‴–C1‴). Connectivity between substructures a to d was established through HMBC correlations from each amide proton to a carbonyl carbon.

The molecular formulas of 2–4 suggested one to three additional methylene units relative to 1, while those of 5 and 6 indicated the loss of two hydrogen atoms relative to compounds 3 and 4, respectively. Analysis of NMR data for 2–6 suggested the identical structure from substructure a to c in 1, and the only difference was shown in substructure d, the terminal short-chain acyl moiety. The structures of the acyl groups of 2–6 were elucidated by COSY and HMBC correlations (Figure d), while the olefin group of 6 was identified as the E configuration according to NOE correlations between H3‴ and the amide proton (Figure e).

Identification of a GDP Derivative (13) Isolated from the man18-Disrupted Mutant

Due to the presence of homologous biosynthetic genes for 10, manlipyrrolamides were also predicted to be synthesized through 10 (Table and Figure ). In the congocidine BGC (the cgc cluster), the oxidation pathway from 9 to 10 was shown to be accomplished by two enzymes: Cgc17, an NAD­(P)-dependent alcohol dehydrogenase and Cgc3, an NAD­(P)-dependent aldehyde dehydrogenase. The upstream pathway leading to 9 was deduced to involve five enzymes: Cgc11, Cgc8, Cgc9, Cgc12, and Cgc10. Cgc11, Cgc8, Cgc9, and Cgc12 convert the putative precursor 7 to 8 in a manner analogous to the biosynthetic pathways of calicheamicin and AT2433, in which glucose-1-phosphate is transformed into U/TDP-4-oxo-alpha-D-Xyl, followed by transamination at the C4 position (Figure S7). ,− Cgc10 then catalyzes the nucleophilic attack of the C4 amino group on C1, followed by spontaneous dehydration, resulting in pyrrole formation.

To further investigate the pyrrole formation pathway by man cluster enzymes, we analyzed metabolites of the disruptant of man18 overexpressing man36. Man18 is a homologue of Cgc10, the previously reported glycosyltransferase-like (GT-like) enzyme. A common type of nucleotide sugar-mediated glycosylation pathway begins with the formation of an NDP sugar, followed by modifications to the sugar moiety, and ultimately the transfer of the modified sugar by a glycosyltransferase (GT). Therefore, we hypothesized that the structure of the substrate for Man18 may provide insights into the upstream steps of the pathway until mature NDP sugar formation. The disruption of man18 caused the complete loss of manlipyrrolamide production, which suggested that Man18 is needed to produce manlipyrrolamides, potentially through the biosynthesis of key precursor 10 (Figure S8). The LC–MS analysis revealed that the prominent peak 13 was absent from the wild type, suggesting the accumulation of a biosynthetic intermediate (Figure a). We hypothesized that 13 represented the direct substrate of Man18.

(a) The EIC of accumulated 13. (b) COSY and HMBC correlations of 13. (c) Relative configuration of the sugar moiety in 13.

The supernatant of the Δman18 strain was treated with activated charcoal, and the combined flow-through and wash fractions were subsequently applied to an anion-exchange column. Compound 13 was eluted with NaCl solution, followed by desalting through pH-adjusted adsorption and elution on charcoal. Final purification was achieved by reversed-phase HPLC, affording pure 13.

The analysis of the COSY spectrum suggested two spin systems in 13 (Figure b). By combining the HMBC correlation that connected the carbons neighboring oxygen, the two sugar moieties were identified as 2-acetamido-2,4-dideoxy-4-amino-pentose and ribose. The pyrophosphate group bound to the pentose and ribose moieties at positions C1 and C5′, respectively, which were supported by phosphorus atom coupling at C1 and C2 in the pentose moiety and C5′ and C4′ in ribose, respectively, in the 13C spectrum (Table S21). The presence of the guanine moiety was confirmed by the combination of HMBC cross coupling (H1′–C8″, H1′–C4″, H8″–C4″, and H8″–C5″) and the presence of two specific carbon signals (δC 154.0 and δC 159.0). The elucidated planar structure was supported by MS/MS data, which showed corresponding fragments of GDP and guanosine monophosphate (Figure S9). The relative configuration at the pentose moiety was elucidated by proton NMR coupling and the NOESY spectrum (Figure c). Coupling between geminal protons (3 J H2,H3 = 11.6 Hz, 3 J H3,H4 = 4.2 Hz) suggested H2, H3, and H4 as the axial, axial, and equatorial configurations, respectively. The NOESY correlation between H1 and H2 indicated their gauche relationship.

It is noteworthy that the GDP derivative was detected in the culture supernatant. However, it remains unclear whether 13 is actively exported from the cell or is detected extracellularly as a result of cell lysis.

In Vitro Assay of Man17

Since 13 is a GDP-linked sugar, we presumed that the pathway began from the activation of a sugar-1-phosphate with GDP derivatization catalyzed by Man17, which was annotated as a nucleotidyl transferase. The anti relationship between H2 and H3 in the pentose moiety of 13presumably retained throughout the biosynthetic sequencesupports 7 as the original sugar precursor (Figures c and a). To confirm 7 as the precursor of the pathway, we performed an in vitro reaction of Man17 using 7 and GTP as the substrates. LC–MS analysis of the reaction mixture revealed the formation of GDP-N-acetylglucosamine (14), which was detected only in the presence of Man17, thus establishing the start of the pathway (Figure ).

(b) EIC of the in vitro formation of GDP-N-acetylglucosamine by Man17 observed with the ESI positive ion mode at m/z 647.11 corresponding to the formula [14 + H]+.

Subsequent conversion from 14 to 13 was likely catalyzed by Man20, Man19, and Man16 (Figure a). Man20 and Man19 showed significant similarity to AtmS8 (E value of 6 × 10–55) and CalS9 (E value of 2 × 10–33), respectively (Figure S7). Cal/AtmS8 and Cal/AtmS9 are reported to catalyze the carboxylate formation at the C6 position of U/TDP-linked sugar and the successive C4 oxidation coupled to the following decarboxylation of C6, respectively. Man16 showed predominant similarity with the characterized C4 amino transferase ArnB (E value of 4 × 10–67) that converts UDP-4-keto-arabinose to UDP-4-amino-4-deoxy-arabinose (Figure S10). Taken together, these observations, in combination with the in vitro assay of Man17, led us to propose the upstream part of the pathway leading to the Man18-catalyzed step in manlipyrrolamide biosynthesis: starting from 7 to form GDP derivative 13 with Man17, Man20, Man19, and Man16 (Figure a).

Conversion of GDP Derivative 13 to Compound X

To confirm the reaction of Man18, we conducted an in vitro enzyme assay of Man18 using 13 as the substrate (Figure ). The LC–MS analysis of the reaction showed the prominent consumption of 13 accompanied by the production of a trace amount of pyrrole 9 (Figure b). These results suggest that while 13 may be converted to 9, other compounds are likely to be formed as the main products. Careful and additional LC–MS data analyses allowed us to find m/z 173 and 191, which correspond to the one and two water molecule(s) adducts on pyrrole 9, respectively, as products of the Man18 reaction (Figure ). The extracted ion chromatograms (EIC) of m/z 173 and 191 displayed identical peak shapes (Figure S11), indicating that both ions originated from the same compound, hereafter referred to as compound X (Figure ). Therefore, we hypothesized that the direct product of Man18 may not be pyrrole 9, but X, and an enzyme responsible for dehydration, is required to accomplish pyrrole ring formation.

Man15 Is a Key Dehydratase

We hypothesized that Man15, which was annotated as glycoside hydrolase (GH), may function as a dehydratase for pyrrole 9 formation. Although GH enzymes have been found in other pyrrolamide BGCs, their functions remain unknown. ,, Since other genes are annotated as carbohydrate-active enzymes, GH may act together with them in the catalytic sequence. Although the results of an InterProScan suggested that Man15 belongs to the GH42 family (Figure S12), its sequence similarity to the characterized protein of GH42 is low (E-value = 4 × 10–5 to the β-galactosidase GanA from B. subtilis strain 168). While GH typically catalyzes the hydrolytic cleavage of glycosidic bonds, their functions are often diversified, and several have been reassigned to unexpected activities, such as auxiliary activity family 9, originally classified as GH61, the members of which function as lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases. − Taken together, these observations support the hypothesis that Man15 catalyzes a dehydration reaction, an activity that is rare but precedented among GH-related enzymes, including dehydration-coupled condensation reactions.

Furthermore, the deletion of cgc13, a homologue of man15, was previously reported to markedly decrease congocidine production, and the addition of 10 to Δcgc13 restored its production. This finding is principally consistent with our hypothesis that Man15 is involved in the formation of 9 from 13 in addition to Man18.

To elucidate the exact role of Man15, we analyzed the metabolite profile of an Δman15 mutant. In addition to the reduction in manlipyrrolamide production to approximately 15% of that by the wild-type strain (Figure S8), we observed the accumulation of a peak that was identical to that of compound X in LC–MS (Figure S13). To investigate whether Man15 exhibited dehydratase activity toward compound X, we conducted sequential enzyme assays involving Man15 and Man18, using 13 as the substrate (Figure ). The addition of Man15 to the reaction mixture led to the abolishment of compound X and the increased production of 9, supporting the conclusion that Man15 serves as a dehydratase in the pyrrole biosynthetic pathway.

Pyrrole Ring Formation Mechanism

Two enzymes, Man18 and Man15, are involved in the formation of pyrrole 9 from GDP derivative 13. While a previous study proposed that the GT-like enzyme Cgc10 catalyzed a nucleophilic attack of nitrogen at C4 on the C1 carbon to generate the putative intermediate 11 (Figure b), no GT have so far been reported to catalyze transfer reactions involving modifications of the sugar moiety, except for the stereochemical inversion at C1 (Scheme a). We instead propose a hydrolytic mechanism: a glycosyltransferase-type reaction in which water acts as both the nucleophile and the acceptor of the sugar moiety, yielding the six-membered ring hemiacetal 15 (Scheme b).

InterProScan suggested that Man18 belongs to either GT family 1 or GT family 4 (Figure S12). Regardless of its specific family assignment, the fundamental reaction of GT is the transfer of an activated sugar to a nucleophile (Scheme a). While some GTs are known to catalyze hydrolysis as a side reaction, none of them catalyze it as a main reaction. To our knowledge, Man18 represents the first GT that utilizes water as an intrinsic nucleophile within GTs investigated (Scheme b).

The five-membered ring hemiaminal intermediate 16 will be formed via ring–chain tautomerization through open form 15′ (15″). The five-membered hemiaminal will undergo spontaneous dehydration to form imine 17, which is spontaneously converted into enamine 11. 11 will be recognized by Man15 for further dehydration to form pyrrole 9. The enzyme activity of Man15 does not resemble any of known GH family reactions but instead resembles that of polysaccharide lyases, which cleave polysaccharides via β-elimination to generate an unsaturated hexenuronic acid residue (Figure S14). , These observations suggest that Man15 homologues represent a new subclass within the GH family.

Compound X, the product of the Man18 reaction, was not sufficiently stable to bear the isolation procedure. We assume that compound X is a mixture of several stable isomers of 15, 15′ (15″), 16, 17, and 11 under equilibrium.

Although secondary metabolites originating from N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) have mostly been nucleoside antibiotics and aminoglycoside antibiotics, they basically retain the GlcNAc moiety in the final molecule. Even in comparisons with rare sugar transformations, such as the conversion of a sugar to a C5 ring in aristeromycin or from a five- to a four-membered ring in oxetanocin, the conversion of a sugar-derived ring to an aromatized ring in the biosynthesis of 1–6 represents a unique strategy in sugar-derived natural product biosynthesis. , In a number of the nonenzymatic formation of pyrroles, several pathways are reported to use a sugar as the reactant. − Pyrrole formation through the Maillard reaction represents the most similar type of pathway in that a sugar serves as the sole carbon source of the ring; however, the mechanism is different. In Maillard-type pyrrole formation, dehydration occurs once prior to and once concomitant with cyclization, and ring closure involves a nitrogen between the C2 and C5 positions (Figure S15). ,

Oxidation of Aldehyde 12 by Xanthine Dehydrogenase

After pyrrole ring formation, the oxidation of alcohol 9 to aldehyde 12 was deduced to be accomplished by the alcohol dehydrogenase Man14 because its homologue Cgc17 has been shown to catalyze the oxidation of 9 (Figure and Table ). Although aldehyde 12 is further oxidized by Cgc3, an NAD­(P)-dependent aldehyde dehydrogenase, to produce carboxylate 10 in the case of congocidine biosynthesis, we did not find any corresponding aldehyde dehydrogenase in the man cluster. These results prompted us to hypothesize that there may be a noncanonical oxidase responsible for the conversion of aldehyde 12 to carboxylate 10 for later condensation with other building blocks through an amide bond in manlipyrrolamide biosynthesis.

Based on this hypothesis, we assumed that xanthine dehydrogenase-like enzymes, encoded next to the biosynthetic genes of 12 in the cluster, may function as the dedicated oxidase (Figure a). Within the man cluster, man6, man7, man10, man11, and man12 encode the subunits of the xanthine dehydrogenase-like enzyme complex (Figures and S16). A canonical xanthine dehydrogenase catalyzes the oxidation of not only hypoxanthine to xanthine but also xanthine to uric acid. While the eukaryotic xanthine dehydrogenase typically forms a homodimer, several types of heteromultimers have been identified in bacterial xanthine dehydrogenases. The xanthine dehydrogenase of B. subtilis is also a heteromultimer encoded by pucABCDE. , The function and mechanism of oxidation of the complex are proposed as follows: PucD, the substrate- and molybdenum cofactor-binding subunit; PucE, the [2Fe–2S]-binding subunit serving as a ferredoxin; PucC, the FAD-binding subunit from the electron transport chain; and PucB and PucA, proposed to function in molybdenum cofactor formation and its recruitment, respectively. Man6, Man7, Man10, Man11, and Man12 are homologues of PucC, PucD, PucA, PucE, and PucB, respectively (Figure S16).

To investigate the function of xanthine dehydrogenase-like genes, we constructed a gene-disrupted mutant of man11, which encodes a ferredoxin. The results of the LC–MS analysis showed the specific accumulation of a compound with MS identical to that of aldehyde 12 in the culture supernatant of Δman11. The structure of 12 was confirmed through isolation and NMR analyses, showing good agreement with previously reported spectral data.

To confirm that the xanthine dehydrogenase-like enzyme complex oxidized aldehyde intermediate 12 to carboxylate 10, a heterologous expression system was constructed in B. subtilis by using a genome integration vector. The conversion of 12 to 10 was examined by the addition of 12 to the culture of the heterologous expression strain. Oxidation was only observed with the strain harboring man6–man13; but not with the strain harboring man9–man13; therefore, we concluded that the xanthine dehydrogenase-like enzyme complex was responsible for the oxidation of 12 to produce 10 (Figure ).

Distribution of Pyrrolamide BGCs in Gram-Positive Bacteria

Enzymatic transformation from 7 to pyrrole 10 resembles the amino sugar biosynthetic pathway common in secondary metabolism: it begins with the activation of the sugar molecule by NDP derivatization followed by modifications to the sugar moiety, which is ultimately transferred to a nucleophile. Therefore, the presence of Man15, a GH-like enzyme identified as the enzyme responsible for pyrrole ring formation via dehydration, is a distinguishing feature for this pyrrole ring formation pathway. Since oxidases are also commonly involved in secondary metabolic processes, Man15 represents a new marker of pyrrolamide biosynthesis.

We searched for novel pyrrolamide compounds by genome mining using Man15 as a query. Man15 homologues from known clusters as well as randomly selected homologues were subjected to a phylogenetic analysis to examine the diversity of Man15-like enzymes and their associated BGCs (Figure a). The phylogenetic tree revealed that Man15-like enzymes could be broadly divided into three clades: clade I, comprising mostly Bacillus strains, and clades II and III comprising actinomycete strains. All known pyrrolamide BGCs belonged to clade II, whereas no known clusters were found in clade III.

To further investigate the gene cluster architecture, we compared the composition and synteny of representative BGCs (Figure b). Most of the BGCs in clades I and II displayed synteny similar to that of the man cluster and the known pyrrolamide clusters, respectively. On the other hand, a subset of the BGCs in clades I and II, as well as all the BGCs in clade III, showed minimal similarity to known clusters, suggesting that they encode the enzymes responsible for the biosynthesis of distinct compounds, potentially expanding the structural diversity of the pyrrolamide family.

GlcNAc is a fundamental building block of bacterial peptidoglycans and eukaryotic chitin. Moreover, O-GlcNAcylation is a well-studied and widely spread posttranslational modification that regulates metabolism in bacteria and eukaryotes. − Within these wide usages, the UDP-linked form was the well-conserved and the only known activation manner of GlcNAc. The only exception has been reported in the biosynthetic pathway of CMP-legionaminic acid, where GDP-glucosamine is acetylated to form GDP-GlcNAc. In this context, it was noteworthy that Man17 homologues are widely conserved in the putative pyrrolamides BGCs, suggesting that the pathway likely starts from the activation of 7 to form GDP-GlcNAc or possibly another nucleotide-linked GlcNAc, but not the UDP-linked form (Figure b). GDP linkage may be necessary for completing the transformation of the sugar moiety and the ultimate cleavage of sugar from the phosphate group in the pyrrole formation pathway. Furthermore, since 7 is a common precursor for both pyrrolamides and peptidoglycans, its GDP and UDP derivatizations direct the molecule into distinct metabolic routes, likely preventing interference or crosstalk between the intermediates of secondary metabolism and peptidoglycan biosynthesis.

Conclusions

Our study characterized manlipyrrolamides A–F (1–6), six new pyrrolamides from B. manliponensis, which represent the first examples of pyrrolamides from a species other than Streptomyces and possess a single pyrrole moiety and hydrophobic groups at both termini, a structural property distinct from known pyrrolamides. Biosynthetic investigation revealed that pyrrole formation initiates from the activation of GlcNAc-1-P (7) via GDP derivatization (Scheme a). Subsequent enzymatic transformations convert the GDP-linked sugar into a pyrrole-containing intermediate (Scheme b), followed by oxidation mediated by the xanthine dehydrogenase-like enzyme complex, a distinct feature from the Streptomyces pathway (Scheme c and Figure b).

Enzyme Reactions Characterized in this Study

The dedicated enzyme Man15, which has previously been overlooked, shows potential as a genomic marker for characterizing putative pyrroleamide BGCs across diverse genomic contexts. In addition, the use of GDP-linked intermediates highlights an intriguing strategy for maintaining pathway orthogonality during complex natural product biosynthesis.
