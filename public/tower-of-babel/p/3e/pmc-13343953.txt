AI in esophageal cancer: advances, barriers to clinical translation, and perspectives for digital health
Authors: Yuqi Yang, Xiao Jia, Xi Wang, Pingdong Cao, Jian Zhu, Chuanxi Wang, Zhe Yang, Qiang Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEsophageal cancer (EC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Accurate staging, treatment planning, and prognostic assessment are essential for improving clinical management and patient outcomes. In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) approaches integrating clinicopathological, imaging, and genomic data have shown considerable potential in these areas.Main bodyOver the past two years, research in this field has advanced rapidly, supported by the growing availability of large datasets and increasing adoption of multicenter external validation. Recent studies suggest that AI can improve real-time diagnosis and enhance the prediction of treatment response in patients with EC.ConclusionsThis review summarizes recent advances in AI applications for esophageal cancer, discusses current challenges, and highlights future directions for research and clinical implementation.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) remains a major cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, largely because it is often diagnosed at an advanced stage and is associated with aggressive biological behavior [1]. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) are the two major histological subtypes, and their distribution varies markedly across geographic regions. ESCC predominates in Asia, whereas EAC is more prevalent in Europe and the Americas [2]. Treatment selection in EC is strongly stage dependent. In early-stage disease, endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) or endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) can be curative in carefully selected patients [3]. For locally advanced disease, treatment depends on tumor location and extent and often includes esophagectomy, concurrent chemoradiotherapy, or a combination of both. Advanced or metastatic disease requires comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation to individualize systemic therapy. Because of substantial heterogeneity in histopathology, tumor biology, and treatment approaches, clinical outcomes and therapeutic responses vary considerably across patients [4]. These challenges highlight the need for more precise strategies to improve early detection, monitor treatment response, and enhance prognostic assessment in EC.

Artificial intelligence (AI) encompasses computational methods that can perform tasks such as prediction, pattern recognition, and decision support based on data-driven algorithms (Fig. 1) [5]. Machine learning (ML), a major branch of AI, enables algorithms to learn from data and perform tasks such as classification and regression without explicit rule-based programming [6]. Deep learning (DL), a subset of ML, uses multilayer neural networks to learn hierarchical representations from large datasets and has shown particular value in medical imaging and predictive modeling [7]. In oncology, these methods are increasingly used to assist image interpretation, automate quantitative analysis, and support clinical decision-making.

Several reviews have summarized AI applications in gastrointestinal oncology or focused on specific areas such as endoscopic diagnosis [8–10]. However, the clinical management of EC involves decision-making at multiple stages, including screening and early diagnosis, tumor segmentation and staging, treatment planning, response assessment, and long-term prognostic evaluation. To date, relatively few reviews have brought these areas together in a way that is closely linked to clinical practice. In this review, we provide an overview of AI applications in EC across diagnosis, staging, treatment evaluation, and prognostic assessment. We also discuss how these tools may support multidisciplinary care, facilitate individualized treatment, and help address current challenges in EC management. In addition, we examine key barriers to clinical translation, including data standardization, model generalizability, and clinical validation, and consider future directions for AI research in esophageal oncology.

Overview of AI in oncology

AI has been increasingly applied in oncology, especially in settings that involve the analysis of high-dimensional and heterogeneous data. In esophageal cancer, deep learning systems have shown encouraging results in tumor detection, grading, and characterization across histopathology, radiology, and endoscopic imaging [11–13]. Under controlled conditions, several studies have reported diagnostic performance comparable to that of experienced clinicians. More broadly, AI has also achieved notable success in other malignancies, including automated detection of lymph node metastases in breast cancer, noninvasive classification of glioma via magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and high-accuracy recognition of gastric cancer in endoscopic images [14–16]. Similar approaches are now being translated into EC for early neoplasia detection, tumor boundary delineation, and histological subtype classification (Fig. 2). These advances provide a useful technical basis for the continued application of AI in EC management.

Beyond diagnostic, AI is also being explored for treatment-response assessment and prognostic modeling in EC. Conventional evaluation criteria, such as the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), rely primarily on uni- or bi-dimensional measurements of tumor size and may not fully capture complex biological changes, including pseudoprogression or immune-related response patterns, particularly during immunotherapy or antiangiogenic treatment [17, 18]. In response, radiomics and deep learning approaches have been developed to extract quantitative features from pretreatment and longitudinal imaging data, often in combination with clinical variables, to generate individualized predictions of therapeutic response and outcome.

At the same time, advances in high-performance computing and multi-omics technologies are expanding the scope of AI in cancer research from single-task prediction to a more integrated characterization of tumor biology. In esophageal cancer, emerging frameworks combine radiomics, digital pathology, genomics, metabolomics, and proteomics to characterize tumor heterogeneity at both intertumoral and intratumoral levels (Fig. 3). By linking imaging-derived and molecular features with biological pathways and spatial context, these models may improve our understanding of the tumor microenvironment and help identify mechanisms of treatment sensitivity and resistance [19]. Despite the promise of AI in diagnosis, response prediction, and prognostic stratification, its use in routine clinical practice remains limited. Variability in imaging protocols, annotation standards, and patient populations continues to affect model robustness and generalizability.

Application

Diagnosis

Endoscopic diagnosis

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy remains the foundation of esophageal cancer diagnosis. Conventional white-light imaging (WLI) has gradually been supplemented by advanced modalities such as chromoendoscopy, confocal laser endomicroscopy (CLE), virtual chromoendoscopy, magnifying endoscopy (ME), and autofluorescence imaging (AFI), each of which provides complementary diagnostic information [20]. Among these techniques, narrow-band imaging (NBI) and blue laser imaging (BLI) are widely used for early detection, with diagnostic accuracy approaching 90% and sensitivity reaching 95.2% when combined with ME [21, 22]. However, visually guided assessment still misses or misclassifies some early lesions, and diagnostic performance remains strongly influenced by operator experience [23]. In this setting, AI may help standardize image interpretation, reduce interobserver variability, and improve the consistency of lesion detection during endoscopic examination (Table 1).

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma

AI-based analysis of endoscopic images for ESCC has progressed from early proof-of-concept studies to real-time diagnostic support. Early studies mainly relied on handcrafted or shallow-feature models. Shin et al. used handcrafted image features and demonstrated the feasibility of automated ESCC detection, although the computational burden limited immediate clinical application [24]. Quang et al. subsequently improved processing speed and enabled near-real-time analysis, helping narrow the gap between algorithmic performance and practical bedside use [25].

Advances in optical hardware have developed alongside algorithmic progress. Expandable optical fiber probes can enlarge the mucosal field of view, but this often comes at the expense of spatial resolution. To address this limitation, super-resolution convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been used to reconstruct structural details from low-resolution probe images. These models achieved diagnostic performance comparable to that of high-resolution reference imaging while further improving lesion visibility, suggesting that AI can compensate for hardware-related image degradation rather than simply interpret acquired images [26, 53]. For image-enhanced modalities such as NBI, the main challenge is not image acquisition itself but operator proficiency. Although NBI enhances mucosal and vascular contrast, its sensitivity may fall to as low as 53% among less-experienced endoscopists [54]. Convolutional neural network-based models trained on large-scale NBI datasets have demonstrated consistently high sensitivity, including reliable detection of small lesions, highlighting the potential of AI to reduce interobserver variability through more standardized image interpretation. In addition, some studies validated these models in consecutively enrolled patient cohorts rather than highly selected image datasets, which more closely reflects routine clinical practice and strengthens the clinical relevance of the findings [27, 55].

Beyond single-image applications, recent deep learning systems have been designed to generalize across WLI, capsule endoscopy, and video-based platforms. These models have reported high area under the curve (AUC) values and, in some settings, performance comparable to or better than that of experienced endoscopists. More importantly, several systems have maintained stable performance during real-time video analysis, even in lower-quality footage, addressing a practical limitation that is often overlooked in still-image studies [28–30]. However, the false-positive burden and its downstream effects on procedural workflow remain insufficiently studied. Repeated bounding-box alerts may increase cognitive load, contribute to alert fatigue, and prolong inspection time, which could ultimately affect procedural efficiency and clinician acceptance.

Barrett’s esophagus or EAC

Barrett’s esophagus (BE) is the main precursor lesion of esophageal adenocarcinoma and can progress from dysplasia to invasive cancer through a stepwise process [56]. Early neoplastic changes in BE are often subtle and may be missed during routine endoscopic surveillance, which can contribute to underdiagnosis. As a result, AI research in this area has mainly focused on detecting subtle morphological abnormalities that are difficult to identify by conventional visual assessment.

Early computer-aided detection systems that BE-associated neoplasia could be identified automatically, although limited lesion delineation and relatively high false-positive rates reduced their immediate clinical usefulness [41]. A few years later, Ebigbo et al. achieved substantially improved sensitivity and specificity for identifying early EAC on WLI and NBI images [45]. More recently, research has shifted from static-image classification to real-time and video-based detection systems that more closely reflect routine surveillance conditions. For example, De Groof et al. developed a computer-aided detection system based on consecutively acquired endoscopic images, which reduced the false-positive rate while maintaining high sensitivity, although the relatively small dataset limited its ability to capture the full range of clinical heterogeneity [46]. To address this limitation, a subsequent multicenter study trained and validated a video-based model using data from 380 patients across 15 centers in seven countries. By incorporating prospectively collected videos under standardized conditions, the study more closely resembled routine surveillance practice and demonstrated high sensitivity together with robust lesion localization, suggesting stronger clinical applicability than earlier single-center image-based models [47]. However, widespread implementation still depends on further validation in real-world practice settings. Multimodal approaches combining WLI, image-enhanced endoscopy, and video input have shown that complementary visual information may further improve lesion detection and localization [48, 49]. This trend suggests that AI research in Barrett’s neoplasia is moving beyond isolated image classification toward systems that are better suited to procedural variability and more capable of supporting reliable surveillance.

Pathological diagnosis

Despite advances in AI-assisted endoscopic diagnosis, histopathology remains the gold standard for the diagnosis of esophageal cancer. However, conventional slide-based assessment is increasingly challenged by growing specimen volumes, morphological heterogeneity, and substantial interobserver variability. In Barrett’s esophagus, agreement on dysplasia grading remains suboptimal, reflecting the subjectivity involved in evaluating subtle architectural and cytologic changes [57]. Early deep learning studies therefore focused on automated whole-slide analysis using fully supervised convolutional neural networks trained on expert-annotated data and showed that AI could improve dysplasia detection and recognition of atypical morphological features [58]. Subsequent region-detection frameworks further advanced this approach by separating localization from classification, which improved performance in borderline categories and more closely mirrored the workflow of human pathologists [59]. Beyond stand-alone classification, hybrid human–AI systems have also been developed as triage or prescreening tools. For example, these systems have been shown to reduce workload substantially while maintaining diagnostic performance across multiple specimen types, supporting their potential use in routine pathology practice [60].

In esophageal adenocarcinoma, weakly supervised learning has enabled cancer detection directly from whole-slide images without requiring pixel-level annotations, thereby reducing the burden of detailed manual labeling. Tomita et al. demonstrated the feasibility of this approach, although the use of retrospective single-center data limited its generalizability [61]. Later multicenter studies incorporated more heterogeneous pathological datasets together with interpretability tools and reported improved robustness, suggesting that dataset diversity may contribute more to clinical reliability than small architectural refinements alone [62].

For esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, researchers have also explored data modalities beyond conventional hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Yang et al. combined spectroscopy-derived biochemical signatures with neural networks to support pathological grading, illustrating that molecular-level information may complement morphology-based assessment [63]. In addition, Aalam et al. developed a pipeline based on ResNet-101 and a feed-forward neural network to distinguish ESCC from EAC, showing that AI may also support subtype classification under tissue-level labeling conditions that more closely reflect routine diagnostic practice [64].

Current pathological assessment still depends largely on two-dimensional sections, which carry an inherent risk of sampling bias and incomplete lesion representation. As a quasi-three-dimensional approach, hyperspectral imaging (HSI) integrates spatial and spectral information and may provide more comprehensive pathological data than conventional H&E slides [65]. Although HSI generates large and complex datasets, AI-based methods may improve its practical feasibility. Barner et al. used deep learning models to prioritize suspicious regions within three-dimensional datasets for expert review, suggesting that AI may serve not only as a classifier but also as a tool for data reduction and case prioritization [66]. AI in pathological diagnosis is moving beyond isolated slide-level classification toward more integrated systems that address grading variability, dataset heterogeneity, and the dimensional limitations of conventional histology.

Imaging diagnosis

Compared with conventional endoscopy and biopsy, noninvasive imaging offers a cost-effective approach for the early detection of esophageal cancer and has additional advantages in repeatability and quantitative assessment. However, several challenges remain in clinical practice. The large volume of imaging data generated during diagnostic evaluation places a heavy burden on radiologists. In addition, the esophagus is prone to collapse or inadequate distension, resulting in variable imaging appearances that may lead to underdetection of subtle or morphologically complex lesions. In this setting, AI may help by enabling more efficient analysis of large imaging datasets, including X-ray, computed tomography (CT) and MRI, and by improving lesion detection and diagnostic consistency.

Double-contrast barium swallow remains highly sensitive for detecting mucosal disruption and luminal irregularities, but its specificity is limited by a relatively high false-positive rate [67, 68]. Early computer-aided systems based on traditional machine learning classifiers mentioned that automated analysis of radiographic images could improve diagnostic performance under controlled conditions [69]. More recently, DL-based detection systems have maintained high sensitivity and, when combined with physician interpretation, have improved both diagnostic accuracy and reading efficiency [70]. Because most prior investigations were based on carefully selected, high-quality barium-swallow images, their performance under mixed-quality real-world conditions remains uncertain.

Computed tomography has evolved from subjective assessment of wall thickening to AI-based quantitative modeling. Early segmentation-based methods showed that objective measurement of esophageal wall thickening could assist lesion detection and substantially improve radiologist sensitivity, although reliance on a single anatomical feature limited diagnostic scope [71]. Subsequent convolutional neural network (CNN) models applied directly to CT images captured a broader range of features than wall thickness alone and improved lesion visibility and overall diagnostic performance [72]. Further refinements, including multi-scale feature extraction and online hard example mining, have led to additional gains in selected datasets [73]. While CT primarily mainly reflects structural abnormalities, positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) provides complementary metabolic information and may improve subtype discrimination and biological characterization [74]. However, most PET/CT studies in esophageal cancer have focused on staging or prognostic stratification rather than primary lesion detection, and fully automated detection remains relatively limited.

AI has also been used to diagnostically challenging scenarios, including tumors at anatomically complex sites and uncommon pathological subtypes. Multiphase CT-based radiomic models have shown strong performance in the diagnosis of esophagogastric junction carcinoma, while radiomics-derived scoring systems have confirmed value in distinguishing rare entities such as esophageal sarcomatoid carcinoma from ESCC [75, 76]. Despite these advances, most imaging AI systems are still trained on retrospective datasets acquired under relatively standardized conditions, whereas real-world imaging varies substantially across scanners, contrast timing, and patient preparation. In addition, the false-positive burden and its effects on workflow efficiency are rarely quantified. Multimodal fusion strategies remain technically challenging, and large prospective validation studies are still limited.

Liquid biopsy diagnosis

Liquid biopsy provides a minimally invasive and repeatable approach for the early detection of esophageal cancer, particularly in high-risk or asymptomatic populations. Among circulating biomarkers, aberrant deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) methylation patterns in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) have been extensively studied as early indicators of malignant transformation, with reported specificity of up to 91% [77]. Qiao et al. developed a machine learning model based on methylation features that further improved diagnostic discrimination, achieving specificity greater than 95% in plasma-based detection. The model also showed reasonable performance in early-stage disease, supporting its potential value in screening [78]. However, targeted methylation assays depend on prior knowledge of specific genomic loci, and the very low abundance of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in early-stage patients may limit detection sensitivity. To address these limitations, Li and colleagues explored alternative strategies based on global molecular profiling. Spectroscopy-based methods capture integrated biochemical signatures in serum without requiring prior genomic targeting. When combined with machine learning classifiers, these approaches achieved a diagnostic accuracy of approximately 85%, suggesting that non-targeted molecular pattern recognition may complement ctDNA-based strategies [79]. Collectively, current AI models for liquid biopsy in esophageal cancer mainly follow two approaches: locus-specific methylation analysis and global biochemical signal profiling.

Staging

Accurate assessment of invasion depth, nodal status, and distant metastasis is essential for TNM staging in esophageal cancer. Endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) remains an important tool for T staging, but its performance is limited by strictures, edema, inflammation, and restricted image resolution. Early deep learning studies based on EUS images showed promising accuracy, particularly for identifying adventitial invasion, but persistent pathological misclassification suggests that image-based models remain vulnerable to subtle boundary ambiguity and dataset-specific bias [80].

In endoscopic imaging, AI systems have shown performance comparable to or even better than that of experienced endoscopists for invasion-depth classification. However, very superficial submucosal invasion is still often misclassified as deeper infiltration, largely because accurate discrimination depends on subtle microvascular and microarchitectural changes that are not always clearly visualized on conventional white-light imaging [81, 82]. AI systems developed from magnifying NBI datasets have improved sensitivity and specificity for identifying deeper submucosal invasion [83]. Interpretable models incorporating microvascular pattern analysis further suggest that structured, knowledge-guided feature modeling may be more reliable than purely black-box approaches [84]. In addition, video-based training methods may improve the practical realism of T-staging systems by more closely reflecting endoscopic workflow [85].

Cross-sectional imaging also plays a central role in evaluating invasion depth and involvement of adjacent structures. AI-assisted CT analysis has evolved from conventional structural assessment to more integrated quantitative modeling. Wu et al. developed the ResoLSTM-Depth model, which combines arterial-phase contrast-enhanced CT features with temporal information and outperformed conventional ResNet-based approaches in assessing muscularis propria invasion [86]. Radiomics studies have similarly shown that intratumoral heterogeneity features derived from contrast-enhanced CT can help predict invasion depth [87]. More recent multimodal frameworks have incorporated systemic physiological indicators, such as skeletal muscle characteristics, and reported further improvement in predictive performance, suggesting that more accurate staging may depend not only on additional parameters but also on better characterization of tumor–host interactions [88]. Meng et al. also developed an intelligent analytical platform integrating multisource data with fog computing and machine learning techniques. Applied to high-resolution MRI, the system showed high concordance with pathological staging, supporting the potential value of multimodal fusion in invasion-depth assessment [89]. In molecular imaging, PET-derived maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) has also demonstrated value in distinguishing superficial from deeper lesions, indicating that metabolic activity may complement morphologic assessment as an indicator of biological aggressiveness [90].

Preoperative lymph node metastasis (LNM) assessment remains another major challenge, as conventional imaging modalities such as EUS, CT, and PET have limited sensitivity for nodal detection. Current AI research in this area has mainly followed two directions: pathology-based deep learning models and preoperative risk-prediction models that integrate clinical and radiologic features. Pan et al. developed a DeepLab v3-based semantic segmentation model using annotated whole-slide images of esophageal lymph nodes and achieved strong performance in independent testing, although such systems are limited to postoperative specimens and therefore cannot directly guide preoperative decision-making [91]. Several groups have therefore developed preoperative prediction models based on clinical and imaging variables. Chen et al. used six clinical risk factors from a large surgical cohort to build an artificial neural network for superficial ESCC, while Huang et al. used multicenter data and class-imbalance correction to construct an elastic-net model for T1-stage disease that outperformed both logistic regression and current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) stratification criteria in external validation [92, 93]. Other studies have similarly suggested that machine learning models may predict nodal involvement in early-stage disease more accurately than reliance on T stage alone [94]. In more specific situation, such as recurrent laryngeal nerve chain nodes, AI-driven models based on clinical variables have shown high negative predictive value for small nodes, supporting a more risk-adapted rather than routinely extensive dissection strategy [95].

Because the 5-year survival rate of metastatic EC remains very low, early identification of distant metastasis is critical for treatment planning and prognostic assessment [96]. Site-specific predictive models for lung, liver, and bone metastases have shown promising performance, indicating that organ-specific metastatic risk stratification is feasible [97–99]. Radiomics-based approaches that combine high-dimensional tumor features with clinical variables have also shown value in metastatic prediction [100]. Notably, models trained on the whole tumor volume rather than selected regions may be more robust because they capture spatial heterogeneity more comprehensively, which is increasingly regarded as a surrogate marker of metastatic potential.

Treatment

Response

Esophageal cancer is characterized by marked biological heterogeneity and a complex tumor microenvironment, which makes individualized prediction of treatment response particularly challenging. At the same time, imaging offers a noninvasive way to quantify tumor heterogeneity and derive biomarkers for response assessment (Table 2). In resectable locally advanced disease, surgery after neoadjuvant therapy remains the standard approach, whereas organ preservation may be considered for patients who achieve a pathological complete response (pCR). For this reason, AI-based response prediction has become an important area of research because it may help identify responders before treatment completion and support more individualized treatment strategies.

Most early studies focused on imaging-based prediction. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, Hu et al. constructed a a convolutional neural network-based framework to predict pCR after chemoradiotherapy and reported promising performance in external validation [101]. CT-based radiomics studies have similarly suggested that high-dimensional texture features can capture spatial heterogeneity associated with treatment resistance [102], while three-dimensional deep learning models have improved representation of volumetric tumor architecture and shown potential for identifying nonresponders [103]. Functional imaging provides additional biological information. PET-based radiomics analyses have indicated that tumors achieving pCR often show more homogeneous metabolic patterns on 18F-FDG PET [105], whereas diffusion-weighted MRI-based radiomics may improve prediction by reflecting therapy-induced changes in cellular density and microstructure [106]. Imaging-based AI can move beyond simple morphologic assessment toward a more biologically relevant characterization of treatment sensitivity, although performance remains variable across cohorts and imaging modalities.

Beyond radiologic imaging, AI models based on endoscopic images have also been explored for response prediction. Kawahara et al. were the first to apply convolutional neural networks to standard white-light endoscopic images for predicting pCR after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, and performance improved further after wavelet filtering was incorporated [107]. Subsequent transfer-learning approaches also reported favorable discrimination in selected analyses [109]. However, endoscopic AI mainly captures mucosal surface changes and may not fully reflect deeper stromal remodeling or molecular response. Its clinical value therefore likely depends on how closely surface morphology reflects the underlying biology of tumor regression.

More recently, predictive models have expanded beyond imaging to include molecular, pathological, and systemic host factors. At the molecular level, Eberz et al. selected a panel of therapy-related genes and developed a neural-network model with promising predictive performance [110]. At the tissue level, Chen et al. used deep learning to automatically detect and quantify tertiary lymphoid structures on routine H&E slides, linking features of the tumor microenvironment with immunotherapy response [111]. Systemic inflammatory and nutritional markers have also been incorporated into predictive models. For example, Zhang et al. identified neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), and albumin as independent predictors of pCR and developed a model with good performance in external validation [113].

Because single-modality models often show limited sensitivity and incomplete biological coverage, recent work has increasingly favored multimodal fusion strategies that combine radiomics with clinical, pathologic, and dosimetric information [117]. Yap et al. incorporated radiotherapy dose maps into the dose contextual representations network (DCR-Net) and showed that dose-contextual modeling outperformed conventional radiomics-only approaches. Digital pathology provides another complementary layer by capturing microstructural and cellular heterogeneity that cannot be resolved radiologically [114]. Building on this idea, Qi et al. integrated clinical variables, CT-based radiomics, and digital pathomics to construct a preoperative multimodal model for predicting pCR after neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy in locally advanced esophageal cancer, with better performance than single-modality approaches [115]. AI-based response prediction is moving toward multimodal models that better reflect tumor biology and may ultimately support decisions on organ preservation, treatment intensification, and individualized treatment planning.

Survival prediction

With advances in the diagnostic and therapeutic management of esophageal cancer, the limitations of the traditional tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system for individualized long-term survival prediction have become apparent [118]. This has increased interest in more refined, data-driven prognostic approaches. Early studies using artificial neural networks suggested that nonlinear modeling could outperform conventional TNM-based methods in predicting long-term survival [119]. Subsequent prognostic studies often used Cox proportional hazards-based nomograms to identify independent risk factors [120–125]. However, their reliance on a limited number of variables, linear assumptions, and point estimates may lead to substantial bias in real-world clinical settings (Table 3).

Researchers have therefore explored more flexible intelligent algorithms. Hybrid models combining rule-based systems with neural networks demonstrated that even small number of biologically meaningful variables could generate survival curves that more closely matched observed outcomes than Cox-based approaches [145]. Feature-selection strategies such as minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) further improved model stability by reducing redundancy among clinical variables. In early-stage ESCC, optimized deep learning models also showed improved performance in 5-year survival prediction than conventional statistical methods [127]. The later development of online prediction tools further improved accessibility and may facilitate clinical use [128].

Despite these methodological advances, most early machine learning models still relied mainly on clinical variables and conventional biomarkers, which limited their ability to capture the biological complexity of esophageal cancer. More recent studies have therefore incorporated multi-omics data into prognostic modeling. For example, an integrated model combining clinical features with cancer/testis antigen gene profiles showed strong performance in external validation and effectively stratified ESCC patients into distinct risk groups, outperforming single-modality approaches [133]. Recent progress in survival prediction has depended on both improved algorithms and the integration of biologically informative data that better reflect tumor aggressiveness, immune context and host condition.

Segmentation

Accurate segmentation is essential for quantifying the location, volume, morphology, and intratumoral heterogeneity of esophageal tumors, and it is a key step in diagnosis and target delineation. In esophageal cancer, manual contouring is time-consuming and subject to interobserver variability because indistinct margins, longitudinal growth, and the absence of a serosal layer make boundary definition difficult. These features make segmentation well suited for AI-assisted automation.

CT-based segmentation has evolved from single-structure delineation to multi-target approaches that are more relevant to clinical workflow. Because of its high spatial resolution, CT remains a major modality for anatomical delineation. Recent deep learning systems have enabled rapid automated segmentation of the primary tumor together with metastatic lymph nodes and adjacent structures on preoperative CT angiography, with case-level inference completed within seconds [146]. To address blurred boundaries and complex tumor morphology, Chen et al. proposed the U-Net Plus model for two-dimensional CT slices and further improved its clinical usability by incorporating a semi-automatic three-dimensional workflow [147]. Histopathology-based segmentation has achieved even higher spatial precision, likely because structural contrast is clearer at the microscopic level [148]. In endoscopy, segmentation is increasingly used to support early lesion detection and biopsy guidance under variable magnification and staining conditions, with real-time systems capable of detecting flat early lesions at the millimeter scale [149].

In radiotherapy, segmentation quality directly affects dose conformity and sparing of organs at risk, making reproducibility and efficiency as important as accuracy. Gross tumor volume delineation on contrast-enhanced CT is particularly variable in esophageal cancer because of low soft-tissue contrast and extensive longitudinal spread [150–152]. AI-based methods have therefore focused on reducing contouring time while improving consistency. Deep learning models performed to 18F-FDG PET/CT and fully automated three-dimensional CT architectures have both shown favorable segmentation performance while substantially shortening contouring time [153, 154]. While single-modality segmentation remains biologically constrained. CT may miss metabolically active but morphologically subtle lesions, whereas PET is susceptible to inflammation-related false positives. Reflecting this complementarity, multimodal fusion of CT and PET has outperformed either modality alone and shown robust performance in external cohorts, likely because it combines anatomical boundary cues with metabolic activity patterns in low-contrast or treatment-altered regions [155].

Despite this progress, many segmentation models are still trained on small, single-center datasets, which increases the risk of overfitting and limits broader clinical use. To address these issues, newer architectures have focused on incorporating both local and contextual information. The 2.5D U-Net integrates intra-slice multiscale attention with inter-slice context fusion, thereby improving robustness in the setting of blurred boundaries and tumor heterogeneity [156]. Other approaches have improved boundary fidelity through geometry-driven augmentation and edge constraints, with promising performance in early lesions [157]. Finally, MRI-guided online adaptive radiotherapy allows patient-specific contour updates as anatomy changes during treatment, reflecting a move from static population-based automation toward more individualized and dynamic segmentation [158].

Other

Esophageal fistula is a severe complication that may result from tumor invasion or treatment-related necrosis and can rapidly lead to infection, hemorrhage, or multi-organ failure, with a very poor prognosis [159]. Because conventional detection relies on operator-dependent endoscopy, bronchoscopy, and imaging, diagnosis is often made only after clinical deterioration. Recent studies have therefore shifted toward earlier risk prediction. An attention-guided multi-view convolutional neural network integrating contrast-enhanced CT with clinical variables achieved strong discriminative performance in a cohort of confirmed cases and identified tumor margins and intratumoral low-attenuation regions as the main predictive areas, supporting its potential value for identifying high-risk patients before or during treatment [160]. Similarly, radiation pneumonitis and hematologic toxicities arise from multiple interacting factors, including dose distribution, host condition, and treatment intensity, making single-parameter predictors inadequate. Deep learning frameworks that combine three-dimensional dose maps with clinical variables have shown good predictive performance and generally outperformed conventional machine learning models [161]. A dual-input network for grade 4 radiation-induced lymphopenia further demonstrated that integrating dose parameters from non-target organs with dose–volume histogram features improved predictive performance, highlighting the importance of dosimetric information and the value of multimodal architectures in toxicity prediction [162].

AI is also improving biomarker assessment by reducing subjectivity iin borderline cases and enabling noninvasive immune profiling. In immunohistochemical evaluation of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), variability in specimen handling, staining conditions, and spatial heterogeneity can reduce interpretive consistency, particularly near clinical decision thresholds. Attention-based CNNs applied to HER2 immunohistochemistry slides have shown high diagnostic performance and improved discrimination in borderline-expression subgroups, addressing an important limitation of routine manual scoring [163]. Beyond tissue assays, radiomics-based CT models have been used to estimate PD-L1 expression and CD8-positive tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte density, indicating that standard CT may encode latent signatures of the immune microenvironment [164].

Despite advances in radiotherapy techniques, the complex anatomy of esophageal tumors continues to make treatment planning time-consuming and highly dependent on clinician experience. Reliable dose-prediction models may help standardize contouring and dose constraints, thereby improving efficiency and overall plan quality. Dose–volume histogram prediction models based on distance-to-target information have shown low prediction error and outperformed conventional baselines, supporting their use for rapid plan-quality estimation and constraint guidance [165]. Compared with histogram-based approaches, end-to-end three-dimensional dose prediction may further improve dosimetric accuracy while shortening planning time and reducing inter-physician variability, with particular benefit for less experienced physicists [166].

Limitations and methodological challenges

With the rapid development of artificial intelligence, its applications in esophageal cancer have expanded to include diagnosis, prognostic assessment, disease monitoring, and molecular classification. Advances in big-data analysis, algorithm design, and improvements in model accuracy and generalizability have strengthened the technical basis for possible clinical use of AI systems [167]. However, routine clinical adoption remains limited. Although multimodal integration is often presented as a major advantage, current evidence still largely reflects feature-level combination rather than biologically meaningful integration. In addition, persistent challenges related to data harmonization, inter-institutional heterogeneity, missing data, and model interpretability continue to limit robustness and external generalizability. Addressing these methodological barriers will be essential if AI in esophageal cancer is to move beyond proof-of-concept studies and become a reliable tool in clinical practice.

First, the limitations of current datasets extend beyond sample size and also involve data completeness, annotation consistency, and inter-institutional heterogeneity. Many existing cohorts are retrospectively assembled, only partially annotated, and imbalanced across disease stages. In multimodal studies, incomplete alignment among imaging, pathology, genomic, and longitudinal follow-up data further reduces the effective sample size available for model development. Under these conditions, models may capture dataset-specific noise rather than biologically meaningful signals, increasing the risk of overfitting and reducing performance in external validation.

Second, insufficient multimodal integration remains a major barrier to further improvement in model performance. Many existing systems still rely on a single data modality, such as radiomics, metabolomics, or genomics, and lack effective fusion strategies, which limits their ability to capture the biological complexity and heterogeneity of esophageal tumors. Even in nominally multimodal models, imaging features, molecular variables, and clinical factors are often combined only at the input level, without explicitly modeling cross-modal interactions. Although such strategies increase dimensionality, they do not necessarily improve biological interpretability. Meaningful multimodal integration requires shared representations that capture the relationships among radiologic phenotypes, molecular pathways, and host inflammatory status. In this area, cross-modal attention mechanisms, joint embedding spaces, and graph-based modeling may offer useful methodological directions. However, current evidence for biologically coherent integration across modalities remains limited. Future studies should incorporate pathway-level validation, assessment of biological plausibility, and uncertainty calibration to ensure that multimodal models reflect tumor biology rather than simple statistical associations.

Third, incomplete data remain a pervasive challenge in real-world clinical environments. Excluding patients with missing modalities can reduce statistical power and introduce substantial selection bias, particularly in models for survival prediction and complication assessment. Advanced strategies such as modality-aware training, generative imputation, and uncertainty-aware modeling may partially mitigate this problem. Bayesian inference and probability calibration may further improve reliability by reducing overconfidence when important data modalities are unavailable.

Fourth, limited transparency in model behavior remains a critical barrier to clinical acceptance. Recommendations that cannot be interpreted are unlikely to be trusted by clinicians or patients, which limits the practical value of AI systems [168]. In many deep learning models, diagnostic or prognostic outputs are generated without a clear explanation of the supporting evidence, decision logic, or degree of uncertainty. Although visualization tools such as heatmaps and attribution maps may improve apparent interpretability, they often offer only superficial explanations and provide little insight into causal or biologically meaningful reasoning. Future systems should move beyond post hoc visualization by incorporating structured explanation modules, uncertainty calibration, and biologically interpretable intermediate representations. Hybrid frameworks that combine deep learning with rule-based knowledge or mechanistic constraints may offer a more trustworthy path toward clinically usable AI without substantially compromising performance.

Additionally, data privacy and regulatory governance remain essential for broader clinical implementation of AI. Federated learning has emerged as a promising strategy for cross-institutional collaboration because it allows encrypted parameter exchange without transferring raw patient data. However, several issues still need to be addressed, including communication efficiency, model drift, and the allocation of legal and regulatory responsibility [169].

Finally, most current studies remain retrospective and single-center in design. Single-center cohorts with short enrollment periods often show reduced performance during external validation. Historical datasets also frequently lack complete follow-up and are shaped by heterogeneity in treatment regimens, while observational studies cannot replace prospective interventional trials. Ultimately, the value of AI should be assessed not only by predictive performance but also by its capacity to improve survival, reduce treatment-related toxicity, and enhance workflow efficiency in real-world clinical settings.

Perspectives and future outlook

Artificial intelligence in esophageal cancer is gradually evolving from task-specific applications toward more integrated systems. Most current studies still focus on individual tasks, such as detection, staging, response prediction, or survival modeling. Although these approaches have shown promising results, they remain fragmented and cannot yet support end-to-end clinical decision-making. Future work should therefore focus on building unified frameworks that can learn jointly from diagnostic imaging, pathology, molecular data, longitudinal clinical records, and treatment outcomes. Such models may enable knowledge transfer across tasks and improve generalizability across different clinical settings.

Beyond models based mainly on imaging or structured data, large language models (LLMs) and advances in natural language processing may provide an additional layer of integration for multimodal clinical information. LLMs can extract and synthesize information from electronic medical records, pathology and radiology reports, and longitudinal clinical documentation, which may help reduce information fragmentation and support clinical decision-making. Their role may also extend beyond text analysis to the coordination of multimodal data, potentially helping to connect imaging features, molecular profiles, and clinical variables within unified representations [170]. This type of cross-domain integration may improve diagnostic refinement, longitudinal monitoring, and individualized treatment planning. However, LLMs remain susceptible to hallucination, bias, and context-dependent inconsistency, making expert oversight essential. Interpretability, privacy protection, and regulatory compliance must therefore remain central considerations, especially in cross-institutional use. If carefully implemented, LLM-based systems may help integrate currently isolated AI tools into broader clinical workflows [171].

Although AI-based survival models have shown potential in oncologic prognostic assessment, their inclusion in clinical guidelines will require stronger validation. First, model generalizability must be established through validation in independent external cohorts across multiple centers, geographic regions, and time periods. Second, evaluation should go beyond discrimination alone and include calibration, clinical utility, and consistency across patient subgroups. This is necessary to determine whether these models provide meaningful prognostic value beyond established systems such as TNM staging, pathological stratification, and molecular biomarkers. Prospective studies and real-world investigations are also needed to assess whether these models can do more than improve statistical prediction and can genuinely support clinical decisions, including adjuvant treatment selection, follow-up intensity, and individualized management within multidisciplinary care.

Interpretability and reproducibility will also be essential if AI models are to be incorporated into routine practice. Conventional machine learning methods, such as logistic regression and random forests, offer a degree of inherent interpretability, whereas more complex deep learning models often function as black boxes and are harder to explain in clinically intuitive terms. Future research should therefore aim to balance predictive performance with interpretability and adopt practical methods to clarify how models generate their predictions. Greater transparency may improve trust among clinicians and patients, strengthen collaboration between clinicians and engineers, and support wider clinical adoption.

At the same time, successful implementation will depend not only on technical progress but also on clinical and institutional readiness. Clinician trust remains fundamental, as concerns about reliability, transparency, and accountability may limit adoption. Institutional infrastructure, standardized training, and equitable access to AI technologies are also important determinants of real-world impact. In addition, AI systems must be adaptable to community healthcare settings, where resources and specialist availability may be limited. Ensuring usability, accessibility, and compatibility with existing clinical workflows across different practice environments will be critical for broad and sustainable integration.

Future research should focus on narrowing the gap between academic development and real-world application. Only through harmonized data infrastructure, biologically grounded multimodal integration, interpretable reasoning, and inclusive clinical validation can AI move from experimental development to a reliable and clinically useful part of precision oncology in esophageal cancer.

Conclusion

Artificial intelligence is becoming an increasingly important tool in esophageal cancer and may improve diagnosis, treatment planning, and outcome prediction. Although current studies suggest improvements in accuracy, efficiency, and standardization, clinical implementation remains limited by challenges related to data quality, external validation, interpretability, and integration into routine workflows. Further progress will require prospective multicenter evidence, harmonized data standards, and multimodal models that are robust, well calibrated, and clinically interpretable. Ultimately, the value of AI will depend not only on predictive performance but also on whether it can be safely integrated into clinical practice to support diagnosis, guide personalized treatment, and improve patient outcomes in esophageal cancer.
