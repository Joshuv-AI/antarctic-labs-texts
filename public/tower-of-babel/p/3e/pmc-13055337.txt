Plasma CHI3L1 as a marker of cognitive and structural brain changes in radiologically isolated syndrome
Authors: Raphael Schneider, Koroboshka Brand-Arzamendi, Timothy Reynold Lim, Lisa Eunyoung Lee, Melanie Guenette, Zixuan Hu, Errol Colak, Suradech Suthiphosuwan, Aditya Bharatha, Anthony Feinstein, Jiwon Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiologically isolated syndrome (RIS) is characterised by incidental MRI findings suggestive of multiple sclerosis (MS) in individuals without typical MS symptoms. People with RIS (pwRIS) may have subtle manifestations, including cognitive impairment. We aimed to examine whether plasma chitinase 3-like 1 (pCHI3L1) levels in pwRIS are associated with cognitive impairment and grey matter volumes in regions known to be vulnerable in MS.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 45 individuals with pwRIS and 21 healthy controls. Cognitive performance was assessed with the Minimal Assessment of Cognitive Function in Multiple Sclerosis (MACFIMS) battery, and global cognitive impairment was defined as failure on two or more MACFIMS subtests. Plasma CHI3L1 levels were measured using the Ella microfluidics system, and concentrations were natural log-transformed to reduce skewness.ResultsHigher log-pCHI3L1 levels were inversely correlated with hippocampal volume (R² = 0.1360, p=0.01) and thalamic volume (R² = 0.1265, p=0.02). pwRIS with global cognitive impairment had higher log-pCHI3L1 levels (p=0.03). Associations were also observed between log-pCHI3L1 levels and design fluency switching (R² = 0.1365, p=0.01) as well as executive function (R² = 0.1636, p=0.01).ConclusionsLog-pCHI3L1 levels were associated with cognitive impairment, hippocampal volume and thalamic volume in pwRIS. These results suggest that pCHI3L1 may represent a blood biomarker of cognitive dysfunction and neurodegeneration in RIS.Keywords: MULTIPLE SCLEROSIS, IMMUNOLOGY, MRI, COGNITION

Introduction

Radiologically isolated syndrome (RIS) is characterised by the incidental discovery of demyelinating lesions in the central nervous system (CNS) on MRI in individuals who do not exhibit ‘typical’ multiple sclerosis (MS) symptoms.1 An early study described that about one-third of people with RIS (pwRIS) progressed to clinically isolated syndrome (CIS) or MS within the first 5 years of follow-up,2 and subsequent studies have demonstrated that the cumulative probability of developing clinical symptoms consistent with MS is approximately 50% at 10 years.3 Key risk factors for pwRIS developing MS symptoms include younger age, male sex, the presence of spinal cord lesions and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-restricted oligoclonal bands. Additionally, the development of new T2 lesions or gadolinium-enhancing lesions on follow-up MRIs has been shown to independently increase the risk of developing MS.3,6

Two recent trials found that dimethyl fumarate and teriflunomide significantly reduced the risk of first MS symptoms in pwRIS (Assessment of Tecfidera in Radiologically Isolated Syndrome study, ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT02739542, and Teriflunomide in Radiologically Isolated Syndrome, ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03122652).7 8 We previously reported that most pwRIS in our cohort (~90%) have a high proportion of central vein sign positive (CVS+) white matter lesions (WMLs), similar to established MS, suggesting that these WMLs result from perivenular inflammation and demyelination.9 Moreover, we demonstrated that about 60% of pwRIS have at least one paramagnetic rim lesion (PRL), indicating chronic, active inflammation even prior to typical clinical symptoms becoming apparent.10 In addition to MRI measures, several studies have demonstrated the potential utility of fluid biomarkers to guide clinical management in pwRIS. A recent study demonstrated that cerebrospinal fluid neurofilament light protein (NfL) levels greater than 260 pg/mL and serum NfL (sNfL) levels exceeding 5.0 pg/mL are independent predictors of clinical conversion from RIS to MS.11 We also previously reported that higher levels of plasma glial fibrillar acidic protein (GFAP) were associated with MRI measures indicative of inflammation and tissue damage, including CVS+lesions, PRLs and black holes (BHs) in pwRIS. At the same time, plasma NfL did not correlate with these measures.12

Chitinase 3-like-1 protein (CHI3L1) is an emerging fluid biomarker in the MS field, involved in tissue remodelling, inflammation and immune response regulation, and is secreted by various cell types, including astrocytes and microglia.13 Previous studies have found that higher CSF CHI3L1 levels are associated with an increased risk for disability progression in MS.14,16 Recently, CHI3L1 has also been measured in serum and elevated levels have been linked to future increases in global disability (based on the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score) in primary progressive MS.17 These findings raise the possibility that CHI3L1 could similarly reflect early pathophysiological changes in RIS.

Our study measured plasma CHI3L1 (pCHI3L1) levels in pwRIS and examined their associations with volumes in specific brain regions that have been associated with disease progression and cognitive symptoms in MS.18 Moreover, we analysed cognitive testing data to evaluate whether pCHI3L1 levels are associated with cognitive measures in pwRIS, building on our group’s earlier findings that cognitive impairment can be present in pwRIS.19

Methods

Study design

This study was a cross-sectional analysis of an observational cohort comprising 45 pwRIS and 21 healthy control (HC) participants recruited from the BARLO MS Centre in Toronto, Canada. The BARLO RIS Cohort Study began in 2015, and the cohort for this specific study was examined between October 2020 and October 2023, with MRI acquisition and plasma collection during that period. Inclusion criteria required that all pwRIS exhibit brain MRI lesions consistent with the 2009 published criteria for RIS.1 Participants contributed demographic and clinical data, underwent brain MRI scans and provided blood samples. Cognitive performance was assessed using the Minimal Assessment of Cognitive Function in Multiple Sclerosis (MACFIMS) battery, a validated neuropsychological assessment tool in MS research.20 The adapted MACFIMS includes the Paced Auditory Serial Addition Test (PASAT) for working memory and processing speed,21 the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) for information processing speed,22 the California Verbal Learning Test - Second Edition (CVLT-II) for verbal learning and memory,23 the Brief Visuospatial Memory Test - Revised (BVMT-R) for visuospatial memory,24 the Controlled Oral Word Association Test (COWAT) for verbal fluency,25 the Delis-Kaplan Executive Function System (D-KEFS) Sorting Test for executive function26 and the Judgment of Line Orientation (JLO) test for visuospatial perception.27 Global cognitive impairment was defined as failure (≥1.5 SD below normative mean) on two or more MACFIMS subtests, consistent with established criteria.28 HCs had MRIs to rule out lesions, although they were not included in volumetric analyses and did not complete cognitive testing.

MRI acquisition

A 3T MRI scanner (Siemens Skyra, Erlangen, Germany) was used to acquire three-dimensional (3D) T1 magnetisation-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (T1-MPRAGE), three-dimensional T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR), and three-dimensional T2*-weighted segmented echo-planar imaging (EPI) with magnitude and phase images of the brain.9 WMLs were segmented using the Sequence Adaptive Multimodal Segmentation tool, which uses both 3D T1-weighted and 3D T2-FLAIR images to improve lesion detection. Volumes are expressed as normalised fractions.

Image analysis

T1-MPRAGE and T2-FLAIR scans were processed using FreeSurfer version 7.4 with the standard ‘recon-all’ pipeline. This enabled automated segmentation of whole-brain, cortical and subcortical grey matter (thalamus and hippocampus). Volumes of bilateral subcortical structures were averaged across hemispheres to yield a single mean value per structure. All volumetric measures were normalised to total intracranial volume (TIV) to account for inter-individual differences in head size. Segmentation outputs were visually inspected for accuracy, and any errors were corrected according to FreeSurfer guidelines. These normalised, averaged regional brain volumes were used in all statistical analyses.

Blood

Venous blood was collected into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes on the same day as the MRI and cognitive testing and immediately centrifuged at 1800 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The plasma supernatants were then transferred to 1.5 mL cryotubes and stored at −80°C. Plasma levels of CHI3L1 were detected using the Biotechne Ella kit (Catalogue # SPCKB-PS-000255), following the manufacturer’s instructions. This assay has a detection limit of 6.68 pg/mL, ensuring high sensitivity in measuring CHI3L1 concentrations in plasma samples, averaging triplicates, as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in RStudio (version 2024.12.1) using base R functions together with the dplyr, readxl, purrr and stats packages. Data visualisation was performed in GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0 (27 May 2025). Differences in pCHI3L1 concentrations between sexes were assessed using a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test. Raw pCHI3L1 values were not normally distributed. To permit parametric analyses, pCHI3L1 concentrations were log-transformed to reduce skewness. Because brain volumetrics strongly correlated with age, they were regressed on age to obtain age-adjusted residuals. Associations between log-pCHI3L1 and MRI volumes or cognitive Z-scores were evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients. The cognitive Z-scores data were calculated using local reference values, and regression-based norms were adjusted simultaneously for age, sex and years of education, according to Walker et al.29 Failure on each test was defined as a score of 1.5 SD below the normative mean, based on the Canadian normative data for MACFIMS. Between-group comparisons of log-pCHI3L1 levels in individuals with and without global cognitive impairment were performed using Welch’s t test. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Correction for multiple comparisons was applied using the Bonferroni method, with statistical significance defined as p<0.05 divided by the number of comparisons.

Results

Discussion

The optimal clinical management of pwRIS remains uncertain, as a significant proportion are at high risk of developing MS symptoms, while others may remain asymptomatic. Recent clinical trials have shown the benefits of dimethyl fumarate and teriflunomide in reducing the risk of pwRIS developing MS symptoms,7 8 highlighting the importance of starting DMT in those at high risk of developing symptoms. It is evident that better prognostic biomarkers to guide clinical management are an unmet need in pwRIS. This study investigated whether pCHI3L1 levels are associated with regional brain volumes and cognitive performance in pwRIS. Our findings suggest that pCHI3L1 levels are associated with cognitive impairment and reduced volumes of key subcortical grey matter (GM) regions. PwRIS with global cognitive impairment had significantly higher log-pCHI3L1 levels than those without impairment. Moreover, domain-specific analyses revealed moderate negative correlations between log-pCHI3L1 and Z-scores on D-KEFS executive function measures, which are known to rely on thalamic and hippocampal integrity.30 31 These findings raise the possibility that pCHI3L1 may reflect subclinical pathological changes in RIS.

CHI3L1 has been implicated in innate immune activity within the CNS and is primarily expressed by microglia, macrophages and astrocytes.32 Its expression is regulated by inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin 6, interferon gamma, interleukin 1 beta, and tumour necrosis factor alpha, and CHI3L1 interacts with several receptors, including interleukin 13 receptor alpha 2, transmembrane protein 219, and the receptor for advanced glycation end products.13 CHI3L1 expression, independent of lymphocyte infiltrates, was correlated with neurodegeneration in a postmortem study.32 Moreover, a very recent study found pCHI3L1 levels were higher in progressive MS and associated with increased brain glial activation on translocator protein positron emission tomography, suggesting that pCHI3L1 could serve as a biomarker for smouldering inflammation.33

Regional GM loss, including thalamic atrophy, has been associated with cognitive and global disability in MS, as well as longitudinal disability accumulation18 34 and may be superior to whole brain measures for predicting disability progression.18 Previous studies have shown that pwRIS have lower normalised cortical, mean thalamic and hippocampal volumes than healthy controls35 36 and that atrophy of these regions is associated with cognitive symptoms and disease progression.37

A previous study demonstrated the potential utility of CSF CHI3L1 as a biomarker for disease activity in MS, with CSF CHI3L1 levels distinguishing between individuals with CIS who did not convert and those who converted early to clinically definite MS.16 In a previous study in pwRIS, CSF CHI3L1 levels were also found to correlate with other CSF findings, such as the presence of oligoclonal bands or elevated IgG index; however, CHI3L1 was not a predictor of faster conversion to relapsing-remitting MS.38 On the other hand, when CHI3L1 was measured in both CSF and serum in people with CIS, only CSF CHI3L1 levels showed significant differences between those diagnosed with MS during the follow-up period and those not. Although serum levels were elevated compared with controls in these CIS participants, they did not meaningfully correlate with MS progression.39 Longitudinal studies will be necessary to establish whether CHI3L1 levels in plasma can predict the risk of future disease progression over time. Moreover, standardisation of CHI3L1 measurement is needed due to variability in sample types and assay methods across studies. We measured CHI3L1 in plasma, as in Ahola et al,33 while Fissolo et al17 used serum. Assay methods also differ across studies (ELISA,33 single molecule array17 and microfluidics/Ella), and a standardised approach has yet to be established. It also remains unclear whether factors such as Body Mass Index, comorbidities or systemic sources influence CHI3L1 concentrations in blood, and this should be examined in future studies.

Our study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causality between pCHI3L1 levels and structural brain changes. Longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the temporal relationship between biomarker fluctuations and disease progression. Second, although our sample size is quite large for a single-centre RIS cohort and adequate for exploratory purposes, the study population is still relatively small due to the rarity of RIS; thus, there is a possibility of Type I and II errors, and our findings should be confirmed in subsequent studies. However, despite the relatively small sample size, the number of RIS subjects we evaluated aligns with prior studies of RIS.40

In summary, we found that higher plasma CHI3L1 levels in pwRIS were associated with reduced thalamic and hippocampal volumes and with poorer cognitive performance on tasks dependent on these regions. These findings suggest that pCHI3L1 may reflect early pathological changes in RIS, even in the absence of clinical symptoms. Longitudinal studies in larger cohorts are warranted to validate these findings and to assess the prognostic value of pCHI3L1 in the earliest stages of MS.
