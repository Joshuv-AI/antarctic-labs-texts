First Transcatheter Pulmonary Valve Replacement in Tanzania
Authors: Megha B. Unadkat, Deogratias Nkya, Stella Mongella, Joao D. Fontes, Gilson Santos, Christopher Leemars, Joanna Ghobrial, Peter Kisenge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTranscatheter pulmonary valve implantation (TPVI) has emerged as a viable alternative to surgical pulmonary valve replacement for patients with congenital heart disease and right ventricular outflow tract dysfunction. However, its adoption in low-resource settings has been limited.Case SummaryWe report the first successful TPVI procedures in Tanzania. Case 1 involved a 16-year-old male with Noonan syndrome and prior homograft conduit for pulmonary atresia and ventricular septal defect. Case 2 was a 16-year-old female with repaired Tetralogy of Fallot who had undergone prior surgical valve replacement with a Trifecta valve. Both patients presented with severe right ventricular outflow tract dysfunction and underwent successful TPVI with a 20-mm Melody valve (Medtronic) without complications. Postprocedural imaging confirmed hemodynamic improvement and symptom relief.DiscussionThese first-in-country cases demonstrate the feasibility of TPVI in resource-limited settings. Broader implementation will require cost-reduction strategies, industry collaboration, and provider training to overcome financial and technical barriers.Take-Home MessageTPVI can be safely implemented in low- and middle-income countries through structured planning and partnerships.

Over the past 25 years, transcatheter pulmonary valve implantation (TPVI) has emerged as the standard of care for native or conduit abnormalities of the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) in patients with congenital heart disease (CHD) to minimize redo-sternotomies.1 The first successful TPVI was reported in 2000 by Bonhoeffer et al2, performed on a 12-year-old boy with stenosis of the Carpentier-Edwards RVOT conduit. This groundbreaking procedure led to the development and approval of the Melody (Medtronic) transcatheter pulmonary valve (TPV) in 2010, marking a significant advancement in CHD management.3 Current indications for TPVI include symptomatic or asymptomatic right ventricular dysfunction, right ventricular dilatation, and arrhythmia arising from moderate to severe native or conduit pulmonary valve stenosis (PS) or pulmonary valve regurgitation (PR), primarily due to repaired CHD.4,5Take-Home Messages•Transcatheter pulmonary valve implantation can be safely performed in low-resource settings with meticulous case selection and detailed planning.•Sustainable transcatheter pulmonary valve implantation programs need coordinated efforts from governments, hospitals, medical device industry, and global partners to ensure affordability, training, and policy support.

Despite these advancements, and adoption of this medical technology in Europe and North America, access to TPVI remains limited in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) due to infrastructure limitations, high procedural costs, and lack of specialized training. However, several LMICs have demonstrated this procedure's feasibility, with South Africa reporting its first 3 successful cases in 2015 at the Universitas Academic Hospital, demonstrating it as a viable alternative to surgical RVOT reconstruction in resource-limited settings.6 In India, a multicenter study demonstrated successful TPVI in 15 patients across 4 centers.7 These examples, although limited, provide compelling support for the safe implementation of TPVI in LMICs through strategic planning, international collaboration, and training.

Building on this global experience, we report the first 2 cases of successful TPVI using the Melody bovine jugular valve (Medtronic) at Jakaya Kikwete Cardiac Institute (JKCI), the only tertiary cardiac hospital in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. JKCI offers a range of services including open percutaneous cardiac interventions, open-heart surgery, and cardiac imaging, including both transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiograms. Advanced imaging modalities are limited by the availability of specialized personnel and infrastructure. The successful execution of TPVI in this context underscores both the clinical capability and growing potential for complex congenital heart procedures in Tanzania and similar low-resource settings.

Case 1

A 16-year-old African male with Noonan syndrome and a history of pulmonary atresia with a ventricular septal defect underwent surgical repair with an 18-mm pulmonary homograft to create a right ventricle-to-pulmonary artery connection at the age of 4. A recent hospitalization for persistent low-grade fever led to a diagnosis of infective endocarditis, treated successfully with 6 weeks of intravenous antibiotics. He re-presented with fatigue and exercise intolerance. Examination revealed dysmorphic features of Noonan syndrome, a systolic ejection murmur (grade 3/6), and a diastolic murmur (grade 2/4). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated moderate pulmonary homograft stenosis with a peak gradient of 57 mm Hg and mean gradient of 29 mm Hg, by using continuous wave (CW) Doppler. There was a pan-diastolic flow reversal on CW, indicating severe PR (Figure 1). This was confirmed by computed tomography (CT) scan, which showed homograft calcification and a conduit diameter of 18 mm (Figure 2). Coronary assessment showed a low risk of coronary compression. Given the resolution of bacteremia, a decision was made to proceed with TPVI as a bridge to a future redo-surgical conduit replacement given the patient's young age and a lack of present resources for open heart surgery. Under general anesthesia, right heart catheterization revealed a right ventricle pressure of 73 mm Hg and main pulmonary artery pressure of 53 mm Hg. Coronary compression testing with balloon inflation of the conduit was negative. A 3.9-cm 8-zig covered Cheatham Platinum stent was deployed, with postdilation using an 18 × 30-mm Tyshak II balloon. A 20-mm TPV was implanted using an 18-mm Ensemble system (Figure 3). Case 2

A 16-year-old African female with Tetralogy of Fallot and absent pulmonary valve underwent surgical repair with a Trifecta bovine pericardium pulmonary valve at the age of 6. At 13 years of age, she developed severe prosthetic valve stenosis requiring pulmonary balloon valvuloplasty. Despite this, she continued to experience progressive fatigue and exertional chest pain. Examination revealed a systolic ejection murmur (grade 3/6) and a diastolic murmur (grade 3/4). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a severely stenotic prosthetic pulmonary valve with a peak gradient of 67 mm Hg and a mean gradient of 32 mm Hg on CW Doppler, which also demonstrated pan-diastolic flow reversal in keeping with severe PR (Figure 4). Interestingly, despite severe PR, the right ventricle was not significantly dilated. CW Doppler (Figure 4C) showed presystolic anterograde flow in the pulmonary artery, indicating restrictive right ventricular physiology—likely from delayed repair and prolonged pressure overload due to progressive PS. Right heart catheterization revealed a right ventricular pressure of 66 mm Hg and main pulmonary artery pressure of 26 mm Hg. Angiography confirmed a severely stenotic prosthetic valve with PR. A 20-mm TPV was crimped onto a 20-mm Ensemble delivery system and implanted using a valve-in-valve approach. Due to the rigid frame of the Trifecta valve and maximal deployment of the TPV, postdilation was not performed (Figure 6). Postimplantation, the right ventricular pressure decreased to 55 mm Hg, with a peak gradient of 19 mm Hg. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated mild flow acceleration with a mean systolic gradient of 22 mm Hg and no PR. Both patients were discharged successfully and received prophylactic antibiotics to prevent infective endocarditis. Both remain on aspirin and under close follow-up at JKCI. At 30-day follow-up, they demonstrated improved exercise tolerance and favorable echocardiographic outcomes, with no complications reported. They continue to do well in follow-up clinics.

Discussion

RVOT dysfunction is a common long-term complication in patients with repaired Tetralogy of Fallot and other CHDs requiring RVOT interventions. Repeat surgical interventions carry significant risks, including increased morbidity, prolonged recovery times, and surgical adhesions. Therefore, TPVI offers a minimally invasive alternative with shorter hospital stays, faster recovery, and reduced long-term morbidity.8

One of the key procedural risks of TPVI is coronary artery compression, mitigated in our cases through detailed preprocedural CT and intraprocedural testing.9 Infective endocarditis also remains a significant concern. In a large multicenter registry of Melody valve recipients, there was a cumulative incidence of infective endocarditis of 9.5% at 5 years and 16.9% at 8 years, particularly in patients with prior endocarditis or elevated residual gradients.10 Our first patient's history of endocarditis warranted rigorous preventive measures including antibiotic prophylaxis, dental hygiene counseling, suppressive antibiotics, and close clinical monitoring. Stent fractures are another complication, often reduced by prestenting, which was performed in the first case and not required in the second case due to the indwelling nonexpandable surgical valve.11 These cases underscore the importance of individualized procedural planning based on anatomical considerations.

The patient profiles and indications in our setting differ from those in Europe and North America. Both patients had delayed primary surgical repair due to limited access to pediatric cardiac surgery, leading to earlier onset of RVOT dysfunction and younger age at TPVI. One also had a history of infective endocarditis, a complication more common in LMICs due to delayed diagnosis and limited preventive care. While long-term valve durability remains under study, TPVI offers a valuable bridge, delaying redo surgeries, easing surgical backlog, and supporting quicker recovery in resource-limited settings.

Despite its advantages, performing TPVI at JKCI led to unique challenges due to its resource-limited setting. The catheterization laboratory has a single, older-generation fluoroscopy biplane unit, which limits procedural accuracy and increases radiation exposure. Unlike hybrid laboratories in high-income countries, there is no capacity to combine real-time multi-imaging modalities. At JKCI, transthoracic echocardiography is the primary imaging method used, and transesophageal echocardiography is not routinely available. Additional imaging techniques such as cardiac CT and magnetic resonance imaging are not available on-site and require referral to a nearby facility (Muhimbili National Hospital).

Devices such as pulmonary valves, sizing balloons, and delivery systems are not available locally and must be imported. These devices are costly and often encounter lengthy customs delays and regulatory challenges. In both cases, the procedures were enabled through donations and collaborative logistics support. Where ideal balloon sizes or imaging equipment were unavailable, procedural decisions were adapted using existing resources to maintain accuracy and safety.

Tanzania has fewer than 3 interventional cardiologists trained in congenital structural interventions, and at the time of these procedures, none had prior TPVI experience. With no local pediatric interventional cardiology fellowship, on-site, real-time guidance was essential. Collaborations with Madaktari Africa and Moyo.Life enabled experienced international teams to provide hands-on training, didactic sessions, and case-based simulations. The TPVIs were performed at JKCI by local cardiologists with international support, which continued postprocedure through remote case discussions and imaging reviews to strengthen local capacity for future complex interventions.

Despite the existing challenges of delivering advanced medical treatments like TPVI in Africa, our success in this case series emphasizes the importance of institutional commitments in enhancing cardiac care in Tanzania. This dedication facilitated collaborations with specialized cardiac centers worldwide, especially from high-income countries, to strengthen human resource capacity for advanced cardiac care in the country.12 It also increased the availability of donated equipment, including pulmonary valves. Local collaboration with neighboring facilities is crucial, as we were able to assess our patients despite the absence of a cardiac CT scan at our institution.

Despite institutional efforts to implement advanced care treatment options, sustainability depends on many other factors, including local government involvement in healthcare infrastructure and acquiring necessary equipment and other related consumables, which have been the main challenge across most LMICs. A promising direction lies in the innovation of cost-effective, regionally manufactured valves. India provides a strong model in this regard: a recent case report described the successful use of a locally manufactured valve in a patient requiring TPVI.13 This significantly lowered procedural costs and proved to be both safe and effective. Such innovations highlight the importance of investing in locally adaptable technologies to scale up access to advanced cardiac interventions across LMICs. Another potential direction to promote sustainability is the potential for medical device manufacturers to offer low-cost equipment and tailored procurement models for LMICs. Industry partnership could help reduce cost, ensure access to essential devices, enabling broader dissemination of therapies such as TPVI, and importantly, help close the global equity gap in CHD care.

The development of local capacity through formal pediatric interventional cardiology training programs is essential. Creating a pipeline of locally trained specialists will significantly reduce dependence on external proctorship. Ultimately, our cases highlight the feasibility of performing this procedure in a developing country that relies heavily on local institutional willpower and long-term collaborations with willing local and international institutions to build and strengthen local capacity.

Conclusions

These cases mark the first TPVI procedures in Tanzania, proving the feasibility of percutaneous valve implantation in low-resource settings and setting a precedent for similar regions. While TPVI avoids risks linked to redo surgeries and long hospital stays, the high cost of valves and accessories limits wider use. Expanding access will require building local expertise, improving procurement, partnering with industry, and supporting affordable innovations like regionally produced valves.
