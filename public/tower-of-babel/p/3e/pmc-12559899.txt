Incidence, risk factors, and CT characteristics of radiation recall pneumonitis induced by COVID‐19 infection in lung cancer
Authors: Wenhua Yun, Junxu Wen, Xueying Zhai, Xiaoyan Yin, Xiangjiao Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRadiation recall pneumonitis (RRP) is a localized inflammatory reaction occurring in previously irradiated lung regions, typically triggered by certain anticancer agents. In clinical settings, we have observed that COVID‐19 infection may also act as a precipitating factor for RRP. However, its true incidence and possible risk factors remain poorly defined.MethodsLung cancer patients who received radiotherapy and were diagnosed with COVID‐19 between November 2022 and February 2023 were included. RRP was defined as pulmonary changes limited to the previously irradiated regions, occurring at least 6 months after radiotherapy. Patients medical records and radiation dose distribution data were analyzed to identify potential contributing factors to RRP.ResultsThe study included 140 patients who underwent thoracic radiotherapy with a minimum six‐month interval before COVID‐19 diagnosis. Among these, 62 patients (44.2%) developed RRP, and 45% of these experienced grade ≥ 2 pneumonitis. No radiotherapy dose‐related factors were significantly associated with RRP. However, statistical analysis showed that RRP incidence was significantly associated with baseline T‐stage (P = 0.034) and the time interval from radiotherapy completion to COVID‐19 infection (P < 0.001).ConclusionsA 44.2% incidence of COVID‐19‐related RRP was identified, which is notably higher than previously reported. While radiotherapy dosimetry did not correlate with RRP risk, baseline T‐stage and timing of COVID‐19 infection after radiotherapy were significantly associated with its occurrence.Keywords: COVID‐19, Lung cancer, Radiation recall pneumonitis

INTRODUCTION

Radiation recall reaction is an acute inflammatory response of uncertain etiology and pathogenesis that occurs in previously irradiated areas and is triggered by the administration of certain systemic drugs. First described by D'Angio et al. in 1959, 1 the condition primarily affects the skin but can also involve other tissues, including the lungs, intestines, oral mucosa, muscle layers, esophagus, and brain. 2 , 3 Among these, radiation recall pneumonitis (RRP) is a less common but more severe manifestation that predominantly affects the lungs. RRP is most frequently observed in patients who have received radiotherapy followed by systemic antitumor therapies. In addition to conventional chemotherapy, 3 , 4 targeted therapies 5 , 6 and immunotherapy agents, 7 , 8 recent studies have reported associations between RRP and COVID‐19 vaccination, 9 , 10 as well as COVID‐19 infection. 11 , 12

In clinical settings, we observed that many lung cancer patients developed new pneumonitis lesions following COVID‐19 infection or exhibited progression of preexisting pneumonitis or pulmonary fibrosis in previously irradiated lung tissue. Based on the timing of symptom onset and the characteristic CT imaging findings, we hypothesize that these cases represent COVID‐19‐triggered RRP. To explore this further, we conducted a retrospective analysis to investigate the incidence, clinical and radiographic features, and potential risk factors of RRP associated with COVID‐19 infection in lung cancer patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

This study was conducted at Shandong Cancer Hospital between November 2022 and February 2023, enrolling lung cancer patients with a history of thoracic radiotherapy (TRT) who were subsequently diagnosed with COVID‐19. The goal was to identify patients who had completed TRT prior to their COVID‐19 infection. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a minimum thoracic radiation dose of 15 Gy, as pneumonitis is unlikely to occur at lower doses, 13 , 14 and (2) RRP was defined as pneumonitis caused by COVID‐19 infection, occurring at least 6 months after completion of radiotherapy. 15 , 16 All new pulmonary abnormalities were systematically evaluated, including those arising in areas of preexisting radiation fibrosis. Infectious causes were excluded by bronchoscopy with microbial testing and/or clinical assessment. RRP was differentiated from tumor recurrence based on imaging findings and follow‐up data.

CT assessment

CT imaging data were collected from each patient before and after their COVID‐19 infection. RRP diagnosis was established using clinical records and serial CT scans, with all cases jointly reviewed and confirmed by a clinical oncologist and a radiation oncologist.

Pneumonitis severity was graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0 (CTCAE v5.0).

Statistics

To identify predictors of RRP development, appropriate statistical tests were applied based on variable type: categorical variables were analyzed using χ 2 or Fisher's exact tests, and continuous variables were assessed using either Mann‐Whitney U or independent t‐tests. Univariate logistic regression models were used to evaluate risk factors for grade ≥2 RRP. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 22.0), with statistical significance defined as two‐sided P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Basic clinical information of patients

From an initial cohort of 469 lung cancer patients with a history of radiotherapy and confirmed COVID‐19 infection, 140 met the inclusion criteria. Among these, 62 patients (44.2%) developed RRP (Figure 1).

Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of the 140 enrolled patients. The cohort consisted predominantly of male participants (76%), with 37% aged ≥ 65 years. Histological analysis showed adenocarcinoma as the most common subtype (41%), followed by small cell lung cancer (32%) and squamous cell carcinoma (27%). Most patients presented with primary tumors staged as T1‐T2 (54%), and a substantial proportion of tumors were located in the upper lobes of the lungs (69%). Curative radiotherapy was administered to 72% of patients, while 28% received palliative treatment. The median interval between radiotherapy completion and COVID‐19 diagnosis was 276 days, ranging from 144 to 2153 days.

CT Features of RRP patients

As detailed in Table 2, the 62 patients diagnosed with RRP exhibited a range of clinical and radiological features. Among these, 40% developed new‐onset pneumonitis, while 60% experienced acute exacerbation of preexisting pneumonitis. Based on CTCAE v5.0 criteria, 34 patients (55%) had asymptomatic grade 1 RRP, 24 (39%) developed symptomatic grade 2 RRP, and 4 patients (6%) experienced severe symptomatic graded ≥ 3 RRP. CT imaging most frequently demonstrated ground‐glass opacities (66%), with a diffuse distribution observed in 73% of cases. The median interval between radiotherapy and RRP onset was 255 days, with a range of 182 to 880 days. Of the 101 patients who received curative radiotherapy, 42 (41.5%) developed RRP. Notably, 11 of these cases occurred more than 1 year post‐radiotherapy, with the longest interval being 880 days (Figure 2).

Risk factor analysis for RRP

We further examined whether radiotherapy‐related variables were associated with the development of RRP. The parameters assessed radiotherapy type (curative vs. palliative), planning target volume, gross tumor volume (GTV), mean lung dose (MLD), lung volume receiving 5 Gy (V5), and lung volume receiving 20 Gy (V20) showed no statistically significant associations with RRP occurrence (P = 0.300, 0.497, 0.200, 0.759, 0.955, and 0.791, respectively). In contrast, both the initial T stage of the tumor (P = 0.034) and the interval between radiotherapy completion and COVID‐19 infection (P < 0.001) were significantly associated with RRP incidence.

Univariate logistic regression analysis was subsequently performed to evaluate predictors of grade ≥ 2 pneumonitis (G2+ pneumonitis) (Table 3). However, no variables demonstrated a statistically significant association with the development of G2+ pneumonitis.

DISCUSSION

This systematic investigation evaluated the incidence and risk factors of COVID‐19‐induced RRP in previously irradiated lung cancer patients. Our cohort demonstrated a notably high RRP incidence rate of 44.2%, substantially exceeding previously reported frequencies in this population.

Radiotherapy remains a cornerstone of lung cancer treatment, significantly improving survival outcomes and quality of life. 17 , 18 However, TRT commonly leads to radiation‐induced lung injuries, which are typically divided into two phases: an acute inflammatory phase and a late fibrotic phase. 19 The acute phase, known as radiation pneumonitis (RP), usually develops within 1 to 6 months after radiotherapy 14 , 20 , 21 , 22 completion. RRP represents a distinct form of RP, characterized by acute inflammation confined to previously irradiated lung regions, and may occur months or even years post‐treatment. The timing of RRP onset varies with the radiotherapy modality. While most studies define onset as at least 15 , 16 6 months after radiotherapy, others differentiate this threshold based on technique, citing 6 months for conventional fractionated radiotherapy and 1 year for stereotactic radiotherapy. 23 In accordance with widely accepted criteria, we defined RRP onset as ≥ 6 months following the completion of radiotherapy.

Although the pathophysiology of RRP remains incompletely understood, three major mechanisms have been proposed: radiotherapy‐induced stem cell mutations, vascular injury responses, and drug‐related hypersensitivity. 24 , 25 , 26 Importantly, radiotherapy is known to elevate proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin‐1 (IL‐1) and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6). Given that both cytokine storms and systemic inflammation play central roles in COVID‐19 27 , 28 pathogenesis, these same inflammatory pathways may also contribute to the emergence of RRP following COVID‐19 infection.

Patients with RRP typically present with a constellation of respiratory symptoms, including non‐productive or productive cough, dyspnea, and pleuritic chest discomfort. 29 The characteristic imaging findings of RRP include ground‐glass opacities and consolidative changes confined to previously irradiated lung regions. Current management is primarily symptomatic and supportive. According to the CTCAE v5.0 pneumonitis grading criteria, grade 1 RRP is usually self‐limiting and managed with routine surveillance. In contrast, patients with grade ≥ 2 RRP require close monitoring and medical intervention. Prednisolone, a commonly used corticosteroid, has demonstrated efficacy in relieving RRP symptoms and improving pulmonary function. 30 Tapering should be gradual to minimize the risk of rebound inflammation. Oxygen therapy may be administered as an adjunctive treatment to alleviate hypoxemia and improve tissue oxygenation. For RRP associated with COVID‐19, corticosteroid‐based anti‐inflammatory therapy should be combined with appropriate antiviral agents to optimize clinical outcomes.

During the COVID‐19 pandemic, some investigators explored the use of low‐dose radiotherapy (LDRT) to manage COVID‐19 pneumonia. Gaurav Dhawan et al. 31 proposed that LDRT (0.3‐0.5 Gy) could suppress the systemic inflammatory response rapidly while avoiding long‐term toxicities. However, David G. Kirsch et al. 32 cautioned that the supporting evidence remains anecdotal, with insufficient preclinical efficacy demonstrated in animal models of viral pneumonia. These opposing perspectives highlight the uncertainty surrounding LDRT's role and underscore the need for caution in clinical application. An editorial in Radiotherapy and Oncology concluded that any consideration of LDRT should involve stringent patient selection, potentially limiting its use to elderly patients with advanced, treatment‐refractory COVID‐19.

Multiple variables may influence the risk of RP, including patient‐specific factors such as age, smoking status, and baseline pulmonary function, as well as dosimetric parameters such as MLD, lung volume receiving 5 Gy (V5), and 20 Gy (V20 33 ). Previous studies have demonstrated that patients with lung cancer undergoing concurrent chemoradiotherapy are at significantly increased risk of developing grade ≥ 2 RP when the V20 exceeds 30%. 34 However, no consistent risk factor has been identified for RRP. In two studies by Chilu Chiang et al. and Cousin et al., MLD and V20 were analyzed, but no radiotherapy dosimetric variables were found to be significantly associated with RRP occurrence. 16 , 23 Similarly, in our study, aside from initial T stage and the interval between radiotherapy completion and COVID‐19 infection, no radiotherapy‐related dosimetric parameters were associated with the development of RRP. Larger, prospective studies with broader samples are needed to validate these findings.

This study has some limitations. First, its retrospective single‐institution design introduces potential sources of bias, including selection bias, heterogeneity in patient characteristics, and limited sample size. Further validation through multicenter prospective studies with larger cohorts is warranted. Second, standardized diagnostic and treatment criteria for RRP have not yet been established. In this study, we defined RRP based on prior literature as CT imaging changes occurring at least 6 months after radiotherapy. Lastly, the time of RRP onset was determined by the first CT scan demonstrating pulmonary changes, which was dependent on the imaging schedule and could have led to overestimation of the true onset time in some cases.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, the unexpectedly high incidence of COVID‐19‐induced RRP highlights the unpredictable nature of this condition. Neither our study nor previous investigations have identified definitive dosimetric parameters associated with its development. These findings underscore the need for further research and prospective validation in larger, multicenter studies.
