Phenotypes based on respiratory drive and effort to identify the risk factors when P0.1 fails to estimate ∆PES in ventilated children
Authors: Meryl Vedrenne-Cloquet, Y. Ito, J. Hotz, M. J. Klein, M. Herrera, D. Chang, A. K. Bhalla, C. J. L. Newth, R. G. Khemani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMonitoring respiratory effort and drive during mechanical ventilation is needed to deliver lung and diaphragm protection. Esophageal pressure (∆PES) is the gold standard measure of respiratory effort but is not routinely available. Airway occlusion pressure in the first 100 ms of the breath (P0.1) is a readily available surrogate for both respiratory effort and drive but is only modestly correlated with ∆PES in children. We sought to identify risk factors for P0.1 over or underestimating ∆PES in ventilated children.MethodsSecondary analysis of physiological data from children and young adults enrolled in a randomized controlled trial testing lung and diaphragm protective ventilation in pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (PARDS) (NCT03266016). ∆PES (∆PES-REAL), P0.1 and predicted ∆PES (∆PES-PRED = 5.91*P0.1) were measured daily to identify phenotypes based upon the level of respiratory effort and drive: one passive (no spontaneous breathing), three where ∆PES-REAL and ∆PES-PRED were aligned (low, normal, and high effort and drive), two where ∆PES-REAL and ∆PES-PRED were mismatched (high underestimated effort, and overestimated effort). Logistic regression models were used to identify factors associated with each mismatch phenotype (High underestimated effort, or overestimated effort) as compared to all other spontaneous breathing phenotypes.ResultsWe analyzed 953 patient days (222 patients). ∆PES-REAL and ∆PES-PRED were aligned in 536 (77%) of the active patient days. High underestimated effort (n = 119 (12%)) was associated with higher airway resistance (adjusted OR 5.62 (95%CI 2.58, 12.26) per log unit increase, p < 0.001), higher tidal volume (adjusted OR 1.53 (95%CI 1.04, 2.24) per cubic unit increase, p = 0.03), higher opioid use (adjusted OR 2.4 (95%CI 1.12, 5.13, p = 0.024), and lower set ventilator rate (adjusted OR 0.96 (95%CI 0.93, 0.99), p = 0.005). Overestimated effort was rare (n = 37 (4%)) and associated with higher alveolar dead space (adjusted OR 1.05 (95%CI 1.01, 1.09), p = 0.007) and lower respiratory resistance (adjusted OR 0.32 (95%CI 0.13, 0.81), p = 0.017).ConclusionsIn patients with PARDS, P0.1 commonly underestimated high respiratory effort particularly with high airway resistance, high tidal volume, and high doses of opioids. Future studies are needed to investigate the impact of measures of respiratory effort, drive, and the presence of a mismatch phenotype on clinical outcome.Trial registration: NCT03266016; August 23, 2017.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-024-05103-x.

Introduction

Mechanical ventilation (MV) may exacerbate both lung stress and diaphragm injury, ultimately increasing mortality, length of ventilation, and long-term morbidity [1–7]. Promoting the patient’s spontaneous breathing may prevent diaphragm atrophy, but if the patient’s respiratory effort is too strenuous, this may induce both diaphragm dysfunction and patient self-inflicted lung injury [8, 9]. Optimal conditions for lung and diaphragmatic protective ventilation are met when the patient and the ventilator synchronize such that the patient can maintain physiologic amounts of respiratory effort with normal neuro-ventilatory drive [10].

Esophageal manometry, in particular the esophageal pressure swings during inspiration (∆PES), is a good estimate of respiratory effort, but requires expertise and is invasive. PES monitoring is not routinely available at the bedside, especially for children [11, 12]. Less invasive methods have been developed to detect potentially harmful levels of respiratory effort using airway occlusion maneuvers. In children expiratory occlusion pressure (Pocc) is the best estimate of ∆PES [13]. Airway occlusion pressure in the first 100 ms of the breath (P0.1) can be measured easily during an end-expiratory occlusion on the ventilator, and many modern ventilators have automated calculation of P0.1. It has been proposed as a target to titrate ventilator support in adults [14–16], although importantly, in contrast to ∆PES, and Pocc, it is a measure that is also affected by the speed of the respiratory effort since it is only calculated in the first 100 ms of the breath. In children with pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (PARDS), our group found that P0.1 could predict ∆PES, although the correlation is weak [13]. In addition, there were many circumstances where ∆PES (effort) and P0.1 (drive) were not aligned, with P0.1 under or overestimating ∆PES.

Understanding situations where P0.1 may over or underestimate ∆PES is important for clinical decision-making, and we hypothesized that in children, the combination of P0.1 and ∆PES could be used to define phenotypes based upon respiratory effort and drive which would have different clinical and physiologic characteristics [17]. Furthermore, these clinical and physiological characteristics could be used to identify circumstances where clinicians should be cautious using P0.1 to estimate ∆PES for clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a secondary analysis of a single center randomized clinical trial testing a lung and diaphragm protective ventilation strategy (REDVent, R01HL124666, Clinicaltrials: NCT03266016) at Children’s Hospital of Los Angeles (CHLA). The study was approved by the CHLA Institutional Review Board. Children and young adults between 1 month and 21 years of age were included in the parent study when they met hypoxemia and radiographic criteria for PARDS. Exclusion criteria comprised contraindications to an esophageal catheter or conditions precluding conventional weaning (i.e. elevated intracranial pressure, pulmonary hypertension, critical airway, do not resuscitate). The complete protocol for the REDVent parent study has been previously published [18, 19].

Patients received synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation Pressure Control/Pressure Support (SIMV PC/PS) or Pressure Support/continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) mode of ventilation using cuffed endotracheal or cuffed tracheostomy tubes connected to an ICU ventilator (Servo I (Maquet, Solna, Sweden), NKV-550 (OrangeMed, Santa Ana, CA), or AVEA (CareFusion, Yorba Linda, CA)). Physiologic waveforms of PES, flow, and airway pressure (PAW) were recorded daily. Each day the patient remained intubated, a target of 3 inspiratory and 3 expiratory hold maneuvers were performed most typically in the morning between 8 and 11 am. PAW and flow were measured proximally at the endotracheal tube along with an airway pressure line and self-calibrating pneumotachometer (Viasys Variflex 51,000–40094; Conshohocken, PA). PES was measured using an age appropriate esophageal balloon catheter (Carefusion, Avea SmartCath 6, 7, or 8 Fr) with air inflated based on a calibration algorithm [20]. All sensors were connected to a custom-made hardware device (New Life Box, Applied Biosignals, Weener, Germany), which recorded data at a frequency of 200 Hz. Offline analyses were processed with a custom-built software program for breath annotation (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Patient days were not included in analysis if the ventilation mode was CPAP without pressure support or high frequency oscillation, and if PES or PAW waveforms were inadequate because of artifacts or inaccurate esophageal catheter position (confirmed on chest X-ray, or suspected by an end-expiratory PES (PES-PEEP) value either too high (> 20 cmH2O) or too low (< 5 cmH2O), with a difference > 3 cmH2O as compared to other days, and a failed occlusion test). We have previously found that plateau pressure measurements on the AVEA ventilator are inaccurate when patients have respiratory effort [13]. Patient days were therefore excluded from analysis if the subject was ventilated with an AVEA.

Measurements

Two investigators (Inter-rater reliability checked using a subset of 10 patient days with kappa statistics > 0.8) measured effort and drive variables during a maximum of three of each of the following recorded breath types for each patient day: inspiratory hold (Pressure Control (PC) hold or Pressure support (PS) hold), expiratory hold, PC breath, and PS breath. Inspiratory and expiratory hold breaths were excluded with air leak above 20% and/or artifact, and inspiratory holds required a clear plateau phase for inclusion. For each measurement, the median of available inspiratory and expiratory holds per day was used for analysis. P0.1 was measured during the spontaneous expiratory holds as previously described [13]. Respiratory effort was measured in patient-triggered breaths (PC or PS) using the inspiratory esophageal pressure swing (∆PES-REAL), which corresponds to the absolute difference between the PES at the end of expiration and the minimal PES reached during inspiration [11, 12, 21] (Additional file 1: Figure E1). Analysis

Each patient day was classified into one of the 6 phenotypes above. Baseline characteristics as well as characteristics related to lung injury, ventilator settings, gas exchange, respiratory mechanics, and sedation, were described in the 6 phenotypes. In addition, we described the trajectory of effort, drive, and phenotypes for each patient day over the course of ventilation, using the thresholds of ∆PES-REAL described above to categorize the level of effort (passive, low effort, normal effort, and high effort), and the thresholds described in previous studies for respiratory drive (passive, low drive if P0.1 was ≤ 1 cmH2O, normal drive if P0.1 was between 1 and 3.5 cmH2O, high drive if P0.1 was > 3.5 cmH2O) [13, 14].

To investigate risk factors for the occurrence of each mismatch phenotype we created two mixed effects logistic regression models adjusted for repeated measures per patient, comparing each of the two to the other phenotypes where spontaneous breathing was present. Because it may be more harmful to miss high effort, our primary analysis focused on the risk factors for being in the high underestimated effort group as compared to others. Secondary analysis investigated risk factors for P0.1 overestimating effort as compared to others.

Variables of interest were described at the patient level and at the patient day level (see Additional file 2). Patient level factors included demographic data, co-morbid conditions and lung injury severity at randomization. Patient daily data included: calculated static respiratory mechanics (resistance (RRS), respiratory system compliance (CRS), chest wall compliance (CCW), lung compliance (CL), lung elastance (EL)/respiratory system elastance (ERS) ratio); respiratory drive (gas exchange and dead space markers, minute ventilation (VME), neural respiratory rate); ventilator settings; and data related to sedation drugs (cumulative opioid/benzodiazepine dosing per day) and pain, sedation, and delirium scales. We also investigated the relationship between neuromuscular weakness and phenotype B membership, using the maximum airway pressure during airway occlusion (PiMAX) (from the first spontaneous breathing trial) as a surrogate for respiratory muscle strength [5]. Data were presented as medians (Interquartile range, IQR) for continuous variables and number (%) for categorical variables.

Univariate modeling was first conducted for each variable, treating the patient as a random effect to account for repeated measures. Multivariable models considered two random effects, the patient, and the patient’s day of ventilation. Candidate variables (p < 0.15 in univariate analyses) which remained statistically different (p < 0.05) and/or changed the estimates of the others (cofounder) were kept in the final multivariable models, with careful attention to avoid collinearity. Detailed statistical analysis is provided in supplement (additional file 2). Data were analyzed using R programming software.

Results

Cohort

Two hundred forty-eight patients were included in REDvent, with 1158 patient days from 225 patients included in this study. Concomitant P0.1 and ∆PES values were available in 692 active patient days, and there were 261 passive patient days, leaving 953 patient days (222 patients) for phenotype analysis (Fig. 1). Median (IQR) age of the total cohort was 67 (19, 151) months, with 76 (34%) patients having previous chronic lung disease. Pneumonia was the most common trigger for PARDS. Amongst all active patient days, median ∆PES was 8.2 (4.7, 12.6) cmH2O, median P0.1 was 0.9 (0.5, 1.6) cmH2O and these did not seem to differ across age categories (Additional file 1: Figure E3).

Among the 953 patient days analyzed for the phenotypes definition, 119 (12%) were classified in the mismatch group with high underestimated effort (P0.1 “low” median 0.7 (0.5, 1.05) cmH2O relative to “high” ∆PES of 15.0 (11.8, 19.7) cmH2O), and 37 (4%) were classified in the mismatch group with overestimated effort (P0.1 “high” median 1.7 (1.45, 2.13) cmH2O relative to “low” ∆PES of 3.2 (2.6, 3.9) cmH2O) (Additional file 2: Table E1). Phenotypes repartition among patient days with spontaneous breathing is presented in supplementary material (Additional file 1: Figure E4).

Evolution of effort, drive, and phenotypes overtime

The proportion of passive patient days decreased across ventilation days (Fig. 2). The frequency of patients with normal effort did not vary over ventilation days while the proportion of high effort seemed to increase over time, from less than 25% of patient days in the early phase of ventilation (day 0 to day 2) to up to almost half of the population after 7 days of ventilation. Low effort decreased overtime (Fig. 2A). Most patient days had low (ie P0.1 ≤ 1 cmH2O) or normal drive. A small number of patient days had high drive (ie P0.1 > 3.5 cmH2O), with the proportion increasing over time (Fig. 2B). The proportion of patient days where there was a mismatch with high underestimated effort increased over time, while the proportion of overestimated effort remained uncommon in all phases of ventilation (Fig. 2C).

Risk factors for P0.1 underestimating high effort

Univariate risk factors for P0.1 underestimating high effort as compared to all the others included patient related variables (home oxygen and/or noninvasive or invasive ventilation, pneumonia as a PARDS trigger, pressure ventilation support), differences in ventilator settings, gas exchange and respiratory mechanics (lower set and patient RR, lower peak pressure (P peak), higher VTe/PBW and VME/PBW, higher RRS), as well as differences in sedation regimens (higher opioid dosing, less pain (lower percentage of face legs activity cry consolability (FLACC) ≥ 3)) (Table 2). Age was not associated with high underestimated effort. Risk factors for P0.1 overestimating effort

Univariate risk factors for P0.1 overestimating effort as compared to all the others were related to oxygenation and gas exchange (more quadrants of consolidate on chest radiography, higher pCO2 and HCO3, higher end-tidal alveolar dead space fraction (AVDSf = [PaCO2–ETCO2]/PaCO2 [22]), respiratory mechanics (higher CRS/PBW and lower RRS) and sedation (higher level of benzodiazepines dosing and more pain on FLACC scale). Patients tended to be older (less infants in patient days where P0.1 overestimated effort, p = 0.130), but PiMAX was not associated with this mismatch group (Additional file 2: Table E2). The variables retained in the multivariable model which were independent risk factors for P0.1 overestimating effort were a higher AVDSf and a lower RRS (Additional file 2: Tables E2 and E3).

Discussion

Using a comprehensive analysis of daily simultaneous measures of ∆PES and P0.1 in mechanically ventilated children with PARDS, we identified circumstances where P0.1 failed to accurately estimate ∆PES. We found that P0.1 frequently underestimated high respiratory effort, with increasing risk over the course of ventilation, as high effort became more common. Risk factors for P0.1 underestimating a high ∆PES included higher opioid dosing, higher tidal volume and increased airway resistance. Circumstances where P0.1 overestimated effort, in contrast, were rare and seemed associated with higher degrees of alveolar dead space, and lower airway resistance.

Our study is the first longitudinal description of respiratory effort and drive in ventilated children with PARDS. We found that high respiratory effort was frequent during the acute phase of MV and increased over the course of ventilation, while the proportion of low effort and passive patients was more frequent in the first few days of ventilation. This trajectory is not surprising as spontaneous breathing effort likely increases as the patient is being weaned from ventilation. This highlights that many children in the early phase of ventilation are at risk for ventilator induced diaphragm dysfunction, with low effort. We also observed an association between PS ventilation and high underestimated effort in univariate analyses. This may be related to the trajectory of illness, with potentially different opioid and sedation targets, as well as greater tolerance for high effort during weaning. While the level of respiratory effort (∆PES-REAL) aligned with values often reported in adults, we found that P0.1 values were considerably lower than those reported in adult studies. Notably, P0.1 was almost never > 3.5 cmH2O, which has been proposed as a threshold for high drive-in adults. We also found minimal differences in the distribution of P0.1 as a function of pediatric age [13]. This highlights that in children P0.1 and ∆PES should not be used interchangeably as a marker for respiratory effort.

We believe there are clear clinical implications for these findings when treating children with PARDS, particularly for the situations when P0.1 underestimates respiratory effort, as high effort could lead to self-inflicted lung and diaphragm injury during mechanical ventilation with abnormal lungs [2, 7]. P0.1 is calculated as the deflection in airway pressure during breath occlusion in the first 100 ms of the breath. Hence, P0.1 is likely to underestimate effort in circumstances where the slope of airway pressure deflection is attenuated or low (i.e. slower deflection) (Additional file 1: Figure E5). We found that higher opioid dosing, higher tidal volume and increased airway resistance were risk factors for P0.1 underestimating high effort. Even though opioids exposure may increase because of tachyphylaxis overtime in the same way as the proportion of high effort increased over the course of ventilation, a high level of exposure and lower pain scales remain associated with high underestimated effort after adjusting for the day of ventilation. Opioids change breathing patterns in a few ways, most notably by slowing the respiratory rate, and potentially lengthening the inspiratory time to maintain a similar or higher tidal volume [23]. This could attenuate the slope of airway pressure deflection, making P0.1 artificially low. The same may occur in patients with high airway resistance, where long, slow respirations provide a mechanical advantage to promote more laminar flow. In addition, artificially low P0.1 can also be seen with intrinsic PEEP, which may be more common with high airway resistance and high tidal volume contributing to dynamic hyperinflation and incomplete relaxation [15]. Higher doses of opioids have also been associated with expiratory muscle activation during anesthesia [24], and may contribute to intrinsic PEEP as has been seen in adults with ARDS [25]. With intrinsic PEEP, the real decrease in PES during the first 100 ms of respiratory effort may not be fully reflected with a similar drop in airway pressure. While previous adult studies have contended that these small differences are not clinically meaningful [26], we found much lower values of P0.1 in children as compared to adults, potentially making the effect of intrinsic PEEP more impactful. Unfortunately, we did not use gastric pressure in conjunction with esophageal pressure, which would have allowed us to quantify this more effectively [27]. Therefore, we believe providers should use caution when using P0.1 as a surrogate for patient’s effort in circumstances of high airway resistance, intrinsic PEEP, or with high doses of opioids.

We found that there were relatively few circumstances when P0.1 overestimated effort (“higher-than-expected” P0.1 values relative to esophageal pressure). A priori we hypothesized that this group would be comprised of patients with respiratory muscle weakness, where high drive (P0.1) did not translate into strong effort. Given the small size of this subgroup, we may have been underpowered to detect significant differences in respiratory muscle strength. We did find that patients in this group had higher alveolar dead space fraction, and lower airway resistance. Alveolar dead space is a major contributor to respiratory drive, so it is not surprising that P0.1 would be higher in these patients. Interestingly, these patients also had more preserved lung compliance than other groups, and lower airway resistance. Hence, it is likely that the high drive, manifest with the higher P0.1, changed slope of the breath, but because these patients had more normal respiratory load (resistance, compliance), effort was not high. This could explain the over-estimation of ∆PES from P0.1. These observations are consistent with previous reports on adults with high drive [28], and high drive (ie P0.1 > 3.5 cmH2O) has been recently shown to be associated with a higher mortality and a longer duration of MV in adults [29]. It would be interesting to specifically study outcomes of this subgroup, where drive is high without high effort, to understand whether the relationship between P0.1 and mortality is related to drive itself, effort, or a combination of the two. Unfortunately, this subgroup is too small, which may make these types of outcomes analyses difficult.

Our study has limitations. First, it was a secondary analysis of a RCT which titrated support in the usual care group based upon respiratory effort. Therefore, some of the values observed for P0.1 and delta Pes may be modulated by the study intervention. This would affect the data presented about longitudinal effort and drive, but it is unlikely that this will affect the respiratory phenotypes we described. Inclusion criteria were also based on the parent study, with a selected population of children with abnormal lungs (ie, PARDS) and the exclusion of patients with intracranial hypertension, severe lower airway disease, and critical airways. There could be important differences between P0.1 and ΔPES in these populations, particularly as it may relate to central drive and airway resistance, which we did not capture in this analysis. Second, P0.1 measurements were obtained through secondary analysis of recorded waveforms, rather than what is being reported directly from the ventilator. While previous studies have corroborated that these values are similar to one another, the pediatric data is more limited. We believe the post-processed data was the most comprehensive to use for this analysis because it uses the gold standard approach (expiratory hold). In addition, we limited variability by taking the median of 3 holds and ensured consistency between investigators. Third, ∆PES is only a surrogate for respiratory effort as it does not account for the contribution from the chest wall like the muscular pressure (Pmus). However, we have previously shown that ∆PES is strongly correlated with Pmus [13] in children, and restricting analysis to patients who can have chest wall compliance calculated (must tolerate inspiratory hold) introduces an inherent selection bias against patients with very high effort and drive. Fourth, the study was conducted at a single site, which may limit generalizability to other healthcare settings.

Conclusion

In children with PARDS, circumstances where P0.1 may underestimate high respiratory effort are not infrequent. Clinicians should be cautious about interpreting relatively low P0.1 as a reflection of normal or low effort, particularly for children who have evidence of airway obstruction, are breathing with a high tidal volume, or are on high doses of opioids. Further studies are needed to investigate the impact of measures of respiratory effort, drive, and the presence of such mismatch phenotypes on clinical outcome.
