Perceptions of medical dramas among medical students in the West Bank, Palestine: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Bessan Hamed Dababseh, Mahdi W Suboh, Israa I Ismail, Ayman G Ikhlayel, Osaid Dabbas, Diar N Abu Khdeir, Abdallah Dababseh, Mones H Atatre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical dramas are widely consumed by medical students globally and may constitute an informal or hidden curriculum influencing professional identity, empathy, and ethical reasoning. Palestinian medical students represent an understudied population navigating a resource-constrained and geopolitically complex healthcare context.ObjectivesTo investigate the perceptions of Palestinian medical students regarding medical dramas, including viewing habits, assessments of clinical and ethical realism, psychological and behavioural impacts, and the potential role of such media as an informal educational resource.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 638 undergraduate medical students from five universities in the West Bank, Palestine, using convenience and snowball sampling. Data were collected via an online structured questionnaire adapted and culturally validated from the Czarny et al. (2008) instrument, incorporating forward-back translation, pilot testing (n = 15), and internal consistency assessment (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81). Descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests were applied.ResultsMean age was 21.1 ± 1.65 years; 66.8% were female. Most participants (73.4%) had watched medical dramas, primarily via digital streaming platforms. While 77.3% perceived clinical scenes as only slightly or moderately realistic, 41.9% considered ethical content to be moderately accurately depicted. Approximately 46.6% reported increased empathy and 47.2% reported increased study motivation. Drama viewers were significantly more likely to rate informal sources—family (p < 0.001), friends (p = 0.021), and online news (p = 0.037)—as important for ethical guidance, compared with non-viewers.ConclusionsPalestinian medical students engage substantially with medical dramas and appraise their content critically. Associations between drama viewing and increased empathy, study motivation, and reliance on informal ethical guidance sources suggest a potential hidden-curriculum effect. Given the cross-sectional design and convenience sampling, causal inferences cannot be drawn. These findings support cautious integration of medical dramas into bioethics and professionalism curricula as supplementary teaching tools.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09868-3.Keywords: Medical education, Medical dramas, Bioethics, Professionalism, Palestinian medical students, Hidden curriculum, Entertainment-education, Cultivation theory

Introduction

Medical dramas, which combine procedural clinical narratives with humanistic and ethical dilemmas, have constituted an influential cultural genre since the 1950s [1]. Contemporary series such as Grey’s Anatomy, House M.D., and The Good Doctor attract global audiences through dramatised portrayals of medical problem-solving, interpersonal dynamics, and emotionally charged decision-making [2].

Although produced primarily for entertainment, medical dramas frequently function as vehicles for ‘entertainment-education,’ potentially shaping viewers’ medical knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions of healthcare institutions [3]. For medical students, these programmes may serve as a pervasive informal or hidden curriculum, influencing empathy, moral reasoning, and the formation of professional identity [4].

Cultivation Theory, originally proposed by Gerbner and colleagues, posits that sustained exposure to mass media narratives may gradually shape individuals’ perceptions of social reality [5]. Applied to the medical context, this theoretical framework suggests that repeated viewing of dramatised clinical scenarios could influence how medicine and healthcare are perceived by students at formative stages of professional development [1]. Nevertheless, frequent depictions of procedural errors, implausibly rapid diagnoses, and unprofessional conduct risk reinforcing misconceptions about clinical practice [3].

Modern audiences, including medical students, increasingly access these dramas through digital streaming platforms rather than conventional broadcast television, enabling on-demand and repeated viewing [6]. While the educational implications of medical drama consumption have been investigated in North American and European contexts [2, 3, 7, 8], research from the Arab world remains limited. A study from Saudi Arabia has demonstrated a significant influence on professional attitudes and ethical perceptions [9].

To our knowledge, no published research has examined these dynamics among Palestinian medical students, who operate within a healthcare system characterised by severe resource constraints and a complex geopolitical context [10]. Understanding how this population perceives dramatised medical portrayals is essential for educators seeking to address potential misconceptions and leverage popular media as a pedagogical resource.

This study aims to investigate the perceptions of medical dramas among undergraduate medical students in the West Bank, Palestine, focusing on: (1) viewing habits and platform preferences; (2) assessments of clinical and ethical realism; (3) psychological and behavioural impacts, including empathy and motivation; and (4) the relationship between drama viewing and sources of ethical guidance.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted among undergraduate medical students enrolled at five Palestinian universities in the West Bank: Palestine Polytechnic University (Hebron), Hebron University, Al-Quds University (Jerusalem), An-Najah National University (Nablus), and Arab American University (Jenin). Data collection was conducted between 1 July and 1 August 2025.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Research Ethics Committee at Palestine Polytechnic University (Approval No.: EA/2025/74). Participation was voluntary; electronic informed consent was obtained prior to survey initiation. All responses were anonymous and stored on password-protected devices accessible only to the research team.

Study sample and participant recruitment

A total of 638 undergraduate medical students were recruited using non-probability convenience and snowball sampling. Invitations were distributed via university-affiliated social media platforms and WhatsApp student groups; participants were encouraged to share the survey link with peers. Eligible participants were undergraduate medical students enrolled in any academic year. Faculty, postgraduate trainees, medical interns, students from non-medical programmes, and students from Gazan universities (excluded for logistical reasons) were ineligible.

Data collection instrument

The questionnaire was adapted from the validated instrument of Czarny et al. [7], originally developed and validated in a North American context. As the original instrument was not designed for use in Arabic-speaking Palestinian populations, a systematic adaptation process was undertaken: (i) forward translation into Arabic by two independent bilingual researchers with medical education expertise, followed by back-translation to verify semantic equivalence; (ii) cultural adaptation, involving the removal of items pertaining to Western medicolegal frameworks not applicable in Palestine, and minor rephrasing for cultural clarity without altering conceptual domains; (iii) pilot testing with 15 students from the target population (responses excluded from final analysis) to assess clarity, content validity, and completion time; and (iv) internal consistency assessment across Likert-scale items, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81, indicative of good internal consistency. It is explicitly acknowledged that the adapted questionnaire constitutes a modified instrument and was not subjected to full independent psychometric re-validation (e.g., confirmatory factor analysis, test-retest reliability). This limitation is discussed in the Limitations section.

Questionnaire structure

The questionnaire comprised four sections: (1) demographics (age, gender, academic year, university); (2) viewing habits (prior-year exposure, frequency, platform, co-viewing); (3) perceptions of clinical realism, ethical accuracy, and professional depictions, assessed via five-point Likert scales; and (4) psychological and behavioural impacts and sources of ethical guidance. Estimated completion time was 4–5 min. Google Forms was used for administration; one response per account was enforced. No email addresses were collected.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Likert-scale items were coded numerically (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) and treated as categorical ordinal variables. Descriptive statistics are reported as absolute frequencies and proportions (%). Between-group associations were evaluated using Chi-square tests of independence; Fisher’s exact test was applied where expected cell frequencies were fewer than five. All tests were two-tailed; significance was defined as p < 0.05. Responses missing entire questionnaire sections were excluded from analysis; item-level missing data were excluded from respective analyses.

Strengths and limitations of study design

The study recruited students from five of the seven accredited medical schools in the West Bank across all six academic years, providing reasonable coverage of the target population. However, the non-probability sampling strategy may have introduced self-selection bias. The cross-sectional design precludes assessment of temporal sequence or causality. These limitations are further addressed in the Discussion.

Results

Participants were distributed across all six academic years; the fourth and fifth years were most represented (19.3% and 20.8%). The largest proportion were enrolled at An-Najah National University (n = 202, 31.7%). Full demographic data are presented in Table 1.

Of all participants, 468 (73.4%) reported having watched at least one medical drama. Most viewers (62.0%) watched fewer than once per week; the majority (84.2%) watched alone. Approximately one-third (35.4%) reported no prior formal bioethics training.

Among drama viewers (n = 468), 65.8% believed medical dramas significantly shape public perceptions of doctors, and 49.6% reported that these shows influenced their interest in a specific medical specialty. Most participants (34.4% slightly realistic; 42.9% moderately realistic) rated clinical scenes as only partially reflective of real practice; only 13.5% rated them realistic or very realistic. For ethical accuracy, 41.9% selected moderately accurate, with only 25.2% choosing very or most accurate (Table 2).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive account of how undergraduate medical students in the West Bank, Palestine, engage with medical dramas and perceive their clinical, ethical, and professional content. The finding that 73.4% had watched medical dramas is broadly consistent with international comparators: 80% in the United States [7] and 74.2% in Saudi Arabia [9], suggesting widespread engagement across diverse cultural contexts.

The predominant use of digital streaming platforms aligns with documented global shifts away from scheduled broadcast television, particularly among younger audiences [6]. On-demand access enables repeated viewing of clinically or ethically complex scenarios at students’ own pace [3]. In the Arab and MENA context, increased digital engagement has been associated with health-related knowledge acquisition and decision-making [11].

Despite substantial exposure, Palestinian medical students demonstrated a critical and discerning stance toward these programmes. Most rated clinical scenes as only slightly or moderately realistic, and a similar pattern emerged for ethical accuracy. Participants identified medical errors, unprofessional behaviour, and portrayals of death as the most salient bioethical themes, mirroring findings from Cambra-Badii et al. [3] and Alahmari [9]. This critical appraisal is consistent with the role of clinical experience in calibrating media perceptions: as students accumulate first-hand clinical exposure, they develop a more nuanced understanding of the gap between dramatised and actual medical practice [8].

Cultivation Theory [5] predicts that sustained media exposure may shape beliefs about social reality. It is critical to note that the cross-sectional design of the present study precludes any causal or directional inference. Our findings do not demonstrate a cultivation effect in the mechanistic sense; rather, they identify an association between drama viewing and attitudinal patterns, such as greater perceived importance of informal ethical guidance sources. This association could reflect pre-existing tendencies among students who self-select to watch medical dramas, rather than media-induced value shifts. The distinct geopolitical environment and resource-constrained healthcare system of the West Bank may additionally provide students with a comparative framework that enhances critical appraisal of ‘Hollywood medicine’ [12].

The ambivalent responses regarding the doctor-patient relationship confirm that medical dramas can function as a negative model, prompting reflection on professional standards through contrast rather than emulation [3, 13]. Faculty-facilitated critical discussions of such portrayals could leverage this dynamic educationally [14, 15].

The associations between drama viewing and increased empathy (46.6%) and study motivation (47.2%) are consistent with prior evidence that narrative formats can enhance emotional engagement with patient stories [16]. Whether such self-reported associations translate into sustained professional behaviours requires longitudinal investigation [17].

The significant association between drama viewing and greater perceived importance of informal ethical guidance sources (family, friends, online news) is particularly noteworthy given that approximately one-third of students reported no formal bioethics training. These findings highlight the potential for a hidden-curriculum effect, whereby media exposure supplements or substitutes for formal ethics instruction [4, 18]. This has direct implications for curriculum design, particularly for early-year students with limited structured bioethics exposure.

These findings suggest that thoughtfully selected medical drama content could serve as an accessible and engaging supplementary pedagogical tool within bioethics or professionalism curricula. Educators could use selected scenes as case prompts to elicit identification of ethical conflicts, critical comparisons with actual clinical decision-making, and reflection on professional standards. Narrative-based approaches have been shown to enhance ethical reasoning and empathy [3, 16], and the availability and familiarity of streaming content makes this approach feasible with minimal resource investment.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, convenience and snowball sampling may have produced self-selection bias, potentially overrepresenting students with greater interest in medical dramas; the findings should not be uncritically generalised. Second, all outcomes rely on self-reported data, which are susceptible to recall bias and social-desirability effects. Third, the adapted questionnaire demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81) but was not subjected to full psychometric re-validation (confirmatory factor analysis, test-retest reliability, construct validity); measurement precision is therefore uncertain. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes any determination of temporal sequence or causality. Fifth, exclusion of students from Gazan universities limits generalisability to all Palestinian medical students. Future research employing probability sampling, longitudinal designs, and qualitative methods would substantially strengthen the evidence base.

Conclusion

Medical dramas appear to be a prevalent informal source of exposure for medical students in the West Bank, Palestine. While students maintain a critical stance toward clinical realism, our findings suggest an association between viewing these dramas and self-reported increases in empathy and motivation. Furthermore, the reliance on non-academic sources for ethical guidance among viewers highlights a potential ‘hidden curriculum’ effect. However, given the study’s cross-sectional design and convenience sampling, these results should be interpreted with caution and cannot imply direct causality. These dramas may serve as a supplementary tool for ‘entertainment-education’ to stimulate critical discussion, particularly for students with limited formal bioethics exposure. Future longitudinal research is needed to further elucidate the long-term impact of such media on professional development.
