A Comparative Study to Assess the Effect of Nonpharmacological Pain Relief Methods on Selected Maternal Parameters among Primigravida Women Admitted in Labor Ward in a Selected Hospital at Perinthalmanna, Kerala
Authors: Ahalya Panneerselvam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Labor pain is a significant challenge for primigravida women, often leading to adverse maternal and fetal outcomes.Aim:This comparative study evaluated the effects of two nonpharmacological pain relief methods—slow paced breathing technique and Hoku point pressure—on selected maternal parameters, including pain perception, behavioral responses, fetal heart rate, cervical dilatation, and vital signs.Methods:A quasiexperimental design was employed with 90 primigravida women divided into three groups: Intervention Group I (slow paced breathing, n = 30), Intervention Group II (Hoku point pressure, n = 30), and Control group (routine care, n = 30). Data were collected using the visual analogue scale (VAS) for pain, behavioral rating scale (BRS) for behavior, and observational checklists for physiological parameters. Pre- and postintervention assessments were conducted during active labor.Results:Results indicated significant reductions in mean pain scores in both intervention groups compared to the control (P < 0.001), with Hoku point pressure showing greater efficacy (postmean: 3.8 vs. 4.5 for breathing). Behavioral scores improved similarly, and maternal parameters like blood pressure and fetal heart rate remained stable without adverse effects.Conclusion:The study concludes that nonpharmacological methods are effective, safe alternatives for labor pain management, potentially reducing the need for pharmacological interventions. These findings support integrating such techniques into obstetric nursing practice.

INTRODUCTION

Childbirth is a profound experience, yet labor pain remains one of the most intense pains women endure, often rated higher than other acute pains. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 140 million births occur annually, with many women experiencing severe distress during labor, particularly primigravida who lack prior experience.[1] This pain arises from uterine contractions, cervical dilatation, and perineal stretching, leading to physiological responses like increased heart rate, blood pressure, and stress hormones, which can adversely affect maternal and fetal health, including fetal hypoxia or prolonged labor.[2]

Traditional management relies on pharmacological agents like opioids or epidurals, but these carry risks such as respiratory depression, nausea, and delayed labor progression.[3] Nonpharmacological methods, rooted in holistic nursing, offer safer alternatives by promoting endorphin release and distraction. Slow paced breathing enhances oxygenation and relaxation, while Hoku point pressure (LI4 acupressure) stimulates nerve pathways to block pain signals, as per gate control theory. Studies in India and globally highlight high labor pain prevalence (70–90%) among primigravida, underscoring the need for effective, accessible interventions in resource-limited settings like Kerala, where cultural preferences favor natural methods.[14]

This research addresses gaps in comparative evidence between these techniques, focusing on maternal parameters in a South Indian context, where maternal mortality rates, though declining, still involve labor complications.

AIM

The aim of this study was to compare the effects of slow paced breathing technique and Hoku point pressure on selected maternal parameters (pain perception, behavioral response, fetal heart rate, cervical dilatation, descent of fetal head, maternal pulse, body temperature, and blood pressure) among primigravida women in active labor.

METHODOLOGY

This quasiexperimental study with a pretest posttest Control group design was conducted at a selected hospital in Perinthalmanna, Kerala, from January 2022 to December 2022. The population comprised primigravida women admitted to the labor ward in active phase (cervical dilatation 4–7 cm). Using purposive sampling, 90 participants were recruited based on inclusion criteria: gestational age 37–42 weeks, singleton pregnancy, no high-risk factors, and willingness to participate. Exclusion criteria included medical complications or prior pain relief use.

Participants were assigned to three groups (n = 30 each): Group I received slow paced breathing (deep inhalation for four counts, exhalation for six counts, repeated for 20 minutes); Group II received Hoku point pressure (firm pressure on LI4 point for 20 minutes); Control group received routine care. Interventions were administered by the researcher during contractions.

Data collection tools included: Demographic proforma, VAS (0–10 scale for pain), BRS (0–4 for behavioral distress), and an observational checklist for physiological parameters (fetal heart rate via Doppler, cervical dilatation via vaginal exam, vitals via monitor). Reliability was established (Cronbach’s alpha 0.89 for VAS, 0.85 for BRS). Preintervention assessment at baseline, post at 30 minutes and 1 hour.

Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board, with informed consent. Data analysis used SPSS v. 25: descriptive statistics (mean, SD, frequency), inferential (paired t-test, analysis of variance (ANOVA), analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to control pretest scores).

RESULTS

DISCUSSION

The findings demonstrate that nonpharmacological methods effectively alleviate labor pain without compromising maternal parameters, aligning with gate control theory where sensory stimulation modulates pain.[5] Hoku point pressure outperformed slow breathing, possibly due to direct endorphin release via acupressure points, consistent with prior studies (e.g., Smith et al., 2020).[2] Pain reduction in interventions (40–48%) exceeds control (14%), supporting hypotheses that these methods improve pain and behavior scores.[67]

Stability in fetal heart rate and vitals suggests safety, contrasting pharmacological risks. Limitations include single-site setting and no long-term follow-up. Implications for nursing: Train midwives in these techniques to enhance patient-centered care, especially in low-resource areas. Future research could explore combinations or multiparous women.
