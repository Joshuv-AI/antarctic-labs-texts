Effectiveness of pain neuroscience education in women with dysmenorrhea: controlled clinical trial
Authors: Çiğdem Inkaya, Osman Çoban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDysmenorrhea is a highly prevalent gynecological condition that negatively affects menstrual pain experience, daily activities, and overall quality of life. Pain neuroscience education (PNE) is a non-pharmacological intervention designed to reshape maladaptive pain beliefs and promote a more adaptive understanding of pain mechanisms. This study aimed to examine the effects of PNE on pain beliefs, menstrual pain intensity, and the functional impact of dysmenorrhea among women reporting symptoms consistent with primary dysmenorrhea.MethodsThis prospective controlled clinical trial included 74 women aged ≥18 years who reported dysmenorrhea symptoms consistent with primary dysmenorrhea. Participants were allocated consecutively to either a PNE group (n = 37) or a control group (n = 37). The PNE group received three weekly online educational sessions, whereas the control group continued their routine activities without intervention. The primary outcomes were pain intensity assessed using the Short-Form McGill Pain Questionnaire (SF-MPQ) and pain-related beliefs assessed using the Pain Beliefs Questionnaire (PBQ). The secondary exploratory outcome was functional impact assessed using the Scale for Assessing the Impact of Dysmenorrhea on Daily Life (SAIDDL). All outcomes were collected using standardized self-reported online questionnaires before the intervention and during the first menstrual cycle following the intervention.ResultsCompared with the control group, the PNE group showed greater reductions in sensory, affective, and total pain scores on the SF-MPQ. The PNE group also demonstrated greater improvement in psychological pain beliefs, while total PBQ findings were more modest and should be interpreted cautiously. Greater improvement in functional impact was also observed in the PNE group, as reflected by lower SAIDDL scores.ConclusionPNE was associated with short-term improvements in self-reported pain intensity, psychological pain beliefs, and daily functioning in women with dysmenorrhea. These findings suggest that PNE may represent a promising complementary non-pharmacological approach. Future studies with randomized designs, larger samples, and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm these findings.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT06732778, Registration date: September 12, 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04530-y.

Introduction

Dysmenorrhea, commonly referred to as menstrual pain, is one of the most frequent gynecological disorders among women of reproductive age, particularly adolescents and young adults [1]. The condition is characterized by cyclic lower abdominal pain that occurs just before or during menstruation and may persist for two to three days [2]. Epidemiological data indicate that dysmenorrhea affects approximately 50% to 90% of women of reproductive age, and about one in four experience symptoms severe enough to impair daily activities. Despite its high prevalence, dysmenorrhea was not recognized as a distinct medical condition until the 1970s [3]. It is now understood to be a substantial public health issue, contributing to decreased academic and occupational performance, absenteeism, and reduced quality of life in affected individuals [4].

The pathophysiology of dysmenorrhea is primarily attributed to excessive endometrial production of prostaglandins, particularly prostaglandin F₂α and E₂, which increase uterine contractility, reduce uterine blood flow, and activate pelvic nociceptors [5]. While dysmenorrhea is mainly considered a cyclic nociceptive condition, emerging evidence suggests that repeated nociceptive input may also influence central pain processing in some individuals [2]. Additionally, neuroendocrine fluctuations and psychological stress may modulate pain perception and contribute to variability in symptom severity [6].

The management of dysmenorrhea typically integrates both pharmacological and non-pharmacological strategies. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) remain the first-line treatment due to their ability to reduce prostaglandin synthesis and uterine hypercontractility [7]. Hormonal contraceptives may further alleviate symptoms by regulating endometrial activity and reducing prostaglandin-mediated nociception [8]. Non-pharmacological approaches—including exercise, heat therapy, yoga, mindfulness, and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS)—have also demonstrated benefits through mechanisms such as improved circulation, modulation of pain perception, and reduction of emotional distress [9–11]. More recently, pain neuroscience education (PNE) has been proposed as a complementary strategy aimed at improving pain understanding, addressing maladaptive pain beliefs, and supporting self-management [12].

PNE is based on a biopsychosocial framework that aims to improve understanding of pain neurophysiology and pain modulation processes. Rather than targeting the primary nociceptive mechanisms of dysmenorrhea, PNE may help address cognitive and behavioral factors associated with the pain experience. Some studies suggest that women with dysmenorrhea may exhibit maladaptive pain cognitions, such as catastrophizing and fear-related beliefs, which could influence pain perception and coping responses [13, 14]. By improving pain literacy and promoting adaptive coping strategies, PNE may contribute to improved pain-related outcomes.

Although educational interventions targeting pain mechanisms have been extensively investigated in chronic pain populations, evidence regarding their role in dysmenorrhea remains limited. In particular, few studies have examined the short-term effects of PNE on pain intensity, pain-related beliefs, and functional impact in women with dysmenorrhea, especially when delivered through accessible online formats. Therefore, further research is needed to clarify whether educational approaches such as PNE may provide complementary benefits alongside conventional dysmenorrhea management.

Therefore, the aim of this controlled clinical trial was to investigate the effects of an online PNE program on pain intensity, pain-related beliefs, and functional impact in women reporting symptoms consistent with primary dysmenorrhea compared with a control group that continued their usual daily activities. It was hypothesized that participants receiving PNE would demonstrate greater improvements in pain intensity and pain-related beliefs compared to the control group.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was designed as a prospective controlled clinical trial and conducted online between October 1, 2024, and February 1, 2025. Ethical approval was obtained from the Üsküdar University Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee (Meeting No: 05; Decision No: 61351342/May 2024-56; Date: May 28, 2024). The study was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT06732778, Registration date: September 12, 2024). All participants provided electronic informed consent prior to enrollment, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants and sampling

The study population consisted of women aged 18 years and older who reported experiencing dysmenorrhea. Eligible participants were required to be at least 18 years of age, to experience dysmenorrhea regularly, and to volunteer to participate in the study. Women were excluded if they were younger than 18 years, had a history of pregnancy, previously experienced pelvic infections, were using hormonal contraceptives or intrauterine devices, had menstrual durations shorter than 3 days or longer than 8 days, or had menstrual cycles outside the 21–35-day range.

A total of 74 volunteers meeting these criteria were enrolled and allocated to two parallel groups using a consecutive allocation procedure: a pain neuroscience education (PNE) group (n = 37) and a control group (n = 37).

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) to determine the required sample size. Based on previous studies reporting moderate associations between dysmenorrhea and functional outcomes [15], a medium effect size was assumed. Assuming a two-tailed α level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 74 participants.Therefore, all eligible participants were included and allocated to the PNE and control groups in a 1:1 ratio.

Intervention procedures

Participants in the intervention group received a structured Pain Neuroscience Education (PNE) program delivered online by the researcher, a physiotherapist trained in pain neuroscience education principles. The intervention consisted of three consecutive weekly sessions, each lasting approximately 30 min.

All sessions were delivered live via an online video conferencing platform in small groups to allow interaction and discussion. The intervention followed a standardized educational framework to ensure consistency across participants.

The educational content aimed to improve participants’ understanding of the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying dysmenorrhea. Topics included the neurophysiology of pain, central sensitization, the interaction between cognitive–emotional processes and pain perception, and evidence-based self-management strategies. Standardized presentation slides, visual educational materials, metaphors (e.g., the nervous system as an “overprotective alarm system”), and interactive discussions were used to enhance engagement and comprehension [16, 17].

Sessions emphasized that pain is a protective output of the nervous system and may be amplified through increased neural sensitivity, even in the absence of tissue damage [18]. Dysmenorrhea mechanisms—such as prostaglandin-related uterine contractions, inflammatory mediators, and transient hypoxia—were explained as physiological rather than harmful. Psychological contributors including stress, catastrophizing, and negative menstrual beliefs were discussed as factors that modulate central processing and intensify pain [19].

Participants were introduced to practical self-management strategies such as diaphragmatic breathing, relaxation exercises, gentle aerobic activity, yoga-based movements, heat application, and general lifestyle regulation. Misconceptions surrounding dysmenorrhea were addressed, and pain-related beliefs were reframed using contemporary pain science principles to support adaptive coping.

Attendance was monitored for each session, and all participants in the intervention group completed the three-session program. The intervention content and delivery format were standardized and not individualized to ensure consistency of exposure.

Participants in the control group did not receive any educational or therapeutic intervention and were instructed to continue their usual daily routines. This passive control design allowed comparison of PNE effects with the natural course of dysmenorrhea and typical coping behaviors.

Outcome assessments

Outcome measures were collected at two predefined time points: at baseline (prior to the intervention) and during the first menstrual cycle following completion of the intervention. All data were obtained using standardized and secure online questionnaires to ensure methodological consistency, data integrity, and participant confidentiality.

The primary outcomes of the study were pain intensity and pain-related beliefs. Pain intensity was assessed using the Short-Form McGill Pain Questionnaire (SF-MPQ), a validated instrument that evaluates both sensory and affective dimensions of pain. Pain-related beliefs were evaluated using the Pain Beliefs Questionnaire (PBQ), which assesses individuals’ cognitive interpretations of pain through organic and psychological belief subscales.

The secondary outcome was the functional impact of dysmenorrhea on daily life, evaluated using the Scale for Assessing the Impact of Dysmenorrhea on Daily Life (SAIDDL). This measure was included as an exploratory indicator of functional interference associated with menstrual pain.

All assessments were administered electronically via secure online forms to ensure standardized administration procedures and to protect participant privacy.

Short-Form McGill Pain Questionnaire (SF-MPQ)

Pain intensity was assessed using the SF-MPQ, a widely validated instrument for evaluating sensory and affective pain components [20]. The SF-MPQ includes 15 descriptors (11 sensory and 4 affective), each rated on a 0–3 scale (0 = none, 3 = severe), producing sensory, affective, and total pain scores. It also contains a global pain intensity item with five verbal categories. In this study, the first two sections of the SF-MPQ were used to assess pre- and post-intervention pain levels.

Pain Beliefs Questionnaire (PBQ)

Pain-related beliefs were evaluated using the PBQ, a validated instrument developed to measure individuals’ cognitive interpretations of pain. The PBQ includes two subscales: Organic Beliefs (8 items) and Psychological Beliefs (4 items). Items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from “never” to “always.” Subscale scores are calculated by averaging item responses, yielding Organic, Psychological, and Total PBQ scores [21].

Scale for Assessing the Impact of Dysmenorrhea on Daily Life (SAIDDL)

Functional impact was measured using a 0–10 numerical interference scale adapted from the conceptual structure of the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), a widely accepted instrument for evaluating subjective symptom intensity [22]. Participants rated how much dysmenorrhea affected their daily activities on the third day of their menstrual cycle, with 0 representing “no impact” and 10 indicating “complete disruption.” Although this tool has not yet undergone formal psychometric validation, its scoring logic and symptom-focused format align with VAS-based pain interference assessments and provide a practical indicator of functional impact. Therefore, findings related to SAIDDL should be interpreted as exploratory and considered preliminary.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables.

The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variances between groups was evaluated using Levene’s test. As the assumptions for parametric testing were satisfied, parametric statistical methods were applied.

Baseline and post-intervention between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests, while within-group (pre–post) comparisons were conducted using paired-samples t-tests. To examine the interaction effects of time and group, a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) (Time × Group) was performed for each outcome variable. The interaction effect was considered the primary statistical comparison for evaluating intervention effectiveness.

Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (η²p) and interpreted as small (0.01), medium (0.06), or large (≥ 0.14). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

No missing data were observed; therefore, no data imputation procedures were required. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the limited number of predefined outcomes, no formal correction for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni adjustment) was applied. However, the primary interpretation was based on interaction effects, and the results were interpreted cautiously to minimize the potential risk of type I error inflation.

Because all participants completed the study and no missing outcome data were observed, analyses were conducted on complete cases.

This controlled clinical trial was designed and reported with reference to the CONSORT 2010 reporting guidelines for transparency of reporting. A completed CONSORT checklist has been provided as supplementary material.

Discussion

This study examined the effects of pain neuroscience education (PNE) on pain intensity, pain-related beliefs, and daily functioning in women with dysmenorrhea. The findings indicated that PNE was associated with improvements in the targeted outcomes, suggesting that educational interventions addressing pain mechanisms may contribute to the management of menstrual pain. By incorporating metaphors, visual explanations, and interactive discussions, the program encouraged participants to reinterpret dysmenorrhea from a threatening, biomedical-oriented experience to a more understandable and manageable neurophysiological process. The successful online delivery of PNE further highlights the feasibility of digital education models, which may help overcome common barriers to accessing non-pharmacological pain management strategies.

PNE is grounded in contemporary pain science, emphasizing the dynamic interplay between peripheral nociception, central modulation, cognitive–emotional processes, and environmental influences [23]. It challenges traditional biomedical assumptions that attribute pain solely to tissue damage, instead promoting an understanding of pain as an output of the nervous system shaped by multiple biological, psychological, and social factors. Prior research has shown that PNE can reduce symptoms, modify maladaptive beliefs, decrease catastrophizing, and improve function in individuals with chronic musculoskeletal, neuropathic, and postoperative pain [16, 24, 25]. The present study extends these findings to dysmenorrhea, suggesting that similar biopsychosocial mechanisms may also play a role in menstrual pain. Given that some women with dysmenorrhea may exhibit altered pain processing and negative cognitive–emotional responses, educational approaches targeting these processes may provide potential benefits.

A wide range of treatments exists for dysmenorrhea, including NSAIDs, hormonal contraceptives, heat therapy, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), exercise, and mind–body practices. Although pharmacological agents—particularly NSAIDs—remain first-line interventions due to their prostaglandin-inhibiting effects, long-term use may be constrained by gastrointestinal, renal, and cardiovascular risks [26]. In recent years, growing attention has shifted toward complementary and non-pharmacological interventions that provide symptom relief without medication-related adverse effects. Evidence from network meta-analyses suggests that interventions such as acupressure, exercise, mind–body techniques, and thermal therapies can reduce menstrual pain with favorable safety profiles [10, 27]. The present findings are consistent with this trend and suggest that PNE may represent an additional non-pharmacological approach that could contribute to improvements in symptom severity and functional capacity.

Several studies support the potential of alternative interventions in dysmenorrhea management. Shah et al. [28] demonstrated that hypnosis produced sustained reductions in menstrual pain, highlighting the value of approaches that influence pain perception and cognitive–emotional processing. The present study is consistent with these findings, suggesting that PNE may contribute to reductions in pain and improvements in daily functioning, possibly through its effects on maladaptive cognitions such as fear-avoidance and catastrophizing.

Thermal interventions, such as topical heat therapy, have also been shown to improve dysmenorrhea symptoms by increasing blood flow, relaxing uterine musculature, and reducing nociceptor sensitivity. Jo and Choi [29] reported that heat application was superior to placebo and comparable to certain pharmacological agents in reducing pain intensity. Although the mechanisms differ from those of PNE, both interventions may provide symptom relief through accessible and non-invasive approaches. In the present study, PNE was similarly associated with improvements in pain outcomes and daily functioning, suggesting that educational interventions may complement traditional symptom-relief strategies.

Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) is another non-pharmacological modality with demonstrated efficacy. Manisha et al. [30] showed that high-frequency TENS produced reductions in lower abdominal and referred pain among adolescent girls. While TENS primarily acts through peripheral gating mechanisms and PNE through cognitive–affective modulation, both approaches aim to influence pain processing pathways. The present findings suggest that PNE may contribute to improved menstrual experiences through enhanced understanding of pain physiology and improved coping strategies.

Exercise-based interventions have also demonstrated beneficial effects in dysmenorrhea. Dehnavi et al. [31] and Arora et al. [32] found that aerobic exercise reduced pain intensity, improved quality of life, and decreased functional limitations. While PNE does not directly produce physiological tissue adaptations, it may support increased engagement in healthy behaviors by reducing fear and improving pain understanding. The improvements observed in daily functioning in this study are consistent with this interpretation.

Mind–body practices and manual therapies have also shown promise. Rakhshaee [33] demonstrated that yoga reduced both the intensity and duration of dysmenorrhea, while Kim et al. [34] reported improvements following abdominal meridian massage. These interventions may exert their effects through mechanisms such as autonomic regulation, stress reduction, and improved pelvic mobility. The improvements associated with PNE in the present study suggest that educational strategies may operate synergistically with such interventions by promoting adaptive coping and self-regulation.

Dietary interventions, including omega-3 fatty acids and vitamin E, have also been shown to reduce pain severity and decrease reliance on rescue medication [35, 36]. These approaches target inflammatory pathways associated with menstrual pain, whereas PNE primarily targets cognitive and neurophysiological aspects of pain processing. The present findings suggest that combining educational and lifestyle-based strategies may provide multidimensional benefits, although this warrants further investigation.

Another limitation of this study is the lack of true randomization and allocation concealment, which may have introduced selection bias. Additionally, outcomes were based on self-reported measures, which may be subject to reporting bias. Another limitation is the evaluation of multiple outcomes without formal correction for multiplicity. Although the primary analyses focused on interaction effects derived from repeated-measures ANOVA, the possibility of inflated type I error cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Furthermore, the relatively short follow-up period limited the ability to determine long-term effects of the intervention. As SAIDDL is an exploratory functional measure without formal psychometric validation, findings related to functional impact should also be interpreted cautiously.

Collectively, the present findings support the potential integration of educational interventions into dysmenorrhea management. By addressing cognitive and neurophysiological contributors to menstrual pain, PNE may contribute to reducing symptom burden, improving functional performance, and promoting self-management skills. As PNE does not involve pharmacological exposure and can be delivered online, it may represent a practical and cost-effective complementary option, particularly in settings where access to non-pharmacological care is limited. Future research is needed to examine long-term outcomes, evaluate randomized designs, and explore synergistic effects when combined with other therapeutic approaches.

Conclusion

This study suggests that pain neuroscience education (PNE) may be a promising non-pharmacological approach for the management of dysmenorrhea. By improving understanding of pain mechanisms and modifying pain-related beliefs, PNE was associated with reductions in pain intensity and improvements in daily functioning. These findings support the potential role of educational strategies as complementary approaches in dysmenorrhea management. Future research with randomized designs, larger samples, and longer follow-up periods is needed to confirm these findings.
