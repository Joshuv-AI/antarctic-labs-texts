Intracardiac Biopsy of a Right Ventricular Outflow Tract Mass: A Case of Interventional and Multimodality Imaging Collaboration
Authors: Benjamin Hofeld, Aaron AH Smith, Divyanshu Mohananey, Michael P Cinquegrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetermining the tissue type of intracardiac masses presents a challenging diagnostic problem given the difficulties that may arise in performing intracardiac biopsies. This case report outlines a technique used to biopsy a mass in the right ventricular outflow track in a patient with suspected metastatic cancer.Keywords: case report, intracardiac mass, multimodality imaging, right ventricular outflow tract

Case report

A 57-year-old man with a medical history of metastatic papillary thyroid cancer, severe mitral regurgitation after mitral valve repair, and mild nonobstructive coronary artery disease underwent a malignancy surveillance computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest, which revealed a new lobulated mass involving the right ventricular (RV) outflow tract (RVOT) proximal to the pulmonic valve (Figure 1A, B). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed an enhancing 4.5- × 2.7- × 2.5-cm mass involving the RVOT with small foci of associated thrombus, which resulted in narrowing of the RVOT (Figure 1C, D and Supplemental Video 1). At the time of the CT scan, the patient was not on active therapy. The RVOT mass likely represented metastatic papillary thyroid cancer. The patient’s oncologist felt it was important to have tissue for further analysis that would inform additional therapy for the patient. The patient was referred to cardiothoracic surgery for biopsy and resection but was declined given the patient’s underlying comorbidities. After a multidisciplinary team discussion, the patient underwent a percutaneous biopsy of the mass under fluoroscopic and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance in the cardiac catheterization laboratory.

Preprocedural TEE imaging of the RVOT mass was obtained in the cardiac catheterization laboratory (Figure 2A, B and Supplemental Videos 2 and 3). Right femoral vein access was obtained with a Pinnacle 8F × 10-cm sheath (Terumo). Owing to the prohibitory length of the biopsy forceps, 90-cm catheters were used, which were attached to a simple 3-way stopcock, as a standard Y-PTCA Tuohy Borst adaptor did not allow for adequate catheter advancement (Figure 3A, B). The left pulmonary artery was wired. An 8F 90-cm JR4 guide catheter was positioned in the pulmonary artery for hemodynamic assessment and then pulled back into the RVOT. Using fluoroscopy (Figure 4) and TEE guidance (Figure 3C), several attempts were made at positioning the guide catheter to align with the mass in the RVOT.

Initial attempts at biopsy were not successful given the lack of position stability of the guide when passing the biopsy forceps. Similar results were achieved when an 8F 90-cm Medtronic multipurpose guide catheter was used. Finally, an 8F 90-cm Cordis multipurpose guide catheter was successfully positioned adjacent to the RVOT mass with a stable position during emergence of the forceps, and 4 biopsy samples were obtained (Figure 5). Figure 2C shows biplane images of the mass and Figure 2D shows a biplane image of the biopsy forcep engaging the mass for sampling (Supplemental Video 4). At the conclusion of the procedure, there was no evidence of tamponade or any change in function of the pulmonic or tricuspid valves. Anesthesia was reversed and the patient underwent extubation in the catheterization laboratory. Pathology revealed a poorly differentiated metastatic carcinoma with a morphology and immunoprofile nonspecific for anaplastic thyroid carcinoma. Molecular phenotyping was favorable for the addition of pembrolizumab to the patient’s biologic therapy regimen.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the utility of multimodality imaging in safely and reproducibly performing transvenous biopsies on a nonobstructive RVOT mass. The molecular data derived from biopsy of this phenotypically divergent, cardioinvasive tumor helped to tailor oncologic therapy, potentially improving the patient’s prognosis. The use of preprocedural CT with multiplanar reconstruction and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging allowed for appropriate access, catheter selection, and sizing. The use of intraprocedural multiplanar and 3-dimensional (3D) TEE provided an essential additional layer of safety, preventing inappropriate endomyocardial tissue biopsy and electromechanical complications. Additionally, intraprocedural 3D TEE ensured accurate sampling and yield through real-time confirmation of apposition of the tumor by the biopsy forceps.

The literature describes a number of image-guided, transvenous biopsies of right atrial masses using digital subtraction angiography with or without TTE, TEE alone, or intracardiac echocardiography (ICE).1, 2, 3 However, case reports describing transvenous biopsies of RV masses are especially rare.4, 5, 6 Of these cases, only 2 RVOT masses were biopsied, 1 without the use of intraprocedural echocardiography, and the other using ICE.4,6 A minimally invasive, transthoracic, CT-guided biopsy technique has also been described, although, to our knowledge, it has never been replicated.7 The ability to perform minimally invasive biopsies in the rare case of RV mass—in a high surgical risk patient—has the potential to either reach a definitive diagnosis or change management plans for existing ones.

To our knowledge, this is the first case study describing transvenous, TEE-guided biopsy of an RVOT mass. Although ICE guidance precludes the need for deeper levels of anesthesia-guided sedation and potentially reduces personnel when compared with that for TEE, TEE confers several advantages in such cases. These include full 3D evaluation, biplanar imaging, improved far-field resolution, and the preclusion of larger-bore venous access.8,9

Our procedural technique diverged from that of Planchat et al,5 who shortened a 100-cm Judkins right 4 Launcher (Medtronic) by cutting and removing 15 cm of from the hub and then inserting a short segment of a 7F Terumo sheath in between the 2 segments of remaining catheter.4 We instead used shorter, 90-cm catheters attached to a simple 3-way stopcock, which further shortened the system, compared with the one containing a standard Y-PTCA Tuohy Borst adaptor. Using this configuration, multiple 90-cm catheters were used without the need for serial length modification.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates how an effective collaboration between multimodality imagers and interventional cardiologists can lead to a safe and successful transvenous biopsy of an RVOT intracardiac mass. To our knowledge, this is the first case describing transvenous, TEE-guided biopsy of an RVOT mass. The research reported has adhered to the relevant ethical guidelines.
